Specific resistance in wheat genotypes to leaf blotch (Zymoseptoria tritici)
Authors: Girma Ababa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the world, including Ethiopia, wheat leaf blotch is one of the most important diseases. Knowing the source of resistance in wheat genotypes is very important for developing varieties, pathogen variability studies, and taking management action. However, in Ethiopia, the study of gene-for-gene or minor gene-for-minor gene interactions and wheat genotype reactions are very limited. Therefore, this study aimed to answer these research gaps. Fifty-four genotypes and one differential line were evaluated against six pathotypes. The 330 treatment combinations with three replications were set up in a completely randomized factorial layout in the greenhouse. The host-pathogen interaction and seedling resistance were identified using the least significant difference method. At a 95% similarity level, the average linkage hierarchical clustering method and euclidean distance were used to classify the cultivars. Resistance assay detected 152 wheat-Z. tritici interactions (16 pathotype-specific resistances, 70 broad-spectrum resistances, and 66 susceptible), and 178 non-wheat-Z. tritici interactions, among all (n = 330) interactions. Genotypes like 21Eeit (046), 21Eeit (027), 41ESWYT (046), and the rest of the thirteen showed resistance to one pathotype (pathotype-specific or minor gene-for-minor gene interactions or vertical resistance). 54ISBWSN (018) and 54ISBWSN (113) indicated resistance reactions to six and five pathotypes, respectively. About 23 (42%) genotypes showed resistance reactions to more than two pathotypes, known as broad resistance. Differential line ESTANZUELA FEDERAL carrying the Lb7 gene was infective against five pathotypes and intermediate against one pathotype. Cluster analysis identified 25 groups based on the similarity of mean disease severity and suggested 45.4% resistance variability of wheat genotypes. The resistant genotypes identified are very important for further breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25073-6.

Introduction

The fungus Zymoseptoria tritici causes wheat leaf blotch disease1,2. It is one of the most severe wheat diseases in Ethiopia, the UK, and other Northern European countries3–6. It is characterized by a long latent phase that typically lasts 8–11 days before visible necrotic lesions appear7. Ababa1 found that small chlorotic patches appear on the wheat leaves as soon as the infection begins.

The importance of leaf blotch on wheat has been mentioned in a number of papers. The disease has distributed and affected wheat yield in several countries, such as Australia, Northern France, Germany, the Mediterranean basin, East Africa, South America, the UK, the United States, Russia, and a number of western European countries8–11. In France, Gouache et al.12 suggested that the average severity of leaf blotch was 40%. Again, 13–42% disease severity was recorded in Northern Tunisia13. El Jarroudi et al.14 reported 19–73.5.5% disease severity in Grand-Duchy. In Ethiopia, the disease intensity, such as severity and incidence, was 9.9–59.3% and 50–100%, respectively15. Currently, Ababa et al.16 also reported 15–34% disease severity in Ethiopia. In France, Germany, and the UK, losses of 5–10% lead to direct costs of €120 million to €700 million. Furthermore, in three countries, fungicide treatment costs farmers between €160 and €500 million5,17. Moreover, 30–54%5,9,18 yield loss have been observed under favorable disease circumstances (high humidity and moisture). Leaf blotch reduced wheat yields in Ethiopia’s main production areas by 25% to 82%19,20.

Ababa1 suggested that a number of management strategies have been described as the disease being crucial in terms of yield losses and distribution. However, the most cost-effective and environmentally friendly method of managing leaf blotch is to plant resistant varieties. Therefore, understanding the resistance mechanism and how Z. tritici interacts with wheat is crucial to developing varieties. For several years, there has long been disagreement on the pathogen’s physiological specialization. However, currently, a number of studies have demonstrated the interaction between Z. tritici pathotypes and wheat genotypes, hence confirming the pathogen’s physiological specialization or host-specific interaction21,22.

The host-specific resistance refers to the genotypes resistant response to a single pathotype6. This resistance type is different from being oligogenic, quantitative, and fully complete in total in wheat to Z. tritici23. Van der Plank24 hypothesized that the quantitative resistance (QR) genes are equally efficient for all pathogen pathotypes. In fact, small isolate/race-specific impacts have been reported, for instance, in the polygenic QR of Hordeum to Puccinia hordei, i.e., leaf rust, considered to be of a race-nonspecific background25,26. Identical observations were found by Truong et al.27, Chen et al.28 and Le Clerc et al.29 in pepper, mustard, and carrot when recognizing pathotype/race-specific QR resistance to Phytophthora capsici, Plasmodiophora brassicae, and Alternaria dauci, respectively. These interactions can only be elucidated by postulating a minor gene-for-minor gene relationship according to26,30 an identical tendency as the major gene-for-major-gene relationships for the system recognized in qualitative resistance. The effectiveness of a QR that depends on a pathotype-specific QTL is predicted to reduce over time because resistant plants select pathogen pathotypes with a high level of aggressiveness31,32. These specific relationships may render quantitative resistance non-stable than qualitative resistance33–35. Nevertheless, the outcomes of specific relationships on the durability of QR are still not well reported36,37.

Holetta Agricultural Research Center is known as a center of excellence for leaf blotch disease. A number of wheat genotypes have been crossed and screened for agronomic and other factors. Once more, CIMMTY has brought a number of genotypes into the country for breeding reasons, including agronomic and other crucial criteria. However, in our country, only two research studies indicated the wheat genotypes reaction to Z.tritici at the seedling growth stage38,39. Moreover, the identification of the gene-for-gene relationship for the crossed and introduced wheat genotypes has not been studied. The responses of released varieties are not fully understood. Therefore, the objective of this research was to (i) identify the host-pathogen interaction and (ii) identify pathotype-specific resistance of introduced genotypes and released varieties.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The current research was done at the Holetta Agricultural Research Center under greenhouse. It is located 38 km west of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia.

Plant materials

A set of 55 wheat genotypes was used for the present study (Table 1). The genotypes were received from the Holetta Agricultural Research Center wheat breeding programs. As a check, one differential line was used. The used genotypes were imported from different countries for high rainfall, international Septoria observation, and Fusarium head blight wheat screening.

Fungal pathotypes

About 40 differential lines were used as trap nurseries during the 2023 production year. Therefore, from the trap nursery, leaves having pycnidia were collected. The virulence of six pathotypes was evaluated on 15 differential lines, each carrying one or more known Lb gene(s). In the current study, these pathotypes were used to evaluate the reaction of wheat genotypes.

Zymoseptoria triticiisolation

Z. tritici isolation was carried out using the technique6,38,39. First, filter papers were wetted with distilled water and placed on petri plates. About 7 cm segments of wheat leaves were placed on the petri plates. These petri dishes were incubated at 24 °C for 2–8 h to produce pycnidiospores oozing through the pycnidium (ostiole), cirrhus. A stereoscopic dissecting microscope was used to test the creation of milky ooze on the top of pycnidia on a regular basis. Following autoclaving with a flame-sterilized small needle, the monopycnidial oozing droplets were transferred onto potato dextrose agar (PDA; potato 200 g/l, dextrose 20 g/l, agar 15 g/l) plates supplemented with 250 mg chloramphenicol40. After 7–10 days of maintaining inoculated petri dishes at 24 °C, fungal growth was seen. The augmentation was calculated taking into account the ocular and the objective (40). Once observable growth had taken place, single spore-derived colonies were moved to a liquid medium that included 1% (w/v) powdered yeast extract and 1% (w/v) sucrose for spore multiplication and storage as an inoculum40.

Inoculation and seedling resistance assay

Five to seven seedlings of each genotype were cultivated in plastic pots with an 8 cm diameter and a 1:1:1 mixture of forest soil, sieved sand, and well-decomposed farmyard manure (FYM). Three replications and a factorial setup were used in a completely randomized design. Each pure colony was grown on PDA and replicated in a liquid medium from the conserved colonies. Z. tritici spore suspensions were then hand-sprayed on ten-day-old cultivar seedlings until run-off, after the spore concentration was adjusted with a hemacytometer to 107 spores/ml and supplemented with 0.15% Tween-206,39. The infected seedlings were covered with plastic bags for 96 h in order to prevent cross-contamination and to create high humidity for the establishment of the infection40,41. Two days after inoculation, the plastic bags were removed, and the seedlings were maintained in the same greenhouse conditions, which included 20 °C/17°C (day/night), a 12-hour photoperiod, and a relative humidity of 90%40.

Data analysis

Disease severity using pycnidia and necrosis in percent was collected at 21 days of inoculation42. The disease severity was normalized using the arcsine square root transformation43. In the generalized mixed model (GMM)44,45, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was made using SAS software version 9.346. The pathotype, wheat, and their two-way interactions were used as a fixed effect, and the replication as random effects44. The least significant difference (LSD) method was used to determine the reaction of genotype and the specific interactions between wheat genotypes and pathotypes6,22,39,44. The LSD was analyzed using the α = 1% and 5% significance levels6,21,22,38. Based on the leaf necrosis parameter, LSDb 1% = 10 value was obtained. Therefore, any interaction below 10% value was considered resistant/avirulent. Any interaction greater than 89% value was considered susceptible/virulent. 99% is the maximum value, so an 89% value for threshold resulted from 99% minus 10%. The pathotype aggressiveness was determined after removing the data for any resistance reactions22. These aggressiveness data were subjected to Minitab version 19 statistical software for cluster analysis46. In the hierarchical cluster analysis, a 95% similarity level was interpolated into the software to determine the number of clusters or cut-off point. The average linkage method and euclidean distance were used to determine the distribution of dendrogram tree6.

Results

Wheat-Z. tritici interactions

Disease severity (necrosis and pycnidia) was analyzed using GMM to determine the source of variation. Therefore, ANOVA revealed wheat genotypes, pathotypes, and their two-way interactions were highly significant (p < 0.01) in Table 2 (Supplementary Materials Fig. 1). Out of 330 pathotype-by-wheat genotype interactions, 152 were wheat-Z. tritici interactions, and 178 were non-wheat-Z. tritici interactions. From 152 wheat-Z. tritici interactions, 16 interactions were pathotype-specific resistance, 70 interactions were broad-spectrum resistance, and 66 interactions were susceptible (Table 3).

Wheat genotype’s reaction to Z. tritici

In the current study, both parameters (necrosis and pycnidia) were used to set resistance and susceptibility thresholds through statistical analysis. As indicated in the methodology part, any interaction that showed less than a 10 value at a (p < 0.01) was considered resistant. Any interaction that showed a greater than 89 value at a (p < 0.01) was considered susceptible (Table 2). Out of 55 genotypes, 23 (42%) showed resistance reactions to more than two pathotypes. Only genotypes 54ISBWSN (018) and 54ISBWSN (113) indicated resistance reactions to six and five pathotypes, respectively. Genotypes like 31ISEPTON (031), 54ISBWSN (137), 21Eeit (045), and 21Eeit (026) indicated resistance reactions to four pathotypes. Nine (16.4%) and 8 (14.4%) genotypes also indicated resistance reactions to three and two pathotypes, respectively. Genotypes such as 54ISBWSN (137), 54ISBWSN (018), and 54ISBWSN (113) exhibited resistance reactions to the highly virulent pathotype EtH-39 (Table 4). About 52 (94.5%) genotypes presented a susceptible reaction to the highly virulent pathotype EtH-39 (Table 3). None of the genotypes were susceptible to all pathotypes tested. ESTANZUELA FEDERAL, carrying Lb7 gene, was susceptible to five pathotypes and intermediate to one pathotype.

Cluster analysis of the wheat genotypes

Hierarchical cluster analysis divided the 55 bread wheat genotypes into 25 clusters based on mean pycnidia coverage (Fig. 1). This indicates 45.4% resistance variability in wheat genotypes. The number of genotypes in cluster 4 and cluster 8 was higher. These clusters had six genotypes. About 11% of the population was grouped into these clusters. Next to these clusters, the majority of clusters had 4 genotypes. The reactions of 21Eeit (065), 41ESWYT (010), and 29HRWYT (049) were the same as they shared the same cluster. Out of 25 clusters, 12 clusters had only one genotype. Genotypes like 54ISBWSN (018), 54ISBWSN (137), and 54ISBWSN (113) in cluster 10, cluster 15, and cluster 16, respectively, showed resistance reactions to all pathotypes. Genotypes such as 23FHBJN (042), 54ISBWSN (084), and 54ISBWSN (111) in cluster 8, cluster 17, and cluster 22 showed gene-to-gene resistance (Table 5; Fig. 1).

Aggressiveness variability among isolates collected from specific area

Out of 6 isolates tested, EtH-39 and EtH-26 were found to be the most aggressive isolates, with 71.7% and 64.5% leaf necrosis area, respectively (Fig. 2). Isolate like EtH-38 showed the highest aggressiveness, with 59% of mean leaf necrosis area. In reverse, EtH-25 showed the lowest aggressiveness.

Discussion

One of the most serious wheat diseases in the world, including in Ethiopia, is leaf blotch. So, it is critical to identify the source of resistance in wheat genotypes. Therefore, researchers indicated that wheat resistance to Z. tritici has been both qualitative and quantitative. Monogenic traits direct qualitative resistance, which displays full or nearly full resistance. Whereas, polygenic characteristics govern quantitative resistance, which exhibits partial resistance to Z. tritici. These resistance types are known as seedling (gene-to-gene) and adult resistances, respectively22,42. However, the existence of physiological specialization of the pathogen on wheat had been debated for a long time. Recently, several reports showed the interaction between Z. tritici pathotypes and wheat genotypes, supporting the pathogen’s physiological specialization21,22. In these reports, the pathotype-specific resistance has been confirmed using differential line selection (pathogen’s physiological specialization)22,38,39. Moreover, so far, scientists have identified 23 major genes using molecular markers. In the wheat and Z.tritici pathosystem, Stb was the name given for the resistant genes, but it was renamed as Lb1. Out of resistant genes, Lb6 in bread wheat is a qualitative resistance that grants specific pathotype resistance23. Lb6 is located at the distal end of the chromosome 3 A short arm. It has been demonstrated to regulate a gene-for-gene interaction23.

Van der Plank24 hypothesized the efficiency of quantitative resistance genes to all pathogen pathotypes. However, the pathotype/race-specific QR resistance in pepper, mustard, and carrot to Phytophthora capsici, Plasmodiophora brassicae, and Alternaria dauci has been reported, respectively27–29. These specific relationships may render quantitative resistances non-stable in an identical way than qualitative resistance33–35. Alternations in pathogen populations may include either augmented pathogenicity generally or a modification in specificity with elevated pathogenicity on cultivars with a particular quantitative resistance gene. Several reports in different host plants characterize QTLs for disease resistance that are durable over various environments, involving researches exploring quantitative resistance to oilseed rape/blackleg, barley/leaf rust, bean/white mold, pea/Aphanomyces root rot, and potato/late blight interactions47,52. Some reports have previously characterized isolate-specific resistance components, proposing that a differential relationship between isolate and plant may be existing in QR25,27,29,33. Parlevliet53 documented more than 40 years ago small but significant isolate-cultivar relationships in quantitatively resistant H. vulgare cultivars. This led him to suppose the ‘minor-gene for minor-gene’ postulate elucidates the nature of QR30,34,54. In fact, the above-mentioned examples, together with the current findings, demonstrate that minor gene-for-minor gene reactions (vertical resistance) do exist in pathogen-plant interactions. In the current study, genotypes such as 21Eeit (046), 21Eeit (027), 41ESWYT (046), and the rest of the thirteen were resistant to one pathotype, indicating a gene-for-gene relationship. Against two isolates, quantitative resistance (Rphq4) worked well in both seedlings and adult plants. However, Rphq4 had an isolate-specific effect and was ineffective against the third isolates in both development stages25. A gene-for-gene was initially discovered in the UK cvv. Flame and Hereward provide resistance to a Dutch Z. tritici pathotype, IPO323. As a result, the resistant reaction to one pathotype that was displayed by the genotypes, cultivars, and differential lines is known as specific resistance. In Ethiopia, Mekonnen et al.38 suggested that 10 cultivars exhibited one pathotype-specific resistance. Again, another study indicated only cultivar Arendeto was resistant to one pathotype39.

Makhdoomi et al.22 proposed that 116 pathotype-specific resistances were found from total interactions (n = 420). Out of 52 genotypes, 26 wheat genotypes were susceptible to all pathotypes and did not exhibit pathotype-specific responses, which could account for the rising Z. tritici outbreaks in Iran’s22. Dalvand et al.55 indicated that there were notable variations (p < 0.01) between wheat genotypes in their response to different Z. tritici pathotypes. These discrepancies appear to have resulted from the genotype-pathotype interaction’s highly significant variations (p < 0.01). Out of the 360 interactions, 60 pathotype-specific resistances were found38. Ababa and Mekonnen6 suggested that the studied pathotypes exhibited differential line-specific pathogenicity in 41 of the 301 interactions. Nevertheless, among the examined pathotypes, 260 interactions did not exhibit differential line-specific pathogenicity. In the current study, out of the 330 interactions, 152 pathotype-by-wheat genotype interactions were involved in the wheat and Z. tritici, while the remaining 178 did not. On the other hand, out of 152 interactions, 16 had pathotype-specific resistance, 70 were broad spectrum, and 66 were susceptible.

Broad-spectrum resistance in wheat is also suggested in different reports. In Iran, Dalvand et al.55 evaluated 26 differential wheat cultivars (carrying Lb1–18 genes) at the seedling growth stage, but none of them were resistant to all pathotypes. Five wheat genotypes were suggested by Makhdoomi et al.22 for breeding purposes; all of these were resistant to the six pathotypes examined in Iran. Mekonnen et al.38 suggested that KM7 revealed resistance to six pathotypes. The commercial cultivar Hidase and the imported genotype MURGA showed resistance to five pathotypes. Similarly, six wheat genotypes exhibited resistance to three Z. tritici pathotypes. While eight genotypes exhibited resistance to two pathotypes. At the seedling growth stage, the following cultivars were designated as broad-spectrum resistant: Danda’a, HONQOLO, Digalu, Dashen, EJERSA, Alemtena, Mosobo, Hitosa, Robe, and Lelisso. Conversely, the wheat cultivars K6295-4 A, LEMU, Yerer, Laketch, and ET-13A2 were sensitive to all pathotypes and did not show pathotype-specific resistance39. In line with previous reports, a resistant reaction to more than two pathotypes was seen in 23 (42%) genotypes. The genotype 54ISBWSN (018) showed resistance reactions to all pathotypes tested. 54ISBWSN (113) showed resistance to five pathotypes. Four genotypes, such as 31ISEPTON (031), 54ISBWSN (137), 21Eeit (045), and 21Eeit (026) showed resistance reactions to four pathotypes, indicating they have broad-spectrum resistance. In the current study, the differential line ESTANZUELA FEDERAL, carrying the Lb7 gene, was used as a susceptible check. This differential line was infective against five pathotypes and intermediate to one pathotype. The study is the same as the previous study, indicating this differential line was infective against several pathotypes38,39.

The resistance variability of wheat cultivars was also indicated in several studies. Hierarchical cluster analysis was used to divide the 32 bread wheat cultivars into two groups based on the mean disease severity39. Moreover, the 45 bread wheat genotypes were divided into two clusters according to the mean disease severity38. Cluster analysis was used to group each genotype using the reaction of each genotype to all pathotypes or the mean disease severity. In the current study, cluster analysis grouped 55 genotypes into 25 clusters, indicating resistance variability among genotypes was 45.4%. Cluster 4 and Cluster 8 had the six largest populations. Genotypes like 21Eeit (027), 41ESWYT (012), 41ESWYT (099), 23FHBJN (042), 54ISBWSN (012), and 54ISBWSN (163) were grouped into cluster 8. This indicates these genotypes have similar mean disease severity. The genotype’s reaction within each cluster differed from one another, and the genotypes inside the same cluster were identical.

In line with the findings of6,38, the current study found that the tested Z. tritici isolates showed a higher degree of aggressiveness. In the previous study, the maximum pycnidia coverage was 88%. In the current study, 66.8% was seen in the most aggressive isolate, EtH-39; the next most aggressive isolates, EtH-26, had mean pycnidia coverages of 59.3%. Pathotype EtH-39 was a highly virulent pathotype, so 52 (94.5%) of the genotypes exhibited a susceptible reaction. The reaction of the genotypes employed in earlier research and the current findings differed, so the lowest aggressiveness data were obtained. The lowest pycnidia coverage was scored by EtH-25.

Conclusion

In the current study, resistant materials were found in the genotypes imported from different countries for high rainfall, International Septoria observation, and Fusarium head blight wheat screening. Resistance variability was identified among these genotypes. Some genotypes showed resistant reactions to all pathotypes, so they are very important for further breeding. It is very important in the future work to identify resistance sources among crosses, imports, and landraces. In these genotypes, none of them showed a susceptible reaction to all pathotypes, so this indicates that resistant materials are found for further breeding. Host-specific resistance (minor gene-for-minor gene interaction) was identified and confirmed among this pathosystem.
