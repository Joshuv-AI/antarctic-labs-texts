Historical innovation in education: the development of an endoscopy technician training program and a novel professional pathway for endoscopy assistants
Authors: Gottumukkala S. Raju, Phillip S. Ge
Published: 2024

Introduction

Behind every successful endoscopist is a cadre of talented and outstanding assistants. When I graduated from my gastroenterology fellowship at the Division of Digestive Diseases at UCLA Medical Center in 2017, I casually asked for advice from one of my endoscopy assistants. She responded, “Make sure to stay the cheerful way you are, and 1 day when you become big, don’t you ever forget about us little people.”

The advice that was given to me is one that I hold dear, and I have always looked up to like-minded colleagues who went out of their way to take care of our hard-working heroes who work tirelessly behind the scenes to bring success to endoscopy day in and day out. Among such colleagues, very few have exemplified this altruism more than Dr Gottumukkala Subba Raju. Widely known to go out of his way to take care of others, Dr Raju is a pioneer and key innovator in the education and professional development of the endoscopy assistant. In a truly novel way, Dr Raju successfully developed the first formalized curriculum for endoscopy technician training at Houston Community College (HCC), turning endoscopy technician training into a formal certification program accredited by the Southern Association of Colleges and Schools and the Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board. This ultimately culminated in the designation of the week of December 5 to 9, 2022, as Endoscopy Technicians Week, officially recognized and signed by the Governor of the State of Texas.

In honor of endoscopy technicians everywhere (Supplementary Video 1, available online at www.igiejournal.org), we are delighted to welcome Gottumukkala Subba Raju, MD, FASGE, as our distinguished guest for the Historical Considerations section of iGIE (Supplementary Video 2, available online at www.igiejournal.org). A passionate educator and champion for junior colleagues, trainees, nurses, and technicians, Dr Raju served as the inaugural Editor-in-Chief for VideoGIE and was recipient of the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) President’s Award in 2019. Dr Raju is currently the John Stroehlein Distinguished Professor at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, where I am proud to regard him as my colleague, mentor, and friend.

Phillip S. Ge, iGIE Section Editor for Historical Considerations

Background

Phillip S. Ge (PSG): Dr Raju, thank you so much for taking the time to do this interview. It is truly an honor and a privilege. Tell us a little about your own background and training in endoscopy.

Developing the endoscopy technician training program has been a group effort. I come from South India, from the east coast of India, in a state called Andhra Pradesh. We come from a farming family in the rich Godavari Delta that is known for its rice paddy fields. My paternal grandfather was the first physician who went out from the village to get educated. He was in the first batch of my medical school, Andhra Medical College, Visakhapatnam, which just held its centenary celebration this past year. I had the good fortune of growing up in his home. Being a country doctor, my grandfather visited homes of his patients, and I accompanied him. Watching his genteel manner helped me. There were very few medications, and it was primarily his hand-holding (ie, placebo effect) that helped his patients. Because of his inspiration, multiple people from my family became physicians. My father is a gynecologist who had a keen interest in education. He was the first in our family to come to the United States in the 1960s for 2 years of advanced training at the University of Pennsylvania and returned back home to join as a faculty member in the medical school. My father took pride in teaching at the medical school and maintained a busy practice. He even took pains to single-handedly write a textbook of obstetrics for medical students, spending hours with an illustrator who used to come to our house and sit and draw pencil diagrams of obstetrics principles and practice. Even though he is now in his 90s, he still reads a lot. He was a source of great inspiration to me.

After finishing medical school in 1982, I had the good fortune to go to the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, Chandigarh, India, where I did my medicine training. I wanted to work with one special mentor, Dr Jang Dilawari, who was different from the rest of his faculty in terms of his background and his thinking.1 He was trained in Germany with Nobel Laureate Adolf Butenandt and Dr Alfred Wiskott, and in London with Sir Francis Avery Jones and Peter Cotton, MD, MASGE, at the Middlesex Hospital before returning to India in the mid-1970s.2 He was the first to perform ERCP and common bile duct stone extraction in India in the late 1970s. He was fun to work with in the sense that he allowed you to think freely, and after my medicine training, I worked with him as a fellow in hepatology. We did a lot of work on portal hypertension, mainly sclerotherapy. I remember at the time I always had a Sengstaken tube in my backpack, so if someone came with a bleed I would place a Sengstaken tube. There was no banding at the time, just sclerotherapy, and we even had to prepare our own sclerotherapy needles. You may be surprised to learn that we did splenoportovenograms and inferior vena cavograms to study portal hypertension. My first article was about splenoportovenograms as a letter in Lancet and my only paper in Gastroenterology also came when I worked there.3,4 In those days, I was not allowed to do endoscopy until the technicians testified that I had washed enough scopes and knew how to handle the scopes. It was a very good learning experience.

In India, after you do your medicine training for 3 years, you get your MD, and then you get your specialty training and that is called DM. I joined Govind Ballabh Pant Hospital, Delhi University, where I completed 2 years of gastroenterology fellowship under Professor S.L. Broor who did a lot of work on acid peptic disease, and Professor B.S. Anand, both amazing mentors and gentlemen.

Following my 2 years of training, I wanted to stay in academic medicine, but unfortunately there were not that many opportunities in India at the time. Dr Anand introduced me to Professor K.D. Bardhan in England, where I worked for 2 years doing general medicine and gastroenterology and nearly 2 years of research (Fig. 1).5,6 Dr Bardhan was a Rhodes Scholar, knighted by the Queen of England, who made a lasting impact on my career, teaching me how to make scientific contribution despite the odds set against you. We worked at Rotherham District General Hospital, where the team consisted of him, the registrar (myself), and 2 interns, and that was it. From these limited resources, he was able to mentor several PhDs and made major contributions to our understanding of acid peptic disease by running the first cimetidine clinical trials. He put a lot of his own earnings into the Bardhan Research and Education Trust, with several million pounds of funding to support science and research at the university level. He used to finish scoping around 6PM, and we would start making rounds at 7 to 8PM. I remember days when I went home at around 1AM after finishing rounds. But it was a fun time. The endoscopy technician training program

PSG: Now that we know about your background and your passion for education, let us move on to discuss your most ambitious and impactful educational project, the creation of the endoscopy technician training program at HCC and its adoption by the ASGE. Tell us about what served as your initial inspiration for becoming invested in the education of your endoscopy assistants.

GSR: I am certain that you have observed the following phenomenon in academic practice. An experienced community physician sends a complex case, let’s say a complex ERCP, to a well-known attending physician at an academic center. You and I both know the attending physician isn’t doing the case; the advanced fellow in their seventh year of postgraduate training is doing the case, and successfully completing it.

How is it that the fellow can complete these complex cases? Why is it that an experienced outside physician would send cases to the academic center knowing that it would be completed by a fellow? I became convinced that the key difference is the endoscopy team and, specifically, the endoscopy assistant. In other words, there must be an extremely experienced endoscopy assistant at the academic center such that even an inexperienced fellow can complete the case, whereas there must be a weak link in the referring endoscopist’s team that is preventing the case from being successfully completed.

After Hurricane Ike, we were temporarily displaced, and we went out to community hospitals in order to still be able to take care of our patients on the weekends. The community hospitals said that we could not bring our teams; they would provide their teams. That was an eye-opening experience for me. Even with simple colonoscopies, how you give a biopsy forceps, how you give a snare, how you close a snare, all of those things matter when it comes to procedure quality and outcomes.

When I came to MD Anderson, endoscopy technicians were assigned to specific rooms, and therefore I had 1 particular technician assigned to me. So I had to train her and get her up to speed, and was successful in doing so. But that thought—of why was the assistant not well trained at the community hospital and how it affected procedure outcomes—always stuck in the back of my mind.

PSG: Our endoscopy assistants are hard-working heroes who bring success to endoscopy day in and day out (Supplementary Video 1, Fig. 8). This is also a role that has morphed tremendously throughout the history of endoscopy, starting with spouses (as with Rudolf Schindler), then laboratory technicians (as with Peter Cotton),2 then nurses, and now with the modern endoscopy technician. Tell us about how this role evolved in the United States.

GSR: For much of my career in the United States, endoscopy nurses ran the room. One nurse was in charge of sedation, monitoring, and record-keeping, while a second nurse was in charge of setting up the scope and assisting the procedure. The documentation demands were not high. In some parts of the Midwest and Northeast, nurses still play the role of the endoscopy assistant.Figure 8The endoscopy technician team at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (September 29, 2017).

The endoscopy technician team at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (September 29, 2017).

By the late 1990s and early 2000s, ambulatory surgical centers were becoming popular, and by 2005 to 2010, electronic health records started creeping into the hospitals. In my view, the biggest catalyst for this change was the advent of the electronic health record. This came with a massive increase in the amount of documentation, and that means the nurse is busy with the computer. That basically tied down 1 nurse, and while the costs of operation have increased, reimbursements have decreased for endoscopy. From a cost point of view, instead of 2 nurses in the room, having 1 nurse and 1 endoscopy technician increasingly made financial sense.

Furthermore, although it is true that nurses are more educated with a higher degree than endoscopy technicians, they are trained for bedside or clinical nurse roles; most nurses nowadays do not have formal training in procedural areas such as endoscopy. There have been consequences to this shift in the landscape. As a result, I have not seen many nurses as “lifers” in the endoscopy unit in the last 15 years, and as the “old guard” retired, the knowledge and skillset either got lost, or hopefully got taken up by endoscopy technicians learning on the job.

PSG: I find it interesting that as endoscopy technicians became increasingly prevalent, up until now little had been done to address the fundamental difference in education and experience between nurses and technicians. This clearly must have resulted in challenges from a human resources (HR) standpoint with regard to promotion, career advancement, and compensation, which I am guessing served as the initial impetus for your ambitious education project.

GSR: As operations started becoming increasingly expensive over the last 10 to 15 years, endoscopists increasingly felt that assistance could be done by a technician, and thus technicians slowly started entering the field of endoscopy. Over time, endoscopy technicians have become very skilled and experienced, but the hospitals never had a system to recognize the technical skills of a technician and pay them appropriately. For example, there is a difference between an entry-level technician doing diagnostic upper endoscopy and colonoscopy, versus an experienced technician assisting with Barrett’s esophagus ablation, endoscopic submucosal dissection, interventional EUS, and complex ERCP. Yet, endoscopy directors across the country could not convince HR departments to pay appropriately for the skillset that a technician achieves. From a HR standpoint, they did not have anything tangible to hang their hat on, such as a certificate or formal extra training. That was a problem regardless of whether it was at the University of Kansas, UTMB, or MD Anderson. But HR departments do recognize certificates, and that was one of the observations that made me start thinking about this whole education process.

PSG: A lot of us have ideas and experiences, but not many of us have the courage to act and invest on them. You took the bold step of pushing forward with the idea. Tell us about how you turned the idea into reality, starting with the initial pitch.

GSR: When I came to MD Anderson, I thought to myself that I would do lots of research like I had done at UTMB. For whatever reason, it never quite materialized. So then I started pitching ideas to prospective collaborators. I pitched ideas to faculty at Rice University to see if we could do work on novel hemostatic agents. I pitched ideas to faculty at the University of Texas at Austin. I got no response.

But I really wanted to work on this concept of endoscopy technician training, so I wrote an e-mail to Dr Cesar Maldonado, Chancellor of the HCC System (Fig. 9). I did not know him, but I wrote an e-mail to him, pitching to him my ideas regarding endoscopy technician training. I explained that surgical assistants go through 2 years of training before they enter the operating room. If you look at the last 40 years, many conditions that used to be managed by surgeons are now all managed by endoscopy, from esophageal varices, to peptic ulcers, to choledocholithiasis, to colon polyps. But, we work with endoscopy assistants who have no formal training in endoscopy, some of whom are fresh out of high school, and expect them to reliably assist in highly complex cases.Figure 9The original letter pitching the idea to Dr Cesar Maldonado, Chancellor of Houston Community College (May 31, 2015).

He therefore conducted a survey of all the nurse managers across the Texas Medical Center, and they all responded that they wanted this program yesterday! The biggest problem for a nurse manager is when someone knocks at their door, and says, “I’m leaving.” You can’t just replace a technician: it takes months to replace a technician, and even if you replace a technician, it takes months to train a new one. PSG: So after multiple years of thinking about the endoscopy assistant, you finally were able to successfully pitch your idea and got buy-in from the community college. Walk us through the complex process of developing the curriculum.

GSR: By this time, I had been working with the ASGE, giving talks, doing hands-on courses, developing CME material, and producing videos. Along with my YouTube experience, I figured that with this skillset it would be a piece of cake to develop a program. I was obviously wrong!

After Dr Nicotera gave his support behind developing a program, we went through a process called DACUM (Developing a Curriculum) (Fig. 11). This is a process for developing a program de novo, when no precedence exists. Basically, if you want to create a training course to teach a particular skillset, they bring a group of people with that skillset and put them in a room with the Dean of Curriculum Development and another senior educator. So those 2 leaders sat with a group of 12 technicians for 16 hours over 2 days, hashing out in detail what the technician does in a given day from the time they clock in until the time they clock out. The technicians all brought different skillsets given that they were from different hospitals. As the educators jotted down on sheets of paper what the technicians do, they were absolutely blown away that endoscopy technicians did not have formal certification. So they created a big list of items, of skillsets that a technician needed to function, and all of the knowledge that is required.Figure 11Development of Curriculum (DACUM) at Houston Community College (July 29-30, 2016).

Development of Curriculum (DACUM) at Houston Community College (July 29-30, 2016).

Dr Nicotera then obtained approval from the community college’s Governing Board, and then we set out to create a curriculum and an advisory board. We created an advisory board consisting of endoscopists, nurses, technicians, and industry partners (Figure 12, Figure 13, Figure 14). We all met for several hours every month for almost 2 years in order to figure out what type of course we were going to create, and whether it would be a Level 2 Certificate or an Associate’s Degree. We also had to create contracts for the students to come and get hands-on clinical experience at each of the partnering hospitals. GSR: With the curriculum approved, we next needed to develop the actual educational material. Because the community college had no experience in developing an endoscopy technician course, I had promised that I would help develop the learning material, because I felt that we as endoscopists know what we want our assistants to do, and we also want our assistants to learn how to read our minds, to be able to look at the endoscopy screen and be prepared. That’s how teamwork should be.

To develop that material, I was very lucky to have Ms Angela Diehl, a medical illustrator who was a faculty member at UT Southwestern before she became a freelancer (Fig. 15). Angela and I had been working together for multiple years, to the point where she knew how to illustrate whatever was on my mind. I was also lucky to recruit Ms Sanji Suresh, who was a graduate of Washington University in St. Louis and had taught high school science in East St. Louis, Illinois, USA, for 2 years as part of Teach for America, earning a Master’s in Education during that time (Fig. 16). We kept all of the learning material simple and uniform, and made all of it available online as we did not want students to buy any books. PSG: Has it been challenging to get faculty to teach the community college course? How does the course stay up to date and relevant with advances in endoscopy? How does the course attempt to remain impartial to industry when industry had such a strong role in its development?

GSR: In the first batch, there were 2 teachers who also taught the surgical technician training program. In the second batch, we had a full-time endoscopy technician with 20 years of experience, who taught them along with a part-time technician. In the third batch, we have 2 formerly well-trained technicians who decided to take up education. In addition to this, industry partners come and teach the students. When it comes to hands-on training, the best trainers are often either endoscopists or industry partners.

The course is set up to be as neutral as possible, without being partial to one company over another. That is why we had an illustrator to formally illustrate the concepts. When we started on this journey, we wanted the community college to recognize endoscopists, technicians, nurses, and industry as equal partners in terms of who can come in and teach. That way, if there is a new device, if an endoscopist has time, they can come and volunteer. If they do not have time, industry would obviously be happy to come and teach. That is the way we set up the course, so that nobody is excluded. But it is critical to stay up to date with industry and with advances in endoscopy, because without planning for new technology, this course can become obsolete very quickly.

The fundamental goal of the community college course is to train a technician who can competently assist with basic outpatient endoscopy, emergency endoscopy, and basic ERCP in the United States. We have a lot more than that, obviously, with Barrett’s esophagus, peroral endoscopic myotomy, endoscopic submucosal dissection, interventional EUS, and complex ERCP. For those skillsets, I am hoping that the younger generations can take it upon themselves to develop the material so that technicians can have advanced education in different subspecialties. What I would like to ultimately see is to get our own endoscopists involved; I believe it is a very good opportunity for us as endoscopists to go to the community college and teach these students, because you have the potential to make a huge impact on somebody’s life there. I am going to leave that to you and your colleagues.

Pathways to professional success

PSG: In my view, the remarkable and historical educational achievement here is not just the creation of the community college program but the development of a pathway to help technicians grow professionally. Tell us about the creation of this pathway.

GSR: As an academician, I always felt that everyone should have an opportunity to grow professionally. An academic physician starts as an assistant professor, and over the course of their career gets promoted to associate professor and ultimately full professor. A nurse has opportunities to grow as charge nurse, nurse manager, director, nurse practitioner, or even higher positions in hospital administration. Even the administrative staff has a ladder for professional growth. But there was nothing for endoscopy technicians. Even the best technicians in their field who assisted the most complex cases, they had a glass ceiling and could not grow professionally. I have seen many endoscopy technicians who were smarter than me, end up retiring as technicians.

Now, there is finally a pathway for technicians to grow. They do not have to stop as an endoscopy technician. Working with the community college, we have now created a pathway that allows them to continue their education. After completing the Level 2 Certification, there is now a pathway for them to obtain an Associate’s Degree with specialization in endoscopy technology. From there, if they want to develop business administration skills, from the Associate’s Degree they can go to a Bachelor’s Degree in Business Administration. Finally, there are ongoing plans for a pathway from the Associate’s Degree to a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN). Imagine an excellent technician who has been working for some time, who then takes opportunity of this pathway to become a BSN, and then comes back to your endoscopy unit. You will have completely different team dynamics.

PSG: Tell us about the role of the ASGE in supporting the professional growth for endoscopy technicians.

GSR: I am delighted to share that in 2022, the ASGE Governing Board approved the creation of a new membership category, Associate Membership for nurses and technicians, a first in the 80-year history of the society (Figs. 21 and 22). Along with this, the ASGE developed a website and learning platform for endoscopy technicians. This also happens to be one of the examinations taken by the community college students for their Level 2 Certification. One other thing that the ASGE has done to encourage technicians and nurses, is that if a group practice joins the ASGE as a group member, then that group’s endoscopy nurses, technicians, and mid-level providers can all become associate members for free. Think about the great advantage of what the ASGE can therefore offer. We did this for our own group at MD Anderson, and as a result all of our nurses and technicians are associate members of the ASGE. With their membership, they can go to the ASGE website, learn for free, and they can also take the test for the ASGE Certificate of Completion. That way we create a pathway for people to professionally grow.

PSG: As I understand it, the ASGE Certificate of Completion is also geared toward existing endoscopy technicians. After all, a question that comes up is how do we professionally support those who are already working as endoscopy technicians?

GSR: There is now a pathway for existing technicians who have at least 4 years of experience. First, either an endoscopist or endoscopy nurse manager needs to attest that they can do certain procedures. Next, they pay a nominal fee to the ASGE to take and pass the ASGE Certificate of Completion examination. Once they have completed those items, they can get close to 19 credit hours from HCC, and then they can complete the rest of the curriculum by finishing the courses online in order to obtain a Level 2 Certificate. They can then join the same pathway to climb the professional ladder toward an Associate Degree and subsequently a Bachelor’s Degree.

Finally, there is even a third pathway now, the U.S. Department of Labor–registered apprenticeship program whereby a student goes to the community college and is simultaneously working full-time in a hospital. In this setup, the hospital provides the apprentice with time to go to the community college in the evenings for their classes. If they work for 1 year, they get a certificate that they have completed their apprenticeship program. We are currently piloting 1 student who is part of this program. After their year, they will be very well trained, and if the institution has an opening, they will have an employment opportunity. If the institution does not have an opening, this sort of experience will make them highly competitive to find a job anywhere.

PSG: It appears that the key here is flexibility, which makes sense, given that our endoscopy technicians come from all walks of life and have all kinds of individual circumstances. It appears that there are really 3 categories here—(1) a student who can go through the formal community college program; (2) a young apprentice who can simultaneously work and take community college classes; and (3) an experienced technician who can test out of a large quantity of credit hours—all of whom can end up in the same pathway for professional growth.

GSR: For the actual community college program, we felt that we needed to set up a program that makes it easy for students to continue to work. So these are people who are working in the daytime in different jobs, and then going to school in the evening 2 days per week. Most of the learning material is available online, but they go on campus for hands-on simulation laboratory 2 evenings per week. They have access to a dedicated hands-on training facility with an endoscopy unit and all the accessories. Then, they go to clinical training facilities once a week during their first semester and twice a week during their second semester. These are partnerships that were developed between the community college and various endoscopy units around the Texas Medical Center and around the city. By the time they come out, they will have the knowledge and skillset, spread over the period of 1 year. They then take their final examination at HCC and also take the ASGE Certificate of Completion examination.

PSG: When I think about the success you have had in endoscopy technician training, I immediately think about how this model and this education can be replicated across the country and even around the world. Two questions come to mind. First, if a different institution in a different city wants to replicate the material to train their local endoscopy technicians, how would they go about doing this?

GSR: To answer this question requires knowledge of how community colleges work. Say, for example, you are the president of a community college, and let’s say you are in California, and you feel there is a need to develop an endoscopy technician training program. You have several options. The first option is the hard way of doing what we did, which is going through the steps of making a curriculum. That would be reinventing the wheel, taking 7 to 10 years to do so, such as in our case. The second option is you can say, let us look at who has this program already available. Typically, community colleges will help each other out and honor each other’s requests. You can therefore reach out to HCC and ask for help, and HCC would help your college set up the course, and your college would just have to set up contracts with local hospitals. In this scenario, there has to be an endoscopy champion who can help make this happen, so that technicians can get hands-on training. Before we started out on this journey, we actually looked around to see if there were any existing community college programs that we could copy and improve, because it is much easier to do that. When we looked at the time in 2016 to 2017, there were 2 other programs, 1 in Florida, and 1 in New Jersey. But when we contacted them, we did not get any response, and that is what led to us starting from scratch. My personal goal is to make it possible for anybody from anywhere to do this.

There is now another option, which is that the ASGE now has the entire educational material, and the ASGE Certificate of Completion is recognized by HCC. I can, for example, be in Chicago or California, become an ASGE associate member thanks to my endoscopists who are group members, and then I go take and pass the examination. I can come to HCC, register and get my credits, and finish whatever remaining credits online.

Finally, for those who are true novices with no endoscopy exposure or experience but who are working, there has to be a local endoscopy champion who is willing to work with their managers in the hospital system to create a hands-on training program. The knowledge is already available, but the hands-on training is necessary to mentor the technician and help them gain technical skills. That is actually possible. Our own institution, MD Anderson, has piloted a 12-week intensive program for the first time, and our ultimate goal is to make the course material available to others.

PSG: The second question is, let’s say there is a promising young endoscopy technician somewhere far away from Houston, perhaps in Hawaii, who reads this article and wants to professionally benefit from the endoscopy technician training program and the subsequent professional pathway. How would they go about doing this?

GSR: When I see that mentality in an endoscopy technician, I get excited. You want to grow, and we want you to grow. That is the most important thing. I am excited because there is now a pathway for anyone out there to grow professionally in healthcare. There are 3 basic requirements. You need: (1) 4 years of experience in endoscopy; (2) your endoscopist or endoscopy nurse manager has to sign off a letter saying that you can do upper endoscopy, colonoscopy, enteroscopy, basic ERCP, and basic EUS; and (3) you have to take and pass the ASGE Certificate of Completion. You can take the examination for free if your group of endoscopists is part of an ASGE group membership. If not, then it’s a nominal fee to take the examination, about $150 or so.

You then submit the documentation of experience, the letters of support, and the ASGE Certificate of Completion, including the transcript, to HCC. You have to register with the community college as a student. Once the community college reviews the material, they will grant a significant portion of the 35 credit hours required (somewhere around 50%-75% of the credits required) through a pathway called the Prior Learning Assessment Pathway. For each remaining course, there is a nominal fee, about $300 to $400 total. The remaining required courses are subjects such as Medical Terminology, Human Anatomy, and Human Physiology, and can all be completed online.

Therefore, this can be done even if you are working in Hawaii, and you would graduate with a Level 2 Certificate with 35 credit hours from HCC. If you add another 30 credit hours, you will attain an Associate Degree with specialization in endoscopy technology from HCC. This is highly attractive for someone who, for example, started after high school and wants to grow but cannot afford to spend a lot of money on a university.

From there, with an Associate Degree, you can then take additional courses and work toward getting a BSN and become a nurse, or toward a Bachelor’s Degree in Business Administration and walk the pathway of an administrator within your hospital.

The key players throughout this process are your own institution or practice, the endoscopists or managers supporting you, the community college providing a platform, and the recognition by the HCC of the ASGE Certificate of Completion. Those elements are key, because that makes it such that the same opportunity is available whether you are in Chicago, Milwaukee, or Hawaii. Although it is true that your fees are lower if you live in Houston, and slightly more expensive if you are outside the county and/or outside the state, note that this pathway does not actually require you to come to Houston. It is an amazing job that has been done by the HCC, and I thank President Dr Nicotera and Dean Dr Jeff Gricar for recognizing the ASGE Certificate of Completion and making this possible.

Thoughts on mentorship

PSG: Dr Raju, you are revered in our field for your calmness and for your spirituality, which seemingly transcend the hustle and bustle of daily medical practice. Tell us about your mentality and approach to life.

GSR: It is an honor to serve others when they depend on us to provide the best care. As humans, we have limitations. I believe that our outcomes depend not only on our knowledge, skill, and teamwork but also on some divine intervention. For me, having a calming atmosphere helps us work best, and hence I listen to Native American flute in my endoscopy room; when you want to really appreciate anything, it is best to see or hear it in a very pure form.

At the end of the day, we are all influenced by our family and by our mentors. I was very lucky to have exceptionally kind mentors. I feel that as physicians, we take care of patients, and we all have a great opportunity to do something on the side. The question of endoscopy technician training bothered me for a long time, and I was very lucky to have a fantastic group of people to work with, and this could not have happened without so many people pitching in. The final product is a reflection of so many people’s hard work, and the timing was great, as I was able to work with the community college, my own institution, philanthropy support, and a fantastic team of nurses, technicians, industry partners, and the ASGE. Many things simply happened, and I enjoyed the ride.

PSG: “Hands that serve are holier than lips that pray.” I believe you consider this to be your favorite quote and is a mantra that you live by. Tell us about Sai Baba, this particular quote, and how it affects your daily life.

GSR: I grew up in India. A lot of people believe Sri Sathya Sai Baba is a divine reincarnation. He believes that your hands do the work, and your hands can serve. For some reason, that quote has stuck in my mind, and I like that quote for whatever reason that I cannot quite explain. We all go through tough times in life, and that quote and Sai Baba have helped me deal with issues throughout the course of my life.

PSG: You are widely known to be a selfless mentor, who gives advice freely, and who gives opportunity to junior colleagues and staff alike without expectation of repayment. This is incredibly admirable. What has influenced you to be so supportive and selfless to others?

GSR: Many people helped me along the way. When you are the beneficiary of support and help, it will automatically mold you as a person to pay it forward. I left my home and my home country quite early, at age 21, and that is a bit unusual in my culture to leave like that. Along my journey, many people have been very kind and very supportive to me, sometimes from totally unexpected people and places. Even the circumstances that resulted in me joining MD Anderson, I never would have thought that I would be here, but it was thanks to Drs Mendelsohn and Stroehlein that I am working here today.

PSG: Thank you again for a wonderful and deeply insightful interview. To close out, I would like to ask you for your personal advice and wisdom. What advice do you have for future generations who wish to become as innovative and insightful as yourself?

GSR: The most important thing is that each one of us needs to know what we want, not what somebody else has. We need to know what we want, and what we want to do, with whatever time we have. I was just looking the other day at average life expectancy; you know our average lifespan is just 28,854 days, if one is lucky. It is therefore very important to not only do the job you need to do in order to put food on the table and take care of your family but also important to constantly be on the lookout to learn something new and exciting, and not be afraid of expressing your views. You should make sure that you don’t compare with others: God gives gifts to different people in different ways, and also challenges different people in different ways. The best thing is to just enjoy life and do good for others, whatever that may be.
