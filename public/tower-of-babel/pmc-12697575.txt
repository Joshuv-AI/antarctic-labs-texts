Real- vs High-Definition Ultrasound-Assisted Liposuction in Men: Long-Term Outcomes and the Role of Helium Plasma Skin Tightening
Authors: Naci Celik, Isil Akgun Demir, Sevgi Kurt, Ilknur Keskin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMale body contouring has evolved from conventional debulking to high-definition liposuction (HDL) etching and, more recently, a “real-definition” c (RDL) approach emphasizing natural contours. The role of helium-plasma skin tightening (HPST) as an adjunct remains uncertain.ObjectivesTo compare long-term patient-reported outcomes, complications, and revision rates between RDL and HDL in men undergoing ultrasound-assisted liposuction (UAL), and to evaluate the selective role of HPST in improving skin quality.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 743 male patients who underwent primary UAL between 2008 and 2024. Patients were grouped by era: conventional (2008-2011), HDL (2011-2014), RDL (2015-2019), and RDL with HPST (2019-2024). Outcomes included BODY-Q satisfaction, complication severity, and revision rates. Regression, mediation, and propensity-score matching analyses were applied to identify predictors of durable satisfaction and recommendation.ResultsMean satisfaction improved across eras: 59.3 (2008-2011), 57.7 (2011-2014), 66.8 (2015-2019), and 73.8 (2019-2024). HDL patients showed the lowest durable scores despite high early ratings and were most associated with self-reported “fake abs,” which correlated with reduced BODY-Q domains. RDL produced more stable outcomes, particularly in men with BMI ≤25 and ≥4 exercise sessions/week. Sports frequency predicted higher satisfaction directly and indirectly via lower body mass index (BMI). Clinically meaningful complications declined from 15.4% to 4.6% across eras. Helium-plasma skin tightening was offered mainly to older, higher-BMI men and, after adjustment, was not independently associated with improved skin quality. RDL was linked to a lower revision risk (odds ratio (OR) 0.32, P = .029).ConclusionsReal-definition liposuction provided natural, durable outcomes and fewer revisions than HDL. Over-etching increased unnatural contours and dissatisfaction. Careful patient selection—BMI ≤25, consistent exercise, and weight stability—is critical, while HPST may be reserved for older men with mild laxity.Level of Evidence: 3 (Therapeutic)

Liposuction is among the most performed aesthetic operations worldwide, according to recent International Society of Aesthetic Plastic Surgery (ISAPS) global surveys.1,2 Modern liposuction evolved in the early 1980s with Illouz's introduction of blunt cannulas and negative-pressure aspiration together with the “wet” technique that reduced vascular trauma and facilitated fat removal.3 Syringe-based lipectomy was used for delicate regions to increase precision and limit bleeding.4 Klein's tumescent anesthesia with large-volume dilute lidocaine and epinephrine further decreased intraoperative blood loss and improved safety.5

Subsequent innovations aimed to enhance efficiency and recovery. Laser-assisted liposuction applies thermal energy, usually 1064 or 1320 nm, to disrupt adipocytes and coagulate vessels, although early risks of thermal injury limited its use.6 Third-generation ultrasound-assisted liposuction (UAL) improved adipose selectivity and tissue preservation, reducing complications compared with earlier systems.7,8 Water-assisted liposuction uses a pressurized tumescent jet for atraumatic fat separation,9 power-assisted liposuction adds mechanical vibration for fibrous zones,10,11 and radiofrequency (RF)-assisted liposuction applies bipolar RF energy for adipocyte apoptosis with simultaneous dermal contraction.12 Most recently, helium-plasma skin tightening (HPST) technology has been adopted as an adjunct for controlled subcutaneous coagulation and skin contraction after liposuction within cleared clinical indications.13,14

Male patients pose unique technical challenges due to thicker skin, denser fibroseptal networks, and truncal fat distribution.15 High-definition liposuction, introduced by Hoyos and Millard, sculpts the rectus abdominis and semilunar lines to achieve an athletic contour.16 High-definition liposuction is a safe procedure in appropriately selected patients, with low complication rates and high satisfaction.17 Careful selection remains critical, as a high body mass index (BMI) or unfavorable fat distribution can limit results and increase irregularities.18

A pragmatic “real-definition liposuction” (RDL) philosophy has emerged in our practice. Although not strictly defined in the literature, RDL focuses on smooth debulking and natural contouring rather than pronounced muscle etching. This method works better for patients with a borderline BMI or less visible muscle definition. By contrast, when carving is taken too far, the results often look uneven and patient satisfaction tends to drop, which argues for a more careful, conservative approach.19 With the addition of adjuncts such as HPST, RDL can deliver stable, natural results while lowering the risk of complications.

This study evaluates long-term outcomes, complications, and satisfaction in male patients who underwent UAL between 2008 and 2024. It compares HDL and RDL techniques, BMI subgroups, and adjunctive technologies to identify predictors of durable and satisfactory results.

METHODS

Study Design and Ethics

This mixed-methods observational study combined a retrospective chart review with a patient-reported outcome survey. All operations and follow-up assessments were performed at a private plastic surgery center by the same surgeon between January 2008 and January 2024. Although this study was not pre-registered, operative workflows were consistent and performed by the same surgeon throughout the study period. Patients were grouped retrospectively according to surgical era rather than by prospectively defined anatomical or clinical criteria; therefore, the groups were not randomized and differed due to temporal changes in technique and patient selection:

Era I (2008-2011): Only deep-fat removal was performed; no superficial liposuction or abdominal etching were applied.

Era I (2008-2011): Only deep-fat removal was performed; no superficial liposuction or abdominal etching were applied.

Era II (2011-2014): Incorporation of superficial liposuction and deliberate creation of negative spaces to accentuate muscle borders, occasionally combined with autologous fat grafting to augment the chest or shoulders.

Era II (2011-2014): Incorporation of superficial liposuction and deliberate creation of negative spaces to accentuate muscle borders, occasionally combined with autologous fat grafting to augment the chest or shoulders.

Era III (2015-2019): Emphasis on full-plane fat removal without exaggerated etching, particularly in higher-BMI patients or those declining muscular sculpting.

Era III (2015-2019): Emphasis on full-plane fat removal without exaggerated etching, particularly in higher-BMI patients or those declining muscular sculpting.

Era IV (2019-2024): This period was characterized by continued use of the RDL technique and the selective integration of HPST in patients with mild-to-moderate preoperative skin laxity. Not all RDL cases received HPST. A limited number of HDL procedures were still performed based on patient preference and anatomical suitability.

Era IV (2019-2024): This period was characterized by continued use of the RDL technique and the selective integration of HPST in patients with mild-to-moderate preoperative skin laxity. Not all RDL cases received HPST. A limited number of HDL procedures were still performed based on patient preference and anatomical suitability.

The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the affiliated ethics committee (Approval No. E-10840098-202.3.02-5367).

Surgical Techniques

All UAL procedures were performed using a third-generation ultrasound-assisted liposuction system (VASER®, Solta Medical, Bothell, WA). Probe selection was tailored to tissue characteristics (single-groove for fibrous regions; two- or three-groove for softer regions). C-mode was generally used in deeper planes and V-mode in superficial layers. Ultrasound energy was applied according to intraoperative tactile feedback.

For patients treated in the most recent era, a helium-plasma skin-tightening device (Renuvion®, Apyx Medical, Clearwater, FL) was applied after liposuction for subdermal tissue contraction at 60%-75% power with a helium gas flow of 2.0-3.5 L/min, in accordance with manufacturer specifications and our routine clinical settings. Throughout all surgical eras, successive VASER® systems with equivalent specifications were used, maintaining consistent probe types, energy settings, and tumescent infiltration techniques. The primary variable that evolved over time was the operative plane of fat removal: in the HDL phase, superficial sculpting was emphasized to highlight muscular contours, whereas the RDL approach favored uniform multi-plane reduction with limited superficial etching, conceptually similar to the variable degrees of muscular definition previously described by Hoyos et al.20 Postoperative management—compression garments, activity restrictions, and the follow-up schedule—remained identical across HDL, RDL, and HPST groups.

Data Collection and Survey Instrument

Clinical data were reviewed for demographics, perioperative details, and postoperative outcomes.

To assess long-term satisfaction, complications, lifestyle changes, and overall perceptions of the procedure, two patient-reported surveys were administered:

BODY-Q, a validated instrument specific to body-contouring outcomes, including the modules Body Image, Satisfaction with Abdomen, Appraisal of Excess Skin, and Satisfaction with Surgeon.

BODY-Q, a validated instrument specific to body-contouring outcomes, including the modules Body Image, Satisfaction with Abdomen, Appraisal of Excess Skin, and Satisfaction with Surgeon.

A custom-designed 26-item supplementary questionnaire capturing parameters not addressed by the BODY-Q, such as recovery (return to work, postoperative discomfort, garment use), weight change, exercise frequency, visible “fake abdominal muscles,” and whether revision surgery had been performed by the operating surgeon or elsewhere.

A custom-designed 26-item supplementary questionnaire capturing parameters not addressed by the BODY-Q, such as recovery (return to work, postoperative discomfort, garment use), weight change, exercise frequency, visible “fake abdominal muscles,” and whether revision surgery had been performed by the operating surgeon or elsewhere.

This supplementary form also contained a single 0-5 Likert-scale item asking, “Would you recommend your doctor to others?”. Responses were analyzed as a recommendation score (0 = definitely not, 5 = definitely yes) and linearly rescaled to 0-100 for comparability with BODY-Q domains.

Both surveys were distributed electronically via e-mail between March and May 2025. Each participant received a personalized link and was given two weeks to respond; nonresponders were subsequently contacted by telephone. The survey was administered once, but it included paired items to capture both early (≈6-month) and current long-term outcomes using patient recall. Participants were asked to rate their satisfaction and domain-specific outcomes at these two distinct time points within the same questionnaire. This approach allowed comparison of patients' initial postoperative impressions with their most recent long-term perceptions. All responses were time-stamped and cross-validated with clinical records. Participation was voluntary, and all respondents provided signed informed consent under institutional policies and data-protection regulations.

Outcome Measures

BODY-Q domain scores were analyzed at two time points—early (≈6 months) and current (latest perception at the time of the 2025 survey)—based on patients' recall responses. The primary outcome was long-term patient satisfaction with body image (BODY-Q, Rasch-transformed 0-100 scale).

Secondary outcomes included early satisfaction (6 months), recommendation scores, abdomen-specific satisfaction, postoperative skin quality, satisfaction with surgeon, complication severity (0-5 scale), recovery parameters, postoperative weight change, exercise frequency, presence of “fake abs”, and revision rates. The recommendation score was derived from the supplementary 26-item questionnaire, not from the BODY-Q instrument, and analyzed as an independent patient-reported measure.

Complication severity was rated by patients on a 0-5 ordinal scale, adapted from previous aesthetic surgery satisfaction surveys.

0 = no complication or concern

0 = no complication or concern

1-2 = mild or expected postoperative events (eg, pain, bruising, swelling, transient numbness)

1-2 = mild or expected postoperative events (eg, pain, bruising, swelling, transient numbness)

3 = moderate complication requiring additional office care (eg, seroma aspiration, delayed return to work)

3 = moderate complication requiring additional office care (eg, seroma aspiration, delayed return to work)

4-5 = severe complication requiring intervention, revision, or prolonged recovery

4-5 = severe complication requiring intervention, revision, or prolonged recovery

For analysis, severity ≥3 was classified as a clinically meaningful complication.

The follow-up interval for each patient was calculated as the time elapsed between the date of surgery and the date of survey completion in 2025. Follow-up duration was recorded to compare eras.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on distribution. Normality was not assumed, and between-group comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc Mann–Whitney U tests). Categorical variables were compared using χ² or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate.

Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between satisfaction and continuous factors such as BMI, exercise frequency, and aspirated fat volume. Independent predictors of satisfaction scores were then identified with multivariable linear regression, with variables chosen beforehand based on clinical relevance. Binary logistic regression was used to identify predictors of high satisfaction (BODY-Q ≥ 70). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess how well the model discriminated outcomes and to identify optimal cutoff values. A mediation analysis was also used to test whether postoperative BMI explained the link between exercise frequency and satisfaction. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to test the effect of HPST while adjusting for BMI, age, and exercise frequency. To compare patients treated with HPST against those who were not, a propensity score matching (1:1 nearest-neighbor) was applied based on baseline variables including age, BMI, exercise frequency, and aspirated fat volume. Predefined subgroup analyses were performed according to exercise frequency (≥4 vs <4 sessions per week), weight stability (±3 kg), smoking status, presence of a self-reported “fake abs”, HPST use by age (≥40 vs <40 years), and survey response status (actual vs predicted).

All analyses were carried out in Python 3.11.4, using standard scientific libraries including Pandas, SciPy, Statsmodels, and Scikit-learn. Receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were obtained with Scikit-learn, and the figures were prepared in Matplotlib and Seaborn. A two-sided P-value of <.05 was set as the level of statistical significance.

Predictors of Satisfaction and Recommendation

Correlation analysis showed that long-term satisfaction was negatively related to preoperative BMI (ρ = −0.478, P < .0001), body weight (ρ = −0.413, P < .0001), and aspirated fat volume (ρ = −0.396, P < .0001). By contrast, satisfaction correlated positively with exercise frequency (ρ = +0.484, P < .0001). Age, however, showed no statistically significant relationship (ρ = −0.023, P = .611) (Supplemental Table 2).

In multivariable logistic regression for high satisfaction (BODY-Q ≥ 70), current satisfaction (odds ratio (OR) = 1.28, P < .0001) and skin quality (OR = 1.05, P = .0002) remained independent predictors. Preoperative BMI, aspirated fat volume, smoking status, and surgical technique (HDL vs RDL) were not independently associated (Supplemental Table 3).

Complications and Technique Comparison

We assessed complications using patient-reported severity scores (0-5). Importantly, patients often rated not only true complications but also expected recovery symptoms—such as pain, bruising, swelling, or a longer healing period—as complications. This also included issues like delayed return to work, repeated seroma aspirations, or the need to wear compression garments for longer than usual. Complication rates varied significantly across eras (χ², P < .01). The combined rate of any complication or patient-reported complaint was 38.5% (era I), 33.3% (era II), 20.7% (era III), and 20.7% (era IV) (Supplemental Table 4). Mean severity scores followed a similar decreasing trend across periods (era I: 0.83 ± 1.17; era II: 0.81 ± 1.24; era III: 0.44 ± 0.93; era IV: 0.43 ± 0.94). When restricted to clinically meaningful complications (severity ≥3), the frequency was 15.4% in era I, 12.3% in era II, 4.7% in era III, and 4.6% in era IV (Supplemental Table 5).

Helium-Plasma Skin Tightening Results: Unadjusted, Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), and Propensity Score Matching

In era IV, 32 patients (36.8%) underwent HDL rather than RDL ± HPST. These individuals were included in the era IV analysis to preserve sample integrity, as subgrouping would have yielded insufficient statistical power.

All HPST-related analyses were performed using current BODY-Q skin-quality scores recorded at the time of the 2025 survey. These values reflect each patient's most recent postoperative perception of skin quality, with variable follow-up durations (approximately 1-5 years) among patients operated between 2019 and 2024. Helium-plasma skin tightening was selectively applied to patients with mild-to-moderate pre-operative skin laxity.

In era IV, unadjusted comparisons showed lower mean current skin-quality scores in HPST patients vs non-HPST (66.1 ± 13.5 vs 75.2 ± 21.0; Welch t-test P = .041; Mann–Whitney P = .099) (Supplemental Table 10).

After adjusting for BMI, exercise frequency, age, and aspirated fat volume in ANCOVA, HPST use was not independently associated with current skin quality (F = 0.54, P = .466) (Supplemental Table 11).

Following 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching on baseline covariates, 58 patients were included in each group. Even after matching, mean current skin-quality scores remained lower in HPST patients than controls (66.1 ± 13.4 vs 80.0 ± 20.3; paired t-test P = .00014) (Supplemental Table 12).

Baseline Comparison of Helium-Plasma Skin Tightening vs No Helium-Plasma Skin Tightening (Era IV)

In era IV, patients receiving HPST were older (39.5 ± 8.5 vs 29.2 ± 8.1 years; P < .0001; standardized mean difference [SMD] 1.23) and had higher pre- and postoperative BMI (25.5 vs 23.9 kg/m², P = .0017; 25.7 vs 24.4 kg/m², P = .0119). Helium-plasma skin tightening was selectively applied to men with mild-to-moderate pre-operative skin laxity, explaining the higher mean BMI and age in this subgroup.

Compression-garment use was more prolonged (3.9 vs 3.3 weeks; P = .0090). Six-month satisfaction was lower (76.3 vs 85.0; P = .0043). However, current natural abdomen scores and complication rates did not differ significantly. Skin quality trended lower in the HPST group (66.1 vs 75.2; P = .0990; SMD −0.55) (Supplemental Table 13).

Nonresponder and Mediation Analyses

Predicted satisfaction in nonresponders (62.0) was slightly lower than observed satisfaction in responders (63.3), without statistical significance (P = .314) (Supplemental Table 14).

Post Hoc Comparative Analysis of Surgical Periods

Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed higher satisfaction, natural abdomen, and recommendation scores in era IV vs earlier eras (all P < .01). Era II had the lowest values across domains, with significant differences vs era III and era IV. Skin quality was notably lower in era II (P < .001 vs era III; P = .005 vs era IV). More minor but significant differences were observed between era I and era III (P ≈ .04) (Supplemental Table 15).

Predictive Modeling and Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis

In multivariable models, current satisfaction (OR = 1.27; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.20-1.36; P < .0001) and skin quality (OR = 1.04; 95% CI 1.02-1.07; P = .00023) independently predicted high recommendation (derived from the supplementary 0-5 Likert item, rescaled 0-100), while BMI, aspirated fat volume, and surgical technique did not (Supplemental Table 16). The model showed excellent discrimination (AUC 0.976). The optimal cutoff for current satisfaction was 67.3.

Expanded Subgroup Analysis: “Fake Abs” Population

Among patients reporting a “fake abs” appearance, the high-satisfaction subgroup had lower BMI, exercised more frequently, and had smaller aspirated volumes than the low-satisfaction subgroup, with corresponding higher BODY-Q satisfaction and recommendation scores (Supplemental Table 17).

Revisions

Among responders, 70/508 (13.8%) underwent repeat liposuction: 21 by the same surgeon and 49 elsewhere. Mean current satisfaction scores were lower in patients with revision than those without revision, and lowest among patients reoperated elsewhere (Table 5). In logistic regression, higher exercise frequency was inversely associated with revision (OR = 0.78, P < .001), and the RDL technique was associated with a reduced risk of revision (OR = 0.32, P = .029). Body mass index, aspirated fat volume, HDL technique, and HPST use were not independent predictors (Supplemental Table 18).

DISCUSSION

Evolution of Technique and Patient Selection

Over 16 years, this series evolved from conventional use of UAL (Era I), through HDL (Era II), toward a predominantly RDL approach (Era III and IV). Era I was associated with moderate satisfaction but the highest complication severity, reflecting the surgeon's learning curve and the absence of standardized techniques such as drainage. Nevertheless, results were consistent with prior reports that conservative, deep-layer UAL yields reliable safety and satisfaction when patient expectations are realistic.21

In contrast, era II—characterized by aggressive superficial contouring—showed the lowest long-term satisfaction and recommendation scores. These findings mirror prospective studies demonstrating that HDL results in male patients depend heavily on optimal BMI and adherence to postoperative exercise.22 Patients who reported “fake abs” typically had higher BMI, larger aspirate volumes, and exercised less frequently. In era IV, HDL was performed only in highly selected men with favorable anatomy—typically those with lower BMI, good baseline muscle tone, and minimal laxity. Therefore, the improved outcomes observed in this era likely reflect refined patient selection and surgical judgment rather than an intrinsic superiority of one technique over another.

The RDL approach emphasized balanced, full-plane fat removal without exaggerated etching. Satisfaction scores were higher relative to HDL, especially in men with BMI <25 and regular exercise activity. Strict patient selection and limited sculpting were associated with a lower risk of unnatural abdominal contours, consistent with the consensus that long-term satisfaction in men depends more on natural contours than on exaggerated etching.23 These differences across surgical eras may also reflect the surgeon's progressive technical refinement, stricter patient selection, and overall learning curve rather than inherent superiority of one approach over another.

Selective Role of Helium-Plasma Skin Tightening in Skin Tightening

Our analysis of patients treated with HPST after 2019 underscores the importance of careful patient selection. In unadjusted comparisons, HPST patients appeared to have lower skin quality scores than nonusers, but after covariate adjustment and multivariable modeling, HPST use was not independently associated with outcomes.

In era IV (2019-2024), even after 1:1 propensity score matching for age, BMI, exercise frequency, and aspirated fat volume, mean skin-quality scores were still lower in HPST patients than in matched controls (66.1 vs 80.0, P = .00014). This ongoing difference likely reflects selection patterns rather than device-related effects. In clinical practice, HPST tended to be used in older men with higher −BMI and greater baseline laxity—characteristics that matching cannot fully adjust for.

When complication severity was reclassified using a stricter threshold (severity ≥3 on a 0-5 scale), the frequency of clinically meaningful complications was low across all eras, including era IV (4.6%). Minor complaints such as pain, bruising, swelling, or numbness were generally rated 1-2 and excluded from this threshold. This confirms that while some patients reported transient postoperative findings such as edema or palpable crepitus, very few considered these to represent moderate or severe complications.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with the selective use of HPST as an adjunct for older or higher −BMI men with mild-to-moderate laxity. Its apparent effect on patient-reported outcomes is explained mainly by underlying candidacy factors rather than device-related harm, and clinically meaningful complication rates remain low.24

Predictors of Long-Term Satisfaction and the “Fake Abs” Phenomenon

Correlation and regression analyses showed that exercise frequency and skin quality were the strongest positive predictors of satisfaction. In contrast, BMI and aspirated fat volume were inversely associated. Current satisfaction and skin quality were the only independent predictors of high satisfaction (BODY-Q ≥ 70) score, indicating that patients' perceived outcome quality rather than demographic or procedural variables determined long-term satisfaction. The model showed excellent discrimination (AUC 0.976). This almost perfect value probably reflects the conceptual overlap between BODY-Q satisfaction scores and the supplementary questionnaire's recommendation item, rather than model overfitting. It also highlights that patients' willingness to recommend is shaped mainly by their subjective experience.

The “fake abs” phenomenon—patient-reported unnatural abdominal etching—was most frequent in era II and correlated with significantly lower BODY-Q scores. This effect may arise even after technically successful HDL surgery, when constant muscular definition in a relaxed posture produces an “always-flexed” abdominal appearance. Although this may look impressive in photographs, many patients find it aesthetically or psychologically uncomfortable, particularly when limb musculature is less developed or when postoperative weight gain accentuates the etched fat compartments. However, RDL patients who reported “fake abs” still maintained higher outcomes, reinforcing the protective effect of natural contouring. These findings indicate that excessive etching in suboptimal candidates is associated with a higher likelihood of unnatural aesthetics and dissatisfaction, as previously cautioned in HDL literature.15,22

Patients who required secondary liposuction, particularly when performed by another surgeon, showed the lowest long-term BODY-Q satisfaction scores. This likely reflects pre-existing dissatisfaction and high expectations rather than surgical quality itself, as such patients often represent a self-selected group with lower tolerance for postoperative imperfections.

Mediation analysis confirmed that the positive effect of exercise frequency on satisfaction was partly mediated through lower BMI (+1.63). It also exerted a direct independent benefit (+2.61), yielding a total effect of +4.24.

Masculine Aesthetics, Gym Culture, and Surgical Disclosure

Quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that men are most satisfied when results stem from disciplined training rather than overt surgery. Higher −BMI and lower activity levels correlated with lower satisfaction and a greater likelihood of reporting unnatural or over-etched abdominal contours. These observations are consistent with psychological studies showing that body image dissatisfaction, media exposure, and gender role stress significantly influence male attitudes toward cosmetic surgery.25,26

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design carries a risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding.27 Patient allocation was nonrandom and patient-driven after counseling, which may have further increased this bias. Still, as Concato et al28 pointed out, the quality of methods in observational research often matters more than the study design itself. When carefully carried out, retrospective studies can provide valid and clinically meaningful evidence. However, neither regression modeling nor propensity score matching can account for unmeasured factors such as tissue tone, skin turgor, or surgeon judgment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Second, all outcomes were derived from patient-reported instruments, including the validated BODY-Q and the supplementary 26-item questionnaire, both of which remain vulnerable to recall bias and inter-individual perception variability. No independent photographic evaluation or blinded aesthetic grading by external plastic surgeons was performed, which could have provided additional objectivity to these patient-reported results. Psychosocial and disclosure-related insights were extracted from qualitative notes rather than structured instruments, underscoring the need for validated tools in future studies.29 In addition, the recommendation score analyzed in this study was obtained from a single custom 0-5 Likert-scale question within the supplementary 26-item questionnaire rather than from the BODY-Q instrument. Although this item directly assessed patients' willingness to recommend their doctor, it represents a nonvalidated but clinically relevant indicator of perceived satisfaction.

Third, follow-up times differed between surgical eras, making it harder to compare long-term results. Outcomes may also have been affected by changes in technique over the years and by stricter patient selection, so not all differences can be explained by the surgical approach alone. Preoperative anatomical parameters such as skin laxity, fat distribution, and baseline muscle definition were not formally graded, and this absence of anatomical control should be considered when interpreting the comparisons between surgical eras.

Fourth, the single-surgeon, single-center setting may limit external generalizability.

Fifth, the “fake abs” variable was based exclusively on patient self-reports without external validation, introducing subjectivity. It reflects a subjective perception of fixed or exaggerated abdominal definition that feels soft or flabby rather than muscular. This differs from the naturally visible six-pack seen in professional or athletic individuals, whose rectus muscles remain palpable and contractile even at rest. The phenomenon therefore represents a perceived mismatch between contour appearance and underlying muscle tone and should be interpreted descriptively rather than as an objective finding.

Sixth, clinically meaningful complications were defined as patient-reported severity ≥3. Mild, expected events such as pain, bruising, swelling, or transient numbness—usually rated at levels 1-2—were excluded. The 0-5 complication severity scale used for this classification was adapted from prior literature and, although clinically informative, has not been formally validated.

Finally, the nonresponder analysis found no meaningful gap between predicted satisfaction in nonresponders and observed satisfaction in responders, suggesting that long-term survey data were only minimally biased.

Future Directions

Upcoming studies should focus on prospective, multicenter designs with standardized follow-up, which would help separate technical outcomes from the influence of patient behavior. Preoperative use of validated psychosocial screening instruments is needed to account for psychological and lifestyle factors, and to evaluate how long-term satisfaction interacts with body image and adherence to exercise regimens.

Objective validation of contour changes should be incorporated in future prospective designs using reproducible imaging modalities such as pre- and postoperative ultrasonography, 3D surface scanning, or ideally MRI-based volumetric analysis. These techniques can quantitatively assess skin and subcutaneous thickness, muscle definition, and overall tissue distribution—providing an external, measurable correlate to patient-reported outcomes and surgeon-based aesthetic grading, which is inherently feasible only within a prospective framework.

Comparative, technique-specific prospective trials are warranted to refine the indications for RDL vs HDL approaches and to clarify the role of adjunctive technologies such as HPST.

Finally, developing male-specific patient-reported outcome instruments that capture constructs such as authenticity, masculinity, and disclosure preferences may provide deeper insights into the psychological determinants of long-term satisfaction in male body contouring.

CONCLUSIONS

Real-definition ultrasound-assisted liposuction (RDL) in men provided higher satisfaction, more natural and durable outcomes, and fewer revisions compared with high-definition liposuction (HDL). Over-etching increased unnatural contours and dissatisfaction. Careful patient selection—particularly BMI ≤25, consistent exercise, and weight stability—is essential for long-term success. Helium-plasma skin-tightening (HPST) may be considered for older patients with mild laxity, though its independent effect on skin quality was limited.
