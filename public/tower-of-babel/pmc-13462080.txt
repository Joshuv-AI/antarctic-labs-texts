Association of stress urinary incontinence with stress, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life in nulligravid college women
Authors: Disha Mittal, Mohammad Sidiq, Jyoti Sharma, Fahad Alanazi, Rajkumar Krishnan Vasanthi, Praveen Rawat, Faizan Kashoo, Akansha Suman, Mohammed M Alshehri, Shaima Alothman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress urinary incontinence (SUI) is increasingly recognized among young women, yet its psychosocial impact in collegiate populations remains understudied. We therefore investigated how SUI affects perceived stress, sleep quality, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in young female students. We conducted a cross-sectional study of 233 collegiate females (mean age 21.1 ± 1.9 years) with SUI symptoms at an Indian university. Participants completed validated instruments: the King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ, urinary incontinence (UI) impact), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and *SF-36* (HRQoL). Regression and mediation analyses identified predictors of HRQoL. High UI impact (mean KHQ: 47.3/100) significantly correlated with elevated stress (mean PSS: 18.8/40; t (231) = 6.01, p < 0.001, d = 0.79) and poorer sleep quality (mean PSQI: 5.3/21; t (231) = 4.57, p < 0.001, d = 0.60). Participants with greater UI impact reported markedly reduced HRQoL (mean SF-36: 62.5/100; t (197.3) = − 7.12, p < 0.001, d = − 0.93). Regression confirmed UI impact (β = −0.45, p < 0.001), stress (β = −0.29, p < 0.001), and poor sleep (β = −0.17, p = 0.004) as independent predictors of diminished HRQoL, explaining 46.2% of variance. Exploratory mediation revealed that perceived stress accounted for 17.8% of the association between UI impact and HRQoL mediated by stress (indirect effect: −0.34, 95% CI: −0.52 to − 0.19); however, given the cross-sectional design, these findings are associative, not causal. UI impact was associated with impaired psychological well-being, sleep, and overall quality of life in young collegiate women. The strong independent associations highlight an urgent need for targeted interventions (e.g., pelvic floor therapy and psychological support) in academic health services to mitigate these psychosocial burdens.Keywords: Stress urinary incontinence, Nulligravid women, Female college students, Perceived stress, Sleep quality, Health-related quality of life, Psychosocial burden, Young women, Health riskSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is characterized by the involuntary leakage of urine during activities that increase intra-abdominal pressure, such as coughing, sneezing, laughing, or physical exertion1–4. Internationally, stress urinary incontinence has emerged as the major cause of incontinence in women6. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study of 2019, about 9.9–75% of the female population is affected by urinary incontinence, with SUI representing approximately 50% of total cases7–10. Although SUI has traditionally been considered to be a disease of older or parous women, a recent data project suggest rising prevalence among younger women including nulliparous and physically active females1–3. This shift demands attention because young women with SUI often experience unacknowledged suffering due to stigma, shame, and the mistaken belief that such conditions are uncommon in their peer group4. Consequently, minimal reporting of symptoms and delayed help‑seeking can initiate adverse psychological and functional consequences5.

Anxiety commonly accompanies SUI in young women, driven by fear of leakage, embarrassment in public, and exercise limitation factors that collectively damage emotional health and reduce social engagement6,7. Associated lower urinary tract symptoms, including nocturia and urgency, frequently co-occur with SUI in young women and may contribute to sleep disturbances8,9. Although urgency and nocturia represent hallmark features of overactive bladder and urgency urinary incontinence rather than SUI per se, emerging evidence demonstrates substantial overlap of LUTS across incontinence subtypes in this population. The RISE FOR HEALTH Study, a large community-based survey of 2,905 US women, found that 45% of participants reported nocturia and 37% reported urgency or a sudden need to urinate in the preceding seven days, with considerable co-occurrence across all incontinence subtypes10–12. Among women aged 18–25 years specifically, 36% reported nighttime frequency and 32% reported urgency, even in those with primarily stress incontinence symptoms13–15. This symptom overlap likely reflects shared pelvic floor dysfunction pathways rather than diagnostic misclassification16. In the context of the present study, the presence of associated LUTS such as nocturia and urgency among participants with self-reported SUI is therefore consistent with contemporary epidemiological findings and does not indicate inclusion of urgency-predominant incontinence cases17–19. Moreover, the impact on health‑related quality of life (physical abilities, emotional well‑being, self‑image, social relationships) may be especially severe in younger individuals navigating identity formation, peer relations, and academic stress20. Yet the complex impact of SUI in younger women remains poorly characterized: most studies focus on aging adults or postpartum females. We therefore conducted a cross‑sectional study to comprehensively examine how SUI relates to stress, sleep quality, and general quality of life among young college‑aged females. Our goal is to fill this knowledge gap, increase awareness, reduce stigma, and promote early screening and treatment in academic and healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design, population setting, and ethical approval

This cross -sectional survey included 323 young collegiate females recruited from the physiotherapy department, Galgotias University, between October and January 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the departmental research committee of Galgotias University (reference DRC/PT/UG/24–25/03, 10-09-2024), and the study was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trial Registry India (CTRI/2024/09/074490), 27/09/2024). MAPI trust granted permission for the King’s Health Questionnaire (Special Terms Agreement no;102496). We followed STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies and the ethical principles of the Helsinki declaration21,22.

Eligibility criteria and sample size calculation

Using Raosoft® software with an estimate of approximately 500,000 female college students in India, a 70% response distribution, 5% margin of error, and 95% confidence level, we calculated a minimum sample of 323. Allowing for 10% attrition, we recruited a larger sample. Inclusion criteria were: (i) female students aged 18–24 years; (ii) currently enrolled in a college or university; (iii) self-reported involuntary urine leakage during activities that increase intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, laughing, or physical exertion); and (iv) engagement in at least light-level physical activity. Participants were required to be in generally stable health (i.e., not acutely ill or hospitalised) at the time of assessment. Exclusion criteria were: (i) pregnancy; (ii) history of pelvic or urological surgery; (iii) acute psychiatric conditions requiring active hospitalisation; and (iv) severe cognitive impairment precluding independent questionnaire completion. Chronic, stable medical conditions (e.g., controlled hypothyroidism, well-managed asthma) and stable psychiatric conditions (e.g., managed anxiety on stable medication) were not exclusion criteria, provided they did not prevent questionnaire completion. Minor surgical procedures outside the pelvis (e.g., appendectomy, orthopaedic or ENT procedures) were not exclusion criteria. The presence of SUI was established through self-reported symptoms and responses to the QUID, without clinical or urodynamic confirmation. Accompanying lower urinary tract symptoms (frequency, urgency, sensation of incomplete emptying) were recorded descriptively from the King’s Health Questionnaire severity/coping domain but were not used as inclusion or classification criteria.

Operational definition of stress urinary incontinence

We employed a two-step approach combining self-reported symptom profile with the validated QUID instrument to classify SUI status. The Questionnaire for Urinary Incontinence Diagnosis (QUID) is a six-item instrument designed to differentiate stress and urge urinary incontinence23. The QUID contains three stress items (leakage with coughing, sneezing, and exercise) and three urge items (leakage with urgency, inability to reach the toilet, and nocturia). Each item uses six frequency-based response options scored from 0 to 5, yielding separate Stress and Urge subscale scores ranging from 0 to 15. The established diagnostic thresholds are: greater than or equal to 4 for stress UI and greater than or equal to 6 for urge UI. Mixed UI classification requires meeting both criteria simultaneously.

Our classification algorithm proceeded as follows: Step 1: Participants self-reported involuntary urine leakage during activities increasing intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, laughing, physical exertion). Step 2: All participants completed the QUID questionnaire. Step 3: Participants were classified as having SUI if they (a) self-reported stress-predominant leakage symptoms, AND (b) scored greater than or equal to 4 on the QUID stress subscale, AND (c) scored less than 6 on the QUID urge subscale. Step 4: Participants scoring above threshold for both stress and urge components (greater than or equal to 4 on stress AND greater than or equal to 6 on urge) were classified as mixed UI and excluded from the primary analysis.

Measurement instruments

King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ). The KHQ is a 21-item, condition-specific instrument assessing health-related quality of life in women with urinary incontinence24. It comprises nine domains scored 0-100 (higher scores indicate greater impairment), plus a symptom severity scale (0–30). The KHQ has demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach alpha = 0.78–0.87 across domains) and validity in women with SUI, overactive bladder, and mixed UI34. A minimally important difference of 5 points has been established25.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). The PSS-10 is a 10-item measure assessing the degree to which respondents perceive situations as unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overwhelming26. Scores range from 0 to 40, with established cutoffs of 0–13 (low), 14–26 (moderate), and 27–40 (high). The PSS-10 has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach alpha = 0.78–0.83) and a two-factor structure (perceived stress and perceived coping) among university students27,28.

Physical activity was assessed using a structured questionnaire adapted from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form, which captures activity type, frequency, duration, and intensity42. Participants reported engagement in specific activities including: walking; light exercise (e.g., yoga, stretching); moderate exercise (e.g., brisk walking, cycling); and vigorous or high-impact exercise (e.g., running, jumping, CrossFit, volleyball, basketball). Based on self-reported activity patterns, participants were categorized into three groups:1 Light activity only2, Moderate activity, and3 Vigorous or very active (including sports participation). This categorization aligns with IPAQ metabolic equivalent (MET) scoring guidelines42. Participants reporting competitive sports participation were asked to specify their primary sport(s). High-impact sports (volleyball, basketball, gymnastics, CrossFit, running) were analyzed as a separate subgroup. Physical activity level (light/moderate/vigorous) and sports participation (yes/no) were included as covariates in all regression models to control for potential confounding.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The PSQI is a 19-item self-report questionnaire assessing sleep quality over the past month29. Seven component scores (0–3 each) are summed to a global score of 0–21; scores greater than 5 indicate poor sleep quality. In college students, the PSQI has shown excellent internal consistency (Cronbach alpha = 0.83–0.87) and di SF-36 Health Survey30. The SF-36 is a 36-item generic health-related quality of life instrument40. It yields eight subscale scores (0-100, higher scores indicate better health) and two summary component scores (Physical Component Summary [PCS] and Mental Component Summary [MCS]). The SF-36 has been validated in over 50 countries and has demonstrated good psychometric properties in young adult populations, agnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.999) for detecting insomnia31,32.

Rationale for dual quality-of-life instruments. The KHQ and SF-36 serve complementary rather than redundant purposes. The KHQ is a condition-specific HRQoL instrument capturing the impact of urinary incontinence on domains directly relevant to SUI (role limitations, physical limitations, social limitations, emotions, sleep/energy, and symptom severity)33. The SF-36 is a generic health status measure enabling comparison of our participants’ health status with general population norms and across different disease conditions40. The combination of disease-specific and generic QoL measures is recommended by international guidelines and allows both targeted assessment of SUI impact and broader health status comparison33. The SF-36 PCS score serves as the primary outcome variable for regression analysis, with the KHQ total score serving as the primary predictor assessing incontinence-related impact.

Procedure

We recruited participants from several academic departments via convenience sampling. They answered a structured questionnaire including standardized scales and personal data. We collected demographic and lifestyle information (age, height, weight, year of study, living status, physical activity), medical history (diagnosed conditions, surgeries, menstrual irregularities, contraceptive use, smoking, alcohol). Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) was measured by self-reported involuntary urine leakage during strenuous activities like coughing, sneezing, or exercise. The King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ) was employed for the assessment of the impact of other domains of the women’s health such as general health, role limitations, physical and social functioning, emotional well-being, and sleep/energy which had higher scores indicting more impairment. Psychological stress scores were assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) with the interpretation of score as below: 0–13, 14–26, and 27–40 (low-moderate-high stress). The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to determine sleep quality as a global score greater than 5 was indicative of poor sleep quality. To measure the overall quality of life, the SF-36 Health Survey with eight subscales (physical functioning, emotional role constraints, vitality, pain, general health) and scores 0–100 (higher scores indicate better perceived health) was utilized. Additionally, the Questionnaire for Urinary Incontinence Diagnosis (QUID) was used to confirm and classify SUI symptoms. The QUID is a validated 6-item instrument that differentiates between stress, urge, and mixed urinary incontinence, with three key items specifically assessing SUI symptoms (leakage with coughing, sneezing, and physical activity). Participants were categorized based on the number of positive SUI items (1 item, 2 items, or all 3 items). Data collection was completed under the supervision of supervisor to ensure following all responses were kept anonymous and their confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and by inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. KHQ, PSS, and PSQI scores approximated normality (p > 0.05), whereas SF-36 scores showed marginal skewness (W = 0.96, p = 0.021). Levene’s test indicated homogeneity of variances for all comparisons (all p > 0.10). No univariate outliers were detected (z < 3.29) and Mahalanobis distance revealed no multivariate outliers. The King’s Health Questionnaire was analysed as a continuous variable in all regression models. This approach preserves statistical power, avoids artificial categorisation, and respects the KHQ’s design as a quality-of-life impact measure rather than a clinical severity classification tool24,34. For supplementary exploratory purposes only, we additionally report a median-split comparison (less than or equal to 45 versus greater than 45), with the explicit caveat that this cut-point is data-driven and reflects impact on quality of life rather than validated clinical disease severity. A simultaneous multiple linear regression examined predictors of the SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS) score, which served as the primary outcome variable. Age, body mass index, physical activity level, PSS-10 total, PSQI global score, and KHQ total score were entered as predictor variables. Physical activity level (light/moderate/vigorous) and sports participation (yes/no) were included as covariates. We also examined the interaction between physical activity level and KHQ scores in exploratory analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 323 individuals were approached for initial screening. Of these, 294 returned completed questionnaires (response rate = 91.2. All 294 returned questionnaires were assessed for eligibility; [19] did not meet age criteria, [18] were pregnant, [2] had a history of pelvic or urological surgery, and [22] incomplete form. The final analytical sample comprised 233 participants with self-reported SUI and complete data (Fig. 1).

Inferential analyses

Predictors of the health-related quality of life

A simultaneous multiple linear regression examined predictors of the SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS) score (Table 4). Age, BMI, physical activity level, PSS-10 total, PSQI global score, and KHQ total score were entered as predictors. The overall model was significant (F(7, 225) = 38.9, p < 0.001) and accounted for 46.2% of the variance (adjusted R squared = 0.462). Higher KHQ scores (beta = -0.45, p < 0.001), higher PSS-10 scores (beta = -0.29, p < 0.001), and higher PSQI scores (beta = -0.17, p = 0.004) independently predicted poorer physical health-related quality of life. Age, BMI, and physical activity level were not significant predictors. The multicollinearity diagnostics were acceptable (all VIF < 1.7). These results indicate that greater incontinence-related quality-of-life impact, higher perceived stress, and poorer sleep quality each contribute independently to reduced physical HRQoL in young women with SUI.

Association between urinary incontinence severity and psychosocial outcomes

In supplementary exploratory analyses, participants were dichotomised at the median KHQ score (less than or equal to 45 versus greater than 45) into lower and higher UI impact groups. The higher UI impact group reported significantly greater perceived stress (t(231) = 6.01, p < 0.001, d = 0.79) and poorer sleep quality (t(231) = 4.57, p < 0.001, d = 0.60). SF-36 PCS scores were markedly lower in the higher impact group (Welch t(197.3) = -7.12, p < 0.001, d = -0.93). It should be emphasised that this median-split categorisation reflects impact on quality of life rather than validated clinical disease severity, and the cut-point is data-driven rather than clinically established24,34.

Exploratory mediation

Because UI severity was strongly related to stress and HRQoL, an exploratory bootstrapped mediation (5 000 samples) was conducted with KHQ as the predictor, PSS as the mediator and SF-36 as the outcome. The indirect effect (ab = − 0.34, 95% CI − 0.52 to – 0.19) was significant, indicating that 17.8% of the effect of UI severity on HRQoL was transmitted through perceived stress.

Discussion

This study aimed examined the impact of SUI on anxiety, sleep quality, and overall health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in young collegiate females. studies have pointed out the adverse effects of SUI on the quality of life, mental health, and daily functioning of females among young collegians6,35–37. This population which is underrepresented in SUI researching is exposed to substantial psychosocial challenges, consisting of higher demands of anxiety, lower sleep quality, and worsened health-related quality of life (HRQoL)38. The results emphasize the significance of treating SUI among young women because they have significant impacts on their psychological and functionality even though they appear physically able and healthy. Subsequent subsections explain the details of anxiety, sleep quality, and the HRQoL of young women affected by SUI39,40.The perceived stress values recorded (mean PSS = 18.79) can also be compared with the results that found relationships between urinary incontinence and social anxiety, embarrassment, and hypervigilance concerning control of bladder16,17. Furthermore, our regression model proved that the effect of stress on reduced HRQoL was independent (p < 0.001, 29) and the second strongest predictor after SUI severity (p < 0.001, 45). The mediation analysis also provided evidence for these associations as its results indicated that about 18% of the impact of UI on quality of life was mediated by the stress levels. This highlights the proximal concerns about psychological distress in this effort to generalize the physical manifestations of incontinence to expression of dissatisfaction with life41,42. Moreover, the sleep quality, assessed by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), was exhibited to be highly compromised in the individuals affected by high impactUI43. This observation resonates with previous studies demonstrating associations between urinary symptoms and sleep dysfunction in women with incontinence22. However, we acknowledge that nocturia and urgency represent hallmark symptoms of overactive bladder and urgency urinary incontinence rather than SUI per se44. In our sample, sleep disturbance likely reflects the psychosocial burden of SUI - including nocturnal voiding related to associated LUTS, anxiety about leakage, and hypervigilance regarding bladder control - rather than nocturnal urgency as a defining SUI symptom31. The PSQI-assessed sleep dysfunction may also capture secondary effects of stress and worry rather than primary nocturnal voiding disturbances45.

This observation that age or BMI does not have any significant predictive ability on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of young adults suffering stress urinary incontinence (SUI) indicates that psychosocial effects of SUI are stronger among young adults7,46. This is congruent with the idea that disturbance in emotional and lifestyle due to SUI in younger women is a serious issue especially against the academic, social, and self-image demands fitting that of the university scene47,48. The study suggests that in the older generations, traditional risk factors of age and BMI are still relevant, but psychosocial effects cause SUI in younger women to have significant comparable effects or even more pronounced49,50.

Conclusion

The presence of SUI UTI in young women in colleges is linked to significant psychosocial distress, poor sleep quality and lower overall well-being. The results confirm the necessity of early screening, educational initiatives, and inclusion of pelvic health-education into the university wellness programs. Treating SUI at a young age group is crucial in enhancing life and avoiding the sequelae of the condition.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study has the strength of using a well-powered sample and having valid scales, it has a number of limitations. One of them is that its cross-sectional nature restricts its potential to draw causal conclusions between SUI, psychological distress and the quality-of-life outcomes. Additionally, although mediation analysis was conducted to explore potential associative pathways between SUI severity, perceived stress and HRQoL, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporal order or casual relationships. Therefore, the mediation findings should be interpreted as indicative of statistical association rather than casual mechanisms. An additional limitation concerns the potential for misclassification between SUI and mixed urinary incontinence. Although we employed a validated two-step classification algorithm using self-reported symptoms and the QUID instrument32, neither pelvic examination nor urodynamic testing was performed. Without urodynamic confirmation, some degree of misclassification between SUI and mixed UI cannot be entirely excluded. The QUID has demonstrated 80% diagnostic accuracy compared with formal clinical evaluation, with sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 71% for stress UI at the threshold of greater than or equal to 432. Future studies incorporating urodynamic assessment would strengthen diagnostic precision.

Physical activity was assessed via self-report using an IPAQ-adapted questionnaire rather than objective measurement. Although the IPAQ is widely validated, self-reported physical activity data may be subject to recall bias and social desirability effects51. Objective assessment via accelerometery would provide more accurate activity quantification in future research.

An additional important limitation is the lack of clinical confirmation of stress urinary incontinence. Although validated self-report instruments (QUID and KHQ) were used to identify and classify the symptoms of SUI, there was no pelvic examination, urodynamic testing, or physician-confirmed diagnosis. Reliance on self-reported symptoms may have led to misclassification, especially between stress, urge and mixed urinary incontinence subtypes. Future studies should include objective clinical assessment to improve diagnostic accuracy and minimize possible misclassification bias.

Longitudinal research is required to study how these variables are temporally related and whether the effect between them is bi-directional. Second, the study sample was collected in the university environment and cannot be typical of all the cultural, socioeconomic, or regionally contained subpopulation of young women in India. Future research should extend geographically and institution wise to increase its generalizability. Third, self-reported measures can involve recall bias or underreporting especially when it comes to urinary symptoms where there is a stigma attached to it. The inclusion of objective evaluations and qualitative interviews may give a deeper interpretation. Future studies are also needed to examine any intervention measures (both behavioral and psychological) that can reduce the effects of SUI among young women as well as enhance awareness, early diagnosis, and the support-seeking behavior.
