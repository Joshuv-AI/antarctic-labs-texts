Headaches and adolescents: why so many failures in their management
Authors: Giovanni Cucchiaro, William Frye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of headaches in children and adolescents is still a challenge, with patients experiencing pain for years and polypharmacy. We reviewed the medical history of 31 patients referred to our pain clinic for chronic headaches between April 2023 and April 2024. There were more female than male patients (73%). Patients have been reporting headaches for 52 ± 44 months on average. Twenty-nine patients (94%) were experiencing different types of pain besides headaches. The most common medication prescribed in this group of patients was topiramate (70%), followed by tricyclic antidepressants (35%) and triptans (21%). Patients had been prescribed and tried, on average, 4.5 ± 2 (range 1–10) different medications to manage headaches and concomitant psychiatric disorders. Twenty-two patients (71%) had been diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder, including depression, anxiety, and PTSD, and 16% had a history of attempted suicide/self-harm. Fourteen of them (45%) had been prescribed antidepressants or benzodiazepines. They had been prescribed, on average, 2 ± 1 (range 1–4) psychiatric stabilizer medications. We agreed with the referral diagnosis in 39% of the patients. We attributed the headaches to more complex chronic pain conditions, including fibromyalgia (15%) and AMPS (15%), autism with sensory integration problems (9%), and major depression (9%). Patients had seen an average of 3 ± 1 (range 1–5) different specialists; none consulted a pain specialist. Patients underwent between 0 (12%) and four tests (6%), including MRI (52%) and CT of the brain (8%). These neuroimaging studies did not demonstrate any brain pathology. We prescribed new medications and treatments, including nerve blocks, in 19 (61%) patients. In 47% of the cases, patients reported improved headaches, while 22% did not feel our recommendations were effective. Twenty-one percent of patients never came back to the clinic for a follow-up. Significant catastrophizing was present in 57% of the patients; 52% of patients had mild to severe anxiety, and 57% had symptoms of depressive disorder. Conclusions: Headaches are often the manifestation of more complex pain syndromes that require a more holistic approach, different from conventional pharmacological management. Background

Headache is one of the most disabling diseases in the world, with an estimated prevalence of 58% in children and adolescents [1]. Primary chronic headache constitutes a significant burden for patients, and it is one of the major contributors to disabilities in young patients [2]. Frequent school absences and overall poor school performance are common in adolescents with chronic headaches [3]. Emotional functioning is equally affected [4, 5]. This review aimed to analyze the healthcare trajectory of adolescents diagnosed with headaches and the care they received before being referred to a pediatric chronic pain clinic. We looked at the medications patients were prescribed over time, the number and type of specialists they consulted, the number and type of tests they took, their referral diagnosis, and the diagnosis our team made.

Methodology

This is a retrospective study of every patient referred to the chronic pain clinic at Johns Hopkins All Children’s Hospital with a headache diagnosis between April 2023 and April 2024. In addition to the demographic information, we recorded the medications that the patient had been prescribed over time to manage their headaches. We also recorded additional pain complaints besides headaches, the number of physicians they consulted because of headaches, and the number and types of tests they underwent. Patients were evaluated by a team that included a pain specialist, a pain psychologist, and a physical therapist within the chronic pain clinic. Patients were subdivided into four headache categories based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD) (3rd edition) criteria [6]: (1) migraine, including migraine variants and chronic migraine; (2) tension-type headache (TTH), including chronic and episodic tension-type headaches; (3) trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia and other primary headache disorders; and (4) secondary headaches.

Patients were asked to complete a set of questionnaires at the time of the intake visit to assess their psychological background. Before their clinic appointment, surveys were administered to patients via QualtricsXM (an online software platform). We utilized the following validated surveys: Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) [7], Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ9) [8], and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD7) [9]. The survey data were collected and stored using REDCap (please refer to Appendix to interpret GAD and PHQ9 scores).

Results

This is a retrospective study of every patient referred to the pain clinic between April 2023 and April 2024. One hundred eight children and adolescents were evaluated for chronic pain in the chronic pain clinic at Johns Hopkins All Children’s Hospital. In this analysis, we included every patient referred to our pain clinic with a diagnosis of headaches. Age and length of headache complaints were calculated from patients’ initial clinic visits. Thirty-one of these patients had a history of headaches (29%). Headache was the main reason for the referral to the pain clinic in 20 of these patients (65%). The remaining 11 patients (35%) were referred to the pain clinic for other pain issues. However, they reported that headaches were the second most relevant pain they were experiencing, and they were prescribed medication to control the headaches.

The number of girls reporting headaches was significantly higher than that of boys (p = 0.01) (Table 1). The age range in this group of patients was 8 to 19 years. There was no statistical difference in age between boys and girls (Table 1).

Twenty-nine patients (94%) were experiencing different types of pain besides headaches.

Five patients (16%) had headaches secondary to brain tumors, ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement, cranioplasty surgery, concussion, and multiple sclerosis. Patients started complaining of headaches on average 52 ± 44 months (range 1–144) before their initial encounter in this clinic.

Patients had been prescribed and tried 4.5 ± 2 (range 1–10) different medications to manage headaches and psychiatric disorders before their initial clinic visit. In particular, patients were prescribed 3 ± 2 different medications to manage headaches. Two patients had unsuccessful trials of Botox.

In addition to psychiatric disorders, the other most common additional diagnoses were joint pain (18%), autism (15%), and amplified musculoskeletal pain (AMPS) (15%).

We confirmed the referral diagnosis in 39% of the patients. We attributed the headaches to more complex chronic pain conditions, including fibromyalgia (15%) and AMPS (15%), autism with sensory integration problems (9%), and major depression (9%).

Before our assessment, patients had seen an average of 3 ± 1 (range 1–5) different specialists (Table 4). Only four patients saw one specialist, and four saw five specialists. Patients saw one or more neurologists in 34% of the cases, a physiatrist in 15%, and a neurosurgeon in 5%. None of these patients had seen a pain specialist. Patients were also seeing a psychologist (19%) and/or a psychiatrist (15%) to manage their baseline psychiatric disorder.

We prescribed new medications and treatments to 19 patients (61%). We did a bilateral supraorbital nerve block in one patient who had a failed cranial vault reconstruction. Discussion

The focus of this study was to examine the medical history of children and adolescents who suffer from chronic headaches. Our population demographics were consistent with previous reports, with headaches being more prevalent in females than males, especially during puberty due to hormonal changes [10]. Estrogen levels have been linked to the higher prevalence of headaches in females [11], a difference that persists throughout life [10].

The majority of our patients were under the care of neurologists and neurosurgeons. Despite over one-third of our patients experiencing chronic pain outside of their headaches and multiple failed treatments, none of our patients had been assessed by providers specializing in widespread chronic pain complaints before being referred to our clinic. Many of our patients sought care from multiple specialists due to dissatisfaction with their treatment, as they were seeking a definitive solution for their pain. However, in these complex presentations, a definitive solution is rarely available. Given the complexity of these patients, evaluation and treatment provided by a multidisciplinary team are often recommended. The role of a multidisciplinary pain clinic is to reduce pain and to improve functional activities and quality of life. While there currently is no data in the literature describing the benefits of an early referral of children to a multidisciplinary pain clinic, we believe a referral should be considered in the first few weeks or months after the onset of pain, once trials of appropriate treatment modalities have been unsuccessful. When looking at our population, it is concerning that it took, on average, more than 4 years of failed treatments before patients were referred to a multidisciplinary clinic. Multidisciplinary clinics can be effective, though we should be upfront with patients and their families concerning their expectations, because there is always the chance that the pain clinic team may be unable to find a curative treatment. Twenty-two percent of our patients did not feel that our recommendations were useful. However, a multidisciplinary clinic can offer a more comprehensive evaluation of these patients and suggest a multifaceted approach while emphasizing the importance of reducing the level of disability rather than trying to achieve the goal of “no pain” [12].

Twenty-eight percent of our patients have consulted more than two specialists for their headaches. While there is no available data on the potential benefits of seeing multiple specialists in pediatric patients, evidence from adult practices suggests that it is common for older patients to seek the opinion of multiple specialists in addition to their primary care physician [13, 14]. Many patients see multiple physicians, which has clear disadvantages. While seeing an additional specialist for a second opinion may be appropriate, seeing multiple specialists can lead to unnecessary visits, duplicate diagnostic tests, and the use of multiple medications. Additionally, different healthcare providers use various electronic medical records software, making it difficult to access notes and test results ordered by other specialists. A study of 196,314 patients revealed that 7.7% of imaging procedures were repeated within 90 days when previous imaging results were inaccessible. [15] Medicare spends $10 billion annually on imaging, a good portion of which is repeat or unnecessary imaging [16]. Sixty percent of our patients underwent multiple radiologic examinations, brain MRI, and CT scans. None showed any brain pathology or abnormalities that could explain the cause of headaches. Unfortunately, there are no precise guidelines on when to order an MRI or CT scan to evaluate headaches in the absence of obvious clinical signs or history suggesting a brain lesion. The diagnostic value of brain MRI in patients with headaches is controversial [17]. Unlike MRIs ordered for other pathologies, like low back pain, most MRI scans requested for headaches (82.8%) were deemed appropriate in a recent survey [18]. However, the recent publications have raised more questions and suggested that MRIs are unnecessary in the absence of additional neurological symptoms, in the absence of papilledema, and in the presence of an otherwise normal neurological examination [19, 20]. Polypharmacy is becoming a growing concern in pediatric care, particularly when looking at trends in psychotropic polypharmacy in adolescents. Recent studies have shown a rising trend in the prescription of three or more psychotropic medications over the past two decades [21]. This trend is more prevalent among youths between the ages of 6 and 18 [22]. This practice has significant consequences, including adverse drug effects, drug-to-drug interaction, hospitalization, poor medication adherence, wastage, and high cost of healthcare [23, 24]. In addition to these concerns, the recent CHAMP trial demonstrated that common medications such as topiramate and amitriptyline provided youth with no significant reductions in headache frequency or impairment compared to placebo [25]. These results emphasize the importance of non-pharmacological strategies that do not have the potential adverse effects of medications and consider other factors that impact headaches.

Several studies published in the last decade have revealed a relationship between migraine or headaches and psychopathology in children [26]. The relationship between anxiety, depression, and headaches is not clear. It is well known that the risk of migraine is higher in patients with comorbid anxiety and/or depression [27] and that anxiety predicts the persistence of migraine and tension-type headaches [28]. It is also well known that there is a large amount of accompanying symptoms and comorbidities in children with chronic headaches: sleep disturbances [29], problems with school [30], the lack of concentration [30], hyperactivity [31], anxiety, and depression [32], as well as panic attacks [33]. These findings have reinforced physicians’ certainty that headache is a multifactorial disorder that includes not only genetic, medical, and neuropsychological factors but also psychological and personality traits [28]. It then seems crucial to examine these patients using a different perspective. We should identify internal and/or external environmental influences, such as psycho-emotional, climatic, hormonal, dietary, or social factors, which can better explain why medications fail and why pain persists for years in these patients. Our data are in line with the literature data. The prevalence of psychiatric disorders in our population was elevated (67%). It was concerning to find that 15% of our patients have been hospitalized for suicidal ideation or attempted suicide. Fifteen percent of our patients were diagnosed with autism. There is minimal data on the relationship between autism and primary headaches. The biological mechanism underlying pain processing in patients with autism and neurodevelopmental disorders in general is poorly understood [34]. Several factors have been taken in consideration, including genetics, neuroinflammation and immune dysregulation, the high prevalence of anxiety in this population, and the impairment in the regulation of several neurotransmitters [34].

A possible explanation for our findings is the practitioners’ failure to recognize that headaches are often one element of a more complex clinical status. Only 15% of our patients had been diagnosed with AMPS or juvenile fibromyalgia before they came to our clinic. None of these patients had received treatments and/or medications that are today considered optimal to manage both conditions [35]. We diagnosed 30% of our patients with fibromyalgia or AMPS. While the recommendations for patients with AMPS included lifestyle changes, exercise, and other complementary interventions, we started Lyrica in patients with fibromyalgia. Trigeminal or occipital neuralgia should also be in the differential in evaluating patients with chronic headaches. We diagnosed three patients with occipital nerve neuralgia and did occipital nerve blocks that were successful in alleviating pain in two of them. Most of the patients improved after following our recommendations.

A subset of patients (22%) did not improve their headaches after our recommendations. There are several reasons why patients with chronic pain may not respond to multiple treatments. The presence of comorbidities such as sickle cell disease and congenital and neurodegenerative diseases can make pain management challenging. Anxiety and depression can lead to avoidance behaviors and functional neurological disorders [36]. Fear of pain and avoidance of activities can impact pain outcomes and make it harder to respond to treatments [37]. Patients who have lower levels of readiness to self-manage pain may also be less likely to respond to treatments [38].

Conclusions

This analysis confirms that dealing with headaches in children and adolescents can be challenging, especially when traditional treatments have been ineffective over months or years. Early referral to a multidisciplinary pain clinic could be helpful, as it allows for a comprehensive assessment of the pain’s physical and psychological aspects. Educating the children and their parents about chronic pain and refocusing on function rather than the pain itself should be critical parts of the assessment, especially in cases where previous treatments have not been effective. It’s essential to consider the social and psychological background of these patients, as headaches can often be indicators of more complex pain syndromes that may require a different, more holistic approach compared to traditional medication-based treatments.
