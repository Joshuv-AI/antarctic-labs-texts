Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of prohexadione calcium-mediated alleviation of salt stress in oilseed rape (Brassica napus L.) seedlings
Authors: Wenji Zhong, Peng Deng, Naijie Feng, Dianfeng Zheng, Youwei Du, Rui Deng, Zhiyuan Sun, Shaobiao Duan, Xixi Yang, Nana Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalt stress severely limits the growth of oilseed rape (Brassica napus L.). Prohexadione calcium (Pro-Ca) has been reported to alleviate salt stress in oilseed rape. However, the underlying mechanisms of Pro-Ca-mediated salt stress alleviation in oilseed rape remains unclear. In this study, experimental materials were the kale-type oilseed rape varieties moderately salt-tolerant ‘Huayouza 158R’ and highly salt-tolerant ‘Huayouza 62’. 0, 100, and 150 mM NaCl were applied before sowing, and samples were collected after 21 days of cultivation to evaluate Pro-Ca priming on morphological, physiological, and molecular responses.ResultsPro-Ca priming with 100 mM NaCl (Pro-Ca + S100) or 150 mM NaCl (Pro-Ca + S150) regulating morphogenesis, improving photosynthetic efficiency, and enhancing the antioxidant system compared with 100 mM NaCl (S100) and 150 mM NaCl (S150) alone. Transcriptomic analysis identified 381 and 832 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Huayouza 158R under Pro-Ca + S100-vs-S100 and Pro-Ca + S150-vs-S150, respectively, and 317 and 184 DEGs in Huayouza 62 under the same. Metabolomic analysis identified 312 and 487 differential metabolites (DIMs) in Huayouza 158R under Pro-Ca + S100-vs-S100 and Pro-Ca + S150-vs-S150, respectively, and 603 and 593 DIMs in Huayouza 62 under the same. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that Pro-Ca priming up-regulated PLC4-related genes in the Huayouza 158R phosphatidylinositol signaling system pathway (ko04070) in S100. Under S150 treatment, Pro-Ca priming up-regulated YUCCA8 and YUCCA9 genes in the Huayouza 158R tryptophan metabolic pathway (ko00380), and down-regulated the abundance of indole-3-acetaldehyde. In Huayouza 62, Pro-Ca priming down-regulated lipoxygenase-related genes in the linoleic acid metabolic pathway (ko00591). In this pathway, lecithin was up-regulated while 9-KODE and 9,10,13-TriHOME were down-regulated in S100. In contrast, in S150, gamma-linolenic acid was up-regulated, while 9-KODE and 9,10,13-TriHOME were down-regulated.ConclusionThe results showed that Pro-Ca priming could alleviate the effects of salt stress in both oilseed rape varieties by regulating morphogenesis, improving photosynthetic efficiency, and enhancing the antioxidant system. However, the specific molecular mechanisms differ between the two varieties. This study provides a molecular basis for the alleviation of salt stress-induced damage by Pro-Ca-mediated in oilseed rape.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08401-4.

Introduction

Salt stress is a widespread abiotic factor that negatively affects plant growth and development, ultimately reducing crop yield [1]. Globally, saline soils now cover 1 billion hectares [2], while in China, saline land accounts for 5.01% of the total land area [3]. Salt stress adversely hampers the growth of most land plants, preventing them from achieving optimal potential [4]. This results in a substantial amount of arable land not being utilized effectively, posing a significant threat to food security.

Oilseed rape (Brassica napus L.) is a major oilseed crop worldwide, providing edible oil, biodiesel, and other industrial products [5], with its seeds also used as animal feed [6]. Furthermore, oilseed rape is considered one of the most advantageous field crops for improving and utilizing saline soils [7]. Improving the salt tolerance of oilseed rape enables its cultivation to grow in saline soils, allowing the effective utilization of saline land. Meanwhile, studying the mechanisms underlying improved salt tolerance in oilseed rape can provide deeper insights into the physiological and biochemical responses and gene expression regulation under salt stress, offering a strong theoretical foundation for the genetic engineering and breeding of oilseed rape.

Prohexadione calcium (Pro-Ca) is a cyclohexanecarboxylic acid-based plant growth regulator with several advantages, including low toxicity and short persistence in plants and soils, and the ability to modulate flowering time, improve fruit set, control excessive vegetative growth, improve fruit yield and quality, and increase stress tolerance [8]. In terms of stress tolerance, Pro-Ca has been shown to regulate plants against various stresses, such as insect infestation [9] and salt stress [10]. Foliar application of Pro-Ca alleviates salt stress in rice by regulating morphogenesis, maintaining sodium and potassium ion balance, increasing chlorophyll content, improving photosynthetic efficiency, inhibiting endogenous cytokinin synthesis, and promoting root growth [11]. In soybean, Pro-Ca treatment increases chlorophyll content and photosynthetic capacity, retardation of the degradation of thylakoid, maintains PSII activity, promotes osmolyte accumulation, and activates antioxidant enzymes to alleviate salt stress [12]. Similarly, Pro-Ca primed before sowing has been found to enhance leaf photosynthetic capacity, stimulate seedling growth, increase antioxidant enzyme activity, and effectively mitigate ionic toxicity under salt stress after 36 days of cultivation [13]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which Pro-Ca priming regulates salt tolerance in oilseed rape seedlings remain unclear.

In this study, we investigated the specific mechanisms of salt resistance in moderately salt-tolerant (Huayouza 158R) and strongly salt-tolerant (Huayouza 62) oilseed rape seedlings under Pro-Ca priming using morphological, physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic approaches. The molecular mechanism of Pro-Ca-mediated salt stress alleviation in oilseed rape has been investigated and the potential practical applications are discussed. We hypothesised that Pro-Ca pretreatment would reprogram transcriptional and metabolic networks differently in the two varieties, and the strongly-tolerant line would rely on linoleic acid metabolic pathway whereas the moderately-tolerant line would depend on phosphatidylinositol signaling system pathway and tryptophan metabolic pathway to maintain growth under salt stress.

Results

Effect of Pro-Ca priming on phenotypic traits of oilseed rape seedlings under NaCl stress

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced plant height in both oilseed rape varieties compared with S0. In Huayouza 158R, Pro-Ca + S0 had no significant effect, whereas in Huayouza 62 it significantly increased plant height. In both varieties, plant height was significantly increased following Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments relative to S100 and S150, respectively (Fig. 1A).

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced stem thickness in both varieties compared with S0. Stem thickness was significantly increased in both varieties under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment relative to S0. Compared with S100 and S150, stem thickness was significantly increased by Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments in Huayouza 62, whereas in Huayouza 158R, it was significantly increased by Pro-Ca + S100, but not significantly increased by Pro-Ca + S150 (Fig. 1B).

Similarly, S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced leaf area of both varieties compared with S0. Leaf area was significantly higher in the Pro-Ca + S0 group than in S0 in both varieties. Compared with S100 and S150, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly increased leaf area in Huayouza 62 but not in Huayouza 158R (Fig. 1C).

The S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced the root length in Huayouza 158R compared with the S0 treatment, while Huayouza 62 showed no significant reduction at S100 and a significant reduction at S150. Root length was significantly increased by 213.27% and 105.92% in both varieties in the Pro-Ca + S0 treatment as compared with S0. Both varieties exhibited significantly increased root length under Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments relative to S100 and S150. Specifically, root length in Huayouza 158R increased by 235.08% and 93.00%, and in Huayouza 62 by 100.84% and 243.44% under Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150, respectively (Fig. 1D).

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced the root surface area in Huayouza 158R compared with S0, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant reduction in S100 and a significant reduction in S150. Root surface area of both varieties was significantly increased by 332.58% and 253.23% under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment as compared with S0. Both varieties exhibited significant increases in Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 compared with S100 and S150. Specifically, root surface area increased by 175.32% and 130.05% in Huayouza 158R, and by 131.03% and 235.89% in Huayouza 62 in Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150, respectively (Fig. 1E).

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced root volume in Huayouza 158R compared with S0, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant reduction in S100 and a significant reduction in S150. Root volume was significantly increased in both varieties under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment compared with S0, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments significantly increased root volume in both varieties compared with S100 and S150 treatments. Specifically, root volume increased by 134.04% and 200.00% in Huayouza 158R and by 145.83% and 268.42% in Huayouza 62 under Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments, respectively (Fig. 1F).

The number of root tips in Huayouza 158R significantly decreased under S100 and S150, while Huayouza 62 showed no significant change compared with S0. Pro-Ca + S0 significantly increased root tip number in both varieties relative to S0. Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly increased the number of root tips in both varieties compared with S100 and S150. Specifically, root tip number increased by 276.28% and 102.39% in Huayouza 158R, and 122.81% and 258.39% in Huayouza 62 under Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments, respectively (Fig. 1G).

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced the dry weight in both varieties compared with S0 treatment. The dry weight of both varieties was significantly increased in Pro-Ca + S0 as compared with S0. Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments significantly increased the dry weight of Huayouza 158R compared with S100 and S150, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant increase in S100 and a significant increase in S150 (Fig. 1H).

S100 and S150 treatments significantly reduced the seedling strength index of both oilseed rape varieties compared with S0. Also, Pro-Ca + S0 significantly increased seedling strength index in both varieties as compared with S0 treatment. Under Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150, Huayouza 158R showed a significant increase in S100 and a nonsignificant increase in S150, whereas Huayouza 62 exhibited a significant increase under both treatments (Fig. 1I).

Effect of Pro-Ca priming on the antioxidant system of oilseed rape seedlings under NaCl stress

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in Huayouza 158R was significantly higher under S100 and S150 treatments compared with S0, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant increase in S100 and a significant increase in S150. In Pro-Ca + S0, SOD activity was significantly increased in Huayouza 158R but significantly decreased in Huayouza 62 as compared with S0. Pro-Ca + S100 significantly enhanced SOD activity in both varieties compared with S100 and S150, whereas Pro-Ca + S150 did not induce a significant change in either variety (Fig. 2A).

Catalase (CAT) activity in Huayouza 62 was significantly higher in S100 and S150 compared with S0, whereas in Huayouza 158R, CAT activity was significantly higher in S100 but was not significantly different in S150. CAT activity was significantly enhanced in both varieties under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment compared with S0. Compared with S100 and S150, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly increased CAT activity in both varieties (Fig. 2B).

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity in Huayouza 158R was significantly reduced under treatments S100 and S150 compared with S0, while Huayouza 62 exhibited a nonsignificant increase in S100 and a significant increase in S150. Pro-Ca + S0 induced only minimal increases in APX activity in both varieties relative to S0. Compared with S100 and S150, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly enhanced APX activity in both varieties (Fig. 2C).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content in both varieties showed a nonsignificant increase in S100 and a significant increase in S150 compared with S0. Under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment, MDA content was not significantly reduced in Huayouza 158R, but was significantly decreased in Huayouza 62 relative to S0. Compared with S100 and S150, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly reduced the MDA content in Huayouza 62, whereas in Huayouza 158R, MDA content showed a nonsignificant increase in Pro-Ca + S100 and a significant reduction in Pro-Ca + S150 (Fig. 2D).

GSH levels were significantly increased under Pro-Ca + S0 treatment in Huayouza 62 and significantly decreased in Huayouza 158R compared with S0. Compared with S100 and S150, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly elevated GSH levels in both varieties (Fig. 2E).

Ascorbic acid (AsA) activity in Huayouza 158R was significantly reduced by S100 and S150 treatments as compared with S0, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant reduction in S100 and significant decrease in S150. AsA activity was significantly increased in both varieties in the Pro-Ca + S0 treatment compared with S0. Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 treatments significantly increased AsA activity in Huayouza 158R compared with S100 and S150, with Huayouza 62 showing a significant elevation in Pro-Ca + S100 and a nonsignificant elevation in Pro-Ca + S150 (Fig. 2F).

Net photosynthetic rate (Pn) was significantly reduced in Huayouza 158R in S100 and S150 groups compared with S0, whereas Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant reduction in S100 and a significant reduction in S150. Pro-Ca + S0 treatment significantly increased Pn in both varieties compared with S0. Furthermore, Pro-Ca + S100 and Pro-Ca + S150 significantly increased Pn in both varieties relative to S100 and S150 treatments (Fig. 3A).

The transpiration rate (Tr) of Huayouza 158R was significantly reduced in S100 and S150 compared with S0, while Huayouza 62 showed a nonsignificant reduction in S100 and a significant reduction in S150. Transcriptomic analysis of oilseed rape seedlings in response to salt stress and Pro-Ca priming

A total of 36 RNA-sequencing libraries from different samples were analyzed. The effective data volume for each sample ranged from 6.2 to 7.21 G, with Q30 scores between 92.28% and 93.27%. Reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome, yielding mapping rates between 94.38% and 94.75% (Supplementary Table S1 and S2).

Inter-sample correlations were calculated and visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 4A), showing strong correlations between biological replicates and confirming data reliability. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that PC1 accounted for 84.87% of total variance (84.87%), while PC2 explained 3.87% (Fig. 4B). PCA clearly distinguished samples by both treatment and variety, highlighting significant transcriptomic differences.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from transcriptome data with q < 0.05 and |log₂FC|> 1.0. Compared with the control, 954 (315 up-regulated and 639 down-regulated) in S100 and 2,467 (786 up-regulated and 1681 down-regulated) DEGs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 158R, 1394 (306 up-regulated and 1088 down-regulated) in S100 and 2046 (577 up-regulated and 1469 down-regulated) DEGs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 62. 381 (269 up-regulated and 112 down-regulated) in S100 and 832 (379 up-regulated and 453 down-regulated) DEGs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 158R under NaCl + Pro-Ca treatment compared with NaCl treatment, respectively. Cluster analysis revealed that Pro-Ca significantly altered the expression patterns of DEGs in oilseed rape seedlings under salt stress (Fig. 4C, D, F, G). Venn diagram analysis revealed 33 shared DEGs in Huayouza 158R and 35 shared DEGs in Huayouza 62 following Pro-Ca priming under different levels of salt stress (Fig. 4E, H).

Next, DEGs identified between Pro-Ca + S100 and S100, as well between Pro-Ca + S150 and S150 were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The results indicated that DEGs in Huayouza 158R were primarily associated with pathways such as carbohydrate metabolism, signal transduction, and lipid metabolism (Fig. 5A). In contrast, DEGs in Huayouza 62 were mainly linked to translation, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and signal transduction (Fig. 5D).

To investigate the functional impact of Pro-Ca priming on metabolic pathways under salt stress, DEGs were mapped to specific biosynthetic and metabolic pathways. In Huayouza 158R, three DEGs (LOC106354659, LOC125579243, and LOC125593068) in the sesquiterpene and triterpene biosynthesis pathway were significantly down-regulated by salt stress and regulated by Pro-Ca under S100 treatment (Fig. 6A). In contrast, nine DEGs (LOC106359824, LOC106361617, LOC106370091, LOC106372559, LOC106375577, LOC106412462, LOC106413796, LOC106418172, and LOC106432027) were identified in Huayouza 158R in the tryptophan metabolism pathway following Pro-Ca priming compared with S150. Five DEGs (LOC106359824, LOC106370091, LOC106372559, LOC106375577, and LOC106413796), were significantly up-regulated under salt stress and Pro-Ca regulation, which were associated with indole-3-pyruvate monooxygenase (Fig. 6B).

Metabolomic analysis of oilseed rape seedlings in response to salt stress and Pro-Ca treatment

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was used to profile metabolites in oilseed rape seedlings. Differential metabolites (DIMs) were identified using p < 0.05 and VIP > 1.0. Compared with the control, 429 (178 up-regulated and 251 down-regulated) in S100 and 482 (257 up-regulated and 225 down-regulated) DIMs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 158R, while 539 (220 up-regulated and 319 down-regulated) in S100 and 539 (239 up-regulated and 300 down-regulated) in S150, respectively, in Huayouza 62 when treated with NaCl. Compared with NaCl treatment, 312 (171 up-regulated and 141 down-regulated) in S100 and 487 (169 up-regulated and 318 down-regulated) DIMs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 158R and 603 (353 up-regulated and 250 down-regulated) in S100 and 593 (407 up-regulated and 186 down-regulated) DIMs in S150 were detected in Huayouza 62, respectively (Supplementary Table S4).

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that PC1 accounted for 45.5% of total variance (45.5%), while PC2 explained 15.1% (Fig. 7A). Cluster analysis revealed that Pro-Ca significantly altered the expression patterns of DEGs in oilseed rape seedlings under salt stress (Fig. 7B, C, D, E).

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis

To conduct inter-sample correlation analyses using transcriptomic and metabolomic data from two rapeseed varieties subjected to two salt stress conditions, we aimed to evaluate the effects of Pro-Ca priming and varietal differences on molecular responses. Inter-sample correlation analysis revealed that Pro-Ca significantly altered the profiles of DEGs and DIMs in both varieties across both salt stress environments (Fig. 9). Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that different rapeseed varieties exhibited distinct patterns of DEGs and DIMs under identical salt stress treatments (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Pro-Ca priming reduces the impact of salt stress on oilseed rape by regulating its morphological traits

Previous study have shown that Pro-Ca alleviates salt stress in oilseed rape [13], yet its underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. This study examines the morphological, physiological, and molecular responses of oilseed rape seedlings to salt stress, and assessed the mitigating effect of Pro-Ca.

Salt stress greatly hampers the growth of oilseed rape, leading to reductions in root length, plant height, and plant biomass [14]. In our research, salt stress significantly reduced the height, stem diameter, leaf area, dry weight, root growth, and seedling vigor index in Huayouza 158R and Huayouza 62, aligning with previous findings [15]. Pro-Ca priming significantly improved plant growth under different stress levels. Specifically, under S100 treatment, it significantly increased plant height, stem thickness, dry weight, root length, root surface area, root volume, root tip number, and the strong seedling index in both varieties. Additionally, it notably increased the leaf area of Huayouza 62. Under S150 treatment, Pro-Ca enhanced plant growth parameters in both varieties, while further increasing stem diameter, leaf area, dry weight, and the seedling vigor index in Huayouza 62 (Fig. 1). Overall, Pro-Ca treatment effectively alleviated salt-induced growth inhibition in both varieties. Furthermore, Huayouza 158R was more sensitive to salt stress, whereas Huayouza 62 exhibited a stronger growth-promoting response to Pro-Ca treatment.

Pro-Ca priming enhances the antioxidant defense system of oilseed rape under salt stress

Salt stress triggers the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anions [16]. To counteract oxidative stress, plants enhance their antioxidant defense system, with the activity and levels of antioxidant enzymes increasing with the severity of the stress [17, 18]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that following Pro-Ca priming, the levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants ASA and GSH, as well as the activities of antioxidant enzymes SOD, CAT, and APX, significantly increased in both oilseed rape varieties (Fig. 2A, B, C, E, F). This increase helps mitigate ROS-induced damage and improve salt tolerance [19]. Such induction of the defense system has been shown in many plants such as rice [20], kiwifruit [21], lettuce [22] and wheat [23]. Meanwhile, salt stress led to an increase in MDA content, indicating elevated membrane lipid peroxidation. However, Pro-Ca priming significantly reduced MDA accumulation in both varieties (Fig. 2D), suggesting alleviated membrane damage. Together, these results demonstrate that Pro-Ca priming strengthens the antioxidant system of oilseed rape, thereby reducing ROS-induced injury and contributing to improved salt tolerance.

Pro-Ca priming alleviates the inhibitory effects of salt stress on photosynthesis in oilseed rape

Photosynthesis is essential for biomass accumulation, providing the substrates and energy required for plant growth and development [24]. Under salt stress, all four photosynthetic parameters in Huayouza 158R were reduced, while in Huayouza 62, three parameters decreased except for Ci, in S100 (Fig. 3). The reduction in photosynthetic efficiency represents a major factor contributing to growth inhibition under salt stress [21]. In this study, Pro-Ca priming markedly enhanced Tr, Pn, and Gs across both rapeseed varieties (Fig. 3). Therefore, Pro-Ca may enhance the growth of the two varieties by improving photosynthetic efficiency. Both stomatal and non-stomatal factors are known to influence photosynthetic efficiency [25]. The reduction in Ci and Gs is primarily due to stomatal factors, whereas an increase in Ci accompanied by reduced Gs is considered to result from non-stomatal factors [26]. In Huayouza 158R, similar trends were observed at both salt concentrations, suggesting that stomatal factors were the primary contributors to decreased Pn. Under S150 treatment, Huayouza 62 showed a similar trend to Huayouza 158R. The main reason for the decline in the Pn of Huayouza 62 in S100 appears to be non-stomatal factors. This suggests that salt stress decreases CO2 utilization efficiency in oilseed rape, resulting in lower Pn and reduced overall photosynthetic capacity. After Pro-Ca priming under salt stress, all four photosynthetic indicators increased in Huayouza 158R, while in Huayouza 62, three indicators increased except for Ci in S100. This pattern implies that the Pro-Ca enhanced the photosynthetic capacity of Huayouza 62 in S100 primarily by maintaining stomatal conductance, thereby improving CO₂ utilization. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Pro-Ca priming alleviates the adverse effects of salt stress on photosynthesis in oilseed rape seedlings, thereby improving plant salt tolerance, although the underlying regulatory mechanisms differ between the two varieties.

Pro-Ca priming mitigates salt stress by regulating the phosphoinositide signaling pathway in oilseed rape

In Huayouza 158R, both DEGs and DIMs (Pro-Ca + S100 vs. S100) were enriched in the phosphatidylinositol signaling pathway (ko04070). Pro-Ca priming significantly up-regulated genes related to phosphoinositide phospholipase C, delta (PLC4, EC:3.1.4.11), a member of the phospholipase C (PLC) family. In plants, PLCs are classified into two groups based on their substrate specificity: phosphoinositide-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC) and non-specific phospholipase C (NPC) [27]. PLC4 belongs to the phosphoinositide-specific phospholipase C (PI-PLC) family and exhibits activity towards phosphatidylinositol under high calcium conditions in vitro. Studies in Arabidopsis have shown that PI-PLC plays an important role in growth, development, and stress responses [28, 29], and overexpression of PLC4 enhances drought tolerance [30]. In our research, salt stress significantly reduced the height, stem diameter, leaf area, dry weight, root growth, and seedling vigor index in Huayouza 158R, pro-Ca priming significantly improved plant growth under different stress levels (Fig. 1). In rice, PLC4 contributes to salt tolerance by regulating phosphatidic acid (PA) and Ca2+ signaling pathways [31], suggesting a similar role in oilseed rape. These results indicate that Pro-Ca may alleviate salt stress damage in Huayouza 158R by up-regulating the expression of PLC4-related genes and regulating PA and Ca2+ signaling pathways.

Pro-Ca priming promotes root growth by modulating tryptophan metabolism in oilseed rape under salt stress

Huayouza 158R showed co-enrichment of DEGs and DIMs (Pro-Ca + S150 vs. S150) in the tryptophan metabolism pathway (ko00380). After Pro-Ca priming, genes encoding indole-3-pyruvate monooxygenase, including YUCCA8 (LOC106359824; LOC106372559; LOC106375577; LOC106413796) and YUCCA9 (LOC106370091), were significantly up-regulated. Auxin plays a crucial role in plant growth and development [32], and indole-3-pyruvate monooxygenase (EC: 1.14.13.168) catalyzes the conversion of indole-3-pyruvate (IPA) to indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a central step in auxin biosynthesis [33]. YUCCA8 and YUCCA9 mainly regulate auxin production in the root system [34], which is consistent with the morphological changes observed in this study (Fig. 1D, E, F, G). Therefore, Pro-Ca priming may promote root growth by up-regulating the expression of YUCCA8 and YUCCA9 in Huayouza 158R, thereby enhancing salt tolerance of oilseed rape seedlings.

Pro-Ca priming alleviates salt stress by regulating the linoleic acid metabolic pathway in oilseed rape

In Huayouza 62, DEGs and DIMs were co-enriched in the linoleic acid metabolism pathway (ko00591). Pro-Ca priming significantly altered the content of oxylipin 9-KODE by down-regulating genes related to lipoxygenase (LOX) (EC 1.13.11.12). LOXs are iron dioxygenase widely present in eukaryotes, whose main function is to promote the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids [35]. It activates the fatty acid oxidation pathway, producing oxylipins and jasmonic acid (JA), both of which play key roles in plant development and defense [36]. Pro-Ca priming enhanced the photosynthetic capacity in Huayouza 62 through stomatal regulation, improving CO₂ utilization efficiency in S100, while via non-stomatal factors in S150. Increased CO₂ fixation may inhibit photorespiration [37] and possibly influence fatty acid metabolism. Fatty acids are one of the substrates for 9-KODE synthesis, which likely explains why 9-KODE was down-regulated in Pro-Ca + S100 vs. S100, but up-regulated in Pro-Ca + S150 vs. S150 in Huayouza 62. LOX contributes to JA biosynthesis [38], and JA accumulation has been shown to inhibit plant growth, resulting in reduced plant height and dry weight in rice [39]. The down-regulation of LOX may thereby promote the growth of oilseed rape, which is consistent with the morphological changes observed in this study (Fig. 1). These results suggest that Pro-Ca priming alleviates salt stress in Huayouza 62 by regulating lipoxygenase-encoding genes, thereby reducing JA production and promoting oilseed rape growth.

DEGs and DIMs co-enrichment in the Huayouza 158R phosphatidylinositol signaling system pathway (ko04070) in S100. Under S150, DEGs and DIMs co-enrichment in the Huayouza 158R tryptophan metabolic pathway (ko00380). In Huayouza 62, DEGs and DIMs co-enrichment in the linoleic acid metabolism pathway (ko00591). However, under S100 or S150, the specific DEGs and DIMs regulated are different. This might be the reason for the difference in salt tolerance between the two varieties.

While this study provides a comprehensive multi-omics analysis of Pro-Ca priming, several limitations should be acknowledged. Gene function predictions were based on orthologs in model species (Arabidopsis and rice) and require validation in rapeseed via VIGS or CRISPR-Cas9. Additionally, greenhouse-based findings need confirmation through field trials under fluctuating environmental conditions. Future studies addressing these gaps will advance molecular marker development for breeding and optimize Pro-Ca application in rapeseed cultivation.

Conclusions

This study employed morpho-physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic approaches to investigate the mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in moderately salt-tolerant Huayouza 158R and highly salt-tolerant Huayouza 62 under Pro-Ca priming. The results showed that Pro-Ca priming could alleviate the effects of salt stress in both oilseed rape varieties by regulating morphogenesis, improving photosynthetic efficiency, and enhancing the antioxidant system. However, the specific molecular mechanisms differ between the two varieties. In S100, Pro-Ca priming likely mitigates salt stress-induced damage in Huayouza 158R by up-regulating PLC4-related genes in the phosphatidylinositol signaling pathway, thereby regulating PA and Ca2+ signaling. In S150, Pro-Ca priming appears to enhance Huayouza 158R root growth and salt tolerance by up-regulating the expression of YUCCA8 and YUCCA9 in the tryptophan metabolic pathway. In Huayouza 62, Pro-Ca priming may alleviate salt stress by down-regulating lipoxygenase-related genes in the linoleic acid metabolism pathway, thereby reducing JA production and promoting seedling growth. These findings suggest that while Pro-Ca priming share common mechanisms for alleviating salt stress in different salt-tolerant oilseed rape varieties, differences in intrinsic salt tolerance and the severity of salt stress contribute to variety-specific regulatory responses.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Two oilseed rape varieties with different salt tolerance were used in this study: the medium salt-tolerant Huayouza 158R and the highly salt-tolerant Huayouza 62 [40]. Prohexadione calcium (Pro-Ca) was used as the plant growth regulator, and the stock solution (5% Pro-Ca) was provided by Sichuan Runer Technology Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China).

The screening test for the optimal concentration of Pro-Ca was conducted from March to June 2022 by members of our research team, and 8 mg/L was determined as the optimal Pro-Ca concentration [13].

Experimental design

The study was conducted from 2022–2023 in a greenhouse under natural light conditions (day/night temperature 25/20 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 60%) at Binhai College of Agriculture, Guangdong Ocean University (21°8′56 ″N, 110°17′58 ″E, altitude 20 m). Seeds of uniform size were selected and sterilized by soaking in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 10 min, followed by three to five rinses with distilled water. Sterilized seeds were evenly placed in Petri dishes (9 cm in diameter) lined with three layers of filter paper, which was moistened with 10 mL of Pro-Ca at 0 mg-L−1 or 8 mg·L⁻1, and sealed with parafilm. The petri dishes were incubated in the dark at 20 ℃ for 8 h. After treatment, seeds were rinsed with distilled water, drained on absorbent paper, and air-dried for 48 h (20–25 °C, relative humidity 40%−45%). Dried seeds were weighed to confirm that they had returned to their initial water content before proceeding to germination tests.

Soil cultivation was performed using 19 cm × 14 cm × 17 cm flower pots containing 2.5 kg of soil (vermicompost to sand, 3:1, v/v). Before sowing, one liter of NaCl solution at different concentrations (0, 100, and 150 mM) was thoroughly mixed with the soil to simulate salt stress environments. Twelve seeds from each treatment were sown evenly per pot, with four pots per treatment, and three replicates. Samples were collected and morphological and physiological parameters measured on the 21 st day after sowing. Measurement items and methods

Determination of morphological indicators

Plant height and root length were measured using a ruler; while stem thickness was determined with an electronic vernier caliper (the base of the stem was 1 cm away from the root system). Leaf area was recorded with a leaf area meter. The root system and above-ground part of seedlings were separated, surface water was removed with blotting paper, and samples were placed in a kraft paper bag and oven-dried at 80 ℃ for 48 h to determine dry weight. The entire root system was scanned using a root scanner (Epson Perfection V800 Photo (Epson Indonesia Inc., Suwa, Nagano, Japan)), and the results were analyzed using root analysis software (WinRHIZO) to determine root length, root surface area, root volume, and average root diameter.

Seedling strength index = (root dry mass/above-ground dry mass + stem thickness/plant height) * (root dry mass + above-ground dry mass) [41].

Measurement of photosynthetic properties

On a sunny afternoon between 14:00 and 16:00, the Pn, Gs, Ci, and Tr of 21-day oilseed rape seedlings were measured using a portable photosynthesis measurement system LI-6800 (LI-COR, Inc., USA) [42]. Each treatment was performed in triplicate.

Determination of membrane lipid peroxidation products

Representative plants from each treatment were selected, washed, and dried with absorbent paper. Roots and leaves were separated, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80℃ until analysis. Determination of MDA content was determined following the method of Stewart and Bewley [43]. Briefly, 0.5 g of root or leaf tissue was ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, mixed with10 mL phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), and extracted with 0.6% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Samples were incubated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 15 min, rapidly cooled on ice, and centrifuged at 7000 × g for 20 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm. MDA content (mmol ∙ g−1 FW) was calculated based on fresh weight as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{MDA}=6.45\times \left(\mathrm{OD}532-\mathrm{OD}600\right)-0.56\times \left(\mathrm{OD}450\right)$$\end{document}

Determination of antioxidant systems

Fresh leaves (0.5 g) were thoroughly washed and placed in a pre-chilled mortar. Pre-chilled 0.05 M phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.8, total 10 mL) was added in three separate portions, and leaf tissue was ground into a homogeneous paste on an ice bath. The homogenate was transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The activity of SOD, CAT, APX, AsA, and GSH were measured in the supernatant of the mixture. SOD activity was calculated by measuring its ability to inhibit the photoreduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) according to Giannopolitis and Ries [44]. According to the methods described by Gupta et al. [45] and Li et al. [46], CAT activity was determined by measuring the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm per minute caused by H₂O₂ consumption. APX activity was determined following Nakano and Asada [47]. AsA content was quantified using the 4,7-diphenyl-1, 10-phenanthroline (BP) colorimetric method [48]. GSH content was determined using the 5,5-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) colorimetric method [49].

Total RNA isolation and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Libraries were sequenced on an Ilumina Novaseq 6000 platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Raw reads of fastq format were firstly processed using fastp [50] and the low quality reads were removed to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 [51]. Gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) [52], and read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count [53]. PCA was performed in R (v 3.;2.0).

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [54]. Differential expression gene (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of q < 0.05 and |log₂FC|> 1. Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed in R (v 3.2.0) to visualize the expression patterns across groups and samples. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) [55] and KEGG pathway databases [56] in R (v 3.2.0).

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed by Shanghai Luming biological technology co., LTD (Shanghai, China). Metabolites were analyzed using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus system (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) coupled with Q-Exactive mass spectrometer, equipped with heated electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Analyses were conducted in both positive and negative ESI modes. Separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) with a binary gradient elution system. Mobile phases consisted of (A) water (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v). The elution gradient was as follows: 0.01 min, 5% B; 2 min, 5% B; 4 min, 30% B; 8 min, 50% B; 10 min, 80% B; 14 min, 100% B; 15 min, 100% B; 15.1 min, 5% and 16 min, 5%B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min and column temperature was maintained 45℃. All samples were kept at 10℃ during analysis.

The mass range was m/z 100- 1,000. The resolution was set at 70,000 for full MS scans and 17,500 for HCD MS/MS scans. Collision energy was set at 10, 20 and 40 eV. The mass spectrometer was operated under the following conditions: spray voltage, 3800 V (+) and 3200 V (−); sheath gas flow rate, 35 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow rate, 8 arbitrary units; capillary temperature, 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C; S-lens RF level, 50.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 25.0. Differences between treatments were evaluated using Dunken's multiple range test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using Origin 2021.
