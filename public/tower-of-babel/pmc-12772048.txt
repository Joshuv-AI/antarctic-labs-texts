Role of fallopian tube and cervical canal occlusion in preventing intra-operative tumour spillage in early endometrial cancer- a pilot study
Authors: Aparajita, Debabrata Barmon, Upasana Baruah, Dimpy Begum, Shiraj Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have found the presence of post- hysterectomy atypical glandular cells in upper vagina in ~ 30% patients of Endometrial Cancer (EC) with implications of causing isolated vaginal recurrence. The primary objective was to determine the effectiveness of cervical canal and fallopian tube occlusion in preventing intra-operative tumour spillage in EC.MethodsA Prospective Interventional Single Institutional pilot study with random allocation to two arms using computer- generated random number sequence was conducted between January 2022 to Feb 2023. Patients with histologically confirmed epithelial endometrial cancer, including endometrioid carcinoma, papillary serous, clear-cell, squamous, mucinous, undifferentiated, and carcino-sarcoma with surgery as primary definitive treatment modality were included. Spillage was detected using vaginal smear and peritoneal wash cytology taken pre- and post- hysterectomy. In interventional arm, cervical os and fallopian tube were occluded using silk sutures as additional steps during surgery. Rate of negative to positive conversion of vaginal smear and peritoneal wash cytology obtained before and after hysterectomy in the interventional arm was compared with control arm who underwent surgery with standard surgical steps. Results: Out of 33 eligibile patients, 3 were excluded due to double primaries and history of radiation pelvis. The two arms of 15 patients each were comparable with respect to clinico-pathological characteristics. The most common histology, grade, degree of myometrial invasion, LVSI, stage, and mean tumour diameter was endometrioid variety (100%), low-grade (83.3%), & >1/2 (50%), negative (83.3%), 1 A (41.7%), and 4 cm in interventional arm and endometrioid (100%), low grade (76.9%), >1/2 (84.6%), negative (92.3%), 1B (84.6%), and 4.5 cm in non- interventional arm respectively. None of the patient in either arm had negative to positive conversion of vaginal and/or peritoneal cytology. Conclusion: We observed no difference of cervical and tubal occlusion on tumour cell spillage in EC.Trial registrationCTRI/2022/05/042348.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04121-5.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, Intra-operative tumour spillage, Vaginal recurrence, Intra-operative surgical manoeuvres, Vaginal cytology, Peritoneal cytology

Background

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is the most common gynaecological malignancy in developed countries and is on rising trend in developing countries. As per GLOBOCAN 2020, uterine corpus cancer is the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer in women, with 417,367 new cases and 97,370 deaths in 2020 [1]. With advancements and better understanding of biology of EC and incorporation of molecular characteristics, there is a paradigm shift in management of EC patients.

Various modes of tumour spillage in EC is described by following 3 routes: Uterine manipulation in the form of pressure on fundus may cause dislodgement of tumour fragments in upper vaginal cavity. Trendelenburg position further facilitates spread of migrated tumour cells in vagina to peritoneal cavity. These tumour cells from vagina may also gain access to the peritoneal cavity at the time of intra-corporeal colpotomy. The second chance of tumour dissemination may occur due to uterine perforation with the use of uterine manipulator. Third, trans-tubal retrograde tumour spillage into the pelvic cavity may occur [2]. There still is no robust prospective data to confirm that intra-operative tumour spillage is an independent factor for recurrence in EC.

Recurrence affects up to 6–13% of all patients with EC with vagina being the commonest site of recurrence in the surgically treated low-risk patients [3–5]. Vaginal recurrences following surgical treatment has been explained owing to the following factors: lymphatic spread following surgical transection, hematogenous spread to sub-urethral area and surgical implantation. It has been postulated that the higher percentage of isolated vaginal recurrence may be due to surgical implantation rather than by hematogenous or lymphatic route. However, such low- risk patients who did not receive adjuvant therapy and develop vaginal recurrence often have good cure rate to subsequent radiotherapy [5]. These observations point towards less aggressive biology of isolated vaginal recurrence. Some authors even suggested that vaginal recurrence is not a true recurrence but instead a colonization of tumour cells intra-operatively leading to less aggressive recurrence [5]. In this context, it has been shown that surgical trauma hampers the immune-protective role of interleukin-2 and lymphokine-activated killer cells [6, 7]. Tumour spillage during surgery can also increase the risk of distant metastasis [8]. Although, less focus on the intra-operative tumour spillage has been put on EC surgeries, implication of tumour spillage in other gynaecological malignancies have been discussed recently. The impact of intra-operative tumour spillage during minimally invasive surgery (MIS) for early cervical cancer was explored by LACC trial [9]. In ovarian cancer, intra-operative tumour spillage upstages the disease and has prognostic and therapeutic implications.

Lacunae in the literature

There is very minimal data on conversion rate of peritoneal cytology in EC due to uterine manipulation in open surgery. The effect of cervical canal and fallopian tube occlusion on intra-operative tumour spillage is largely unknown in EC. Hence, we planned this study to establish whether occluding cervical canal and fallopian tube can merely be a ritualistic practice or a scientific approach?

Sample size calculation

This study was designed with two independent samples and has dichotomous end-point. Assuming the incidence of outcome measure in arm 1 as 46% and in arm 2 as 8%, alpha-error of 0.05 and beta-error of 0.2, and power of 0.8, the sample size came out to be 20 patients in each arm.

Sampling method

Computer generated sequence of random numbers.

Discussion

Tumour cell dissemination can occur at various steps during the timeline of evaluation and treatment of EC. There is a possibility of spillage of cells through the cervix during and after the diagnostic dilation and curettage, diagnostic hysteroscopy and, during the surgery itself by manipulating the uterus by pressing the fundus in open surgery or by use of uterine manipulator in MIS [2, 12–14]. The glandular tumour cells of EC may detach and drop into the vagina while the tumour is bleeding in cases of large polypoid tumours, or in those involving the cervix. The possibility of vaginal cuff recurrence resulting from operative tumour dissemination has been noted since the early 1900s. Isolated vaginal recurrence demands vaginal brachytherapy as treatment which has several acute and late complications including vaginal toxicity in the form of inflammation, laceration, deep vein thrombosis, fibrosis, short or narrow vagina, dyspareunia and bladder perforation, and bowel obstruction [15]. Hence, protective measures holds promise in EC surgeries so as to avoid vaginal brachytherapy in case of vaginal recurrence.

Fallopian tube and cervical occlusion have been proposed as potential measures to prevent intra-operative tumour spillage during hysterectomy for EC [16–19]. Historically, many practitioners routinely employed cervical occlusion techniques to decrease the risk of direct implantation of tumour cells via the cervical canal with good results [2, 6, 16, 17, 20–22]. A cross- sectional survey conducted among practicing U.S. gynaecologic oncologist revealed that ~ 60% had experienced tumour spill while making colpotomy during surgery for EC and ~ 10–15% had to modify the adjuvant treatment based on intraoperative uterine perforation or tumour spill. They seldom ablated or ligated fallopian tubes before performing the hysterectomy [21]. The effect of any surgical intervention on intra-operative tumour spillage has not been studied so far.

We designed our study to assess the rate of intra-operative tumour spillage in EC by determining the rate of vaginal and peritoneal cytology conversion (from negative pre- TH cytology to positive post-TH cytology results) after hysterectomy. We further incorporated an interventional arm with cervical os and tubal occlusion to determine any difference in the spillage rate when compared to non-interventional standard arm. Apart from studying the vaginal cytology conversion rate, we also analysed any change in peritoneal cytology as trans-tubal passage of tumour cells is a proven route of dissemination due to excessive manipulation of uterus [2, 23]. In spite of careful intra-operative precautions, complete prevention of carcinoma cell lodging to the vaginal stump and its surroundings or the peritoneal cavity cannot not be promised as long as carcinoma cells are already present at those sites due to pre-operative shedding of tumour cells. Therefore, the patients with positive pre-hysterectomy peritoneal and vaginal cytology were excluded to eliminate the confounding effect of pre-operative vaginal contamination with malignant cells due to natural course of the disease itself.

The two cohorts were comparable with respect to clinic-demographic and pathological features and duration of surgery. The median age of our study subject was slightly lower than the average of 60–70 years in western literature but comparable to median age of 54 years in Indian data [24]. The rate of detection of pre-operative vaginal contamination with dropped off malignant endometrial glandular cells was found to be 6.67% in our study which is significantly lower than cited in the literature. Several retrospective studies have reported the rate of PAPs positivity of 31.9% to 89.6% in EC. In one review study, among 51.1% of abnormal, only 27% were malignant cells [15]. Abnormal cytology is dependent on various pathological factors of the lesion including histological sub-type, tumour size, cervical infiltration, myometrial invasion, lympho-vascular invasion and advanced stage [25]. Higher positivity is noted in cases of aggressive variety like serous, clear cell, and mixed malignant Müllerian tumor [26]. The lower rate of malignant cell detection in our study may also be due to predominance of favourable endometrioid histology in our cohorts, and utilisation of conventional PAPs smear instead of liquid based cytology. Liquid-based Pap tests are ideal for assessing glandular lesions due to their thin layer of cells in a clean background [15].

The vaginal and peritoneal cytology conversion rate from negative to positive in both the interventional and non-interventional arm was null in our study. This was an unexpected result. The overall low detection of malignant cells in vaginal smears may be due to favourable pathological characteristics of our cohorts. Recently, Stolnicu et al. did a prospective study to determine the role of vaginal contamination due to intra- operative tumour spillage and showed that in 50% of cases, vagina is contaminated pre-operatively. He noted 30% positive conversion rate among 10 analysed patients of EC [17]. The high rate of vaginal smear positivity as opposed to our cohorts might be explained by presence of large tumour size (median 6 cm), polypoid type (80%), infiltrating the cervix (70%), and infiltrating more than half of the myometrium (60%) [17]. Our interventional arm and non-interventional arm had median tumour dimension of 4 cm and 4.5 cm respectively. Though the lesions were mostly proliferative in nature, there was absence of cervical infiltration in all the analysed subjects.

No conversion in non-interventional arm in our study may be explained due no effect of uterine manipulation on shedding off of tumour cells. Unlike cervical carcinoma which behaves like a surface malignancy, endometrial lesion lies within a relatively closed chamber. The pattern and implications of intra-operative tumour spillage in cervical cancer cannot be replicated in EC. Among others, one of the contrasting behaviour is the conflicting significance of positive peritoneal cytology in EC after hysteroscopy [27, 28]. The major risk of tumour spillage in EC can be attributed to uterine manipulator leading to uterine perforation, however, open surgery is devoid of such manoeuvre. In open surgery, uterine holding clamps are usually applied bilaterally at the cornual ends for better uterine manipulation which limits the direct extrinsic fundal pressure which might lead to decreased shedding off of tumour cells. We did also evaluate the impact of duration of surgery on intra- operative tumour spillage with the hypothesis of increased rate of tumour cell spillage with increased duration of surgery. However, no such statistical significant effect was found in our study. Intra-operative vaginal douching with betadine and suction/mopping of peritoneal cavity might be the reasons for positive to negative of cytology in the excluded patients.

A recent study shows higher rate of recurrence in patients with EC who had intra-operative tumour spillage [29]. Different protective manoeuvre to prevent or minimise tumour cell contamination of vaginal vault have been described in the literature. Vaginal wash with soap- water and its wiping just before cutting the vagina, packing the uterus with dry gauze, use of contraceptive diaphragm or even suturing the vagina over the cervix, preoperative suture closure of the cervix, Teflon cervical obturators, metal obturators etc [6]. The intervention followed by us for this study is very simple to practice and can be performed by general gynaecologist also. It aimed to occlude both the passage of tumour cells dissemination. Though the trans-tubal dissemination is very rare but is theoretically possible due to presence of floaters in few EC cases [23]. Hence, apart from already occluded cornual ends with uterine- holding clamps, fimbrial ends were also tied. Although this ended as a negative trial, it was planned with the anticipated benefits of preventing tumour spillage in endometrial cancer. The major limitation of this study is its small sample size, single- institutional study, lack of incorporation of molecular studies and use of conventional PAPs for vaginal cytology. Studies with larger sample size and studying its effect on survival outcome can be conducted in future for throwing more light on this topic.

Conclusion

The implication of intra-operative tumour spillage in gynaecological malignancies has gained momentum recently after establishing the detrimental effect of minimally- invasive approach in cervical cancer. The iatrogenic intra-operative trans- vaginal and trans-tubal dissemination of tumour cells in endometrial cancer has already been proven. Our study designed to find out the efficacy of cervical os and fallopian tube occlusion showed no impact of such manoeuvre on tumour cell spillage in endometrial cancer. Future studies with larger sample size is warranted to provide stronger and reliable results. It would also be interesting to see whether extrapolation of protective manoeuvres in minimally- invasive surgery for EC holds similar implications.
