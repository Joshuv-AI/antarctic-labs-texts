Exchange controls coarsening of surface condensates Preprint: APS/123-QED
Riccardo Rossetto, Marcel Ernst, David Zwicker

Abstract
Biological membranes often exhibit heterogeneous protein patterns, which cells control. Strong patterns, like the polarity spot in budding yeast, can be described as surface condensates, formed by physical interactions between constituents. However, it is unclear how these interactions affect the material exchange with the bulk. To study this, we analyze a thermodynamically consistent model, which reveals that passive exchange generally accelerates the coarsening of surface condensates. Active exchange can further accelerate coarsening, although it can also fully arrest it and induce complex patterns involving various length scales. We reveal how these behaviors are related to non-local transport via diffusion through the bulk, rationalizing the various scaling laws we observe and allowing us to interpret biologically relevant scenarios.



I
Introduction

Biological cells are enclosed by lipid membranes, which are crucial for interacting with the surrounding.
These membranes are complex, and cells control their composition in space and time.
In part, they achieve this by exploiting phase separation to form condensates embedded in the membrane
Banjade and Rosen 2014
;
McAffee et al. 2022
;
Litschel et al. 2024
;
Kamatar et al. 2024
.
One example is the polarity spot of budding yeast, which is a dense accumulation of multiple proteins at one region of the membrane, forming in preparation of cell division
Chiou et al. 2017
.
In this case, the proteins exchange with the bulk cytosol is apparently controlled by cells to ensure faithful division
Gulli et al. 2000
. However, the general principles of controlling condensates in membranes by material exchange remain elusive.

Models of pattern formation on membranes have been discussed in various context ranging from cluster descriptions
Turner et al. 2005
to spatially resolved models.
Prominent examples of the latter are reaction-diffusion models
Lang and Munro 2022
;
John and Bär 2005
;
John and Bär 2005
;
Geßele et al. 2020
;
Brauns et al. 2021
, which explain the emergence of patterns, but cannot describe physical interactions required for phase separation. Phase separation has been included in various thermodynamic models, e.g., in the specific case of surface wetting
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2025
and in more general theories that prescribe fixed exchange rates
Foret 2005
;
Caballero et al. 2023
.
However, it is unclear how thermodynamic constrains from physical interactions impact material exchange and the resulting patterns.

In this letter, we develop a minimal, thermodynamically consistent model of phase separation in surfaces including passive and active material exchange with the bulk (Fig.
1
A).
We find that passive exchange generally accelerates coarsening, whereas activity can either accelerate or suppress it, depending on details.
We characterize the state of the surface by the area fraction field
\phi(\bm{r},t)
of solute molecules, so the solvent fraction is
1-\phi(\bm{r},t)
.
In contrast, the state of the bulk is described by the mean solute fraction
\psi(t)

since we assume fast diffusion in the bulk.
The associated dynamics are governed by

The energy difference

Taken together, Eqs. (

II
Results

II.1
Passive exchange leads to faster coarsening

To understand the dynamics of phase separation in the surface, we first consider passive systems (
k_{\mathrm{a}}=0
). In particular, we perform numerical simulations of Eqs. (
1
)–(
3
) for a two dimensional surface with periodic boundary conditions
Zwicker 2020
, which leads to droplets of various sizes (Fig.
1
B).
We quantify the dynamics of these droplets by their average radius as a function time (Fig.
1
C).
Without exchange (
k_{\mathrm{p}}=0
, red line), Eq. (
1a
) reduces to regular Cahn–Hilliard dynamics
Cahn and Hilliard 1958
, explaining the observed Lifshiz–Slyozov coarsening of
R\sim t^{1/3}

Lifshitz and Slyozov 1961
;
Marqusee 1984
.
Enabling passive exchange (
k_{\mathrm{p}}>0
, blue lines) accelerates coarsening, which is expected since the exchange provides an additional transport channel between droplets.
A linear stability analysis of the equations supports this conclusion since it shows that
k_{\mathrm{p}}
increases the growth rate of perturbation modes with long wavelengths (SI.
V
).
Beside the quantitative acceleration of the coarsening, we also observe a transition to a qualitatively different coarsening regime, where the coarsening law approaches
R\sim t^{1/2}
for sufficiently large droplets.

II.2
Diffusive efflux and internal unbinding control coarsening

To study why passive exchange can alter coarsening qualitatively, we next evaluate the fluxes between droplets that formed in the surface.
Focusing on an individual droplet of radius
R
, the equilibrium fractions
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\phi_{n}^{(0)}(1+l_{\mathrm{c}}/R)
for
n\in\{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out}\}
are slightly elevated by surface tension effects, which is quantified by the capillary length
l_{\mathrm{c}}

Zwicker et al. 2025
.
We approximate the dynamics inside and outside the droplet by expanding Eqs. (
1
) around the coexisting fractions
\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{out}}
,

\partial_{t}\phi_{n}\simeq D_{n}\nabla^{2}\phi_{n}-k_{n}(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n}^{(0)})\;;

(4)

for
n\in\{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out}\}
;
see SI.
IV

Combining the resulting diffusivities
D_{n}\simeq\Lambda\nu_{\mathrm{s}}f_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
and the rate constants
k_{n}=-s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
, we define
the reaction-diffusion length scales
\ell_{n}=(D_{n}/k_{n})^{1/2}
, which quantify how far solute molecules diffuse in the surface before unbinding.
Mass balance implies the droplet growth rate
Zwicker et al. 2025

\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{J_{\mathrm{diff}}+J_{\mathrm{int}}}{2\pi R\bigl(\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}-\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{out}}\bigr)}\;,

(5)

where

J_{\mathrm{diff}}=-2\pi RD_{\mathrm{out}}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\prime}(R)

denotes the diffusive flux from the interface to the surrounding dilute phase, whereas
J_{\mathrm{int}}
captures the internal unbinding flux from the droplet to the bulk.
In the quasi-stationary limit,
J_{\mathrm{int}}
equals the the internal unbinding flux, implying
J_{\mathrm{int}}=2\pi k_{\mathrm{in}}\int_{0}^{R}r(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r)-\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})\mathrm{d}r
for a polar symmetric system.
We next show that these two fluxes can lead to very different coarsening behavior.

To obtain an expression for the flux
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
from the droplet interface toward the dilute phase, we solve Eq. (
4
) at stationarity assuming polar symmetry.
Using the boundary condition
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(R)=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)
at the interface and no-flux conditions far away from the droplet, we find

J_{\mathrm{diff}}=2\pi R\frac{D_{\mathrm{out}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{out}}}\frac{K_{1}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{out}})}{K_{0}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{out}})}\Bigl(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}\Bigr)\;,

(6)

where
K_{0}
,
K_{1}
are modified Bessel functions of the second kind.
If the exchange is slow (
R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
),
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
behaves as a purely diffusive flux and
J_{\mathrm{int}}
is negligible, leading to the expected Lifshitz–Slyozov scaling of
R\sim t^{1/3}
; see SI.
VI
.
In the converse limit (
R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
), fast exchange implies a homogeneous dilute phase,
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(r)=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}
, so
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
becomes proportional to the size of the droplet’s interface, implying
R\sim t^{1/2}

Wagner 1961
;
Lee 2021
; see SI.
VI
.

The internal unbinding flux
J_{\mathrm{int}}
follows from an analogous evaluation of the profile
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r)
with boundary conditions
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(R)=\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)
and
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}(r=0)=0
,

J_{\mathrm{int}}=2\pi R\frac{D_{\mathrm{in}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{in}}}\frac{I_{1}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{in}})}{I_{0}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{in}})}\Bigl(\phi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathrm{in}}(R)-\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}\Bigr)\;,

(7)

where
I_{0}
,
I_{1}
are modified Bessel functions of the first kind.
For slow exchange (
R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
), diffusion equilibrates the profile inside the droplet,
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r)\simeq\phi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathrm{in}}(R)
, so
J_{\mathrm{int}}
becomes proportional to the droplet’s area.
Assuming that internal unbinding dominates transport (
J_{\mathrm{diff}}\simeq 0
),
this regimes leads to a linear scaling
R\sim t
.
Conversely, if
R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
, the material unbinds before being able to diffuse across the droplet, so material exchange is confined to a region close to the interface, implying
R\sim t^{1/2}
. These coarsening laws are indeed observed in effective numerical simulations (SI.
IX
).

The effective fluxes allow us to interpret the numerically observed coarsening (Fig.
1
C).
When the exchange is fast in both phases (
R\gg\ell_{n}
), the internal unbinding flux
J_{\mathrm{int}}
behaves as an interface flux and dominates transport (Fig.
1
D), leading to the scaling
R\sim t^{1/2}
observed for the dark blue curve in Fig.
1
C.
Conversely, when the exchange is slow (
R\ll\ell_{n}
), diffusion dominates (
J_{\mathrm{diff}}\gg J_{\mathrm{int}}
, Fig.
1
D), implying
R\sim t^{1/3}
.
For intermediate exchange kinetics (light blue curves in Fig.
1
C), we find that diffusion dominates early, when droplets are small and close to each other, whereas exchange dominates later for large droplets that are far apart.
In contrast, the ballistic regime (
R\sim t
), is inaccessible because it would require large
J_{\mathrm{int}}
in the slow exchange regime (
R\ll\ell_{n}
) where
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
dominates instead; see Fig.
1
D.
Our analysis highlights that the two different fluxes can lead to qualitatively different coarsening regimes, which further depend on the relative size of droplets to the reaction-diffusion length scale.

II.3
Coarsening with passive exchange cannot explain fast formation of polarity spot

We next use the effective fluxes to approximate the relevant timescales governing the dynamics of single droplets and droplet pairs, where we for simplicity consider
\ell_{\mathrm{in}}=\ell_{\mathrm{out}}=\ell
and
D_{\mathrm{in}}=D_{\mathrm{out}}=D
.
We start by estimating how long it takes to form one large, stationary droplet in a finite system
Zwicker et al. 2015
.
We estimate the corresponding time scale
T_{\mathrm{single}}
from a linear stability analysis around this stationary state (SI.
VII
).
This time scale describes the exchange of material between bulk and surface, which is slow for large
\ell
(Fig.
1
E).
In this limit,
T_{\mathrm{single}}
is simply given by
T_{\mathrm{load}}\sim\ell^{2}/2D
, which describes homogeneous exchange.
In contrast, for small
\ell
, a droplet in the surface exchanges material with the bulk only in a region close to its interface, leading to slower dynamics.
Our analysis shows that
T_{\mathrm{single}}
grows with
\ell
, highlighting that passive exchange accelerates droplet formation.

For a passive system, multiple droplets always coarsen until one droplet remains.
We estimate the corresponding coarsening time
T_{\mathrm{coars}}
from the rate at which the sizes of droplets separated by a distance
L
diverge (SI.
VII
).
Fig.
1
E shows that
T_{\mathrm{coars}}
exceeds
T_{\mathrm{single}}
, essentially because material exchange between droplets is slower than exchange with the bulk.
Moreover, the two time scales exhibit different scaling laws as a function of
\ell
:
For fast exchange (
R,L\gg\ell
), we find (SI.
VII
)

T_{\mathrm{coars}}^{\mathrm{int}}\simeq\frac{R_{*}^{2}\ell(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})}{Dl_{\mathrm{c}}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}+\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})}\;,

(8)

which is independent of the separation
L
since diffusive and internal unbinding fluxes are interface-limited.
In contrast, for slow exchange (
R,L\ll\ell
), the internal unbinding flux is area-limited and molecules diffusing between droplets rarely unbind from the surface,
leading to the timescale (SI.
VII
)

T_{\mathrm{coars}}^{\mathrm{diff}}\simeq\frac{R_{*}^{3}\log(L/R_{*})(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})}{Dl_{\mathrm{c}}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}}\;,

(9)

which is independent of
\ell
, because the dynamics are essentially confined to the surface.

We next compare the estimated time scales to experimental measurements of polarity spots in budding yeast
Chiou et al. 2017
.
We estimate the diffusivity
D\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$/\mathrm{s}
and the exchange rate
k\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$
using measured values for the key protein Cdc42
Rutkowski et al. 2024
.
Additionally assuming a capillary length of
l_{\mathrm{c}}\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
based on molecular sizes, and considering two polarity spots of size
R_{*}\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
at distance
L\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, we find
T_{\mathrm{single}}\sim$30\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
and
T_{\mathrm{coars}}\sim$7\text{\times}{10}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.
Since polarity spots form within about
2
minutes, this suggests that passive exchange is consistent with their formation, but it fails to account for sufficiently fast resolution of two spots.
Instead, we would expect that evolution devised a process that resolves multiple spots significantly more quickly to ensure robust formation of the polarity spot.
This suggests that the experimentally observed activity
Chiou et al. 2017
is crucial.

II.4
Homogeneous activity leads to futile cycles

To study active unbinding from the surface (
k_{\mathrm{a}}\neq 0
), we first consider the simple case where the exchange is independent of position (
k_{\mathrm{p}},k_{\mathrm{a}},\Delta\mu
constant), the behavior is qualitatively similar to the passive case.
To see this, we linearize the exchange flux given by Eq. (
2
) using
\sinh(x)\approx x
, which implies

s_{\mathrm{p}}=-s_{\mathrm{a}}\approx\Delta\mu k_{\mathrm{p}}k_{\mathrm{a}}/[k_{\mathrm{B}}T(k_{\mathrm{p}}+k_{\mathrm{a}})]
at stationarity.
The system thus never reaches equilibrium when
k_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\Delta\mu
are finite.
Specifically, for
\Delta\mu>0
, material is removed from the surface using the external energy input, whereas it rebinds through the passive exchange flux.
However, if the rates
k_{\mathrm{p}}
and
k_{\mathrm{a}}
are constants, these fluxes are balanced everywhere, leading to futile cycles, which do not alter the dynamics
Kirschbaum and Zwicker 2021
.

To find interesting behavior, we need to break the futile cycles so that binding toward the surface at one place is balanced by unbinding at another place.
To do this, we consider constant
k_{\mathrm{p}}
combined with composition-dependent active unbinding,

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=k

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=k\Bigl[\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\phi+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}(1-\phi)\Bigr]\;,

(10)

motivated by enzymes segregating into droplets
Kirschbaum and Zwicker 2021
.
For simplicity, we here assume that passive and active exchange occur with similar rate
k
, but the active flux is modified by the asymmetry parameter
\alpha\in[-1,1]
.
For
\alpha>0
and
\Delta\mu>0
, active unbinding happens predominately inside droplets, where
\phi
is large.
Conversely, for
\alpha<0
(but still
\Delta\mu>0
), the active flux is biased toward dilute regions, and
\alpha=0
corresponds to homogeneous active exchange, which exhibits passive dynamics.
We can thus use
\alpha
to control the direction of circular fluxes between dense and dilute surface regions and the bulk.

II.5
Active unbinding from dilute regions accelerates coarsening

We first focus on the case where activity promotes unbinding from dilute regions (
\alpha<0
).
Fig.
2
A shows that such activity (orange lines) accelerates coarsening compared to the passive case (gray lines).
The data suggests that not only is the pre-factor increased, but also that the coarsening exhibits a different power-law, where the average droplet radius scales linearly with time for slower exchange.
To understand this behavior, we again study the fluxes
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
and
J_{\mathrm{int}}
affecting a droplet of radius
R
.
In contrast to the passive case discussed above, the exchange flux now does not vanish at phase equilibrium (
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}=\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}=\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{out}}
).
The difference between phase equilibrium and exchange equilibrium is quantified by fractions
\Delta\phi_{n}
,
which imply the following reaction-diffusion equations in the phases
n=\text{in},\text{out}

Weber et al. 2019
;
Zwicker 2022
,

\partial_{t}\phi_{n}\simeq D_{n}\nabla^{2}\phi_{n}-k_{n}(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n}^{(0)}-\Delta\phi_{n})\;,

(11)

where
k_{n}=-s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
and
\Delta\phi_{n}=s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})/k_{n}
.
For passive systems (
\Delta\phi_{n}=0
), this equation is identical to Eq. (
4
).
Consequently, the associated fluxes
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
and
J_{\mathrm{int}}
can be obtained via replacing
\phi^{(0)}_{n}
by
\phi^{(0)}_{n}+\Delta\phi_{n}
in Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
), respectively (SI.
IV
).
For
\alpha<0
, we find
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}<0
and
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}>0
, which enables systems where
J_{\mathrm{int}}
dominates for small droplets (
R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
, Fig.
2
B), which is impossible in passive systems (Fig.
1
D) for
\ell_{\mathrm{in}}=\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
.
In this case, we predict ballistic growth (
R\sim t
), consistent with our data (Fig.
2
A), and even super-linear scalings become accessible for strong activity (Fig.
S1
C).
However, our theory predicts a transition to
R\sim t^{1/2}
when droplets are sufficiently large, so that
J_{\mathrm{int}}
eventually becomes interface-limited (SI.
VI
).
For faster exchange rates, this transition takes place at smaller droplets, implying that
R\sim t^{1/2}
is observed earlier, consistent with data (Fig.
2
A).

Overall, our numerical simulations indicate that active unbinding in dilute regions accelerates coarsening.
Qualitatively, this is because active unbinding effectively undersaturates the dilute region (
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}<0
), while adding material to dense regions (
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}>0
).
The resulting circular fluxes enhance material transport from small to large droplets, thus accelerating coarsening.
To investigate this acceleration quantitatively, we again estimate the time
T_{\mathrm{coars}}
needed to resolve two spots separated by
L
.
Using the same technique as above and considering parameters compatible with polarity formation in budding yeast, we find that a relatively small energy input (
\Delta\mu=2\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T
) can lead to significant acceleration (
T_{\mathrm{coars}}^{\mathrm{act}}\sim${10}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
, SI.
VII.3
).
This suggests that budding yeast uses active processes to promote unbinding from dilute regions to ensure the formation of a single polarity spot quickly and robustly.

In the opposing case where activity promotes unbinding from dense regions (
\alpha>0
), coarsening is slowed (Fig.
3
A).
Interestingly, the slow-down is strongest for intermediate exchange rates (pink line), where it seems to halt completely.
To understand this slow-down, we again study the total flux toward the droplet,
J_{\mathrm{tot}}=-J_{\mathrm{int}}-J_{\mathrm{diff}}
, which governs droplet growth; see Eq. (
5
).
For
\alpha>0
, we find
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}>0
and
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}<0
(SI.
IV
), which implies that
J_{\mathrm{tot}}
exhibits two roots (Fig.
3
B).
While the first root corresponds to the critical radius below which droplets shrink, the second root is a stable state indicating that droplets of that size are stable.
This situation is akin to externally maintained droplets where chemical conversion of material drives similar fluxes, leading to arrested coarsening
Weber et al. 2019
;
Kirschbaum and Zwicker 2021
;
Zwicker et al. 2025
.
This similarity is also supported by linear stability analysis (SI.
V
), which reveals a band of unstable modes.
Taken together, material exchange between surface and bulk plays a similar role to the previously described reactions in bulk.
Intuitively, the arrested coarsening is caused by active unbinding from the dense region (
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}<0
), and rebinding at the dilute region (
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}>0
).
The resulting fluxes transport material from large to small droplets, thus opposing coarsening and supporting regular hexagonal patterns (Fig.
3
C).
From analyzing chemically active droplets, we expect that smaller reaction rates (i.e., exchange rates in our case) lead to patterns with larger length scale
Glotzer and Coniglio 1994
;
Christensen et al. 1996
;
Muratov 2002
;
Zwicker et al. 2015
;
Köstler et al. 2025
.
Fig.
3
A indeed suggests that slower exchange affects coarsening less (blue line), and we predict that the average droplet radius reaches a stable stationary state eventually.
Taken together, we find that size-control of droplets eventually limits coarsening.

We have shown that larger exchange rates arrest droplets at smaller sizes, implying a stronger suppression of coarsening.
While this is confirmed by Fig.
3
A, very large exchange rates surprisingly lead to fast coarsening again,
which hardly differs from the passive case (yellow line in Fig.
3
A).
In these cases, we do not observe any size-control, and the system instead coarsens toward a single droplet (Fig.
3
D).
The droplet sizes evolve as
R(t)\sim t^{1/2}
, consistent with passive exchange (Fig.
1
C).
For the large rates considered here, the exchange becomes interface-limited (Fig.
3
D), implying a correction to the interface behavior, whereas phase coexistence behaves effectively as a passive system
Qiang et al. 2025
;
Cho and Jacobs 2025
.
Taken together, this suggest that strong reactions merely affect the interface, but the large-scale behavior is essentially unchanged.

In the case where activity promotes unbinding from dense regions, faster exchange kinetics (large
k
) generally accelerate coarsening when size-control is not yet relevant (early times in Fig.
3
A), similar to passive exchange.
However, in some cases active unbinding from dense regions introduces fluxes between droplets that can limit their sizes.
This size-control sets in earlier for larger exchange rates
k

Köstler et al. 2025
, explaining the observed earlier slow-down of coarsening (e.g., pink curve in Fig.
3
A).
However, for even larger
k
, we do not observe any size-control, and instead the system coarsen indefinitely.
This distinction between size-control and coarsening suggests that there is a critical exchange rate, although we also observe multi-stability, where the behavior depends on initial conditions (Fig.
S2
).
Interestingly, we sometimes even observe coexistence between multiple droplets of equal size and a single large droplet (Fig.
3
E), which we interpret as coexistence between a patterned phase and a dense macrophase.
In that case, we observe continuous coarsening of the macrophase, whereas the patterned phase plays the role of the dilute phase.

III
Discussion

In summary, we showed that passive exchange accelerates coarsening (Fig.
1
), whereas active exchange either accelerates (Fig.
2
) or suppresses coarsening (Fig.
3
).
The precise coarsening dynamics depend on how droplets exchange material, which either happens by diffusion along the surface or via diffusion through the bulk upon unbinding.
The exchange dynamics determine whether these fluxes are limited to regions around the interface or not, which affects the coarsening dynamics qualitatively.
The suppressed coarsening is akin to chemically active droplets
Weber et al. 2019
;
Zwicker et al. 2025
;
Zwicker 2022
, which also exhibit multi-stability
Bauermann et al. 2025
;
Köstler et al. 2025
.
Beyond these similarities, we additionally observe the coexistence of many small droplets with one large homogeneous region.
Interestingly, similar patterns have been observed in phase-field crystals
Thiele et al. 2013
and systems with non-local elasticity
Qiang et al. 2024
.
In analogy, the exchange via the bulk might thus play the rolf of a non-local interaction in our system, suggesting that non-local interactions are key to controlling various patterns
Seul and Andelman 1995
.

Our results have implications for membrane patterns in biology:
For instance, we propose that active exchange is necessary to quickly and robustly form only one polarity spot in budding yeast.
However, our general theory applies to many pattern forming systems, including PAR protein patterns in
C. elegans

Lang and Munro 2017
, post-synaptic densities in neurons
Zeng et al. 2016
, LAT receptor clusters in bacteria
Benn et al. 2021
, Min protein patterns
Loose et al. 2011
;
Brauns et al. 2021
;
Würthner et al. 2022
, and Noc proteins
in vitro

Babl et al. 2022
.
To describe these systems in detail, our model likely needs to be extended, e.g., by including dimerization
Bland et al. 2024
;
Rossetto et al. 2025
, advection
Goehring et al. 2011
, exchange with linear compartments
Ernst et al. 2025
;
Morin et al. 2022
, chemical reactions, membrane interactions
Winter et al. 2025
, and fluctuations.

IV
Thin interface approximation

We derive the thin-interface approximation by considering an individual droplet of radius
R
.
We can then approximate the thermodynamically consistent dynamics given by Eqs. (
1
) in the main text inside and outside the droplet by perturbing the full equations around the coexistence fractions, using
\phi_{n}\approx\phi^{(0)}_{n}+\delta\phi_{n}
for
n=\text{in},\text{out}
.
We further assume that the overall binding equilibrium between surface and bulk is reached faster than time scales relevant for coarsening, implying that dynamics in the surface leave the bulk unchanged,
\delta\psi\sim 0
.
We thus obtain

\partial_{t}\delta\phi_{n}=\Lambda\nu_{\mathrm{s}}f_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})\nabla^{2}\delta\phi_{n}-\Lambda\kappa\nabla^{4}\delta\phi_{n}+s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})+s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})\delta\phi_{n}\;,

(S1)

for
n=\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out}
.
Assuming that concentration variations are small, we neglect the fourth-order derivative,

\partial_{t}\delta\phi_{n}=D_{n}\nabla^{2}\delta\phi_{n}-k_{n}\delta\phi_{n}+s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})\;,

(S2)

where we also defined the diffusion constants
D_{n}=\Lambda\nu_{\mathrm{s}}f_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
and binding rates
k_{n}=-s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
.
For passive systems, both the dense and dilute phases satisfy binding equilibrium
Rossetto et al. 2025
, implying
s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})=0
and thus

\partial_{t}\delta\phi_{n}=D_{n}\nabla^{2}\delta\phi_{n}-k_{n}\delta\phi_{n}\;.

(S3)

In contrast, basal fluxes
s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
are present in the active case.
To evaluate them, we consider the limit of small activity (
\Delta\mu\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
), so the passive flux is close to equilibrium (
\mu_{\mathrm{b}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
) and we thus have

s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})\simeq k\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{b}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)+k\Bigl[\frac{1-\alpha}{2}+\alpha\phi\Bigr]\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{b}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}-\Delta\mu}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)\;.

(S4)

Further assuming strong phase separation
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}\sim 1
and
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}\sim 0
and absorbing a constant term in the bulk chemical potential
\tilde{\mu}_{\mathrm{b}}=\mu_{\mathrm{b}}-\Delta\mu/(2k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
, we obtain the inside and outside exchange fluxes
s_{\mathrm{in}}
,
s_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{in}}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\simeq k\Bigl(\frac{3+\alpha}{2}\bigl[\tilde{\mu}_{\mathrm{b}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})\bigr]-\frac{\alpha\Delta\mu}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)

(S5a)

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{out}}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\simeq k\Bigl(\frac{3-\alpha}{2}\bigl[\tilde{\mu}_{\mathrm{b}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})\bigr]+\frac{\alpha\Delta\mu}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)\;,

(S5b)

where
\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})=\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})
since we assume local equilibrium at the interface.
At stationarity, the two fluxes given by Eqs. (
S5
) must have opposite signs to maintain coexisting phases.
Since
\alpha\in[-1,1]
, the first term in the fluxes have the same sign, implying that the second term must determine the sign.
In particular, we require
s(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})>0
for
\alpha<0
, and
s(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})<0
for
\alpha>0
, while the outside basal flux
s_{\mathrm{out}}
has opposing signs.
Since
s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})<0
is needed for the stability of the dense and dilute states, we know that
k_{n}>0
, implying that the sign of the basal fluxes equals the sign of the corresponding fractions
\Delta\phi_{n}
.

Linear stability analysis

To analyze the stability of a generic stationary state
(\phi^{*}(\bm{r}),\psi^{*})
of the thermodynamically consistent dynamics, we expand Eqs. (
1
) of the main text by introducing the perturbations
\phi=\phi^{*}+\delta\phi(\bm{r},t)
and
\psi=\psi^{*}+\delta\psi(t)

The bulk perturbation
\delta\psi

\bar{\phi}+\eta\psi=\Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}

\delta\psi(t)=\frac{1}{\eta A}\int_{A}\delta\phi(\bm{r},t)\mathrm{d}A=-\frac{\delta\hat{\phi}(0,t)}{\eta}\;,

(S6)

\delta\phi(\bm{r},t)=\int\delta\hat{\phi}(\bm{q},t)e^{i\bm{q}\bm{r}}\mathrm{d}^{d}q

Perturbing the surface dynamics to linear order in
\delta\phi

\partial_{t}\delta\phi=\Lambda\nu_{\mathrm{c}}f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{*})\delta\phi-\Lambda\kappa\nabla^{4}\delta\phi+s\;,

(S7)

s\simeq\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigl[\bigl(\nu_{\mathrm{b}}f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{*})\delta\psi-\nu_{\mathrm{c}}f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{*})\delta\phi+\kappa\nabla^{2}\delta\phi\bigr)\bigl(1+C(\phi^{*})\bigr)\\
+\alpha\delta\phi\bigl(\mu_{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{*})-\mu_{\mathrm{c}}(\phi^{*})-\Delta\mu\bigr)\Bigr]\;,

(S8)

with
C(\phi)=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\phi+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}(1-\phi)

Transforming to Fourier space, we then find the dispersion relation

\tilde{\omega}(\bm{q})=-\Lambda\kappa\bm{q}^{4}-\Lambda\bm{q}^{2}\Bigl[\nu_{\mathrm{c}}f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{c}}(\phi^{*})+\frac{k\kappa}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\bigl(1+C(\phi^{*})\bigr)\Bigr]\\
+\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigl[\alpha\bigl(\mu_{\mathrm{b}}(\psi^{*})-\mu_{\mathrm{c}}(\phi^{*})-\Delta\mu\bigr)-\nu_{\mathrm{c}}f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{*})\bigl(1+C(\phi^{*})\bigr)\Bigr]\;,

(S9)

where we assumed
\bm{q}\neq 0
.
The special mode
\bm{q}=0
requires a correction due to the bulk perturbation
\delta\psi
, which can be written as the spatial average of the surface one; see Eq. (
S6
).
We thus find

\omega(\bm{q})=\tilde{\omega}(\bm{q})-\begin{cases}\dfrac{k\nu_{\mathrm{b}}}{\eta k_{\mathrm{B}}T}f_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime\prime}(\psi^{*})\bigl(1+C(\phi^{*})\bigr)&\bm{q}=\bm{0}\\[10.0pt]
0&\bm{q}\neq\bm{0}\end{cases}\;,

(S10)

leading to a discontinuous dispersion relation, which only depends on
q=|\bm{q}|
because of isotropy.
We evaluate
\omega(q)
for homogeneous stationary states obeying exchange flux balance.
For the passive case (
\alpha=0

\omega(q)>0
at

and
\omega(q)<0
at
q=0
(blue curve).
This implies a stable homogeneous state
\omega(q=0)<0

\omega(q\rightarrow 0)\neq 0

For
\alpha=1

Diffusive efflux and internal unbinding flux

We here evaluate the effective fluxes given by Eqs. (
6
) and Eq. (
7
) in the main text in limiting cases.
For the diffusive efflux
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
we find

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{diff}}(R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\pi D_{\mathrm{out}}}{\gamma+\log(\frac{R}{2\ell_{\mathrm{out}}})}\Bigl(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}\Bigr)

(S11a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{diff}}(R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi RD_{\mathrm{out}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{out}}}\Bigl(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}\Bigr)\;.

(S11b)

For
R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
, we recover the diffusive regime, whereas we recover a flux proportional to the interface (and
D_{\mathrm{out}}/\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
) for
R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Equating the two limits, we predict a transition between the two regimes at
R^{*}=-\ell_{\mathrm{out}}/W_{-1}(-e^{\gamma/2})
, where
W(z)
is the Lambert
W
function obeying
z=We^{W}
, and
\gamma
is Euler’s constant.
For the internal unbinding flux
J_{\mathrm{int}}
, we find

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{int}}(R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi R^{2}D_{\mathrm{in}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}}\Bigl(\phi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathrm{in}}(R)-\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}\Bigr)

(S12a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{int}}(R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi RD_{\mathrm{in}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{in}}}\Bigl(\phi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathrm{in}}(R)-\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}\Bigr)\;,

(S12b)

showing that the limit
R\ll\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
gives an area-limited flux, and the limit
R\gg\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
gives an interface-limited flux. Equating the two limits, we predict a transition at
R^{*}=2\ell_{\mathrm{in}}
.

We next estimate the dynamics of the droplet radius in the limiting cases of fast and slow binding.
The boundary conditions at the droplet surface are fixed due to Laplace pressure, resulting in the Gibbs-Thomson relation
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)=\phi_{n}^{(0)}(1+l_{\mathrm{c}}/R)

Zwicker et al. 2025
.
Here, we assumed identical capillary lengthscales
l_{\mathrm{c}}
in both phases for simplicity.
In the case of fast binding (
R\gg\ell_{n}
), both fluxes are interface-limited.
From
\partial(\pi R^{2})/\partial t\propto J_{\mathrm{diff}}+J_{\mathrm{int}}
, we find
R(t)\propto(l_{\mathrm{c}}t[D_{\mathrm{out}}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}/\ell_{\mathrm{out}}+D_{\mathrm{in}}\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}/\ell_{\mathrm{in}}])^{1/2}
, leading to the
R\sim t^{1/2}
scaling, in agreement with previous works
Wagner 1961
;
Lee 2021
.
In the case of slow binding (
R\ll\ell_{n}
),
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
is purely diffusive and
J_{\mathrm{int}}
is area-limited, leading to different behavior, which we analyze separately assuming that one of the mechanisms dominates transport.
Purely diffusive flux leads to
R(t)\propto(D_{\mathrm{out}}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}l_{\mathrm{c}}t)^{1/3}

Lifshitz and Slyozov 1961
.
For dominating area flux, we find
R(t)\propto D_{\mathrm{in}}\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}l_{\mathrm{c}}t/(2\ell_{\mathrm{in}})

Effective simulations confirm the scalings we predict from this analysis (Fig.
S1
B).
See SI.
IX

In the active case described by Eq. (
11

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{act}}

\displaystyle=2\pi R\frac{D_{\mathrm{out}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{out}}}\frac{K_{1}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{out}})}{K_{0}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{out}})}\Bigl(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}-\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}\Bigr)\;,

(S13a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{act}}

\displaystyle=2\pi R\frac{D_{\mathrm{in}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{in}}}\frac{I_{1}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{in}})}{I_{0}(R/\ell_{\mathrm{in}})}\Bigl(\phi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\mathrm{in}}(R)-\phi^{(0)}_{\mathrm{in}}-\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}\Bigr)\;.

(S13b)

The estimated transitions between the different regimes are left unchanged since activity does not modify the

We next estimate again the scaling behaviours in the active case for the enhanced coarsening regime
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}>0

\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}<0

For small droplets

In contrast, for large droplets

However, we typically consider small activity and strong phase separation, so
\Delta\phi_{n}\ll 1

Indeed, we observe scalings consistent with the passive fluxes in our numerical simulations (Fig.

Simulations with large activity

Timescales estimation

VII.1
Growth of a single droplet

To estimate the timescale for the growth of a single droplet, we consider a finite system, in which the final macroscopic droplet is a stationary state of the dynamics.
We use the perturbation decay rate around this stationary state to obtain a characteristic time scale of droplet growth.
Consequently, we expand the equations around the equilibrium concentrations
\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{eq}}
and
\psi^{\mathrm{eq}}
, which obey
s(\phi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{eq}},\psi^{\mathrm{eq}})=0
.
We consider the dynamics of a single droplet,

\partial_{t}\phi_{n}\simeq D_{n}\nabla^{2}\phi_{n}+\partial_{\phi_{n}}s(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{eq}})\delta\phi_{n}+\partial_{\psi}s(\psi^{\mathrm{eq}})\delta\psi\;,

(S14)

where we now cannot neglect the bulk perturbation since the bulk evolves during the dynamics.
Here, we have defined the effective binding kinetics
k_{n}=-\partial_{\phi_{n}}s(\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{eq}})
.
To evaluate the variation of the binding flux with respect to the equilibrium bulk concentration in terms of the binding kinetics, we impose the conservation law for the average fractions,

\frac{A(t)}{A_{\mathrm{s}}}\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\Bigl(1-\frac{A(t)}{A_{\mathrm{s}}}\Bigr)\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{out}}(t)+\eta\psi(t)=\Phi_{\mathrm{tot}}\;,

(S15)

where
A(t)
represents the area of the droplet,
A_{\mathrm{s}}
the area of the surface and
\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)
,
\bar{\phi}_{\mathrm{out}}(t)
the average fractions in the dense and dilute phases at time
t
.
We can then use
\partial s/\partial\psi=\partial s/\partial\phi_{n}\cdot\partial\phi_{n}/\partial\psi
together with approximating the variation of the inside and outside fractions by the variation of their averages,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{in}}}{\partial\psi}\Big|_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta A_{\mathrm{s}}}{A_{\mathrm{eq}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{out}}}{\partial\psi}\Big|_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta A_{\mathrm{s}}}{A_{\mathrm{s}}-A_{\mathrm{eq}}}\;,

(S16)

where
A_{\mathrm{eq}}=\pi R_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}
represents the equilibrium volume of the droplet.
Inserting this in Eq. (
S14
), we obtain the same functional form as Eq. (
S3
) with the time-dependent binding equilbrium

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{eq}}+\frac{A_{\mathrm{s}}}{A_{\mathrm{eq}}}\eta(\psi(t)-\psi_{\mathrm{eq}})

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}+\frac{A_{\mathrm{s}}}{A_{\mathrm{s}}-A_{\mathrm{eq}}}\eta(\psi(t)-\psi_{\mathrm{eq}})\;.

(S17)

We then assume that the motion of the interface is slower than the equilibration of the concentration profile, and solve the Helmolz equations in the two phases at stationarity.
For the dense phase, we impose the boundary conditions
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(R)=\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)
and
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}(r=0)=0
.
For the dilute phase, we consider boundary conditions
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(R)=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R)
and
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(L)=\phi_{\mathrm{dil}}(t)
.
Taken together, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}A(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}-\phi_{\mathrm{dil}}(t)}\Bigl(-J_{\mathrm{diff}}-J_{\mathrm{int}}\Bigr)

(S18a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\mathrm{dil}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{s}}-A(t)}\Bigl(J_{\mathrm{diff}}-J_{\mathrm{ext}}\Bigr)

(S18b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{\mathrm{b}}}{V_{\mathrm{b}}\nu_{\mathrm{s}}}\Bigl(J_{\mathrm{int}}+J_{\mathrm{ext}}\Bigr)\;,

(S18c)

where
\phi_{\mathrm{dil}}(t)
is the volume fraction on the surface far away from the droplet,
\psi(t)
denotes the volume fraction in the bulk, and
V_{\mathrm{b}}
the volume of the bulk.
The exchange between the compartments is given by the diffusive efflux
J_{\mathrm{diff}}
, which quantifies the amount of material leaving the droplet on the surface via diffusion through its interface, and the internal and external unbinding fluxes
J_{\mathrm{int}}
and
J_{\mathrm{ext}}
, respectively quantifing the amount of the material going from the dense and dilute phases to the bulk.
These can be determined using the profiles calculated above,

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{diff}}

\displaystyle=-2\pi RD_{\mathrm{out}}\Bigl(\partial_{r}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(r)|_{R}\Bigr)

(S19a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=-2\pi\int_{0}^{R}r(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(r,t)-\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}(t))\mathrm{d}r,

(S19b)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{ext}}

\displaystyle=-2\pi\int_{R}^{L}r(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(r,t)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}(t))\mathrm{d}r\;,

(S19c)

upon expanding the binding flux as above. The larger eigenvalue of the Jacobian of Eqs. (
S18
) evaluated at the large droplet stationary state
(A_{\mathrm{eq}},\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}},\psi^{\mathrm{eq}})

determine the timescales shown in the blue curve in Fig.
1
E in the main text.

VII.2
Coarsening timescale

To evaluate the timescale for coarsening, we assume the overall binding equilibrium between the surface and bulk has been reached, and any dynamics is exclusively due to material exchange between droplets (potentially mediated by the bulk).
In particular, we study the dynamics of a droplet of radius
R_{1}
surrounded by stable droplets of radius
R_{2}
at distance
L
.
We can then determine the concentration profiles considering again that the motion of the interface is slower than the equilibration of the concentration profiles, and solving Eq. (
S3
) quasi-statically.
Such droplets are in equilibrium with the bulk, implying
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R_{2})
, such that they change only in response to the dynamics of the first droplet, and are otherwise stationary.
The associated boundary conditions in the dilute phase are
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(R_{1})=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R_{1})
and
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(L)=\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R_{2})
.
From the resulting concentration profile, we can determine the diffusive flux
J_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{coars}}=-2\pi R_{1}D_{\mathrm{out}}\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{\prime}(R_{1})
as above, which now depends on the distance
L
between droplets and the second droplet’s radius
R_{2}
.
Similarly, to determine the dynamics due to the internal flux, we impose that the overall binding equilibrium was already reached internally, so
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}=\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(R_{2})
.
Inserting this in Eq. (
7
) of the main text, we determine the internal flux, which now depends on
R_{2}
.
The evolution of the first droplet with radius
R_{1}
then reads

\partial_{t}R_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi R_{1}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})}\Bigl(-J_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{coars}}(R_{1},R_{2})-J_{\mathrm{int}}(R_{1},R_{2})\Bigr)\;.

(S20)

We consider the solution in which all droplet have the same radius
R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{*}
and consider the evolution of small perturbations using linear stability analysis.
This perturbation grows exponentially with a timescale
T_{\mathrm{coars}}=1/(\partial_{R_{1}}f(R_{1},R_{2})|_{R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{*}})
, determining the orange curve shown in Fig.
1
E in the main text.

VII.3
Active coarsening timescale

We next repeat the calculation above including the active corrections
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{n}}
introduced in Eq. (
11
) in the main text.
In this case, the stationary configuration considered above with all droplets of the same size becomes dependent on the exchange kinetics
k
, and thus on the reaction diffusion lengthscale
\ell
.
This implies we cannot calculate the timescale depending on
\ell
as done above, since the stationary state now also depends on it, so we consider a particlar case.
Focusing on buddying yeast, we are interested in estimating the activity needed for a speed-up that makes the coarsening timescale consistent with the experimentally measured one.
We calculate the active corrections
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{n}}=-s(\phi_{n}^{(0)})/s^{\prime}(\phi_{n}^{(0)})
using Eq. (
S5
), assuming small activities.
We here consider the case of active unbinding from dilute regions (
\alpha=-1
), which leads to accelerated coarsening.
We calculate the derivatives as
s^{\prime}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})=-kf_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})
,
s^{\prime}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})=-2kf_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})
.
Moreover, to calculate the basal fluxes we use that their sign is determined by the active part (SI.
IV
), so we consider the simple case that this term dominates,

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{in}}(\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\sim k_{\mathrm{p}}\Bigl(\frac{\Delta\mu}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{out}}(\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\sim k_{\mathrm{p}}\Bigl(-\frac{\Delta\mu}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\Bigr)\;.

(S21)

We choose
\chi=3.5
,
\phi_{\mathrm{in}}^{(0)}=0.95
,
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}=0.035
, and
\Delta\mu=2k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, resulting in
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{in}}\simeq 0.07
and
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{out}}\simeq-0.02
.
Inserting this in the equation for the dynamics of the radius given by Eq. (
S20
) assuming
R_{1}=R_{2}
and correcting for
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{n}}
, we find a stationary radius
R_{*}\sim 100\,l_{\mathrm{c}}
, consistent with the size of the polarity spot.
The system relaxes back to such stationary state exponentially, with a timescale
T_{\mathrm{coars}}^{\mathrm{act}}\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.

VIII
Multi-Stability

We performed numerical simulations that demonstrate multistability in the regime associated with the regular coarsening in the case of fast exchange with active unbinding from dense regions (yellow curve Fig.
3
A in the main text).
Initialising the system in the homogeneous binding equilibrium (zeros of Eq. (
2
) in the main text) and perturbing it leads to coarsening (Fig.
S2
A).
In contrast, initializing the system with multiple droplets leads to a stationary state with multilple droplets of the same size (Fig.
S2
B), similarly to what happens for intermediate exchange rates (pink curve Fig.
3
A in the main text) and area-limited droplets.
