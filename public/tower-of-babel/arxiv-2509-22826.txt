Droplet rebounds off a fluid bath: kinematic match simulations and experiments
Elvis A. Agüero 1,2, Carlos A. Galeano-Rios 3,4,5, Clodoaldo Ragazzo 6, Chase T. Gabbard 1, Daniel M. Harris 1, Paul A. Milewski 7,4

Abstract
We present a method to simulate non-coalescing impacts and rebounds of droplets onto the free surface of a liquid bath, together with new experimental data. The method is derived from first principles and imposes only natural geometric and kinematic constraints on the motion of the impacting interfaces, yielding predictions for the evolution of the contact area, pressure distribution, and wave field generated by the impact on both impacting masses. This work generalises an existing kinematic-match method whose prior applications dealt with deformation in the surface of the bath only; i.e., neglecting that of the droplet. The method’s extension to include droplet deformation gives predictions that compare favourably with existing experimental results and the new experiments in the low Weber number regime using submillimeter-sized droplets.

Introduction

Droplet impacts are integral to a diverse range of natural and industrial processes and have thus been extensively studied
(
Mohammad Karim 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
. In nature, raindrops impacting plant surfaces can facilitate the transport of pathogens among living organisms
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
)
. Thus, studying droplet impacts can inform models used to design plant pesticides
(
Park et al. 2020
;
Jung 2023
)
, and bio-mimicry applications, such as self-cleaning surfaces
(
Perumanath et al. 2023
)
. Industrial applications where modelling droplet impacts is important include spray cooling, which benefits from high and uniform heat transfer rates
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Kim 2007
)
, and the design of anti-icing materials, which is aided by studying the energy lost during impact and contact time
(
Esmeryan et al. 2022
)
. Droplet impacts also underpin manufacturing techniques like inkjet printing of smart materials
(
Lohse 2022
;
Wijshoff 2018
;
Karim et al. 2021
)
. Similar capillary-scale phenomena observed in nature have also inspired biomimetic designs and experiments in fundamental science, including water-walking robots for environmental exploration
(
Bush & Hu 2006
;
Kwak & Bae 2018
)
, and are relevant in experimental designs in diverse fields throughout engineering
(
Erfani et al. 2023
)
.

Despite this breadth of applications, accurately capturing the dynamics of droplet impacts, particularly on deformable interfaces, presents significant challenges. Starting with the seminal work of
Worthington 1882
, research efforts have sought to understand the plethora of phenomena observed when droplets impact solid and liquid surfaces
(
Josserand & Thoroddsen 2016
;
Rein 1993
)
. The majority of these efforts have focused on moderate and high Weber number impacts, which can result in spreading, retraction, or splashing
(
Yarin 2006
)
for impacts on solid surfaces, and may additionally lead to corona splashing and coalescence on liquid surfaces
(
Thoroddsen et al. 2008
;
Kavehpour 2015
;
Burzynski et al. 2020
)
. Notably, coalescence is primarily governed by the presence and drainage of a thin film of air separating the droplet and bath during impact
(
Couder et al. 2005b
;
Yarin 2006
;
Kavehpour 2015
)
. At moderate Weber numbers impacting droplets partially or fully rebound
(
Alventosa et al. 2023
;
Burzynski et al. 2020
)
, provided that the separating gas film does not thin below a critical thickness
(
Sprittles 2024
;
Chubynsky et al. 2020
)
. The outcome of an impact event depends critically upon the substrate. For example, by heating or coating a solid substrate the surface may be rendered non-wetting and promote droplet rebound
(
Onda et al. 1996
;
Gabbard et al. 2025
)
. For droplet-bath impacts, coalescence can be delayed indefinitely by vertically oscillating the substrate, which replenishes the intervening air layer
(
Couder et al. 2005b
)
.

The sustained bouncing of droplets on vibrating baths has attracted significant interest, in large part due to their dynamics along the bath surface showing quantum-like behaviour, analogous to that of pilot-wave theory
(
Bush & Oza 2020
)
. More recently, bouncing droplets that self-propel through interaction with their own wavefield, generated on a bath oscillating at two superimposed natural frequencies, have revealed new multi-droplet interaction behaviours and the emergence of ‘superwalkers’, which are droplets exceeding the maximum stable size for sustained bouncing on a bath oscillating at a single frequency
(
Valani et al. 2019
)
.
Galeano-Rios et al. 2019a
derived a model for superwalkers with detailed features for the wave field. Their work treated the bouncing droplet as a rigid sphere and only partially captured the behaviour of superwalkers, suggesting that the large deformation observed in superwalkers plays an integral role in their dynamics.

A large body of work has mapped rebound and coalescence during droplet–bath impact in the regime where viscous and gravitational effects are negligible compared to inertia and surface tension. Coalescence proceeds once the intervening gas film drains to about
10^{2}
nm, with the bounce–coalescence threshold often expressed by a critical Weber number that depends on fluid properties, drop size, and bath depth
(
Couder et al. 2005b
;
Yarin 2006
;
Kavehpour 2015
;
Tang et al. 2019
)
. Across fluids, experiments report contact times that are weakly dependent on impact speed except at very low Weber numbers, and a saturation of translational energy recovery at intermediate Weber numbers
(
Zhao et al. 2011
;
Zou et al. 2011
;
Wu et al. 2020
)
. Bath-depth effects can modulate rebound yet leave contact time approximately constant over a wide range of conditions
(
Zou et al. 2011
)
, while high-speed imaging has quantified coalescence times and early-time dynamics of the air film and interface deformation
(
Thoroddsen et al. 2008
)
. Most datasets focus on moderate Weber numbers; targeted measurements for submillimetric, low-Weber-number impacts at normal incidence on deep baths remain sparse.

While these experiments establish the main rebound scalings, they do not resolve the detailed droplet and bath deformations, motivating complementary modeling approaches. Fully resolving the dynamics of a deformable impactor is challenging, as numerical multiscale effects typically span three orders of magnitude
(
Couder et al. 2005a
)
, and natural time scales present at the start of impact as the droplet spreads and retracts are short
(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2017
, see figure 3b of)
. Several seminal works have made progress in addressing these issues, namely through asymptotic analysis and linearised formulations of the equations of motion of the free surface for impacts against an inviscid, incompressible fluid
(
Wagner 1932
;
Korobkin 2004
;
Howison et al. 1991
)
.

A weakly viscous framework introduced in
Lamb 1895
, and revisited by
Dias et al. 2008
, has been combined with a formulation that incorporates the drop-bath coupling interaction, using the
kinematic match
(KM or full KM) model, which imposes natural geometric and kinematic constraints to solve for the evolution of the contact area between the impacting bodies
(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2017
;
Galeano-Rios et al. 2019a
;
Galeano-Rios et al. 2021
)
. The KM model does not take into account the thin gas film, replacing it with an infinitely thin contact surface that transmits the pressure between the fluid bodies. Although providing remarkable agreement in the low to moderate Weber number range for the weakly viscous regime, the implementations were focused on rigid bodies impacting a fluid bath.

Alventosa et al. 2023
recently implemented a simplified version of the KM (1PKM) model that allowed both impacting bodies to deform, via orthogonal mode decompositions, and imposed contact at a single point. Their model for drop–bath impacts captured detailed characteristics of non-coalescing bounces close to the inertio-capillary regime, including trajectories, coefficient of restitution, contact times, and maximum deflection, in close agreement with experiments and DNS. They further showed that the approach remains accurate over a broad parameter space with no adjustable parameters, the only assumption being a polynomial profile for the pressure due to contact.

Phillips et al. 2024
and
Phillips & Milewski 2024
formulated models that include the thin-layer gas film to solve for the pressure contact of a deformable droplet impacting against a fluid bath. These models are particularly well-suited for weakly viscous flows impacted at low speeds, and were validated in two-dimensions against direct numerical simulations of the Navier Stokes equations, and in three-dimensions for rigid body impacts. Other notable reduced-order modelling attempts introduced droplet deformation
(
Blanchette 2016
;
Blanchette 2017
)
, first using a single vertical spring coupled to a linear bath response and later an octahedral spring network calibrated to the natural oscillation modes of a drop; demonstrating analogues that can capture key features of bouncing dynamics, including coefficient of restitution and contact time.

Existing applications of the full KM model are designed from first principles and have, so far, treated droplets impacting a free surface as approximately rigid
(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2019b
)
. They were generally able to predict details about the impact, such as coefficient of restitution, contact time, and surface pressure profile during contact. However, its use for detailed impact dynamics for cases when the droplet deformation is non-negligible represented an ongoing challenge
(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2019b
)
.

In this manuscript, we introduce a full KM model that allows for both the droplet and the bath surface to deform upon impact. It follows the same weakly viscous bath model as in
Galeano-Rios et al. 2021
and
Alventosa et al. 2023
and imposes full contact kinematic match conditions, allowing droplet deformations by spectrally decomposing the representation of the surface of the droplet in spherical coordinates as a sum of Legendre polynomials, as in
Phillips & Milewski 2024
and
Alventosa et al. 2023
. This new model yields detailed predictions for the evolution of the pressure field and pressed area without the need to solve the air layer dynamics. We also extend available experimental measurements to the submillimetric, very-low impact velocity regime near the lower rebound limit, reporting rebound metrics such as contact time, penetration depth, and coefficient of restitution. These data fill a still sparse region of parameter space and offer benchmarks for validating models of deformable droplet–bath impacts.

2
Problem formulation

We consider the three-dimensional incompressible flow of a fluid bath of infinite depth and infinite lateral extension, which is initially at rest. The fluid has density
\rho
, kinematic viscosity
\nu
, and surface tension
\sigma
. At time
t=0
, the undisturbed free surface of the bath is subject to a non-coalescing normal impact by a droplet of density
\rho_{d}
, kinematic viscosity
\nu_{d}
and surface tension
\sigma_{d}
. The interface of the droplet is assumed to be spherical at the moment of first contact, having a radius
R_{d}
and zero relative surface velocity with respect to the centre of mass of the droplet.

We use cylindrical coordinates
(r,\varphi,z,t)
with the
z
pointing normally to the undisturbed free surface and away from the bath (i.e., gravity is given by
-g\hat{\bf{z}}
), with
z=0
corresponding to the undisturbed free surface, as illustrated in figure

1
. The axial symmetry of the problem around the
z
axis allows us to ignore the dependence on
\varphi
, as is henceforth done.

2.1
The bath model

We model the bath using a linearised, quasi-potential flow approximation, initially presented in
Lamb 1895
and revisited by
Dias et al. 2008
, and follow closely the notation and formulation presented in
Galeano-Rios et al. 2017
. This fluid model assumes that the free-surface elevation can be described by
z=\eta(r,t)
, and introduces a velocity potential
\phi(r,z,t)
, as well as the pressure on the free surface due to the effect of the impacting droplet
p(r,t)
. We further assume that the integrity of the separating air layer is maintained throughout the impacts and simplify our model by assuming that this air layer is infinitely thin and serves only to transfer pressure between the drop and the bath. Furthermore, the impact is assumed to be perfectly non-wetting, so that the contact angle where the free surface detaches from the drop is constant and equal to
180^{\circ}
at all times during contact.

We define
V_{0}
to be the impacting velocity of the droplet with the bath at
t=0
, when contact is initiated (i.e., the centre of mass of the droplet is at a distance
R_{d}
above the free surface).

Taking
R_{d}
,
T_{d}=\sqrt{\rho_{d}R_{d}^{3}/\sigma_{d}}
and
\rho_{d}R_{d}^{3}
to be the characteristic length, time and mass, respectively; we adopt the following dimensionless numbers

\Rey=\frac{R_{d}^{2}}{T_{d}\nu},\quad Bo=\frac{gT_{d}^{2}}{R_{d}},\quad O\kern-0.39993pth_{d}\,=\frac{\nu_{d}T_{d}}{R_{d}^{2}},\quad W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,=\frac{\rho_{d}V_{0}^{2}R_{d}}{\sigma_{d}},\quad D\kern-0.39993ptr\,=\frac{\rho_{d}}{\rho},\quad Sr=\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\sigma}.

(1)

That is, the Reynolds, Bond, Ohnesorge and Weber numbers, the density ratio (similar to an Atwood number) and the ratio of the surface tensions of the two fluids.
The non-dimensional quasi-potential formulation
(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2017
)
results in the following set of equations:

\partial_{t}\eta=\frac{2}{Re}\Delta_{H}\eta+\partial_{z}\phi,\quad z=0;

(2)

\phi_{t}=-B\kern-0.39993pto\,\eta+Sr\,Dr\,\kappa[\eta]+\frac{2}{\Rey}\Delta_{H}\phi-Dr\,p,\quad z=0;

(3)

which are subject to

\eta,\phi,|\nabla\phi|\to 0\ \text{as}\ r\to\infty,\ \text{for}\ z=0,

(4)

and

\eta(r,0)=0,\quad\forall r\geq 0,

(5)

\phi(r,z,0)=0,\quad\forall r\geq 0,\ z\leq 0.

(6)

Here,
\Delta_{H}=\partial_{rr}+(1/r)\partial_{r}
is the two-dimensional Laplacian (for axially symmetric functions), and
\kappa
is twice the (linearized) mean curvature operator. The function
\phi
is harmonic on its domain (
\Delta\phi=0,\ z\leq 0
), thus allowing for the definition of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for the Laplace problem in said domain. This operator is given by:

\partial_{z}\phi(r,z=0,t)=N\Phi(r,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B(r,\epsilon)}\frac{\Phi(r,t)-\Phi(s,t)}{|r-s|^{3}}dA(s)

(7)

(
Galeano-Rios et al. 2017
, see)
, in which, the correspondence
\{(x,y,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2}
has been made, and
\Phi(r,t)\coloneqq\phi(r,z=0,t)
. Therefore, equation (
7
), together with axial symmetry, reduces this three-dimensional problem to a one-dimensional configuration.

2.2
The droplet model

The droplet is subject to gravitational forces and to the pressure distribution from the air layer that separates the bath and droplet in the non-coalescing impact. The pressure on the droplet is assumed to be identical to the pressure on the bath, as the present model neglects any effects due to the dynamics of the intervening air layer and hence any shear stresses.

2.2.2
Droplet deformation

To analyse the droplet deformation, we use spherical coordinates
(\xi,\theta,\tau)
whose origin is at the centre of mass of the droplet. Figure
2
, shows that the spherical coordinates are based on the axes
x^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}
, which were chosen to be respectively parallel to the
r
and
z
axes from the cylindrical coordinates that are used to describe the fluid of the bath. That is

x^{\prime}=r,\ \ z^{\prime}=z-h(t),\ \ \xi=\sqrt{r^{2}+\left(z^{\prime}\right)^{2}},\ \ \theta=\arccos\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{\xi}\right).

(13)

Moreover, the axial symmetry of the problem implies that we can ignore the dependence on the azimuthal angle
\tau
.

We assume that the radial coordinate that describes the shape of the drop
\xi^{\prime}
can be written in terms of a perturbation away from a spherical shape,
\xi^{\prime}(\theta,t)=R_{d}+\zeta(\theta,t)
. We also adopt the hypothesis of small deformations, that is, terms of the order of
\zeta^{2}
are neglected, and we assume
\partial_{\theta}\zeta\ll 1
. We can thus express the surface perturbation as

\zeta(\theta,t)=\sum\limits_{l=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{A}_{l}(t)P_{l}(\cos(\theta)),

(14)

where
P_{l}
is the
l
-th order Legendre polynomial. These polynomials forms a complete set of orthogonal functions defined on the interval
[-1,1]
. The assumptions of small deformations and fluid incompressibility imply that the term
l=0
does not appear in equation (
14
). The term
l=1
is also absent from (
14
) because the centre of mass is fixed at
z^{\prime}=0
.

We also decompose the pressure distribution due to contact, obtaining

p\left(\theta,t\right)=\sum_{l\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{l}(t)P_{l}\left(\cos(\theta)\right).

(15)

We note that all terms
l\geq 0
appear in the sum in this case. We recover
B_{l}
from
p(\theta,t)
via

\mathcal{B}_{l}(t)=\frac{1}{2l+1}\int_{0}^{\pi}p(\theta,t)P_{l}(\cos(\theta))\sin(\theta)d\theta.

(16)

The surface modes satisfy the following set of equations
(
Alventosa et al. 2023
;
Lamb 1895
;
Phillips & Milewski 2024
;
Blanchette 2016
)
:

\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{l}=\mathcal{U}_{l},\text{ for }\ l\geq 2,

(17)

R_{d}^{3}\rho_{d}\dot{\mathcal{U}}_{l}=-\sigma_{d}l(l+2)(l-1)\mathcal{A}_{l}-2R_{d}\mu_{d}(2l+1)(l-1)\mathcal{U}_{l}-R_{d}^{2}l\mathcal{B}_{l},\text{ for }\ l\geq 2.

(18)

Where
\mu_{d}=\rho_{d}\nu_{d}
is the dynamic viscosity of the droplet. Observe that, when the pressure terms are zero in (
18
), the solution for the uncoupled surface modes of the drop are damped harmonic oscillators, with natural frequencies
w_{l}^{2}=l(l-1)(l+2)\sigma_{d}/\left(\rho_{d}R_{d}^{3}\right)

(
Lamb 1895
)
. This motivates the choice for characteristic time,

, in (
1

Using the same characteristic units as for the bath equations, the dimensionless form of equations (

) and (

) is

\dot{A}_{l}=U_{l},\text{ for }\ l\geq 2,

(19)

\dot{U}_{l}=-l(l+2)(l-1)A_{l}-2O\kern-0.39993pth_{d}\,(2l+1)(l-1)U_{l}-lB_{l},\text{ for }\ l\geq 2;

(20)

where
A_{l}=\mathcal{A}_{l}/R_{d}
,
U_{l}=\mathcal{U}_{l}T_{c}/R_{d}
,

, which are subject to

(21)

We note that equations (

(

2.3
The contact model

We define
r_{M}(t)
as the smallest distance from the
z
axis to the point on the surface of the droplet for which the plane that is tangent to the interface of the droplet is vertical. That is,
r_{M}(t)
is an upper bound for the maximum possible contact radius that a given drop shape can have when in contact with the fluid.

We can then introduce the function
z_{d}(r,t)
such that
h(t)+z_{d}(r,t)
describes the vertical height of the bottom section of the surface of the droplet for all
r\leq r_{M}(t)
, and we set
z_{d}(r,t)=\infty
for all other radii. Points on the axisymmetric surface of the drop are parameterised by
\xi(\theta,t)=\xi^{\prime}(\theta,t)/R_{d}
and
\theta
; where
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, and
t\geq 0
. Thus the function
z_{d}
is given by

z_{d}(r,t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\bigl\{\cos(\theta)\,\xi(\theta,t)\colon\sin(\theta)\,\xi(\theta,t)=r\bigr\},&r\leq r_{M}(t),\\[4.30554pt]
\infty,&r>r_{M}(t).\end{cases}

(22)

Given the definition above, we can express our KM constraints as

p(r,t)=0,\qquad r>r_{c}(t),t\geq 0,

(23)

\eta(r,t)=h(t)+z_{d}(r,t),\quad r\leq r_{c}(t),t\geq 0,

(24)

\eta(r,t)<h(t)+z_{d}(r,t),\quad r>r_{c}(t),t\geq 0,

(25)

and

\partial_{r}\eta(r,t)=\partial_{r}z_{d}(r,t),\quad\text{at}\ r=r_{c}(t),t\geq 0;

(26)

where the final constraint requires that the drop and the bath share the same tangent cone at the boundary of the contact area. In other words, the KM imposes that contact angle must be exactly
\pi
.

3
Discretisation and numerical approximation

We delineate herein the approximations that convert the continuous system above into a discrete set of evolution equations. We shall truncate the series of the surface deformation and velocity series at mode
M+1
, hence the summations are taken over
l=2,\ldots,M+1
. Spatially, we discretise

uniformly in intervals
\delta_{r}
, denoting

, for

. At the domain boundary,

We introduce an adaptive time step

,
B_{l}
,

and

We use superscripts to indicate time indices, and subscripts to indicate radial position.
We thus define

, and similarly
\phi^{k}_{i}
and

The vectors

,

,

in

whose components are given by the values for

are represented by the omission of the subscript. We define

as the approximant to

, and similarly for

and

.
Finally, we also define

as the approximant to

and similarly for
B_{l}^{k}
and
U^{k}_{l}
.
The vectors
A^{k}
,

and

in

whose components are given by the values for

are represented without subscripts.

Typically, we set

to guarantee sufficient resolution under the droplet and chose

(noting these are non-dimensionalised with the drop radius) which is large enough to prevent waves from reaching the boundary of the domain over the course of a rebound in order to mimic the behaviour of a bath of infinite lateral extension.

The pressed area

is a circular disk with radius
r_{c}(t)
(see figure

r_{c}^{k}=\delta_{r}(m(t^{k})-1/2)
if

,

otherwise, where the function

takes non-negative integer values and represents the number of contact points of the discretized system. The

term added to the expression of

reflects the assumption that the boundary of the contact area is found exactly at the mid-point between nodes
m(t^{k})-1
and
m(t^{k})

To find the value of

in our implicit finite difference scheme, we formulate a different system of equations for each possible number of contact points
q

, and set

to be equal to the value of q that best satisfies the restrictions imposed by (

Time derivatives are approximated using an implicit, variable-step backward difference formula of order 2 (VS-BDF2) scheme

(32)

(33)

and approximate all time derivatives by

\dot{f}(t^{k+1})\approx\dot{f}^{k+1}\coloneqq\frac{a^{k}f^{k+1}+b^{k}f^{k}+c^{k}f^{k-1}}{\delta_{t}^{k}}.

(34)

and

Two distinct features arise in the full KM predictions. First, as the droplet approaches its maximum spread, small oscillations in the radius of the contact area are visible. 4.2
Rebound metrics

To compare our simulation results to experimental values, we measure the contact time
t_{c}
as the difference between the times when the north pole of the droplet crosses

during descent and rebound; that is, following the experimental convention. Moreover, the coefficient of restitution
\alpha
is defined as the square root of the ratio of the rebound energy
E_{r}

to impact energy
E_{0}
:

\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{E_{r}}{E_{0}}}=\sqrt{\frac{g(h_{r}-R_{d})+0.5V_{r}^{2}}{0.5V_{0}^{2}}},

5
Discussion

This is the first work to implement the kinematic match method to a deformable impactor colliding with a liquid bath. Our results show that this method accurately reproduces our experimental results and experimental and numerical results found in the literature at reduced computational cost compared to the direct numerical simulations of
Alventosa et al. 2023
. A typical run of the full kinematic match takes 4 CPU hours, representing a twelvefold reduction compared to the average DNS runtime reported in
Alventosa et al. 2023
.

Our results demonstrate the usefulness and versatility of the kinematic match approach, when coupled with linearised fluid models, for problems involving complex contact dynamics, particularly in the low Weber number limit. This capability is especially relevant relevant for improving models of superwalkers
Valani et al. 2019
, other bouncing droplet phenomena where droplet deformation is essential, and naturally applies to problems where the liquid bath is replaced with an elastic medium
(
Agüero et al. 2022
)
. These findings encourage further exploration of the KM method, including its use with non-linear fluid models, extension to non-axisymmetric cases, and use within variational formulations.

A significant insight from the present work is that the non-linear problem of determining the pressed area between two deformable impactors can be successful addressed by dividing it into two iterative cycles. The external cycle explores the possible shapes of one impactor, while the internal cycle determines the extent of the pressed area. A remaining avenue for exploration is to reduce the iterative nature of the solution by parameterising the geometry and solving a non-linear system of equations in which the geometric non-linearities are absorbed into algebraic non-linearities of the parameterisation.

Lastly, this study offers new insights into the physics of droplet rebound on a liquid bath, enabling direct comparisons between impacts on rigid and liquid substrates at low Weber number. For small droplets on a liquid bath, the energy recovered during rebound remains nearly constant at moderate
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,
but increases when
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,\lesssim 1
for low
O\kern-0.39993pth_{d}\,

(
Zhao et al. 2011
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Alventosa et al. 2023
)
. We further show that at even lower
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,
,
\alpha
exhibits a rolloff that depends critically on
B\kern-0.39993pto\,
, analogous to behavior observed for droplets impacting non-wetting substrates
(
Gabbard et al. 2025
)
. However, our experiments and model reveal that a deformable substrate shifts this rolloff to significantly higher
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,
, suggesting that substrate compliance may limit droplet transport via bouncing. The contact time
t_{c}
scales with
T_{d}

(
Zhao et al. 2011
;
Alventosa et al. 2023
)
similar to droplets bouncing on a rigid substrate
(
Richard et al. 2002
)
, but shifted toward longer interaction times. At lower
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,
, the contact time increases, with
t_{c}\rightarrow\infty
for large
B\kern-0.39993pto\,
droplets that can transiently float on the bath
(
Couder et al. 2005b
)
, while for low
B\kern-0.39993pto\,
contact time progressively increases until it exceeds the drainage time for the intervening air film, leading to coalescence. These results provide the most detailed characterization to date of submillimeter-sized droplet impacts in the low-
W\kern-1.49994pte_{d}\,
regime. The exceptional accuracy of the kinematic match model in this limit makes it a powerful predictive tool for a range of timely applications, including the spread of cough ejecta
(
Bourouiba 2021
)
and agricultural sprays
(
Makhnenko et al. 2021
)
along liquid surfaces.

A.1
Construction of
\mathbf{L}(\ell)

In the main text we introduced the matrix operator
\bm{\mathsfbi{L}}(\ell)
that projects the discrete pressure values

\{p(\theta_{i})\}
in the droplet onto the Legendre‐mode coefficients
B_{\ell}
. In this appendix we derive
\bm{\mathsfbi{L}}(\ell)
, rendering
B_{\ell}
as explicit linear combinations of the nodal pressures
p(\theta_{i})
. We begin by recalling the continuous projection

B_{\ell}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\pi}p(\theta)\,P_{\ell}\bigl(\cos\theta\bigr)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta,

(56)

We split the integral to be approximated and use the change of variables formula to get:

B_{l}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\int_{0}^{\pi}p(\theta)P_{l}(\cos(\theta))\cdot\sin(\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\int_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}p(x)P_{l}(x)dx.

(57)

Under the assumption that
p
is a piecewise linear function of
x=\cos(\theta)
on the original spherical coordinate system, we employ a piecewise linear representation,

p(x)=ax+b=p(\theta_{i+1})\frac{x-\cos(\theta_{i})}{\cos(\theta_{i+1})-\cos(\theta_{i})}+p(\theta_{i})\frac{x-\cos(\theta_{i+1})}{\cos(\theta_{i})-\cos(\theta_{i+1})}\ ,

where
a
and
b
are chosen so that
p(\cos(\theta_{i}))
is equal to the prescribed query values at
\cos(g(i\delta_{r},z^{k,j})):=cos(\theta_{i})
, where
g
is a function that translates from cylindrical coordinates to spherical coordinates. We can integrate each term in
57
exactly, resulting in a linear combination of the pressure values at the endpoints of each subinterval:

\int_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}p(x)P_{l}(x)dx=a\int_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}xP_{l}(x)dx+b\int_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}P_{l}(x)dx.

(58)

We compute these integrals exactly since the antiderivatives
F_{l}(x)=\int P_{l}(x)dx
and
G_{l}(x)=\int xP_{l}(x)dx
satisfy the following Bonnet-type recurrence relations:

(2l+1)P_{l}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(P_{l+1}(x)-P_{l-1}(x))\implies F_{l}=\frac{P_{l+1}-P_{l-1}}{2l+1},

(59a)

(l+1)P_{l+1}(x)=(2l+1)xP_{l}(x)-lP_{l-1}(x)\implies G_{l}=\frac{(l+1)F_{l}+lF_{l-1}}{2l+1},

(59b)

The estimation of
B_{l}
then becomes

B_{l}\approx\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\left(a_{i}G_{l}\bigg|_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}+b_{i}F_{l}\bigg|_{\cos(\theta_{i})}^{\cos(\theta_{i+1})}\right)

(60)

where

a_{i}=\frac{p(\theta_{i+1})}{\cos(\theta_{i+1})-\cos(\theta_{i})}+\frac{p(\theta_{i})}{\cos(\theta_{i})-\cos(\theta_{i+1})}\ ,

(61)

b_{i}=p(\theta_{i+1})\frac{-\cos(\theta_{i})}{\cos(\theta_{i+1})-\cos(\theta_{i})}+p(\theta_{i})\frac{-\cos(\theta_{i+1})}{\cos(\theta_{i})-\cos(\theta_{i+1})}\ ,

(62)

Approximating the legendre modes from the pressure values thus requires computing the value of
P_{l}(cos(\theta_{i}))
,
\forall l=2,\dots M+1
,
\forall i=0,\dots q
. This is done in linear time as a function of
q
and
M
, the only assumption being the linearity of the pressure as a function of
x=\cos(\theta)
. It can be noted that recurrence relations exist for antiderivatives of the form
\int x^{n}P_{l}(x)dx
too, thus allowing for integration of the pressure assuming piecewise polynomial behaviour of arbitrary order.

B.3
Numerical implementation considerations

To guarantee a physically continuous evolution of the pressed surface
S(t)
, we enforce a smooth update of the discretized contact radius via

\bigl\lvert r^{k+1}_{c}-r^{k}_{c}\bigr\rvert\leq\delta_{r}.

Whenever this criterion is violated, we halve the time step (
\delta_{t}^{k}\leftarrow\delta_{t}^{k}/2
) and discard all candidate solutions computed with the larger step. The search then restarts under the refined temporal resolution, ensuring that each update of the contact area proceeds without jumps.

The numerical implementation further ensures that surface waves do not reach the boundaries of the computational domain, preserving the validity of the imposed assumptions. Additionally, condition
|\nabla\eta|<1
is verified for all solutions at all times, ensuring the free surface slope remains within the linear limit consistent with the linearized curvature approximation adopted in equation (
27
).

As described abaove, time derivatives are discretized with a second-order backward difference formula (BDF2). To bootstrap BDF2, the first increment uses a first-order implicit Euler step, providing the two initial time levels needed for the higher-order scheme.

Integral operators
\mathbf{S}(q)
and
\bm{\mathsfbi{L}}(l)
used in equations (
38d
)and (
38h
) are implemented using a trapezoidal rule, as described in Appendix
A
.

Most of the simulations reported here use a spectral representation of the droplet with
M=20
modes (equation
14
). Moreover, all simulations were repeated with twice as many modes to ensure a relative change of at most 5% in all relevant variables.

To sum up, the full algorithm is laid out in Algorithm
2
. After initializing the solution and taking the first time step, the main loop generates multiple candidates (indexed by
q
), evaluates them against the tangent-error metric (
73
), and selects the optimal value of contact points to advance. Any overly rapid numerical change in
r^{k}_{c}
triggers the aforementioned time-step reduction and recomputation.
