Exact analysis of transient behavior of finite-capacity MAP-driven queues
Michel Mandjes, Daniël Rutgers and Werner Scheinhardt

Abstract
. This paper studies the workload distribution of a finite-capacity queue driven by a spectrally one-sided Markov additive process (MAP). Our main result provides the Laplace–Stieltjes transform of the workload at an exponentially distributed time, thereby uniquely characterizing its transient distribution. The proposed approach combines several decompositions with established fluctuation-theoretic results for spectrally one-sided Lévy processes. For the special case of Markov-modulated compound Poisson input, we additionally derive results for the idle time and the cumulative amount of lost work. We conclude this paper with a series of numerical experiments. Keywords. 1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the workload of a queue that is driven by a
spectrally-positive Markov additive process
(MAP)
Y\equiv(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, with the special feature that an upper boundary
K>0
is imposed on the workload level. Let
V\equiv(V(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the resulting doubly-reflected workload process, so that
0\leqslant V(t)\leqslant K
for all
t\geqslant 0
.
Our objective is to uniquely characterize, for any initial workload
x\in[0,K]
, the distribution of
V(t)
at an arbitrary time
t
. We achieve this characterization by identifying the distribution of
V(T_{\beta})
, where
T_{\beta}
is an exponentially distributed random variable with parameter
\beta>0
, sampled independently of everything else.
The driving process
Y
, initialized at
Y(0)=0
, is commonly referred to as the
free process
, or alternatively as the queue’s
input process
.

It is noted that within our finite-capacity framework, the above formulation also covers the case where
Y
is a spectrally-
negative
MAP. In this setting, the reflected process
(K-V(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is a doubly-reflected spectrally-positive MAP, and hence the results of this paper apply directly. This means that in this paper’s main results we characterize the workload distribution of a finite-capacity queue driven by a
spectrally one-sided
MAP.

Model.
The spectrally-positive MAP is defined as follows. Let
J\equiv(J(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
be a continuous-time Markov chain on
{\mathfrak{D}}:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
, which serves as the
background process
, or
modulating process
. We do not impose any structure on this Markov chain; in particular we do not require it to be irreducible. Its transition matrix is given by

Q\equiv(q_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d},

with the convention
q_{i}:=-q_{ii}=\sum_{j\neq i}q_{ij}
for any
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
. If the background process resides in state
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
, then the workload process is driven by a spectrally-positive Lévy process
Y_{i}\equiv(Y_{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, i.e., a Lévy process without negative jumps, that is characterized via its Laplace exponent
\varphi_{i}(\cdot)
. Examples of spectrally-positive Lévy processes are a Brownian motion with drift, a compound Poisson process or a combination of the two (but the class is considerably broader). In our model the workload can also increase at transitions of the background process: in case of a transition from
i
to
j\not=i
, a job arrives with a size that is distributed as the non-negative random variable
B_{ij}
with Laplace-Stieltjes transform (LST)
{\mathscr{B}}_{ij}(\cdot)
. The evident independence properties are imposed.

The process
V
, with initial condition
V(0)=x\in[0,K]
, is then the doubly-reflected version of
(x+Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, where we refer to
K
as the queue’s
capacity
. As discussed in detail in e.g.
[
3
, §1]
, this means that one can write, for any
t\geqslant 0
,

(1)

V(t)=x+Y(t)+U^{-}(t)-U^{+}(t),

where
U^{-}\equiv(U^{-}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
and
U^{+}\equiv(U^{+}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
are non-decreasing right-continuous processes, sometimes referred to as
regulators
, that solve the associated (double sided)
Skorokhod problem
. Specifically,
the process
U^{-}
can only increase when
V
is at the lower boundary 0, and
U^{+}
only
when
V
is at the upper boundary
K
.
Note that we work with
partial rejection
: if an arriving job does not fully fit into the buffer of capacity
K
, the workload level is truncated at
K
. It is crucial in our setup that the state space
{\mathfrak{D}}
of the background process is
finite
. MAPs with a countable state space have been studied as well; see, for example, associated laws of large numbers and central limit theorems in
[
24
]
.

Objective.
This paper aims to evaluate, for all
i,j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
and
x\in[0,K]
, the LST

(2)

\chi_{ij}(x)\equiv\chi_{i,j}(x,\alpha,\beta):=\mathbb{E}_{x,i}\left(e^{-\alpha V(T_{\beta})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right),

for
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0;
the subscripts in the rightmost expression indicate that the initial condition is
V(0)=x
and
J(0)=i.
This object uniquely describes the distribution of the queue’s transient workload. More particularly, it can, up to a multiplicative constant, be seen as a double transform; indeed, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\chi_{ij}(x)}{\beta}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{\beta}t}\,\mathbb{E}_{x,i}\left(e^{-\alpha V(t)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{J(t)=j\}}\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{y\in[0,K]}e^{-\alpha y-\beta t}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{x,i}\left(V(t)\in{\rm d}y,J(t)=j\right)\,{\rm d}t.

This means that the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{x,i}\left(V(t)\in{\rm d}y,J(t)=j\right)
can be found
[
1
,
14
]
from the transform
\chi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)
by numerical Laplace inversion (with respect to
\alpha
and
\beta
, that is); it is noted that
[
14
]
provides a technique particularly suited to handle the
double
inversion.

Literature.
We proceed by giving a non-exhaustive overview of the related literature.
Queues without a cap on the workload have been much more intensively studied than their finite-capacity counterparts, but the latter topic has attracted substantial attention, too. Within the branch of the literature on finite-capacity queues, early work focused on the characterization of the stationary workload distribution of M/G/1-type systems
[
9
,
31
]
, with subsequent studies addressing the time-dependent case
[
28
,
29
]
. In later work, the finite-capacity queue was cast into the more general framework of a stochastic process under double-sided Skorokhod reflection
[
4
,
13
,
20
]
; in this framework the finite-capacity M/G/1 queue is interpreted as a compound Poisson process with upward jumps and negative drift upon which double reflection has been imposed. An in-depth survey on the broader class of doubly-reflected Lévy processes can be found in
[
3
]
.

Most of the literature on the workload in the Markov-modulated compound Poisson-fed queue pertains to the case of infinite-capacity and stationarity. There is a one-to-one correspondence to an associated Cramér-Lundberg model under regime switching, which renders the results of e.g.
[
10
,
11
,
19
]
directly applicable; see also the general frameworks
[
12
,
19
]
and the textbook treatments in e.g.
[
6
,
7
]
. The stationary distribution in the finite-capacity case is briefly discussed in
[
3
, §16]
. Regarding the workload at an exponentially distributed time, we explicitly mention the contributions
[
15
]
, which studies Markov-modulated Brownian motion under double reflection, and
[
10
, §3.3]
, which treats MAPs under double reflection in the special case that the workload process starts at level
0
or
K
and that the background process is irreducible.

Contributions.

Our work is novel in that it combines the elements of (i) spectrally one-sided Markov additive input, without imposing any requirements on the chain structure of the underlying background process, (ii) finite capacity (i.e., a cap on the workload), (iii) time-dependent behavior, and (iv) a general starting level. In more detail, the contributions of this paper are the following:

\circ

We succeed in providing a procedure that produces, based on a series of carefully constructed decompositions, for any
i,j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
and
x\in[0,K]
, the LST

\chi_{ij}(x)
as defined in (
2
), in terms of the
2d^{2}
constants
\chi_{ij}(0)
and
\chi_{ij}(K)
. We then provide a system of linear equations that uniquely characterizes these constants.
Notably, in our analysis we allow for background states to be ‘subordinator states’, i.e., states
i
for which the underlying Lévy process
Y_{i}
is almost surely non-decreasing, for which we rely on ideas from
[
19
]
.

\circ

In the case that
Y
is a Markov-modulated compound Poisson process, we point out how this procedure can be generalized to also cover the total amount of work lost up to
T_{\beta}
, as well as the total amount of idle time up to
T_{\beta}
.

\circ

As a by-product, our approach can be used to compute the moments of the workload process and the probability that the system is empty at a given point in time. Both quantities can be obtained directly, without the need to derive them from the LST.

Organization.
The paper begins in Section
2
with a collection of results for the case
d=1
(that is, when
Y
is a spectrally-positive Lévy process), which are used extensively throughout the remainder of the paper. In Section
3
, we express
\chi_{ij}(x)
in terms of a set of auxiliary objects, whose explicit evaluation is carried out in Section
4
. Section
5
then analyzes the cumulative amount of lost work and the total idle time up to
T_{\beta}
in the case where
Y
is of Markov-modulated compound Poisson type. Finally, numerical experiments are presented in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we consider the case
d=1
, that is, a workload process being fed by a single spectrally-positive Lévy process
Y
; cf. the analysis in
[
27
]
. We treat this Lévy-driven case first because several of the results obtained here will be used in later sections when analyzing the more general MAP-driven workload model.

In our approach a crucial role is played by the following stopping times: for
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\sigma(u_{-})

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:-Y(t)>u_{-}\}

\displaystyle\tau(u_{+})

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:Y(t)>u_{+}\}.

These stopping times should be interpreted as the first time the free process
Y
drops below the level
u_{-}
or
strictly
exceeds the level
u_{+}
, respectively.
We also define what we from here on call
hitting probabilities
, which include the expiration of the ‘exponential clock’
T_{\beta}
(a concept often referred to as ‘killing’). We define, for
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
with
u_{-}+u_{+}>0
and
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\delta_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma(u_{-})\leqslant\min\{\tau(u_{+}),T_{\beta}\}\right)

\displaystyle\delta_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau(u_{+})\leqslant\min\{\sigma(u_{-}),T_{\beta}\}\right).

The hitting probability
\delta_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
thus denotes the probability that the process
Y
drops below the negative level
-u_{-}
before it exceeds the positive level
u_{+}
and before being killed; the hitting probability
\delta_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
has an analogous interpretation.

Let
\varphi(\alpha):=\log\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha Y(1)}
denote the Laplace exponent of
Y
, and let
\psi(\cdot)
denote its right-inverse. The
(primary) scale function

W^{(\beta)}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is then defined as the function whose Laplace transform satisfies

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha y}W^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{\varphi(\alpha)-\beta},

for
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
whenever
\varphi(\alpha)>\beta
; we define
W^{(\beta)}(y):=0
for
y<0
. We also work with the
secondary scale function

Z^{(\beta)}(u):=1+\beta\int_{0}^{u}W^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

For a full discussion on the primary and secondary scale functions of spectrally one-sided Lévy processes, which play a key role in fluctuation theory, we refer to e.g.
[
23
, §VIII.3]
,
[
8
, §VII.2]
, and
[
22
]
. For more background on the numerical evaluation of scale functions, we refer to
[
30
]
.

We analyze in Subsection
2.1
the distribution of the workload at the exponentially distributed time
T_{\beta}
for an arbitrary initial workload, and in Subsection
2.2
the overshoot of the free process
Y
over level
u\geqslant 0
.

2.1.
Workload distribution

This subsection studies the probability density

(3)

\mathbb{P}_{x}(V(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y):=\mathbb{P}(V(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y\,|\,V(0)=x),

for
x\in[0,K]
and
y\in[0,K]
, and the corresponding LST

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(e^{-\alpha V(T_{\beta})}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\alpha V(T_{\beta})}\,\middle|\,V(0)=x\right)

for
x\in[0,K]
,
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
. We distinguish between the cases where the Lévy process
Y
is a subordinator (i.e., non-decreasing) and where it is not.

Non-subordinator case

We first consider the case in which
Y
is
not
a subordinator — for example, a compound Poisson process with negative drift between jumps or a Brownian motion. The hitting probabilities in this setting are well known from the literature and admit the following simple representations in terms of the scale functions.

Lemma 1
.

For
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
with
u_{-}+u_{+}>0
and
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\delta_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{(\beta)}(u_{+})}{W^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})},

\displaystyle\delta_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=Z^{(\beta)}(u_{+})-Z^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})\frac{W^{(\beta)}(u_{+})}{W^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})}.

Proof.

Follows immediately from
[
23
, Theorem 8.1]
; note that the result there is for spectrally-negative Lévy processes not necessarily starting at
0
, so minor adjustments are required.
∎

The following lemma states the main result of
[
18
]
and gives an expression for
\mathbb{P}_{x}(V(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y)
in terms of the scale functions.

Lemma 2
.

(i) For
x\in[0,K]
,
y\in(0,K]
and
\beta>0
,

(5)

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(V(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)=\left(\frac{Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{(\beta)}(K)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}W^{(\beta)}(y)-W^{(\beta)}(y-x)\right)\mathrm{d}y.

(ii) For
x\in[0,K]
and
\beta>0
,

(6)

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(V(T_{\beta})=0\right)=Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)\frac{W^{(\beta)}(0)}{W^{(\beta)}(K)}.

Using the expressions in Lemma
2
, the following lemma derives the LST (
4
) in terms of scale functions. The proof can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 3
.

For
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
,

(7)

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(e^{-\alpha V(T_{\beta})}\right)=\left(e^{-\alpha K}+\int_{0}^{K}\alpha e^{-\alpha y}\frac{W^{(\beta)}(y)}{W^{(\beta)}(K)}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)-\beta e^{-\alpha x}\int_{0}^{K-x}e^{-\alpha y}W^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Remark 1
.

A key result for queues with spectrally-positive Lévy input is what could be termed the ‘time-dependent version of the generalized Pollaczek-Khinchine formula’ (see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
). This result states that, in the setting of infinite capacity, the LST of the workload at time
T_{\beta}
is given by

(8)

\frac{\beta}{\beta-\varphi(\alpha)}\left(e^{-\alpha x}-\frac{\alpha}{\psi(\beta)}e^{-\psi(\beta)\,x}\right),

with as before
x
the initial workload level,
\varphi(\cdot)
the Laplace exponent of
Y
, and
\psi(\cdot)
its right inverse. Importantly, the expression (
7
) extends this classical result to the finite-capacity setting.

It is readily verified that (
8
) is consistent with (
7
) by taking the limit
K\to\infty
in (
7
). Indeed, from the definition of the scale function,

-\lim_{K\to\infty}\beta e^{-\alpha x}\int_{0}^{K-x}e^{-\alpha y}W^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\beta}{\beta-\varphi(\alpha)}e^{-\alpha x},

In addition,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}

\displaystyle\left(e^{-\alpha K}+\int_{0}^{K}\alpha e^{-\alpha y}\frac{W^{(\beta)}(y)}{W^{(\beta)}(K)}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\to\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-\alpha y}\frac{W^{(\beta)}(\min(y,K))}{W^{(\beta)}(K)}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha y}\,W^{(\beta)}(y)\,{\rm d}y\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{\beta}(K)}=-\frac{\alpha}{\beta-\varphi(\alpha)}\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{\beta}(K)}.

By
[
22
, Lemma 3.3]
,
W^{(\beta)}(y)e^{-\psi(\beta)\,y}\to 1/\varphi^{\prime}(\psi(\beta))
and
Z^{(\beta)}(y)/W^{(\beta)}(y)\to\beta/\psi(\beta),

so that

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{\beta}(K)}=\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{Z^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{\beta}(K-x)}\frac{W^{(\beta)}(K-x)}{W^{\beta}(K)}=\frac{\beta}{\psi(\beta)}e^{-\psi(\beta)\,x}.

Adding up the two contributions, we readily obtain (
8
).
\Diamond

Subordinator case

For the remainder of this subsection, we assume
Y
is a subordinator, i.e., almost surely non-decreasing. Examples include the compound Poisson process with non-negative drift and the gamma process. For details on subordinators, see
[
23
, §2.6.2]
.
Again, our goal is to characterize the distribution of
V(T_{\beta})
when starting at the boundaries
0
or
K
.
We note the subtlety that
Y(T_{\beta})
may now have one or more atoms, as is the case, for example, with a compound Poisson process with no drift and discrete jumps.

The following two lemmas are straightforward and included for completeness. Note that since
Y
is non-decreasing, we have
\sigma(u_{-})=\infty
almost surely for any
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
. As a consequence,
\delta_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)\equiv 0
and
\delta_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)=\mathbb{P}(\tau(u_{+})\leqslant T_{\beta})
.

Lemma 4
.

(i)
For
y\in[0,K)
and
\beta>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(V(T_{\beta})\leqslant y\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y(T_{\beta})\leqslant y-x\right).

(ii)
For
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(V(T_{\beta})=K)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y(T_{\beta})\geqslant K-x\right).

Lemma 5
.

For
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(e^{-\alpha V(T_{\beta})}\right)=e^{-\alpha K}\mathbb{P}\left(Y(T_{\beta})\geqslant K-x\right)+e^{-\alpha x}\int_{[0,K-x)}e^{-\alpha y}\,\mathbb{P}\left(Y(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y\right).

2.2.
Overshoot transform

We next consider the overshoot of the Lévy process
Y
over a level
u\geqslant 0
and analyze its LST on the event it crosses
u
before killing:

\eta(u,\alpha,\beta):=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\alpha(Y(\tau(u))-u)}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\tau(u)\leqslant T_{\beta}\}}\right)

for
\alpha\geqslant 0,\beta>0
and
u\geqslant 0
. We analyze
\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)
by evaluating the associated Laplace transform with respect to
u
: for
\gamma\geqslant 0
,

(9)

\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u}\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Let
\bar{Y}(t):=\max\{Y(s):s\in[0,t]\}
denote the running maximum of
Y
until time
t
. For
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, let
\mathscr{Y}(\alpha,\beta)
denote the LST of
\bar{Y}(T_{\beta})
. By e.g.
[
11
, Section 16.3]
,

(10)

\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\frac{\mathscr{Y}(\gamma,\beta)}{\gamma-\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}(\gamma,\beta)}-\frac{1}{\mathscr{Y}(\alpha,\beta)}\right).

In the following two lemmas we present closed-form expressions for
\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, distinguishing between subordinators and non-subordinators.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
Y
is not a subordinator. For
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
,
\beta>0
and
u\geqslant 0
,

(11)

\displaystyle\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta-\varphi(\gamma)}\left(\frac{\varphi(\alpha)-\varphi(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}-\frac{\varphi(\alpha)-\beta}{\psi(\beta)-\alpha}\right),

(12)

\displaystyle\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha u}\left(1-(\varphi(\alpha)-\beta)\int_{0}^{u}e^{-\alpha y}W^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)+\frac{\varphi(\alpha)-\beta}{\psi(\beta)-\alpha}W^{(\beta)}(u).

Proof.

By
[
11
, Equation (15.5)]
,

\mathscr{Y}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\psi(\beta)-\alpha}{\beta-\varphi(\alpha)}\frac{\beta}{\psi(\beta)},

Substituting this into (
10
) and simplifying yields (
11
). The validity of (
12
) follows by verifying that its Laplace transform with respect to
u
equals (
11
). Since
\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)
is continuous for all
u\in[0,\infty)
in the non-subordinator case, it is uniquely determined by
\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Suppose
Y
is a subordinator. For
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
,
\beta>0
and
u\geqslant 0
,

(13)

\displaystyle\zeta(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta-\varphi(\gamma)}\frac{\varphi(\alpha)-\varphi(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha},

(14)

\displaystyle\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta-\varphi(\alpha)}{\beta}e^{\alpha u}\int_{(u,\infty)}e^{-\alpha y}\mathbb{P}(Y(T_{\beta})\in\mathrm{d}y).

Proof.

We now have
Y(T_{\beta})=\bar{Y}(T_{\beta})
, so that trivially
\mathscr{Y}(\alpha,\beta)={\beta}/({\beta-\varphi(\alpha)})
.
It is easy to verify that substituting this LST into (
10
) yields (
13
). Transforming (
14
) with respect to
u
yields (
13
). Here it should be noted that transforming a similar expression as in (
14
), but integrating the probability measure over
[u,\infty)
instead, yields (
13
) as well. However, since
\tau(u)
denotes the first time
Y

strictly
exceeds the level
u
,
\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)
has to be right-continuous in all
u\in[0,\infty)
and therefore (
14
) is the correct characterization of
\eta(u,\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

3.
Analysis

In this section we present our main results. We express the transform
\chi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)
in terms of a number of auxiliary objects, which we subsequently identify in Section
4
.
We follow a similar line of reasoning as in
[
26
, Exercise 5.5]
, noting that there are various additional challenges arising from the fact that in this paper we work with a general dimension
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and spectrally-positive Lévy processes, including subordinators. We also remark in passing that the analysis in
[
26
, Exercise 5.5]
contains a minor, easily correctable flaw, which does not affect the overall approach.

Remark 2
.

It turns out that our framework can be extended relatively easily to allow for
state-dependent killing
; see, for example,
[
26
, §3.1]
. In this setting, we work with a killing time
T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is now a vector, with
\beta_{i}>0
for all
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
.
Conditional on the background process being in state
i
,
T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is exponentially distributed with parameter
\beta_{i}
.

\Diamond

In what follows, all matrices are of dimension
d\times d
, unless stated otherwise. E.g., we define the matrix
\boldsymbol{\chi}(x)
which has entries
\chi_{ij}(x)\equiv\chi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta),\ i,j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
.

3.1.
Decomposition

We condition on the first ‘event’: this can be (i) hitting zero, (ii) hitting
K
, or (iii) the killing at time
T_{\beta}
. We therefore define two

3.2.
Finding

Bearing in mind how we have rewritten

We define the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\alpha,\beta)
through its entries: for
\alpha\geqslant 0,\beta>0

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right),

recalling that

In addition, with the Laplace exponent

Furthermore, observing that

\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
via, for
\alpha\geqslant 0,\beta>0

\eta_{ij}(u,\alpha,\beta):=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha(Y(\tau(u))-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(u)\leqslant T_{\beta},\,J(\tau(u))=j\}}\right).

This quantity is the LST of the overshoot, on the event that level

For now, we assume that

Lemma 9
.

For
\alpha\geqslant 0,\beta>0
and
x\in[0,K]
,

(19)

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x)=e^{-\alpha x}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-e^{\alpha x}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(x)-e^{-\alpha(K-x)}\boldsymbol{\eta}(K-x,\alpha,\beta)+e^{-\alpha(K-x)}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(x)\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(K,\alpha,\beta)\right)\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\alpha,\beta).

Proof.

We start by decomposing
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x)
. It is readily verified that

(20)

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x)=e^{-\alpha x}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\alpha,\beta)-{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)-{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)\right),

where the matrices
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
have entries

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\sigma(x)\leqslant\min\{\tau(K-x),T_{\beta}\},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right),

\displaystyle\Psi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\tau(K-x)\leqslant\min\{\sigma(x),T_{\beta}\},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right).

Hence, to analyze
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x)
, we may consider all sample paths of the free process
Y
except those on which the level
-x
or
K-x
is reached first, before
T_{\beta}
. We proceed by analyzing (i)
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
and (ii)
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
.

(i) By the strong Markov property, we find that the entries of
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
are simply given by

\displaystyle\Xi_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)=e^{\alpha x}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{-,ik}(x)\,{\Phi}_{kj}(\alpha,\beta).

The reasoning for this decomposition is that in this scenario the level
-x
is reached (with equality, due to the absence of negative jumps) before
T_{\beta}
and
\tau(K-x)
, where the summation over

takes care of the possible states of the background process at time
\sigma(x)
.

(ii) We further decompose
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
by considering all paths in which the level
K-x
is reached before
T_{\beta}
except those in which the level
-x
is reached before the level
K-x
. This gives

(21)

{\Psi}_{ij}(x,\alpha,\beta)={\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\tau(K-x)\leqslant T_{\beta},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right)-{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\sigma(x)\leqslant\tau(K-x)\leqslant T_{\beta},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right).

The first component in the right-hand side of (

{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\tau(K-x)\leqslant T_{\beta},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right)=e^{-\alpha(K-x)}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\eta_{ik}(K-x,\alpha,\beta)\,\Phi_{kj}(\alpha,\beta),

since in this case the level
K-x
is reached before

, with

and

accounting for the background state and the overshoot at time

The second component in the right-hand side of (

{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha Y(T_{\beta})}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{\sigma(x)\leqslant\tau(K-x)\leqslant T_{\beta},\,J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\delta_{-,ik}(x)\,e^{-\alpha(K-x)}\eta_{k\ell}(K,\alpha,\beta)\,\Phi_{\ell j}(\alpha,\beta),

where we argue that, in this case, the paths first reach level
-x
(with equality) and subsequently reach level
K-x
(or, equivalently, level
K
in a system shifted by
x
and starting at
0
) before
T_{\beta}
.

Inserting the found expressions for
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)
into (

\boldsymbol{\Psi}(x,\alpha,\beta)

{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(x,\alpha,\beta)=e^{-\alpha(K-x)}\left(\boldsymbol{\eta}(K-x,\alpha,\beta)-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(x)\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(K,\alpha,\beta)\right)\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\alpha,\beta)

Applying the identity (

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x)
in a straightforward manner.

Finding

Lemma 10

For
\alpha\geqslant 0
and

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(0)\\

Lemma 11
.

For any
\beta>0
, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{P}}
is strictly diagonally dominant.

Proof.

We are to show that
(\boldsymbol{P}^{\{0,0\}}+\boldsymbol{P}^{\{0,K\}}){\boldsymbol{1}}<{\boldsymbol{1}}
as well as
(\boldsymbol{P}^{\{K,0\}}+\boldsymbol{P}^{\{K,K\}}){\boldsymbol{1}}<{\boldsymbol{1}}
, where these inequalities are meant in the component-wise sense. We focus on the former claim, as the latter claim is proven analogously. Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigl(P^{\{0,0\}}_{i\ell}+P^{\{0,K\}}_{i\ell}\bigr)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{[0,K]}\mathbb{P}_{0}\!\left(\min\{V_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})+B_{ik},K\}\in{\rm d}y\right)\bigl(\delta_{-,k\ell}(y)+\delta_{+,k\ell}(y)\bigr)

for any
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
.
Observe that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\delta_{-,k\ell}(y)+\delta_{+,k\ell}(y)\leqslant 1
for any
k\in{\mathfrak{D}}
and any
y\in[0,K]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigl(P^{\{0,0\}}_{i\ell}+P^{\{0,K\}}_{i\ell}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{[0,K]}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\min\{V_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})+B_{ik},K\}\in\mathrm{d}y\right)=\frac{q_{i}}{q_{i}+\beta}<1

for any
\beta>0
.
This proves that
\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}
is strictly diagonally dominant.
∎

The matrix
\boldsymbol{\chi}(x)

4.
Computing auxiliary objects

To complete the analysis, we determine several objects introduced in the previous section, namely: (i) the overshoot transform
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
via its transform in

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(x)
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{+}(x)
using the

framework.
We distinguish between background states that are subordinators and those that are not. Let
d_{-}\leqslant d
denote the number of non-subordinators and set

{\mathfrak{D}}={\mathfrak{D}}_{-}\cup{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}

4.1.
Overshoot transform

The objective of this subsection is to uniquely characterize, for
\alpha,u\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, the overshoot transform matrix
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
defined in (
18
); for
d=1
this was already done in Section
2.2
.
We analyze
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
by evaluating the associated transform, for
\gamma\geqslant 0

\zeta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u}\,\eta_{ij}(u,\alpha,\beta)\,{\rm d}u.

In the following two subsections we distinguish between whether the state

is a subordinator state or not and derive
\zeta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
for both cases.

Non-subordinator states

We first focus on states

and set up a system of equations for the entries of
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
. Following the approach of
[
26
, §1.3]
, we condition on the first event (which is either a background-state transition or killing). Applying the
Wiener-Hopf decomposition

[
23
, Chapter VI]
then yields the following ‘master equation’:

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}(u,\alpha,\beta)=\>\>\>\>\>

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\alpha(Y_{i}(\tau_{i}(u))-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau_{i}(u)\leqslant T_{\omega_{i}}\}}\right)\,+

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}\frac{q_{ij}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{0}^{u}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})-Y_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}z\right)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\cdot\int_{u-(y-z)}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ij}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,e^{-\alpha(y-z+\nu-u)}\,+

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{0}^{u}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})-Y_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}z\right)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\cdot\int_{0}^{u-(y-z)}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ik}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,\eta_{kj}(u-(y-z+v),\alpha,\beta),

where
\bar{Y}_{i}
denotes the running maximum process pertaining to

. Note that, using that

is spectrally-positive,

is exponentially distributed with parameter
\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})
, where
\psi_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the right inverse of
\varphi_{i}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}

\displaystyle(u,\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\alpha(Y_{i}(\tau_{i}(u))-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau_{i}(u)\leqslant T_{\omega_{i}}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>+{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}\frac{q_{ij}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{0}^{u}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})\,e^{-\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})z}\int_{u-(y-z)}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ij}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,e^{-\alpha(y-z+\nu-u)}\,{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>+\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{0}^{u}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})\,e^{-\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})z}

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\cdot\int_{0}^{u-(y-z)}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ik}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,\eta_{kj}(u-(y-z+v),\alpha,\beta)\,{\mathrm{d}}z.

Next, we multiply the equation by

and integrate over

.
Recall, from the proof of Lemma

{\mathbb{E}}\,e^{-\alpha\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega})}=\frac{\psi_{i}(\omega)-\alpha}{\omega-\varphi_{i}(\alpha)}\frac{\omega}{\psi_{i}(\omega)},

where
\varphi_{i}(\alpha)
denotes the Laplace exponent of the Lévy process corresponding to background state

. We use Lemma
6
to analyze the Laplace transform of the first term of the master equation. For the transform of the second and third term, we apply a number of changes of variables, and in addition we interchange the order of integration, so as to obtain

\displaystyle\zeta_{ij}

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)={\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\frac{1}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\left(\frac{\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}-\frac{\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\omega_{i}}{\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>+{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}\frac{q_{ij}}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\left(\frac{\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\alpha)-\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}-\frac{\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\alpha)-\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))}{\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})-\alpha}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>+\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\left(\mathscr{B}_{ik}(\gamma)\zeta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)-\mathscr{B}_{ik}(\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))\zeta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))\right),

for any
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
. A full proof of the derivation of this Laplace transform is given in Appendix
A.1
.

Subordinator states

We now consider states

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}(u,\alpha,\beta)=\>

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\alpha(Y_{i}(\tau_{i}(u))-u)}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau_{i}(u)\leqslant T_{\omega_{i}}\}}\right)\,+

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}\frac{q_{ij}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{[0,u]}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{(u-y,\infty)}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ij}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,e^{-\alpha(y+\nu-u)}\,+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}}\int_{[0,u]}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega_{i}})\in\mathrm{d}y\right)\int_{[0,u-y]}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{ik}\in\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\,\eta_{kj}(u-(y+v),\alpha,\beta).

We again take the Laplace transform with respect to
u
. Using Lemma
7
from Section
2.2
, the Laplace transform of the first term directly follows. By interchanging the order of integration in the second and third terms, so that the integral with respect to
u
becomes the innermost one, and by applying the change of variables
u\to w:=u-y+\nu
, we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta_{ij}

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)={\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\frac{1}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\frac{\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}\,+

(24)

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}\frac{q_{ij}}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\frac{\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\alpha)-\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}+\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}\frac{q_{ik}}{\omega_{i}-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}\,\mathscr{B}_{ik}(\gamma)\,\zeta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),

where we used that in this subordinator case we have
\mathbb{E}\,e^{-\alpha\bar{Y}_{i}(T_{\omega})}={\omega}/({\omega-\varphi_{i}(\alpha)})
for any
\omega>0
.

Solving the system of equations

The next step is to rewrite the full system, (
4.1
) and (
24
), in a more convenient form by moving all linear combinations of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
to the left-hand side and the remaining terms to the right. This yields, for suitably defined functions
\kappa_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
,

(\varphi_{i}(\gamma)-\omega_{i})\,\zeta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+\sum_{k\neq i}q_{ik}\mathscr{B}_{ik}(\gamma)\,\zeta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\kappa_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).

Introducing the compact notation, for
j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
,

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\equiv(\zeta_{1j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),\ldots,\zeta_{dj}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma))^{\top},\>\>\>{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\equiv(\kappa_{1j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),\ldots,\kappa_{dj}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma))^{\top},

the system of equations can be written in matrix-vector form. With the matrix
\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)
as has been defined in (
17
), we thus obtain, for any
j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
,

(25)

(\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)-\beta\boldsymbol{I})\,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)={\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).

Observe that, for given
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, the quantities
\eta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))
are unknown constants. For that reason, we split
\kappa_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
into (i) a term that is fully expressed in terms of the model primitives, and that depends on
\alpha
and
\gamma
, and (ii) a term that depends on these unknown quantities and
\alpha
and
\beta
.
More concretely, we write

\kappa_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma):=\check{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\gamma)+\bar{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)
, with

\check{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\gamma):=-{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\frac{\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\varphi_{i}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}-{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}q_{ij}\frac{\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\alpha)-\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\gamma)}{\gamma-\alpha}

for
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
, and

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:={\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i=j\}}\frac{\varphi_{i}(\alpha)-\omega_{i}}{\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})-\alpha}+{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\{i\neq j\}}q_{ij}\frac{\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\alpha)-\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))}{\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})-\alpha}

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>\>+\sum_{k=1,k\neq i}^{d}q_{ik}\mathscr{B}_{ik}(\psi_{i}(\omega_{i}))\,\zeta_{kj}(\alpha,\beta,\psi_{i}(\omega_{i})),

for
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
, and
\bar{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)=0
for
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}
.
In evident notation, the following result collects the above findings.

Proposition 1
.

For
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
,

(\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)-\beta\boldsymbol{I})\,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\beta)+\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\gamma).

Interestingly, the matrix
\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\gamma)
satisfies a compact form, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 12
.

For
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
,

(26)

\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\gamma)=\frac{\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)-\boldsymbol{F}(\alpha)}{\gamma-\alpha}.

Proof.

This is easily verified by breaking down the right-hand side of (
26
) using (
17
).
∎

In order to uniquely characterize
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, we are left with determining, for given
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, the unknown constants
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\beta)
. From this point on we fix a background state
j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
. In the sequel we denote by
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)
the matrix
\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)-\beta\boldsymbol{I}
, so that we have the system

\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma).

The procedure followed to identify
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
mimics the one
laid out in
[
26
, §3.3]
. We distinguish two steps.

(i) We start by applying Cramer’s rule for matrices, so as to write, for
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
,

\zeta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\frac{\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},i}(\beta,\gamma)}{\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)},

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},i}(\beta,\gamma)
is defined as the matrix
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)
with its
i
-th column replaced by the vector
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
; note that in the notation
\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},i}(\beta,\gamma)
we have suppressed the dependence on
\alpha
.

(ii) The next step is to analyze the roots of
\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)
for a fixed
\beta>0
. By
[
17
, Theorem 1]
, it follows immediately that, for each
\beta>0
,
\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}(\beta,\gamma)
has exactly
d_{-}
roots in the right half of the complex
\gamma
-plane; see also
[
19
, Lemma 3]
. We denote these roots by
\gamma_{1}(\beta),\dots,\gamma_{d_{-}}(\beta)
and assume them to be simple. The case of higher multiplicities can be handled using Jordan chains, for which we refer to the detailed treatment in D’Auria et al.
[
10
]
.

As
\zeta_{ij}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is finite for all
\gamma\geqslant 0
, any root of the denominator has to be a root of the numerator as well. This implies that we necessarily have

(27)

\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},i}\left(\beta,\gamma_{k}(\beta)\right)=0

for all
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}
and
k\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
; recall that we fixed
j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
.
Because
\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{j}(\alpha,\gamma)
is a known function of the model primitives, this means that we are left with finding the
d
entries of the vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta)
, of which only the first
d_{-}
are unknown.
Observe that, for our given
j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
, (
27
) seemingly yields
d\cdot d_{-}
equations (that are linear in the entries of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{j}(\alpha,\beta)
). It can be seen, however, that
d\cdot d_{-}-d_{-}
of these equations are essentially redundant: as argued in
[
26
, §III.3]
and
[
19
]
,
the
d\cdot d_{-}
equations can be thinned to just
d_{-}
equations that ‘contain all information’.
This concretely implies that we can simply focus on the subsystem given by the equations

(28)

\det\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},1}\left(\beta,\gamma_{k}(\beta)\right)=0

for
k\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
; these are
d_{-}
linear equations in
\bar{\kappa}_{ij}(\alpha,\beta)
,
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
.

We summarize the results of this subsection in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

For
\alpha,\gamma\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, the double transform
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is given by

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(\boldsymbol{F}(\gamma)-\beta\boldsymbol{I})^{-1}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\beta)+\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\gamma)\right),

where
\check{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\gamma)
is given by Lemma
12
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\alpha,\beta)
satisfies the system of linear equations in (
28
).

A consequence of this theorem is that, for
u\geqslant 0
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
can be numerically obtained by applying an inverse Laplace transformation algorithm to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
with respect to the parameter
\gamma
.

Remark 4
.

An alternative approach to deriving
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
in the absence of subordinators, that is, when
d=d_{-}
, is presented in
[
18
, Corollary 4]
. One can verify that this approach and ours yield the same overshoot transform
\boldsymbol{\eta}(u,\alpha,\beta)
by comparing their respective Laplace transforms with respect to
u
.

4.2.
Hitting probabilities

The remaining objective is to evaluate the hitting probability matrices
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
, which we introduced in the beginning of Section
3.1
. For the specific case that none of the Lévy processes are subordinators the evaluation of these hitting probability matrices is covered by
[
18
]
. In this subsection we show how those results can be extended using the results from Section
4.1
to cover the more general model which includes subordinators.

The analysis relies heavily on the (
primary
)
scale matrix
, which extends the primary scale function for spectrally-positive Lévy processes discussed in Section
2
to the matrix setting. The scale matrix is the matrix-valued function
\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(y):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{-}}
such that, for any
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha y}\,\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\alpha)-\beta\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}_{-}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},

recalling the definition of
\boldsymbol{F}(\alpha)
from (
17
), where
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{-}
denotes the
d_{-}\times d_{-}
identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{0}
a
d_{+}\times d_{-}
all-zeroes matrix. We denote the restriction of
\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(y)
to the first
d_{-}
rows by
\boldsymbol{W}_{-}^{(\beta)}(y)
.
For background on the existence of
\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(y)
and the invertibility of
\boldsymbol{W}_{-}^{(\beta)}(y)
, see e.g.
[
18
]
.

In the remainder of this subsection, we present expressions for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
in terms of the primary scale matrix. We start with the case
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
, which is simpler since hitting
-u_{-}
involves no undershoot in our spectrally-positive setup.

Lemma 13
.

For
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
, with
u_{-}+u_{+}>0
, and
\beta>0
,

(29)

{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{-}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})\right)^{-1},&\boldsymbol{0}\,\end{pmatrix},

where
\boldsymbol{0}
denotes a
d\times d_{+}
zero matrix.

Proof.

The expression for the first
d_{-}
rows and columns of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
follows directly from
[
18
, Theorem 1]
; this corresponds to the cases in which the background process is in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
both at time 0 and at time
\sigma(u_{-})
. Note that
[
18
]
considers a spectrally negative Markov additive process, with only downward jumps, whereas our setting involves upward jumps. Applying these results therefore requires a few elementary transformations. The remaining
d_{+}
columns are identically zero, since the process
Y
cannot drop below the level
-u_{-}
while
J
is in a subordinator background-state. Finally, the entries with
i\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{+},j\in{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}
[was: The rows with index in
{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}
] follow from
[
16
, §7.6]
.
∎

To derive an expression for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
, we use the matrix
\boldsymbol{P}_{+}(u,\beta)
denoting the probabilities of exceeding a level
u\geqslant 0
before killing, whose entries are defined by, for
u\geqslant 0,\beta>0
and
i,j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
,

(30)

P_{+,ij}(u,\beta):=\mathbb{P}_{i}\left(\tau(u)\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(u))=j\right).

Observe that
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u,\beta)={\boldsymbol{\eta}}(u,0,\beta)
, which is characterized in Theorem
2
via its Laplace transform
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(0,\beta,\gamma)
.
In the following lemma we express
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u,\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
.

Lemma 14
.

For any
u_{-},u_{+}\geqslant 0
with
u_{-}+u_{+}>0
and
\beta>0

(31)

{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)={\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u_{+},\beta)-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u_{-}+u_{+},\beta)

Proof.

We start by noting that we can alternatively write
\delta_{-,ij}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
and
\delta_{+,ij}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
as

\displaystyle\delta_{-,ij}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\beta\sigma(u_{-})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\sigma(u_{-})\leqslant\tau(u_{+}),\,J(\sigma(u_{-}))=j\}}\right)

\displaystyle\delta_{+,ij}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\beta\tau(u_{+})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(u_{+})\leqslant\sigma(u_{-}),\,J(\tau(u_{+}))=j\}}\right);

cf.
[
26
, Lemma 5.2]
.
As a consequence, by some elementary rearranging, we observe that the entries of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
obey

\displaystyle\delta_{+,ij}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\beta\tau(u_{+})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(u_{+})<\infty,\,J(\tau(u_{+}))=j\}}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(e^{-\beta\tau(u_{+})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\sigma(u_{-})<\tau(u_{+}),\,J(\tau(u_{+}))=j\}}\right),

where the first term on the right-hand side is simply
P_{+,ij}(u_{+},\beta)
.
We continue by analyzing the second term on the right side. Considering the individual entries of the matrix, we obtain, as a consequence of the strong Markov property,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}

\displaystyle\left[e^{-\beta\tau(u_{+})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\sigma(u_{-})<\tau(u_{+}),\,J(\tau(u_{+}))=j\}}\right]=\mathbb{P}_{i}(\sigma(u_{-})<\tau(u_{+})\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(u_{+}))=j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}(\sigma(u_{-})\leqslant\min\{\tau(u_{+}),T_{\beta}\},\,J(\sigma(u_{-}))=k)\,\mathbb{P}_{k}(\tau(u_{-}+u_{+})\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(u_{-}+u_{+}))=j).

In matrix-vector form this identity can be compactly written as

\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\beta\tau(u_{+})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(u_{+})>\sigma(u_{-}),\,J(\tau(u_{+}))\}}\right)={\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u_{-}+u_{+},\beta).

Upon combining the above we find the claimed statement.
∎

Remark 5
.

In the absence of subordinators, i.e.,
{\mathfrak{D}}_{-}={\mathfrak{D}}
, the evaluation of the hitting probability matrices for spectrally-negative MAPs was already covered by
[
18
]
. In this case the primary scale matrix
{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(\beta)}(y)
is a square
d\times d
matrix and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{-}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
does not have any zero columns as the level
-u_{-}
can be crossed in any state. In addition the secondary scale matrix is often considered as well, defined by

\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u):=\boldsymbol{I}-\int_{0}^{u}\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\left(Q-\beta\boldsymbol{I}\right).

It can be verified, by tedious calculations, that in this case
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(u,\beta)
can be expressed in terms of both scale matrices as

(32)

\boldsymbol{P}_{+}(u,\beta)=\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u)+\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u)\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(0,\beta)

by taking the Laplace transforms (with respect to
u
) of both sides and show that they are the same using Theorem
2
for
\alpha=0
.

Substituting (
32
) into (
31
) then yields for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})+\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(0,\beta)\>-

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})\left(\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})\right)^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})+\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(0,\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})-\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{+})\left(\boldsymbol{W}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+})\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\beta)}(u_{-}+u_{+}).

This result is consistent with
[
18
, Corollary 3]
, after incorporating killing and setting
\alpha=0
in the formulation given there. Interestingly, the above derivation reveals that in order to derive an expression for
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
we actually do not need to know the precise form of the matrix
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(0,\beta)
in (
32
), in that the only crucial property is that we have (
32
) for some matrix
\bar{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(0,\beta)
which does not depend on the level
u
.

5.
Idle time and lost work

When
Y
is a Markov-modulated compound Poisson process with strictly negative drifts, the workload experiences idle periods during which the system is empty, and any arriving work that exceeds the available capacity
K
is lost. In this section, we analyze the idle time and the amount of lost work in a finite-capacity queue driven by Markov-modulated compound Poisson input. Throughout,
Y_{i}
denotes a compound Poisson process with arrival rate
\lambda_{i}
, non-negative jump sizes distributed as a generic random variable
B_{i}
, and an added negative drift
r_{i}
.

Let
I\equiv(I(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the total idle time up to
t
, and
L\equiv(L(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
the cumulative lost work due to overshoots; both processes are inherently non-negative and non-decreasing.
In this section, we study the joint distribution of
I(T_{\beta})
and
L(T_{\beta})
along with the quantities considered previously. Specifically, for all
i,j\in{\mathfrak{D}}
and
x\in[0,K]
, our object of interest is the LST

(33)

\tilde{\chi}_{ij}(x)\equiv\tilde{\chi}_{i,j}(x,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta):=\mathbb{E}_{x,i}\left(e^{-\alpha_{1}V(T_{\beta})-\alpha_{2}I(T_{\beta})-\alpha_{3}L(T_{\beta})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{J(T_{\beta})=j\}}\right),

for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
,
\alpha_{k}\geqslant 0
for
k=1,2,3
, and
\beta>0;
the subscripts in the rightmost expression indicate, as before, that the initial condition is
V(0)=x
and
J(0)=i
.
In our analysis of this object we make use of the following overshoot LST: for any
u\in[0,K]
,

(34)

\tilde{\eta}_{ij}(u)\equiv\tilde{\eta}_{ij}(u,\alpha,\beta):={\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha\left(Y(\tau(u))-u\right)}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(u)\leqslant\min\{\sigma(K-u),T_{\beta}\},J(\tau(u))=j\}}\right).

It is noted that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(u,0,\beta)={\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(K-u,u,\beta)
.
In addition,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(u)
is closely related to the overshoot LST
\boldsymbol{\eta}(x)
defined in (
18
) and characterized in Section
4.1
. In particular, the following lemma shows that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(u)
can be expressed in terms of
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\cdot)
, similar to what was done in Lemma
14
for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{+}(u_{-},u_{+},\beta)
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{+}(\cdot,\beta)
.

Lemma 15
.

For
u\in[0,K],\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
,

(35)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(u)=\boldsymbol{\eta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(K-u)\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(K).

Proof.

As a first step, observe that we can decompose
\tilde{\eta}_{ij}(u)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{ij}(u)=\eta_{ij}(u)-{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left(e^{-\alpha\left(Y(\tau(u))-u\right)}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\sigma(K-u)<\tau(u)\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(u))=j\}}\right).

Applying the strong Markov property to the second term,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i}

\displaystyle\left(e^{-\alpha\left(Y(\tau(u))-u\right)}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\sigma(K-u)<\tau(u)\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(u))=j\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{-,ik}(K-u)\,{\mathbb{E}}_{k}\left(e^{-\alpha\left(Y(\tau(K))-K\right)}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\tau(K)\leqslant T_{\beta},J(\tau(K))=j\}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{-,ik}(K-u)\,\eta_{kj}(K).

The claimed statement (
35
) follows immediately.
∎

In the following lemma, which can be considered to be a counterpart of Lemma
8
, we show how
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(x)
can be decomposed in terms of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(0)
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(K)
. Note that at
x=0
(i.e., the workload process’ lower boundary) this decomposition trivially holds, as
{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(0)}
is simply
{\boldsymbol{I}}
and the entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(K,\alpha_{3},\beta)
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(0)
are all zero.

Lemma 16
.

For
\alpha\geqslant 0,\beta>0
and
x\in[0,K]
,

(36)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(x)=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{-}(x)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(0)+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(K-x,\alpha_{3},\beta)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(K)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\star}(x).

Proof.

By the strong Markov property we have

\tilde{\chi}_{ij}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{-,ik}(x)\,\tilde{\chi}_{kj}(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\tilde{\eta}_{ik}(K-x)\,\tilde{\chi}_{kj}(K)+\delta_{\star,ij}(x).

The validity of this identity can be justified as follows.
The first term on the right-hand side corresponds to the case in which the first event is hitting the level
0
. The second term corresponds to the case in which the first event is hitting the level
K
. In this case,
L(\cdot)
increases by the amount of the overshoot at time
\tau(K-x)
. Finally,
\delta_{\star,ij}(x)
denotes the case in which killing occurs before the process hits either
0
or
K
. In the first and the last case, both
I(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
do not increase up to
\sigma(x)
or
T_{\beta}
, respectively. Rewriting this decomposition in matrix notation yields
(
36
)
.
∎

Finally, we determine the
2d^{2}
entries in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(0)
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(K)
. To this end, we decompose
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(0)
, with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=\beta\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
In the setting considered, the driving process
Y
being a Markov-modulated compound Poisson process, we can distinguish between three types of events: the arrival of a job, a background-state transition, and killing. We thus obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{ij}(0)

7.
Discussion and concluding remarks

In this paper, we have developed a procedure to evaluate the LST of the workload for a doubly reflected spectrally one-sided MAP at an exponentially distributed time. The framework we consider is general in several important respects. In particular, no assumptions are imposed on the structure of the background process, which need not be irreducible. Moreover, the spectrally one-sided Lévy processes underlying the MAP are allowed to be subordinators. Finally, apart from the initial background state, also the initial workload may be chosen arbitrarily, so that the analysis does not require the system to start in an empty or full state.

Arguably, the most significant restriction of the setup considered is the spectrally one-sided nature of the driving MAP. A natural next step would be to address the case in which jumps in one direction have a general distribution, while jumps in the other direction are of phase-type or are assumed to have rational Laplace transforms. For the case of a singly-reflected Lévy process, this approach has been successfully implemented
[
25
]
. We also refer, for example, to
[
5
,
21
]
for illustrations of highly general classes of spectrally two-sided Lévy processes that may permit relatively explicit evaluation.

In the case where
Y
is a Markov-modulated compound Poisson process, we have also succeeded in analyzing the cumulative amounts of lost work and idle time (at killing). Another open problem remains the identification of the joint LST that, for
Y
being a general spectrally one-sided MAP, additionally includes the regulators
U^{-}(T_{\beta})
and
U^{+}(T_{\beta})
, as defined in (
1
); cf. the concept of
loss rate
in
[
3
]
.
