Dual‐Camera Arthroscopic Bone Block Cerclage Technique Using a Tricortical Iliac Crest Autograft for Glenoid Reconstruction in Patients With Anterior Shoulder Instability
Authors: Joshua C. Y. Ong, Rachel Jayesh Patel, Magnus Arnander, Eyiyemi Pearse, Duncan Tennent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArthroscopic‐assisted grafting of the anterior glenoid in instability surgery offers lower morbidity and fewer complications than open techniques but faces challenges in operative field visualization. The 1.9 mm NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) enables simultaneous arthroscopic views from opposing angles, providing anterior glenoid visualization without camera port switching. This reduces triangulation time and bleeding, and the small size also enables the NanoScope to remain in an anterior portal while additional instruments are inserted.

Dual‐camera arthroscopic bone block cerclage technique using a tricortical iliac crest autograft for glenoid reconstruction in patients with anterior shoulder instability. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70158.

The extent of glenoid bone loss significantly contributes to recurrent shoulder instability following soft‐tissue stabilization, making bony augmentation critical for substantial defects. 1 The threshold for grafting currently lies at approximately 15%. 2 , 3 The surgical options include either coracoid transfer or free bone graft which can be from a variety of sources. Clinical outcomes of iliac crest bone graft compared with the Latarjet procedure have similar outcomes. 4 , 5 Arthroscopic techniques have comparable graft positioning, defect coverage, and clinical outcomes to open techniques though arthroscopic approaches may have steeper learning curves and minor radiographic variances. 6 However, arthroscopic iliac crest bone graft allows smaller incisions and internal fixation risks. 6 Using the cerclage technique without reliance on metal implants reduces hardware failure, residual pain, or graft resorption. 7 , 8 Applications of needle arthroscopy has expanded since its introduction in 2019, but never in a simultaneous double‐camera technique. 9 , 10 , 11

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Setup

Under general anesthesia with interscalene block, the patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position with the operative extremity placed in 30° of abduction and neutral rotation.

A diagnostic arthroscopy is performed through a posterior and an anterior rotator interval portal to confirm the size and morphology of the glenoid defect and other concomitant pathologies that require attention (Video 1).

Capsulolabral Complex Release and Glenoid Preparation

Viewing from the posterior portal, the capsulolabral complex is mobilized. The anterior glenoid is debrided with a motorized rasp (PowerRasp, Arthrex, Naples, FL) to achieve a flat surface and induce bleeding for improved glenoid bone graft contact and osteointegration.

Once the glenoid has been prepared, it is necessary to view the neck to confirm that it is flat and the soft tissues have been released. To achieve this, a 1.9 × 125 mm chip‐on‐tip NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Figure 1) is introduced via an accessory anterior portal (typically superior). If further shaving is required, this can be done under direct vision using the primary rotator interval portal, leaving the NanoScope in situ as it is stable due to its relatively short length and light weight (Figure 2).

NanoScope (1.9 mm width, 125 mm length; Arthrex, Naples, FL) with 10° sheath, oriented away from the flow port.

Patient set up (lateral decubitus position, left shoulder, 30° arthroscope through posterior portal) 10° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) through anterior superior portal, not requiring support. Orientation depicted by Lego figurine.

Glenoid Drilling (Dual‐Camera Guidance)

An arthroscopic posterior drilling guide (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is introduced into the joint through an accessory posterior portal, and the hook component is placed on the glenoid neck defect with the appropriate offset of the cartilage surface. 12 Placement of the drilling guide requires visualization from both anterior and posterior. Through the anterosuperior portal, the NanoScope allows confirmation of hook position (Figure 3), subsequent drilling, and suture passing, negates the port switching if a standard arthroscope was used. Visualization of both the glenoid face and docking site is excellent, especially when coupled with a double‐window arthroscopic stack (Synergy Vision Imaging System, Arthrex, Naples, FL).

Glenoid preparation (lateral decubitus's position, left shoulder, 10° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) through anterior superior portal). The drill guide hook engages the anterior glenoid surface to direct the drill tips at the graft docking site.

Using the drill guide, 2 tunnels are drilled from the posterior scapula neck to the glenoid defect on the anterior scapula neck, with the NanoScope observing that the drill exit point matches the jig (Figure 4). After removal of the central pins from the cannulated drills, 2 nitinol wires with loops (1 for each tunnel) are passed and retrieved from the anteroinferior portal (Figure 5). This is done under direct vision as the NanoScope does not cause port overcrowding anteriorly during this maneuver. The drill guide, hook, and drills are then removed. The nitinol wires are used to pass 2 loop sutures through the tunnels, and these are used to pass both of the FiberTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) cerclage sutures through the superior tunnel. The sutures are then passed through the graft and back through the inferior tunnel to exit the glenoid posteriorly.

Glenoid drilling (lateral decubitus's position, left shoulder, 10° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) through anterior superior portal). The inferior drill hole has been created with a cannulated drill bit, avoiding the shoulder capsule.

Suture retrieval (lateral decubitus's position, left shoulder, 10° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) through anterior superior portal). The arthroscopic grasper is inserted through the anterior portal to retrieve the nitinol from the inferior glenoid drill bit.

Bone Graft Fixation

The cerclage sutures are simultaneously shuttled drawing the graft toward the glenoid. During this step, the graft can be directed and the orientation corrected using an instrument from the anterior portal. The graft can be viewed simultaneously from both the posterior portal with the standard arthroscope and from the anterior portal with the NanoScope. The small size of the NanoScope with the 10° viewing angle enables circumferential viewing of the graft docking on the glenoid neck with matching contact (Figure 6). In this way, the view ensures that the shuttle sutures do not twist or tangle and that the graft level matches the articular surface. The docking of the graft is evaluated simultaneously for contact and later compression via the NanoScope. Once the graft position is confirmed, the FiberTape sutures are interconnected to create a continuous loop and symmetrical tensioned. 12 The knots are sequentially tensioned and tied, completing the grafting.

Iliac crest autograft docking (lateral decubitus's position, left shoulder, 10° NanoScope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) through anterior superior portal). The large autograft is inspected for circumferential docking contact that appears to be 100%.

Viewing from the posterior arthroscopic camera, the capsulolabral complex is reattached to the glenoid over the graft, covering the graft in an extra‐articular position. The completed repair can finally be inspected from the anterior portal using the NanoScope.

The portals are closed with Steri‐Strips (3M, St. Paul, MN), with an additional No. 3‐0 Monocryl subcuticular suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) for the anterior portal. The arm rests in a broad arm sling with a body strap for 3 weeks, during which elbow, wrist, and hand mobilization and shoulder external rotation only to a neutral position are permitted. A standard anterior stabilization rehabilitation protocol is followed thereafter.

DISCUSSION

The dual‐camera arthroscopic bone block cerclage using iliac crest bone graft and NanoScope offers metal‐free glenoid reconstruction with reduced operative time (~30 minutes saved), excellent visualization, and minimal soft‐tissue trauma (Tables 1 and 2). It preserves anatomy, avoids hardware complications, and achieves outcomes comparable to other methods. This methodology is applicable to arthroscopic Latarjet shoulder stabilization and has also been reported in rotator cuff repair. 9 , 10 We posit that multiple viewing angles liberate the surgeon from confinement of 2‐dimensional workspace, increase depth perception, and avoid blind spots, ultimately increasing intuitive spatial orientation. This improves surgical precision and real‐time decision‐making.
