Effects of pelvic floor electrical stimulation with an implantable device at different frequencies on stress urinary incontinence in rats
Authors: Bohong Long, Chen Li, Han Deng, Haoyu Sun, Limin Liao, Xing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost pelvic floor electrical stimulation (PFES) is intravaginal, with little research on how stimulation frequencies affect stress urinary incontinence (SUI) treatment. This study aimed to investigate the effects of different stimulation frequencies on improving SUI through PFES using an implantable PFES device in rats.MethodsA miniature, implantable, wireless PFES device (NuStim) was used to stimulate the pelvic floor muscles of 24 female Sprague–Dawley rats. All animals underwent vaginal distension (VD) to mimic postpartum SUI. PFES was applied at four frequencies (6 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz and 50 Hz) to four experimental groups for 2 consecutive weeks. Multiple cystometrograms (CMGs) were performed to measure leak point pressure (LPP) and maximal bladder capacity (MBC).ResultsVD-induced SUI significantly reduced the LPP to 21.1 ± 2.5 cmH2O, 24.1 ± 4.6 cmH2O, 24.0 ± 5.3 cmH2O and 25.7 ± 3.5 cmH2O compared to the control levels (46.5 ± 3.7 cmH2O, 50.1 ± 9.2 cmH2O, 49.3 ± 10.3 cmH2O and 53.3 ± 8.6 cmH2O, P < 0.01) across the four groups. PFES at 6 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz and 50 Hz significantly increased the LPP to 50.9 ± 8.0 cmH2O, 53.3 ± 13.5 cmH2O, 49.6 ± 9.9 cmH2O and 54.0 ± 5.2 cmH2O compared to the respective VD levels (P < 0.001). No frequency-dependent differences were detected (P > 0.05). MBC did not show significant differences at any of the tested frequencies.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that different PFES frequencies could all promote the recovery of urinary control function, with no significant differences in therapeutic effects. However, further randomized controlled studies were needed to assess additional frequencies and the long-term efficacy of PFES.

Introduction

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is defined by the International Continence Society (ICS) as “the complaint of any involuntary loss of urine on effort, physical exertion, or during sneezing or coughing” [1]. It is common in women and often associated with a reduced quality of life. The underlying pathophysiology involves urethral hypermobility and/or intrinsic urethral sphincter deficiency [2]. Risk factors include pregnancy, vaginal childbirth, menopause, cognitive impairment, obesity, and advanced age [3].

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), introduced by Arnold Kegel in 1948, is the first-line therapy recommended for women with SUI [4]. Treatment for SUI focuses on strengthening the pelvic floor muscles. Studies have consistently demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing symptoms and improving quality of life [2, 5]. However, many women struggle to properly contract their pelvic floor muscles or fail to continue long-term exercises [6, 7].

Pelvic floor electrical stimulation (PFES) is being increasingly widely used in clinical practice. For patients who cannot adequately control their pelvic floor muscles, PFES can be adopted, and it can also be used in combination with PFMT [8]. PFES enhances pelvic floor muscle strength by applying electrical current externally to the pelvic floor. Numerous studies have shown that PFES can significantly improve symptoms of urinary incontinence and increase urinary control [9, 10]. Despite this, there are ongoing debates regarding the optimal application, duration, amplitude, frequency, and timing of PFES therapy [11]. Meanwhile, several limitations restrict its application. For instance, PFES applied to the skin and vaginal mucosa can cause discomfort, potentially reducing patient compliance. In addition, PFES typically requires treatment at specialized medical institutions, increasing both time and economic burdens. Furthermore, the long-term efficacy of PFES after treatment cessation remains unclear. Some patients experience symptom recurrence after discontinuing treatment, necessitating retreatment.

Previously, several implantable devices have been used to treat overactive bladder (OAB) through neuromodulation [12]; however, this is the first animal study using an implantable PFES device for SUI. Recently, we developed a miniature wireless neuromuscular stimulator, NuStim, which can be implanted precisely into pelvic floor muscles through minimally invasive surgery. This device delivers PFES to induce continuous muscle contractions for training purposes. In our previous study, we have verified the efficacy and safety of this stimulator [13], which prevented excessive collagen degradation, promoted muscle fiber regeneration, and improved tissue structure. The balanced expression of matrix metalloproteinases and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases in the pelvic floor muscles and anterior vaginal wall provides a molecular basis for the recovery of pelvic floor tissues and the improvement of SUI symptoms [14].

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) can improve biomechanical properties and tissue regeneration, enhance blood flow and increase the number of fibroblasts in damaged tissues [15]. The frequency of the electrical current affects muscle contractility, muscle fatigue, and blood flow. One study suggests that the pelvic floor muscles are rich in type 2 muscle fibers, and the most suitable frequency for different types of muscle fibers also varies [16]. However, other studies argue that different stimulation frequencies have no impact on the final treatment effect [17, 18]. Currently, most studies on the effects of electrical stimulation frequency on muscle function have focused on skeletal muscles. Stimulation parameters in traditional PFES were largely based on the clinical experience of individual medical centers, with a lack of high-level evidence regarding how PFES frequency affects pelvic floor function. Whether PFES using the novel NuStim system has frequency-dependent characteristics remains to be determined. This study aimed to explore the effects of different stimulation frequencies of NuStim in treating SUI, providing an experimental basis for its clinical application.

Materials and methods

Research design

A miniature, implantable, wireless PFES device was used to stimulate the pelvic floor muscles of 24 female Sprague–Dawley rats. The rats were randomized using a computer-generated sequence, assigned to one of the four groups. All animals underwent urodynamic tests to measure the baseline values of leak point pressure (LPP) and maximum bladder capacity (MBC), followed by vaginal distention (VD) to mimic postpartum SUI. Urodynamic tests were performed 1 week later to measure the values after VD. PFES was applied at four frequencies (6 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz, and 50 Hz) for 2 consecutive weeks, after which urodynamic tests were conducted to measure the values at 2 weeks. The experimental design flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. NuStim supported frequency range of 4–50 Hz. Therefore, we first selected the maximum and minimum values near the supported stimulation frequency range of the stimulator. Second, 30 Hz and 50 Hz are commonly used in PFES. In addition, 15 Hz and 30 Hz are close to the quartile points of the supported stimulation frequency range. Meanwhile, 15 Hz is a frequency commonly used in clinical sacral neuromodulation and tibial nerve stimulation. Thus, we selected these four frequencies for investigation. We calculated the sample size, and the effect size was 2.63. Using G*Power software, with 90% power and α = 0.05, a minimum of 3 rats per group was calculated. Finally, we set the number of animals in each group to 6. In this study, stimulation frequency was the independent variable, with LPP and MBC as dependent variables. Device introduction

The NuStim device, implanted in the rats, comprises three components: a stimulator (Fig. 2A), a radio frequency (RF) cushion (Fig. 2B), and an Android pad (Fig. 2C). The stimulator, implanted in the rat, is cylindrical, with metal electrodes at both ends and a glass side, and has good biocompatibility. It has a diameter of 3 mm and a length of 10 mm. It receives signals from the external RF cushion and generates electrical stimulation signals. The stimulation parameters, including intensity (0–16 V), frequency (4–50 Hz), and pulse width (40–450 μs), are configured via the Android application and transmitted to the RF cushion.Fig. 2Characteristics of the implanted device: (A) the stimulator was used to receive electromagnetic signals from the RF cushion and generate electrical stimulation; (B) the RF cushion was used to receive Bluetooth signals from the App terminal and generate electromagnetic signals to activate the stimulator; (C) the App interface was used to record the rat’s basic information, adjust stimulus parameters, test and implement the stimulus protocol, and send feedback to the professionals. Urodynamic tests

The experimental procedure was identical to that in our previous study [13]. One of the experimenters in our lab who was blinded to the allocation of the rats performed the urodynamic tests to measure LPP and maximal bladder capacity (MBC). Rats were anesthetized with urethane (1.2 g/kg, intraperitoneal injection) and positioned in a supine position. A paraffin-coated epidural catheter was carefully inserted into the bladder via the urethra, extending 2–3 cm. The catheter was connected to an 8 F two-lumen catheter (Cook Company, Spencer, IN), with one lumen connected to the MP 150 multichannel physiologic recorder and the other to a microperfusion pump (infusion rate, 10 ml/h). MBC was determined as the average value across three urination cycles when the first urine leak coincided with a rapid increase in bladder pressure. After bladder emptying, methylene blue solution was infused at approximately half the MBC. Manual pressure was applied above the pubic region until leakage occurred, and the peak bladder pressure at leakage was recorded as the LPP [19, 20]. Each rat underwent three measurements, with the average values recorded. Baseline MBC and LPP were measured before model preparation.

Model preparation

The study involved 24 non-pregnant female Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 2–3 months and weighing 250–300 g. All experimental procedures were approved by the Capital Medical University (AEEI-2024–055). The SUI model was established using VD [21, 22]. Each rat was anesthetized with urethane (1.2 g/kg, intraperitoneal injection) and placed supine on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. An 8Fr catheter, lubricated with paraffin oil, was inserted 2–3 cm into the vagina. The balloon was inflated with 5 ml of saline, and the labia majora were secured with 5–0 absorbable sutures to prevent balloon displacement. A 0.3 kg sandbag was then suspended vertically from the catheter for 8 h [23]. After removing the catheter, intraperitoneal penicillin was administered for 3 days to prevent infection.

One week later, each rat underwent urodynamic testing using the previously described method. The measured MBC and LPP were recorded as post-VD values. Subsequently, the sneeze test was conducted. This involved emptying the bladder and infusing a methylene blue solution at approximately half the MBC. A rat whisker was then gently inserted into the nostril to induce sneezing. The urethra was carefully monitored for fluid discharge. Detection of fluid leakage indicated a positive sneeze test, confirming successful establishment of the SUI model [24].

Stimulator implantation

After successful modeling, each rat underwent stimulator implantation. A 1 cm incision was made 0.5–1 cm lateral to the perianal area to access the pelvic cavity. The stimulator was inserted approximately 2 cm into the pelvic floor muscle, with the anode oriented outward and the cathode inward. The incision was closed with sutures. Successful implantation was confirmed by rhythmic anal contractions during stimulation testing. Stimulation protocol and post-treatment urodynamic tests

One week after stimulator implantation, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned perpendicular to the ground on a surgical table. The RF cushion was placed horizontally at a 90° angle to the stimulator (Fig. 3C). Biphasic exponential pulses with a pulse width of 0.2 ms were used for stimulation. The threshold (T) was defined as the minimum intensity required to induce observable anal muscle contractions. However, the minimum PFES intensity may vary due to individual differences among animals. Stimulation was administered daily at frequencies of 6 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz, and 50 Hz (6 rats per frequency) at an intensity of 2 T for 30 min per day for 2 weeks. After completing the treatment, urodynamic tests were performed as previously described, with the recorded MBC and LPP values representing the results 2 weeks post-treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. If the data conformed to a normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to analyze inter-group differences, and one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to analyze intra-group differences; Bonferroni post-hoc test to assess statistical significance. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

SUI is a common condition in urology, with a prevalence of approximately 46% among adult women [25]. PFMT is a primarily therapy introduced by physiotherapists widely used to treat SUI symptoms by increasing muscle volume and contraction strength in response to increased intra-abdominal pressure [26]. Although the effectiveness of PFMT has been well-established, poor adherence remains a significant challenge, limiting its overall success. Electrical stimulation plays a significant role in the human body, particularly in tissue stimulation and regeneration. It can markedly enhance the regeneration of nerves, bones, cardiovascular tissues, and wounds [27]. Previous PFES methods have limitations in therapeutic efficacy due to insufficient current penetration and uneven distribution in underlying tissues. In a three-layer tissue model (1 mm skin, 1 mm fat, 96 mm muscle), current intensity attenuated to 10% of the skin-applied current at 10 mm from the electrode [28]. In the quadriceps muscle, current detected at a depth of 0.5 cm (approximately 2.5 cm from the skin surface) was only 1% of the current applied by a (2 cm × 2 cm) electrode placed over the quadriceps [29]. Since the urethral sphincter is adjacent to the vagina, transvaginal PFES can improve urethral sphincter function and increase urethral closure pressure, thereby alleviating symptoms. However, one study showed that transvaginal PFES did not improve bladder neck mobility [30]. This suggests that transvaginal PFES may not effectively stimulate deep pelvic floor tissues to improve the suspension structure of the urethra. These findings indicate that the long-term efficacy of PFES requires further validation. Therefore, there is a need for devices that facilitate home-based, long-term PFES targeting deep pelvic tissues. The development of minimally invasive devices has become particularly important with the aging population growth.

Implantable devices play an important role in the treatment of lower urinary tract dysfunction. Sacral neuromodulation (SNM) uses electrodes to stimulate sacral nerve roots and regulate the micturition reflex, primarily for treating OAB. The stimulator mainly consists of a tined electrode and a battery-containing main device, and is usually relatively large in size. SNM is performed in two stages, which is invasive and costly [31]. Previous studies have also explored the treatment of OAB by implanting an electrical stimulator near the tibial nerve [32]. The clinical benefits of implanting eCoin (Valencia Technologies Corp., Valencia, CA, USA) for treating OAB have been well-demonstrated. However, its beneficial effect has not been validated in multicentre clinical randomized studies. In a previous animal study, we used the implanted TibialStim (Genral Stim Inc., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) device to inhibit the micturition reflex under physiological conditions, but the implantable system carries potential risks of allergy, migration, and nerve injury [33, 34]. One implantable stimulator (Advanced Bionics Corp., Valencia, California), certified with CE marking in Europe, is designed to stimulate the pudendal nerve for treating urgency urinary incontinence. However, this stimulator relies on batteries and is relatively large in size [35]. In contrast, NuStim is implanted into the pelvic floor muscles via minimally invasive surgery. It targets the structure and function of pelvic floor muscles through PFES, thereby improving urethral closure ability. Moreover, NuStim is small in size, with a length of 10 mm, and is powered remotely via a cushion. Previous studies have focused on regulating the micturition reflex through neuromodulation, whereas this series of studies is the first to use an implantable device to treat female SUI through PFES.

Our device offers several advantages that can improve patient compliance. First, it avoids repeated irritation of the skin or mucous membranes caused by electrodes. Second, patients can use the NuStim at home without restrictions on their basic daily activities. For example, they can read or watch television during the treatment. Third, patients can adjust the parameters using an Android pad at home, and the data are quickly transmitted to medical staff, enabling remote monitoring and feedback. This eliminates the need for patients to set aside specific time to travel to and from professional medical institutions regularly, thereby reducing their time and transportation costs. Fourth, there is no requirement to insert stimulation electrodes into the vagina or rectum, and the treatment can be completed at home, which protects patients' privacy.

The pelvic floor structure of rats is most similar to that of humans. VD is the most commonly used method for establishing SUI rat models and is widely applied in studies on the physiology, pathology, and therapeutic applications of SUI [36]. In previous studies, the duration of VD for modeling was relatively short, resulting in a model success rate of only 38%, which imposed limitations on animal experiments. The positive rate of the sneeze test after treatment was not collected in this study. Huang et al. extended the VD duration to 8 h, and the positive rate of the SUI sneeze test reached approximately 60% on the 14th day [23].

In our initial trials, it was verified that when compared to the control group which mimicked postpartum SUI via balloon VD without any treatment, the 6 Hz stimulation notably ameliorated the symptoms of urinary incontinence and exhibited potential for clinical translation [13]. In this study, we employed the same animal model to explore four stimulation frequencies (6 Hz, 15 Hz, 30 Hz, and 50 Hz), and PFES at all frequencies significantly increased the LPP (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found among the four frequencies (P > 0.05).

One study showed no statistical differences among frequencies in oxygen uptake, ventilation, or symptoms of dyspnea and fatigue [18]. Another study suggested that NMES equally enhances quadriceps strength, whether using 35 Hz or 50 Hz frequencies [17]. The pelvic floor muscles and quadriceps are both classified as skeletal muscles. Although there may be certain differences in the specific content of muscle fiber types, this still holds certain guiding significance.

Previous studies have explored the therapeutic effect and mechanism of PFES at 20 Hz and 50 Hz by comparison. The 50 Hz group showed advantages in terms of collagen fiber repair effect, collagen protein expression level, and collagen synthesis mediated by the TGF-β1–Smad2/3 pathway in mechanism research. However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the evaluation of T-type calcium channel function and the positive rate of the sneeze test, which serves as an indicator for final therapeutic effect. This indicates that different PFES frequencies may have varying therapeutic effects in specific therapeutic mechanisms, but their final therapeutic effects are consistent. The therapeutic mechanism that plays a decisive role in the treatment still needs further exploration [37]. These results suggest that different frequencies may be similarly effective, allowing future optimization based on patient comfort or compliance. Alternatively, future research could investigate whether combining different frequencies achieves better therapeutic effects than a single frequency.

MBC is used to evaluate the functional capacity of the bladder and urethral closure function during the urine storage phase. A study comparing the effects of ovariectomy performed at different times after VD on urodynamic characteristics showed that performing oophorectomy simultaneously with VD and conducting urodynamic tests 1 week later had no effect on MBC, whereas performing oophorectomy 1 week after VD and conducting urodynamic tests 2 weeks later reduced MBC. The researchers suggested that MBC is affected by detrusor compliance and maximum urethral closure pressure. The ovaries secrete estrogen, progesterone, and small amounts of androgens. Estrogen deficiency can reduce the blood supply to the bladder detrusor, resulting in decreased detrusor compliance, which in turn leads to a reduction in MBC. This reduction is time-dependent, meaning that MBC will further decrease as time progresses. This study did not affect sex hormone secretion in rats, which may explain the lack of statistically significant changes in MBC pre- and post-VD [38]. In this study, the decrease in LPP caused by VD did not further affect MBC, and VD had no impact on detrusor compliance. Previous studies have shown that electrical stimulation of the pudendal nerve at 5 Hz can significantly increase MBC [39]. However, in this study, different PFES frequencies had no effect on MBC, which indicates that the stimulator used in this study does not affect detrusor function by stimulating the pudendal nerve. In addition, in clinical diagnosis and treatment, most patients with SUI have triggering factors of increased abdominal pressure (such as laughing, coughing, etc.) when experiencing urine leakage symptoms, and urodynamic examination results often show that the MBC of SUI patients does not decrease significantly.

This study has several limitations. First, only four frequencies were tested. Further studies are needed to validate the effects of additional frequencies and to determine whether specific stimulation frequencies are safe for long-term use. Second, chronic stimulation is typically utilized in clinical settings. Since we only assessed the effects of the stimulation system over 2 weeks, this brief duration fails to adequately reflect long-term treatment results. However, one study indicated that PFES can improve incontinence-specific quality of life and reduce urine leakage both in the short term and long term [40]. Therefore, although the present study only involves short-term treatment, its results still hold guiding significance. Third, the small sample size may limit the detection of differences between different frequencies. Further research with a larger sample size is still needed. Fourth, due to anatomical differences, the findings from the rat model may not be directly extrapolated to humans. Fifth, based on the preliminary experiments conducted by our team, by setting up a control group, a sham stimulation group, and a 6 Hz stimulation group, it has been verified that without therapeutic intervention, the LPP cannot return to the baseline level; in contrast, after 2 weeks of PFES, the LPP can be significantly increased to the baseline level. In line with the 3R principles of laboratory animal ethics, this study minimizes the number of laboratory animals used on the premise of ensuring the reliability of experimental data. The therapeutic efficacy is confirmed through the before-and-after self-control of the experimental animals themselves. If there were no prior experimental basis, establishing a control group would make the experimental data more reliable.

In summary, this study demonstrates that different PFES frequencies can all promote the recovery of urinary control function, with no significant differences in therapeutic effects. Considering comfort, 6 Hz can be the first choice. However, the flexibility to adjust frequencies may enable customization of therapy based on individual patient comfort and device parameters. Further randomized controlled research is still needed to explore a broader range of frequencies and systematically assess the long-term effectiveness of this device.
