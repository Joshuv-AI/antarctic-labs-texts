Interaction-driven flat band and charge order in Fe5GeTe2
Authors: Qiang Gao, Gabriele Berruto, Khanh Duy Nguyen, Chaowei Hu, Paul Malinowski, Haoran Lin, Beomjoon Goh, Bo Gyu Jang, Xiaodong Xu, Peter Littlewood, Jiun-Haw Chu, Shuolong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flat electronic bands enable fascinating emergent phenomena such as superconductivity and charge orders. A prevailing approach to realizing flat bands is to engineer lattice geometric constraints in twisted or kagome-like materials. An alternative approach is to utilize purely electronic-interaction-driven flat bands, yet a fundamental challenge is that extreme flatness requires ultrastrong interaction strength, which often leads to incoherent states. Here we demonstrate the concurrent formation of an interaction-driven flat band at the Fermi level and a 3×3R30° charge order in a van der Waals magnet Fe5GeTe2 using high-resolution angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy. This charge order is manifested by band folding within 30 meV below the Fermi level, with its nesting driven by flat bands. The presence of this flat band throughout the Brillouin zone and the logarithmic temperature dependence of its spectral weight suggest a phenomenological Kondo-like, coherent Fermi liquid emerging from strong correlations. Our work establishes a paradigm where an interaction-driven flat band promotes large-scale electronic ordering.

INTRODUCTION

Flat electronic bands in solid-state systems can fundamentally reshape the collective ground states and lead to fascinating orders such as charge orders (1), superconductivity (2, 3), correlated insulators (4), as well as integer (5) and fractional topological orders (6–8). Flat bands can be obtained by engineering lattice geometric constraints, most notably via moiré potentials in twisted 2D materials and via destructive quantum interference in special lattices. Yet, in twisted 2D materials, the flat-band condition is often unstable against sample handling, and the uniformity of the moiré pattern is restricted to <1 μm. In lattices with special geometries, such as Lieb (9, 10), kagome (11–15), and clover (16) lattices, the flat bands are often hundreds of meV away from the Fermi level (EF), making them irrelevant for low-energy ordering phenomena. Moreover, such flat bands are fully determined by the crystal structures and exhibit minimum tunability. Therefore, there has been a growing interest in finding alternative flat-band systems that are robust, tunable, and extendable to large spatial scales, ideal for investigating the interplay of electron correlations, topology, and charge orders at EF (17, 18).

Electronic-interaction-driven flat bands provide a different possibility. Strong electron-electron, electron-phonon, or electron-spin interactions lead to band structure renormalization close to EF (19). In fact, in some twisted 2D materials (20) or geometrically constrained lattices (21), electronic interactions have been shown to contribute substantially to band flattening. It is thus inspiring to explore purely interaction-driven flat bands for emergent quantum phenomena. Such band flattening can be robust against sample handling, tunable by carrier doping or temperature variation, and extendable to macroscopic length scales. It can provide an avenue to induce and tune charge orders or charge density waves, which have been prevalent in transition metal dichalcogenides and kagome-like materials (22–28). However, a fundamental challenge is that extreme band flatness often requires a diverging interaction strength, which in turn can lead to incoherent states. Such incoherent interaction-driven flat bands (29) cannot be utilized to enable emergent orders near EF.

Here we focus on Fe5GeTe2 which is a van der Waals magnetic material holding an exciting potential for flat-band engineering. Fe5GeTe2 has multiple structural phases distinguished by the ordering of the Fe (1) atoms, which can occupy one of the up and down sites relative to the Ge atom in each unit cell (30–35). In the so-called site-ordered phase, the Fe (1) atoms follow an “up-down-down” or “down-up-up” (UDD or DUU) arrangement to form a clover lattice (Fig. 1B), where flat bands away from EF emerge due to destructive quantum interference. Meanwhile, theory predicted that an effective Kondo-like interaction between the flat bands and the dispersive conduction bands in a clover lattice leads to new flat bands exactly at EF (16). A recent scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) study (36) revealed that flat bands appear concomitantly with a 3×3R30° charge order in the “UUU” phase of Fe5GeTe2. The crucial difference between this charge order and the structural 3×3 order in the UDD or DUU phase is that the former does not exhibit the lattice reconstruction and is purely electronically driven. However, so far flat bands at EF associated with either the electronic or structural 3×3 order in Fe5GeTe2 have not been clearly revealed using angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) (35, 37–40), making it difficult to investigate how flat bands impact electronic orders in this van der Waals magnet. We note that recent studies on Fe5GeTe2 in the slow-cooled phase (41, 42) reported flat bands close to EF, yet no Fermi surface reconstruction due to a 3×3 order has been reported, making it not suitable for studying the interplay between flat bands and charge orders.

In this article, we report the discovery of a 3×3R30° charge order driven by flat bands at EF in Fe5GeTe2. Multiple phases are identified by high-resolution ARPES. In one phase unrevealed by previous ARPES studies, electronic structures are folded to the 3×3 reconstructed Brillouin zone, yet the band folding is limited to a small binding energy range less than 30 meV, indicating the purely electronic origin of this order. The nesting vector connects segments of the Fermi surfaces where the flat band is pronounced in the ARPES spectrum. The characteristics of the flat band, that it is omnipresent throughout the Brillouin zone and exhibits a logarithmically growing spectral weight towards lower temperatures, point to a phenomenological Kondo-like, coherent Fermi liquid emerging from strong correlation physics. Taken together with the calculation of Lindhard response functions for both the dispersive-band and flat-band regimes, we conclude that the flat band dramatically enhances the tendency for Fermi surface nesting. This makes Fe5GeTe2 a unique non-twisting system to demonstrate the generalizable scheme where interaction-driven flat bands at EF lead to electronic ordering.

RESULTS

The electronic structures of two distinct phases of Fe5GeTe2 are revealed by ARPES using 21.2 eV photons (Fig. 1). Importantly, we use 6 eV laser-based micrometer-scale ARPES to visualize the microscopic mixture of different phases (fig. S1). This enables us to select the material allowing helium-lamp-based ARPES at 21.2 eV with a 0.5 mm beam spot to investigate regions dominated by a single phase. By rapidly quenching the sample from 550 K to 273 K we preferentially obtain Phase I (Fig. 1, E to G). Here, a hexagonal hole pocket and a quasi-circular electron pocket are observed at the Γ¯ and K¯ points of the unreconstructed Brillouin zone. These observations are generally consistent with those in the previously revealed site-ordered phase (35). Moreover, a geometrically induced flat band at −0.5 eV is observed in this phase (fig. S2), reinforcing the assignment to the site-ordered phase (35). Consistent with the prior study on the site-ordered phase (35), we also do not observe band folding in this phase, possibly due to the short coherence length of each “UDD” or “DUU” domain (36). By slowly cooling the sample from 550 K to 300 K with a 2–4 K/min cooling rate we have a higher probability of observing Phase II, where diffusive features are observed at the Γ¯ and K¯ points in the Fermi surface map (Fig. 1H). Six triangular features are resolved at the 6 corners of the reconstructed Brillouin zone. The Fermi surface topology is consistent with a 3×3 reconstructed Brillouin zone. The rather diffusive features in the Fermi surface map reflect flat band dispersions in the energy-momentum cuts (Fig. 2D). We also reveal a more subtle band folding at the binding energy of 30 meV (Fig. 1I), where the original hexagonal contour at Γ¯ leads to folded replicas at the 6 K¯ points. The low-energy electronic ordering is also manifested in the replication of the band dispersions at Γ¯ and K¯ (Fig. 2C). Finally, we do not observe band folding at the binding energy of 80 meV (Fig. 1J). The confined energy range for band folding is inconsistent with a lattice-based 3×3 order, indicating that the charge order in Phase II is likely electronically driven.

A flat band at EF is observed in the charge-ordered Phase II. This is seen by plotting the energy-momentum cuts along the high-symmetry trajectories in the momentum space (Fig. 2B). Figure 2D shows that the Fermi surface feature at Γ¯ and the triangular features at the folded zone corners K′¯ are related to flat band dispersions (α). To better visualize the α band, the energy-momentum spectra (Fig. 2, D, F, and G) are divided by the Fermi-Dirac function convolved with the energy resolution. Raw spectra are plotted in fig. S4. The energy distribution curves (EDCs) taken around Γ¯ and K′¯ exhibit a pronounced peak which is relatively momentum-independent and pinned at EF (Fig. 2E), demonstrating a non-dispersive band at EF. In addition, a weakly-dispersive, electron-like band (β) at 30 meV below EF is observed by both 21.2 eV (Fig. 2D) and 6 eV photons with a p-polarization (Fig. 2F). S-polarized 6 eV light preferentially enhances the α band over a broader momentum range (Fig. 2, G and H). Figure 2G also suggests that the α feature connects to a dispersive hole-like band at momenta beyond [−0.2, 0.2] Å−1. The p- and s-polarized light yield stronger cross-sections on the right and left part of the electronic structure, respectively. A more detailed study suggests that the linear dichroism follows the C3 symmetry (fig. S5), as expected for the rhombohedral stacking of Fe5GeTe2 slabs (30). In a traditional electronically driven charge-density wave, the tendency for nesting is the strongest when there is a nesting vector connecting parallel segments of the Fermi surface (43). However, such a nesting vector defined by the hexagonal pocket (solid red in Fig. 2A) is along Γ¯−M¯, inconsistent with the folding between Γ¯ and K¯. Instead, the nesting vector directly connecting Γ¯ and K¯ links the momentum-space segments where the flat band feature α is pronounced. We will show in the Discussion section that such a flat band configuration dramatically promotes electronic ordering. Moreover, Fig. 2, D and F illustrate that flat dispersions are seen both at 0 and 30 meV below EF, which explains the fact that band folding at these energies is detectable, as shown in Fig. 1, H and I.

We discuss the possible structural origin of Phase II. Importantly, structural phase assignments of Fe5GeTe2 in the ARPES literature (35, 37–42) have been particularly controversial due to the multiple possible structural configurations overlooked by early ARPES studies. Even though some studies (38, 40) claimed to have observed band folding between Γ¯ and K¯, the replication of the Fermi surfaces was not clearly resolved. The folded band observed in Ref. (38) extends to 200 meV below EF, with no concomitant flat bands near EF, indicating a structural origin different from our Phase II. Later ARPES studies (35, 39, 41, 42) discovered multiple structural configurations leading to distinct electronic structures. Wu et al. (35, 41) identified the electronic structures for the site-ordered phase obtained by rapid thermal quenching, and the site-disordered phase by slow cooling. Huang et al. (39) assigned two types of electronic structures to the Fe (1) and Fe (5) terminations. Our Phase I exhibits an electronic structure that mimics both the site-ordered phase in Wu et al. and the Fe (5) termination in Huang et al.: dispersive hole pockets near Γ¯ and a small electron pocket near K¯. Here we mainly follow the phase assignments in Ref. (35) considering its explicit investigation of the lattice symmetry using optical second harmonic generation. While it is tempting to attribute our Phase II to the site-disordered phase according to the thermal trajectory, Phase II exhibits a markedly different electronic structure. The site-disordered phase features multiple dispersive hole pockets near Γ¯ and dispersive electron pockets near K¯. Neither the 3×3 Fermi surface reconstruction nor the pronounced flat band at EF below 100 K was observed in the site-disordered phase (35). Moreover, we utilized 6 eV laser-based micro-ARPES and discovered two kinds of electronic structures different from that of the site-ordered phase (fig. S3). One corresponds to Phase II where diffusive Fermi surface features appear at Γ¯ and K¯ associated with flat band dispersions (Fig. 2A). The other (Phase III) shows dispersive hole bands near Γ¯ but no strong feature at −0.5 eV. The feature at −0.5 eV is a signature of the site-ordered phase (fig. S1B). This comparison suggests that Phase III is more consistent with the characteristics of the site-disordered phase, but Phase II is yet a different phase. We point out that there is a strong similarity between the flat band observed in our Phase II and that in the slow-cooled phase in Huh et al. (42), yet the 3×3 Fermi surface reconstruction was missing in Ref. (42), possibly due to sample quality or photon-energy-dependent matrix element effects. Considering the observation of the 3×3 Fermi surface reconstruction and that of the flat bands, we conclude that the most likely scenario is that Phase II corresponds to the charge-ordered state identified with the “UUU” configuration in a previous STM study (36).

We proceed to reveal the nature of the flat band at EF by conducting temperature-dependent measurements (Fig. 3). For this study we primarily use 6 eV laser-based ARPES with ∼10 μm spatial resolution. Raw spectra are divided by the Fermi-Dirac function convolved with the energy resolution. This analysis shows the band dispersion above EF within a limited energy range. Below 100 K, α and β bands can be distinguished (Fig. 3, A to D). Above 100 K, the two bands seemingly merge into a hole pocket with the band top ∼10 meV above EF (Fig. 3F). Our observations do not exclude the possibility that the broadening of the α and β bands at high temperatures leads to the appearance of “merging”, yet they remain as separate bands throughout the measurement temperature range. Additional Helium-lamp-based ARPES measurements indicate that the band structure near EF should be best interpreted as intercepting electron and hole bands (fig. S6). Near the Fermi crossings of the electron and hole bands, dramatic band flattening appears at low temperatures (Fig. 3G). In Fig. 3K, we plot the temperature-dependent spectral weight of the α feature, integrated in the range of [−50,0] meV, after subtracting the incoherent background modeled by a Fermi-Dirac function. In addition, we plot the spectral height of the α feature after subtracting the Fermi-Dirac background. Both quantities scale linearly with −log(T), where T is the temperature. The pronounced temperature dependence of both the flat band spectral weight and the charge order (fig. S7) suggests that these phenomena do not originate from the structural 3×3 order observed by x-ray scattering (44), which exhibits only weak temperature dependence.

These observations allow us to examine various theoretical scenarios for the formation of the flat band. First, strong electron-phonon coupling can lead to renormalized band dispersions near EF. The extreme case is the formation of polarons, which give rise to peak-dip-hump features with the dip energy close to the phonon energy (45–47). Under this scenario, we may associate the spectral dip between α and β (Fig. 3A) with phonons near 15 meV (48, 49). However, such a modification of the single-particle spectral function near EF should conserve the integrated spectral weight (fig. S8), which is not the case in our results when integrating the background-subtracted spectral weight in the range of either [−50,0] meV (Fig. 3K) or [−230,80] meV (fig. S9E). In addition, even with an artificially high electron-phonon coupling constant of λ=1.0, the polaronic band structure (fig. S8) still does not yield the extremely flat band α (Fig. 2D), rendering this scenario unlikely. Another possibility is the formation of excitonic band structure, given that the 3×3 charge order folds an electron pocket from K¯ to Γ¯ to hybridize with the hole pocket at Γ¯. In the weak-coupling limit, the lower branch of the hybridized band appears in an “M” shape, but in the strong-coupling limit it can have a flat top (fig. S10) (21, 50–51). However, this scenario does not explain the formation of the flat band also near the K′¯ point (Fig. 2D). In addition, Fe5GeTe2 is metallic (31) where excitonic instabilities should in general be screened. We also considered, through density functional theory plus dynamical mean-field theory (DFT + DMFT) calculations, a scenario where spin-dependent scattering leads to flat dispersions near EF (fig. S11). However, the calculations do not reproduce the experimentally observed log (T) spectral-weight evolution (Fig. 3K), nor do they capture the monotonic temperature dependence of the linewidth (fig. S9D). Therefore, the Kondo-like description discussed below should be viewed as phenomenological rather than as a quantitative conclusion directly established by DFT + DMFT. Motivated by the previous STM study (36), we thus consider a phenomenological Kondo-like mechanism. The log(T) scaling of the coherent spectral weight and of the spectral height is consistent with the Kondo-like mechanism (52, 53). In this Kondo-like picture, as temperature increases there is a spectral weight redistribution from the Kondo resonance peak at EF to the lower and upper Hubbard bands, which are characterized by the energy scale of the Hubbard U (54) and is likely out of our data acquisition window. Moreover, the temperature-dependent full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the α band agrees with the theoretically predicted linewidth of an effective Kondo resonance peak (fig. S9D), w(T)=2(πkBT)2+2(kBTK)2, where T and TK are the sample temperature and the single-ion Kondo temperature; kB is the Boltzmann constant (55–57). However, this comparison does not allow us to accurately determine TK, as the theory-experiment agreement is not obviously better for any specific TK value (fig. S9D). Our experimental linewidth determined from ARPES is overall smaller than that reported by fitting the Fano line shape in STM in the comparable temperature range (36). We speculate that this is because STM probes the momentum-integrated density of states, while micro-ARPES allows us to focus on a small momentum region which helps exclude contributions from other momentum regions without pronounced flat bands.

DISCUSSION

The interaction-driven flat band in Fe5GeTe2 is highly intriguing. Even though Kondo physics is traditionally associated with f-electrons, Kondo-like systems with d-electrons have been identified, such as Fe3GeTe2 (58) and YFe2Ge2 (59). A fundamental conceptual difficulty is how the extended d-electron wavefunctions enable Kondo-like interactions. It was argued by Xu et al. (59) that the kinetic frustration, band hybridization, and electronic correlations for d-electrons can lead to quasi-localized states, which play the role of the localized states in f-electron systems. One should note that such a scenario is by no means equivalent to traditional Kondo lattices, as the coherent quasiparticle peak and hybridization gap do not vanish entirely above the coherence temperature (T∗) (59). The d-electrons are also not fully localized at high temperatures, but rather exhibit a coherent-to-incoherent crossover as shown in our Fig. 3. Such a coherent-to-incoherent crossover can emerge from a generic Hubbard model with a high coordinate number, where no explicit localized state is required but a heavily renormalized Fermi-liquid-like state emerges due to strong correlation effect (54, 60–70). However, the observable quantities, such as extremely flattened band dispersions at EF, can be effectively described by a Kondo-like interaction, which was shown theoretically (54). It is worth pointing out that the charge order can provide a degenerate localized state which helps facilitating the Kondo-like behavior. Lacking a standard terminology to describe this strong correlation effect, the word “Kondo” here is used to only represent the interaction-driven flat band exhibiting temperature scaling.

The discovery of interaction-driven, phenomenological Kondo-like flat bands helps us understand unusual characteristics of Fe5GeTe2 by electrical transport and magnetism measurements. A number of resistivity measurements on Fe5GeTe2 reported a rapid downturn when the temperature is lowered below 100 K (30, 31, 35). While these measurements were likely conducted on samples with mixed phases, such resistivity drops are characteristic of Kondo lattices where coherent interactions between localized and itinerant bands lead to suppressed resistivity (71). A recent study on the flat-band phase of Fe5GeTe2 reported the emergence of a Fermi-liquid-like behavior in the low-temperature resistivity, which reinforces this Kondo-like picture (42). A polarized neutron scattering experiment showed that the Fe (1) atoms become magnetically ordered below 100 K in the site-disordered phase (41), which is inconsistent with Kondo-like behaviors. As we discussed before, the site-disordered phase should be distinguished from our Phase II which is likely associated with the “UUU” configuration of the Fe (1) atoms (36). At this stage, it is still challenging to directly compare the surface-sensitive STM (36) and micro-ARPES measurements with bulk-sensitive electron (32) and x-ray (44) diffraction experiments. The diffraction experiments integrate signals from multiple structural phases distributed both in-plane and out-of-plane. The 3×3 order resolved by these measurements is weakly temperature dependent and predominantly reflects the structural order in the UDD or DUU phase. On the other hand, resonant inelastic x-ray scattering measurement at the Fe L3-edge revealed a dual character of magnetic excitations in Fe5GeTe2 (44): spin waves originating from localized spins and magnetic continuum from spin-spin interactions of itinerant electrons, which may suggest a Kondo-like contribution. To understand the phase and strain distribution in the bulk of the Kondo-like Phase II, one future avenue is to fabricate a phase-pure, few-layer-thick Fe5GeTe2 flake and perform spatially-resolved electron and x-ray diffraction measurements.

We evaluate the impact of the flat band on the tendency for Fermi surface nesting by calculating the static Lindhard response function (43)χ(q)=∫dk(2π)dfk−fk+qϵk−ϵk+q(1)where k and k + q represent the initial and final momenta, ϵk is the band dispersion, and f is the Fermi-Dirac function. For a 2D Fermi surface associated with a dispersive band dispersion, χ(q) is maximized along the direction where the nesting vector connects parallel segments of the Fermi surface (Fig. 4A). However, from our experimentally resolved Fermi surfaces (Fig. 2A) this criterion does not lead to nesting along Γ¯−K¯. On the other hand, with the flat bands present at Γ¯ and K¯, χ(q) is maximally enhanced with the nesting vector connecting Γ¯ and K¯, leading to the observed 3×3R30° charge order (Fig. 4B). Another scenario is to start with a flat band near Γ¯ and a dispersive electron-like band near K¯ (Fig. 4C), and the 3×3R30° charge order folds the quasi-flat band from Γ¯ to K¯. Such a configuration also leads to maximally enhanced χ(q) along Γ¯−K¯ (Fig. 4C). Therefore, the flat band at EF plays a pivotal role in driving the electronic ordering in Phase II of Fe5GeTe2. This also explains why the band folding is restricted to 30 meV below EF (Fig. 1, H and I), where the flat bands reside. We note that this Lindhard-response analysis is based on simplified low-energy band models constructed to capture the experimentally observed Fermi-surface topology and flat-band configurations. It should therefore be viewed as illustrating a plausible nesting-driven instability rather than providing a full ab initio derivation of the charge order.

Importantly, besides Fermi surface nesting, we also evaluate structural instability (72), electron-phonon coupling (24, 73), and orbital ordering (74) as possible contributors to the charge order. These mechanisms typically involve a spatially modulated lattice distortion, through superstructure modulation, phonon frequency softening or Jahn-Teller-type interactions. The lattice modulation was not observed in the STM measurement on the UUU phase (36) and inconsistent with electronic structure reconstruction only in the vicinity of EF, rendering these scenarios not the driving force for our observed charge order in Fe5GeTe2.

In summary, we reveal a distinct phase of Fe5GeTe2 in which a flat band is formed at EF and drives a 3×3R30° charge order. Clear signatures of the band folding within 30 meV below EF are observed by high-resolution ARPES, coinciding with the energy range where the flat band resides. Taken together with our calculations of the Lindhard response function, our results suggest Fe5GeTe2 as a model system where the interaction-driven flat band directly drives the electronic order. Such a flat band may arise from phenomenological Kondo-like interactions. More interestingly, the intrinsic magnetism in Fe5GeTe2 suggests that the time-reversal symmetry is spontaneously broken, leading to the theoretical possibility to realize Chern bands and topological phases (16). This highlights the general impact of our observations for low-energy electronic order engineering, and a pressing need to combine micro-device fabrication and low-temperature transport to probe the topological electronic properties.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample growth

Single crystals were grown via iodine-assisted chemical vapor transport following previous methods (31). Fe powder, Ge powder, and Te powder were weighed in the molar ratio of 5:1:2, mixed, and placed within a quartz tube along with 2.5 mg/cm3 of I2 pieces. The tube was then sealed under a low-pressure Ar atmosphere, and the sealed tubes were placed in a horizontal furnace using the natural temperature gradient with the source material at the center of the furnace. The furnace was ramped to 760°C in 12 hours, dwelled for 2 weeks, and then slowly cooled to room temperature for slow-cooling crystals. Some crystals were subsequently re-sealed in a quartz tube under vacuum, placed in a furnace at 750 K for two hours, and quenched in ice water to get quenched crystals. We could also create a quenched sample by heating it for 20 minutes on a hot plate at 573 K, and quenching it by thermal contact with a metal kept at 77 K.

ARPES measurements

The high-resolution ARPES measurements were performed on a Multi-Resolution Photoemission Spectroscopy platform at the University of Chicago (75). The 6 eV laser was generated by frequency quadrupling of a Ti:Sapphire oscillator with an 80 MHz repetition rate. The 6 eV laser spot size is 10 × 15 μm2 (FWHM) and incident on the sample at ≈45°. The analyzer slit is perpendicular to the plane defined by the laser propagation direction and the sample normal. The energy resolution of the 6 eV laser ARPES is 4 meV. The 21.2 eV ARPES measurements were carried out by using a helium discharge lamp (Scienta VUV5000) with an energy resolution of 6 meV.

DFT + DMFT calculations

DFT + DMFT calculations were performed using Rutgers DFT + Embedded DMFT (eDMFT) functional code (76). The correlation effect of Fe 3d orbitals is treated by a DMFT loop on top of an effective one-electron Hamiltonian obtained from the WIEN2k calculation, which employs the full-potential augmented plane wave method (77). Due to the computational cost, we modeled a Fe5GeTe2 monolayer for the calculations, rather than the bulk structure which contains three layers per unit cell due to stacking. The five inequivalent Fe sites within the structure were treated as five different impurity problems within the DMFT framework. A local DMFT basis was constructed by diagonalizing the hybridization matrix at zero frequency in the real-harmonic representation. The hybridization energy window, spanning from −10 to 10 eV with respect to the Fermi level, was adopted for the charge self-consistent calculations. The interaction parameters were chosen as U = 5 eV and J = 0.8 eV, and the nominal double-counting correction was adopted. The impurity problem was solved using continuous-time quantum Monte Carlo (CTQMC) method (78).
