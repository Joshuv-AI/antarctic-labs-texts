Three-dimensional virtual surgical planning enhances precision and safety in percutaneous transforaminal endoscopic discectomy: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Yang Fuguo, Chen Kang, Luo Yuanchao, He Renjian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous transforaminal endoscopic discectomy (PTED) is efficacious for lumbar disc herniation but harbours a steep learning curve and considerable reliance on intraoperative fluoroscopy. This often results in prolonged procedural times and elevated radiation exposure, particularly for novice surgeons.ObjectivesThis study evaluates whether preoperative three-dimensional virtual planning using Mimics software improves procedural accuracy and reduces radiation exposure.MethodsSixty-five patients with single-level lumbar disc herniation (L1/2 to L5/S1) were allocated to two cohorts: a conventional group (n = 31) treated based on surgeon experience, and a 3D-planning group (n = 34) undergoing preoperative simulation of skin entry points and trajectories via Mimics. Primary outcomes included fluoroscopy frequency, number of puncture attempts, operative duration, and complication rates. Clinical outcomes (VAS, ODI) were documented as secondary measures.ResultsThe 3D-planning group demonstrated significantly reduced channel establishment time (15.39 ± 2.42 vs. 19.29 ± 5.98 min, P < 0.01), fewer intraoperative fluoroscopies (14.48 ± 2.66 vs. 18.56 ± 2.55 exposures, P < 0.01), and shorter endoscopic operating time (52.16 ± 12.54 vs. 75.09 ± 10.28 min, P < 0.01). Complication rates were lower in the 3D group (6.45% vs. 14.71%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.05). Both groups exhibited comparable improvements in VAS and ODI at 6-month follow-up.ConclusionPreoperative 3D virtual planning enhances procedural precision, reduces radiation exposure, and improves safety in PTED, proving particularly beneficial for surgeons during the initial learning curve.Keywords: Mimics, Three-dimensional visualisation, Virtual surgery, Foraminoscopy, Lumbar disc herniation

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation represents a leading cause of disability worldwide, significantly compromising patients' functional status and quality of life. Among minimally invasive surgical options, percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy (PETD) has emerged as an effective treatment modality that enables adequate neural decompression while preserving spinal stability through a tissue-sparing approach under local anaesthesia [1–3]. The technical efficacy of this procedure hinges critically on the creation of an optimally oriented working channel [4, 5], a requirement that presents substantial challenges particularly for surgeons in their early learning phase.

The steep learning curve associated with PETD manifests primarily in trajectory planning difficulties, with imprecise instrument navigation to the superior articular process contributing to several clinically significant complications. Suboptimal puncture trajectories not only jeopardise complete disc removal and adequate nerve root decompression [6], but also introduce substantial safety concerns. Excessive insertion angles risk visceral and vascular injury through peritoneal violation, while inadequate depth penetration may leave residual nucleus pulposus material [7, 8]. Such technical imperfections frequently necessitate repeated intraoperative fluoroscopy, elevating radiation exposure for both patients and operating room personnel.

Anatomical variations further compound these technical challenges. Structural characteristics including high-riding iliac crests, hypertrophic L5 transverse processes, and prominent osteophytes frequently obstruct conventional puncture pathways, substantially increasing procedural failure rates [9–11]. While conventional C-arm fluoroscopy remains indispensable in current practice, its inherent limitation as a two-dimensional imaging modality fails to adequately represent complex three-dimensional anatomical relationships between neural elements and bony structures [12, 13]. This visualisation deficit often results in cumulative radiation exposures approaching or exceeding recommended safety thresholds.

Current literature reveals a conspicuous knowledge gap regarding systematic methodologies for enhancing procedural precision in PETD. Existing research predominantly focuses on technical modifications rather than addressing fundamental planning deficiencies [11, 14, 15], highlighting an urgent need for innovative solutions that can transition current practice from experience-dependent estimation to precision-based intervention.

Our study accordingly developed and validated a novel three-dimensional virtual planning platform specifically designed to overcome these limitations. This methodology integrates patient-specific anatomical modelling with dynamic intraoperative guidance to address three critical aspects of PETD: (1) systematic avoidance of bony obstructions through preoperative simulation; (2) enhanced nerve protection via dynamic safe triangle planning; and (3) substantial reduction in fluoroscopic dependence through optimised trajectory mapping. By establishing a reproducible framework for surgical planning, our approach not only addresses immediate clinical challenges but also provides an anatomical database for future development of intelligent navigation systems in spinal surgery.

Materials and methods

Study Design: Retrospective comparative study approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No.: Ethics (M)-20225-025).

Patient characteristics

Sixty-five patients with single-level lumbar disc herniation (L1/2 to L5/S1) treated between January 2020 and December 2024 were included. Participants were allocated to either the 3D-planning group (n = 31) receiving virtual planning-assisted PTED, or the conventional group (n = 34) undergoing experience-based PTED. Baseline characteristics demonstrated no significant intergroup differences (P > 0.05), confirming comparability (Table 1).

Implementation protocol

Preoperative assessment and 3D virtual planning and dual-axis matching workflow

Preoperative Examination: All patients underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation alongside lumbar dynamic radiographs, CT, and MRI examinations. Preoperative imaging data were integrated with clinical findings to identify the symptomatic level and characterise lesion location, dimensions, morphology, and lumbar stability.CT and MRI examinations were performed with patients in the supine position. Metal markers were affixed to the skin overlying the posterior superior iliac spine and spinous processes to facilitate intraoperative registration.

Preoperative 3D Reconstruction and Virtual RehearsalThe patient's DICOM-formatted lumbar CT data were imported into Mimics 21.0. A 3D model of the lumbar spine, including the vertebrae, iliac crest, and relevant anatomy, was generated via threshold segmentation (Figs. 1, 2). The surgeon then performed a “virtual rehearsal” of the procedure on this model (Figs. 3, 4). This involved:Identifying the optimal skin entry point and instrument trajectory to safely access Kambin’s triangle whilst avoiding bony obstructions (Figs. 5, 6).Quantitatively measuring the distance from the posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) to the planned skin entry point, and the craniocaudal relationship between the iliac crest and the target disc (Fig. 7).Calculating the precise sagittal and axial insertion angles for the needle (Fig. 8).These calculated parameters formed the “surgical blueprint”.

In the conventional group, puncture, cannula placement, and surgical manoeuvres were performed based on surgeon experience, with trajectory angles estimated from preoperative wide-field-of-view MRI. Both groups adhered strictly to standard transforaminal endoscopic spine system protocols, with C-arm monitoring ensuring adherence to planned trajectories.

Outcome measures

Surgical Parameters: Number of puncture attempts(the number of times the needle was advanced percutaneously toward the target under real-time C-arm guidance until the desired trajectory was achieved), intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, channel establishment time, and endoscopic operating duration.

Complication Assessment: Documented incidents included dural tear, transient dysesthesia, and residual fragment retention.

Radiological Evaluation: Two blind radiologists evaluated intraoperative puncture images to assess the placement of the trocar tip within the pre planned safe triangle and the adequacy of intervertebral foramen shaping surgery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses employed SPSS version 20.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student's t-test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analysed via χ2 tests. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study establishes that preoperative 3D virtual planning substantially enhances the safety, efficiency, and accessibility of Percutaneous Transforaminal Endoscopic Discectomy (PTED), offering a particularly robust scaffold for surgeons during their early learning curve. The observed reductions in fluoroscopy frequency, cannula establishment time, and operative duration are not merely incremental improvements but indicative of a fundamental shift towards a structured, reproducible, and less hazardous surgical workflow. The lower complication rate in the 3D-planning group, albeit not statistically significant in this cohort, underscores a clinically vital trend towards enhanced patient safety for novice operators.

Learning curve acceleration and complication prevention

The learning curve for complex spinal procedures such as PTED is notoriously steep. A study involving 55 patients with lumbar disc herniation indicated a PTED learning curve of 31 cases [16].Another study involved 120 patients with lumbar disc herniation (PETD group: 87 cases, UBED group: 33 cases), Using cumulative sum analysis to evaluate the learning curve, it is shown that mastering PETD requires a learning curve of 40 cases, while UBED requires at least 15 cases to achieve proficiency [17]. Another study underlines the need for an experienced mentor for surgeons to safely adopt PTED (> 20 cases), beacuse duration of surgery and risk of repeated surgery are increased during the learning curve [18].Our 3D virtual planning platform directly confronts this challenge. By providing a patient-specific, interactive anatomical modelon’s initial experience before the first incision, it effectively front-loads the cognitive and technical rehearsal process. The surgeon can pre-emptively identify anatomical obstacles like a high-riding iliac crest and simulate a safe trajectory into Kambin’s triangle, thereby reducing the intraoperative trial-and-error that characterises early learning. This proactive rehearsal is crucial for mitigating complications such as nerve root irritation or dural tear, which are frequently encountered during this vulnerable phase. Our data suggest that this approach flattens the learning curve, enabling surgeons to achieve competency with greater confidence and a superior safety profile.This is highly consistent with the research results of Feng et al., that is the simulated PTED included puncturing and establishing the working channel (PEWC) and endoscopic discectomy offers a good realistic design and high education value [19].

Practical utility and accessibility in resource-varied settings

A deliberate innovation of our platform is its emphasis on practical utility over technological complexity. The “Dual-Axis Matching” method is designed to integrate seamlessly with the standard surgical workflow, using conventional C-arm fluoroscopy equipment available in most operating theatres. It bridges the pre-operative 3D plan with familiar intraoperative 2D views, providing an intuitive visual guide for real-time trajectory correction without the need for costly capital investment in intraoperative navigation systems [20]. This design philosophy makes the platform particularly suitable for adoption in resource-limited institutions, where access to advanced technology is often constrained. The significant reduction in fluoroscopy exposure and operative time achieved with this simple platform demonstrates that substantial improvements in surgical care are attainable without prohibitive expense, thereby democratising access to advanced surgical planning.Chai et al. used a simplified optical navigation system draw the same conclusion, using optical tracking technology and 3D Slicer software can effectively assist percutaneous lumbar transforaminal puncture procedures[21]. By provides direct visualization of the target anatomy, enables precise localization of puncture points and accurate adjustment of needle trajectories, thereby reducing procedural uncertainty and technical difficulty,decreasing both the number of puncture attempts and fluoroscopy exposures.

Preoperative planning as a mechanism for reducing early complications

The early phase of a surgeon's PTED practice is inherently linked to a heightened risk of complications, often stemming from suboptimal cannula positioning [22, 23]. Our 3D planning system serves as a powerful risk mitigation tool by enabling precise, quantitative preoperative planning. The platform allows for the meticulous design of the skin entry point, insertion angle, and depth to ensure the working cannula optimally engages the herniated fragment while respecting the boundaries of the safe triangle. This precision directly addresses common causes of failure in early cases, such as incomplete decompression from residual fragments or iatrogenic nerve injury. The preoperative 3D planning coordinates must be mapped onto intraoperative C-arm anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic images, followed by dynamic calibration between the 3D virtual trajectory and intraoperative 2D fluoroscopic images (G-arm/C-arm) to achieve real-time trajectory correction. Combining Dual Axis Matching Technology, by transforming the procedure from an empirical exploration to the execution of a verified plan, the cognitive load on the surgeon is markedly reduced. Notably, during actual surgery, puncture accuracy may significantly decline due to factors such as patient positional changes, localization deviations, and tissue traction. This allows the operator to focus on technical execution rather than spatial orientation, thereby minimising the risk of procedural errors. The trend towards fewer complications in our 3D group is a direct clinical reflection of this enhanced control and foresight.By integrating preoperative 3D visualization models with intraoperative fluoroscopy, the puncture trajectory can be calibrated in real time. Preoperative 3D visualization reconstruction reduces intraoperative nerve traction and injury risks, significantly improving puncture accuracy.

Comprehensive cost-effectiveness analysis

The economic implications of integrating 3D virtual planning extend beyond the immediate surgical session and present a compelling value proposition. The 22%(4/18) reduction in fluoroscopy frequency directly lowers consumable costs and reduces the occupational radiation burden on staff. The significantly shorter operative and cannula establishment times translate into more efficient use of valuable operating theatre resources, potentially increasing procedural throughput.

Most importantly, the observed reduction in complication rates carries profound economic weight. Postoperative complications, such as dural tears or residual fragments, frequently necessitate costly interventions including revision surgery, prolonged hospitalisation, and additional imaging [24, 25]. Research in spinal fusion has demonstrated that surgical strategies which effectively reduce complications can lead to substantial cost savings [26, 27]. For instance,

A study on the long-term cost-effectiveness of spinal cord stimulation therapy for patients with failed back surgery syndrome showed that from 2000 to 2012, only 4.3% of FBSS patients in the United States received SCS treatment. Compared with conventional treatment patients, the long-term annual total cost of these patients is significantly reduced. Although implantation of the SCS system may result in short-term cost increases within one year, the cumulative annual cost significantly decreases in the following 9 years after implantation [28]. Whilst our study did not perform a formal cost-utility analysis, the logical extrapolation is that the initial investment in planning software and time would be offset by downstream savings from fewer adverse events and more efficient resource utilisation. This positions 3D planning not as an added expense, but as a cost-effective strategy for improving the value of spinal care.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the promising results, this study has limitations. The retrospective design and single-centre experience may introduce selection bias. The relatively small sample size limits the statistical power to detect significant differences in complication rates. Additionally, the cost-benefit analysis was inferred rather than directly measured; future prospective studies should include formal economic evaluation to validate the cost-effectiveness of the platform.Discrepancies in puncture angles due to differences between preoperative CT scans and intraoperative positioning, as well as the need for improved precision in adapting preoperative surgical parameters during the procedure.

Further research should focus on multicentre validation and explore the integration of emerging technologies such as patient-specific 3D-printed guides derived from virtual plans. Additionally, the application of artificial intelligence for automated trajectory optimisation or predictive complication modelling could further enhance the precision and accessibility of the platform.

Conclusion

Preoperative three-dimensional virtual planning significantly enhances procedural precision, reduces radiation exposure, and improves safety parameters in percutaneous transforaminal endoscopic discectomy. This approach proves particularly valuable for surgeons during the initial learning curve, while simultaneously establishing an anatomical foundation for future navigational advancements in spinal endoscopy.
