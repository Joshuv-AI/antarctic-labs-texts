Periodontal disease and cardiovascular events: mechanisms, biomarkers, and clinical translation
Authors: Teng Qi, Ting-Xuan Huang, Jin-Suo Xiao, Zhen-Tao Zhu, Si-yu Ren, Pei-Lin Xie, Ling-Jun Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review integrates epidemiological, mechanistic and clinical evidence demonstrating that patients with periodontitis carry higher risks of cardiovascular events than healthy individuals. Literature from major databases was analyzed narratively. Findings indicate higher risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, and atrial fibrillation driven by systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, vascular invasion, oral-gut-vascular, and epigenetic pathways. Biomarkers show diagnostic potential, while therapy improves surrogate outcomes but not hard endpoints. The review integrates evidence to inform risk stratification and prevention strategies.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases

Introduction

The association between the oral cavity and the cardiovascular system is not a recent discovery, yet its systematic examination within the framework of modern medical “oral-systemic health” remains a dynamic and controversial frontier field. Chronic oral infectious diseases such as periodontitis and dental caries affect billions of people worldwide, and their burden coexists with that of cardiovascular disease, the leading global cause of death. This has prompted researchers to explore the potential pathophysiological links between the two that extend beyond mere comorbidity. Numerous observational studies have consistently indicated that periodontitis is significantly associated with an increased risk of various adverse cardiovascular events, including atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, stroke, and atrial fibrillation1–3. However, the nature of this association, whether it represents a direct causal relationship, a concomitant phenomenon driven by shared risk factors such as smoking, diabetes, and socioeconomic status, or a bidirectional vicious cycle of mutual promotion, has long been a central topic of academic debate4–6. Existing evidence has revealed a strong biological plausibility involving complex mechanisms such as chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, direct microbial invasion, immune dysregulation, and endothelial dysfunction7–9. Nevertheless, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) face methodological challenges and heterogeneous results when it comes to confirming that periodontal interventions can clearly reduce hard cardiovascular endpoints5,7,10. This review aims to collect the latest evidence on the association between oral diseases and cardiovascular health, and conduct an integrated analysis of existing literature from multiple dimensions including epidemiological associations, molecular and microbial mechanisms, biomarker potential, intervention strategies, and future research directions. This narrative review is based on a targeted literature search in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, focusing on high-quality meta-analyses, prospective cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and mechanistic investigations published from 2010 to 2026. It is intended to clarify the boundaries of current understanding and provide evidence-based references for interdisciplinary clinical practice and public health decision-making. This review is structured around three core questions: the strength and limitations of epidemiological evidence, the biological plausibility of pathological pathways, and the clinical value of periodontal interventions and oral biomarkers.

Epidemiological evidence for the association between oral diseases and adverse cardiovascular events

Epidemiological evidence supporting the link between oral diseases, particularly periodontitis, and cardiovascular disease risk has been documented in numerous studies. A meta-analysis encompassing more than 7.66 million participants across 36 studies provided observational evidence suggesting that patients with periodontal disease have a higher risk of stroke than healthy individuals. Among them, patients with periodontitis carry a higher risk than those with gingivitis, and the risk of ischemic stroke is higher than that of hemorrhagic stroke3. Similarly, periodontitis is associated with an increased risk of acute coronary syndrome (ACS); studies have identified periodontitis as an independent risk marker for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and patients with periodontal disease are often accompanied by more severe cardiovascular complications1. In the field of cardiac arrhythmias, individuals with moderate to severe periodontitis have a significantly elevated risk of developing atrial fibrillation (AF), and existing AF patients with comorbid periodontitis face higher risks of arrhythmia recurrence, stroke, and other cardiovascular events2.

Tooth loss, the ultimate manifestation of deteriorating oral health, is also closely associated with cardiovascular risk. A cohort study of the American Indian population found that edentulism at baseline was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events during follow-up11. Another study based on nationally representative data in the United States also indicated that tooth loss is a significant predictor of cardiovascular disease mortality12. Notably, these associations appear to be independent of gender; a meta-analysis showed that the link between periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease is significant in both men and women with no notable gender differences13.

However, the interpretation of epidemiological associations requires caution. While observational studies yield highly consistent results, Mendelian randomization studies have not provided evidence of a genetic causal relationship between periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease5,6. This suggests that the observed strong association may be largely driven by shared environmental and behavioral risk factors (e.g., smoking, unhealthy diet, low socioeconomic status) and chronic inflammatory states throughout the life course, rather than direct genetic causal pathways6,14. In addition, heterogeneity exists among studies in terms of periodontal disease definition (e.g., clinical indicators, self-reporting, diagnostic codes), confirmation of cardiovascular endpoints, and adequacy of confounding factor adjustment, which may lead to variations in the strength of the association4,15.

In addition to the above considerations, several contradictory or null findings warrant attention. A study reported that although cross-sectional analyses showed a positive association between apical periodontitis and cardiovascular disease, the pooled odds ratio decreased and lost statistical significance in case-control studies16. This suggests that current evidence is insufficient to support a strong association between apical periodontitis and cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, while periodontal treatment improves local inflammation and some systemic inflammatory markers, whether these changes translate into reduced cardiovascular endpoints remains uncertain10,17. The observed associations between periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease may be largely driven by shared environmental and behavioral risk factors such as smoking18,19, unhealthy diet20,21, and low socioeconomic status22,23, rather than direct causal pathways. These confounders are closely interrelated and operate throughout the life course. Heterogeneity in study design, including differences in periodontal disease definition, validation of cardiovascular endpoints, and adequacy of confounding adjustment, further contributes to inconsistent results4,15.

Despite these caveats, the extensive and consistent epidemiological evidence, combined with strong biological plausibility, is sufficient to establish periodontal disease as an important marker of cardiovascular risk, and indicates that attention to oral health should become an indispensable part of the comprehensive risk management of cardiovascular disease. Overall, epidemiological studies consistently report a positive association, but residual confounding limits causal inference, which calls for further mechanistic exploration.

Molecular and microbial mechanisms underlying oral disease-driven cardiovascular pathophysiology

Building on the population-level epidemiological observations, mechanistic studies have uncovered multiple interconnected pathways through which periodontal pathogens may drive vascular damage, providing biological plausibility for the observed association. Oral diseases, especially periodontitis, are not isolated local infections; they systemically affect cardiovascular health and drive pathological processes such as atherosclerosis through a variety of intertwined molecular and microbial mechanisms. The core lies in the transformation of local chronic inflammation into a sustained, low-grade systemic inflammatory burden.

Systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction

Periodontal pathogens and their products (e.g., lipopolysaccharide, LPS) cross the epithelial barrier to enter the systemic circulation, or induce the production of a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) in local gingival tissues, leading to elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP)7,8,24. This persistent inflammatory state directly impairs vascular endothelial function (Fig. 1). Endothelial cells are key regulators of vascular health, responsible for producing nitric oxide (NO) to maintain vascular relaxation and anti-inflammation. Inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress derived from periodontal tissues (e.g., reactive oxygen species generated via NADPH oxidase) can reduce the bioavailability of NO and cause uncoupling of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), thereby triggering endothelial dysfunction, the initiating step of atherosclerosis8,25,26. Animal experiments have confirmed that LPS from Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) can induce cardiac oxidative damage, cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrosis by activating the TLR4/NADPH oxidase signaling pathway, ultimately leading to cardiac insufficiency27,28. In addition, periodontitis-related systemic inflammation can exacerbate poor healing after myocardial infarction by upregulating factors such as Ccl12 to inhibit the transformation of fibroblasts into reparative myofibroblasts and delay scar formation29.

Direct microbial invasion

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the “oral-vascular” hypothesis proposes that oral pathogens can directly colonize the vascular wall via transient bacteremia. A variety of periodontal pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), Tannerella forsythia (T. forsythia) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), have been detected in atherosclerotic plaques30,31. These bacteria are not passively present; they actively participate in plaque development and destabilization through specific virulence factors. For example, F. nucleatum, a so-called “bridge bacterium”, not only promotes the formation of oral biofilms but also binds to host cell carbohydrates through its outer membrane autotransporters (e.g., Fap2), and has been found to play a role in systemic diseases including cardiovascular disease and colorectal cancer30,32,33. P. gingivalis can increase vascular permeability through its outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) and directly degrade endothelial cell adhesion molecules (e.g., PECAM-1, VE-cadherin), disrupting vascular integrity34–36. The presence of certain periodontal bacteria (e.g., F. nucleatum) in the oral cavity has also been found to be independently associated with the severity of cerebral small vessel disease in patients with ischemic stroke37.

Emerging Mechanisms

Research has moved beyond the traditional “bacteremia-inflammation” paradigm to reveal a more complex network of interactions, which we refer to as the emerging mechanistic framework. As illustrated in Fig. 1, oral pathogens drive systemic inflammation, activate the oral-gut-vascular axis, and directly invade blood vessels, leading to endothelial injury and atherosclerosis. The concept of the “oral-gut-vascular axis” has been proposed to explain how oral bacteria reach the gut via swallowed saliva, colonize the intestinal environment, and subsequently influence systemic inflammation and cardiovascular pathophysiology38,39. Specific bacterial species have been implicated in this process. For instance, Veillonella spp., identified by Constantino-Jonapa et al. in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, can traverse the gastrointestinal tract and potentially modulate the microbiome along this axis38. P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and Klebsiella spp. are among the periodontal pathogens that can survive gastric acidity, colonize the gut, and induce gut dysbiosis39. This dysbiosis leads to increased intestinal inflammation and permeability, promoting the systemic dissemination of bacterial products. A key downstream consequence is the alteration of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles, particularly reductions in butyrate and propionate, which play crucial roles in maintaining gut barrier integrity and exerting anti-inflammatory effects via G protein-coupled receptors38. These SCFA changes correlate with elevated systemic inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP, thereby linking gut microbiome perturbations directly to vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis progression38,39.

Epigenetic reprogramming is another cutting-edge mechanism. Chronic periodontal infection may induce persistent epigenetic changes (e.g., alterations in DNA methylation patterns) in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. Even after the infection is controlled, immune cells (e.g., neutrophils) remain in a “hyper-reactive” state, continuously promoting arterial inflammation40,41. In addition, the molecular mimicry mechanism has also attracted attention; for example, cross-immune reactions between bacterial heat shock proteins and their human homologs may trigger autoimmune attacks on vascular tissues8. These mechanisms together form a complex pathological network through which periodontal disease affects the cardiovascular system, explaining why local oral infections can have profound effects on distant organs42.

Potential of oral biomarkers in cardiovascular risk assessment and early warning

The elucidated pathological mechanisms further support the potential of oral-derived molecules as non-invasive tools for cardiovascular risk stratification. Given the close pathological connection between the oral cavity and the cardiovascular system, oral-derived biomarkers show great potential in cardiovascular disease risk stratification and early risk prediction due to their non-invasiveness and ease of acquisition. These biomarkers are mainly derived from saliva, gingival crevicular fluid, and oral microbiome analysis.

Salivary biomarkers have emerged as a promising area of investigation. As a “mirror of the body”, saliva contains a wealth of proteins, nucleic acids, microorganisms and metabolites that can reflect local and systemic conditions. Studies have shown that specific microbial profiles in saliva can serve as predictive indicators of systemic risk. For example, a study based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found that the log-ratio of periodontitis-associated Treponema to health-associated Corynebacterium in the baseline salivary microbiome, referred to as the Microbial Indicator of Periodontitis (MIP), can independently predict cardiometabolic disease mortality, and its predictive ability even exceeds that of traditional clinical periodontal indicators43. In addition, levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and markers reflecting endothelial dysfunction (e.g., asymmetric dimethylarginine, ADMA) in saliva are altered in patients with periodontitis and cardiovascular disease, and may serve as auxiliary indicators for risk monitoring44–46.

The oral microbiome acts as a promising predictive tool. The diversity of the oral microbiome and the load of specific pathogens or serum antibody levels are associated with cardiovascular disease. In addition to the aforementioned MIP, studies have found that the α and β diversity of the oral microbiome in patients with STEMI differs from that in healthy individuals, and the abundance of specific genera (e.g., Prevotella) is positively correlated with the level of the inflammatory marker IL-647. Serum antibody titers against periodontal pathogens (e.g., P.gingivalis) have also been found to be independently associated with a history of myocardial infarction48. These findings suggest that detection based on salivary metagenomics or serum antibody profiles may help identify individuals at high cardiovascular risk.

Clinical indicators and comprehensive models also have indicative significance. Simple oral clinical indicators are informative: for example, the periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA), an indicator quantifying the total burden of periodontal inflammation, has been found to be an independent predictor of the severity of coronary atherosclerosis49,50. In the future, integrating multi-dimensional oral data (clinical indicators, microbiome, salivary proteome/metabolome) and constructing predictive models using artificial intelligence/machine learning is a highly promising direction51–53. For example, existing studies have used electronic dental record data and machine learning algorithms to successfully predict the risk of periodontal disease progression and identify new associated factors, including a history of cardiovascular disease52. Despite promising predictive performance, most oral biomarkers currently lack validation in large independent cohorts, and standardization of detection protocols and reference ranges remains a major barrier to routine clinical application. Nevertheless, their value as low-cost, non-invasive supplementary screening tools cannot be ignored, especially in resource-limited settings or scenarios requiring dynamic monitoring46,54.

Impact of oral health interventions on cardiovascular risk and multidisciplinary management strategies

Given the established epidemiological association and mechanistic links, a key clinical question is whether periodontal treatment can translate into reduced cardiovascular risk. Existing evidence mainly comes from interventional studies evaluating the effects of periodontal therapy on cardiovascular surrogate endpoints (e.g., inflammatory markers, endothelial function), and the results generally support a positive intervention effect.

Periodontal therapy improves systemic inflammation and vascular parameters. Multiple randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have shown that non-surgical periodontal therapy (e.g., scaling, root planing) can significantly reduce the levels of systemic inflammatory markers, including CRP, interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)7,55,56. At the same time, therapy can also improve endothelial function, such as increasing flow-mediated vasodilation57 (Fig. 2). These positive changes can also be observed in patients with comorbid CVD, and periodontal therapy as an adjuvant measure may help optimize their systemic inflammatory state56,58. A study even found that patients who respond poorly to periodontal therapy (with significant inflammation remaining after treatment) have a higher risk of future cardiovascular events than those with a good treatment response, indicating the importance of successful control of periodontal inflammation59.

Therapeutic benefits of periodontal intervention on cardiovascular risk reduction.

Periodontal interventions, including scaling and root planing, mitigate cardiovascular risk through three key intermediate pathways. These interventions reduce systemic levels of pro-inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). They also improve vascular endothelial function by enhancing vasodilation and restoring endothelial integrity. Additionally, periodontal intervention reduces atherosclerotic plaque burden within vascular walls. Together, these intermediate improvements translate into tangible clinical benefits: a reduced incidence of cardiovascular events, improved long-term patient prognosis, and a lower risk of hospital readmission. While evidence for partial clinical benefits is clear, data supporting hard clinical endpoints remains limited.

Evidence regarding the impact on hard endpoints remains limited and challenging to interpret. However, large-scale, long-term randomized controlled trials on whether periodontal therapy can directly reduce hard cardiovascular endpoints such as myocardial infarction and stroke are still lacking. Observational studies provide some clues: for example, maintaining good oral hygiene behaviors (e.g., brushing teeth ≥3 times a day) is associated with a lower risk of stroke60, and periodontal therapy is associated with a reduced risk of atrial fibrillation2. But definitive causal conclusions still await specially designed cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOT)10. Nevertheless, from the perspective of risk-benefit ratio and overall health, treating periodontal disease has independent health value, and its improvement of quality of life and masticatory function is an important goal in itself.

Moving towards an integrated multidisciplinary management strategy is imperative. Oral diseases and various systemic diseases (especially diabetes and cardiovascular disease) share risk factors and pathological bases, which strongly call for collaborative interdisciplinary management14,61 (Fig. 2). Clinicians should establish clear communication channels between dentists and physicians or cardiologists. Patients at high cardiovascular risk should receive basic oral health screening and education; conversely, patients with moderate to severe periodontitis, especially young patients, should be regarded as individuals with potentially increased cardiovascular risk and advised to undergo cardiovascular risk factor assessment1,62. Management strategies can follow the “common risk factor pathway” and the “targeted inflammation pathway”. The former emphasizes joint intervention of shared behavioral risk factors such as smoking, unhealthy diet, and lack of physical activity63. The latter focuses on reducing the shared inflammatory burden through periodontal therapy, pharmacotherapy (e.g., statins may have pleiotropic effects that improve periodontal status while lowering blood lipids64,65), and even novel pro-resolving inflammatory therapies (e.g., the use of pro-resolving lipid mediator analogs66,67). Incorporating oral health assessment and promotion into the primary and secondary prevention systems of cardiovascular disease is a key step to achieve patient-centered, comprehensive health management68,69.

Critical evaluation of residual confounding and implementation barriers

Building on the epidemiological and clinical evidence discussed above, it is essential to critically appraise the limitations that may affect causal inference and the translation of these findings into clinical practice.

Although shared risk factors such as smoking, diet, and socioeconomic status are frequently discussed, residual confounding remains a major limitation in observational studies. Even after multivariable adjustment, unmeasured or imprecisely measured confounders including cumulative stress, health literacy, and lifetime access to dental care may bias effect estimates. For example, individuals with poor oral hygiene often have other unfavorable lifestyle behaviors that are difficult to fully capture in epidemiologic models. Moreover, the timing and duration of exposure to periodontitis are rarely accounted for, which may lead to underestimation or overestimation of cardiovascular risk. Therefore, while periodontal disease is a robust risk marker, its independent causal contribution to cardiovascular disease remains uncertain without more rigorous study designs such as natural experiments or long-term randomized trials targeting hard endpoints.

Despite growing recognition of the oral-cardiovascular link, translating this knowledge into clinical practice faces substantial barriers. First, lack of awareness among cardiologists and primary care physicians about the relevance of periodontal disease means that oral health is rarely assessed during cardiovascular risk evaluation. Second, reimbursement structures do not support interdisciplinary collaboration; dental and medical insurance systems are largely separate, creating financial disincentives for referrals or shared care models. Third, patient adherence to periodontal therapy and recommended oral hygiene practices is often low, particularly in high-risk populations who may also struggle with adherence to cardiovascular medications. Overcoming these barriers will require educational initiatives for medical and dental professionals, integrated care pathways with appropriate funding models, and patient-centered strategies to improve self-management.

Future research directions and technological frontiers

Despite considerable progress, the field of oral-cardiovascular health still faces many unknowns. Future research needs to seek breakthroughs in three dimensions: deepening mechanistic understanding, overcoming methodological limitations, and promoting clinical translation.

Deepening and integrating mechanistic research is essential. Future research needs to move beyond studies of single pathogens or linear pathways to explore the complex network of interactions between the oral microbiome (including bacteria, viruses, fungi) as a functional whole and the host immune system. Key points to clarify include: 1) the specific action pathways and key regulatory nodes of systemic communication between the oral cavity and distant vascular sites38,39; 2) how epigenetic memory mediates the long-term effects of previous periodontal infection on the cardiovascular system and whether such “imprinting” is reversible41,70; 3) the precise molecular mechanisms by which specific periodontal pathogens (e.g., F.nucleatum, P.gingivalis) promote thrombosis, plaque instability and cardiac arrhythmias2,30,32. It is necessary to combine animal models (e.g., ApoE-/- mouse models71,72) and emerging organ-on-a-chip technology to study the dynamic interactions of host-microbe-drugs at the vascular interface under conditions that simulate the physiological microenvironment73,74.

Methodological innovation and precision medicine are pivotal. Large-sample, prospective cohort studies and well-designed RCTs are crucial for establishing causal relationships and evaluating intervention effects. Future RCTs should aim to assess the effects of periodontal interventions targeting hard cardiovascular endpoints and fully consider individual differences in treatment response10. Artificial intelligence and machine learning will play a transformative role in this field, including: mining electronic health record data to identify new risk patterns and predictive models52; analyzing multi-omics data (metagenomics, metabolomics, proteomics) to discover new diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets75,76; and realizing personalized assessment of oral disease risk and cardiovascular risk. Methods such as Mendelian randomization can also help clarify confounding factors at the genetic level and infer causal relationships more rigorously5.

Clinical translation and public health practice are the ultimate goals of research. Research must ultimately serve clinical and public health practice. Key directions include: 1) developing and validating simple oral risk screening tools that can be used in primary care or cardiovascular specialist clinics, such as saliva-based non-invasive test panels46,54; 2) evaluating the cost-effectiveness of different periodontal treatment regimens (non-surgical vs. surgical, adjuvant antibacterial/anti-inflammatory therapy) for populations at high cardiovascular risk; 3) exploring innovative therapies targeting shared inflammatory pathways, such as drugs that regulate the inflammatory resolution pathway (pro-resolving lipid mediators) or vaccines against specific virulence factors66,77; 4) carrying out community-based integrated programs for oral health promotion and cardiovascular disease prevention, with special attention to populations with limited medical resources and inadequate services to address health inequities78,79. Through close cooperation of interdisciplinary teams (dentistry, cardiology, epidemiology, bioengineering), it is expected to translate scientific consensus into practical actions to improve global cardiovascular health.

Conclusion

The association between periodontal diseases and adverse cardiovascular events is supported by extensive epidemiological, mechanistic, and clinical evidence, yet critical distinctions remain. While observational studies consistently show a positive link, Mendelian randomization analyses have not confirmed genetic causality, and residual confounding by shared risk factors such as smoking, unhealthy diet, and low socioeconomic status likely explains a substantial portion of the observed association; therefore, periodontitis should be viewed primarily as a robust risk marker rather than an established independent cause. Nevertheless, improving oral health has intrinsic benefits including reduced local inflammation and better quality of life. Clinically, we recommend that cardiologists and primary care physicians routinely inquire about oral health and refer patients with periodontitis for dental evaluation, while dentists should encourage patients with moderate to severe periodontitis to undergo cardiovascular risk assessment. Priority research questions include long-term randomized controlled trials powered for cardiovascular events, mechanistic studies of the oral-gut-vascular axis and epigenetic reprogramming, validation of salivary biomarkers, and development of integrated care models that overcome barriers in awareness, reimbursement, and patient adherence. Thus, although causal proof is still lacking, incorporating oral health assessment into comprehensive cardiovascular risk management is a low-risk, high-value strategy.
