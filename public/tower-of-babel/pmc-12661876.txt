Sacral florid reactive periostitis: a case report
Authors: Xianwei Han, Huijun Jiang, Ming Chen, Tao Huang, Li Xiong, Tongtao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFlorid reactive periostitis is a rare benign periosteal lesion predominantly affecting short tubular bones of the hands and feet, with only occasional reports in long bones. This case study discusses the clinical diagnosis and treatment of a patient with extremely rare florid reactive periostitis involving the sacrum.Case presentationA 12-year-old Chinese female presented with unexplained sacrococcygeal pain and swelling. Comprehensive evaluation included computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging, histopathological analysis, and immunohistochemistry (SATB2:+, p53: weak + , Ki-67: 60%). USP6 gene fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing was performed to exclude malignancy. Imaging revealed expansive bone destruction with periosteal reaction and soft tissue edema, initially suspicious for malignancy. Pathological examination confirmed florid reactive periostitis, characterized by fibroblastic proliferation, osteoid formation, and chronic inflammation. Conservative treatment (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and immobilization) led to significant symptom resolution and radiographic improvement at 2-month follow-up.ConclusionThis case expands the anatomical spectrum of florid reactive periostitis to include the sacrum. Diagnosis requires multidisciplinary correlation of clinical, imaging, and pathological findings to differentiate florid reactive periostitis from malignant lesions. Conservative management may be effective for symptomatic sacral florid reactive periostitis.

Background

Florid reactive periostitis (FRP), also known as fibro-osseous pseudotumor, is a rare benign periosteal reactive lesion. This lesion was first described and named by Spjut and Dorfman [1] in 1981. It is common in adolescent females and occurs more frequently in the short tubular bones of the hands and feet [2–4]. There are also a few reports on long bones, such as the humerus, ulna, radius, clavicle, and femur [5–12]. There are few research reports on FRP, and no patients with sacral lesions have been reported in the literature. It is an extremely rare condition, and its occurrence is likely influenced by a combination of unique factors present in this case. This paper focused on exploring the clinical characteristics, imaging, pathological manifestations, differential diagnosis, and treatment of FRP to improve clinicians’ understanding of this disease.

Case presentation

A 12-year-old Chinese female patient was admitted to the hospital 2 months ago owing to sacrococcygeal swelling and pain without any apparent cause. There were no clinical indicators of infection. The patient denied any history of trauma. There was tenderness (+) in the sacral region upon palpation but without obvious abnormality in the skin sensation. Computed tomography (CT) imaging showed extensive bone destruction in the sacrum. There was uneven density within the lesion, surrounding sclerotic osteophytes, and nodular high-density shadows around the lesion (Fig. 1A, B). The remaining sacral morphology was normal. Given these changes in the sacrum, our preliminary clinical diagnosis favored a benign lesion. However, malignancy could not be excluded, prompting us to investigate with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI showed abnormal signals at the distal end of the sacrum, with expansive growth, soft tissue masses around the sacrum, local cortical destruction, periosteal reaction, uneven internal signals, and obvious bone marrow edema in the local high signal intensity area (Fig. 1C–D). MRI results showed possible infectious lesions or malignant tumors. At the same time, no obvious abnormalities were found in the patient’s hematology. After obtaining the patient’s informed consent, a senior physician with 30 years of experience in bone tumors performed a puncture biopsy to obtain pathological information. Histopathological examination of the specimens showed bone tissue with chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, fibrous tissue hyperplasia, and dead bone formation with a small number of atypical cells observed locally (Fig. 2A). However, these findings could not fully resolve the possibility of malignancy. Immunohistochemical analysis showed the indices of the atypical cells as SATB2 (+), CK (−), p53 (weak+), p63 (−), and Ki-67 (+ , approximately 60%), as shown in Fig. 2B–D. Subsequently, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) detection revealed uncertain positivity of the USP6 gene. With the morphology and immunohistochemical results, florid periostitis was favored. Therefore, based on the pathological diagnosis and imaging findings, we diagnosed the sacral lesion as FRP. After evaluation, we did not perform surgical treatment but provided the patient with symptomatic treatment, including nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and local immobilization. Significant pain relief occurred within 2 weeks of NSAID therapy. The patient’s symptoms improved significantly after 2 months (Fig. 3A, B), demonstrating significant clinical improvement, with complete resolution of the soft tissue mass, remodeling of the previously destructed sacral cortex, and disappearance of bone marrow edema and periosteal reaction. The patient remained asymptomatic at 8-month and 1-year follow-ups. Serial monitoring showed persistent but stable presacral effusion at 8 months (Fig. 3C, D), while the anterior sacral bone destruction remained unchanged. At the 1-year follow-up (Fig. 3E, F), presacral effusion decreased compared with the 8-month evaluation, though with increased signal intensity in the posterior sacral space, probably attributed to prolonged sitting after the patient’s return to school activities. Discussion

FRP is a benign fibro-osseous lesion characterized by periosteal reaction and soft tissue inflammation [9, 10]. The exact cause of the disease is still unclear, and the main inducing factor is trauma [8]. However, there are also cases without a clear history of trauma [6]. The disease can be diagnosedon the basis of clinical manifestations, imaging, and pathological examinations. According to previous literature reports, FRP mainly affects the short tubular bones of the limbs and rarely involves the long bones; there have been no reports of sacrum involvement [6–13]. Its clinical manifestations include local swelling, pain, tenderness, and congestion on the skin surface. Rapid periosteal ossification and no change in soft tissue swelling on imaging may be the most typical manifestation of FRP [13]. In the case reported by Park et al. [6], the swelling was primarily soft tissue swelling, and ossification did not occur until 4 weeks after the onset of swelling. However, the swelling showed rapid progressive ossification and adjacent periosteal reaction after 4 weeks; this radiographic change is the most diagnostic feature of FRP. This imaging change is also similar to the case reported in our study. In the early stages of the disease, the lesions grow along the bone surface; in later stages, the lesions may ossify [13]. New bone is formed beneath the periosteum, and the underlying bone cortex is occasionally eroded [14, 15]. Histological examination typically shows lesions with varying amounts of osteoid, bone, cartilage, and proliferative fibroblastic tissue [16], with the fibroblastic component readily showing mitosis. Osteoclast giant cells, capillaries, chronic inflammatory cells, and myxoid matrix have also been reported [16]. Owing to the mild course of the disease, it was managed by observation after the initial biopsy. Considering the early stage and localization of the lesions, conservative treatment was adopted, including limb immobilization for rest and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for pain relief, while closely monitoring the progression of the lesions.

This case highlighted the first reported instance of florid reactive periostitis (FRP) involving the sacrum, an extremely rare occurrence not previously documented in Chinese or English literature. Clinicians should be aware that FRP can potentially involve atypical sites such as the sacrum, expanding the known spectrum of affected anatomical regions. Typical clinical presentations of FRP include local swelling, pain, and tenderness. Imaging features such as expansive growth, soft tissue masses, periosteal reaction, and rapid periosteal ossification are key indicators. In this case, the MRI findings of abnormal signals at the distal end of the sacrum, with expansive growth and obvious bone marrow edema, were particularly diagnostic. These features should prompt consideration of FRP in the differential diagnosis. Given the benign nature of FRP, conservative treatment strategies such as nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs and local immobilization can be effective, as demonstrated in this case. Close monitoring of the lesion’s progression is also important. The favorable response to conservative treatment in this patient underscores the importance of a tailored management approach based on the specific clinical scenario.

Diagnostic differentiation

The differential diagnosis of FRP typically includes bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP), osteomyelitis, myositis ossificans, and malignancies of the bone and cartilage surfaces, including periosteal and parosteal osteosarcomas [10]. BPOP is generally described as an outgrowth on the cortical surface of the phalanges of the hands and feet [17]. It consists of a disorganized mixture of bone, cartilage, and fibrous tissue, and the degree of involvement of the upper limbs is four times that of the lower limbs [16–18]. The lesion can present at any age, but individuals in their 20s and 30s are at higher risk [17–19]. Owing to its high recurrence rate, proliferative nature, and atypical microscopic manifestations, it is sometimes mistaken for a malignant process [20]. Some authors believe that the histological features of FRP and BPOP often overlap [13, 21], and as FRP develops or progresses to a certain stage, it exhibits the appearance of BPOP [13, 22]. However, in a patient, the imaging and histology of FRP and BPOP may not always be synchronized. Regardless of the imaging or histologic manifestations of the disease, the clinical relevance lies in recognizing that these aggressive lesions are benign and reactive rather than malignant [23]. Another benign lesion associated with long bones is myositis ossificans. This lesion often presents with a clear history of trauma and is more likely to occur in the arm or thigh muscles. On imaging, soft tissue ossification is visible at 4 weeks, and peripheral ossification that is not connected to the adjacent bones is a characteristic CT manifestation [24]. It is easily confused with FRP, and careful analysis of the relationship between the lesion and the adjacent bones is the key to differentiation.

Chronic osteomyelitis can also be used in the differential diagnosis. It is characterized by the presence of hematologic abnormalities in routine patients, with the imaging examination showing an osteolytic center with a circle of sclerosis. Bacterial culture of biopsy tissue can support the diagnosis and identification of specific pathogens. Histology usually only shows bone sclerosis with chronic inflammatory cell infiltration [25]. As it is an infectious lesion, treatment includes surgical debridement and sensitive antibiotic therapy.

Finally, malignant osteoblastic tumors, including parosteal and periosteal osteosarcomas, are considered as objects of differential diagnosis. Among them, parosteal osteosarcoma often occurs in the metaphysis of the distal femur and less frequently in the proximal tibia and humerus. It is composed of dense osteoid components that extend from the outer cortex through a narrow area [26], grow on the periosteal surface, and often lack a periosteal reaction. Histologically, it shows extensive atypical fibroblasts with osteogenic formation and visible nuclear divisions, while abundant cartilage components and cartilage ossification are uncommon. In terms of radiological manifestations, a narrow radiolucent zone (line sign) between the tumor and the attached bone is the characteristic imaging manifestation of parosteal osteosarcoma. Periosteal osteosarcoma [26] is seen in the diaphysis or metaphysis of the tibia and femur. Macroscopically, the tumor may form a cavity deep in the cortex with radiating striations, which may enter the medullary cavity as the disease progresses. Histologically, there are more moderately differentiated cartilage components, tumor-like bone, and interstitial cells with obvious atypia. This tumor has obvious cartilage components and a small amount of osteoid. Therefore, it should also be differentiated from FRP.

Conclusion

In this study, we report a case of FRP involving the sacrum, which was finally diagnosed by combining modern molecular techniques with clinical, radiological, and histological evaluations. This disease has not been reported in Chinese or English literature. The main clinical and imaging manifestations of FRP include local swelling, pain, MR mass, and adjacent linear periosteal reaction, characterized by rapid periosteal ossification. When such typical clinical and imaging manifestations appear, the possibility of FRP should be considered.
