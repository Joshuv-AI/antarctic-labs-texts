The Incidence of Sensorineural Hearing Loss Diagnoses in People with and without Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Kaisa Hokkinen, Aarno Dietz, Pasi Lampela, Sirpa Hartikainen, Anna‐Maija Tolppanen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) are both prevalent conditions among older adults. Although increasing number of studies have investigated SNHL as a risk factor for PD, it is not well known how diagnosed PD affects the likelihood of SNHL diagnosis.ObjectivesTo evaluate the temporal changes in the amount of SNHL diagnoses in relation to PD diagnosis.MethodsThis nationwide register‐based study included 22,189 community‐dwelling persons who received a clinically verified PD diagnosis in 1996–2015 in Finland and a 1:1 matched comparison cohort. PD diagnoses were identified from the Special Reimbursement Register and SNHL diagnoses were extracted from Care Register for Healthcare. Incidences of the diagnoses were calculated per 100 person‐years (PY) for each six‐month period from ten years before to ten years after PD diagnosis.ResultsBefore PD diagnosis the incidence of SNHL diagnosis was higher among people with PD (0.65/100 PY) than in the comparison cohort (0.58/100 PY, hazard ratio, 95% CI 1.11, 1.05–1.18) and increased similarly in both groups. After PD diagnosis the incidence was lower among people with PD (1.00 and 1.28/100 PY, hazard ratio, 95% Cl 0.78, 0.74–0.83) and declined shortly after PD diagnosis whereas it continued to increase in the comparison group.ConclusionsAfter PD diagnosis, the incidence of SNHL appears to decline compared to the control group, suggesting a possible deprioritization of hearing care. Our findings are also consistent with previous research showing an association between SNHL and PD, with a higher incidence of SNHL observed in individuals with PD.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 The incidence and prevalence of PD have risen rapidly during the last decades, 2 at least partly due to the aging of the general population, as PD mainly affects persons over the age of 50 years. 3 PD is a progressive disorder marked by aberrant protein accumulations that damage dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra located in the basal ganglia, resulting in parkinsonian motor symptoms. 4 According to the Movement Disorder Society Diagnostic Criteria, the core feature of PD is slowness of movement combined with tremor and/or muscular rigidity, or both. 5 However, PD is also a multisystem disease, and the pathological changes involve the whole nervous system causing a wide range of additional symptoms. 4 These lesser known non‐motor symptoms of PD can precede the motor symptoms even by a decade. 6 They include constipation, REM sleep behavior disorder, olfactory dysfunction, asymmetric shoulder pain, depression, 2 fatigue, autonomic dysfunction and visual disturbances. 4 There is accumulating evidence that auditory dysfunction could be a non‐motor feature or a pre‐diagnostic manifestation of PD. 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 In comparison to age‐ and sex matched controls, people with PD have higher hearing thresholds in pure‐tone average (PTA), 8 , 11 poorer speech discrimination 12 and lower otoacoustic emission responses. 13 There is emerging evidence of PD pathology involving both the peripheral and central parts of the auditory system. 4

Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), which also encompasses age‐related hearing loss, is another condition the prevalence of which increases with age, rising sharply after the age of 50. 14 SNHL is already recognized as a risk factor for cognitive impairment 15 and there is emerging evidence that SNHL starting in midlife could also be associated with risk of PD. 7 , 8 In the general population, hearing rehabilitation is underutilized regardless of its many known benefits, 14 but there are very few, if any, studies on hearing rehabilitation in people with PD and the auditory dysfunction associated with PD is not yet widely recognized in clinical practice. To the best of our knowledge, it has not been studied whether diagnosed PD alters the diagnostic trajectories of hearing loss.

To investigate this, we conducted a nationwide register‐based cohort study to calculate the incidence of SNHL diagnosis in relation to PD diagnosis from ten years before to ten years after PD diagnosis. Our aim was to evaluate the temporal changes in the amount of SNHL diagnoses in relation to PD diagnosis.

Methods

Study Design and Cohort

The Finnish Study on Parkinson's disease study (FINPARK) includes 22,189 persons who got a clinically confirmed diagnosis of PD between 1996 and 2015 and were community‐dwelling at the time of diagnosis. Data were extracted from the Finnish nationwide healthcare registers, including the Prescription Register (1995–2015; purchased prescription drugs in pharmacies), the Special Reimbursement Register (SRR) (1972–2015; recorded special reimbursements for medications for certain chronic comorbidities), the Finnish Care Register for Health Care (FCR) (1972–2015; diagnoses and procedures of hospital admissions) and the Statistics Finland (mortality 2005–2015, and socioeconomic data from 1972 to 2012). The researchers had access to pseudonymized data, and participants were not contacted at any point of the study. Due to this, informed consent or ethics committee approval were not required according to the Finnish law. The study conformed with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Identification of PD and Comparison Cohorts

We used the SRR maintained by the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (SII) to identify persons with incident PD diagnosis. A total of 29,942 people were eligible for reimbursement of anti‐Parkinson drugs. Special reimbursement for anti‐Parkinson drugs is only granted when a neurologist has confirmed the PD diagnosis and sent a written application to SII. The applications are reviewed in the SII and the eligibility for reimbursement requires anamnesis and description of the characteristic clinical features of PD. During the study period, the diagnostic criteria were consistent with the United Kingdom Parkinson's Disease Society Brain Bank's criteria. 16 As these reimbursed drugs can also be used for other conditions than PD, we excluded those who did not have ICD‐10 code G20 recorded in the SRR (n = 1244), those who were less than 35 years old at the time of PD diagnosis (n = 53) and those with diagnoses whose symptoms may be confused with PD (n = 6456) within 2 years of PD diagnosis (Table S1). This procedure resulted in a sample size of 22,189 persons. An age (±1 year), sex and region‐matched comparison group was identified from the SII database covering all residents. Each person with PD was matched to 1–7 comparison persons who were identified from the Finnish Population Register. The index date was the date of PD diagnosis for the matched referent. The comparison persons were not allowed to have purchases of dopaminergic PD medication (ATC code N04B) or the reimbursement code ever before the index date or 12 months after and during the diagnosis month of the referent with PD. They also had to remain alive and community‐dwelling during the month of index date, leaving altogether 148,006 comparison people.

Other Characteristics

We used three different Finnish nationwide health care registers (Table S2) to extract data on comorbidities (cardiovascular diseases, stroke, diabetes, asthma/COPD, any mood disorder other than bipolar disorder, traumatic brain injury, cataract surgery).

Identification of Age‐Related Hearing Loss and Associated Diagnoses

Data on SNHL and related diagnoses were extracted from the Care register for health care, a Finnish nationwide healthcare register, which includes both public and private healthcare services. We defined SNHL as having a diagnosis of sensorineural hearing loss (H90.3, H90.5), presbycusis (H91.1), sudden idiopathic hearing loss (H91.2) or hearing aid (Z45.3, Z46.1, Z97.4). In contrast to our previous study concerning AD and SNHL17, due to the young age of the study population, we excluded unilateral sensorineural hearing loss diagnosis H90.4 as it could confound the results. Incidence of presbycusis and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (H90.3, H91.1) and hearing aid use (Z45.3, Z46.1, Z97.4) were also studied as a sensitivity analysis. Detailed list of codes and diagnoses is given in the Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the software STATA 14 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were carried out using means, standard deviations (SD), and percentages. The results are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To compare characteristics between groups, an independent sample T test for continuous variables with normal distribution, and chi‐square test for categorical variables were applied.

The rate of incident SNHL diagnoses per 100 person‐years was calculated for the entire follow‐up, and separately for time periods before the index date and after the index date. Cox proportional hazard models with clustered sandwich estimator were used to compare the hazard of SNHL to the comparison group. People with SNHL before the follow‐up were removed from the incidence rate calculations. In addition, the rate in every 6‐month period starting from ten years before and until ten years after the index date were calculated for better visualization of changes in incidence. Rates were calculated by using stptime command and the confidence intervals were calculated using the quadratic approximation to the Poisson log likelihood for the log‐rate parameter. Competing risk models with Fine & Gray method were fitted to assess the impact of competing risk of death on the incidence of SNHL. The follow‐up ended on conversion to PD, death, or at the end of study follow‐up on December 31, 2019. In addition, comparison persons were censored on the date of eventual PD diagnosis.

The rates were calculated for the entire study population, and in age‐stratified analyses with the following categorization: <65 years, 65–74 years, and 75 years or older based on age on index date.

Data Sharing

Data is available from the authors upon reasonable request and with permission of the register maintainers.

Results

Characteristics of People with and without Parkinson's Disease According to Sensorineural Hearing Loss

The study included 22,189 persons with PD and 148,006 matched comparison persons without PD. Altogether 1402 persons with PD (6.3%) and 8447 persons without PD (5.7%) were diagnosed with SNHL before the index date of PD diagnosis (Table 1). There was no clinically relevant age difference at index date in persons with (76.9 years) and without (76.6 years) PD who had SNHL. The people with SNHL were older in both groups. Of people without SNHL diagnosis before the index date, approximately 7% with PD and 9% without PD were diagnosed with SNHL after the index date. All comorbidities were more prevalent among people with SNHL, in both people with and without PD.

Incidence of Hearing Loss

The incidence of SNHL diagnosis before the index date of PD diagnosis was higher among people with PD (0.65/100 PY) than in the comparison cohort (0.58/100 PY, hazard ratio, 95% CI 1.11, 1.05–1.18) and increased similarly in both groups when assessed in the whole cohort which included persons between the ages of 35–98 (Table 3 and Fig. 1). After the index date the incidence was lower among people with PD (1.00 and 1.28/100 PY, hazard ratio, 95% Cl 0.78, 0.74–0.83) and declined already shortly after PD diagnosis whereas it continued to increase in the control group (Fig. 1). During the entire follow‐up the incidence was lower among people with PD (0.79 and 0.88/100PY, 95% CI 0.92, 0.88–0.95). The association remained after accounting for competing risk of death.

When examined by age groups, the incidence of SNHL diagnosis before the index date was higher in persons with PD than without PD in the 75–98 year‐old people (1.19 and 1.07/100PY, hazard ratio, 95% CI 1.11, 1.04–1.19, respectively) (Table 3). The SNHL diagnosis incidence was higher in the older age groups in both the PD and control cohorts. After the index date the incidence of SNHL diagnosis was lower in persons with PD in all ages. When the entire follow‐up time was assessed, the incidence was lower in persons with PD also in all separate age groups.

Discussion

As our main finding, we have shown that the incidence of SNHL diagnosis in people with PD declines shortly after PD diagnosis and is lower than in people without PD. Considering the lower incidence of SNHL diagnosis in people with PD also during the whole follow‐up period, the finding is not explained by higher rate of SNHL diagnoses before the index date. Previous studies have reported that sensorineural hearing loss is not well perceived or reported by people with PD, 11 , 18 which may explain our findings. It is also plausible that individuals with newly diagnosed PD do not always prioritize reporting their hearing difficulties. Health care professionals encountering people with PD might also concentrate on other aspects of the sometimes complex medical care of this disease and the auditory evaluation gets thus postponed. In Finland, there is currently no systematic hearing screening program that would facilitate timely and easy access to hearing care. For people with PD—and their caregivers when they are involved—this poses a particular challenge, as they may not have the resources to initiate the often‐lengthy process of obtaining a personally fitted hearing aid, especially when faced with multiple other healthcare appointments. The patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals can consider the everyday use and maintenance of a hearing aid too challenging, particularly if help from another person is required with it. However, as there are no studies on the hearing rehabilitation of people with PD, these possible explanations for our findings remain speculative.

To our knowledge this is the first study to describe the temporal changes in the incidence of SNHL diagnosis before and after PD diagnosis, but our main finding is in line with the results of an earlier cohort study concerning AD, that showed a declining incidence of SNHL diagnosis very shortly after AD diagnosis. 17 One could speculate that cognitive decline due to these neurodegenerative diseases would add the challenges and lessen the benefits of diagnosing and treating hearing loss which could explain the findings of the present study and of the earlier one concerning AD. However, this viewpoint is not well‐grounded. According to the study protocol and diagnostic criteria (UK Brain Bank Diagnostic Criteria) at the time, severe cognitive decline at the time of PD diagnosis was an exclusion criterion. Moreover, it is known that approximately a quarter of PD patients can live even 20 years or more after the diagnosis. 19 Many aspects highlight the importance of hearing rehabilitation already in early PD. People with PD have some specific issues related to poor hearing, in addition to the general detrimental effects of hearing loss, which include social isolation, depression and cognitive decline. 20 PD often causes problems with speech production, eg, hypophonia, and for rehabilitation purposes it would be critical for people with PD to hear themselves and the ones they are communicating with. Poor hearing also makes auditory cues in eg, gait training less feasible. 20 There is not yet knowledge of the benefits of hearing rehabilitation in people with PD in particular, but it is known that people who are diagnosed with a major neurocognitive disorder have a better life quality after hearing rehabilitation. 21 One could assume that people with PD would benefit from hearing rehabilitation in much the same way as the general older adult population or people with major neurocognitive disorders.

We also found that the incidence of SNHL diagnosis was higher in the PD group than in the comparison group before PD diagnosis. Even though this study can only confirm a temporal but not a causal association with PD, these results are consistent with previous studies reporting that hearing loss, or auditory dysfunction, might be a non‐motor feature or a pre‐diagnostic manifestation of PD. 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 There is emerging evidence that supports the hypothesis that hearing loss is a risk factor for PD. 10 It has also been shown in a small study population of PD patients under 55 years of age, that hearing loss was more common than in age‐ and gender‐matched controls. 18 Interestingly, none of the study participants with PD and verified hearing impairment had reported any hearing difficulties. The higher prevalence of hearing loss in a subgroup of PD patients of an age where presbycusis is not yet frequent could signal that hearing loss is a non‐motor characteristic of PD. 18 Sensorineural hearing loss was recently also associated with incident PD in a large cohort study on veterans and the distribution of hearing aids seemed to reduce the risk for PD. 9 Still, nearly all sensory modalities are known to be affected by PD. It has, for example, been shown that olfactory dysfunction is a part of PD pathology. 22 PD patients have also been shown to have an array of visual disturbances 23 and changes of skin sensation. 22 It can be speculated whether hearing loss is a true non‐motor feature or prodrome of PD or just one general marker of neurodegeneration in the dopaminergic nerve cells. It is also possible that the multitude of prodromal symptoms of PD lead to more assessments within healthcare settings and concomitant hearing loss is identified in the process as well. Further studies are needed before the knowledge of the association is of clinical relevancy.

A strength of this study is the excellent registrar system in Finland which results in an extensive amount of useful data for researchers. Another strength is that the source of the data, the Finnish health care system, is publicly funded and widely used by persons with different socioeconomic backgrounds, which makes the results generalizable. The weakness of this study is the retrospective layout of the study and relying on diagnoses which are not always recorded as advised in the clinical context. However, we anticipate that the possible neglect of recording diagnoses does not differ between the study groups and does not thus affect the rate ratio of the results although the absolute rates may be underestimated. Another weakness is that we do not know the severity of PD at the time of PD or SNHL diagnosis as this knowledge cannot be deduced from in the registers. However, hearing rehabilitation in Finland is centralized to the secondary healthcare hearing centers where hearing loss is diagnosed and the diagnoses are recorded in the electronic medical record by otorhinolaryngologists. Recording SNHL diagnosis codes was mandatory at the time of the study for billing purposes and therefore, missing or wrong diagnose codes were routinely checked, which greatly increases the reliability of SNHL diagnoses and strengthens our results. Additionally, because hearing aids are fully reimbursed by state funding, most patients receive hearing rehabilitation in public hearing center settings.

In conclusion, our results show that the incidence of SNHL diagnosis in persons with PD declines shortly after PD diagnosis and is lower than in people without PD. The clinical implication of our finding is that there should be more awareness of the positive effects of hearing loss rehabilitation among doctors and other healthcare professionals treating PD patients, as well as among patients and caregivers. Health care professionals should actively facilitate the diagnostic process of hearing loss, particularly when patients or their caregivers lack the resources to seek treatment on their own. This requires ensuring easy access to hearing care services and implementing simple, widely available hearing screening methods that focus on everyday hearing performance rather than only on threshold levels.
