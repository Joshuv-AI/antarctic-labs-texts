Living with risk and uncertainty: a qualitative phenomenological study of women’s pregnancy and postpartum experiences in Saskatchewan during COVID-19
Authors: Daniel A. Adeyinka, Brandace Winquist, Joanne Sivertson, Leah Thorp, Elora Lake, Mamata Pandey, Barb Sauve, Jessica R. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted maternal healthcare globally, yet localized experiences within structurally constrained settings remain underexplored. In Saskatchewan, Canada, pre-existing inequities relating to geography and health care access shaped how women experienced pregnancy and early motherhood during the pandemic. This study explored the lived experiences of women who were pregnant and gave birth during the COVID-19 pandemic, with particular attention to how healthcare disruptions, social restrictions, and structural conditions shaped care experiences, distress, and resilience.MethodsThis qualitative interpretive phenomenology study drew on 35 individual interviews and three focus group discussions with a total of 51 women who were pregnant or gave birth between March 2020 and December 2022. Participants were purposively sampled across urban, rural, and northern Saskatchewan. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes were identified: living with pandemic risk and uncertainty; fragmentation of maternal care; emotional toll of isolation; and coping and resilience. Women described heightened fear and moral responsibility, disrupted access to prenatal and postpartum services, depersonalized communication, and limited mental health and lactation support, particularly in rural and northern regions. While virtual care improved accessibility for some women, it often lacked relational and embodied dimensions of care. Family support, trusted healthcare providers, and digital resources facilitated coping and resilience.ConclusionThe pandemic intensified existing inequities in maternal healthcare, revealing vulnerabilities in communication, continuity, and culturally responsive care. Findings underscore the need for equity-oriented emergency preparedness, hybrid models of care, strengthened maternal mental health services, and meaningful involvement of people with lived experience in planning future responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09224-9.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic triggered an unprecedented disruption to healthcare systems worldwide, with substantial consequences for maternal, newborn and child health services [1]. As health systems redirected personnel and other resources toward pandemic response, routine maternal care was interrupted through clinic closures [2], reduced in-person visits [2], and rapidly changing service delivery models such as transition to virtual care [3, 4], and restrictive hospital policies [5, 6]. Public health measures such as lockdowns and physical distancing further discouraged pregnant women from accessing laboratories, physician offices, and hospital-based care, resulting in widespread disruptions to prenatal and postpartum services and altered care experiences for expectant families [7, 8].

Beyond disruptions to care delivery [9–12], the pandemic profoundly affected the psychological well-being of pregnant and postpartum women. Fears of infection in clinical settings, particularly concerns about fetal health [13, 14], reduced availability of social support [15], economic uncertainty, unclear public health messaging, and limited access to healthcare contributed to increased psychological distress among pregnant and postpartum women [16–27]. This distress commonly manifested as heightened anxiety and depressive symptoms. Importantly, these mental health challenges are known to have downstream effects on both maternal and infant outcomes, including impaired maternal–infant bonding [28–32].

Pregnant women were uniquely affected during this period, both due to increased risks associated with COVID-19 infection and the indirect effects of healthcare system disruption. Empirical evidence shows that COVID-19 infection during pregnancy is associated with higher risks of severe maternal illness and adverse perinatal outcomes, including preeclampsia, preterm birth, stillbirth, renal failure, ante/peripartum infections, and hemorrhagic complications [28, 33–35]. These risks were particularly pronounced among women with symptomatic or severe infection, underscoring the importance of timely access to maternity care during public health emergencies [28, 33–37].

Evidence on whether prenatal care disparities widened during the pandemic is limited. However, anecdotal reports suggest that the pandemic’s impacts were not uniform; underserved populations bore a disproportionate burden. Public health countermeasures often exacerbated longstanding inequities in access to timely, quality care [38–40]. For example, Indigenous women and those living in rural and remote communities in Canada faced compounded barriers related to geographic isolation, limited healthcare infrastructure, and longstanding structural inequities [41]. Unexpectedly, an Ontario population-based study that used the Revised-Graduated Prenatal Care Utilization Index (R-GINDEX) found declines in overall use of prenatal care without widening disparities by socioeconomic status, maternal age, or rural residence [42]. However, R-GINDEX defines adequacy using a ≥ 13-visit guideline-based threshold, and the study’s rural–urban classification may misclassify some rural residents. These measurement choices may mask important geographic and experiential barriers to care. In contrast, qualitative evidence highlights heightened anxiety, uncertainty, and logistical challenges, particularly for rural women, related to long-distance travel and shifting health policies [9, 22, 26, 43–45]. These contrasting findings underscore the need for caution when relying solely on system-level utilization metrics to assess equity.

Saskatchewan represents a particularly important yet understudied context in which to examine these issues. The province is characterized by a largely rural and remote population [46, 47], long travel distances for maternity services, persistent healthcare workforce shortages [48], and one of the highest proportions of Indigenous peoples in Canada [49, 50]. Although virtual care was widely promoted by the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada as a strategy to maintain continuity of maternity services during the pandemic [44, 51, 52], its implementation depends on internet connectivity, digital resources, and patients being comfortable with this format, which may be lacking among socioeconomically disadvantage populations [44, 51, 52].

Despite the growing body of literature on perinatal experiences during COVID-19, a critical gap remains in understanding the lived experiences of pregnant and postpartum women in Saskatchewan. While qualitative studies from other Canadian provinces have documented psychosocial and healthcare challenges among pregnant women [9, 22, 26, 43–45], no published study to our knowledge has focused specifically on the province of Saskatchewan. This gap is notable given the province’s distinct demographic, geographic and healthcare contexts, as well as provincial and territorial differences in pandemic response that contributed to heterogeneous pandemic effects across Canada [53]. These factors shape perinatal care access and experiences in ways that cannot be readily extrapolated from findings in other jurisdictions. Although quantitative studies have described system-level trends in service utilization during the pandemic [54–57], many rely on administrative data with limited or imprecise measures of rural and remote residence, race, Indigenous identity, and household income. As a result, important inequities may be underestimated or obscured, and such data do not capture the personal, cultural, and emotional dimensions of navigating pregnancy and childbirth under prolonged public health restrictions.

To address this gap, this study used a qualitative phenomenological approach to explore the lived perinatal experiences of pregnant and postpartum women in Saskatchewan during the COVID-19 pandemic. By centering women’s voices, this study provides context-specific insights into how healthcare access, social support, and public health measures shaped perinatal experiences during a public health crisis. These findings aim to inform more equitable maternal health policy and service planning in Saskatchewan and offer transferable lessons for other rural and underserved settings facing future health emergencies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This phenomenological qualitative study was conducted as part of a larger mixed-methods study, Pregnant During a Pandemic, which examined the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on maternal and child health services and outcomes in Saskatchewan, Canada. A qualitative approach was selected to capture women’s lived experiences in their own words and to understand the meanings attached to these experiences. The study was guided by an interpretivist paradigm and informed by Heideggerian hermeneutics [58], recognizing that pregnancy and childbirth are embedded in social, cultural, and structural contexts. Data collection occurred between December 2023 and March 2024.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were women aged 18 years and older who resided in Saskatchewan at the time of pregnancy and/or childbirth, had given birth between March 2020 and December 2022 (a period characterized by shifting public health restrictions), and were able to speak and understand English. Purposive sampling was used to capture diverse experiences across age, parity, timing of birth, and geography (urban, rural, and northern regions). Oversampling from rural and northern regions was undertaken to address their historical underrepresentation in maternal health research.

Recruitment was facilitated through clinics, community organizations, social media, and word-of-mouth referrals. During recruitment, women were informed that participation could occur through either a one-on-one interview or a focus group discussion (FGD). Mode of participation was determined based on participant preference and comfort, as well as availability. Some women expressed discomfort with sharing personal or sensitive experiences in a group setting and therefore opted for individual interviews, while others preferred FGDs for the opportunity to share and reflect collectively. No participants were assigned to FGDs based on specific demographic or clinical characteristics.

In total, 51 women participated: 35 through individual interviews and 16 through three FGDs (4–7 participants per group). Demographic, obstetric and COVID-19 related information were collected via a short questionnaire. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. No prior relationship existed between participants and interviewer before study commencement.

Data collection

Semi-structured interview and FGD guides were developed by the research team, drawing on relevant literature and programmatic experience of the research team (see Additional files 1 and 2). Guides explored experiences of prenatal care, labor and delivery, postpartum care, mental health, social support, and interactions with health services during the pandemic. The guides were pilot-tested and refined before use. Open-ended questions and probes encouraged participants to describe their experiences and reflect on the impact of COVID-19 and the provincial response on their perinatal journeys.

All interviews and FGDs were conducted virtually via Webex platform by a trained female qualitative researcher (research specialist and PhD trainee) with experience in qualitative interviewing and focus group moderation. Participants were informed of the interviewer’s roles, the purpose of the study, and its focus on lived experiences of pregnancy and early motherhood during COVID-19. The same researcher moderated all FGDs and conducted all interviews to ensure consistency. She documented detailed field notes during and immediately after each session to capture contextual observations, group dynamics, non-verbal cues observable in the virtual environment, and early analytic insights.

Interviews ranged from 20 to 90 min, while focus groups 60–120 min. All sessions were conducted in English, audio-recorded with participant consent, and transcribed verbatim. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. Data saturation was defined as the point when no new themes or substantive insights emerged.

Member checking

Interview transcripts were shared electronically with participants within two weeks of completing their interviews. Participants were invited to review their transcripts for accuracy and to clarify or expand on their perspectives if desired. Minor clarifications were provided by some participants; no substantive changes to meaning were requested. Transcripts from FGDs were not shared with participants due to the interactive and multi-voiced nature of the group discussions and to preserve participant confidentiality within the group setting. Each participant received a CAD$50 gift card.

Data analysis

Data were manually analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework [59]. Transcripts were read repeatedly for familiarization. An initial codebook was developed after close reading of a subset of transcripts, capturing both descriptive and interpretive codes (e.g., fear of infection, fragmented care, loss of social support, adaptation and resilience). Coding proceeded inductively, with new codes added and existing codes refined as analysis progressed.

Codes were conceptually elevated into categories and themes through constant comparison across transcripts, analytic memo writing, and team discussions. For example, codes relating to delayed ultrasounds, missed laboratory tests, and cancelled services were grouped under broader categories reflecting healthcare system disruption, which were subsequently integrated into higher-order themes (e.g., systematic fragmentation in maternal care). The analytic process from raw data to themes is provided in Appendix file 3.

Coding was conducted by the first author (DAA) in collaboration and the research specialist. The second author (BW) reviewed and verified codes, sub-themes, and themes to enhance analytic rigor.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team included a multidisciplinary group comprising a patient partner (EL), obstetrician (JS), health system leaders (BW, JC, LT), and researchers with expertise in perinatal health (DAA, JS, BW), primary care and Indigenous health (MP, BS). The research team has experience in qualitative research and maternal health in Saskatchewan.

Reflexivity was integral to the phenomenological orientation of the study, guiding attention to participants’ meanings and preventing the imposition of pre-existing assumptions about pandemic-related care disruptions. Throughout data collection and analysis, the team engaged in reflexive memoing and analytic discussions to examine how researchers’ professional backgrounds and personal experiences of the pandemic could shape interpretation. These reflexive practices directly informed analytic decisions, including revisiting and refining codes when participants’ accounts challenged initial expectations, retaining themes related to resilience and positive care experiences alongside disruption, and integrating interview and focus group data to preserve both individual and shared meanings. This process supported an interpretive stance grounded in participants’ lived experiences rather than researchers’ preconceptions.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Credibility was enhanced through triangulation across interviews and FGDs, reflexive team discussions, and confirmability, and participant transcript review [60]. Confirmability was supported through audit trails and reflexive journaling. Transferability was enhanced through detailed description of context and participant characteristics. Dependability was ensured through systematic documentation of analytic procedures. Illustrative quotations are presented in the results to anchor findings in participants’ voices. Demographic details were self-reported; in some focus groups, age, parity, or year of delivery were not provided and are therefore not reported in those quotations. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [61] is included in Appendix file 4.

Results

Sample characteristics

All the 51 respondents were postpartum women. Demographic characteristics, obstetric history and COVID-19 experiences are described in Tables 1, 2 and 3. The average age of participants was 31 years (standard deviation: 4 years), with range of 24 to 40 years. The participants were diverse in geography: 22 (43%) from northern, 16 (31%) from southern, and 13 (26%) from urban (Regina/Saskatoon) regions of Saskatchewan. Twenty-one (41%) participants were primiparous, and most births 25 (49%) occurred in the year 2020 (first year of COVID-19 pandemic). The majority, 44 (86.3%) initiated prenatal care in the first trimester. Most participants were delivered by obstetricians 27 (52.9%), followed by midwives 12 (23.5%) and family physicians 8 (15.7%). Nearly all births 47 (92.9%) occurred in healthcare facilities and carried to full-term 43 (84.3%). Less than half of the participants 20 (39.2%) reported that either themselves or their family members tested positive for COVID-19, with 37 (72.5%) received at least one dose of COVID-19 vaccine.

Emergent themes

Analysis of interviews and FGDs generated four overarching themes and 15 subthemes that captured how women described pregnancy, birth, and early motherhood during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The themes were: (1) living with pandemic risk and uncertainty, (2) systematic fragmentation in maternal care, (3) emotional toll of isolation and disconnection, and (4) coping, resilience, and adaptive strategies.

These themes were developed inductively from participants’ accounts. Consistent with a phenomenological approach, subsequent interpretive analysis attended to how these experiences were lived — as embodied vulnerability, disrupted temporality, relational strain, and shifting trust in care systems (see Table 4). Experiences varied by geography, parity, and phase of the pandemic, shaping women’s sense of safety, support, and responsibility over time.

Theme 1: Living with pandemic risk and uncertainty

Women described pregnancy during the pandemic as an embodied state of persistent uncertainty, experienced as a continuous backdrop shaping daily decision-making, emotional well-being, and perceptions of safety. The absence of clear, consistent information intensified women’s sense of responsibility for protecting their unborn child, often generating guilt, fear, and moral tension.

Subtheme 1.1: Fear of biological consequences and ambiguity of public health measures

Many women experienced pregnancy as a period marked by anticipatory anxiety, rooted in uncertainty about the virus’s effects on fetal health. This fear was amplified by exclusion of pregnant women from early COVID-19 research and rapidly changing public health guidance. One woman reflected on the moral weight of pregnancy during the pandemic, questioning whether continuing daily routines constituted a risk to her unborn child:“I wondered… what could it mean for people who are pregnant? Was I being irresponsible, or dangerous for my family and this potential baby?” (36 years old, Urban, 2020, Multipara, Interview).

“I wondered… what could it mean for people who are pregnant? Was I being irresponsible, or dangerous for my family and this potential baby?” (36 years old, Urban, 2020, Multipara, Interview).

This sentiment was also evident among younger, first-time mothers, who described fear without a reference point of prior pregnancy experience:“That this was my first kid. I was that fearful. Like, I’ve never experienced anything like that.” (29 years old, Urban, 2021, Primipara, Interview).

“That this was my first kid. I was that fearful. Like, I’ve never experienced anything like that.” (29 years old, Urban, 2021, Primipara, Interview).

Uncertainty was sustained through constant exposure to distressing media coverage, which many participants described as emotionally overwhelming and difficult to escape. For some, this created a sense of living in suspended time, where pregnancy milestones unfolded against a backdrop of fear and unpredictability.“Whatever you turned to—the news, the radio, the TV—it was all about people dying. And in the midst of that, I was about to bring a baby into the world. I was emotionally traumatized… mentally unstable… like I was going crazy” (28 years old, Northern, 2021, Multipara, FGD).

Clinical spaces, typically associated with reassurance, were instead experienced as sites of potential exposure. Even routine prenatal visits became emotionally charged, reinforcing women’s sense of vulnerability.“Most times, I was scared of going to the hospital—even just for my checkups. I was scared of stepping out at all. As a pregnant woman, I saw myself as vulnerable, and I didn’t want anything to disrupt my health or that of my unborn child.” (30 years old, Northern, 2020, Multipara, Interview).

Subtheme 1.2: Trust in and tensions around safety protocols

Although women recognized the necessity of public health restrictions, safety protocols altered the relational and sensory experience of care. Masking, movement restrictions, and visitor limitations reshaped how women experienced birth, often creating emotional distance at moments typically associated with connection and reassurance.“The only strange part was my partner wearing a mask during labor. Not seeing his face or expressions made me anxious.” (35 years old, Urban, 2021, Multipara, Interview).

“The only strange part was my partner wearing a mask during labor. Not seeing his face or expressions made me anxious.” (35 years old, Urban, 2021, Multipara, Interview).

While some women expressed trust in these measures as protective, they also described how such protocols constrained autonomy and comfort during labor and postpartum recovery. Masking intensified physical discomfort during breathing and pushing,“I’d say the regulations are good precautions because they are designed to protect both me and the baby” (29 years old, Urban, 2020, Primipara, Interview).“The mask was an inconvenience as I could say and it’s probably not ideal having a mask on while you’re going through labor.” (33 years old, Southern, 2021, Multipara, Interview).

Subtheme 1.3: Vaccine decision-making and information challenges

Vaccine decision-making was experienced as a complex moral and emotional process. Theme 2: Systematic fragmentation in maternal care

Pandemic-related disruptions fractured the continuity of maternal care, leaving many women navigating fragmented systems during a period of heightened vulnerability. Missed appointments, delayed diagnostics, and provider turnover contributed to feelings of instability and loss of control. The rapid shift to telehealth became part of this changing system. For some, it improved access to care, while for others it introduced new challenges that reshaped how care was experienced.

Subtheme 2.1: Disrupted access to timely and routine care

Women described repeated cancellations and delays that disrupted expectations of predictable prenatal monitoring such as ultrasounds and laboratory testing. This unpredictability undermined reassurance and intensified anxiety about fetal well-being.“I didn’t get the first ultrasound until much later than I had with my firstborn.” (29 years old, Urban, 2020, Multipara, Interview).“Appointments were canceled several times due to outbreaks… I couldn’t easily meet with a doctor in person.” (35 years old, Southern, 2020, Multipara, FGD).

Theme 3: Emotional toll of isolation and disconnection

Social distancing measures reshaped pregnancy and early motherhood as profoundly isolating experiences. Women described grief over missed milestones, loss of shared rituals, and reduced emotional support.

Despite challenges, women demonstrated resilience by adapting routines, strengthening relationships, and drawing on social and relational resources. Subtheme 4.1: Family bonding and reconnection

Increased time at home and reduced external demands strengthened family bonds, despite the loss of in-person social rituals such as baby showers. Discussion

Pregnancy is often understood as a period of anticipation and preparation. During the COVID-19 pandemic, however, pregnancy and early motherhood were marked as prolonged states of uncertainty, vulnerability, and ongoing adaptation. This study explored the lived experiences of women who gave birth in Saskatchewan during the pandemic, demonstrating how biological vulnerability, social disruption, and strain within healthcare system intersected to reshape maternal experience.

At the same time, the findings from this study align with an ecological understanding of health, wherein women’s experiences were perhaps formed by dynamic interactions between personal, interpersonal, healthcare system, and broader structural factors. At the individual level, pregnancy was experienced as embodied vulnerability, with heightened awareness of bodily risk and responsibility for fetal well-being. At the interpersonal level, partners, families, and healthcare providers played critical roles in shaping women’s capacity to cope, particularly when services were disrupted. At the institutional level, fragmented care pathways, reduced continuity, and constrained communication altered women’s trust in and engagement with maternal healthcare. At the structural level, geography, public health policies, digital access, and historical inequities influenced how women navigated pregnancy, birth, and the postpartum period. Viewing these experiences ecologically reveals that maternal distress and resilience during the pandemic were not individual phenomena, but outcomes produced through layered systems of support and constraint.

Consistent with prior public health emergencies such as severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), Zika, and Ebola [62–65], fear and uncertainty were central to women’s experiences [16–27, 66]. However, our findings underscore the moral dimension of pregnancy during a pandemic such as COVID-19: women described feeling personally responsible for preventing harm to their unborn child, often internalizing guilt and fear when guidance was unclear or inconsistent. Vaccine decision-making exemplified this tension. While most participants (73%) ultimately accepted vaccination, the exclusion of pregnant women from early trials generated ethical uncertainty and feelings of being “guinea pigs,” echoing broader critiques of gendered gaps in biomedical research [67–69]. Trust in individual healthcare providers emerged as a critical moderating factor, reinforcing the importance of relationship-based, pregnancy-specific communication during health crises.

The pandemic offered important insights for strengthening maternal health care in Saskatchewan. It highlighted areas where access and continuity of care can be improved, particularly for women in rural and northern communities. Temporary disruptions to appointments, diagnostics, and provider availability underscored the importance of adaptable and resilient systems. While some of these challenges existed before COVID-19 [70], the pandemic brought renewed attention to how travel limits, financial strain, and gaps in digital access (such as limited internet service) affect care experiences. These findings align with the Saskatchewan Health Authority’s ongoing efforts to advance equity through enhanced primary health care. They point to opportunities to strengthen service distribution, improve emergency preparedness, and support equitable access for all communities. In addition to strengthening service distribution, practical supports such as expanded transportation assistance and sustained availability of regional providers remain critical for women facing geographic barriers.

At the point of care, some participants described breakdowns in communication and a lack of personalized care that contributed to maternal distress. Women spoke of rushed consultations that left them feeling unheard and unsupported, consistent with prior studies of maternity care during COVID-19 [27, 71]. When healthcare systems were under strain, interactions sometimes became more task-focused rather than relational, affecting women’s sense of agency, autonomy, and satisfaction with care. At the same time, many participants also highlighted the dedication of person-centred providers who made intentional efforts to prioritize patient well-being despite challenging circumstances. These experiences reflect the variability in care during the pandemic and underscore the critical role of compassionate, relationship-based practice. These findings reinforce the importance of sustaining person-centred communication and individualized support alongside infection control measures during public health emergencies.

Although Indigenous health and equity are recognized as priorities within provincial policy, participants’ accounts revealed ongoing gaps between policy commitments and lived experiences. Indigenous participants described culturally discordant or dismissive interactions and limited responsiveness to cultural needs, suggesting that pandemic pressures further constrained the delivery of culturally respectful care. This underscores the need to embed Indigenous-led, culturally safe models of care as core components of emergency preparedness and maternal health systems.

Social distancing measures transformed pregnancy and early motherhood into isolating experiences marked by loss of shared milestones and diminished emotional support. Restrictions on support persons during appointments and birth disrupted relational experiences, while the absence of communal rituals such as baby showers symbolized broader disconnection. These losses were not merely social inconveniences, but meaningful disruptions to maternal identity and emotional well-being. These findings suggest the value of structured virtual peer mentorship programs and facilitated online prenatal classes to help sustain relational connection when in-person gatherings are restricted. Postpartum mental health and breastfeeding challenges were compounded by limited access to timely, in-person assessment and long waitlists for services. Women’s discomfort with virtual mental health screening highlights the importance of embodied presence and relational cues in identifying distress, consistent with broader perinatal mental health literature [72, 73].

Despite significant challenges, women demonstrated resilience through adaptive strategies that were often relational rather than individual. Partner involvement functioned as a stabilizing source of support, helping redistribute emotional and logistical responsibilities when healthcare systems were under significant strain. Family support, continuity of care with trusted providers, and access to reliable information resources helped women regain a sense of control and stability. Multiparous women often drew on prior experience to navigate uncertainty, while first-time mothers described greater vulnerability, suggesting experiential knowledge as a protective factor. These findings align with qualitative studies indicating that while crises heighten vulnerability, they can also prompt recalibration of priorities and strengthening of close relationships [20, 22].

The rapid expansion of telehealth produced mixed experiences. Virtual care improved access and reduced travel burdens, particularly for women in remote areas. However, many appointments were conducted by telephone rather than video, which may have further limited opportunities to observe body language and other non-verbal cues. Participants noted that this sometimes reduced relational depth and constrained opportunities for physical assessment. These findings highlight the importance of considering modality when evaluating the quality and experience of virtual maternity care [18–21, 44, 67, 71, 74]. Notably, women described the My Saskatchewan Pregnancy prenatal app as a helpful adjunct, offering accessible, pregnancy-specific information that supported self-management when in-person care was limited. This suggests that well-designed digital tools may complement, but not replace, relational and embodied care [21]. Moving forward, hybrid models that prioritize in-person visits for essential assessments while expanding access to video-based consultations may help balance relational depth with geographic accessibility. Strengthening digital tools, including prenatal apps with symptom tracking, and extending virtual postpartum supports such as lactation consultation and mental health follow-up may further improve continuity for women in rural areas.

From a policy perspective, our findings, when considered alongside other local evidence, provide both immediate and long-term insights for improving maternal healthcare in Saskatchewan. The pandemic revealed systemic gaps in maternal healthcare services, particularly for marginalized groups and those living in rural areas. These findings highlight the need to formally recognize pregnant women and children as priority groups during health emergencies. To ensure the continuity of perinatal care, emergency preparedness and response plans must guarantee access to in-person care, screening and diagnostic testing, and clear, consistent risk communication tailored specifically for pregnant women. Prioritizing in-person visits for critical assessments, such as ultrasounds and high-risk consultations, is crucial to avoid delays frequently reported by women in rural settings. Timely updates on evolving hospital policies, such as visitation rules and mask mandates, along with available care options, should be provided through multiple communication channels like SMS and prenatal apps to alleviate confusion and anxiety during public health crises.

Periodic review of Saskatchewan’s emergency response plans is necessary to incorporate lessons learned from recent health crises, including the COVID-19 pandemic and forest fires. Such continuous evaluation can identify practical strategies to strengthen coordination of maternal and child health services during crises. During the pandemic, Maternal and Children’s Provincial Program developed a conceptual model with operational plan and provincial standards of care, clinical resources, tools, and patient handouts to align with restrictions [75]. Insights from this study can support continued evolution of pandemic planning specific to maternal-child healthcare delivery.

Investment in equitable infrastructure, workforce capacity, and culturally responsive care model [50] is also needed to improve maternal health outcomes. Growing media attention has further increased pressure for responsive maternal health policies in Saskatchewan [76, 77]. Improving access also requires addressing digital inequity, with over one-quarter of rural residents lacking reliable internet access [78]. The $21 million federal investment in the Saskatchewan Indian Institute of Technologies (SIIT) Virtual Health Hub and telerobotic ultrasound in northern Indigenous communities are promising steps toward Indigenous-led digital health solutions [79].

This study contributes novel insights by integrating phenomenological analysis with an ecological understanding of maternal health during a public health emergency. It foregrounds how women lived through system fragmentation, uncertainty, and adaptation over time, rather than treating disruptions as isolated service gaps. By centering women’s voices across diverse geographic contexts, it extends existing literature beyond urban-centric analyses and highlights relational continuity, cultural safety, and system flexibility as critical components of maternal care during crises. These findings offer actionable insights for maternal health policy, emergency planning, and hybrid care design.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its qualitative depth, and inclusion of women from diverse geographic regions across Saskatchewan. Also, this study captures not only challenges but also women’s adaptive strategies and perceptions of resilience, offering a more balanced account than deficit-focused narratives. It situates personal experiences within structural and policy contexts, providing insights relevant for health system reform. However, several limitations warrant attention.

Data collection was conducted in English, potentially underrepresenting experiences of linguistic marginalization. Indigenous participants were included; however, the study was not specifically designed, nor ethically structured through Indigenous-led or community-based approaches, to explore Indigenous women’s experiences in depth. The REB approval and analytic plan did not include a distinct Indigenous-focused component, and findings should therefore not be interpreted as representative of, or specific to, Indigenous women. Virtual-only interviews may have excluded women with limited internet access. Although approximately 20% of participants reported very low income, self-selection bias may still have favored women with higher education levels, who were more connected to healthcare services or had the digital access and flexibility required to participate. The provincial focus and qualitative design limit transferability to other contexts. To build on these insights, a quantitative study using provincial administrative health data is currently underway to provide complementary evidence within the same context, with particular attention to key population subgroups. Future research could deepen our understanding of long-term maternal outcomes through larger, mixed-methods studies. In addition, evaluating the effectiveness of telehealth and culturally specific interventions for Indigenous and marginalized populations remains a priority.

Conclusions

Women’s experiences of pregnancy, birth, and early motherhood during the COVID-19 pandemic reveal the central importance of continuity of care and relational continuity in shaping maternal well-being. When provider relationships were sustained and communication was clear, women described greater trust, reassurance, and capacity to adapt. When care became fragmented or impersonal, distress intensified. The pandemic exposed structural vulnerabilities in maternal health systems, particularly for women facing geographic and social barriers, but it also highlighted the protective role of relational support and adaptive strategies. Embedding relational continuity, equitable access, and pregnancy-specific communication into emergency preparedness planning is essential for building maternal health systems that are both resilient and responsive in future crises.
