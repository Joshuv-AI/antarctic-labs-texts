The Mathematical Theory of Double Descent as Shrinkage not Overfitting: Matching Bounds for Path-Regularized Multilayer Neural Networks
Hao Yu

Abstract
We give a nonasymptotic generalization theory for multilayer fully connected neural networks trained with path regularization, for general Lipschitz losses and without assuming the loss bounded, the architecture sparse, or any width or depth limit. Approximation error is modelled explicitly, and along the way we settle an open problem of E et al. on approximation rates in generalized Barron spaces. We treat the underparameterized and overparameterized regimes separately, which is what allows the resulting curve to be non-monotone in the width. We then prove lower bounds on the error itself, valid at every target and at every local minimizer, and combine them with the upper bounds into a theorem asserting that the error – not merely our bound on it – descends, rises and descends again as the width grows, with a peak exceeding the final plateau by a factor diverging in the sample size. Having both sides of the bound also identifies what drives the ascent, and it is not overfitting: once the regularization exceeds the dual norm of the noise, which the prescribed schedule does at every width, a slow-rate oracle inequality controls the error with no variance term at all. The rise is instead the shrinkage demanded by the regularization that the underparameterized regime requires, and we show it cannot be removed by any width-independent regularization under which the theory’s guarantees hold. This mechanism is a property of the estimator analyzed here; we do not claim it accounts for the double descent reported for unregularized training.

Introduction

I-A

Neural Networks and Their Generalization Theory

The advent of neural networks (NNs) has greatly enhanced and revolutionized artificial intelligence. Although research on neural networks can be traced back to the early 1980s with Hinton et al.
rumelhart1986learning
;
hinton1986learning
;
hinton1981implementing
;
rumelhart1986general
, their power only began to emerge in recent decades. NN models have been immersed in many areas of AI, including computer vision, natural language processing, speech, reinforcement learning, etc., and have witnessed thrilling breakthroughs in AI development. Although realistic NNs work very well in practice, they still defy rigorous mathematical understanding.

Recently, there has been a surge of research on the theoretical foundations of NNs. One line of research concerns the training dynamics of neural networks, especially for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithms. For example,
Jin et al. (2017)
shows that noisy SGD can escape from saddle points.
Fang et al. (2019)
explains that SGD can also escape from saddle points with high probability. Many works

;

;

show that SGD can converge to global minima under certain conditions.

This paper focuses on another prominent problem: understanding the generalization property of a trained neural network. This can be studied either under the assumption that the network is trained by some specific optimization algorithm or by simply assuming that a global minimum has been found without knowledge of the optimization algorithm. The relationship between generalization error and empirical error is a classic topic that has been well studied historically. For neural networks, most existing literature focuses on two-layer ReLU neural networks as a starting point. For example,

;

;

Neyshabur et al. ()
.

However, as far as we know, rigorous studies (rather than empirical or heuristic studies) of generalization properties for multilayer neural networks are few, albeit there are many works providing generalization error bounds in various ways. It appears like a puzzle for readers to understand their complex relationships. A superficial reason is that two-layer ReLU neural networks are quite close to linear functions, making them easier to transfer to traditional and well-studied statistical problems, whereas multilayer neural networks are highly nonlinear and nonconvex.

I-B

Path Regularization

Path regularization was proposed in works
Neyshabur et al. (2015a)
;
Neyshabur et al. (2015b)
as a new form of regularization with favorable properties for ReLU neural networks. There are many discussions or variations
luo2021pathbn
;
zheng2019basispath
;
neyshabur2016pathnorm
;
latorre2023pathnorm
. And the recent studies
gonon2024phd
;
barron2019complexity
;
jiang2020neurips
;
dziugaite2021search
emphasize that it is the most promising candidate quantity for measuring the generalization properties of neural networks. We now discuss in details why we study the learning problem Eq. (
16
) with PeSV regularization (a slight variant of path regularization). From a nonparametric or functional standpoint, a neural network should be considered as a nonlinear function rather than a machine with a set of parameters. This view has been adopted by many works. Under this consideration, a Banach function space associated with ReLU neural networks with finite depth and infinite width was developed by
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
. There is a very natural norm on this space with a well-defined mathematical foundation, and the path norm is simply its discrete analog (strictly, the
\ell^{1}
path norm, while the PeSV norm is a slight variant of the
\ell^{1}
path norm). In contrast, viewing neural networks as a set of parameters with weight decay regularization seems too naive to be a correct foundation for the theory of neural networks. Furthermore, the authors show that under this norm, the unit ball in the space for
L
-layer networks has low Rademacher complexity and thus favorable generalization properties, implying that using path norm as a regularizer is a priori expected to train a network that generalizes well. Another related piece of evidence is the observation that the
\ell^{p}
path norm is related to the per-unit
\ell^{p}
norm (also called max
\ell^{p}
norm) in that, for ReLU networks, it is the minimum of the per-unit
\ell^{p}
norms over all linearly rescaled networks representing the same function. Since per-unit
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
norms have shown great generalization properties, this suggests that the
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
path norms are important regularizers. Last but not least,
Neyshabur et al. (2015a)
advocates that the geometric invariance of ReLU networks under scaling should be taken into account and suggests that optimization algorithms should incorporate this invariance. Path norm is scaling-invariant, and that work develops Path-SGD, a scaling-invariant optimization algorithm that numerically achieves much better generalization than SGD and Adam. The work
Ergen and Pilanci (2023)
suggests studying path norm for training neural networks and shows that it has an equivalent convex formulation that favors GD/SGD in finding a global minimum.
Gonon et al. (2023)
develops a first toolkit specialized for path-norm regularization to facilitate challenging concrete promises of path-norm-based generalization bounds. With all these considerations, it is worthwhile and desirable to mathematically investigate the generalization properties of learning algorithms with path norm as a regularizer. Here we take a slight variant of the path norm, the PeSV norm (path enhanced scaled variation norm), which is an
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
mixed version of the path norm. If we use the
\ell^{1}
norm for the first layer, we recover the
\ell^{1}
path norm. The
\ell^{2}
norm here is not essential, and all our conclusions hold for the pure path norm. Thus we take problem Eq. (
16
) in Section
V
as the object of study in this work.

Another point we need to emphasize is that the path regularization can be computed efficiently by using dynamic programming, as opposed to the apparently exponential complexity
Neyshabur et al. (2015a)
. This is very important for practical network training.

Although path regularization has favorable properties only for ReLU networks, we find that our results actually hold for general Lipschitz activations. Therefore, in the following sections, we present the results in this generality unless otherwise specified.

In passing, the advantage of path regularization for general Lipschitz activations remains to be explored and will be the subject of our future research.

I-C

Our Contributions

Based on our review of existing generalization theories for multilayer neural networks available in the literature, in the next section, we propose a framework for what constitutes a satisfactory generalization theory. This paper attempts to fill the gap between theory and practice by providing a fairly general rigorous characterization of the generalization property of a general multilayer neural network with path regularizations for quite general problems, including regression and classification, under mild assumptions. For the squared loss and ReLU activations the overparameterized rate cannot be improved uniformly in the radius of the target ball. Most importantly, the bound demonstrates the double descent phenomenon observed in practice for such networks.

Another work most closely related to ours is perhaps
Parhi and Nowak (2022)
. They set up a correct space of functions of second-order bounded variation in the Radon domain, which turns out to be the same as the variation space
Siegel and Xu (2023)
studied before for associating shallow ReLU neural networks with the same form of learning problem, and finally establish a similar conclusion to ours in this space. One novel difference is that they choose estimators in this function space instead of the original shallow neural networks. Their main tool for proving the
L^{2}
generalization error is a metric entropy argument corresponding to the underparameterized regime in our work; however, this would not be optimal for the aforementioned reason: overparameterization and underparameterization are different.

We believe that the main meaning and contributions of this work are as follows.

1.

We give the first near-complete and optimal nonasymptotic generalization error bound for multilayer neural networks with path regularization for general loss functions
. It is flagged with no constraints placed on network structures (e.g., intrinsic sparsity or boundedness of certain norms), no constraints on any particular optimization algorithm used, and no boundedness assumption on the loss. We explicitly model the target function space and take the approximation error into account. The latter point is notable as it is almost
ignored
in previous works.

2.

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to establish the double descent phenomenon for deep networks with path regularization without relying on asymptotic analysis
. We do so in both directions: the upper bound exhibits the curve, and lower bounds valid at every target show that the error itself, and not only the bound, is non-monotone in the width. It also reveals the intrinsic mechanism of why the double descent phenomenon occurs. The analysis can be extended to other machine learning models. In principle, they will follow the same pattern to exhibit double descent. This paves the way to the final complete understanding of double descent nonasymptotically.

3.

We solve an open question posed in
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
by Weinan E et al. regarding approximation error rates in generalized Barron spaces
.

Our work is one of very few works that goes beyond two-layer neural networks by overcoming several challenges. The characteristics of our work can be listed as follows:

1.

We explicitly distinguish between the overparameterized regime and the underparameterized regime and estimate the generalization error separately, obtaining results that are as good as possible. Previous works either give a single bound that is not optimal or focus only on the overparameterization regime.

2.

When the loss is MSE and the activation is ReLU, we establish an information-theoretic lower bound showing that the overparameterized rate cannot be improved uniformly in the radius of the target ball. We are careful to state what this does and does not give: over a ball of fixed radius and in fixed dimension the bound is not minimax optimal, as the two-layer case shows (Remark
X.2.1
).

3.

We establish an optimal approximation bound for multilayer neural networks with ReLU activations in a suitable continuous function space called the generalized Barron space, greatly strengthening previously known results. To the best of our knowledge, this result is new.

4.

We show that the generalization upper bound can predict the double descent phenomenon, and that lower bounds on the error confirm it. Previous works have never been able to predict the double descent phenomenon from a theoretical point of view alone, still less to certify it from below.

Although our results require path-norm regularization, recent work relates the stochastic gradient methods used to train neural networks to path norms
lyu2020iclr
;
gunasekar2018icml
;
lebeurrier2026arxiv
;
wu2023implicit
. In particular, this work
wu2023implicit
theoretically proves that for two layer ReLU networks, the stationary point found by SGD has a minimal path-norm, which is strong evidence that the same may hold for deep networks. Should that be the case, the present work would partially explain the double descent phenomenon for networks trained by SGD without explicit path regularization. And if one still uses common regularizations like weight decay, the regularization effect enforced by explicit regularization will not change the global double descent landscape but only change the smoothness or dynamic behavior of this landscape. This discussion is not rigorous, but it indicates directions in which a full understanding of the phenomenon might be sought.

Sec. II
Related Work

We review relevant work on the generalization properties of neural networks, including both shallow (two-layer) and deep architectures, while abstracting away specific activation functions and regularization techniques. We subsequently discuss their relations and, especially, differences with our work. We suggest readers consult
Wang and Lin (2023)
and the references therein first for thorough discussions of the background and motivation of this problem, as well as several relations with other related theories, e.g., high-dimensional statistics and recent understanding of bias-variance tradeoff
Derumigny and Schmidt-Hieber (2023)
.

Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
Jacot et al. (2018)
;
Chizat et al. (2019)
is a method to understand the training dynamics of NNs by formulating them as kernel gradient descent with the neural tangent kernel. When the number of neurons approaches infinity, this kernel is constant during training, facilitating easy analysis. This method requires the parameters to stay in a small neighborhood of their initial values, which does not align with realistic NNs. The generalization analysis is therefore not useful.

Mean field theory
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos (2020)
;
Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden (2022)
aims to formulate the training dynamics of NNs as a stochastic partial differential equation in the continuous limit. The generalization property analysis via SPDE poses difficulties that do not seem easy to solve.

There are a number of works analyzing the generalization properties of two-layer neural networks. Most of them leverage the law of large numbers or the central limit theorem to represent the training dynamics of SGD as a stochastic partial differential equation in the continuous limit.
Mei et al. (2018)
proposes a mean field analysis of the training dynamics of two-layer NNs via SGD by recasting it as an SPDE in the continuous limit. However, it is not clear how this method can be generalized to multilayer NNs, and the analysis of the resulting SPDE is far from easy except under specific cases; therefore, generalization property analysis is also challenging.

Since the discovery of the double descent phenomenon
Belkin et al. (2019)
, many works have attempted to understand it theoretically under various assumptions.
Hastie et al. (2022)
;
Muthukumar et al. (2020)
;
Belkin et al. (2020)
started detailed analyses for classical linear models or other simple models, e.g., the random feature model, which is equivalent to a two-layer neural network with the first-layer parameters fixed. Another line of attack is to perform asymptotic analysis for these models
Deng et al. (2022)
;
Liang and Sur (2022)
;
Mei and Montanari (2022)
;
Yang et al. (2020)
, i.e., letting depth, width, dimension, and the number of training data approach infinity separately or jointly. Random matrix theory is largely leveraged in these works. Using this theory, they can find explicit generalization error expressions in the asymptotic regime and show that its curve with respect to the structural parameter demonstrates the “double descent” shape. In this line, a lower generalization error bound for two-layer neural networks is also developed in
Seroussi and Zeitouni (2022)
. Notably, a “multiple descent” phenomenon in infinite-dimensional linear regression and kernel ridgeless regression is proposed in
Liang et al. (2020)
;
Li and Meng (2021)
. Despite these advances, it is still unclear how these simple models can be helpful or insightful for multilayer NN models. In our opinion, the gap between them is potentially large. The reader can also check a very recent thorough discussion of double descent
Schaeffer et al. (2023)
.

Several works have derived generalization properties for two-layer neural networks in certain variation spaces
Bach (2017)
;
Parhi and Nowak (2021)
;
Parhi and Nowak (2022)
. Most of the existing work focuses on variational formulations of the empirical risk minimization problem. However, the network width of the solution is required to be smaller than the sample size, which does not fall within our regime of overparameterized NNs. Another attempt
E et al. (2019)
allows the network width to grow to infinity; however, the
\ell_{1}
path norm regularization induces potential sparsity in parameters, which does not seem to have implications for intrinsically overparameterized NNs.

For deep neural networks, there are some empirical analyses regarding the phenomenological aspects of generalization ability. One of the most surprising features is that any deep neural network with a number of parameters greater than the number of data points has perfect finite-sample expressivity, which forces a rethinking of what generalization means
Zhang et al. (2021)
.
Nagarajan and Kolter (2019)
empirically found that generalization crucially depends on a notion of model capacity that is restricted through the implicit regularization of
\ell_{2}
distance from initialization.
Neyshabur et al. (2017a)
discussed different candidate complexity measures that might explain generalization in neural networks. It also outlines a concrete methodology for investigating such measures and reports on experiments studying how well the measures explain different phenomena.
Zhao and Zhang (2021)
;
Schiff et al. (2021)
propose some new measures, e.g., sparsity, Gi-score, and Pal-score, that can explicitly calculate and compare generalization ability. A deep insight was analyzed in
Neyshabur et al. (2017a)
, where they show quantitative evidence, based on representational geometry, of when and where memorization occurs, and link double descent to increasing object manifold dimensionality.

To the best of our knowledge, an incomplete list of rigorous generalization theories for multilayer neural networks (more than two layers) includes
Neyshabur et al. (2017b)
;
Bartlett et al. (2017)
;
Bartlett et al. (2019)
;
Tirumala ()
;
Gu et al. (2023)
;
Wang and Ma (2022)
;
Allen-Zhu et al. (2019)
;
Jakubovitz et al. (2019)
;
Neyshabur et al. (2018)
. Early classical works
Neyshabur et al. (2017b)
;
Bartlett et al. (2017)
;
Bartlett et al. (2019)
;
Tirumala ()
bound the generalization error using complexity measures such as VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, and Bayesian PCA. It is well known that these methods often require the loss function to be bounded, which is not practical. As classical theory focuses on bounding the generalization error by empirical errors and other complexity quantities related to neural networks, it does not pay attention to the estimation of empirical error, meaning that it does not offer a complete picture of the generalization error. These bounds are also not optimal, as they do not distinguish between the overparameterization and underparameterization regimes that should have fundamental differences, as the salient double descent phenomenon indicates, and thus the upper bound will never predict the double descent phenomenon.
Tirumala ()
does not rely on the aforementioned complexity norms but uses direct analytic analysis to establish concentration inequalities that are, in spirit, similar to our methods, but it still requires boundedness of the loss function and is not optimal for the same reason. For recent work, a kind of generalization error bound analysis appears in
Gu et al. (2023)
, but it is for two- and three-layer neural networks only and studies how generalization error converges under the gradient descent algorithm.
Wang and Ma (2022)
provides a generalization error bound for multilayer neural networks under the stochastic gradient descent algorithm. Again, it focuses on a specific optimization algorithm. Furthermore, these two works do not consider the target function space, which means they do not take into account approximation errors and do not estimate empirical errors. The second work also imposes boundedness constraints on certain norms of multilayer neural networks. Our work differs from theirs in that we do not require knowledge of the optimization algorithm beforehand, do not impose any structural constraints on the multilayer neural networks, and integrate the target function space (and thus approximation error) into our generalization bound expression.

After reviewing the aforementioned works, we find that the generalization theories they present either deal with two-layer neural networks, consider only the asymptotic regime, impose structure constraints on neural networks, depend on particular optimization algorithms, require boundedness of the loss function, or are not aware of potential differences between underparameterization and overparameterization and thus are not predictive of the double descent phenomenon, and they do not consider approximation error a priori.

Through inspecting people’s experience in deep learning in recent decades, we propose here what we think a satisfactory generalization theory should be. We call a set of generalization error expressions or bounds for multilayer neural networks
complete
if:

1.

it measures the difference between the estimated model and the true model in a nonasymptotic fashion;

2.

all terms in the expressions or bounds are computable without running experiments first, meaning that it must involve estimation of empirical error;

3.

it applies to all realistic neural networks without
essential
constraints on their structures, e.g., sparsity, or asymptotic regime;

4.

it sheds light on the famous double descent phenomenon in practice (otherwise it is not tailored for neural networks and not optimal);

5.

it applies to most activation functions;

6.

it applies to most common loss functions in practice; in particular, it does not require boundedness of loss functions, as most common losses are not bounded;

Although the aforementioned empirical findings and partial results are necessary steps toward a better understanding of the generalization properties of NNs, no established, even near-complete, theory has been made public for the simplest deep NN: the multilayer ReLU NN.

Sec. IV
Multilayer Neural Space and Its Properties

As we explicitly take into account the space of true models rather than ignoring it completely as in previous works, to ensure that a machine learning model learned from a predefined model class exhibits favorable empirical and generalization properties, a necessary condition is that this model class possesses the capability to approximate the underlying true model well so that we can perform approximation theory. In this section, we define the true model space specifically associated with multilayer neural networks. We follow the setup of
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
.

Keep in mind that
\sigma
below is any Lipschitz activation with
\sigma(0)=0
, although
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
deals with ReLU. Our definition of the function space relies on the construction of the
generalized Barron space
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{X,K}
, called the generalized Barron space modeled on
X
, where
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact set and
X
is a Banach space such that:

1.

X
embeds continuously into the space
C^{0,1}(K)
of Lipschitz functions on
K
, and

2.

the closed unit ball
B^{X}
in
X
, which is a Polish space, is closed in the topology of
C^{0}(K)
.

\mathcal{B}_{X,K}
is constructed as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{X}}\sigma(g(\cdot))\,\mu(dg)

(4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{X,K}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|\mu\|_{M(B^{X})}:\mu\in M(B^{X})\text{ s.t. }f=f_{\mu}\text{ on }K\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{X,K}

\displaystyle=\{f\in C^{0}(K):\|f\|_{X,K}<\infty\}

Here,
M(B^{X})
denotes the space of (signed) Radon measures on
B^{X}
, and
\mu
is a finite signed measure on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
B^{X}
. The integral
\int_{B^{X}}
is the Bocher integral, and
\|\mu\|_{M(B^{X})}
is the total variation norm of the measure
\mu
. We refer the readers to
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
for more details.

We briefly explain the historic development of this concept. The approximation properties of two-layer neural networks have been a subject of enduring interest, particularly concerning their ability to break the curse of dimensionality when approximating certain high-dimensional functions. This phenomenon is rigorously characterized by the theory of
Barron spaces
. Originally formulated by
Barron (1993)
, the classical Barron space consists of functions that can be represented as an infinite integral over neurons, parameterized by a spectral measure.

A function
f
belongs to the Barron space if it admits a representation of the form

f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}a\sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)\rho(da,d\mathbf{w},db)+c

(5)

where
\sigma
is a bounded activation function (e.g., a sigmoid or ReLU
k
),
\rho
is a probability measure over the neuron parameters
(a,\mathbf{w},b)
, and
c
is a constant. The complexity of the function is measured by the
Barron norm
,
|f|_{\mathcal{B}}
, which is typically defined as the infimum over all such representations of the total variation of the spectral measure associated with the output weights
a
.

Generalized Barron spaces
extend this concept to a broader, more functional analytic framework. They are defined by replacing the specific integral form with a more abstract representation using the
inverse Radon transform
or, equivalently, by considering functions that lie in the dual space of a particular function space induced by the activation function.

A particularly powerful modern formulation defines the generalized Barron norm for a function
f
with respect to an activation function
\sigma
as

|f|_{\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}}=\inf_{h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|h(a,\mathbf{w},b)|dad\mathbf{w}db

(6)

where
f
has a representation

f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}h(a,\mathbf{w},b)\sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b)dad\mathbf{w}db.

(7)

This perspective offers several key advantages for machine learning theory:

1.

Convexity:
The space
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
is a Banach space, turning the non-convex training of width-limited networks into a convex optimization problem over measures in the infinite-width limit.

2.

Representation Cost:
The Barron norm precisely quantifies the implicit bias of gradient-based methods (like gradient descent) when training over-parameterized two-layer networks, often correlating with the weight decay regularizer.

3.

Approximation Theory:
Functions with a finite Barron norm can be approximated by a finite-width neural network with a rate independent of the input dimension
d
, providing a mathematical justification for why neural networks excel in high dimensions
(
Bach, 2017
)
.

4.

Generalization Bounds:
The norm serves as an effective capacity measure, leading to generalization bounds that do not explicitly depend on the number of parameters, aligning with the observed behavior of large neural networks
(
Neyshabur et al., 2018
)
.

In summary, generalized Barron spaces provide a rigorous mathematical setting for understanding the approximation, optimization, and generalization properties of shallow neural networks, bridging the gap between finite networks and their infinite-width limits.

Theorem IV.1

(Theorem 2.7 in
Wojtowytsch et al. (2020)
)
.

The following are true:

1.

\mathcal{B}_{X,K}
is a Banach space.

2.

\mathcal{B}_{X,K}\hookrightarrow C^{0,1}(K)
and the closed unit ball of
\mathcal{B}_{X,K}
is a closed subset of
C^{0}(K)
.

3.

If
\sigma
is positive homogeneity, then
X\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{X,K}
and
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}_{X,K}}\leq\frac{2}{L_{\sigma}}\|f\|_{X}
.

Here positive homogeneity means
\sigma(\lambda x)=\lambda x
for
\lambda>0
.

The function space that we consider is defined recursively as follows:

1.

W^{1}(K)=(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\oplus\mathbb{R}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is the space of affine functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
(restricted to

We take the standard Euclidean

-norm on

-norm of

2.

For

We call this space the

The above definition is somewhat abstract. We will define three kinds of

We start from the definition of a finite-width multilayer neural network and then generalize to infinite width, i.e., the continuous case:

Definition IV.2
.

where

and

.

Note that what we term an

For the

If

,

The path enhanced scaled variation norm can also be written in terms of weight matrices. Let the vector
W
be the sequential product of the weight matrices except for the first layer, i.e.,
W:=a^{L}\prod_{i=2}^{L-1}w^{i}
. Let

be its

\nu(\theta):=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}|W_{k}|\,\|w^{1}_{k}\|_{2}.

(13)

Note that when

,

becomes the scaled variation norm denoted in
Wang and Lin (2023)
. Thus Eq. (

) is indeed a multilayer generalization of the scaled variation norm. This regularization (or scaled variation norm) is not as strange as it appears. The origin of the scaled variation norm was discussed in
Parhi and Nowak (2021)
;
Parhi and Nowak (2022)
, where it corresponds to the second-order total variation of a function in a certain function space in the offset variable of the (filtered) Radon domain. The authors established a representer theorem for two-layer ReLU networks as the solution of a variational problem with this total variation as the regularization. When the function is represented by a two-layer ReLU network, it reduces to the scaled variation norm. It is argued that when trained on two-layer ReLU neural networks, it promotes a sparse superposition representation of ReLU ridge functions as the solution. It also shows that the scaled variation norm is equivalent to weight decay regularization for two layer networks in the sense that the minimizers of problems with these two regularizations are the same.

As aforementioned, any multilayer network with a Lipschitz activation
\sigma
with
\sigma(0)\neq 0
can be transformed into a network with a Lipschitz activation with

. This can be easily seen from the following expression:

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right)=\mathbf{I}\left(\sigma^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right)\times 1+\sigma(0)\times 1\right)

where

so that
\sigma^{*}(0)=0
and

is the identity activation. We can immediately derive from this expression that our assertion is correct. Thus our work also works for general Lipschitz activations.

All the results in the following sections are presented in the language of multilayer neural networks without biases and activations

with
\sigma(0)=0
. As we have discussed above, multilayer neural networks with biases or activations
\sigma
with

can be considered as the former with some weights fixed a priori. It is easy to adapt our results to this situation.

Sec. V
Problem Setup

The framework of the learning problem we discuss in this work is described as follows: suppose we have observed predictors
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and responses
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
generated from the nonparametric regression model

y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n

(15)

where
f^{*}
is an unknown function to be estimated and
\epsilon_{i}
are random errors.

In order to learn
f^{*}
from the training sample, we adopt the penalized empirical risk minimization (ERM) framework and seek to minimize

\displaystyle J_{n}(\theta;\lambda)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-g(x_{i};\theta))^{2}+\lambda\nu(\theta)

(16)

where
g(\cdot;\theta)
is the
L
-layer neural network
IV.2
,
\nu(\theta)
is the PeSV norm in Eq. (
13
), and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter.

We denote the solution of this optimization problem in the space
X_{m_{L-1},\dots,m_{1};\mathbf{B}^{d}}
by

\hat{\theta}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}J_{n}(\theta;\lambda).

(17)

We impose the following assumptions on our problem Eq. (
15
), following
Wang and Lin (2023)
.

Assumption V.0.1
.

f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}\equiv\{f\in W^{L}:\|f\|_{W^{L}}\leq M\}
for a prespecified
L
and some constant
M>0
.

Assumption V.0.2
.

x_{i}\sim\mu
independently, where
\mu
is supported on
\mathbf{B}^{d}
.

Assumption V.0.3
.

\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
independently and are independent of
x_{i}
.

These assumptions are quite standard and commonly adopted in most literature. e.g., Assumption
V.0.2
holds because the predictors are normally bounded and can be normalized. See
Wang and Lin (2023)
for further discussion of these assumptions. Since we follow the aforementioned assumptions in the remainder of the paper where
\mu
is supported on a unit ball, for convenience we abbreviate
W^{l}(\mathbf{B}^{d})
to
W^{l}
, unless otherwise specified.

In Section
X
, we extend our theory to general Lipschitz loss functions (under some very mild conditions). Thus the problem we study also includes other common machine learning problems, not restricted to regression problems.

Since the PeSV norm is a slight modification of the path norm, and given the superior performance of the scale-invariant optimization algorithm Path-SGD for ReLU networks proposed in
Neyshabur et al. (2015a)
, it is highly plausible that the variant of Path-SGD optimization adapted to the PeSV norm obtains better results than SGD and Adam for ReLU networks, justifying the value of this regularization. On the other hand, the scaled variation norm is derived from the second-order total variation of a target function in the offset variable of the (filtered) Radon domain as mentioned before; the parallel result for the PeSV norm is an interesting open question.

Sec. VI

We establish the approximation property of the multilayer neural space

, then the maximum decreasing power sequence of

is
\mathbf{m}^{\uparrow}=\{128,64,32,16,8,4,2\}

Theorem VI.2
.

With the same assumptions as in Theorem

, except that the activation can be any Lipschitz function

, and given a depth

Sec. VIII
Overparameterized Regime

Starting from this section, we will state our key results in compliance with what the title of this paper claims. We distinguish between the case where the number of parameters is large enough or small enough in the sense precisely specified in the results below, i.e., the overparameterized regime and the underparameterized regime, as they are approached by rather different ways. This distinction is the key reason why our bounds can exhibit the double descent phenomenon and why previous results in the literature cannot. This argument indicates that when the number of parameters grows, some underlying mechanisms controlling the network’s variance undergo essential changes. The detailed analysis is left to Section
X
. Note also that the ”overparameterized regime” and ”underparameterized regime” are not mathematically rigorous distinctions, but rather conceptual ones. A priori, and without calculating their explicit ranges, there is no guarantee—nor any requirement—that they be sharply separated. The Propositions are clearly presented, and all proofs are deferred to the Supplementary Information
XVII
.

Interestingly, this upper bound Eq. (

We can discuss this in details. It is clear from the above deduction that we decompose the empirical and generalization error into its bias and variance terms, which is analogous to the classical bias-variance tradeoff formula. The first term in Eq. (
51

\mathbf{m_{0}}=\{m_{0},m_{1},\cdots,m_{L-1}\}
of the form
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}
for a width vector
\mathbf{x}
(as we only care such structure in a given multilayer neural network) and let
\mathbf{m}=k\mathbf{m_{0}}
with
k
running from 0 to infinity. When
k
is very small,
\mathbf{m}
will be in the underparameterized regime. In this regime, when
k
increases, the test performance will get better and better and then get worse and worse, with a valley around
k_{0}
such that
H^{2}(k_{0}\mathbf{m_{0}})=k_{0}^{\beta}\frac{(2dm_{0,1}+4m_{0,1}m_{0,2}+4m_{0,2}m_{0,3}+\cdots+2m_{0,L-1})\log n}{n}
with
\beta

33

k
enters into the overparameterized regime, the variance will become saturated and the bias still decreases, resulting in a rotated
\mathbf{S}
-shape performance curve. More precisely, when the width
m
is very small, the decrease of the first term is greater than
\frac{\sqrt{5}L_{T}}{m(m+1)}\|f\|_{W^{L}}^{2}
, and as we are in the underparameterized regime, the increase of the second term is generally no greater than
\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}\sqrt{f(n)/n}
(omitting the smallness of
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
). When

\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}\sqrt{f(n)/n}\leq\frac{\sqrt{5}L_{T}}{m(m+1)}

m
increases continuously, after the point
k_{0}
, the above inequality will be reversed and the tendency starts to flip, and then the generalization error curve begins to increase. When
m
is large enough that it escapes the underparameterized regime (note that the underparameterized and overparameterized regimes have some overlap), i.e. after the point
k_{1}=\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}(\frac{f(n)n}{\log^{2}n})^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}\frac{1}{2dm_{0,1}+4m_{0,1}m_{0,2}+4m_{0,2}m_{0,3}+\cdots+2m_{0,L-1}}
or roughly
4m^{2}dL\geq\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}(\frac{f(n)n}{\log^{2}n})^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}
. One can verify that
k_{1}>k_{0}
so
k_{1}
does occurs after
k_{0}
. In the overparameterized regime, the second term becomes constant and the bias continues to diminish, thus the second decreasing period begins. The limit it decreases to is
Bottom_{o}:=C(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}^{2})\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}\sqrt{\frac{f(n)}{n}}
.

It remains to verify two things: that

, which is what makes the curve a double descent rather than a single one, and that

lies below the bottom of the
\mathbf{U}

We know that
k_{0}\sim(\frac{n}{\log n})^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}
and
k_{1}\sim(\frac{f(n)n}{\log^{2}n})^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{f(n)}{n}}\leq\frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}

(53)

we arrive at

B=C(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\left(\frac{(L-1)^{2}(2+R)^{2}L^{2}_{\sigma}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}

is larger than a certain threshold, then the second valley will be lower than the first, resulting in a better generalization error when the size of the network becomes sufficiently large This is understandable, as the decrease in bias will dominate the increase in variance.

Remark X.1.1

(On the exponent

It is worth isolating why

does not degrade as the network deepens. At

and

recovering the two-layer rate

of

Concretely, if
\mathbf{m}=\{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{L-1}\}
, people usually take a series of networks indexed by
k
with width vector
k\mathbf{m}:=\{km_{1},km_{2},\dots,km_{L-1}\}
and let

. Since

as in

does not decrease when

increases and approaches infinity as

approaches infinity, the analysis above shows that a double descent of this kind occurs along such a sequence.

Qualitatively, when
\mathbf{m}
is small, one can bound the variance by something proportional to the number of weights (and the norm, of course). This bound is better than the bound depending on the number of training data only, as the neural network is not a good approximator to its image in the multilayer neural space (reflected by the metric entropy). But when
\mathbf{m}
becomes large, the bounds based on metric entropy are no longer valid. The intrinsic property of the multilayer neural network as an element in the multilayer neural space begins to control the behavior of the neural networks. This is because when
\mathbf{m}
is large, the norm
\mu(\theta)
and
\mu(f(\cdot;\theta))
are very close, and thus the effect of the number of weights on the variance is eliminated. In this case, by Eq. (
133
), the variance caused by considering
\hat{\theta}
no longer plays a role.

This behavior indicates that neural networks have demonstrated phase changing, akin to the same phenomenon in condense matter field. There are many other phase changing effects in other fields, for example, in Ising model, percolation model or more general discrete probability models for two dimensional statistical physics. As we mentioned in the aforementioned sections, the bounds in the overparameterized regime is not optimal for the underparameterized regime, so that this estimate does not reflect the behavior of neural networks in the underparameterized regime. That is why we need the bounds
IX.2
. To confirm that the term ”phase transition” we use is appropriate, we can use gas as a metaphor of the underparameterize regime and liquid or solid as a metaphor of the overparameterized regime (the two regimes overlap, and we do not assume the transition occurs at a single width). When matters are in gas state, the particles have less interactions with each other,so there is no much constraints between them, meaning that they have much freedom degrees. But when they are in liquid or solid state, particles interact much strong so their behaviors tend to form a global state. This metaphor explains the very methods we take for different regimes. One can see this by just carefully checking the principles of these methods.

Even though this is only an upper bound, it is still nontrivial that it exhibits the double descent phenomenon. Through the proofs, we boost our understanding of the behavior of neural networks: the number of weights is not a correct measure of the complexity of neural networks. When the network is overparameterized, its behavior (at least its generalization property) is akin to its corresponding function in the multilayer neural space and is controlled by it. Therefore, we should view this neural network as a nonparametric function (in a certain function space) rather than a parameterized model. This also coincides with the philosophy and benefits of path-norm regularization: we should take neural networks as functions mapping inputs to outputs and control their holistic complexity rather than their number of parameters. If there is still room to improve upon the generalization upper bound in the overparameterized regime, we may further obtain a more refined understanding of the behavior of neural networks, but the near-optimality stated below makes such improvement rather challenging. This near-optimality also suggests that our explanation is the intrinsic mechanism of why the double descent phenomenon occurs for such networks. However, there may still be room for further refinement of parameters in a certain bounded region, similar to the underparameterized regime. For example, it may admit an upper bound
C(\mathbf{m},L)(\frac{\log n}{n})^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<0.5
in a certain bounded region at the beginning of the overparameterized regime. This would give a more precise characterization of generalization error curves.

In fact, we have endeavored to prove the matching lower bounds recently and based on our progress we are quite confident that this is true. Then this means that our bounds are optimal up to a constant. Such results can form a mathematical rigorous grounds that our theory reveals the true underlying mechanism of double descent. Together with the work
wu2023implicit
, it might hope that our results form a first mathematical rigorous theory of double descent phenomenon for two layer neural networks.

This shows that in different regimes a neural network should be treated as a different kind of object. The deduction of the empirical error bounds does not reflect the underlying behavior for neural networks in the underparameterized regime, as it does not generalize in that regime. It only generalizes in the overparameterized regime. The similar analysis applies for empirical error bounds
IX.1
. As it is near optimal in the overparameterized regime, it is highly possible that our view of neural networks as different objects (in different regimes) manifests their true behaviors, and thereby identifying the mechanism behind the double descent behavior. We state this as a plausibility claim rather than a proof: a competing theory with comparable quantitative content would be equally admissible a priori, and we do not argue otherwise. Optimizing the generalization error bound in the underparameterized regime seems possible, and this will be left as a future research direction.

We also believe that the above idea and strategy to show double descent occurs can be extended to many other machine learning models. In principle, we think they share the same underlying mechanism for the occurrence of the double descent phenomenon.

Our theory can also explain scaling law. This can be easily reflected from the expression of the bound Eq. (
51
). For example, when all the widths form a decreasing power sequence as
\{m^{L-1},\cdots,m^{2},m\}
, then the total number of parameters

\sim m^{2L-3}

\sim 1/m

with respect to the total number of parameters. This explains the scaling law.

The reader may object at this point that all of the above concerns an upper bound, and that an upper bound with a local maximum does not entail that the error itself has one. This is a fair objection, and Section

The reader who is chiefly interested in the double descent phenomenon may wish to read that section immediately after the present one.

Sec. XI
Lower Bounds and the Double Descent of the Risk

Up to this point every statement about the shape of the generalization error as a function of the width vector has been a statement about an
upper bound
. Theorem
X.1
exhibits a curve that descends, rises and descends again, but an upper bound with a local maximum does not by itself entail that the risk has one: the risk is dominated by the bound, and could in principle be monotone underneath it. To settle the matter one needs lower bounds on the error itself, valid at the same target and for the same estimator. That is what we establish in this section.

Two features of the problem make this harder than it may appear, and it is worth stating them at the outset because they dictate the form the results must take. First, no lower bound can hold for
every

f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}
without qualification: if
f^{*}\equiv 0
and
\lambda
exceeds the noise level, then
\hat{\theta}=0
and the error vanishes identically, so the curve is constant. Any valid statement must therefore either carry an explicit nondegeneracy condition or, as below, be stated as a minimum of two quantities one of which is
\|f^{*}\|
itself. Second, no argument of the Fano type can produce the peak. A packing placed inside
W^{L}_{M}
bounds from below the minimax risk over
W^{L}_{M}
, and the minimax risk over
W^{L}_{M}
is already attained, up to the overparameterized rate, by the estimator at large width; hence such a bound can never exceed that rate, let alone exhibit a maximum above it. The route we take instead is through the optimality condition of problem Eq. (
16
), which turns out to give everything we need and to require almost nothing.

Throughout this section we write
\langle u,v\rangle_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(x_{i})v(x_{i})
, and we recall from the proof of Theorem
VII.1
the quantity

\xi\;=\;\frac{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\epsilon\|_{2},

(55)

which is the empirical process term that the choice
\lambda\geq\lambda_{1}
is designed to dominate. We also abbreviate
\Lambda_{L,d}:=\max\{16L_{\sigma}^{L-1},\,2^{L+1}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}
, so that
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{L,d}\sqrt{f(n)/n}
and the limiting value of the bound of Theorem
X.1
as the width grows — which we call the
plateau
— is
C\,\Lambda_{L,d}(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}^{2})\sqrt{f(n)/n}
. Finally we set

A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})\;:=\;\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{m},\infty)}\|f-f^{*}\|_{2},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{Q}\;:=\;\{f\in W^{L}:\|f\|_{W^{L}}\leq Q\},\qquad\|h\|_{*}\;:=\;\sup_{\|f\|_{W^{L}}\leq 1}\langle f,h\rangle_{2}.

(56)

The floors below are stated in terms of
\|f^{*}\|_{n}
and
\|f^{*}\|_{2}
, and it is worth settling
at once how these relate to the quantity that Assumption
V.0.1
actually controls,
namely
\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
. One direction is immediate; the other fails, and the way in which it
fails dictates the form of every statement in this section. We write

\rho(f^{*})\;:=\;\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{2}}{\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}}\;\in\;\left(0,\ L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\right].

(57)

Lemma XI.0.1
.

Let
B:=\|f^{*}\|_{\infty}
, so that
B\leq L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|f^{*}\|_{n}\ \geq\ \frac{\|f^{*}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\;\geq\;1-\exp\left(-\frac{3n\|f^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{28B^{2}}\right)\;\geq\;1-\exp\left(-\frac{3n\,\rho(f^{*})^{2}}{28\,L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)}}\right),

(58)

and
\|f^{*}\|_{n}\leq B
always. Consequently every bound below in which
\|f^{*}\|_{n}
appears may
be read, on an event of that probability, with
\rho(f^{*})\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}/\sqrt{2}
in its place.

Remark XI.0.1
.

There is no converse: no constant
c>0
satisfies
\|f\|_{2}\geq c\|f\|_{W^{L}}
for all

f\in W^{L}
, so
\rho
cannot be bounded below in terms of the data of
Assumption
V.0.1
alone. The embedding
W^{L}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mu)
is compact, and
its inverse is correspondingly unbounded. An explicit witness for
L=2
,
d=1
is the
triangular bump

\displaystyle b_{h}(x)\;=\;\sigma(x)-2\sigma\!\left(x-\tfrac{h}{2}\right)+\sigma(x-h),

(59)

supported on
[0,h]
with height
h/2
: its PeSV norm is
\approx 4
uniformly in
h
, while

\|b_{h}\|_{2}\approx h^{3/2}/\sqrt{6}\to 0
, so
\rho(b_{h})\to 0
as
h\to 0
.

Two consequences should be stated plainly. First, the lower bounds of this section hold for
each target with constants depending on
\rho(f^{*})
, and are
not
uniform over the
ball
W^{L}_{M}
; this is unavoidable rather than an artefact, and it is the exact counterpart of
the fact that the upper bounds use Assumption
V.0.1
only through the one-sided
inequality
\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}\leq M
. Second,
\rho
enters Lemma
XI.0.1
inside an
exponent, so a target that is spread very thinly relative to its PeSV norm requires
correspondingly more data before the empirical and population norms may be identified.

Remark XI.0.2
.

The parameter
\rho
and the coherence
\mu
of Theorem
XI.2
measure the same
degeneracy from two sides. Since
\|h\|_{*}\leq L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\|h\|_{2}
for every
h
, we have

\displaystyle 1\;\leq\;\mu(f^{*})\;\leq\;\frac{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}{\rho(f^{*})},

(60)

so the condition
\mu(f^{*})Q_{\lambda}<\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
under which the floor of
Theorem
XI.2
is positive is implied by

Q_{\lambda}<\rho(f^{*})\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}/L_{\sigma}^{L-1}
, and both forms degrade together as the
target is spread more thinly.

XI-A

Three Floors

The first and most useful of the three rests on a single observation: although
\nu
is neither convex nor everywhere differentiable as a function of
\theta
, both
g(x;\theta)
and
\nu(\theta)
are positively homogeneous of degree one in the last weight matrix
a^{L}
, and of degree
L
in
\theta
as a whole. Restricting the objective to the corresponding ray through
\hat{\theta}
therefore reduces the first-order condition to the derivative of a univariate polynomial, and no subgradient of
\nu
is ever needed.

Theorem XI.1
.

Let
\hat{\theta}
be any local minimizer of
J_{n}(\cdot\,;\lambda)
. Then under Assumption
V.0.2
and the assumption that
\max_{i}\|x_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
, for
every

f^{*}
and every
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}\;\geq\;\min\left\{\;\|f^{*}\|_{n}\;,\;\frac{(\lambda-\xi)_{+}}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}\;\right\}.

(61)

No assumption is made on
f^{*}
beyond Assumption
V.0.1
, none on the location of
\hat{\theta}
, and none on the differentiability of
\nu
.

Remark XI.1.1
.

The threshold
\lambda>\xi
in Eq. (
61
) is exactly the threshold at which the term
2(\xi-\lambda)\nu(\hat{\theta}\ominus\theta^{*})
in the proof of Theorem
VII.1
turns nonpositive. The same inequality thus governs both halves of the theory, and it is instructive that it does so in opposite directions: the upper bound requires
\lambda\geq\xi
in order to close, and it is precisely for
\lambda>\xi
that the estimator is prevented from tracking the noise and the floor Eq. (
61
) becomes positive. We return to this point in Remark
XI.5.1
.

The second floor requires no information about the design points at all, and is therefore stated directly in the
L^{2}(\mu)
norm.

Theorem XI.2
.

For every
f^{*}
, every
\mathbf{m}
, every
\lambda>0
and every realization of the data,

\displaystyle\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\leq\;Q_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{2n\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2},\qquad\text{and consequently}\qquad\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{2}\;\geq\;\operatorname{dist}_{2}\!\left(f^{*},\mathcal{B}_{Q_{\lambda}}\right)\;=\;\sup_{\|h\|_{2}\leq 1}\Big\{\langle f^{*},h\rangle_{2}-Q_{\lambda}\|h\|_{*}\Big\}.

(62)

Writing
\kappa:=\|f^{*}\|_{2}
,
M_{*}:=\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
and
\mu(f^{*}):=\|f^{*}\|_{*}M_{*}/\kappa^{2}\;(\geq 1)
, the choice
h=f^{*}/\kappa
gives the explicit form

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{2}\;\geq\;\kappa\left(1-\mu(f^{*})\frac{Q_{\lambda}}{M_{*}}\right)_{+},\qquad\mathbb{E}\,Q_{\lambda}=\frac{\kappa^{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2\lambda}.

(63)

The next result concerns the estimator itself rather than its error, and will be used to
sharpen Theorem
XI.2
. It is obtained from the same identity Eq. (
261
)
that underlies Theorem
XI.1
, read in the two opposite directions.

Proposition XI.2.1
.

Let
\hat{\theta}
be any local minimizer of
J_{n}(\cdot\,;\lambda)
and suppose

\max_{i}\|x_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\textup{(i)}\qquad\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\geq\;\frac{\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})\right\|_{n}}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}\;\geq\;\frac{\left(\|f^{*}\|_{n}-\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}\right)_{+}}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}},

(64)

and, if in addition
\lambda>\xi
,

\displaystyle\textup{(ii)}\qquad\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\leq\;\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}}{4(\lambda-\xi)}.

(65)

Remark XI.2.1
.

Part (i) says that the estimator cannot collapse: combining it with the empirical error bound
of Theorem
VII.1
or Theorem
IX.1
, whichever applies, gives the
explicit floor

\displaystyle\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}\left(\|f^{*}\|_{n}-\sqrt{\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{m})}\right)_{+},

(66)

where
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{m})
denotes the right-hand side of the applicable bound. Thus as
soon as the error bound falls below
\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}
– which, by Theorem
X.1
, happens
for every width once
n
is large – the path norm of the fitted network is bounded away from
zero by a quantity of the order of
\|f^{*}\|_{n}
. As it must, the bound degenerates when

\|f^{*}\|_{n}
is small: for
f^{*}\equiv 0
and
\lambda\geq\xi
one has
\hat{\theta}=0
exactly, so
no bound independent of
f^{*}
is available.

Remark XI.2.2
.

Part (ii) is sharper than the budget
Q_{\lambda}
of Theorem
XI.2
: for

\lambda\gg\xi
their ratio is approximately
\|f^{*}\|_{2}^{2}/\left(2(\|f^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
and it is much smaller still when the noise dominates the signal. Theorem
XI.2

therefore holds with
Q_{\lambda}
replaced by

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{\lambda}:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2n\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2},\ \frac{\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}}{4(\lambda-\xi)}\right\},

(67)

which lowers by a factor of at least
2(1+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}/\|f^{*}\|_{2}^{2})
the value of

\lambda
at which the floor becomes positive, and so widens the range of widths over which
the ascending branch of Corollary
XI.2.1
is visible in the
L^{2}(\mu)
norm.

Part (i) of Proposition
XI.2.1
is tied to the error bound, and therefore to
the width through the approximation term
H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}
, which does not
vanish with
n
. The following gives a floor for
\nu(\hat{\theta})
that is free of both. Write

\|h\|_{*,n}\;:=\;\sup_{\nu(\theta)\leq 1}\ \langle h,\ g(\cdot;\theta)\rangle_{n}

(68)

for the empirical dual norm, so that
\|\epsilon\|_{*,n}\leq\xi
by Lemma
XVII.0.3
and

\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\leq\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}\,\|f^{*}\|_{*,n}
by duality.

Proposition XI.2.2
.

Let
\hat{\theta}
be a global minimizer of
J_{n}(\cdot\,;\lambda)
and suppose

\max_{i}\|x_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\geq\;\frac{\left(\|y\|_{*,n}-\lambda\right)_{+}^{2}}{2L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)}\,\|y\|_{*,n}}.

(69)

If moreover
\lambda+\xi\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|f^{*}\|_{*,n}
, then

\displaystyle\nu(\hat{\theta})\;\geq\;\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{*,n}}{12L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)}}\;\geq\;\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}}{12L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}},

(70)

and hence, on the event of Lemma
XI.0.1
,

\nu(\hat{\theta})\geq\rho(f^{*})^{2}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}\big/\big(24L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)}\big)
.

Remark XI.2.3
.

The hypothesis
\lambda+\xi\leq\frac{1}{2}\|f^{*}\|_{*,n}
is of the right shape and cannot be
dispensed with: whenever
\lambda\geq\|y\|_{*,n}
one has
\hat{\theta}=0
exactly, since then

\langle y,g(\cdot;\theta)\rangle_{n}\leq\lambda\nu(\theta)
for every
\theta
and the objective
is minimized at the origin. The threshold therefore separates the regime in which the signal
is detectable above the penalty from the one in which the estimator is annihilated, and the
case
f^{*}\equiv 0
of Remark
XI.2.1
is its degenerate instance. Combining with

\|f^{*}\|_{*,n}\geq\|f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}/\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
, a sufficient form of the hypothesis is

\lambda+\xi\leq\rho(f^{*})^{2}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}/4
on the event of Lemma
XI.0.1
.

Remark XI.2.4
.

At the widths
\mathbf{m}_{\sharp}
of Theorem
XI.3
, where
\lambda_{2}\to C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}
,
the hypothesis of Proposition
XI.2.2
becomes a signal-to-noise condition,

C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\leq\rho(f^{*})^{2}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}/4
. It may fail, in which case

\hat{\theta}=0
at those widths. This does not affect the conclusions of
Section
XI
: when it happens the minimum in Theorem
XI.1
is attained
by its first branch
\|f^{*}\|_{n}
, which is likewise a quantity not depending on
n
, so the
peak of Theorem
XI.3
stands either way. The two statements describe the
same shrinkage from opposite sides – one bounds how far the estimator is pushed from the
target, the other how much of it survives.

Remark XI.2.5

(The empirical norm cannot be traded for
L^{2}(\mu)
at the peak)
.

Theorem
XI.1
is stated in
\|\cdot\|_{n}
because the optimality condition lives
there. One might hope to convert it: Lemma
IX.1.1
gives

\|h\|_{n}^{2}\leq\|h\|_{2}^{2}+Z_{n}(\gamma)
for
\|h\|_{2}\leq\gamma
, so a floor
\mathrm{Fl}
on

\|\cdot\|_{n}
yields one on
\|\cdot\|_{2}
provided

\mathrm{Fl}\geq\tfrac{4}{3}F\sqrt{(dm_{1}+m_{1}m_{2}+\cdots+m_{L-1})\log n/n}\asymp F\sqrt{\delta_{n}/L_{\sigma}}
,
where
F
bounds the PeSV norm of the difference. This cannot be arranged at
\delta_{n}\approx 1
:
there the number of weights is of the order of
n
, the localization radius is of order one, and
the two norms genuinely decouple, a class able to interpolate carrying no information from the
sample to the population. Any transfer argument must therefore stop short of the interpolation
threshold – which is exactly where the ascent of Corollary
XI.2.1
culminates. This
is the reason Theorem
XI.2
, which never touches the design points, is
indispensable rather than merely convenient.

The third is immediate but worth recording, since it is the only one of the three that is positive at every width precisely when the target is
not
realizable by any finite network — that is, when
f^{*}
lies in
W^{L}_{M}
but on no finite-width slice of it.

Proposition XI.2.3
.

For every
f^{*}
, every
\mathbf{m}
, every
\lambda
and every realization,
\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\|_{2}\geq A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})
. In particular
A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})>0
for all
\mathbf{m}
if and only if
f^{*}\notin\bigcup_{\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{m},\infty)
, and
A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})\to 0
as the width grows, at the rate given by Theorem
VI.2
.

XI-B

The Regularization Schedule

The floors of the previous subsection grow with
\lambda
, so before reading off a shape we
must fix which
\lambda
is used at which width. Recall the two prescribed values:

\lambda_{1}=\max\{8L_{\sigma}^{L-1},2^{L}cL_{\sigma}^{L-1}\sqrt{d}\}\sqrt{f(n)/n}
, required by
Theorem
VIII.1
in the overparameterized regime, and

\lambda_{2}=C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\max\{\delta_{n},H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\}
, required by
Theorem
IX.2
in the underparameterized regime
\delta_{n}<1
. Each is the
smallest value admissible in its own regime.

Remark XI.2.6

(Why
\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}
is not the right prescription)
.

Both theorems are stated as “
\lambda\geq\lambda_{i}
”, which invites the reading that any
larger
\lambda
is equally good. It is not. Returning to Eq. (
133
), the bound
actually proved is

\displaystyle\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\|_{n}^{2}\;\leq\;C_{1}H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}^{2}+4\lambda\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}+2(\xi-\lambda)\nu(\hat{\theta}\ominus\theta^{*}),

(71)

whose second term is
4\lambda\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
, not
4\lambda_{1}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}
; the right-hand
side displayed in Theorem
VIII.1
is the value of Eq. (
71
) at

\lambda\asymp\lambda_{1}
. Enlarging
\lambda
beyond
\lambda_{1}
therefore does not preserve
that conclusion – it degrades it proportionally – and Proposition
XI.2.2
and
Theorem
XI.1
show the degradation is real rather than an artefact of the
proof. Taking
\lambda=\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}
at every width would consequently forfeit
the overparameterized rate wherever
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}
, which is most of the
underparameterized regime.

The correct prescription is the regime-wise one: use each regime’s own value inside that
regime,

\lambda(\mathbf{m})\;=\;\begin{cases}\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{m}),&\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m})<1\quad\text{(underparameterized)},\\[2.0pt]
\lambda_{1},&\text{otherwise}\quad\text{(overparameterized)}.\end{cases}

(72)

This is also what makes the
\min
in Theorem
X.1
the correct reading of the upper
bound, in the sense of
Wang and Lin (2023)
: on the underparameterized range both
theorems apply, since
\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{1}
there, and one keeps whichever bound is smaller;
past
\delta_{n}=1
only Theorem
VIII.1
is available and
\lambda
must revert to

\lambda_{1}
. The upper bound is therefore the lower envelope of two curves, each used only
where its hypotheses hold, and the switch of
\lambda
at
\delta_{n}=1
produces a drop in both
bounds at that width rather than a continuous join.

XI-C

The Shape of the Risk

Collecting Theorems
XI.1
,
XI.2
and
Proposition
XI.2.3
, the error obeys at every width the floor

\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}\;\geq\;\mathrm{Fl}(\mathbf{m}):=\max\left\{\;A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})\;,\;\min\Big\{\|f^{*}\|_{n},\tfrac{(\lambda(\mathbf{m})-\xi)_{+}}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}\Big\}\;\right\},

(73)

the first entry being the irreducible approximation error and the second the price of
shrinkage. Retaining the approximation term is what keeps the floor positive after the
regularization drops back to
\lambda_{1}
, and is what makes the shape of the lower bound match
that of the upper bound.

Corollary XI.2.1
.

Under the schedule Eq. (
72
), and with
f^{*}\notin\bigcup_{\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{m},\infty)

so that
A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*})>0
at every width, the floor Eq. (
73
) behaves as
follows.

(i)

(First descent.) For small
\mathbf{m}
both entries decrease:
A_{\infty}

by the approximation theorem, and
\lambda_{2}\asymp C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}H(\mathbf{m})^{2}
because

H
decreases.

(ii)

(Valley.)
\mathrm{Fl}
attains an interior minimum at the width where
the two entries of Eq. (
73
) cross; the minimum is strictly positive, being at least

A_{\infty}
there.

(iii)

(Ascent.) Once
\delta_{n}
overtakes
H(\mathbf{m})^{2}
the second entry
grows like
C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\delta_{n}
and eventually dominates, so
\mathrm{Fl}
increases,
reaching
\min\{\|f^{*}\|_{n},(C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}-\xi)/L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\}
as
\delta_{n}\to 1^{-}
.

(iv)

(Second descent.) At
\delta_{n}=1
the schedule reverts to
\lambda_{1}
and

\mathrm{Fl}
drops to
\max\{A_{\infty}(\mathbf{m},f^{*}),(\lambda_{1}-\xi)_{+}/L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\}
,
thereafter decreasing with
A_{\infty}
towards
(\lambda_{1}-\xi)_{+}/L_{\sigma}^{L-1}
.

The upper bound of Theorem
X.1
has the same four phases, so both bounds are a

U
-shaped curve followed, after a drop at
\delta_{n}=1
, by a decreasing one.

XI-D

Double Descent of the Generalization Error

We can now state the phenomenon as a property of the error rather than of a bound on it. Write

\mathbf{m}_{0}
for a width at the valley of the underparameterized bound, where

H(\mathbf{m}_{0})^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}\asymp(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}})\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{0})
,
and
\mathbf{m}_{\sharp}
for a width at the end of the underparameterized regime,

\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{\sharp})\to 1^{-}
.

Theorem XI.3

(Double descent)
.

Let Assumptions
V.0.1
–
V.0.3
hold, let
\lambda
follow the
schedule Eq. (
72
), and let
\hat{\theta}
be a minimizer of
J_{n}(\cdot\,;\lambda)
.

(a)
(Empirical norm; no further hypothesis.)
Put

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fl}_{\sharp}\;:=\;\min\left\{\frac{\|f^{*}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},\ \frac{C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}-\xi}{L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}\right\},

(74)

a quantity that does not depend on
n
. Then, on an event of probability at least

1-e^{-f(n)}-\exp\big(-3n\rho(f^{*})^{2}/(28L_{\sigma}^{2(L-1)})\big)-O(n^{-C_{2}})
,

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}_{0}})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2C\big(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}\big)\,\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{0}),

(75)

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}_{\sharp}})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\mathrm{Fl}_{\sharp}^{2},

(76)

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;C\Big\{H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}+\Lambda_{L,d}\big(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}\big)\sqrt{\tfrac{f(n)}{n}}\Big\}\quad\text{for }\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m})\geq 1.

(77)

(b)
(Generalization error.)
Assume in addition
C_{1}>L_{\sigma}^{L-1}\mu(f^{*})
and
that
M_{*}/\sigma_{\epsilon}
lies in the interval of Remark
XI.0.2
, and put

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fl}^{(2)}_{\sharp}\;:=\;\|f^{*}\|_{2}\left(1-\frac{\mu(f^{*})\big(\|f^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\big)}{2C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}}\right)_{+}\;>\;0,

(78)

again independent of
n
. Then, on an event of the same probability,

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}_{0}})-f^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2C\big(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}\big)\,\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{0}),

(79)

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}_{\sharp}})-f^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\big(\mathrm{Fl}^{(2)}_{\sharp}\big)^{2},

(80)

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}_{\mathbf{m}})-f^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;C\Big\{H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}+\Lambda_{L,d}\big(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}\big)\sqrt{\tfrac{f(n)}{n}}\Big\}\quad\text{for }\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m})\geq 1.

(81)

In either part,
\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{0})\to 0
and
\sqrt{f(n)/n}\to 0
while the floor is fixed,
so the ratio of the middle display to either of the others diverges: the error descends,
rises by a factor tending to infinity, and descends again.

Remark XI.3.1

(Why the peak changes floor between the two parts)
.

Part (a) is the statement in the empirical norm and uses the shrinkage floor
(Theorem
XI.1
), which lives there natively; the two upper bounds are the
empirical versions, Theorems
VII.1
and
IX.1
. Part (b) is the
statement for the generalization error, and there the shrinkage floor
cannot
be
used: transferring it from
\|\cdot\|_{n}
to
\|\cdot\|_{2}
requires the localized concentration
inequality, which degenerates precisely at
\delta_{n}\approx 1
– that is, exactly at the width

\mathbf{m}_{\sharp}
where the peak sits, as explained in Remark
XI.2.5
.
Part (b) therefore uses the budget floor (Theorem
XI.2
), which is a statement
about
\|\cdot\|_{2}
from the outset because it never touches the design points, and pays for
this with the two-sided signal-to-noise hypothesis.

This is not a defect of the argument but a feature of the problem: at the interpolation
threshold the class can interpolate, so the empirical and population norms genuinely decouple,
and any floor for the generalization error at that width must be obtained without passing
through the sample. We regard the pair (a)–(b) as the correct form of the statement: (a) is
unconditional but concerns the training-sample error, (b) concerns the quantity one cares
about and costs a hypothesis on
f^{*}
.

Remark XI.3.2
.

Three features of this statement are worth isolating. First, it uses both halves of the
theory and neither suffices alone: Eq. (
75
) and Eq. (
77
) are
Theorems
IX.1
and
VII.1
, Eq. (
79
) and Eq. (
81
)
their counterparts Theorems
IX.2
and
VIII.1
, while the two middle
displays are Theorems
XI.1
and
XI.2
under the
schedule Eq. (
72
). An upper bound with a local
maximum does not entail a maximum of the error; a lower bound alone says nothing about what
happens on either side. Second, the ascent is located at the end of the underparameterized
regime, that is at the interpolation threshold
\delta_{n}=1
, and its height

does
not shrink with the sample size, whereas both valleys do. Third, the mechanism is the one
described in Remark

: what grows with the width is the regularization that
Theorem
IX.2
requires, and
\mathrm{Fl}_{\sharp}
is the shrinkage it causes. Combining
with Remark

, no width-independent

avoids this, since by
Theorem

a single

cannot both validate the underparameterized
bound near the threshold and reach the plateau.

Remark XI.3.3
.

The two curves may also be read off directly. On
\delta_{n}<1
the upper bound of
Theorem
IX.2
and the floor Eq. (

) are both
U

because
H(\mathbf{m})^{2}
falls while
\delta_{n}
rises, the latter because

\lambda_{2}=C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\max\{\delta_{n},H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\}
does the same; at

and the

regularizations; one may of course interpolate the two curves across a neighbourhood of

XI-E

Why the Ascent Cannot Be Tuned Away

It is natural to ask whether the ascent is an artefact of tying
\lambda
to the width vector, and would disappear for a regularization parameter chosen once and for all. It would not, and the reason is that the two regimes make incompatible demands.

Theorem XI.4
.

Suppose a single
\lambda
, independent of
\mathbf{m}
, is used at all widths, and that one requires both

(i)

the conclusion of Theorem
IX.2
at some width with
\delta_{n}(\mathbf{m})\geq\delta
, which demands
\lambda\geq C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}\delta
, and

(ii)

that the error at large widths reach the plateau.

Then (i) and (ii) are incompatible as soon as

\displaystyle\delta\;\geq\;\frac{2L_{\sigma}^{L-1}}{C_{1}\sigma_{\epsilon}}\sqrt{C\Lambda_{L,d}(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}})}\left(\frac{f(n)}{n}\right)^{1/4},

(82)

that is, as soon as the number of weights exceeds
n^{3/4}
up to logarithmic factors.

Remark XI.4.1
.

Consequently, for
every
schedule
\mathbf{m}\mapsto\lambda(\mathbf{m})
that both validates Theorem
IX.2
at widths of that size and attains the plateau at large widths, the floor at those widths exceeds the plateau, and
\lambda
must afterwards decrease. The ascent is a consequence of the two requirements rather than of the particular schedule we prescribe. What is
not
claimed is that a regularization parameter tuned to minimize the error separately at each width — carrying, in consequence, no guarantee of any kind — must also produce a peak; such a choice would fall below
\xi
in the underparameterized range, where Theorems
XI.1
and
XI.2
are vacuous. The precise statement is that the peak occurs for every regularization under which the theory’s guarantees hold.

XI-F

What Drives the Ascent

Theorem XI.5
.

If
\lambda\geq\xi
, then for every
f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}
and every
\mathbf{m}
,

\displaystyle\left\|g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}\right\|_{n}^{2}\;\leq\;C_{1}H(\mathbf{m})^{2}\|f^{*}\|^{2}_{W^{L}}+4\lambda\|f^{*}\|_{W^{L}}.

(83)

Inequality Eq. (
83
) is a coarsening of Eq. (
133
), and we record it separately because of what it does
not
contain: there is no term involving the number of weights, and none involving
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
divided by
n
. Above the threshold
\lambda\geq\xi
, therefore, the error carries no variance contribution of the form
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}(dm_{1}+m_{1}m_{2}+\cdots)/n
for any target whatsoever.

Remark XI.5.1
.

This identifies the mechanism of the ascent, and it is not the one a reader might expect. Above the threshold the estimator does not track the noise: a displacement along a tangent direction of the network manifold improves the data-fitting term by at most
\|h\|_{2}^{2}
while costing at least
\lambda\|h\|_{2}^{2}/\|h\|_{*}
in the penalty, so it is profitable only when
\lambda
is at most of the order of
\xi
. The rise of the error with the width is instead the price of the shrinkage that Theorem
IX.2
demands: as the number of weights grows,
\delta_{n}
grows,
\lambda_{2}
grows with it, and the estimator is pushed further from the target. The two regimes of Sections
VIII
and
IX
are thus separated not by a change in how much noise is absorbed, but by a change in how much regularization is required in order for the error to be controlled at all; and the peak marks the width at which that requirement is most severe. We regard this as a sharpening of, rather than a departure from, the discussion following Theorem
X.1
: the two regimes do behave differently, and the transition is real, but the quantity that changes is the admissible regularization rather than the absorbed variance.

Remark XI.5.2
.

The lower bounds above complement Theorem
X.2
rather than replacing it. That result is a minimax statement in the sample size, and pins the plateau; Theorems
XI.1
and
XI.2
are statements in the width at fixed sample size, and pin the shape. Neither implies the other, and both are needed: without the former the plateau could be far from optimal, and without the latter the shape would remain a property of an upper bound. We also note that no minimax quantity can supply the latter, since
\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}}
and
\inf_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{m},\infty)}\sup_{f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}}
are monotone in
\mathbf{m}
, the first by enlarging the class over which the supremum is taken and the second by enlarging the class from which the estimator is drawn.

Sec. XII
General Lipschitz Loss Functions

In this section, we extend the previous results from the mean squared loss to any Lipschitz loss function under some very mild conditions. This includes the mean squared loss, cross-entropy loss, hinge loss, etc. Such generalization is highly nontrivial, for example, involving localization of Rademacher or Gaussian complexity and uniform estimations. We first make the following assumption.

Assumption XII.0.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(f,y)
denote the loss function, and assume
\mathcal{L}
is differentiable almost everywhere with respect to
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

\mathcal{L}
is Lipschitz in the predictor, uniformly in
y
: for all
f_{1},f_{2}
and all
y
,

|\mathcal{L}(f_{1},y)-\mathcal{L}(f_{2},y)|\leq L_{1}|f_{1}-f_{2}|.

We write
L_{1}^{\prime}
for the corresponding Lipschitz constant of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
, so that
L_{1}^{\prime}\leq L_{1}
.
Moreover, for each
y
, it is
\gamma
-strongly convex in the predictor
f^{*}
with respect to the
L^{2}(\mu)
norm for some uniform
\gamma>0
, i.e.,

\int_{\mathbf{B}^{d}}\big(\mathcal{L}(f,y)-\mathcal{L}(f^{*},y)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f}(f^{*},y)(f-f^{*})\big)\,d\mu\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|f-f^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

for any
f
(note that this is not
\gamma
-strong convexity in the usual sense).

Loss functions satisfying this assumption include MSE loss and, under some mild conditions (e.g. on the form of target functions and/or data distribution), also cross-entropy loss, hinge loss, and Huber loss. See Section 14.3 in
Wainwright (2019)
for a detailed discussion of this property. Thus, it applies to most common machine learning problems.

On a dataset
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
, the empirical version of the loss function is denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{n}(f,y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(f(x_{i}),y_{i})
, where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})
. By the Cauchy inequality,
|\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{1},y)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(f_{2},y)|\leq L_{1}\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{n}
.

Since we are now facing a general Lipschitz loss, there is not always an explicit expression like
\|\hat{f}-f^{*}\|_{2}
as the measure of the difference between the estimator and the true model. We propose such a measure. First, let
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(g(x;\hat{\theta}),f^{*}(x)):=\mathbb{E}_{y}\mathcal{L}(g(x;\hat{\theta}),y)
, the expectation over
y
conditioned on
x
. It must be Lipschitz with respect to the first argument
g
as we similarly have
\forall x,|\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(g_{1},f^{*})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(g_{2},f^{*})|\leq L_{1}|g_{1}-g_{2}|
and its empirical version. We denote its Lipschitz constant by
L_{1}^{\prime}
. We suggest using

\mathbb{D}(g(\cdot;\hat{\theta}),f^{*}(\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(g(x;\hat{\theta}),f^{*}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{x}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(f^{*}(x),f^{*}(x))

as the measure of the difference between
g(\cdot;\hat{\theta})
and
f^{*}
(without using the data
(x,y)
). For example, for regression problem Eq. (
15
),
L
is MSE, and
\mathbb{D}(g(\hat{\theta}),f^{*})=\int_{x}\|(g(x;\hat{\theta})-f^{*}(x)\|_{2}^{2}dx+\sigma^{2}
; for binary classification problem,
L
is
ylogp(x)+(1-y)\log(1-p(x))
, and
\tilde{L}=p^{*}logp(x)+(1-p^{*})\log(1-p(x))
, and
\mathbb{D}(g(\hat{\theta}),f^{*})=\int_{\mathbf{B^{d}}}p^{*}logp(x)+(1-p^{*})\log(1-p(x))d\mu
. Both agree with our common practice, possibly up to a constant. For a given training dataset
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
, we similarly have the empirical version
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(g(x;\hat{\theta}),f^{*})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(f^{*},f^{*})
. The second term
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(f^{*},f^{*})
plays the role of a normalization factor, so that
\mathbb{D}(f^{*},f^{*})=0
, which is required.

Generalization to an arbitrary loss function satisfying the above assumption is completely nontrivial, requiring more techniques and mathematical tools. All the techniques and methods are detailed in the Supplementary Information

Sec. XIII
Comparison with Existing Results

Classical data-dependent generalization error bounds, e.g., Rademacher complexity-based bounds, typically have the following form.

Proposition XIII.0.1
.

Assume the loss function
\mathcal{L}
is 0-1 valued. For any
\delta>0
, with probability greater than
1-\delta
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f(x),y)\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(x_{i}),y_{i})+R_{n}(\varphi\circ f)+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{n}},

(104)

where
\varphi
is a dominating cost function,
\varphi\circ F=\{(x,y)\rightarrow\varphi(f(x),y)-\varphi(0,y),f\in F\}
, and
R_{n}(f)
is the sample Rademacher complexity with respect to the training data
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq n
.

This kind of expression has some features different from ours. First, it works for any predictor
f
regardless of how the model and training process are; second, it uses the McDiarmid concentration inequality to relate the left and right sides since the loss is 0-1 valued; third, it uses the Rademacher complexity of the predictors and does not consider any other complexity measures.

Since we do not require the loss function to be bounded, we rely on the Talagrand and local Talagrand concentration inequalities for the overparameterized and underparameterized regimes, respectively, instead of the McDiarmid concentration inequality, but the same thing is that we all need to estimate the Rademacher complexity required by these concentration inequalities. The second essential difference is that we need to estimate the empirical error
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, which highly relies on the optimality of the network estimator. The third is that we explicitly distinguish between the overparameterized and underparameterized regimes since their behavior is quite different, making the error bounds more accurate and predictive of the double descent phenomenon.

Sec. XIV
Discussion

1.

We present the first
near
-complete generalization theory for
almost
general multilayer fully connected feedforward neural networks with path regularizations.
Near
and
almost
mean that we impose some very mild conditions on activation functions and loss functions. The theory is nonetheless widely applicable. Its overparameterized rate is sharp uniformly in the radius of the target ball when the loss is MSE and the activation is ReLU, with the qualification of Remark
X.2.1
. This theory predicts the double descent phenomenon.

2.

The regularization term is needed: without it the empirical error bounds, for instance
Theorem
VII.1
, are no longer valid.

3.

Our upper bounds use the hypothesis
f^{*}\in W^{L}_{M}
only through a bound on
\nu(\hat{\theta})
,
and such a bound is exactly what the dynamical stability of SGD supplies: a global minimum
that is linearly stable at learning rate
\alpha
has bounded path norm
(
wu2023implicit
)
.
In the overparameterized regime, where that analysis applies, the basic inequality is therefore
available for the unregularized estimator as well, and the upper bounds carry over with the
learning rate playing the role of an inverse regularization parameter. We develop this
elsewhere. The lower bounds do not transfer in the same way, because they are driven by the
penalty itself, which is absent there.

4.

The empirical and generalization error bounds are, however, not optimal. Establishing a lower bound for the error estimation will give us a hint and insight into what the optimal error upper bound should be. Having a stronger approximation theory for multilayer networks will lead to a better bias bound. One critical direction is that we do not fully leverage the structure of the minimizer of our learning problem in our estimation of variance terms, as it is hard to explicitly characterize it for highly nonlinear neural networks. Any breakthrough in this aspect will make our variance estimation sharper and more useful (i.e., dependent on the width vector), which will also be our future research direction.

5.

To fully understand the generalization theory of neural networks, fully connected multilayer neural networks are only the first step. There remains a number of research questions. First, what is the corresponding theory for more general loss functions and activation functions? Second, what is the corresponding theory for other regularizations, including implicit regularization like early stopping? Lastly, what is the corresponding theory for other types of neural networks, e.g., LSTM, CNN, or transformer and combinations thereof? We expect the techniques developed here to adapt to these settings. Convolutional and recurrent networks are multilayer networks with symmetry constraints on the weights, so the argument should carry over with little change; transformers would require separate treatment of their arithmetic operations.
