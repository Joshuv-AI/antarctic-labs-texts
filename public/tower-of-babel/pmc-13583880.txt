Spiritual wellbeing in advanced cancer patients receiving palliative care in a tertiary cancer centre in northern Kerala, India: a qualitative study
Authors: M S Biji, Rajasree KC, Shoba Nair, Praveen Kumar Shenoy, Odette Spruyt, Chitra Venkateswaran, Satheesan B, Dehannathparambil Kottarathiil Vijayakumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesSpiritual well-being is an essential component of holistic palliative care. However, patients’ lived experiences of spirituality in advanced cancer remain underexplored in low- and middle-income settings. This study aimed to explore spiritual well-being from the perspectives of patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care in a tertiary cancer centre in Kerala.DesignQualitative descriptive study using face-to-face, in-depth, semistructured interviews and conventional content analysis conducted between November 2022 and December 2024.SettingOutpatient palliative care services of a tertiary cancer centre in Kerala, India.Participants20 adults with advanced cancer receiving palliative care were recruited using purposive sampling. Interviews were conducted in Malayalam. Data were audiorecorded, transcribed verbatim, translated into English and analysed using conventional qualitative content analysis as described by Graneheim and Lundman. Data collection continued until saturation was achieved. Trustworthiness was ensured through credibility, dependability, confirmability and authenticity.ResultsA total of 127 codes were identified and organised into 18 subthemes and four overarching themes: (1) multidimensional suffering and reflection in advanced cancer, encompassing physical suffering, psycho-existential distress, social concerns, financial burden and moral reflection; (2) healing through social support and relationships, emphasising the supportive roles of family, community and healthcare professionals; (3) coping mechanisms and inner strength, including acceptance, peer inspiration, prior life experiences and adaptive psychological and physical strategies and (4) spiritual beliefs and practices, where faith, prayer, rituals and spiritual questioning contributed to meaning-making and emotional regulation.ConclusionsSpiritual wellbeing in advanced cancer extends beyond religious practices and is shaped by interconnected experiences of illness, relationships, spiritual beliefs and cultural context. Addressing these dimensions through holistic and culturally sensitive palliative care may enhance emotional resilience and overall quality of life.Keywords: PALLIATIVE CARE, Quality of Life, ONCOLOGY

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 In India, including Kerala, where awareness and healthcare access are relatively high, a substantial proportion of patients continue to present with advanced disease, for whom curative treatment may no longer be feasible. In such situations, palliative care plays a critical role not only in symptom management but also in addressing the psychological, social and spiritual dimensions of suffering.2

Spiritual well-being is increasingly recognised as a key component of quality of life in advanced cancer and those receiving palliative care.3 In this study, spiritual well-being is understood as a multidimensional aspect of human experience that relates to the way individuals seek and express meaning, purpose and connectedness and the way in which they experience relationships with themselves, others, nature or the sacred. This understanding is consistent with the definition proposed in the international consensus on spiritual care in palliative care.4 Accordingly spiritual well-being is conceptualised as something more than a coping strategy or source of social support, encompassing existential, relational and faith related dimensions that influence how a person interprets his or her sufferings. While spiritual concerns are often viewed as end-of-life issues, evidence suggests that they may emerge at any point following diagnosis, influencing adjustment throughout the illness trajectory.5 6 This study explores how patients with advanced cancer experience and express these dimensions within their sociocultural and religious context.

A growing body of literature demonstrates that higher levels of spiritual well-being are associated with lower anxiety and depression, improved coping and better overall quality of life among patients with advanced cancer.7 Conversely, unmet spiritual needs have been linked to poorer satisfaction with care and increased healthcare utilisation at the end of life.8 9 Studies have also shown associations between spiritual distress and symptom burden, including pain, fatigue, anxiety and depression in patients with serious illness.10–12 In an Indian study exploring spiritual concerns among patients receiving palliative care, commonly reported issues included faith in God, acceptance of illness, belief in karma and rebirth, concern about the future and existential questioning such as ‘Why me?’.13

Although evidence supports the role of spiritual care, it remains insufficiently addressed in routine clinical practice, particularly in non-Western contexts.13 14 In these settings, spiritual well-being is strongly shaped by cultural, religious and relational worlds, which may not be adequately reflected in assessment tools developed in Western healthcare systems.

In Kerala (a southern state of India where Malayalam is the primary official and vernacular language), recent efforts to linguistically validate spiritual well-being measures, such as the Malayalam version of the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Spiritual-Well-being-Expanded, highlight a growing recognition of the need for culturally appropriate assessment.15 However, quantitative tools alone may not fully capture the nuanced, contextual and deeply personal experience of spiritual well-being in advanced illness. Qualitative research offers rich insights into patients’ lived experiences, bringing to light aspects of spirituality that are difficult to measure, such as culturally rooted beliefs and rituals, moral reflections, relationships and existential concerns.6

To date, there is limited qualitative research exploring how patients with advanced cancer in Kerala understand and experience spiritual well-being within the context of palliative care. Addressing this gap is important for developing culturally sensitive spiritual care and strengthening holistic palliative care practice.

This qualitative study forms part of a larger mixed-method PhD research project assessing the spiritual well-being among patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care. This study builds on the previous quantitative part, which found that higher spiritual well-being was associated with better quality of life.16 However, the quantitative findings did not explain how patients understood and experienced spiritual well-being. This study aimed to explore spiritual well-being from the perspectives of patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care in a tertiary cancer centre in Kerala.

Methods

Study design: A qualitative descriptive study design was used.17

Study setting: he study was conducted at the outpatient palliative care services of a tertiary-level autonomous cancer centre under the Government of Kerala, located in Thalassery, South India.

The palliative care service follows a holistic approach addressing physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs. Spiritual concerns are explored when relevant during clinical interactions, with supportive listening and discussion of patients’ beliefs, values, sources of meaning and concerns related to illness and suffering. Patients may be supported in accessing family, community or faith-based resources according to their preferences.

Study participants and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants who could provide relevant information about their spiritual well-being. The sampling technique allowed selection of participants with varying age, diagnosis, socioeconomic status and treatment status to capture a range of experiences.

Eligible participants were recruited from patients attending the palliative care outpatient services of the centre. Inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged 18 years or older; (2) diagnosis of advanced cancer, defined as metastatic, recurrent or progressive malignant disease; (3) receiving palliative care and (4) ability to read and comprehend Malayalam.

Patients were excluded if they had cognitive impairment or communication difficulties that prevented meaningful participation in the interview, were clinically unstable as determined by the investigator (including those requiring hospitalisation or intensive care) or were unwilling to participate due to time constraints.

Potential participants were approached by the first author (MSB) who explained the purpose and procedures of the study in detail. Some participants may have been familiar with MSB through clinical care at the palliative care service. However, no specific relationship was established prior to recruitment for the study. Written informed consent, including consent for audiorecording of the interviews, was obtained prior to participation. Participants were assured that their information would remain confidential and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their care.

Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted between November 2022 and December 2024.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, diagnosis, performance status, type of treatment received and time since completion of primary treatment, were extracted from medical records and recorded in a structured data collection sheet (online supplemental file 1). Additional information, including marital status, religion, family structure and participants’ awareness of their disease and prognosis, was obtained through direct interviews and documented in the same form. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the ‘Modified Kuppuswamy Socioeconomic Scale’.18

Data were collected through individual, face-to-face, in-depth, semistructured interviews using an interview guide (online supplemental file 2) developed by the research team based on relevant literature and clinical expertise.13 19 The guide was used flexibly to ensure consistency but at the same time allowed participants to elaborate on issues of importance to them. Interviews were conducted in Malayalam, the native language of both the interviewer and participants, by the first author (MSB), a female PhD candidate in palliative medicine. She is a palliative care clinician with more than 20 years of clinical experience in palliative care, particularly in symptom management and has undergone training in qualitative research methods.

Interviews were conducted in a private room within the outpatient palliative care service to ensure confidentiality and minimise interruptions. Caregivers were permitted to be present only at the participant’s request for support and did not participate in the interviews. Interviews lasted for 45–60 min, were audiorecorded with participants’ consent and field notes taken to document contextual details and non-verbal cues. No repeat or follow-up interviews were done in any of the participants.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim within 48 hours. Transcripts were translated into English and back-translated to Malayalam by the first author to ensure accuracy and preserve participants’ meanings. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new codes or concepts emerged from successive interviews, as determined by the research team.

Data analysis

Audiorecorded interviews and field notes were checked for accuracy and de-identified prior to analysis. The research data were managed and analysed using QDA Miner Lite V.3.0.8 (MSB).20 Qualitative content analysis was conducted using a conventional approach as described by Graneheim and Lundman, which enables interpretation of both manifest and latent content across increasing levels of abstraction.21

Analysis was conducted on the translated English transcripts. First, transcripts were imported into QDA Miner Lite and read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data. Meaning units relevant to the study aim were then identified, condensed and assigned codes (MSB). An inductive approach was used, allowing codes to directly emerge from the data. Coding initially focused on participants’ experiences of suffering and the way in which they overcame these sufferings. The codes were exported from the software for manual sorting and grouping into subthemes based on the similarities in content (MSB, PKS). The resulting subthemes were organised into overarching themes representing the latent meaning of the text.21 Data analysis was iterative and reflective, requiring movement back and forth between the data, codes and emerging themes, with revisions made when new insights emerged. The final codes, subthemes and themes were reviewed and discussed among the research team (BSM, PKS, RKC, SN, OS and CV) until consensus was reached.

A total of 127 codes were generated and subsequently organised into 18 subthemes and four overarching themes.

Trustworthiness of the findings

The trustworthiness of the study was ensured by addressing credibility, dependability, transferability, confirmability and authenticity, in line with established qualitative criteria.21 Credibility was improved by doing the interviews in a supportive setting where participants felt comfortable sharing openly, by using their exact words to show their experiences of spiritual well-being and through repeated discussions among the research team during analysis. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction and participants did not provide feedback on the preliminary findings. Dependability was strengthened by keeping clear records of how we analysed the data and having the research team check the codes and themes. Transferability was supported by clearly describing the study setting, participant characteristics and research procedures. Confirmability was supported by reflexive notes and an audit trail. Authenticity was strengthened by including at least one quotation for each subtheme and by presenting views from different participants.

Reflexivity statement

The first author is a palliative care clinician with a long-standing interest in spiritual well-being, and this background could have influenced how the data were collected and interpreted. For example, while interviewing one participant (P19) the interviewer asked “Is it because your faith was low before your illness and has increased now?” This question contains an implicit assumption that illness may lead to stronger faith. However, the participant immediately rejects this assumption (“No”) showing that the participant was able to express views that differed from those anticipated by the interviewer. To address this, reflexive notes were written after each interview, and interpretations were regularly discussed with co-researchers. These steps helped examine assumptions and ensured that the findings reflected participants’ accounts as much as possible rather than the researcher’s own views.

Reporting guidelines

This qualitative study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.22 A completed COREQ checklist is provided as online supplemental file 3.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Theme 1: Multidimensional suffering and reflection in advanced cancer

Participants described how the diagnosis and progression of cancer affected their daily functioning, emotional state, relationships and sense of self. Physical symptoms such as pain, fatigue and reduced mobility were common. One participant described the persistent nature of the pain:

The trouble is because of this pain. The pain is always there… even with medicines it doesn’t go fully. (P14, female, 40–50 years).

Despite these challenges, many participants described adapting to their limitations through practical adjustments and a determination to maintain independence. For example, one participant living with stoma explained, “The main difficulty I face is having this stoma bag… I always carry a spare stoma bag—you never know when it will leak.” (P10, male, 50–59 years)

Alongside physical symptoms, participants described fear, uncertainty and emotional distress, especially at diagnosis. One participant reflected, “When I realized the severity of this disease, a sudden fear came over me, and I did not know what to do.” (P07, female, 40–49 years). Illness also affected participants’ social lives and relationships. Some expressed sadness about reduced social participation while others experienced losing previous roles.

I do not talk about my disease to others because once they know, they begin to look at me differently. People feel that after cancer comes, it never goes away and remains in the body forever. (P10, male, 50–59 years)

It has been two years since my wife and daughter left me, and I no longer have any relationship with them. (P13, male, 50–59 years)

Financial hardships emerged as another important source of distress. The cost associated with treatment, travel and loss of income placed considerable strain on patients and families. “Monthly check-ups require a lot of money for travel. That is my main difficulty.” (P15, Female, 40–49 years)

Many participants were worried about becoming a burden to their loved ones. Dependence on family members for physical, emotional and financial support often generated feelings of guilt and concern.

My elder sister’s son and my younger sister’s son are the ones who take care of me. Both of them also have their own families to support, and none of us are financially well off. To avoid burdening them, we decided to proceed with radiation therapy instead of surgery. (P04, female, 50–59 years)

I can't do anything alone; I have to rely on someone else for everything. (P14, female, 40–49 years)

Alongside these experiences of suffering, participants engaged in deep inner questioning about past actions, regrets or life purpose.

I feel it may be the result of a serious sin from my previous life. (P08, female, 70–79 years)

Over time many participants described a gradual process of acceptance and adaptation. Rather than remaining focused on their illness, they sought ways to continue living meaningfully and move forward with their lives.

Now I don’t think too much… I have accepted it. Life has its ups and downs. (P02, female, 20–29 years)

Within a year of completing treatment, I returned to my studies and did not think about anything else. (P12, female, 20–29 years)

Theme 2: Healing through social support and relationships

Participants consistently described relationships as an important source of strength, comfort and healing throughout their illness journey. Support extended beyond immediate family to include friends, neighbours, religious communities, support groups and healthcare professionals. Many participants valued the practical and emotional support they received from their wider social networks.

Many people travelled long distances to visit me… as a token of gratitude for the small services I had done. (P03, female, 50–59 years)

I am part of a community called FORCE. The convener regularly calls to check on my health, at least once a week. (P01, female, 50–59 years)

Family relationships were particularly important. Participants frequently described spouses, children, siblings and extended relatives as their primary source of emotional, physical and financial support contributing to a sense of security and hope.

My father’s younger brother supports me, both financially and in other ways. (P18, female, 40–49 years).

When my daughter got married, I forgot everything and have not thought about my disease since then. (P19, female, 50–59 years).

Many participants explained that being able to talk, express feelings and be listened to provided emotional relief. Feeling heard made them feel valued.

When I opened up to someone about things I had never shared before, it brought me a sense of relief and happiness. (P10, male, 50–59 years)

Positive interaction with healthcare professionals further contributed to participants’ well-being. Trust, reassurance, kindness and clear communication helped patients feel emotionally comfortable and safe.

The staff here are all very supportive. (P09, female, 40–49 years)

Then the doctor reassured me. Um, there’s nothing to be afraid of. He said that I'll be fine with this one treatment and nothing else. (P19, female, 50–59 years)

Theme 3: Coping mechanisms and inner strength

Participants used a variety of internal and external strategies to cope with illness and its challenges. Many found strength by observing how others managed similar or even more difficult circumstances. Some compared themselves positively with others who were more ill.

There was an 18-year-old girl beside me with cancer in her stomach. After hearing her story, I felt a little stronger. (P02, female, 20–29 years)

Previous life experiences also served as a source of resilience. Participants reflected on past hardships, caregiving responsibilities and personal challenges that had prepared them to face their present condition.

I faced my mother’s illness with her… I did everything I could for her. (P01, female, 50–59 years)

Many participants coped by maintaining daily routines and engaging in meaningful activities that provided a sense of normalcy and purpose. Practical strategies such as exercise, relaxation techniques, household tasks and gardening helped distract from suffering and promoted a sense of well-being.

I have faith in yoga. I do breathing exercises, and when I reach relaxation, my mental difficulties are reduced. (P01, female, 50–59 years)

I grow bananas and vegetables, but I cannot stir the field. With help from others, I am able to plant. (P17, female, 60–69 years)

Participants also described cognitive and emotional strategies that helped them maintain stability in the face of uncertainty. Acceptance, positive thinking, distraction and consciously avoiding excessive worry were commonly used to manage distress and preserve inner peace.

I try not to think too much… that helps me stay calm. (P10, male, 50–59 years)

Theme 4: Spiritual beliefs and practices

Spiritual beliefs and practices played an important role in how patients understood and responded to their illness. Many participants described faith in God as a source of strength, comfort and hope during periods of uncertainty. Religious practices such as prayer, visiting places of worship and reading religious texts were commonly used to cope with emotional distress and maintain a sense of inner peace.

I have great faith in prayer and keep chanting as I go about my day. (P03, female, 50–59 years)

Participants frequently described prayer as providing relief from fear, worry and suffering. For some, prayer created a sense of calm and temporary escape from illness-related concerns, while others felt it helped reduce their experience of physical suffering.

Yes, I feel relief when I pray and when I light a lamp. Then I don’t think about anything; it’s like I have forgotten everything. (P20, female, 50–59 years).

When I pray, to be honest, it feels like my pain is less. (P06, female, 40–49 years)

At the same time, spirituality was not always experienced as a source of comfort. Some participants engaged in spiritual questioning as they tried to understand the meaning of their illness and suffering. They reflected on the divine purpose of their disease, questioned divine intentions and at times expressed doubts about their faith.

When I pray, I ask, ‘Why did God give me this disease?’ (P15, female, 40–49 years)

I was a believer. But when disease keeps increasing like this, our faith does not get any result. Then why, what faith? (P07, 50–59 years)

Discussion

This qualitative study explored spiritual well-being among patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care and identified four interrelated themes: multidimensional sufferings and reflection in advanced cancer, healing through social support and relationships, coping mechanisms and inner strength and spiritual beliefs and practices. The findings show that spiritual well-being is dynamic and shaped by physical suffering, emotional distress, relationships with others, meaning-making and cultural spiritual practices.23 24

The present findings complement our previous quantitative study by explaining how spiritual well-being is experienced and shaped within the personal, social, cultural and existential context of advanced cancer.16

Participants’ narratives suggest that spiritual well-being cannot be understood separately from the broader experience of living with advanced cancer. Physical symptoms, functional decline, uncertainty about the future, social stigma, financial hardship and concerns about burdening family members were closely interconnected and often contributed to psychological and existential distress. These findings support the concept of ‘total pain’, which recognises suffering as a multidimensional experience encompassing physical, emotional, social and spiritual distress.25 This interpretation is further supported by a systematic review, which describes cancer-related suffering as extending beyond physical symptoms to include existential, relational and spiritual dimensions.26 At the same time, participants demonstrated considerable resilience through acceptance, positive reframing, relying on past life experiences, engaging in meaningful activities and maintaining social connections. Together, these findings suggest that spiritual well-being is not simply the absence of distress but a dynamic process through which individuals seek meaning, maintain hope and adapt to the challenges of advanced illness. Similar observations have been reported in studies of patients with life-limiting illnesses, where meaning-focused coping and resilience were associated with reduced existential distress and improved spiritual well-being27 28

Another important finding was the central role of relationships and spirituality as sources of strength and meaning. Family members, relatives, community networks and healthcare professionals provided emotional reassurance, practical assistance and a sense of belonging. These relational resources were closely intertwined with participants’ spiritual lives, reflecting a view of spirituality that extended beyond individual religious belief. Faith in God, prayer and rituals provided comfort, peace and temporary relief from suffering, consistent with previous studies in advanced cancer and palliative care settings.4 27 29 However, spirituality was not experienced solely as a source of comfort. Some participants expressed spiritual questioning and doubts regarding divine purpose and justice leading to spiritual struggle or distress. This varying spiritual response seen in advanced cancer patients highlights the need to recognise spiritual distress as well as spiritual strengths.23 30

Cultural and contextual considerations:

The findings must be interpreted within the Indian sociocultural context, where strong family ties, community involvement and religious practices are commonly part of everyday life.31 32 In this study, participants rarely described spiritual well-being as a personal experience. Instead, it was closely intertwined with community connectedness, family relationships, religious practices and concerns about loved ones. This relational understanding of spiritual well-being is similar to the observations from Indian and other Asian cultures where the self is understood in relation to one’s family and community rather than as the individual alone.33

These findings suggest that spiritual care in Kerala may need to extend beyond individual beliefs and acknowledge relational and family-centred dimensions of suffering and meaning.

Concerns about healthcare costs and being a burden to family members significantly influenced emotional experiences and treatment-related decisions.34 35 These challenges are common in low- and middle-income settings and should be considered when providing culturally sensitive palliative care and spiritual care.36 37

Clinical implications

The findings add to the evidence that spiritual concerns are many and addressing these is a necessary part of palliative care. Healthcare professionals should proactively assess spiritual distress, create opportunities for emotional expression, support family and community involvement and respect patients’ spiritual and religious beliefs.4 24

Economic distress is a major but often overlooked source of spiritual and existential suffering, particularly in settings where patients and families bear the cost of care. Spiritual care should therefore acknowledge financial suffering, facilitate access to social support and address the moral distress among healthcare providers arising from overwhelming poverty and uncertainty to enable truly holistic palliative care.24 34

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the in-depth exploration of patients’ lived experiences, providing rich insights into spiritual well-being from the patients’ own perspectives using a rigorous qualitative content analysis approach.21 As all participants were recruited from a single palliative care unit, perspectives may reflect institution-specific approaches to spiritual care. The predominance of female participants, largely due to the high proportion of breast cancer patients, may have limited the representation of male perspectives particularly regarding family relationships, caregiving roles, emotional expressions and spiritual coping.

In addition, the religious composition of the study sample did not fully reflect the religious diversity of Kerala.38 Hindu participants were over-represented, with limited representation from Christian participants and no representation from the Muslim community. The themes that emerged in this study may primarily reflect the Hindu cultural and spiritual perspectives prevalent in Kerala. As spiritual beliefs, practices and interpretation of illness are often shaped by religious traditions, these findings may not be fully transferable to people from different religious backgrounds.39 40

Future research

Future research should explore spiritual well-being in gender-balanced and religiously diverse populations, examine caregivers’ perspectives and longitudinal changes in spiritual well-being across the illness trajectory and evaluate structured spiritual care interventions in palliative care settings especially in low- and middle-income settings. Further cross-cultural studies may deepen understanding of how spirituality is experienced and expressed in different socioeconomic contexts.36 41

Conclusions

Spiritual well-being in advanced cancer extends beyond religious practices and is shaped by interconnected experiences of illness, relationships, coping strategies and spiritual beliefs. Addressing these dimensions through holistic and culturally sensitive palliative care may enhance emotional resilience and overall quality of life.4 23 24
