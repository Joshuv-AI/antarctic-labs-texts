Comparative clinical evaluation of demineralized freeze-dried bone allograft (DFDBA) alone versus DFDBA with platelet-rich fibrin membrane in the management of intrabony defects: A step toward enhanced periodontal regeneration
Authors: Sarita Parihar, Aishwarya Pandey, Shweta Ahlawat, Kajol Kumari Rajak, Anjali Patel, Ajit Vikram Parihar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Periodontal regeneration in intrabony defects remains a critical goal in periodontics. Demineralized freeze-dried bone allograft (DFDBA) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) have shown potential in enhancing clinical outcomes. This study compared the clinical efficacy of DFDBA alone versus DFDBA combined with PRF in the treatment of intrabony periodontal defects.Material and Method:A randomized, double-blinded, split-mouth clinical trial was conducted on patients diagnosed with Stage III or IV, Grade B or C periodontitis, each presenting with paired infrabony defects. A total of 15 defect sites were treated with DFDBA alone (control group), and 15 sites with DFDBA and PRF (test group). Clinical parameters, including probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PI), and gingival index (GI), were recorded at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25, with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:Both groups showed significant intragroup improvements in all parameters (P < 0.001). The DFDBA + PRF group demonstrated significantly greater probing pocket depth (PPD) reduction (P = 0.01) and CAL gain (P = 0.04) compared to DFDBA alone at 6 months, while PI and GI showed no significant intergroup differences.Conclusion:Both DFDBA alone and DFDBA combined with PRF significantly improved periodontal parameters. However, the adjunctive use of PRF yielded greater reductions in PPD and superior clinical attachment gains, indicating potential clinical advantages in terms of graft stabilization and soft-tissue healing. Further studies with extended follow-up and histologic evaluation are recommended to substantiate these findings.

INTRODUCTION

A primary challenge in periodontal treatment is the restoration of lost periodontal tissues. Various strategies have been suggested to enhance periodontal healing, including barrier membranes, bone graft materials, biological mediators, and enamel matrix proteins. One such material, demineralized freeze dried bone allograft (DFDBA), is a well established grafting material known for its osteoinductive potential, promoting new bone formation by stimulating progenitor cell differentiation into osteoblasts.[12] Numerous growth/differentiation factors, such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), in particular BMP 2, 4, and 7, are present in DFDBA, responsible for periodontal repair and regeneration. Histologic analyses demonstrate that DFDBA promotes true periodontal regeneration in infrabony defects, characterized by the regeneration of functional periodontal structures, including newly formed cementum, organized periodontal ligament fibers, and alveolar bone architecture, whereas open flap debridement (OFD) predominantly yields reparative healing marked by the long junctional epithelium without significant regeneration of the attachment apparatus.[3]

First described by Choukroun et al. in 2001, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) is a second-generation autologous platelet concentrate comprising a three-dimensional fibrin scaffold embedded with a concentrated population of platelets and leukocytes, facilitating sustained release of growth factors and promoting tissue regeneration. When combined with bone graft materials, PRF confers multiple clinical benefits, such as enhancing osteogenesis and soft tissue healing, accelerating bone maturation, providing graft stabilization and hemostasis, promoting wound closure, and optimizing the biomechanical handling characteristics of grafting materials.[4] Dohan Ehrenfest et al. provided compelling evidence that PRF exerts a pronounced and sustained mitogenic effect on various human primary cell cultures. Their research demonstrated that PRF significantly enhances the proliferation and biological activity of gingival fibroblasts, pre-keratinocytes, and osteoblasts derived from maxillofacial region. These findings underscore the bioactive potential of PRF in modulating cellular behavior critical to tissue regeneration and wound healing processes in both soft and hard tissues.[5] Contemporary clinical studies evaluating the treatment strategies for management of periodontal intrabony defects have reported that incorporating PRF as an adjunct to conventional OFD results in significantly improved clinical parameters and regenerative outcomes compared to OFD alone.[67]

This clinical study aimed to assess and contrast the effectiveness of DFDBA alone versus its combination with PRF in enhancing periodontal tissue regeneration in infrabony defects.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

15 systemically healthy individuals (8 males and 7 females; mean age: 40± 2.2 years) diagnosed with Stage III or IV, Grade B or C periodontitis, as classified by the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions, were recruited for the study. Each participant exhibited at least one pair of contralateral infrabony periodontal defects suitable for comparative analysis [Flowchart 1]. The clinical trial was conducted between April 2024 and January 2025 in the outpatient unit of the institution. This investigation followed randomized, double-blind, controlled design and employed a split-mouth, parallel approach to compare the therapeutic efficacy of DFDBA alone versus DFDBA combined with PRF in the surgical management of intrabony periodontal defects. The study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, with comprehensive written informed consent obtained from each subject following a thorough explanation of the study’s objectives, procedures, and potential risks before their participation.

CONSORT Flowchart of study participants

Approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to its commencement with ethical clearance no. Dean/2023/EC/6084 and was registered in Clinical Trials Registry – India with CTRI No. CTRI/2024/03/063595.

The inclusion criteria for this study required participants to present with a pair of interproximal infrabony periodontal defects located in asymptomatic posterior teeth. These defects were characterized by a probing depth (PD) of ≥ 5 mm, measured 4 weeks following completion of Phase I periodontal therapy, and a radiographically confirmed vertical bone defect depth of ≥ 4 mm. Subjects were required to demonstrate optimal plaque control, with both plaque and gingival index (GI) scores ≤ 1 at the time of re-evaluation.

Exclusion criteria encompassed the presence of any systemic condition that could potentially affect periodontal healing, the use of medications known to interfere with bone metabolism or wound healing, pregnancy or lactation, tobacco use in any form, a history of previous periodontal surgical intervention at the site of interest, defects with only a single bony wall, and sites exhibiting furcation involvement.

The primary outcome variables in this study were clinical attachment level (CAL) gain and probing pocket depth (PPD) reduction. Secondary outcome parameters included changes in plaque index (PI) and GI. The required sample size was calculated based on data derived from the study by Agarwal et al. (2016) using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7. With a significance level (α) of 5% and a statistical power of 95%, the estimated sample size was determined to be 15 defect sites per group.[8]

Randomization was through a lottery method where an independent researcher not involved in the trial was asked to randomly select sealed envelopes containing the allocated treatment modality for each newly enrolled participant. The envelopes were only opened after patient enrolment, ensuring that neither the researchers nor the participants were aware of group assignments beforehand. Thus, the patients were randomly allocated to two groups:

Preoperative assessment

Following the completion of initial nonsurgical periodontal therapy—which encompassed the provision of personalized oral hygiene instructions along with thorough scaling and root planing—participants who demonstrated satisfactory plaque control (plaque score < 25%) and compliance with oral hygiene protocols were considered eligible for study inclusion.

Two separate, blinded examiners, who were unaware of the treatment allocation, performed all clinical and radiographic assessments (pre and post) to minimize observer bias and maintain the integrity of the data collection process [Figures 1(a and b), 2(a and b)]. Prior to the commencement of data collection, a calibration exercise was undertaken to ensure consistency and reliability of measurements between the two investigators. This process involved each examiner independently assessing the same set of pilot participants—who were not included in the main study cohort—on two separate occasions spaced 1 week apart. The purpose was to evaluate intra- and inter-examiner reliability for both clinical and radiographic parameters. Clinical parameters were assessed at baseline (prior to surgical intervention) and 3 months and 6 months postoperatively. The same blinded examiners performed all baseline and follow-up assessments (3 and 6 months), and the operating surgeon was not involved in outcome recording to ensure complete examiner blinding. These included PPD, defined as the distance from the gingival margin to the base of the periodontal pocket, and CAL, measured from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the base of the pocket. All measurements were obtained using a standardized University of North Carolina No. 15 periodontal probe (UNC-15, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). In addition, the PI and GI were evaluated to assess oral hygiene status and gingival inflammation, respectively.

Interventions

All surgical procedures were carried out by a single calibrated clinician, ensuring procedural consistency.

After administration of local anesthesia, 2% lignocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline (Lignox 2%, Indico Warren, India), intrasulcular incisions were carefully made on both the buccal and lingual/palatal aspects of the affected tooth. These incisions were extended mesially and distally to include one adjacent tooth on either side, thereby ensuring adequate surgical access and flap mobility. All incisions were performed using a No. 15c surgical blade to allow for precise tissue dissection and flap design. Each defect site was surgically managed through the elevation of a full-thickness (mucoperiosteal) flap, with careful attention to preserve the integrity of the surrounding soft tissues. Following flap reflection, the exposed root surfaces and associated osseous defects were meticulously debrided using manual curettes under continuous sterile saline irrigation to ensure thorough removal of granulation tissue and bacterial deposits. For the test sites (Group A), PRF was prepared autologously from the participant’s venous blood according to standardized centrifugation protocols.[9] A volume of 10 mL of freshly drawn peripheral blood was obtained via venipuncture from the antecubital vein using a sterile 10 mL syringe without the addition of any anticoagulant agents. Immediately following collection, the blood sample was subjected to centrifugation using a digital tabletop centrifuge (Velab VE-4000, TX, USA) equipped with a fixed-angle rotor (45° angle, maximum rotor radius: 10.6 cm). The centrifugation was performed at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for a total duration of 10 minutes at room temperature. Approximately 1 ml of the resulting PRF was carefully aspirated using a sterile syringe under aseptic conditions for immediate clinical application. The aspirated PRF gel was further compressed using PRF box [Figure 3a and b]. DFDBA was placed within the defect, followed by the application of PRF membrane [Figures 4a and 5].[9] Control sites (Group B) received DFDBA alone without adjunctive use of PRF [Figure 4b]. Upon completion of graft placement, the flaps were repositioned to their original anatomical level and secured using 3-0 resorbable vicryl sutures. Primary wound closure was achieved utilizing interrupted suturing technique [Figure 6a and b].

Postoperative care

Patients of both the groups were prescribed a combination antibiotic (amoxicillin 500 mg and clavulanic acid 125 mg) to be taken twice daily for 7 days, and a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug 400 mg Ibuprofen TID daily for 3 days. Oral hygiene instructions included avoiding brushing and flossing in the surgical area for 2 weeks, with twice-daily rinsing using 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate solution for 1 minute. Sutures were removed 2 weeks postoperatively. Thereafter, patients were advised to resume mechanical plaque control using an ultrasoft toothbrush. Follow-up appointments were scheduled weekly during the first postoperative month, and subsequently at 3 and 6 months. Compliance with oral hygiene instructions was reinforced at each recall visit. Adherence was objectively monitored using PI and GI scores. All patients maintained good compliance throughout the study period, and no cases required exclusion for inadequate oral hygiene.

Statistical analysis

Data for all variables assessed in this study were compiled using Microsoft Excel 2021 and analyzed statistically with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; Chicago, Illinois, USA). The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between groups, whereas the Friedman test was utilized for within-group analyses. Significance values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction.

RESULTS

Both the control group (DFDBA alone; Group A) and the test group (DFDBA + PRF; Group B) demonstrated statistically significant intergroup and intragroup improvements in all clinical parameters—PPD, CAL, GI, and PI—from baseline to 6 months (P < 0.001) [Figures 7a, b, 8a, b and Table 1]. Within-group comparisons confirmed consistent reductions in PPD, GI, and PI, as well as gains in CAL over time, indicating favorable healing responses in both treatment modalities. At baseline, mean PPD and CAL were comparable between groups (P > 0.05). By 6 months, Group B exhibited significantly greater reductions in PPD (3.47 ± 0.61 mm vs. 3.91 ± 0.75 mm; P = 0.01) and greater CAL gain (3.24 ± 0.73 mm vs. 4.20 ± 0.76 mm; P = 0.04) than Group A. At 6 months, the effect size for intergroup differences in PPD reduction was moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.64; 95% CI: –0.09 to 1.38), while the effect size for CAL gain was large (Cohen’s d = 1.29; 95% CI: 0.50 to 2.07), indicating a clinically meaningful advantage with the adjunctive use of PRF. Although both groups showed reductions in GI and PI, intergroup differences at all time points were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that while both interventions were clinically effective, the adjunctive use of PRF with DFDBA yielded superior improvements in key periodontal parameters.

DISCUSSION

The continued presence of intrabony periodontal defects following nonsurgical therapy signifies the ongoing progression of periodontal disease and subsequent attachment loss.[10] These residual osseous defects serve as persistent sites of inflammation and microbial colonization, potentially compromising long-term periodontal stability. Consequently, the fundamental objective of tailored, patient-specific periodontal treatment strategies is the effective resolution of these defects. This includes not only halting disease activity but also promoting the regeneration of the tooth-supporting apparatus, encompassing new formation of alveolar bone, periodontal ligament, and cementum, with the ultimate goal of restoring the structural and functional integrity of the periodontium.[11] The underlying rationale for the management of intrabony periodontal defects is to achieve a significant reduction in residual PDs, thereby enhancing the long-term prognosis and stability of affected teeth.[11] Over the past few decades, a broad spectrum of therapeutic modalities in addition to nonsurgical debridement techniques has been developed and applied to address these defects. These include a variety of surgical interventions—ranging from conservative and resective procedures to advanced regenerative approaches.[12131415] While each of these strategies has demonstrated varying degrees of clinical success, regenerative techniques, in particular, have garnered attention for their potential to restore the lost periodontal attachment apparatus through the formation of new bone, cementum, and periodontal ligament.

Ideal bone graft materials for periodontal applications must exhibit key biological and physical characteristics to ensure therapeutic efficacy. Specifically, such materials should possess osteoinductive potential to stimulate the differentiation of progenitor cells into osteoblasts, facilitating new bone formation with favorable handling properties that allow for easy adaptation to the defect morphology, as well as sufficient mechanical integrity to maintain space and provide structural support at the graft site. DFDBA has demonstrated considerable promise in this regard. Histologic evidence supports its regenerative potential, with studies reporting complete periodontal regeneration in up to 80% of treated sites.[3] The osteoinductive capacity of DFDBA is primarily attributed to its demineralization process, which exposes embedded BMPs.[1617] These BMPs play a pivotal role in promoting mesenchymal cell recruitment and osteoblastic differentiation, thereby enhancing the regenerative outcome in intrabony periodontal defects. There has been growing scientific and clinical interest in autologous platelet concentrates as novel regenerative biomaterials in periodontal therapy, demonstrating favorable clinical outcomes in the treatment of various periodontal defects.[18] Among these, PRF, used either alone or in conjunction with bone graft materials, has been shown to significantly improve clinical parameters such as PPD reduction and CAL gain.[19] These regenerative effects are largely attributed to PRF’s biologically active three-dimensional fibrin matrix, which facilitates cellular migration, adhesion, and differentiation. This scaffold is enriched with platelets and leukocytes that continuously release a wide array of growth and differentiation factors at the wound site, thereby promoting tissue regeneration.[20] Furthermore, advancements in centrifugation protocols—specifically the introduction of the low-speed centrifugation concept—have led to the development of enhanced PRF formulations, such as PRF, characterized by improved cellular content and more homogeneous distribution of bioactive components.[21]

The findings of the present study demonstrated statistically and clinically significant improvements in PPD and CAL at the 6-month follow-up in both treatment groups. As detailed in Table 1, intergroup comparisons revealed a statistically significant difference in PPD and CAL outcomes at 6 months (P < 0.05), indicating favorable therapeutic effects. In periodontal regeneration, even a 1 mm gain in CAL may positively influence prognosis by reducing disease recurrence and improving tooth survival. The effect size analysis further supports this, with a moderate effect observed for PPD and a large effect for CAL, underscoring the clinical impact of PRF as an adjunct. These results are consistent with previous investigations, wherein the application of PRF yielded significant reductions in PPD and gains in CAL.[2223] The outcomes reported in those studies were likewise found to be both statistically robust and clinically meaningful, further supporting the regenerative potential of PRF in the management of periodontal intrabony defects. These findings suggest that PRF may be particularly advantageous in certain clinical situations, such as defects with greater baseline depths and morphologically unfavorable intrabony lesions where graft stabilization is critical, and in patients where enhanced soft tissue healing is desirable. The absence of intergroup significant differences in PI and GI may be attributed to strict plaque control and rigorous oral hygiene monitoring in both groups, thereby masking any potential anti-inflammatory advantage of PRF. Intragroup analysis revealed statistically significant improvements in all clinical parameters (PPD, CAL, GI, and PI) from baseline to 6 months in both groups, indicating effective periodontal healing irrespective of the treatment modality.

However, the study’s findings should be viewed in light of its limitations. The relatively short follow-up period of 6 months may not fully capture the long-term stability of the regenerative outcomes. This time frame was chosen because it represents the early healing phase in which the majority of clinical improvements in PD reduction and attachment gain are typically observed, while also minimizing the risk of patient attrition. Nevertheless, longer follow-up periods of 12 months or more would provide more accurate evidence regarding the stability and sustainability of the regenerative outcomes. Furthermore, although all randomized patients completed the trial with no attrition during the follow-up phase, three patients were excluded prior to randomization. Consecutive enrolment and random allocation were adopted to minimize selection bias. However, the relatively small sample size remains a limitation that may restrict the external validity of our findings. The absence of histologic evaluation and advanced imaging modalities limits confirmation of true periodontal regeneration as clinical measurements alone cannot differentiate between repair and regeneration. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, histologic analysis, and advanced imaging are recommended to validate these findings.

CONCLUSION

Considering the limitations of this randomized clinical trial, both DFDBA alone and its combination with PRF resulted in notable improvements in PD reduction and clinical attachment gain after 6 months. Although overall regenerative outcomes were favorable in both groups, the adjunctive use of PRF demonstrated superior improvements in PPD and CAL. The combination offers a biocompatible, autologous option that may support periodontal regeneration by promoting sustained growth factor release and enhancing tissue healing. Nonetheless, larger-scale studies with longer follow-up periods and advanced imaging are warranted to confirm these outcomes.
