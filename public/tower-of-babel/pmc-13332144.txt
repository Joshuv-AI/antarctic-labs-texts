Synthesis and characterization of Ag doped CuO nanorods electrode for non-enzymatic glucose sensing
Authors: Vinuta Shetty, Raju Patel, Tanmoy Majumder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of stable and highly sensitive non-enzymatic electrochemical glucose sensors is essential primarily to treat diabetes. In this work, Ag-doped CuO nanorods electrode were prepared using hydrothermal technique and used for non-enzymatic glucose detection. The structural, morphological, and chemical composition characteristics of the pristine and Ag-doped electrode were analysed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), high resolution transmission electron microscopy(HRTEM) with high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) elemental mapping and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), confirming successful formation of nanorods and incorporation of Ag in CuO. Electrochemical glucose sensing performance of doped and undoped CuO nanorods was characterized using cyclic voltammetry (CV), chronoamperometry (CA), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). In comparison to the pristine CuO nanorods, the electrocatalytic activity toward glucose oxidation is found to be enhanced after incorporation of Ag into the CuO lattice effectively. Ag-doped CuO exhibits a sensitivity of 2520 µAcm–2 mM–1 in the linear range of 5 µM to 900 µM with a detection limit of 2.5 µM. The sensor also shows good reproducibility, stability, and selectivity against common interfering species.Keywords: Cyclic voltammetry, Nanorods, Sensitivity, Limit of detection, RepeatabilitySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Glucose is an essential monosaccharide which plays a crucial role in energy conversion in human body while participating in many biochemical reactions carried out to support the body metabolism and normal functioning of muscles and other organs1. Hence maintaining glucose in human body at its optimal level is most important. If one’s body fails to properly regulate glucose it can lead to chronic health conditions such as diabetes. This condition may be genetically inherited or may arise due to immune system disorders and detrimental lifestyle practices. These factors ultimately cause diabetes since it deteriorates the function of islets of Langerhans and leads to glucose level imbalance while acquiring an insulin resistance2. Diabetes is linked to a range of health issues such as heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, nerve damage, and eyesight loss. Monitoring glucose level in a human body is essential for both diabetic individual and as well as a healthy person. In a diabetic individual the insulin dosage can be adjusted if precise glucose level is known before injecting, since some diabetic patients rely on insulin injection regularly so that they are assured that glucose levels are kept within a safe range and minimising the risk of complications. Whereas healthy people can monitor glucose levels to adjust their diet and exercise patterns and have a healthy lifestyle while keeping chronic diseases such as diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular diseases at bay. But this traditional diabetes management often involves multiple daily finger-prick tests, a painful and inconvenient routine that patients must endure. In order to make these painless, noninvasive methods to sensing is largely been explored. Saliva naturally contains glucose, at a lower concentration than blood, which can directly correlate with blood glucose levels. So salivary glucose sensors are one such non-invasive route to adapt but it expects the sensor to have much lesser LOD and very good sensitivity than invasive sensors.

Clark and Lyons developed the first enzymatic electrode in the 1960s3 ever since the glucose sensors have gone through a lot of developmental phases starting from first to third generation as enzymatic glucose sensor. These type of sensors even though they are well established commercially, they may fall short in terms of stability and longevity because of the environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and pH4. To overcome these drawbacks the fourth-generation glucose sensors are made non-enzymatic by incorporating nanostructured electrodes replacing the enzymes which are addressed as nano-zymes possessing large surface areas and exceptional electrocatalytic ability for direct glucose oxidation5.These nanozymes can be noble metals6–8, conductive polymers9–11, transition metals and their oxides4,12–14, as well as bimetallic combinations of these metals15–19 metal organic frameworks (MOF)20–24 and MXenes25–28 have been employed for non-enzymatic glucose sensing. Among various nanomaterials, metal oxides such as nickel oxide29 cobalt oxide30, and copper oxide31,32 have been the ideal choice to be utilized as an electrocatalytic agent for glucose detection owing to their high stability, low cost, and proper redox performance13. Compared to other oxides, CuO has relatively higher conductivity, lower cost and higher sensitivity, making it widely used in glucose sensing33,34. The electrochemical activity of CuO is depended on its structure, morphology and particle size. Its performance can also be enhanced by sensitization with noble metal oxide or through doping. Ag is found to possess good catalytic properties and excellent conductivity and doping CuO with Ag will improve the electrochemical activity by enhancing the electrical contact among the substrate, the active materials and the electrolyte35. Although surface-modified Ag electrodes and Ag-CuO nanocomposite-based electrodes have been extensively investigated for non-enzymatic glucose sensing, to the best of our knowledge, no study has reported based on Ag-doped CuO nanorods for electrochemical glucose sensing. Herein, we report for the first time an enzyme-free electrochemical glucose sensor based on Ag-doped CuO nanorods.

Results and discussion

The surface morphology of pristine and Ag-doped CuO nanostructures was examined using FE-SEM. The top-view SEM micrograph image of Pristine CuO and Ag doped CuO arrays grown on the FTO substrate is depicted in the Fig. 1. It is evident from the top view image that the hydrothermally grown pristine CuO that is shows nanorod structure as in Fig. 1(a) Whereas after doping CuO with Ag there is slight change in the microstructure the nanorods do not possess uniform diameter along the length rather it shows a leaf like morphology (Fig. 1b). This is because HMTA decomposes to release OH⁻ gradually under the influence of heat and leads to controlled formation of Cu(OH)2, which then converts to CuO upon the annealing at 300 °C. HMTA plays the role of selectively favouring 1D growth along preferred directions. After Ag(NO)₃ is added, the Ag+ ions can form a complex with HMTA36, which alters the rate of OH⁻ release. This leads to non-uniform growth kinetics, promoting branched or leaf-like architectures. The adsorbed Ag+ can increase the nucleation density and reduce surface energy anisotropy, weakening the strong directional 1D growth and promotes secondary lateral growth37. The average density of 7.9 × 109 nanorods per cm2 and 4.5 × 109 nanorods per cm2 in pristine and Ag doped CuO, respectively. This suggests that the Ag incorporation have influenced nucleation and growth kinetics38 with fewer nanostructure which grown laterally to some extent exhibiting leaf like morphology. Although the change in nanostructure is observed its primary one-dimensional growth nature is preserved and lateral edges exposed for adsorption enhancing the performance.

The XRD patterns of pristine CuO and Ag-Doped CuO is depicted in the Fig. 3(a) reveals the presence of the tenorite phase, since the characteristic peaks may be identified as 32.78° (110), 35.85° (002), 39.03 ° (111), 51.71° (112), 53.66° (020), (022), 68.31 (220) and 78.48° (004). As per the PDF card (JCPDS No. 48–1548) this configuration corresponds to a monoclinic structure consisting lattice parameters of a = 4.683 Å, b = 3.423 Å, c = 5.129 Å, and angle β = 99.54°41. After doping CuO with Ag the diffraction peaks of Ag-CuO NR have been shifted towards lower 2theta value since Ag with larger atomic radii (1.15 Å) has replaced Cu2+ (0.73 Å) ion42. The Raman spectra of CuO and Ag-CuO exhibit two prominent modes corresponding to the monoclinic tenorite phase of CuO (Fig. 3b). The main Ag mode appears at for 297.82 cm–1 and 296.64 cm–1 CuO and Ag Doped CuO respectively. Red shift was observed from 297.82 cm–1 to 296.64 cm–1 (red shift ~ 1.18 cm–1) and in the case of Bg mode 631.70 cm–1 to 630.58 cm–1 (red shift ~ 1.12 cm–1) for pristine and Ag doped CuO respectively. The other additional peaks like 59.62 cm–1 is due to Lattice translational vibration and 175.72 cm–1 is due to Bg mode and 342.66 cm–1 is due to Bending vibration43. These peaks remain present after Ag doping, confirming that the monoclinic CuO phase is preserved without forming a secondary phase. The intensity is also found to slightly enhanced in the case of Ag doped CuO. This enhancement is primarily attributed to defect-induced chemical (charge-transfer) effects arising from Ag incorporation, which increases oxygen-vacancy concentration and Cu3+ content, thereby enhancing the polarizability of Cu-O bonds44.

From the XPS analysis we determine that Cu2p exhibit photoelectron peaks at 931.97 and 951.84 eV corresponding to Cu2p3/2 and Cu2p1/2 respectively in the case of Ag Doped CuO and energy difference between then is 19.87 eV indicating the formation of copper (II)oxide. The typical XPS full scan in Fig. 4(a) shows the presence of copper (Cu3p Cu3s, Cu2p, and its Cu LMM Auger), oxygen (O1 s), Silver (Ag 3d) and carbon (C1 s) on the surface of nanomaterials45 In the high resolution XPS of Ag have two distinct peaks at 373.50 eV (Ag 3d 3/2) and 367.48 eV(Ag 3d 5/2) with spin–orbit splitting difference of 6.02 eV indicating the presence of Ag (0) and Ag(I) oxidation states. Furthermore, the Ag 3d spectrum can be deconvoluted into two sets of peaks based on different oxidation states (Fig. 4f). The feature (pink curve) at the lower binding energies of 367.60 and 373.70 eV can be assigned to Ag+ and whereas the one (cyan curve) at the higher binding energy at 368.04 and 374.03 eV can be assigned to metallic Ag0 of Ag 3d5/2 and Ag 3d3/2 respectively. These Ag0-like features should not be interpreted as metallic Ag nanoparticles but rather as highly dispersed interfacial or partially reduced Ag species arising from Ag–O–Cu bonding environments since XRD does not show any diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Ag or crystalline Ag2O, indicating the absence of segregated Ag nanoparticles or secondary crystalline phases42. The Cu 2p deconvoluted spectrum of both CuO and Ag doped CuO is compared (Fig. 4b and d). The Cu⁺ and component exhibits only a minimal shift (~ 0.06 eV).In contrast, the Cu²⁺ component shows a pronounced positive shift of 0.6 eV (Cu2+2p 3/2) and 0.9 eV(Cu2+2p 1/2) upon Ag doping, indicating modification of the Cu2+–O bonding environment. This larger shift arises from charge redistribution associated with partial substitution of Cu2+ by Ag+, the formation of oxygen vacancies and leading to enhanced Cu–O covalency. In both pristine CuO and Ag doped CuO the dominant O 1s peak at 529.20 eV (CuO)-529.31 eV (Ag doped CuO) corresponds to lattice O2– in the Cu–O framework, confirming that the CuO crystal structure is preserved after Ag doping. Second peak is related to surface defects especially oxygen vacancies at 530.63 eV Fig. 4 (c) and (e). Notably, here the Ag-doped CuO exhibits a more pronounced peak than pristine CuO which are associated with oxygen vacancies and defect-related oxygen species. This enhancement indicates that Ag incorporation induces additional oxygen vacancies for charge compensation, consistent with partial substitution of Cu2+ by Ag+ within the CuO lattice. The high binding region at 531.77 (Ag doped CuO) eV and 532.43 eV(CuO) can be ascribed to the adsorbed oxygen/water species when sample is exposed to ambient condition46. Together, these observations indicate that Ag is incorporated predominantly through substitutional or defect-assisted mechanisms within the CuO lattice, inducing oxygen vacancies and electronic modulation, while any surface AgOx species, if present, are highly dispersed and below the detection limit of XRD. Therefore, Ag is not present as segregated metallic Ag or Ag2O nanoparticles but substitutionally doped into the CuO lattice. The Ag atomic percentage(A%) is also determined from XPS Spectrum is ~ 1.67% which confirms lightly doped Ag on CuO crystal lattice.

The cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements are carried out in the potential range of 0–1 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) with the scan-rate of 50 mV/S. The cyclic voltammogram of pristine and Ag doped CuO for different glucose concentration ranging from 50 µM,100 µM, 200 µM, 500 µM and 1 mM is depicted in the Fig. 5(a) and (b), here the peak current is found to increase gradually with increase in glucose concentration. In the CV plot as in Fig. 5(a) and (b) the oxidation current is found increase after adding 0.1 mM concentration of glucose for both pristine CuO and Ag doped CuO but the later has slightly higher current density in the voltage region (0.8–1 V) indicating better electrocatalytic activity. An obvious oxidation peak is observed at 0.5 V corresponding to the oxidation of glucose to gluconolactone and a small oxidation bump at 0.72 V that have occurred more likely due to the intermediate oxidation of glucose via partial reduction of Ag+ to Ag0. Therefore, the Ag doping has enhanced the electron transfer kinetic for glucose oxidation. It is observed that at 0.15 V there is a small cathodic dip defining the reduction peak more evidently in Ag doped CuO than pristine CuO showcasing promising reversibility of redox reaction. The generally accepted mechanism of non-enzymatic electro-oxidation of glucose on CuO electrode surface occurs as copper exists predominantly as Cu(II). When glucose is present in an alkaline medium and a suitable potential is applied, Cu(II) is oxidized to Cu(III) (Eq. 1). Since Cu(III) is a strong oxidizing agent that reacts with glucose, oxidizing it to gluconolactone (Eq. 2). As Cu(III) oxidizes glucose, it is reduced back to Cu(II), thus completing the redox cycle and regenerating the active sensing material. Glucose interacts with the Cu(III) species at the electrode interface and it undergoes oxidation to form gluconolactone losing electrons in the process. Gluconolactone can subsequently undergoes hydrolyzation to gluconic acid in an aqueous environment (Eq. 3). Schematic of glucose sensing mechanism shown in Fig. 5 (c).

One of the primary concerns in designing a salivary glucose sensor is to achieve good selectivity despite of the presence of other oxidizing components other than glucose which are more likely to be present in saliva. This selectivity of the Ag doped CuO electrode is tested by performing amperometric i-t at 0.55 V vs. Ag/AgCl in 0.1 M NaOH with addition of interfering molecules like 0.1mM urea(UA), NaCl, KCl, sucrose (SU) and ascorbic acid (AA) and Dopamine (DA). Figure 8(a) shows the changes in the current density after adding 0.1mM of Glucose, Urea, NaCl, AA,0.1mM glucose, KCl, SU, AA, DA and 0.1mM glucose respectively. There is no significant change or no change at all after adding the interfering agents but the change in current density for 0.1 mM of glucose is 0.4794 mA/cm2, 0.4189 mA/cm2 and 0.2891 mA/cm2 respectively when added in sequence along with the other interfering agents in the interval of 30 s as shown in the Fig. 8(a) The interferants like NaCl, KCl primarily contribute to the ionic background without interacting with the Cu²⁺/Cu³⁺ catalytic sites responsible for glucose oxidation hence the response current is preserved. After addition of sucrose and ascorbic acid and dopamine there is an evident decline in the response produced by glucose added following the three interferents. Sucrose an organic compound and are prone to interact with the electrode surface, although it is not electroactive under alkaline conditions, can weakly adsorb onto the CuO surface and partially block the active Cu³⁺ catalytic sites for glucose molecules, leading to a slight suppression of the current. Whereas ascorbic acid (AA), can possibly undergo oxidation or adsorption at the electrode surface being an electroactive species and may compete with glucose for active sites58. In spite if this the subsequent glucose addition still produces a pronounced current increase, confirming that the Ag-doped CuO nanorods retain their catalytic activity and that glucose oxidation remains the dominant process. The stability of Ag doped CuO nanorod electrode is tested by running 50 cycles of CV with 100µM of glucose added. All the 50 cycles are depicted in Fig. 8(b) and the current density at 0.6 V for 1st cycle is 0.7033 mA/cm2 and for the 50th cycle 0.66867 mA/cm2 showing very minimum difference ensuring the good stability of the electrode. The repeatability test is conducted for Ag doped CuO nanorod by using 5 consecutive amperometric i-t test of the same Ag doped CuO nanorod sample. Figure 8(c) shows the 5 consecutive amperometric i-t responses after adding 100 µM glucose solution. The bar plot inset of Fig. 8(c) shows that all the five measurements have the current density value close enough to prove the excellent repeatability of the Ag doped CuO glucose sensor. Silver (Ag) doping plays a crucial role to enhanced the glucose sensing performance of Ag-CuO nanorods compare pristine CuO nanorods. Ag in CuO also acts as an extra supporting electrocatalytic promoter by proving additional electrocatalytically active sites and enabling glucose adsorption and oxidation in alkaline NaOH electrolyte, which enhanced electrode sensitivity. The synergistic interaction between Ag and CuO creates efficient carrier transfer pathways, which accelerating Cu(II)/Cu(III) couple redox reactions and resulting in higher current density response. Ag doping further reduces charge transfer resistance (Fig. 7) leading to better repeatability and long-term durability.

Conclusion

In this study a simple and cost-effective hydrothermal process is employed to synthesize Ag-doped CuO nanorod and employed as an electrode material for non-enzymatic electrochemical glucose sensing application. The structural analysis like SEM revealed the successful formation of nanorod. XRD, Raman and XPS analysis successfully confirmed the incorporation of Ag into the lattice of CuO. Electrochemical sensing studies performed in the alkaline medium shows good sensitivity, stability and selectivity toward glucose in pristine CuO nanorod which is found to be enhanced by doping Ag into it. The sensitivity towards glucose detection was found to be 2060 µAcm-2 mM-1 with a detection limit of 3.63 µM for pristine CuO. For Ag doped CuO electrode the sensitivity was calculated 2520 µAcm-2 mM-1 in the linear range of 75 µM with a detection limit of 2.5 µM. Doping of Ag in CuO has resulted in increasing sensitivity by 22% and LOD is reduced by 31.2% approximately.

Experimental details

The chemicals used for synthesis of silver doped copper oxide nanorods are of analytical grade and they are used without further purification. Cupric acetate monohydrate Cu(CO2CH3)2(SRL), Copper (II) nitrate trihydrate, Cu(NO3)2 3H2O (merck,99%), Hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA)( (CH2)6N4) (merck), Methanol (CH3OH), Diethanolamine (C4H11NO2), Silver nitrate (AgNO3) (SRL), Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) (SRL), D-Glucose(C6H12O6) (merck,99%) are the chemicals used for the synthesis.

For the synthesis of Ag doped Copper oxide nanorod a two-step chemical route is followed which include, the seed layer deposition by sol gel spin coating and hydrothermal deposition of CuO nanorod with Ag as a dopant. Fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO) is used as a substrate for the deposition. The sol is prepared using copper acetate as a precursor, methanol as a solvent with ethanolamine as a stabilizing agent. The sol is then spin coated on the cleaned and dried FTO substrate which is then dried at 120 °C for 10 min and later annealed at 300 °C to improve the crystallinity. The substate with seed layer is then dipped vertically in 20 mM equimolar solution of copper nitrate and HMTA and 1 ml of 10 mM of AgNO3 solution is added in 20 ml screw capped borosilicate glass vials. For comparison pristine CuO NR is also prepared at the same time. The vials are kept in the water bath maintained at 85 °C ± 3 °C for about 5 h. The as grown film is then washed thoroughly with DI water and annealed at 350 °C for 3 h to improve the crystallinity. The schematic representation of synthesis method is as shown in the Fig. 9. Apart from the controlled addition of AgNO₃, all synthesis parameters were kept constant. This ensured that any observed changes in morphology or electrochemical performance could be unambiguously attributed to Ag incorporation rather than synthesis variability.

Schematic of synthesis mechanism of Ag-doped CuO nanorod.

The electrochemical measurements for glucose sensing are carried out in a three-electrode system by an electrochemical workstation. The electrolyte used here for all the experiment is aqueous solution of 0.1 M NaOH. A platinum wire is used as a counter electrode, Ag-CuO on FTO substrate used a working electrode and Ag/AgCl is used as a reference electrode. Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) is carried out at a potential scan rate of 50 mV/S. Amperometric i-t is carried out in an electrochemical cell containing 30 ml of electrolyte and with continuous stirring with speed of 240 rpm. The continuous stirring speed was fixed after the many trials with different speed lower speed than 240 rpm could not accurately determine the rapid current change and higher speed will create a turbulence and noise. Higher or lower speed than 240 rpm did not improve the sensitivity but rather reduced the signal stability. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is carried out in the similar set up with applied frequency range of from 1 MHz to 1 Hz.
