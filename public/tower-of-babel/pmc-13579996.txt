Advancing equitable and sustainable school-based nutrition interventions
Authors: Gozde Aydin, Claire Margerison, Janandani Nanayakkara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schools are key settings for improving children’s nutrition, but interventions often focus narrowly on classroom education. This Collection highlights the value of whole-school action addressing food environments, implementation, equity and sustainability. We aimed to attract research that strengthens scalable, context-sensitive school nutrition strategies and reduces disparities in children’s dietary outcomes.

Advancing equitable and sustainable school-based nutrition interventions

Schools are a critical setting for supporting children’s nutrition, shaping dietary behaviours, and building food literacy [1]. Evidence syntheses show that school-based nutrition interventions can improve dietary outcomes, yet many approaches remain narrowly focused on classroom education and individual behaviour change, with less attention to the broader systems that shape children’s food experiences at school [1]. Increasingly, research highlights the importance of understanding and intervening within school food systems where food provision, school policies, governance, leadership, and partnerships influence what is feasible to implement and sustain [2]. Whole-school frameworks reinforce this perspective, emphasising coordinated action across curriculum, school environments, and the wider school community to support student health [3].

This broader framing also sharpens attention to equity and feasibility. Even well-designed interventions may have limited impact if they are difficult for schools to adopt, support or sustain over time [2, 4]. Schools differ substantially in staffing capacity, infrastructure, competing priorities, and the contextual constraints associated with socioeconomic disadvantage, all of which shape the feasibility, reach and sustainability of the interventions [2]. Implementation questions (e.g., acceptability, adoption, resourcing, and sustainment) are therefore central to the research agenda, particularly where interventions rely on discretionary staff time or short-term funding [2, 4].

Food insecurity adds urgency to this agenda, with evidence that schools are increasingly taking on roles in responding to hunger and supporting students experiencing food hardship, often via locally tailored strategies [5]. At the same time, the broader contexts shaping children’s eating extend beyond schools: parents report multiple barriers to providing healthy school lunches, including cost, time constraints, convenience, and children’s preferences, which influence what children bring to school and consume during the school day [6, 7]. Together, these findings suggest that effective school-based nutrition strategies need to be realistic, context-responsive, and designed with an explicit equity lens, rather than assuming families and schools have equal capacity to act [2, 5–7].

Sustainability is another increasingly important consideration. Schools are well placed to support food-related behaviours aligned with both human and planetary health, but sustainable school-based nutrition initiatives must be feasible, culturally responsive, and appropriate to local contexts [4, 8]. Evidence on environmentally sustainable school food system interventions highlights the growing interest in integrating sustainability into school food policies and practices, while also underscoring the challenges of implementation and the need for practical, scalable approaches [8]. Work focusing on long-term programme sustainability similarly emphasises that interventions must fit within real-world school systems - supported by resources, governance, and implementation capacity - if they are to endure beyond initial enthusiasm or pilot funding [4].

This BMC Nutrition Collection brings together three studies that illustrate these real-world complexities and highlight important advances, challenges, and opportunities for future research.

Burton et al. examine how vegetable gardens are used, managed, and resourced in Australian primary schools using a mixed-methods design [9]. The study shows that gardens are widely used for experiential learning activities (including growing and harvesting produce, and in many schools, eating and cooking with produce), but that utilisation and curriculum integration vary considerably across schools [9]. The authors highlight practical factors central to sustained delivery, including the demands of time and funding, the importance of supportive leadership, clear roles for staff and volunteers, and access to training and community involvement to maintain gardens and embed activities into routine practice [9]. These findings reinforce wider evidence that school-based nutrition initiatives are more likely to be sustained when they are system-supported rather than dependent on a small number of motivated individuals or “extra” activities vulnerable to staff turnover and competing priorities [2, 4].

Railton et al. evaluate New Zealand’s Ka Ora, Ka Ako free, healthy school lunch programme using a quasi-experimental approach comparing participating and non-participating schools over time [10]. The study reports evidence of improved eating patterns, including a statistically significant programme effect for a composite “any unhealthy food” indicator consistent with a protective effect against worsening unhealthy intake trends observed in comparison schools [10]. In contrast, the authors report no significant changes in child-reported food insecurity indicators, wellbeing, or overweight/obesity over the study period, noting that longer follow-up may be needed to detect anthropometric impacts [10]. These results highlight an important implication for policy and evaluation: dietary pattern changes may be detectable earlier than downstream outcomes such as wellbeing or weight status, which are shaped by multiple determinants and can involve longer causal pathways [10].

Ognjenovic and Dong provide a comparative analysis of primary school meal nutrition across low- and high-poverty boroughs in Inner London, combining nutrient analysis with an objective nutrient profiling assessment and borough-level child measurement data [11]. They find significant differences in nutrient composition by area: lunches in high-poverty boroughs were lower in total energy and some macronutrients and sugars, but also lower in selected micronutrients (including iron, zinc and vitamin A) compared with menus in more affluent areas [11]. Using the nutrient profiling model, meals in high-poverty boroughs scored more favourably overall for both main courses and desserts [11]. Despite these menu differences, borough-level prevalence of overweight and obesity remained higher in high-poverty boroughs, suggesting that socioeconomic disparities in weight outcomes likely reflect broader drivers beyond school lunch nutrition alone [11].

Taken together, the studies in this Collection underscore several cross-cutting challenges and knowledge gaps. First, implementation and sustainment remain pivotal: interventions that cannot be routinely delivered, resourced, or supported within school systems are unlikely to achieve sustained or equitable impacts [2, 4, 9]. Second, equity must be treated as a design principle rather than a secondary analysis; policy and intervention effects may differ by school and community context, and inequities can persist even within shared policy environments [5, 11]. Third, family and household constraints remain central to what children eat during the school day, reinforcing the importance of approaches that bridge school and home contexts and address practical barriers related to cost and time [6, 7]. Fourth, integrating sustainability into school nutrition strategies requires careful attention to feasibility and local context to avoid promoting idealised approaches that are not maintainable or culturally appropriate [4, 8].

Conclusions

This BMC Nutrition collection highlights the importance of moving beyond isolated nutrition education towards integrated, whole-school approaches that address food provision, equity, implementation, and sustainability. Future efforts should prioritise research that advances scalable, context-responsive strategies to improve children’s nutrition and wellbeing.
