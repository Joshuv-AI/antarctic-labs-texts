Adaptive Reduced Order Modelling of Discrete-Time Systems with Input-Output Dead Time Journal: Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing
Art J. R. Pelling, Ennes Sarradj

Abstract
While many acoustic systems are well-modelled by linear time-invariant dynamical systems, high-fidelity models often become computationally expensive due the complexity of dynamics. Reduced order modelling techniques, such as the Eigensystem Realization Algorithm (ERA), can be used to create efficient surrogate models from measurement data, particularly impulse responses. However, practical challenges remain, including the presence of input-output dead times, i.e. propagation delays, in the data, which can increase model order and introduce artifacts like pre-ringing. This paper introduces an improved technique for the extraction of dead times, by formulating a linear program to separate input and output dead times from the data. Additionally, the paper presents an adaptive randomized ERA pipeline that leverages recent advances in numerical linear algebra to reduce computational complexity and enabling scalable model reduction. Benchmarking on large-scale datasets of measured room impulse responses demonstrates that the propsed dead time extraction scheme yields more accurate and efficient reduced order models compared to previous approaches. The implementation is made available as open-source Python code, facilitating reproducibility and further research.

2.2
Discrete-time
lti

A discrete-time system

The dynamical action of a

\mathbf{y}(k)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h(t-k)\mathbf{u}(t),

(1)

where

denotes the system’s

where

Let

satisfying

\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}^{p\times m}(\mathbb{D})}=\biggl(\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left\lVert\mathbf{G}(e^{\imath\omega})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega\biggr)^{1/2}<\infty

(2)

The space

Throughout this document, we will use

2.5
Discrete-time dead time systems

Many dynamical systems exhibit dead times in their dynamical behaviour. In a
siso
setting, this means that there exists some index
T>0
for which
\mathbf{y}(t)=0
for
t<T
for any
\mathbf{u}\in\ell_{2}
. In other words, it takes a minimal time
T
for any action on the input to be observable at the output. By
Eq.

1
, this property is equivalent to
h(t)=0
for
t<T
. The largest such index
T
is called the dead time of the system.

For
mimo
systems, the definition of dead time is more involved, see
[
51
,
52
]
and
[
40
, Ch. 11]
for details. We now introduce a discrete-time adaptation of the definition of dead time systems in
[
51
]
. For input dead times
\boldsymbol{\tau}=[\tau_{1}~\cdots~\tau_{m}]
and output dead times
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\theta_{1}~\cdots~\theta_{p}]
, let

\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}(t-\tau_{1})&\cdots&u_{m}(t-\tau_{m})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\mkern-12.0mu,~\mathbf{y}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}(t+\theta_{1})&\cdots&y_{p}(t+\,\theta_{p})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

denote the delayed input and output signals, respectively. Analogously to
Eq.

3
, the dynamics of a discrete-time dead time system are then characterized by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)

\displaystyle=A_{0}\mathbf{x}(t)+B_{0}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{0}\mathbf{x}(t)+D_{0}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t),

for
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
C_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
and
D_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
. The system
\mathbf{G}_{0}=(A_{0},B_{0},C_{0},D_{0})
models the dead time free dynamics of the system. In the following, we will always assume that
\mathbf{G}_{0}
is minimal and free of dead time. Taking the
\mathcal{Z}
-transform of the above yields the transfer function

\mathbf{G}(z)=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(z)\mathbf{G}_{0}(z)\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(z)=\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(z)\bigl(C_{0}(zI_{n}-A_{0})^{-1}B_{0}+D_{0}\bigr)\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(z),

(12)

where
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(z)=\operatorname{diag}(z^{-\tau_{1}},\,\cdots,\,z^{-\tau_{m}})
and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(z)=\operatorname{diag}(z^{-\theta_{1}},\,\cdots,\,z^{-\theta_{p}})
, reveals that dead times can be modelled multiplicatively in the frequency domain.

It should be noted that a dead time
\delta\in\mathbb{N}
manifests as a rational term

in discrete-time, which means that the input and output dead time operators
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are rational functions of degree
\bar{\tau}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}
and
\bar{\tau}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}
, respectively. In continuous-time, a dead time takes the form of an exponential term
e^{\delta s}
(with Laplace variable
s
) and, in consequence,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are both infinite dimensional. This is a key advantage of discrete-time models because the dead time operators can be directly realized as finite dimensional systems, whereas one has to approximate the exponential terms, e.g. by Padé approximation
[
53
]
, in continuous-time.

The
siso
pure dead time system with dead time
0<\delta\in\mathbb{N}
can be easily formulated in state-space by a canonical realization formula, e.g. controllable canonical form
[
54
]
as

A_{\delta}=\!\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\phantom{\cdots}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&1\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\!\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{\delta\times\delta},~B_{\delta}=\!\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\!\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{\delta\times 1},~C_{\delta}=\!\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\mkern-12.0mu\!\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{1\times\delta},~D_{\delta}=0.

(13)

The
mimo
input dead time operator
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=(A_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\,B_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\,C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\,D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
can be written as a combined parallel system
[
55
]
of the
siso
dead time system
Eq.

13
via

\displaystyle A_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blk\,diag}\left(A_{\tau_{1}},\,\cdots,\,A_{\tau_{m}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{\tau}\times\bar{\tau}},

(14)

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blk\,diag}\left(B_{\tau_{1}},\,\cdots,\,B_{\tau_{m}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{\tau}\times m},

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{blk\,diag}\left(C_{\tau_{1}},\,\cdots,\,C_{\tau_{m}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\bar{\tau}},

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\left(D_{\tau_{1}},\,\cdots,\,D_{\tau_{m}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

where
D_{\tau_{i}}=1
if
\tau_{i}=0
. The output dead time operator
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},C_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is realized analogously. It is easy to verify that

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}A_{\delta}&B_{\delta}\\
\hline\cr C_{\delta}&D_{\delta}\end{array}\right]^{\mathsf{H}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}A_{\delta}&B_{\delta}\\
\hline\cr C_{\delta}&D_{\delta}\end{array}\right]=I_{n+1},

i.e., all above-mentioned dead time systems are unitary. Specifically, it holds

\mathbf{G}_{\delta}(e^{\imath\omega})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}_{\delta}(e^{\imath\omega})=1

which reveals the allpass nature of a dead time transfer function. Furthermore, the Hankel singular values of a dead time are all equal to one. The reader is referred to
[
56
]
for a compilation of properties of unitary systems.

3
Theory and calculation

3.1
Problem statement

As mentioned earlier, the presence of dead times in the measurement data increases the order of the minimal realization and can drastically reduce the quality of reduced order approximations. Any system theoretic model reduction that exploits a decay of the spectrum of the Hankel operator must lead to large errors when applied to pure dead time systems, since their Hankel singular values are all equal to one. These errors manifest drastically in the time domain as so-called pre-ringing, a phase distortion also referred to as
impulse smearing

[
28
,
57
]
. In order to see exactly how an unstructured model
Eq.

3
containing
io
(
io
) dead time is impacted, we expand the system concatenation
Eq.

12
. According to
[

]
, and instating the notation of

, the realization of the concatenated systems is given by

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}A&B\\
\hline\cr C&D\end{array}\right]\!\!=\!\!\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{matrix}A_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&0&0\\
B_{0}C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&A_{0}&0\\
B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}D_{0}C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}C_{0}&A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}B_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\\
B_{0}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\\
B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}D_{0}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}D_{0}C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}&D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}C_{0}&C_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\end{matrix}&D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}D_{0}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\end{array}\right]\!\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{(\bar{\tau}+n+\bar{\theta}+p)\times(\bar{\tau}+n+\bar{\theta}+m)}.

Hence, the McMillan degree of the dead time system

, assuming that the interconnection does not result in a loss of controllability or observability

Yet, if the input and output dead times

\vdots&&\vdots\\
h_{p1}(t+\tau_{1}+\theta_{p})&\cdots&h_{pm}(t+\tau_{m}+\theta_{p})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}.

The proposed approach not only preserves the dead time structure

Given measurements of finite duration input and output trajectories

3.3
Dead time splitting

The problem setting considered in this work is more specific than the

\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
rather than
\boldsymbol{\delta}
. Imposing the delay structure
Eq.

11
implies that
\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathsf{T}}
which is equivalent to the following linear system of equations

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbold{1}_{m}&\cdots&0&I_{m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\mathbbold{1}_{m}&I_{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}=F\mathbf{x}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}\right),

(18)

where
\mathbbold{1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the vector of ones and
\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)=[\delta_{11}~\delta_{21}~\cdots~\delta_{(p-1)m}~\delta_{pm}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{pm}

Similar formulations can be found in
[

. This is a special case because it is the only

and

18

Generally, it cannot be guaranteed that

11

. This leads to the following linear program

with

and

as in

3.4
Numerical implementation

In light of the considerations above, we derive a numerical procedure as follows:
Given
ir

1.

solve the

,

2.

3.

construct a

4.

reapply the dead times to enforce a structured reduced order model

15
.

Since

is sparse,

19

[
70
]
. Therefore, the main numerical challenge is posed by the computation of the
svd
of the Hankel matrix
Eq.

7
because it can become very large for real-world acoustical measurement data. As was demonstrated previously in
[
16
,
32
]
and according to the listed matrix sizes in
Table

1

[

]
by employing CUR decompositions. Later, the use of

(
rsvd
) for

has been investigated in
[
34
]
. Recently, tensor-based decompositions such as the Tucker decomposition were considered in
[
71
, Sec. 6.1.]
.

In this work, we employ randomized
era
from
[
34
]
since it performed remarkably well for the considered benchmarks in the past
[
16
,
32
]
. In the standard formulation of

From an application standpoint, this is not ideal because the computations need to be repeated from scratch whenever the reduced order model does not possess the desired accuracy. As demonstrated in the following section, even with randomization techniques, the computational effort can be substantial for large datasets. Therefore, we reformulate randomized
era
as an adaptive pipeline which can improve the approximation quality by drawing additional samples of the Hankel operator and reuses calculations of previous iterations with a QR-updating scheme.
Additionally, we equip randomized

with a

[

with which the approximation quality of the

Proposed error estimator

The adaptive formulation of randomized

\mathbb{E}\bigl(\left\lVert\smash{\mathcal{H}-U_{r}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\bigr)=\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r),

(20)

where

denotes the expected value, and according to

, it remains to relate
\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)
to the

-error of the

. We can achieve this by following considerations made in

]
. To this end, let

\eta_{k}=\begin{cases}1,&k=0,\\
k,&1\leq k\leq s,\\
2s-k,&s<k<2s,\end{cases}

denote the absolute frequency of elements of the (block-)Hankel matrix. With
Eq.

2
, let

\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}=\biggl(\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\left\lVert h_{k}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}

(21)

denote the time-weighted

. Since

\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}=\!\biggl(\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta_{k}}{s}\left\lVert h_{k}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}\!\!\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\leq\!\biggl(\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\left\lVert h_{k}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}\!\!=\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta},

which means that both norms are equivalent. By

5
and

\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}=\bigl(\left\lVert\mathcal{H}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\lVert h_{0}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}.

, the approximation error can be bounded by

\displaystyle\left\lVert G-G_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\smash{\mathcal{H}-U_{r}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}+\left\lVert\smash{\mathcal{H}_{r}-U_{r}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}},

using the triangle inequality for the last part.

Ideally, we would like to obtain an efficient error estimator that is fast to evaluate. To achieve this, we need to simplify
Eq.

significantly. Firstly, we drop
\left\lVert\smash{\mathcal{H}_{r}-U_{r}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}

2
2

2

Note that this simplification is reasonable, since the approximation

Section

, the approximation error follows the

\left\lVert\smash{\mathcal{H}-U_{r}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
asymptotically in practice.
. Leveraging
Eqs.

and

, yields the following approximation of the expectation of the relative error:

\mathbb{E}\biggl(\,\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}\biggr)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(\left\lVert\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}})}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}\leq\frac{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}.

(23)

Observe that for

,
\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)\rightarrow\left\lVert\mathcal{H}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}
. By assuming that the variance of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}(r)
is negligible and applying a normalization to the right-hand side of

\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_{r}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}}\approx\frac{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}}.

(24)

As desired, it holds

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}}=1

and the estimator does not rely on any computations involving
\mathbf{G}_{r}
. Further,
\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}
needs to be computed only once directly from the data. In conclusion, any desired tolerance
\gamma>0
for the relative error of the reduced order model can be prescribed by simply setting

when computing the

Results and discussion

For each of the benchmark scenarios listed in
Table

. The accuracy of the

In this section, the parameter

in

refers to the model order of the unstructured parts, i.e. the dimension of

Eq.

in the following, we refer to the dimension of

.

Error bounds and estimators

The performance of our proposed error estimator
Eq.

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}(r)=20\operatorname{log}_{10}\biggl(\,\frac{\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LOO}}(r)}{\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2},\eta}}\biggr),

(26)

and the normalized erroneous bound given by

K_{\text{✗}}(r)=10\operatorname{log}_{10}\biggl(\,\frac{\sqrt{r+m+p}\cdot\sigma_{r+1}(\mathcal{H})}{\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}}\biggr).

(28)

As reported in
[

and

5.2
Dead time extraction

We now focus our attention on the performance of the proposed dead time extraction via the solution of the associated
dts
problem
Eq.

19
. So far, the problem of dead time extraction is not well-studied. The only other approach for dead time extraction the authors are of is the removal of the least common dead time which was pursued by the authors previously
[
16
,
32
]
as well as in
[
33
,
76
]
. In this obvious approach, the least common dead time present in all
siso
transmission paths given by

\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{LC}}=\!\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,\,\dots,\,p\}\\
j\in\{1,\,\dots,\,m\}\end{subarray}}\{\delta_{ij}\}

is extracted. In terms of the notation introduced in
Section

2.3
, this entails splitting the unstructured system into a dead-time-rectified system
\mathbf{G}_{0}
and a single pure dead time system
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LC}}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{LC}},\,\dots,\,\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{LC}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\min\{m,p\}}
that is chained either to the input or output of
\mathbf{G}_{0}
depending on its dimension to ensure a minimal representation.

Therefore, we compare the proposed dead time extraction method to the extraction of the least common dead time, as well as omitting the extraction altogether. To facilitate a meaningful comparison, the relative error
Eq.

25
is depicted over the
dof
of the model rather than model order in
Fig.

8
. For a
rom
without dead time extraction, the
dof
are given by

\textsc{dofs}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{ROM}}=(r+p)\cdot(r+m).

(29)

For our method, the
dof
are given by

\textsc{dofs}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{DTS}}=\textsc{dofs}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{ROM}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tau_{j},

(30)

i.e. the number of non-zero entries in
Eq.

15
. For the extraction of the least common dead time, we have

\textsc{dofs}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{LC}}=\textsc{dofs}_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{ROM}}+\min\{m,p\}\cdot\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\textsc{LC}}.

(31)

Our numerical experiments reveal that an extraction of the least common dead time achieves the same relative error with less
dof
compared to an omission of dead time extraction. Further, our proposed
dts
-based dead time extraction method strictly outperforms both other considered methods.

5.3
Computational performance

The overall performance of the proposed adaptive randomized
era
method in
Algorithm

1
is very positive for the considered benchmarks both regarding
rom
accuracy and computational efficiency.
The asymptotic decay of the relative error and the proposed error estimator is mainly dictated by the complexity of dynamics, i.e. the number of (dynamically independent) inputs and outputs, the decay of the
ir
, and the amount of dead time in the data. As outlined in the previous subsection, our proposed dead time extraction scheme can extract most dead times but depending on the scenario, residual dead times can remain.
Considering
Fig.

9(a)
, one can see that the error decay is lower for the scenarios where the source plane spans a larger spatial area, thus a higher amount of residual dead times is present. Furthermore, the addition of a reflection in R2-C1 clearly manifests in a larger error, opposed to A2-C1. Somewhat surprisingly, the error decay is noticeably lower for A1-C1 compared to A2-C1 when, at first sight, both scenarios should be fairly similar after rectification of the dead times. This phenomenon cam be explained by the directivity of the employed measurement loudspeaker
[
32
, Fig. 5]
. For the closer source-receiver-plane distance, a wider range of radiation angles is captured by the measurements, leading to a higher complexity of dynamics that need to be encoded in the
rom
. This effect of source directivity is negligible in the
mird
scenarios because, here, the source speakers are always pointed directly at the receivers. Because the geometric arrangement is not altered across the scenarios, the different error decays in
Fig.

9(b)
are explained entirely by the different reverberation times of the scenarios.

Theoretically, the computational runtime of randomized
era
scales linearithmically (“n-log-n”) with the length of the
ir
and quadratically with the order
r
of the constructed
rom

[
34
, Table 3.1]
. For the considered benchmarks, the runtimes indeed are in
\mathcal{O}(r^{2})
as can be gathered from
Fig.

12
. As the reported runtimes not only include the adaptive
era
routine but also the perpetual a posteriori evaluation of the relative error, this speaks towards the efficiency of our implementation.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents significant advancements in the field of reduced order modelling for discrete-time
lti
systems with input-output dead time based on
ir
measurement data, particularly in acoustical engineering applications. We introduce a novel technique for extracting input and output dead times from measurement data by formulating and solving a linear program, which we refer to as the
dts
problem. This approach enables the construction of more accurate, therefore efficient,
rom
by better compensating for a wider range of propagation delays compared to previous methods.
We further develop an adaptive, randomized
era
pipeline that leverages recent advances in numerical linear algebra, such as a randomized
loo
error estimator and a memory-efficient QR algorithm. This enables scalable model reduction for large-scale datasets. Our software implementation is made available as open-source Python code
[
77
]
, promoting reproducibility and enabling engineers and further research.
The proposed method is benchmarked on high-dimensional datasets, including
miracle

[
78
]
and
mird

[
75
]
, representing the largest application of
era
in terms of input data dimension and constructed model size.
Further, this paper identifies and corrects a typing error in the classical
era
error bound
[
43
]
, providing a more accurate theoretical foundation for error estimation.

The proposed method can be extended to other fields where large-scale, data-driven
mor
is required, such as control systems, signal processing, and multi-physics simulations.
The computational efficiency and robustness of the pipeline could be improved further, possibly by integrating more advanced randomized algorithms or out-of-core computations, to enable the construction of larger
rom
.
Recent development of tensor-based approximation methods offer ways to further exploit structure in the measurement data. Both a comparison to direct low-rank tensor modelling methods
[
76
]
but also the incorporation of low-rank tensor approximations directly into the
era
pipeline as done in
[
71
]
could be avenues of future research. A
dts
-based dead time extraction might also prove beneficial in both approaches.
Even though we show that the proposed randomized error estimator performs well in our applications, it is only heuristic and lacks a thorough mathematical analysis. Recent work
[
79
,
80
]
demonstrates that the classical results on error analysis for
era
are worth revisiting and offer new approaches.

Adaptivity

The

Power iterations

In previous numerical experiments, we observed that power iterations (or subspace iterations) greatly improve the accuracy of
rsvd

[
16
,
32
]
. Hence,
Algorithm

2
includes power iterations (line
5

loo

Communication-avoiding orthogonalization

During numerical tests, it became apparent that the perpetual orthogonalization of the random samples is heavily constrained by memory bandwidth for larger model orders, rendering the default (modified) Gram-Schmidt algorithm in pyMOR ineffective. Since access to individual matrix elements is not possible in pyMOR’s abstract vector array interface, a so-called communication-avoiding
triangular orthogonalization
algorithm based on the shifted CholeskyQR algorithm
[
42
]
was implemented instead. To enable our proposed adaptive pipeline, an updating scheme was developed based on
Proposition

A.1
. The orthogonalization step with the proposed procedure was up to
300
times faster than Gram-Schmidt for the larger benchmarks.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
R\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
be a QR decomposition
QR=Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and let
R_{b}
be given by the Cholesky factorization

R_{b}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{b}=(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})=Y_{b}^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b}-Y_{b}^{\mathsf{T}}QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b}

for some
Y_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times b}
. Then,

\tilde{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}Q&(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})R_{b}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\tilde{R}=\begin{bmatrix}R&Q^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b}\\
0&R_{b}\end{bmatrix}

is a QR decomposition of
\tilde{Y}=[Y\quad Y_{b}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(r+b)}
.

Proof.

Firstly, we have

\tilde{Q}\tilde{R}=\begin{bmatrix}QR&QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b}+(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})R_{b}^{-1}R_{b}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Y&Y_{b}\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{Y}.

Secondly,
\tilde{R}
is upper triangular and
\tilde{Q}
is orthogonal as can be verified by

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{Q}\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\begin{bmatrix}Q^{\mathsf{T}}Q&Q^{\mathsf{T}}(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})R_{b}^{-1}\\
R_{b}^{-\mathsf{T}}\!(Y_{b}^{\mathsf{T}}-Y_{b}^{\mathsf{T}}QQ^{\mathsf{T}})Q&R_{b}^{-\mathsf{T}}\!(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}\!(Y_{b}-QQ^{\mathsf{T}}Y_{b})R_{b}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=I_{r+b}.

∎
