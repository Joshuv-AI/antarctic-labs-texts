Strata-Sword: A Hierarchical Safety Evaluation towards LLMs based on Reasoning Complexity of Jailbreak Instructions
Shiji Zhao, Ranjie Duan, Jiexi Liu, Xiaojun Jia, Fengxiang Wang, Cheng Wei, Ruoxi Cheng, Yong Xie, Chang Liu, Qing Guo, Jialing Tao, Hui Xue, Xingxing Wei

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have gained widespread recognition for their superior comprehension and have been deployed across numerous domains. Building on Chain-of-Thought (CoT) ideology, Large Reasoning models (LRMs) further exhibit strong reasoning skills, enabling them to infer user intent more accurately and respond appropriately. However, both LLMs and LRMs face the potential safety risks under jailbreak attacks, which raise concerns about their safety capabilities. Current safety evaluation methods often focus on the content dimensions, or simply aggregate different attack methods, lacking consideration of the complexity. In fact, instructions of different complexity can reflect the different safety capabilities of the model: simple instructions can reflect the basic values of the model, while complex instructions can reflect the model’s ability to deal with deeper safety risks. Therefore, a comprehensive benchmark needs to be established to evaluate the safety performance of the model in the face of instructions of varying complexity, which can provide a better understanding of the safety boundaries of the LLMs. Thus, this paper first quantifies ”Reasoning Complexity” as an evaluable safety dimension and categorizes 15 jailbreak attack methods into three different levels according to the reasoning complexity, establishing a hierarchical Chinese-English jailbreak safety benchmark for systematically evaluating the safety performance of LLMs. Meanwhile, to fully utilize unique language characteristics, we first propose some Chinese jailbreak attack methods, including the Chinese Character Disassembly attack, Lantern Riddle attack, and Acrostic Poem attack. A series of experiments indicate that current LLMs and LRMs show different safety boundaries under different reasoning complexity, which provides a new perspective to develop safer LLMs and LRMs. This paper contains model-generated content that might be offensive.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs)
(

;
Liu et al. 2024a
;

However, the same advancements also amplify potential safety concerns: adversaries can exploit these capabilities through jailbreak prompts, which are the well-designed user instructions used to bypass safety alignment and generate harmful contents
(

;

;

;

;

;

)
.

The Illustration of Strata-Sword

In particular, large reasoning models (LRMs), built upon the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paradigm
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, extend LLMs with enhanced reasoning ability by generating intermediate steps. While such capabilities generally improve task performance and strengthen safety alignment, they also expose the model to jailbreak prompt attacks. Specifically, when instructions exhibit high linguistic or logical complexity, they may obscure the safety ability of LRMs to detect harmful intent, increasing the risk of generating malicious content. This vulnerability likely stems from an over-reliance on reasoning coherence, which can lead the model to faithfully execute malicious requests embedded within complex prompts, thereby breaching its safety boundaries in real-world applications
(
Ying et al. 2025
)
. Hence, it is necessary to systematically judge the safety ability of both the LLMs and LRMs in more diverse dimensions.

Existing safety evaluations have examined LLM robustness from various perspectives, such as refusal rate, harmful content detection, and adversarial prompt resistance
(
Röttger et al. 2023
;
Zou et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Souly et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Qiu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Ying et al. 2025
)
. However, they largely overlook a critical dimension: the reasoning complexity of jailbreak instructions. In real-world adversarial scenarios, harmful prompts are rarely uniform, ranging from direct and explicit harmful requests to deeply obfuscated instructions that conceal malicious intent behind layers of linguistic ambiguity, multi-step logical dependencies, or high-overhead computational tasks. The model’s safety performance on simple instructions reflects whether its underlying values are aligned with human preferences. In contrast, its performance on complex instructions reveals whether this safety alignment is merely superficial, as these prompts can be intentionally crafted to exploit the model’s reasoning process, bypassing surface-level safety filters while maintaining semantic plausibility. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of LLMs across different levels of instruction complexity. This will help to systematically understand the safety capabilities of LLMs and provide guidance for developers to adopt more robust AI safety alignment strategies.

To address the above problem, this paper introduces
Strata-Sword
, a multi-language hierarchical jailbreak benchmark organized by reasoning complexity. Guided by three key aspects of reasoning complexity: logical depth, linguistic ambiguity, and task overhead, we collect 15 jailbreak attack methods and categorize them into three levels: Basic Instruction (Level 1), Simple Reasoning (Level 2), and Complex Reasoning (Level 3). Level 1 attacks directly expose harmful intent; Level 2 attacks are slight modifications of original instructions that require simple reasoning to infer the harmful intent; and Level 3 attacks involve multiple intent modifications or intrinsically complex techniques, requiring models to carefully deconstruct the jailbreak instructions through complex reasoning.

We also account for language-specific characteristics by tailoring attack methods to Chinese and English when constructing Strata-Sword. In particular, we introduce Chinese-specific Level 3 methods such as acrostic poems, lantern riddles, and Chinese character-disassembly attacks. Strata-Sword helps reveal the differing safety boundaries of various LLMs and LRMs when confronted with hierarchical instruction attacks: some models lack basic human-aligned safety preferences, while others show vulnerabilities under more complex reasoning attacks. In short, our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We systematically define the “Reasoning Complexity” from safety dimension and divide harmful jailbreak attacks into three different levels according to three key elements of reasoning complexity.

•

We construct a hierarchical Chinese-English safety evaluation benchmark named Strata-Sword based on the reasoning complexity of jailbreak instructions. Strata-Sword is applied to evaluate the safety ability of LLMs and LRMs under different levels of reasoning complexity.

•

A systematic experimental results shows that different LLMs and LRMs have different safety boundaries when facing hierarchical jailbreak attacks. The results provide a new evaluation dimension for re-examining safety alignment from the perspective of AI Red Teaming.

2
Reasoning Complexity of Jailbreak Instructions

When processing input instructions, LLMs must first comprehend the instructions and then provide an appropriate response. Consequently, when an input instruction explicitly reveals the user’s intention, e.g., “
How to make a bomb
”, the intention can be easily identified without complex reasoning, and the response of LLMs can directly reflect the basic preference.
However, when the input instruction does not directly expose the user’s intention, such as “
steps to make the following chemical reaction products:
C_{7}H_{5}N_{3}O_{6}
”: LLM should know that “
C_{7}H_{5}N_{3}O_{6}
” is the main ingredient for making TNT bomb, and then notice that making bomb is harmful, which requires complex reasoning based on professional domain knowledge to discern the user’s intention, therefore, under threats posed by harmful instructions of varying reasoning complexities, the model’s safety capabilities may exhibit obvious differences. To better understand the safety boundaries, it is necessary to comprehensively evaluate the large language models at different reasoning complexities.

Thus, we perform a disentangled analysis of the reasoning complexity of instructions from a general perspective. For a typical instruction, its reasoning complexity generally consists of three key elements: Logical Depth, Linguistic Ambiguity, and Task Overhead:

•

Logical Depth
: Instructions require multi-step, conditional, or causal reasoning to understand or fulfill their intention. The longer and more structured the reasoning chain, the higher the complexity.

•

Linguistic Ambiguity
: Instructions use metaphors, vague references, implicit requests, or syntactic complexity, making the real intention difficult to extract without pragmatic or contextual inference.

•

Task Overhead
: Apart from the real intention itself, actual instructions involve additional complex tasks, such as programming, mathematical derivation, requiring more computational overhead to solve the corresponding tasks and further extract the real intention.

Based on these fundamental elements of reasoning complexity, we can roughly classify jailbreak attack methods into three different levels
Level 1 (Basic Instruction)
,
Level 2 (Simple Reasoning)
, and
Level 3 (Complex Reasoning)
:

•

Level 1 (Basic Instruction)
: reasoning complexity of this type of attack is extremely low. For the
logical depth
, L1 attacks directly contain harmful intent, the statement expression is straightforward without
linguistic ambiguity
, and the
task overhead
is low. The typical jailbreak attacks are Harmbench
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
, and Advbench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
.

•

Level 2 (Simple Reasoning)
: reasoning complexity is simple. For the
logical depth
, L2 attacks contain relatively simple logical depth (1-2 steps), e.g., DoAnythingNow
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
, or Contradictory. For the
linguistic ambiguity
, the semantics exhibit partial ambiguity, e.g., Text Shuffle Attack
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
. For the
task overhead
may exist but relatively simple, e.g., Simple ASCII Drawing. When a type of attack meets any of these conditions, we attribute it to level 2.

•

Level 3 (Complex Reasoning)
: reasoning complexity is simple. For the
logical depth
, L3 attacks contain complex logical depth (more than 3 steps). For the
linguistic ambiguity
, the semantics exhibit complex ambiguity. The
task overhead
may refer to very complex tasks, even involving professional field knowledge. When a type of attack meets any of these conditions, or meets the two different factors in level2, we attribute it to level 3. And the typical jailbreak attacks are Multi-Round Dialogue Attack
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
and Code Attack
(
Kang et al. 2024
)
.

After classifying different attacks into different levels, we can evaluate the safety boundaries of large language models at different levels of reasoning complexity. When the model cannot defend against even level 1 attacks, the large language model may not be in line with human values and may not even perceive the most basic safety risks. When the model can defend against level 1 attacks, but performs poorly when facing level 2 attacks, the model may have passed the basic safety alignment, but cannot defend against jailbreak attacks that require simple reasoning. This shows that the model’s safety alignment is only superficial. When the model can defend against level 1 and level 2, but cannot defend against level 3, this shows that the model has preliminary safety capabilities in different scenarios, but the model is slightly insufficient to handle more complex safety risks. If the model performs well under three different levels of risk attacks, it means that the model has sufficient risk response capabilities in real and complex application scenarios. Therefore, evaluating the capabilities of these three levels separately can help developers to have a more comprehensive understanding of the safety levels.

3
Strata-Sword: A Chinese-English Hierarchical Benchmark for Safety Evaluation

Based on the criterion of reasoning complexity outlined in Section
2
, we construct a hierarchical safety evaluation named Strata-Sword. Different Chinese and English jailbreak instructions are categorized into different levels of complexity.

Specifically, we randomly select 100 basic harmful instructions from AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
as our Level 1 English basic jailbreak instruction set and translated them into Chinese to create our Level 1 Chinese basic jailbreak instruction set. Based on those basic instructions, we construct Level 2 and Level 3 jailbreak attack instructions. For Level 2, The jailbreak attacks only involve in Simple Reasoning complexity. We choose six types of Chinese and English jailbreak attack methods. Specifically, Level 2 attack includes ASCII drawing
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
, Contradictory Tone, Text Shuffle
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, Template Embedding, Long Text
Anil et al. 2024
, and Opposing Responses, with 100 jailbreak instructions each for Chinese and English.

For Level 3, we take into account the cultural characteristics of each language. For example, English is more phonetic and is more suitable for symbolic attacks, such as Code attacks
(
Kang et al. 2024
)
, while Chinese is pictographic and its radicals have unique meanings when separated; therefore, we design a Chinese character disassembly attack based on this characteristic. In addition, we utilize some linguistic features of Chinese culture and design culturally specific jailbreak attack methods, such as acrostic attacks and lantern riddle guessing attacks. Finally, we choose seven types of Chinese and English jailbreak attack methods. Level 3 includes CodeAttack (English only)
(
Kang et al. 2024
)
, DrAttack (English only)
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
, Character Disassembly Attack (Chinese only), Acrostic Poem Attack (Chinese only), Lantern Riddle Attack (Chinese only), Script + Template Embedding Attack, Multi-turn Dialogue Attack
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, and Text Shuffle + Template Embedding Attack. Level 3 contains 100 Chinese and 200 English jailbreak instructions; details can be found in Table
1
. The harmful instructions include the following risk types: legal violations, ethical issues, bias and discrimination.

4.2
Evaluation Criteria

Following the criteria in Oyster, LLMs are utilized as our risk assessment evaluators: we categorize different questions into various safety risk categories, each corresponding to distinct safety response levels. The evaluation responses are divided into three tiers: “Safe”, and “Unsafe”. If the evaluation model responds with “Safe”, the response is considered safe. If the evaluation model responds with “Unsafe”, the response is deemed unsafe. As for the judge model, we apply QWQ-32B as our default setting. Here we apply the attack success rate (ASR) as our evaluation metric, representing the rate of unsafe responses in the total responses. For the convenience of testing, it should be mentioned that all the models are evaluated in the black-box setting for Strata-Sword and the jailbreak attack instructions are fixed. We note that the LLM-based judge can yield inconsistent outputs that stray from the required format. To address this, whenever a response fails to match the format, we re-query the model up to five additional times. If the output remains non-conforming, the trial is discarded and excluded from the reported attack success rate.

4.3
Performance of Strata-Sword

Here we present the performance of our Strata-Sword towards 23 different LLMs on Table
2
. Based on these results, our Strata-Sword effectively diagnoses an LLM’s safety capabilities under varying reasoning complexities, which can provide an overall judgment for LLM developers.

Specifically, we find that for most LLMs, the attack success rate increases with attack level in both Chinese and English. For example, the ASRs for Open-Source Large Language Models across Levels 1–3 are: 23.83%, 30.64%, and 64.42% (English); 28.33%, 40.67%, and 70.13% (Chinese), respectively. The results show that most LLMs maintain human-aligned safety preferences against basic jailbreak instructions, but as the reasoning complexity of the attacks increases to Simple Reasoning (L2) and complex Reasoning (L3), both open-source and closed-source LLMs exhibit progressively more serious safety vulnerabilities.

Insight 1:

While most current LLMs can effectively defend against basic jailbreak instructions, they remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks with simple and complex reasoning.

From a linguistic perspective, our analysis reveals that LLMs exhibit distinct safety profiles for Chinese and English. For instance, the Llama, Vicuna, and Mistral series demonstrate stronger safety in English, whereas the Qwen3 and Oyster series excel in Chinese. Furthermore, we observe similar linguistic safety disparities in closed-source models, such as Gemini-2.5-Pro.

More interestingly, a comparison between the base Qwen3-8B and its fine-tuned version, DeepSeek-R1-0528, highlights a nuanced issue. While the fine-tuned model shows enhanced safety for the English jailbreak instructions (from 27.71% to 16%), the Attack Success Rate increases obviously for the Chinese ones (from 19.67% to 24%). We reasonably infer that the fine-tuning dataset was heavily weighted towards English content, which bolsters its safety capabilities, while the corresponding Chinese data was less comprehensive, ultimately leading to a pronounced safety imbalance between the two languages.
This result underscores a broader concern regarding the imbalanced distribution of languages in the training data of even closed-source LLMs.

Insight 2:

Cross-lingual characteristic needs to be taken into account during safety alignment.

The results point to two obvious patterns: a scaling law within model series and a temporal trend in safety performance. Regarding scaling, models from the same family (e.g., Llama, DeepSeek) consistently show improved safety with increased model sizes. Specifically, Vicuna-13B has a low attack success rate compared with Vicuna-7B (42.61% vs 57.66% in English, 53.25% vs 66.67% in Chinese). Meanwhile, DeepSeek-R1-14B also exposes fewer vulnerabilities than DeepSeek-R1-14B (33.95% vs 48.69% in English, 34.21% vs 51.07% in Chinese). These results show that more parameters can effectively improve the safety capabilities of the LLMs.

Regarding the temporal trend, there has been a marked improvement in safety over time. Initially, early open-source LLMs (e.g., Vicuna, Mistral) focus on matching the general performance of commercial LLMs, often leaving safety capabilities underdeveloped. However, the current open-source LRMs, such as Llama3, Stair, and Oyster, are now closing the safety gap, achieving competitive robustness compared with closed-source LLMs. This progress highlights the shift of safety alignment from a fringe concern to a key element.

The safety performance of LLMs exhibits clear scaling law and temporal trends.

4.4
Performance of Different Types of Attacks

This section analyzes the performance of individual attack methods within Strata-Sword, with the average Attack Success Rate (ASR) for each detailed in Figure
2
. While higher-level attacks are generally more effective, we observe obvious performance variance within each level, indicating that not all attacks of a given complexity are equally harmful.

Specifically, within Level 2, the Opposing Response and Template attacks are notable outliers, achieving obviously higher ASRs. The success of the Opposing Response attack may stem from its dual-task nature, which could divert the model’s cognitive resources away from its safety alignment. As for the Template attack, its potency likely arises from its novelty: the template we applied is an out-of-distribution (OOD) example not found in common public jailbreak datasets like “DoAnythingNoW”
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
, making it a more challenging prompt for current LLMs.

In Level 3, CodeAttack shows the most effective attack performance. Its effectiveness is likely because discerning the malicious intent requires the model to first parse and reason about the code, a task of high reasoning complexity. Conversely, the Multi-turn attack shows relatively low efficacy, and we attribute this to our experimental setup: for efficiency, all multi-turn contexts are concatenated into a single input. This approach may not fully replicate the dynamics of a sequential, interactive dialogue, potentially diluting the attack’s impact. Meanwhile, the three Chinese attack methods we proposed: Lantern Riddle, Acrostic Poem, and Chinese Acrostic Poem Attack, show a pretty attack success rate, showing that the current LLMs are very vulnerable to attacks with language characteristics.

This, in turn, enables them to more clearly perceive the harmful intentions contained within the instructions and generate appropriate safe responses. In this paper, we decoupled reasoning complexity along three core dimensions: Logical Depth, Linguistic Ambiguity, and Task Overhead. Then we introduce Strata-Sword, a hierarchical, bilingual (Chinese-English) evaluation benchmark. We applied fifteen attack methods, categorizing them into these levels while adapting them to the unique characteristics of each language. A key contribution is our introduction of novel attacks that exploit Chinese-specific linguistic features, including Chinese Character Disassembly, Lantern Riddle, and Acrostic Poem Attack. Our evaluation of mainstream open-source and closed-source LLMs using Strata-Sword reveals that models exhibit markedly different safety boundaries when faced with varying levels of reasoning complexity. These findings provide a clear guidance for developers to implement targeted improvement strategies that address vulnerabilities specific to each complexity level. We believe that Strata-Sword offers crucial new insights and a valuable tool for advancing the safety and robustness of large language models.
