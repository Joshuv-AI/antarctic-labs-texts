Improved Upper Bounds for Numerical Radius Inequalities of Operator Matrices and Their Applications
M.H.M. Rashid

Abstract
This study presents new upper bounds for the numerical radii of operator matrices, with a focus on n × n \displaystyle n\times n and 2 × 2 \displaystyle 2\times 2 block matrices acting on Hilbert space direct sums. By employing techniques such as the Hölder-McCarthy inequality, Jensen’s inequality, Bohr’s inequality, and extensions of the Buzano inequality, we derive improved estimates that refine classical results by Kittaneh, El-Haddad, Dragomir, Buzano, and others. Our main contributions include bounds for general operator matrices, specific results for off-diagonal matrices, and inequalities for sums and products of operators. Through detailed comparisons and special cases, we demonstrate that our results enhance existing numerical radius inequalities. A key feature of our work is the use of parameter-dependent refinements with constants derived from extended Buzano-type inequalities. The applications of these results span quantum mechanics, integro-differential equations, and fractional calculus, providing sharper tools for stability analysis and numerical approximation in mathematical physics.

Introduction

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the
\displaystyle C^{*}
-algebra of all bounded linear operators
on the complex Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}
. An operator
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
positive
if
\displaystyle\left<Ax,x\right>\geq 0
holds for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{H}

and we write
\displaystyle A\geq 0
.
For
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the numerical radius
\displaystyle\omega(\cdot)
and the
usual operator norm
\displaystyle\left\|\cdot\right\|
are, respectively, defined by

\omega(A)=\sup_{\left\|x\right\|=1}\left|\left<Ax,x\right>\right|\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\left\|A\right\|=\sup_{\left\|x\right\|=1}\left\|Ax\right\|.

It is clear that
\displaystyle\omega(\cdot)
defines a norm on
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Moreover, it is known that
\displaystyle\omega(\cdot)
is
equivalent to the usual operator norm
\displaystyle\left\|\cdot\right\|
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and with the following two
sided inequality

\frac{1}{2}\left\|A\right\|\leq\omega(A)\leq\left\|A\right\|\,\,\mbox{for all $\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$}.

(1)

Consequently, if
\displaystyle A^{2}=0
, then

\omega(A)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|A\right\|.

(2)

An important property for the numerical radius is the power inequality, which
says that

\omega(A^{n})\leq\omega^{n}(A)\,\,\mbox{for all $\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$}.

For basic properties of the numerical radius, we refer to
Halmos
.

In
Kittaneh
, Kittaneh provided refinements of the bounds in (
1
) by showing that

\frac{1}{4}\left\||A|^{2}+|A^{*}|^{2}\right\|\leq\omega^{2}(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\||A|^{2}+|A^{*}|^{2}\right\|\,\,\mbox{for all $\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$},

(3)

where
\displaystyle|A|=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
is the absolute value of
\displaystyle A
.
In
Had-Kit
, El-Haddad and Kittaneh provided a generalization for the second inequality
in (
3
) by showing that

\omega^{2r}(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\||A|^{2r}+|A^{*}|^{2r}\right\|\,\,\mbox{for all $\displaystyle r\geq 1$ and $\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$}.

(4)

In
Drag4
, Dragomir presented an important upper bound for the numerical radii of
products of two operators by showing that if
\displaystyle T,S\in B(H)
and
\displaystyle r\geq 1
, then

\omega^{r}(S^{*}T)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\||T|^{2r}+|S|^{2r}\right\|.

(5)

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{H}_{n}
be Hilbert spaces. If
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{j}
and
\displaystyle S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, then the operator

\displaystyle S
can be represented as an
\displaystyle n\times n
operator matrix, i.e.,
\displaystyle S=[S_{ij}]_{n\times n}
with
\displaystyle S_{ij}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{H}_{i})
,
the space of all bounded linear operators from
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{j}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{i}
. Operator matrices provide a useful
tool for studying Hilbert space operators, which have been extensively studied in the literature.
The block-norm matrix
\displaystyle\hat{S}
associated with an operator matrix
\displaystyle S=[S_{ij}]_{n\times n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{S}=\left[\left\|S_{ij}\right\|\right]_{n\times n}
which is an
\displaystyle n\times n
non-negative matrix. Hou et al.
Hou
established some estimate
for the numerical radii, operator norm and spectral radii of an
\displaystyle n\times n
operator matrix
\displaystyle S=\left[S_{ij}\right]
.
In particular, they showed that if
\displaystyle S=[S_{ij}]_{n\times n}
is an operator matrix and
\displaystyle\hat{S}=\left[\left\|S_{ij}\right\|\right]_{n\times n}
is
its block-norm matrix, then

\omega(S)\leq\omega(\hat{S}),\quad\left\|S\right\|\leq\left\|\hat{S}\right\|,\quad\mbox{and}\,\,r(S)\leq r(\hat{S}),

(6)

where
\displaystyle r(S)
denotes the spectral radius of
\displaystyle S
. Bani-Domi and Kittaneh
Dom-kit1
presented a new
numerical radius inequality, which is an improvement of the inequality (
6
). They proved that
if
\displaystyle S=[S_{ij}]
is an operator matrix in
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{j}\right)
, then

\omega(S)\leq\omega\left([s_{ij}]\right),\,\,\mbox{where}\,s_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|S_{ii}\right\|+\left\|S_{ii}^{2}\right\|^{1/2}\right),&\hbox{if $\displaystyle i=j$;}\\
\left\|S_{ij}\right\|,&\hbox{if $\displaystyle i\neq j$.}\end{array}\right.

(7)

Further, Abu-Omar and Kittaneh
AF
improved the inequality (
7
) which states that if
\displaystyle T=[T_{ij}]

is an
\displaystyle n\times n
operator matrix with
\displaystyle T_{ij}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{H}_{i})
, then

\omega(T)\leq\omega\left([t_{ij}]\right),\,\,\mbox{where}\,t_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega(T_{ij}),&\hbox{if $\displaystyle i=j$;}\\
\left\|T_{ij}\right\|,&\hbox{if $\displaystyle i\neq j$.}\end{array}\right.

(8)

In
Buz
, Buzano obtained the following extension of the celebrated Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in a real
or complex inner product space
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\left|\left<x,z\right>\left<z,y\right>\right|\leq\frac{\left\|z\right\|^{2}}{2}\left(\left\|x\right\|\left\|y\right\|+\left|\left<x,y\right>\right|\right)\,\,\left(x,y,z\in\mathcal{H}\right).

(9)

When
\displaystyle x=y
this inequality becomes the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\left|\left<x,y\right>\right|^{2}\leq\left\|x\right\|^{2}\left\|y\right\|^{2}.

(10)

Recently, Khosravi et al.
KDM
proved that if
\displaystyle x,y,z\in\mathcal{H}
and
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, then

\left|\left<x,z\right>\left<z,y\right>\right|\leq\frac{\left\|z\right\|^{2}}{\left|\alpha\right|}\left(\max\left\{1,\left|\alpha-1\right|\right\}\left\|x\right\|\left\|y\right\|+\left|\left<x,y\right>\right|\right).

(11)

Remark 1.1
.

When
\displaystyle\alpha=2
the inequality (
11
) becomes the Buzano inequality (
9
).

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish new upper and lower bounds for the numerical radius of
\displaystyle 2\times 2
operator matrices, improving upon existing results in the literature. Our approach builds on several key lemmas that we present first.

2.1
Preliminary Lemmas

We begin with a result about the numerical radius of non-negative matrices:

Lemma 2.1
.

(
Horn
, p. 44)

Let
\displaystyle A=[a_{ij}]\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be such that
\displaystyle a_{ij}\geq 0
for all
\displaystyle i,j=1,2,\ldots,n
. Then

\omega(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}r([a_{ij}+a_{ji}]).

The following lemma is the well-known Hölder-McCarthy inequality:

Lemma 2.2
.

kit1
[Hölder-McCarthy inequality]
Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\displaystyle A\geq 0
and let
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{H}
be any unit vector. Then:

(i)

\displaystyle\langle Ax,x\rangle^{r}\leq\langle A^{r}x,x\rangle
for
\displaystyle r\geq 1
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\langle A^{r}x,x\rangle\leq\langle Ax,x\rangle^{r}
for
\displaystyle 0<r\leq 1
.

Next, we state a version of Jensen’s inequality for power functions:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle a,b>0
and
\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Then

a^{\alpha}b^{1-\alpha}\leq\alpha a+(1-\alpha)b\leq\left(\alpha a^{r}+(1-\alpha)b^{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\quad\text{for}\quad r\geq 1.

(12)

The following result concerns convex functions of positive operators:

Lemma 2.4
.

Silva

Let
\displaystyle\psi
be a non-negative convex function on
\displaystyle[0,\infty)
and
\displaystyle T,S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be positive operators. Then

\left\|\psi\left(\frac{T+S}{2}\right)\right\|\leq\left\|\frac{\psi(T)+\psi(S)}{2}\right\|.

In particular,

\|(T+S)^{r}\|\leq 2^{r-1}\|T^{r}+S^{r}\|\quad\text{for all }r\geq 1.

We also need Bohr’s inequality (see
Bha
;
Bohr
;
AA
):

Lemma 2.5

(Bohr’s inequality)
.

Let
\displaystyle a_{i}
,
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n
be positive real numbers. Then

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)^{r}\leq n^{r-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{r}\quad\text{for }r\geq 1.

(13)

The generalized mixed Schwartz inequality will be particularly useful:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\displaystyle T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathcal{H}
be any vectors.

(i)

If
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\geq 0
with
\displaystyle\alpha+\beta=1
, then
\displaystyle|\langle Tx,y\rangle|^{2}\leq\langle|T|^{2\alpha}x,x\rangle\langle|T|^{2\beta}y,y\rangle
.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle f,g
are non-negative continuous functions on
\displaystyle[0,\infty)
satisfying
\displaystyle f(t)g(t)=t
(
\displaystyle t\geq 0
), then
\displaystyle|\langle Tx,y\rangle|\leq\|f(|T|)x\|\|g(|T^{*}|)y\|
.

The next lemma provides fundamental properties of numerical radii for operator matrices:

Lemma 2.7
.

HK

Let
\displaystyle T,S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then:

(a)

\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}\right)=\max\{\omega(T),\omega(S)\}
.

(b)

\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&T\\
S&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&S\\
T&0\end{bmatrix}\right)
.

(c)

\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}T&S\\
S&T\end{bmatrix}\right)=\max\{\omega(T-S),\omega(T+S)\}
.
In particular,
\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&S\\
S&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\omega(S)
.

Finally, we state a result about numerical radii of operator matrices:

Lemma 2.8
.

(
AF
, Theorem 2)

Let
\displaystyle S=[S_{ij}]
be an
\displaystyle n\times n
operator matrix with
\displaystyle S_{ij}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{H}_{i})
,
\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then

\omega(S)\leq\omega([s_{ij}]),\quad\text{where }s_{ij}=\begin{cases}\omega(S_{ij}),&\text{if }i=j,\\
\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}O&S_{ij}\\
S_{ji}&O\end{bmatrix}\right),&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

3
Results

The upcoming outcome pertains to the numerical radius inequality of an
\displaystyle n\times n
operator matrix featuring a solitary non-zero row.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle S_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\mathcal{H}_{1})
, (
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq n
). Then

\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}&\cdots&S_{n}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega(S_{1})+\left(\omega(S_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left\|S_{j}\right\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
and the inequality (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}&\cdots&S_{n}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)\leq\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}\omega(S_{1})&\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{2}\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)&\cdots&\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{n}\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
S_{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
S_{n}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}\omega(S_{1})&\frac{1}{2}\left\|S_{2}\right\|&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}\left\|S_{n}\right\|\\
\frac{1}{2}\left\|S_{2}\right\|&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{2}\left\|S_{n}\right\|&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r\left(\begin{bmatrix}2\omega(S_{1})&\left\|S_{2}\right\|&\cdots&\left\|S_{n}\right\|\\
\left\|S_{2}\right\|&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\left\|S_{n}\right\|&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega(S_{1})+\left(\omega(S_{1})+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left\|S_{j}\right\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=[S_{ij}]
be an
\displaystyle n\times n

\displaystyle S_{ij}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{i})

\left\|S_{ij}\right\|^{s},&\hbox{if $\displaystyle i\neq j$.}\end{array}\right.

(14)

for all
\displaystyle s\geq 1

Proof.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle n^{2s-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<f^{2}(|S_{ii}|)x_{i},x_{i}\right>^{s/2}\left<g^{2}(|S_{ii}^{*}|)x_{i},x_{i}\right>^{s/2}+n^{2s-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\

\displaystyle\displaystyle n^{2s-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<f^{2s}(|S_{ii}|)x_{i},x_{i}\right>^{1/2}\left<g^{2s}(|S_{ii}^{*}|)x_{i},x_{i}\right>^{1/2}{\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2s-2}}+n^{2s-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\mbox{by Lemma \ref{Holder}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{n^{2s-2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\left(f^{2s}(|S_{ii}|)+g^{2s}(|S_{ii}^{*}|)\right)x_{i},x_{i}\right>{\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2s-2}}+n^{2s-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\left\|S_{ij}\right\|^{s}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{s}\left\|x_{j}\right\|^{s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{n^{2s-2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega\left(f^{2s}(|S_{ii}|)+g^{2s}(|S_{ii}^{*}|)\right)\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2s}+n^{2s-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\

\left\|x_{2}\right\|^{s}\\
\vdots\\
\left\|x_{n}\right\|^{s}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}$}.

Since
\displaystyle\left\|z\right\|\leq 1

\left|\left<\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\right>\right|^{s}\leq\omega\left([s_{ij}]\right).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
C&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2})
,
\displaystyle r\geq 1
, and let
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g

be as in Lemma

\omega^{r}(\mathbb{T})\leq 2^{r-2}\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(f^{2r}(|B|)+g^{2r}(|C^{*}|)\right)\omega^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(f^{2r}(|C|)+g^{2r}(|B^{*}|)\right).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}
be any unit vector in
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\left\|x_{1}\right\|^{2}+\left\|x_{2}\right\|^{2}=1

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\left<\mathbb{T}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\right>\right|^{r}=\left|\left<Bx_{2},x_{1}\right>+\left<Cx_{1},x_{2}\right>\right|^{r}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\left[\left|\left<Bx_{2},x_{1}\right>\right|+\left|\left<Cx_{1},x_{2}\right>\right|\right]^{r}\left(\mbox{by the triangle inequality}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2^{r-1}\left[\left|\left<Bx_{2},x_{1}\right>\right|^{r}+\left|\left<Cx_{1},x_{2}\right>\right|^{r}\right]\left(\mbox{by Lemma \ref{Logain1}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2^{r-1}\left[\left<f^{2}(|B|)x_{2},x_{2}\right>^{r/2}\left<g^{2}(|B^{*}|)x_{1},x_{1}\right>^{r/2}+\left<f^{2}(|C|)x_{1},x_{1}\right>^{r/2}\left<g^{2}(|C^{*}|)x_{2},x_{2}\right>^{r/2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\mbox{by Lemma \ref{Lem2.3}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2^{r-1}\left[\left<f^{2r}(|B|)x_{2},x_{2}\right>^{1/2}\left<g^{2r}(|B^{*}|)x_{1},x_{1}\right>^{1/2}+\left<f^{2r}(|C|)x_{1},x_{1}\right>^{1/2}\left<g^{2r}(|C^{*}|)x_{2},x_{2}\right>^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\mbox{by Lemma \ref{Holder}}\right)

Theorem 3.25

Lemma 3.29
.

Let
\displaystyle a,b,e\in\mathcal{H}
with
\displaystyle\left\|e\right\|=1
. Then for all
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
,
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\left<a,e\right>\left<e,b\right>\right|^{2n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k}\lambda_{2}^{n-k}\left\|a\right\|^{2k}\left\|b\right\|^{2k}\left\|a\right\|^{n-k}\left\|b\right\|^{n-k}\left|\left<a,b\right>\right|^{n-k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{n}\left\|a\right\|^{2n}\left\|b\right\|^{2n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k}\lambda_{2}^{n-k}\left\|a\right\|^{2k}\left\|b\right\|^{2k}\left\|a\right\|^{n-k}\left\|b\right\|^{n-k}\left|\left<a,b\right>\right|^{n-k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{n}\left\|a\right\|^{n}\left\|b\right\|^{n}\left|\left<a,b\right>\right|^{n},

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\frac{\beta+(\beta+1)\max\left\{1,\left|\alpha-1\right|^{2}\right\}}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}(\beta+1)}
and

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\frac{1+2(\beta+1)\max\left\{1,\left|\alpha-1\right|\right\}}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}(\beta+1)}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle a,b,e\in\mathcal{H}
with
\displaystyle\|e\|=1
. By Lemma
3.7
(which holds for
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
), we have

|\langle a,e\rangle\langle e,b\rangle|^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|a\|\|b\||\langle a,b\rangle|,

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\frac{\beta+(\beta+1)\max\{1,|\alpha-1|^{2}\}}{|\alpha|^{2}(\beta+1)}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\frac{1+2(\beta+1)\max\{1,|\alpha-1|\}}{|\alpha|^{2}(\beta+1)}
. Since both sides are nonnegative, raising to the
\displaystyle n
-th power preserves the inequality:

|\langle a,e\rangle\langle e,b\rangle|^{2n}\leq\left(\lambda_{1}\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|a\|\|b\||\langle a,b\rangle|\right)^{n}.

Expanding the right-hand side using the binomial theorem, we get

\left(\lambda_{1}\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|a\|\|b\||\langle a,b\rangle|\right)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(\lambda_{1}\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}\right)^{k}\left(\lambda_{2}\|a\|\|b\||\langle a,b\rangle|\right)^{n-k}.

Simplifying each term, we have

\left(\lambda_{1}\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}\right)^{k}=\lambda_{1}^{k}\|a\|^{2k}\|b\|^{2k},\quad\left(\lambda_{2}\|a\|\|b\||\langle a,b\rangle|\right)^{n-k}=\lambda_{2}^{n-k}\|a\|^{n-k}\|b\|^{n-k}|\langle a,b\rangle|^{n-k}.

Combining these, the expression becomes

\left|\left<a,e\right>\left<e,b\right>\right|^{2n}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k}\lambda_{2}^{n-k}\|a\|^{2k}\|b\|^{2k}\|a\|^{n-k}\|b\|^{n-k}|\langle a,b\rangle|^{n-k}.

Thus, the inequality holds as stated.
∎

4
Applications

The numerical radius inequalities developed in this work have broad applications across mathematical physics and engineering. In this section, we demonstrate their utility in quantum mechanics, integro-differential equations, and fractional calculus. The derived bounds serve as essential tools for analyzing operator matrices in these domains, particularly in stability analysis and solution estimation (see
Evans
;
Kato
).

Our approach builds on foundational contributions in the literature. The link between numerical radius inequalities and quantum system stability was first established by
Halmos
and later advanced by
Kittaneh
. Applications to integro-differential equations were introduced in
Drag4
, while extensions to fractional calculus follow the framework of
Had-Kit
. By unifying and extending these perspectives, our new inequalities provide sharper tools for stability analysis and numerical approximation.

The selected applications highlight both the computational advantages and theoretical insights offered by our results. Each example illustrates how these bounds enhance the analysis of stability and approximation in key areas of mathematical physics.

4.1
Application of Numerical Radius Inequalities in Atomic Energy

The study of numerical radius inequalities for operator matrices has significant applications in quantum mechanics and atomic energy research. In particular, the bounds on numerical radii can be used to analyze the stability and energy levels of quantum systems, which are fundamental to understanding atomic behavior and nuclear reactions.

4.1.1

Consider a quantum system described by the Hamiltonian operator
\displaystyle H

\displaystyle 2\times 2
block matrix:

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{11}&H_{12}\\
H_{21}&H_{22}\end{bmatrix},

where
\displaystyle H_{11}
and
\displaystyle H_{22}
represent the self-energy of two subsystems, and
\displaystyle H_{12}
,
\displaystyle H_{21}
represent the interaction between them. The numerical radius
\displaystyle\omega(H)
provides a measure of the system’s energy dispersion and stability.

Problem Statement

We aim to find an upper bound for the numerical radius of
\displaystyle H
to ensure the system remains stable under perturbations. Using Theorem
3.2
from the paper, we can derive such a bound.

Solution

By Theorem 2.10, for
\displaystyle H=[H_{ij}]_{2\times 2}
, the numerical radius satisfies:

\omega^{s}(H)\leq 2^{2s-2}\omega([h_{ij}]),\quad\text{where}\quad h_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\omega\left(f^{2s}(|H_{ii}|)+g^{2s}(|H_{ii}^{*}|)\right),&\text{if }i=j,\\
\|H_{ij}\|^{s},&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

For simplicity, let
\displaystyle s=1
,
\displaystyle f(t)=g(t)=\sqrt{t}
, and assume
\displaystyle H_{12}=H_{21}^{*}
. Then:

\omega(H)\leq\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\omega(|H_{11}|+|H_{11}^{*}|)&\|H_{12}\|\\
\|H_{21}\|&\frac{1}{2}\omega(|H_{22}|+|H_{22}^{*}|)\end{bmatrix}\right).

Numerical Example

Let the subsystems and interactions be defined as:

H_{11}=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{22}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad H_{21}=H_{12}^{*}.

1.

Compute
\displaystyle\omega(H_{11})
and
\displaystyle\omega(H_{22})
:

•

For
\displaystyle H_{11}
, the eigenvalues are 3 and 1, so
\displaystyle\omega(H_{11})=3
.

•

For
\displaystyle H_{22}
, the eigenvalues are 1 and 3, so
\displaystyle\omega(H_{22})=3
.

2.

Compute
\displaystyle\|H_{12}\|
and
\displaystyle\|H_{21}\|
:

•

\displaystyle\|H_{12}\|=\|H_{21}\|=1
.

3.

Construct the matrix
\displaystyle[h_{ij}]
:

[h_{ij}]=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\omega(|H_{11}|+|H_{11}^{*}|)&\|H_{12}\|\\
\|H_{21}\|&\frac{1}{2}\omega(|H_{22}|+|H_{22}^{*}|)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\
1&3\end{bmatrix}.

4.

Compute
\displaystyle\omega([h_{ij}])
:

The eigenvalues of
\displaystyle[h_{ij}]
are 2 and 4, so
\displaystyle\omega([h_{ij}])=4
.

5.

Final bound:

\omega(H)\leq 4.

Interpretation

The numerical radius
\displaystyle\omega(H)\leq 4
provides an upper limit on the energy dispersion of the quantum system. This ensures that the system’s energy levels remain bounded, which is crucial for stability in atomic energy applications.

4.2
Applications in Integro-Differential Equations

Numerical radius inequalities for operator matrices have important applications in the analysis of integro-differential equations, particularly in studying solution stability and convergence properties. These inequalities provide bounds on operator norms that are crucial for estimating solution behaviors.

4.2.1
Application to Volterra Integro-Differential Equations

Consider the Volterra integro-differential equation of the form:

\frac{dy}{dt}=A(t)y(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(t,s)y(s)ds,\quad y(0)=y_{0}

(43)

where
\displaystyle A(t)
is a time-dependent operator and
\displaystyle K(t,s)
is the kernel operator.

4.2.2
Operator Matrix Formulation

We can reformulate this problem using operator matrices. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=L^{2}([0,T])
and define the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
by:

\mathcal{L}=\begin{bmatrix}A&K\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}

where
\displaystyle A
is the multiplication operator by
\displaystyle A(t)
,
\displaystyle K
is the integral operator with kernel
\displaystyle K(t,s)
, and
\displaystyle I
is the identity operator.

4.2.3
Stability Analysis

The numerical radius
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{L})
provides crucial information about the system’s stability. Using Theorem
3.2
, we can estimate:

\omega(\mathcal{L})\leq\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(\omega(A)+\omega(A^{*}))&\|K\|\\
1&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)

4.2.4
Comprehensive Example

Consider the specific case where:

\displaystyle\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-1&t\\
0&-2\end{bmatrix},\quad 0\leq t\leq 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle K(t,s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-(t-s)}&0\\
0&e^{-2(t-s)}\end{bmatrix}

4.2.5
Solution

1.

Compute
\displaystyle\omega(A(t))
:
For fixed
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle A(t)
is triangular, so its numerical radius equals its spectral radius:

\omega(A(t))=\max\{1,2\}=2

2.

Compute
\displaystyle\|K\|
:
The integral operator
\displaystyle K
has norm:

\|K\|=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\int_{0}^{t}\|K(t,s)\|ds=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}ds=1-e^{-1}

3.

Apply Theorem
3.2
:
Construct the comparison matrix:

M=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(\omega(A)+\omega(A^{*}))&\|K\|\\
1&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1-e^{-1}\\
1&0.5\end{bmatrix}

4.

Compute
\displaystyle\omega(M)
:
The numerical radius of
\displaystyle M
is bounded by:

\omega(M)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2+0.5+\sqrt{(2-0.5)^{2}+(1-e^{-1}+1)^{2}}\right)\approx 2.3

4.2.6
Interpretation

The bound
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{L})\leq 2.3
implies that solutions to the integro-differential equation will grow at most exponentially with rate
\displaystyle 2.3
. This guarantees stability for sufficiently small time intervals and provides a quantitative estimate for numerical solution methods.

4.2.7
Convergence of Numerical Methods

These bounds are particularly useful when analyzing discretization schemes for integro-differential equations. The numerical radius estimates:

•

Provide stability constraints for time-step selection

•

Yield error bounds for approximate solutions

•

Help establish convergence rates for iterative methods

For instance, when applying a Runge-Kutta method to (
43
), the numerical radius bound ensures the method remains stable if the time step
\displaystyle h
satisfies
\displaystyle h\omega(\mathcal{L})<C
for some constant
\displaystyle C
depending on the method.

4.3
Application to Fractional Integro-Differential Equations

Numerical radius inequalities play a crucial role in analyzing the stability and convergence of solutions to fractional integro-differential equations. Corollary
3.18
provides refined bounds that can be applied to operator matrices arising in such equations.

4.3.1
Problem Formulation

Consider the fractional integro-differential equation:

D^{\alpha}y(t)=A(t)y(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(t,s)y(s)ds,\quad y(0)=y_{0}

(44)

where:

•

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}
is the Caputo fractional derivative of order
\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1)

•

\displaystyle A(t)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a time-dependent operator

•

\displaystyle K(t,s)
is the kernel operator

4.3.2
Operator Matrix Representation

We can represent the solution operator as a
\displaystyle 2\times 2
block matrix:

\mathcal{T}=\begin{bmatrix}A&K\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}

where
\displaystyle I
is the identity operator. The numerical radius
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{T})
governs the system’s stability.

4.3.3
Application of Corollary
3.18

Applying Corollary 3.18 with
\displaystyle r=2
and
\displaystyle\mu=1/2
gives:

\omega^{4}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{3\beta+2}{16(\beta+1)}\left\||A|^{4}+|K^{*}|^{4}\right\|+\frac{2\beta+3}{16(\beta+1)}\left\||A|^{2}+|K^{*}|^{2}\right\|\omega(\mathcal{T}^{2})+\frac{1}{8}\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})

4.3.4
Comprehensive Example

Consider the fractional system with:

\displaystyle\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-2\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\displaystyle K(t,s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}&0\\
0&(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\end{pmatrix}

Solution Steps

1.

Compute operator norms:

\left\|A\right\|=2,\quad\left\|K\right\|=\frac{T^{\alpha}}{\alpha}

2.

Apply Corollary
3.18
with
\displaystyle\beta=1
:

\omega^{4}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{5}{32}\left\||A|^{4}+|K^{*}|^{4}\right\|+\frac{5}{32}\left\||A|^{2}+|K^{*}|^{2}\right\|\omega(\mathcal{T}^{2})+\frac{1}{8}\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})

3.

For
\displaystyle T=1
,
\displaystyle\alpha=0.5
:

\omega^{4}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{5}{32}(16+4)+\frac{5}{32}(4+2)\omega(\mathcal{T}^{2})+\frac{1}{8}\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})

4.

This yields the quadratic inequality in
\displaystyle\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T})
:

\omega^{4}(\mathcal{T})\leq 3.125+0.9375\omega(\mathcal{T}^{2})+0.125\omega^{2}(\mathcal{T}^{2})

4.3.5
Interpretation

The derived bound:

•

Provides a quantitative stability criterion

•

Ensures solution remains bounded when
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{T})<\rho
(solution of the inequality)

•

Guides time-step selection in numerical schemes

This application demonstrates how Corollary
3.18
yields practical stability bounds for fractional integro-differential equations, extending the classical results to the fractional calculus setting.

4.4
Application to Fractional Partial Differential Equations

Numerical radius inequalities for operator matrices are particularly valuable in analyzing the stability of solutions to fractional partial differential equations (FPDEs). The results from Section 3, especially Theorem
3.2
, provide powerful tools for establishing solution bounds in such systems.

4.4.1
Problem Formulation

Consider the time-fractional diffusion equation with a nonlinear source term:

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}u}{\partial t^{\alpha}}=\nabla\cdot(D(x)\nabla u)+f(x,u),\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T]

(45)

where:

•

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}
is the Caputo fractional derivative of order
\displaystyle\alpha
(
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1
)

•

\displaystyle D(x)
is a spatially varying diffusion coefficient

•

\displaystyle f(x,u)
is a nonlinear reaction term

•

Boundary conditions:
\displaystyle u|_{\partial\Omega}=0

•

Initial condition:
\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)

4.4.2
Semi-Discrete Formulation

Using finite element spatial discretization, we obtain the matrix system:

D^{\alpha}\mathbf{u}(t)=-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u}(t))

where:

•

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}
is the stiffness matrix (discrete Laplacian)

•

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})
represents the nonlinear term

This can be represented as an operator matrix:

\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{K}&\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

4.4.3
Stability Analysis via Numerical Radius

Applying Theorem
3.2
with
\displaystyle s=1
, we obtain:

\omega(\mathcal{A})\leq\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\omega(-\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{K}^{*})&\|\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{u})\|\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\right)

4.4.4
Concrete Example

Consider the 1D case with
\displaystyle\Omega=(0,1)
,
\displaystyle D(x)\equiv 1
, and
\displaystyle f(u)=u-u^{3}
.

1.

Discretization
:
Using linear finite elements with
\displaystyle N=3
nodes (
\displaystyle h=0.25
), the stiffness matrix is:

\mathbf{K}=\frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&0\\
-1&2&-1\\
0&-1&2\end{bmatrix}=4\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&0\\
-1&2&-1\\
0&-1&2\end{bmatrix}

2.

Nonlinear Term
:
The Jacobian of
\displaystyle\mathbf{F}
at equilibrium is:

\mathbf{J}=\text{diag}(1-3u_{i}^{2})

3.

Compute Components
:

•

\displaystyle\omega(-\mathbf{K})=\|\mathbf{K}\|=\frac{4(2+2\cos(\pi h))}{h}\approx 16
(for small
\displaystyle h
)

•

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}\|\leq 1
(near zero solution where
\displaystyle|u_{i}|\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
)

4.

Apply Theorem
:
The numerical radius of the
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrix
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}8&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
is computed using the formula:

\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
d&b\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{|a+b|+\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+(c+d)^{2}}}{2}

For our matrix
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}8&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
:

\omega\left(\begin{bmatrix}8&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{|8+0|+\sqrt{(8-0)^{2}+(1+1)^{2}}}{2}=\frac{8+\sqrt{64+4}}{2}=\frac{8+\sqrt{68}}{2}=4+\sqrt{17}\approx 8.123

4.4.5
Stability Interpretation

The bound
\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{A})\leq 8.123
implies:

•

The semi-discrete system is stable if the time step satisfies
\displaystyle\Delta t^{\alpha}<C/\omega(\mathcal{A})

•

For
\displaystyle\alpha=0.5
, this gives
\displaystyle\Delta t<(C/8.123)^{2}

•

Provides a practical criterion for adaptive time-stepping

4.4.6
Extensions

The results can be further refined by:

•

Using Corollary
3.18
for higher-order bounds

•

Incorporating spatial refinement effects

•

Accounting for time-dependent coefficients

This application demonstrates how the numerical radius inequalities from the paper provide concrete, computable stability criteria for FPDEs, bridging the gap between abstract operator theory and practical numerical analysis.
