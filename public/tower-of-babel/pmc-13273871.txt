Oral immunosuppression as a bladder-preserving strategy for cystitis cystica et glandularis with intestinal metaplasia
Authors: John Dudinec, Jayanth Doss, Jordan Foreman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystitis cystica et glandularis (CCEG) with intestinal metaplasia (IM) is a proliferative condition of the bladder driven from chronic inflammation. Disease ranges from asymptomatic, incidental lesions to end-stage bladder disease with upper tract deterioration.We present the case of a 33-year-old male who presented with a six-year history of pelvic pain and voiding symptoms, found to have a diffusely edematous and inflamed bladder with hydroureteronephrosis requiring nephrostomy placement. Transurethral sampling of abnormal tissue confirmed the diagnosis, and the patient symptomatically and clinically improved on oral immunosuppression. This case demonstrates the spectrum of presentation of intestinal type-CCEG and management strategies.

Introduction

Cystitis glandularis (CG), cystitis cystica (CC) and cystitis cystica et glandularis (CCEG) are proliferative disorders of the urothelium, developing from Von Brunn's nests in the lamina propria which undergo cystic dilation (CC) or glandular metaplasia (CG) after exposure to chronic irritation and/or inflammation.1 CCEG describes lesions in which features of cystitis cystica and cystitis glandularis coexist. Two histologic subtypes of cystitis glandularis have been described, conventional and intestinal. Cystitis glandularis with intestinal metaplasia (IM) occurs in approximately 10-25% of CG cases and is characterized histologically by tall columnar cells with mucin secretion, goblet cells and Paneth cells.1 While CC/GG/CCEG with or without IM are felt to be benign lesions and often an incidental finding on cystoscopy,2,3 patients can present with extremely bothersome lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), and rarely upper tract obstruction.4,5 Gold standard diagnosis is with transurethral resection and histopathology, necessary to differentiate these benign lesions from urothelial cancer.6 No standardized treatment protocol currently exists, and various medical treatments have been described for symptomatic cases,5 with rare cases of florid disease requiring cystectomy.7 We present a case of CCEG with intestinal metaplasia causing significant LUTS, pelvic pain and upper tract obstruction managed with oral immunosuppressants.

Case report

A 33-year-old veteran male, who initially presented to an outside urologist with multiple chronic complaints including bilateral testicular pain, penile pain, painful ejaculation, perineal pain, urinary frequency and nocturia 3-4x/night for six years. An MRI pelvis two years prior had noted irregular trabeculations and posterior enhancement of his bladder wall. He had not undergone prior cystoscopy. He was an active smoker but reported no other medical or surgical history. Physical exam was notable for a boggy prostate measuring 30g, his exam otherwise unremarkable and a urinalysis was obtained and bland. He underwent a one-month course of empiric sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim without improvement in symptoms. Office cystoscopy revealed bullous edema and inflammation most notably at the trigone. A CT scan was obtained and notable for left hydroureteronephrosis to the left ureterovesical junction (UVJ) with a 3.9 × 2.2cm soft tissue lesion noted at the bladder base near the left UVJ, and asymmetric left wall thickening (Fig. 1).

CT imaging of bladder mass prior to Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumor.

He then underwent a transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) with resection of a 6cm mass at the trigone and left wall. His left ureteral orifice was unable to be visualized during the case. He was admitted for observation and was found to have an acute kidney injury (AKI) on POD 1 with a serum creatinine of 2.2 (mg/dL) from a baseline of 0.8 (mg/dL) and new-onset bilateral flank pain. A non-contrasted CT was obtained which revealed moderate bilateral hydronephrosis. He underwent bilateral percutaneous nephrostomy tube (PCN) placement with resolution of his AKI and flank pain. Pathology revealed cystitis cystica et glandularis with intestinal metaplasia. He continued to endorse pelvic and voiding symptoms after PCN placement.

He was counseled on surgical management including bilateral ureteral reimplant, prostate sparing cystectomy with neobladder, cystoprostatectomy with neobladder or trial of medical therapy with prednisone 5mg QD. He elected to trial medical therapy, ultimately with a total of 6 weeks on prednisone with a four-week course, a two-week treatment holiday, then an additional two weeks on treatment. He noted marked improvement in his LUTS/pelvic symptoms with the prednisone course; however, his pelvic pain and LUTS returned after his prednisone trial was completed.

He then presented to our institution for a second opinion in management options, approximately two months after completion of his prednisone course and three months after initial TURBT. A nephrostogram was obtained which showed no evidence of obstruction and a repeat cystoscopy found a diffusely inflamed, non-distending bladder with bullous edema (Fig. 2). Given no evidence of obstruction on his nephrostogram, he underwent a clamp trial with subsequent nephrostomy tube removal.

(A) Office cystoscopy demonstrating small capacity bladder with inflammation. (B) Office cystoscopy with bullous edema and inflamed mucosa.

A renal ultrasound 1 months after PCN removal showed no hydronephrosis. A repeat cystoscopy revealed heaped, inflamed mucosa involving 30-40% of his bladder. A Lasix renal scan noted normal right sided drainage, with mildly delayed drainage on the left. He continued to endorse pelvic pain. He was referred to rheumatology for discussion of immunosuppressive therapy given symptom improvement on prednisone. He underwent a 12-week prednisone taper with drastic improvement in his pelvic symptoms. Repeat cystoscopy after completion of the steroid course revealed decreased mucosal inflammation, though was notable for a 3cm midline trigonal mass. He underwent a TURBT of this lesion, which revealed focal intestinal metaplasia and a nephrogenic adenoma in a background of edema and chronic inflammation.

He was started on azathioprine with symptomatic improvement, cystoscopy four months after initiation noted dramatic visual improvement in mucosal inflammation, estimated to be less than 5% involving the bladder neck. Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) and nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15) testing was completed at treatment initiation and was normal; reduced function of either enzyme is associated with an increased risk of azathioprine-related side effects. Mild nausea was reported during his first week of azathioprine treatment, which resolved without intervention with no further reported adverse reactions. Laboratory monitoring has included complete blood counts, liver enzymes and serum creatinine to evaluate for myelosuppression, hepatic injury and renal insufficiency. Labs were obtained monthly for the first three months of therapy, then spaced to every three months. He has been followed with q6 month cystoscopies which continue to show visual improvement, and his pelvic and urinary symptoms remain controlled. He is planned for repeated upper tract imaging and confirmatory TURBT.

Discussion

Cystitis glandularis (CG) and cystitis cystica et glandularis (CCEG) ± intestinal metaplasia (IM) present on a spectrum of disease, from asymptomatic cases diagnosed incidentally on cystoscopy8 to disease causing renal failure from upper tract obstruction9 and end-stage bladder requiring cystectomy.10 The histopathologic hallmarks of these conditions include cystic dilation of Von Brunn's nests in CC; glandular columnar or cuboidal cells lining these cystic structures in CG; and IM, which shares similar glandular architecture with CG but is distinguished by the presence of mucin-secreting goblet cells.11 Specific immunohistochemical stains can differentiate between CG and IM. Sung et al. examined the staining profiles of CG versus IM, focusing on three markers: CDX2, a transcription factor involved in intestinal epithelial differentiation; CK7, a cytokeratin expressed in normal urothelium and some glandular proliferations of the bladder but absent in colonic adenocarcinoma; and CK20, a cytokeratin expressed in gastrointestinal epithelium and superficial urothelium. CK7 expression was more commonly found in CG than in IM, whereas CDX2 and CK20 expression was more frequently observed in IM.11 The true incidence of these entities is poorly understood, with some reports noting an incidence of ∼1% in the general population,12 though autopsy studies have reported features of CC, CG or CCEG in 60% in grossly healthy bladders.13 Literature on CG and CCEG ± intestinal metaplasia (IM) is limited to case reports and small retrospective series and there is currently no consensus on management.

The malignant potential of CG and CCEG ± IM is debated. Pathologically these lesions can be difficult to differentiate from adenocarcinoma14 and prior small case reports noted malignant transformation.15 However, other retrospective series do not show this concern for malignant potential. Agrawal et al. reported a series of 64 patients with CCEG, 12 with IM and at a median follow up of 5 years found no patients developed adenocarcinoma.2 This was supported by the work of Khan et al. who at a median follow up of 9.2 years reported no cases of de novo urothelial carcinoma in a series of 64 patients with CC, CG or CCEG with 30 (44.8%) patients having CG and 16 (23.9%) having intestinal metaplasia.1 Smith et al. additionally examined 136 patients with either CCEG (n = 117) or IM (n = 19). Concurrent carcinoma was identified in 33% of the CCEG patients and 37% of the IM patients, with only one CCEG patient later developing de novo urothelial carcinoma, in the setting of prior upper tract urothelial carcinoma.3 Given the contrasting evidence, debate remains if these lesions are truly precancerous or are seen in conjunction with carcinoma as the two pathologies share common risk factors.

No standardized treatment pathway exists for the management of CG or CCEG ± IM, owing to the paucity of data on the subject. Tissues diagnosis is required for pathologic examination thus transurethral resection is commonly advocated, as well as reversal of any causative inflammatory triggers.5,16 Numerous next-line therapies for refractory or recurrent symptoms have been described in case reports, including oral treatments such as corticosteroids,5 NSAIDS,17 COX-2 inhibitors,18 intravesical treatments such as BCG,19 chemotherapy5 or steroids,20 and in rare circumstances, cystectomy.10 Given the lack of consensus on management, treatment pathway should be a shared-decision making process. Intestinal metaplasia has been linked to increased recurrence after resection,16 as noted in our case. Given our patients young age and unclear duration of therapy, steroid sparing medications were pursued to avoid the long-term complications from chronic steroids. Follow-up protocols are also non-standardized and at the discretion of the treatment team, though serial cystoscopy is often advocated given the debated natural history of CG/CCEG.16

Conclusion

Cystitis glandularis and cystitis cystica et glandularis ± intestinal metaplasia presents on a clinical spectrum, with disease ranging from asymptomatic to debilitating. We present a case of CCEG + IM complicated by severe symptoms and upper tract obstruction response to transurethral resection and chronic oral immunosuppressants. Oral steroid sparing immunomodulating medications represent a viable treatment option.
