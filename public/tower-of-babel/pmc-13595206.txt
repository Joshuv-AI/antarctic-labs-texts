Auditory associative learning drives synaptic responses in tail of the striatum spiny projection neurons
Authors: Adrien Stanley, Se Joon Choi, Anika Frank, Emily Makowicz, Stefano Cataldi, N. Shashaank, Eugene V. Mosharov, Clay Lacefield, Maria Concetta Miniaci, David Sulzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe association of cues with the absence or presence of danger is crucial to survival. The synaptic changes that enable association with the absence of danger are unknown, but a role for the tail of the striatum (TS) has been implicated. To investigate this, we conditioned mice to associate a contextual environment with the threat of foot-shock, which led to freezing, and a sound cue (CS) with the absence of shock, which suppressed contextual freezing. After learning, the CS strongly activated SPNs relative to controls, including a large direct pathway (dSPN) calcium response to sound onset that persisted during anesthesia. In the brain slices of conditioned mice, dSPNs exhibited enhanced synaptic responses to auditory thalamic inputs, with higher NMDA and AMPA receptor-mediated responses, decreased GABAergic inputs, and increased density of dendritic spines. Thus, auditory association with the absence of danger involves enhancement of TS synaptic responses to the associated sound.

Introduction

The ability of animals to learn to associate stimuli such as sound with safety or danger is critical to survival. For example, learned calls by prairie dogs are used by the colony to safely forage and avoid predators.1 Auditory associative learning is also involved in the acquisition of language and the comprehension of music, and aberrations in these forms of auditory associative learning are thought to be related to disorders of excessive avoidance including anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder.2,3,4,5,6 While much research has characterized learned threat associations,7 the neural mechanisms mediating suppression of threat behavior are unknown.2,8

In conditioned threat suppression, a sensory cue, such as a sound, suppresses responses to threatening cues.9,10,11,12,13 Threatening cues evoke defensive behaviors such as freezing by mice, and the magnitude of freezing suppression reflects the degree of auditory associative learning. Here, we adapt a well-characterized conditioned threat suppression paradigm, formerly called “safety learning”, in which an auditory cue suppresses freezing in a threatening environmental context. This learning paradigm features key characteristics of threat suppressive cues,10 including impaired conversion to a threat associated cue (also known as fear retardation),9 as well as suppression of threat response to anxiogenic areas of an open field or a light-dark box (indicative of summation),11 and is formally a summation test of the threat response to the context and the threat suppressing response of the sound cue.

To identify the underlying neural mechanisms of this form of auditory associative learning, we focused on the tail of the striatum (TS), a functionally distinct region of the striatum that receives strong auditory inputs from cortical and thalamic regions.14,15,16 The TS possesses two populations of primary neurons, known as direct pathway spiny projection neurons (dSPNs) that project to the substantia nigra reticulata (SNr) and globus pallidus internal (GPi), and indirect pathway SPNs (iSPNs) that indirectly project to the SNr/GPi via the globus pallidus externa.17,18,19,20 The auditory thalamic inputs to the TS and to the auditory cortex both originate from the medial geniculate body (MGB), which forms glutamatergic synapses on SPNs.16,21,22 The TS also receives dopaminergic (DA) input from the substantia nigra lateralis.14

This anatomical arrangement would seem to put the TS in a position to modulate behavioral responses to sound. Indeed, threat suppression-associated sounds evoke strong local field potentials in the TS,11 and recent work has highlighted a role in learning threat promotion-associated sounds.23,24 Sounds associated with threat evoke responses in SPNs of both the dorsal TS (dTS) and ventral TS (vTS).25,26 Notably, in the vTS, dSPNs but not iSPNs exhibit increased activation to threat-associated sounds, and inhibition of vTS dSPNs, but not iSPNs, disrupts the association of sound with threat.26 Additionally, novel sounds evoke DA release in the dTS23,24 and threat-promoting associations are known to require DA signaling.25 Distinct responses of dTS dSPNs versus iSPNs to threat-associated sounds are unknow but are interesting as they receive more auditory cortical input than SPNs in the vTS.14 Additionally, responses of distinct SPN subtypes to sounds associated with threat suppression are unknown.

Here we focus on dTS and investigate responses of distinct SPN subtypes during auditory associative learning. We further investigated electrophysiological changes that occurred in thalamo-striatal synapses following threat suppression associations, focusing on dTS dSPNs, as they showed the most robust and anesthesia-resistant responses. As DA induces synaptic plasticity in SPNs,27 we additionally investigated its role in acquisition and recall of threat suppression associations.

In the conditioned threat-suppression group, the conditioned stimulus (CS; sound cue) and unconditioned stimulus (US; foot shock) were presented separately.10,13

The conditioned threat-promotion group received an identical sound cue that co-terminated with a foot shock for 3 days. Although learned threat associations occur quite rapidly,26 an established three-day protocol of training9 was used for both paradigms in order to compare the same number of training days and aversive experiences between conditioned threat-suppression and threat-promotion.

The tone-alone control group was presented with an identical CS and no foot shock for 3 days.

All mice underwent a recall session to test their behavioral response to the cue in the absence of foot shock. Conditioned threat-suppression and tone-control mice were kept in the same context to observe cue-induced suppression of contextual threat. The threat-conditioned mice were placed in a novel context to observe CS-elicited freezing behavior that was independent of the context.

As expected, tone-alone controls developed no behavioral responses to the CS (Figures 1B–1E, 1N, and 1O).

During threat-suppression conditioning, mice did not exhibit any behavioral responses on training Day 1, but on Days 2 and 3 they showed extensive freezing when placed in the context. This freezing to the context (termed preCS) was suppressed by the CS (Figures 1F–1I), indicating that the sound association with the absence of threat was successfully learned. On Day 4, we conducted a recall analysis in which the response to the CS was assessed in the absence of foot shocks. Threat-suppression conditioned mice continued to display context freezing and a robust locomotor response during the CS presentation (Video S1, Figures 1N, 1O, and S1A–S1K). We note that the increased movement did not occur on training day 1 and was absent in tone control mice, arguing that this behavioral response reflects associative learning rather than a distraction by the CS tone.

In contrast, the conditioned threat-promotion group rapidly developed freezing to the CS on training Day 1, with still higher freezing on the following training Days (Figures 1J–1M). There was little difference between preCS and CS responses, indicating that both the context and the tones had become aversive. Although there was a fear response to the novel context on Day 4, additional freezing responses to the tone were retained on the recall test, indicating that a sound-threat association was produced, a result consistent with prior literature using this paradigm (Video S2, Figures 1N–1Q and S1A–S1J).9 While the amount of threat association is similar to the prior publication without fiber implantation, we note that a drawback of our behavior protocol is that the optical measures of neuronal activity require the presence of an optical fiber apparatus, and this likely contributes to the contextual association at recall.

We found that mice that received local infusion of either TTX or DA antagonists immediately prior to conditioned threat-suppression during training (and did not receive the drugs during the recall test) displayed reduced freezing during preCS, but not during the CS on the recall days, demonstrating that the drugs interfered with the acquisition of contextual threat learning (Figures 2C–2F). Thus, the application of the drugs during training occluded our ability to measure the suppressive effect of the threat-suppression CS since there was little contextual freezing to suppress.

In naive mice, the sound produced little Ca2+ response in dTS SPNs. This result is consistent with prior reports indicating that few dSPNs show calcium responses to sound alone.26

Foot shock evoked rapid increase in Ca2+ transients in dSPNs that lasted for the duration of the shock and rapidly returned to baseline after the shock (Figures 3D and 3E), a result also consistent with prior reports.26 In contrast, the mean shock-induced iSPN response tended to increase during the shock but was not statistically significant, and was followed by a significant decrease after shock termination (Figures 3F and 3G). To assess the origins of the shock response in dTS dSPNs, we repeated the experiment in D1:cre mice in which an optofluid cannula was implanted in the TS to provide simultaneous drug infusion and recording of dSPN calcium activity (Figures 1H and 1I). Local infusion of the sodium channel blocker, TTX, ablated dSPN’s response to the foot shock, as expected (Figure 3J–3L). To assess a role for dopamine in the response, the experiment was repeated with a cocktail of DA antagonists (D2 receptor antagonist: sulpiride, and D1 receptor antagonist: SCH23390), which had no effect on the foot shock response in dSPNs (Figures 3M–3O).

We next analyzed dTS SPN calcium activity during control, threat-suppression, or threat-promotion association learning and during control conditions in the absence of foot shock. We found that foot shock responses were observed in both dTS SPN populations during acquisition of threat-suppression or threat-promotion associations (Figure S2).

During threat-suppression conditioning, we measured no SPN calcium responses to the CS on Day 1 (Figures 4D–4F and 5D–5F). In contrast, on Days 2 and 3 of training and on the recall day, the CS evoked an increase in the activity of both dTS SPN populations that lasted throughout the tone duration (Figures 4J, 4M, 4N, 5J, 5M, and 5N), concomitant with the acquisition of the learned behavioral responses to the CS (Figures 1F–1I).

During threat-promotion conditioning, both dSPNs and iSPNs exhibited heightened activity at the onset of the CS (0–2 s) and returned to baseline by ∼5 s (Figures 4M and 5M). The amplitude of the signal at the tone onset increased on Days 2 and 3 of conditioning and was maintained on the recall day (Figures 4G–4I, 4M, 4O, 5G–5I, 5M, and 4O), which also coincided with the increased behavioral freezing on those days (Figures 1J–1M). The increased response at the onset of threat-promoting associated sounds is consistent with prior reports.26 Interestingly, an additional peak was evoked in dSPNs, but not iSPNs, that immediately followed the shock omission at recall (Figures S3A–S4F), which we label an offset component.

Overall, fiber photometry recordings demonstrate that the conditioned threat-suppression and threat-promotion protocols induced CS-related and association-specific changes in the activity of both populations of SPNs that were retained even in the absence of the foot shock.

As expected, there was no CS-evoked activity in anesthetized tone-only mice (Figures 6B and 6C). In the conditioned threat-suppression group, anesthesia completely inhibited iSPN responses to the CS. However, dSPN responses displayed both an anesthesia-resistant burst of activity at the onset component of CS (0–2 s) and an anesthesia-sensitive sustained component (2–20 s) (Figures 6D and 6E).

In conditioned threat-promotion mice, anesthesia likewise completely inhibited iSPN responses (Figure 6G), consistent with a prior report indicating that iSPNs are preferentially modulated by movement rather than by threat promotion-associated sounds.26 In contrast, anesthesia partially decreased the calcium transient in dSPNs at CS onset (Figure 6F). The offset component that was present in awake mice during recall when foot shock is omitted was abolished by anesthesia, indicating a requirement for consciousness and/or movement for that response (Figures S3G–S3I).

Correlation analysis of CS-elicited dSPN activity relative to locomotion (Figures S4) showed that the dSPN onset component of activity did not correlate with movement during recall (Figures S4E and S4G). In contrast, the dSPN sustained component was correlated with movement (Figures S4F and S4H), consistent with the blockade of this component by anesthesia.

Together, these results indicate that cues that evoke either threat-suppression or threat-promotion induce an initial rapid anesthesia-resistant response to the cue onset in dSPNs likely related to attention or salience.

As expected, local TTX infusion in the dTS abolished all calcium responses to the CS (Figure 7C). Infusion of TTX significantly decreased contextual freezing (Figures 7E–7G), indicating a role for the TS in recall of contextual threat. In contrast to TTX, local infusion of the DA antagonists had no effect on CS-induced calcium transients in dSPNs (Figure 7D) and did not affect CS evoked suppression of freezing (Figures 7E–7G). These results suggest contextual threat learning requires local dopamine signaling in the TS, but that recall of the learned response does not.

We found that intrinsic membrane properties, including resting membrane potential, input resistance, and membrane capacitance of dTS dSPN were unaltered by threat-suppression learning (Figures S5A–S5D). Consistently, threat-suppression conditioning did not alter the excitability of dSPN, as shown by identical rheobase values and identical responses to depolarizing step currents of current ramps (Figures Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 10E–S3G). We also did not observe significant changes in inward-rectifying potassium (Kir)32 (Figures Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 10H and S3I), and A-type potassium (Ka)33 currents (Figures S5J and S5K). Together, these results indicate that sound-cued threat-suppression learning did not alter the intrinsic excitability of dTS dSPNs.

An alternative possibility for the enhanced dTS dSPNs response to CS is an increase in excitatory inputs or synaptic response to the inputs from the cortex and/or thalamus.34 We thus measured spontaneous excitatory and inhibitory inputs to dSPNs in dTS slices from threat-suppression conditioned and control mice. Miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC) amplitudes were significantly higher than controls, while the mEPSC frequency was unchanged (Figures 8B–8F). Conversely, conditioned threat-suppression reduced the amplitude of miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs), but not their frequency (Figures 8G–8K). Together, these results indicate that conditioned threat-suppression induced postsynaptic changes in dSPNs that enhanced their response to glutamatergic inputs and decreased responses to GABAergic inputs.

To further examine the basis for the changes in dSPN synaptic response, we first stimulated synaptic inputs by activating a stimulation electrode on the corpus callosum in the presence of the GABAAR antagonist picrotoxin and recorded dTS dSPN responses by whole-cell patch clamp. We found that sound threat-suppression association enhanced the excitatory synaptic responses by dTS dSPNs (Figures S6A and S6B), consistent with the enhanced mEPSC amplitudes.

We then recorded dSPN responses activated by local electrical stimulation of dTS in the presence of the glutamate antagonists CNQX and APV. We found that threat-suppression conditioned mice showed a decrease in IPSC amplitude (Figures S6C and S6D) consistent with decreased postsynaptic mIPSCs.

To examine dSPN responses to auditory thalamic synaptic inputs of the MGB, which sends axonal inputs to both auditory cortex and the dTS,35,36,37 we injected a Cre-independent Chronos virus into the MGB and a Cre-dependent tdTomato virus into the dTS of D1:cre mice. After preparing acute coronal brain slices from threat-suppression-conditioned and control animals, MGB axons within the dTS were optically stimulated (470 nm, 5 ms) to induce optical EPSCs (oEPSCs) (Figure 9A). Threat-suppression conditioning enhanced the amplitude of dSPN responses to oEPSCs (Figures 9B and S6F), consistent with increased excitatory postsynaptic spontaneous and evoked responses. We observed that oEPSCs in dSPNs from conditioned threat-suppression mice showed a greater increase at depolarized holding potentials (Figure S6E), indicating that the NMDA components of dSPNs were enhanced by threat-suppression conditioning. Recordings of the NMDAR and AMPAR components of the dSPN responses to MGB input revealed that both were enhanced by the association of sound with threat-suppression (Figure S6F), with a large increase in the NMDAR-to-AMPAR ratio (Figure 9D). This was confirmed by pharmacological isolation, which showed that NMDAR oEPSCs in dSPNs from threat-suppression-conditioned mice were significantly larger than those in control mice (Figure 9E).

We then examined if the enhanced thalamostriatal response altered long-term depression (LTD).38 The induction and maintenance of LTD at thalamostriatal dSPN synapses by low frequency (10 Hz) thalamic stimulation paired with postsynaptic depolarization was identical in control and threat-suppression conditioned mice (Figures S6G and S6H).

In the dTS, auditory cortical and auditory thalamic inputs both converge onto SPN dendritic spines.39 To determine if the enhanced responses by dTS dSPN glutamatergic inputs correlated with morphological alterations, we examined biocytin-labeled dTS dSPNs. Scholl analysis revealed that there were fewer dendritic branch intersections in dTS dSPNs and a higher density of dendritic spines in threat-suppression conditioned mice than in controls (Figures 9F–9H and S6I–S6M). No significant difference was observed in dendritic length, number of primary dendrites, soma surface area, or number of branch points (Figures S6J–S6M).

Discussion

Auditory associative learning has been studied for over a century, including studies by Pavlov that provided the foundations of contemporary disciplines of psychology and neuroscience.2,9,10,11,12,40 Here we extend these studies to examine the physiological basis for the association of sound with threat-suppression using protocols that have been described as “safety learning”. We report that sounds which become associated with threat-suppression induce specific synaptic plasticity in SPNs of the dTS (Figure 10). We found that dTS SPN activity is required for the acquisition and subsequent expression of contextual threat responses, as both were disrupted by local application of TTX. In contrast, blockade of dopaminergic signaling in the dTS disrupted the acquisition but not the expression of contextual threat responses to the first tone after the response was learned.Figure 10Summary schematic of behavioral and neuronal changes that occur following association of sound with threat-suppression.Associating a sound with threat suppression increases movement response to the associated sound and is accompanied by an increase in dTS SPN calcium activity. Additionally, postsynaptic plasticity is enhanced at auditory thalamus to TS dSPN synapses, which likely drives the observed calcium response to the learned sound cue.

Summary schematic of behavioral and neuronal changes that occur following association of sound with threat-suppression.

Associating a sound with threat suppression increases movement response to the associated sound and is accompanied by an increase in dTS SPN calcium activity. Additionally, postsynaptic plasticity is enhanced at auditory thalamus to TS dSPN synapses, which likely drives the observed calcium response to the learned sound cue.

When the learned behavioral response was blocked by anesthesia, dTS dSPN, but not iSPN, responses contained an anesthesia-resistant component that was present at the onset of a sound associated with either threat-promotion or threat-suppression. Electrophysiological analysis following conditioned threat-suppression learning revealed that this was coincident with the induction of a form of postsynaptic plasticity at auditory thalamostriatal synapses of dSPNs.

The anesthesia-sensitive component of SPN response to the CS likely relates to the motor behavior, with elevated activity occurring during movement in response to sounds associated with threat-suppression, and decreased activity with movement reduction in response to threat-promotion-associated sounds. Indeed, a recent study demonstrated that a subpopulation of dTS dSPNs encode movement onset during threat-associated sounds.26 It is possible that the calcium response to tone onset and the movement-sensitive later components are driven by different dSPN populations.

Prior reports indicate that dTS DA is critical for the acquisition of associations of sound with threat-promotion.25 A role for DA during the acquisition phase is further consistent with recent reports that novel stimuli enhance DA release in the dTS,41 which may prime dTS SPNs for plasticity induced by associated excitatory inputs from thalamus or cortex. Unfortunately, in this study we could not ascertain if local dTS antagonism altered acquisition of sound association with threat-suppression because of the effective impairment of contextual threat response acquisition. This resulted in a floor effect that prevented us from observing further reductions in freezing. However, during the recall, blockade of local dopaminergic signaling in the dTS did not alter the behavioral response or the dTS dSPN responses to the threat-suppression-associated sound during the recall phase. This suggests that DA in the dTS is required for acquisition of contextual threat but is not required for the maintenance of the learned behaviors.

Electrophysiological recordings indicate an overall increase in excitatory responses by dSPNs following association of sound with threat-suppression, as demonstrated by the elevated amplitudes of mEPSCs and evoked EPSCs with concomitant decrease in mIPSCs and evoked IPSCs amplitudes. Optogenetic activation of MGB axons in the dTS revealed increased postsynaptic responses driven by both increased NMDA and AMPA currents. An important role for thalamic inputs to dTS dSPNs in auditory associative learning is consistent with previous findings including (1) thalamic but not cortical activity is reported to be critical for threat-suppression and threat-promotion learning,42,43 (2) thalamic signals are relatively better preserved under anesthesia than cortical signals,37,44,45,46,47,48,49 and (3) the persistence of dTS dSPN activity following dopaminergic blockade indicates the absence of presynaptic filtering, a process found to occur for cortical but not thalamic inputs.50,51

The potentiation of glutamatergic response by threat-suppression learning is consistent with the increase in calcium response to the CS, as NMDA receptors gate calcium influx, and GluA1-containing AMPA receptors are calcium-permeable.52,53,54 The enhanced calcium response by the dSPNs may sensitize them to simultaneous or subsequent excitatory input from cortex or other areas, perhaps by promoting NMDA responses or driving them to upstate transitions,55 local dendritic spike modulation,56 or the induction of new synapses.

TS SPNs receive strong glutamatergic afferents from the auditory cortex and the MGB of the thalamus. Precise presynaptic inputs to the TS SPNs are challenging to isolate in the slice preparation, as the MGB also acts as a relay station to both the TS SPNs as well as additional striatal neurons and cortical regions, but an increased response to cortical activation is consistent with the increased response to local electrical stimulation of the corpus callosum. Thus, the clear evidence for increased MGB-TS postsynaptic response does not exclude important roles for cortical and other local inputs including dopaminergic, cholinergic and GABAergic synapses in auditory CS learning. Future research will be required to dissect the specific roles of these multiple presynaptic inputs.

How might enhanced postsynaptic responses of dTS dSPNs drive learned behavioral responses to sound? Recently, a direct projection from dTS SPNs to the lateral amygdala, a region critical to the formation of sensory outcome associations, was shown to regulate threat-promotion association learning,25 although its role in the association of sound with threat-suppression is not known. The dTS dSPNs are GABAergic neurons that project to the lateral SNr, which in turn sends GABAergic projections to multiple nuclei that control motor output, including the periaqueductal gray (PAG), superior colliculus (SC), inferior colliculus (IC), and ventral anterior thalamic nucleus (VAmc).57,58,59,60 Freezing is controlled by the PAG and modulated via projections from the SC and IC.61,62 The SC and IC are also implicated in auditory perception, sensory cue-elicited movements,63,64 and defensive behaviors.58,62,65 The VAmc regulates motor activity via excitatory projections to the pre-supplementary motor cortex (preSMA).66,67,68,69 Therefore, activation of dTS dSPNs by learned threat-suppression cues could promote locomotor activity by disinhibiting the VAmc and exciting the preSMA. In contrast, dTS iSPNs are GABAergic neurons that project indirectly to the lateral SNr via the GPe and subthalamic nucleus (STN) and disinhibit the SNr and so act to inhibit the VAmc and the preSMA.67,70,71

During locomotion, both dSPNs and iSPNs are active in the anterior dorsal (also known as the motor) striatum, where dSPNs are widely thought to promote desired movements while iSPNs inhibit unwanted movements.72,73 The SPNs in the dTS may serve an analogous function in modulating freezing or locomotor activity in a sensory-dependent manner. In support of this hypothesis, dTS D1 SPNs promote while dTS D2 SPNs suppress threat avoidance.74 Alternatively, dTS dSPNs may enhance, and iSPNs may inhibit sensory cortices, analogous to the role of anterior dorsal striatal projections to motor cortices.75 A prefrontal cortex (PFC)-TS-GPe-thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN)-auditory thalamus pathway is reported to be critical for suppressing attention to irrelevant sensory stimuli,76 suggesting that dTS dSPNs promote and dTS iSPNs inhibit sensory attention.77,78,79,80,81 Such a role is consistent with reports that optogenetic stimulation of dTS dSPNs or auditory inputs to the dTS biases movements contralateral to the side of stimulation in an auditory-guided decision task.78,79,80,81,82

Together, these results indicate that auditory learning-driven dTS synaptic plasticity consists of increased excitatory postsynaptic responses in SPNs to an associated sound. The identification of this plasticity may suggest treatment targets for disorders with impaired threat-suppression processing, including post-traumatic stress disorder5,6,83 and Parkinson’s disease.84 They also implicate synaptic mechanisms that could underlie other forms of sound associative learning, including language acquisition, in which dTS activity is implicated.85

Limitations of the study

Given that fiber photometry lacks single-cell resolution, we did not ascertain whether the two components of the dTS dSPN learned calcium response derive from altered activity of individual neurons or from two distinct populations of neurons. While our current approach has the important advantage that it produces far less damage to the cortical and thalamic excitatory inputs that activate the TS neurons, future work using single-cell resolution techniques will be required to measure changes in the response by individual cells. Prior literature using a GRIN lens to achieve single cell resolution of activity in ventral TS revealed that sound threat association caused new cells to respond that were not initially activated prior to the association.26 Together, our present results and the previous single cell recording study suggest that association learning in the TS recruits additional neurons in the response, although further work is required to confirm.

In order to compare the observation of direct pathway neuron alterations in conditioned threat-suppression and threat promotion, we used the three-day conditioning protocol for both, as published by Pollak et al.9,13 While the prior publication did not report behavioral responses during the acquisition phase, we found that a learned CS drives a strongly increased behavioral and TS dSPN neuronal response in both forms of auditory associative learning after a single day of conditioning (Figures 1F–1I). Most safety conditioning protocols do not demonstrate threat-suppression association after a single day of conditioning.9,86,87,88,89,90 Thus, our data indicating that this occurs may be useful for future research”.

Experimental model and study participant details

All experimental procedures were approved by the Columbia University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC approval number AC-AABT0618). All mice in this study were on a C57BL/6J background, typically group housed with littermates of the same sex, and given access to food and water ad libitum. Mice were kept on a reverse light cycle (lights on from 10:00 p.m. to 10:00 a.m.) and testing was performed during the dark period under red light. Adult male and female mice between 2 and 12 months of age (6 months average, see Figure S1J) were used for photometry experiments. The following mouse lines were used in this study: D1:Cre (strain 030989-UCD) sourced from Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers and A2A:Cre (strain 010687) sourced from The Jackson Laboratories. These mouse strains have been validated for selective expression in dSPNs and iSPNs respectively.28,31 Heterozygous D1:Cre and A2A:Cre mice were used for all experiments and were maintained by backcrossing to C57/B6J wild-types. Experimental groups contained similar numbers of male and female mice. No sex differences were observed in learned threat suppression CS responses, although subtle sex differences in contextual threat responses were observed in D1:cre mice (Table S1).

Method details

All schematics were created in Biorender.

Surgical procedures

Injection and implant surgeries were performed under 2% isoflurane anesthesia using a stereotaxic instrument (Kopf instruments). A small incision was made in the scalp and burr holes were drilled in the skill at the appropriate stereotaxic coordinates (AP, ML, and DV relative to bregma): −1.06 AP, −3 ML, −3.25 DV for dTS; −3.2 AP, −2 ML, −3.8 DV for MGB. Viruses were infused using a glass micropipette, backfilled with mineral oil, connected to microinjector (Nanoject II Drummond Scientific Company) at a rate of 23 nL/min. The injection pipette was slowly withdrawn 5 min after the end of infusion. For fiber photometry experiments, optical fibers (Doric Lenses, 0.22 NA, 4 mm length, 300 μm diameter flat tip) were implanted 0.1 mm above the target injection site. For optofluidic photometry experiments, optofluidic cannula’s (Doric Lenses, 0.37 NA, 5 mm fiber length, 300 μm diameter flat tip, 4.9 mm cannula bore length) were implanted 0.1 mm above target injection site.

Viral volumes, sources and titers

For fiber photometry experiments targeting the dTS, 230 nL of AAV9-Syn-Flex-GCaMP6f-WPRE-SV40 (addgene,1∗10ˆ13 vg/ml) was unilaterally injected at a rate of 23 nL per minute. An optical fiber (Doric Lenses; MFC 300/370-0.22_4 mm_MF2.5_FLT) or optofluid cannula (Doric Lenses; OmFC_MF1.25_300/370-0.22_3.2_FLT_3) was implanted 0.1 mm above the target site. For electrophysiology experiments, AAV5-Syn-Chronos-GFP (addgene,1∗10ˆ13 vg/ml) was injected in the MGB and AAV9-Flex-tdTomato (addgene,1∗10ˆ13 vg/ml) was injected in the dTS. Mice were given 4 weeks for surgery recovery and viral expression before conducting experiments.

General histology procedures and imaging

Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB). Brains were removed, post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA in 0.1M PB, sectioned coronally at room temperature on a VT1200 vibratome (Leica Biosystems) and stored in cryoprotectant (0.1 M PB, 30% glycerol, 30% ethylene glycol) at −20°C. For immunofluorescence analysis, 40 μm sections were washed in TBS for 10 min three times and then blocked and permeabilized for 1 h at room temperature with 10% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and 0.1% Triton X- in TBS. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody (Chicken anti-Green Fluorescent Protein; 1:500; ab13970, Abcam) for GCaMP staining in 2% normal donkey serum, 0.1% Triton X- in TBS. Sections were then washed for 10 min in TBS three times and incubated in secondary antibody (Goat anti-Chicken Alexa Fluor 488; 1:500; A-11039, Invitrogen) for 1 h. Sections were then washed for 10 min in TBS three times and mounted on microscope slides. Images were obtained using an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus IX81 Microscope, Boston Industries, Inc. Walpole, MA) equipped with 10× objective lens.

Slice preparation

Whole-cell recordings were performed on 4–6-month-old D1:cre mice. These mice underwent stereotaxic injection of cre independent Chronos virus in the MGB and cre dependent tdTomato virus in the dTS to allow for identification of dSPNs as well as selective stimulation of auditory thalamic terminals. After 4–5 weeks to allow for viral expression, mice were threat suppression conditioned for 3 days and were euthanized by cervical dislocation on day 4. These mice were decapitated and coronal 250 μm-thick slices were prepared on a vibratome (VT1200; Leica, Sloms, Germany) in oxygenated ice-cold cutting-artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing 194 mM sucrose, 30 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 6 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, and 10 mM D-glucose (pH 7.4, 290 ± 5 mOsm). Slices containing the dTS, indicated by the presence of the basolateral amygdala, were collected and transferred to oxygenated normal ACSF containing 125.2 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.3 mM MgCl2·6 mM H2O, 2.4 mM CaCl2, 0.3 mM NaH2PO4, 0.3 mM KH2PO4, and 10 mM D-glucose (pH 7.4, 290 ± 5 mOsm) at 34 °C and allowed to recover for at least 40 min before electrophysiological recordings.

Whole-cell patch clamp recording

The recording chamber was continuously perfused with normal ACSF (1.5–2 mL/min) at 34°C. Electrophysiological recordings were performed on an upright Olympus BX50WI differential interference contrast microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 40× water immersion objective and an infrared video camera. Visualization of dTS dSPNs expressing tdTomato was achieved with 555 nm light illumination. Patch pipettes (5–6 MΩ) were pulled using P-97 puller (Sutter instruments, Novato, CA) and filled with solution indicated below. Whole-cell patch clamp recordings were performed with a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Förster City, CA) and digitized at 10 kHz with InstruTECH ITC-18 (HEKA, Holliston, MA).

Intrinsic membrane properties were measured in current clamp mode using a pipette solution containing 115 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgCl2, 20 mM KCl, 2 mM MgATP, 1 mM Na2-ATP, and 0.3 mM GTP (pH = 7.3; 280 ± 5 mOsm). To induce current-voltage responses, 500 ms duration somatic step currents were injected through the patch pipette. Rheobase was obtained by ramp current injection from 0–800 pA at 200 pA/s. In voltage clamp mode, glutamatergic synaptic transmission was measured at −70 mV holding potential in the presence of 25 μM picrotoxin with a pipette solution containing 120 mM CsMeSO3, 5 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 1.1 mM EGTA, 2 mM Mg2+-ATP, 0.3 mM Na-GTP, 2 mM Na-ATP, 10 mM TEA-Cl (pH = 7.3, 280 ± 5 mOsm). Miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) were detected in the presence of (1 μM TTX).

To induce evoked corticostriatal synaptic transmission, concentric tungsten electrode (WPI, Sarasota, FL) was placed on the corpus callosum and electrical stimuli were delivered by Master-9 (A.M.P.I., Jerusalem, Israel) with stimulation intensity adjusted by Iso-Flex (A.M.P.I.). To standardize stimulation conditions, we 1) used consistent electrode placement, so that for both EPSC and IPSC recordings, we adhered to anatomical landmarks for electrode placement. EPSC stimulation was consistently delivered to the corpus callosum at a fixed distance from the recording site, while local IPSC stimulation was precisely positioned within a defined radius (<100 μm) from the cell body to minimize variability in fiber recruitment. 2) To ensure that the number of recruited fibers was comparable between groups, we analyzed the input-output (I-O) relationships normalized to their respective maximal responses. For both eEPSCs and eIPSCs, the normalized curves exhibited a nearly identical trajectory, suggesting that the sensitivity to stimulation and the fiber recruitment profiles were consistently matched across the Control and ST groups.

Optical stimulation (470 nm) of auditory thalamic terminals was achieved by LED illumination (Colibri 2, Zeiss, Germany) through 40× objective at 10–80% of light intensity controlled by Master-9. To adjust optical stimuli to allow comparison between slices and recording sites, we first increased the light intensity (5 msec pulses) to find the maximal response as in Figure 9B. Then, we decreased the light intensity to the half-maximal intensity response.

To measure NMDA components, cells were held at +70 mV and currents were measured 50 ms after the time of optical stimulation. 25 μM of the NMDA antagonist APV was bath applied to pharmacologically isolate AMPA currents. Long-term depression of auditory thalamus to dTS dSPN synapse was induced by 10 Hz presynaptic stimulation (3000 pulses for 5 min) paired with +70 mV postsynaptic depolarization. Voltage gated ion channel currents were examined in the presence of TTX (1 μM) and synaptic blockers. Inward-rectifying (Kir) and A type potassium currents (Ka) were measured using K-gluconate pipette solution. Kir currents were induced by 1 s step voltages (Vh = −70 mV -150 to −50 mV + 10 mV increments), and Ka currents were measured by 0.5 s step voltages (Vh = −100 mV, 0 - +120 mV, +10 mV increments). CsMeSO3 pipette solution was used to measure voltage-gated Ca2+ currents with 100 ms step voltages (Vh = −110 mV, −70 - +40 mV, +10 mV increments). Data was sampled at 20 kHz and filtered at 4 kHz. The ion currents were analyzed after leak subtraction using P/4 protocol. All chemicals for ACSF, pipette solution, and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Tocris. GABAergic miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) were recorded in the presence of 25 μM APV, 10 μM CNQX, and 1 μM TTX. Cells were held at −70 mV with pipettes containing 140 mM CsCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgATP, 1 mM Na2-ATP, 0.3 mM GTP and 5 mM QX314 (pH = 7.3, 280 ± 5 mOsm). To induce evoked IPSCs, the stimulus electrode was placed ∼100 μm away from the recorded neurons.

Data was acquired using WINWCP software (developed by John Dempster, University of Strathclyde, UK) and analyzed using Clampfit (Molecular Devices), Igor Pro (Wavemetrics, Lake Oswego, OR), and MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) and graphed with Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc, CA). The violin plots are displayed as median ± quartiles and line graphs are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data in the text are expressed as mean ± SEM. Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used for group comparison of distribution of miniature events (mEPSC and mIPSC). The statistical tests used are listed in the figure legends and text. Unless indicated, two-way ANOVA and non-parametric unpaired t-tests were used to compare groups for all figures.

Biocytin diffusion and morphological analysis

Direct pathway SPNs were identified by somatic tdTomato expression and whole-cell patch clamp recording was performed. One cell per slice was filled with 0.1–0.2% of biocytin for at least 15 min. Slices were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and transferred to phosphate buffered saline (PBS). After three washes in PBS, slices were transferred to Tween -tris buffered saline (TTBS) (0.6%) with streptavidin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 or DyLight 649 (1:200) and incubated for 72 h. Mounted slices were dried overnight and then a coverslip with an anti-fading mounting medium (Fluoromount G) was applied. Images were acquired using the Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a 63x/1.4 NA Zeiss lens. Whole-cell images were obtained at 0.15 μm pixel resolution with 0.5 μm z-steps. For planar Sholl analysis, a maximum projection of the z-stacks was computed, and binary masks were derived by manually tracing the dendrites. Using the SNT toolbox for analysis of neuronal morphology in ImageJ, skeletonized cell masks were analyzed at every 1.2 μm. Furthermore, soma size, primary dendrites and the total number of branch points were counted manually. For dendritic spine density analysis, high magnification images were obtained from dendritic segments with 0.14 μm pixel resolution with 0.14 μm z-steps. Dendritic spine density was counted on five dendritic segments (28–32 μm medium length) of each cell at an average distance of 66.2–70.0 μm from the soma.

Behavioral procedures

Threat suppression/Pomotion conditioning

Mice were handled for 10 min each day for three days before threat-suppression, threat-promotion or control conditioning. Subsequent conditioning took place over 3 days and recall took place 24 h after the last conditioning session. Conditioning and recall sessions took place in behavioral chambers (MED Associates, Vermont, USA) housed within a sound-proof box and were recorded with infrared camera. All tone and shock deliveries were triggered via an Arduino and photometry and camera were triggered simultaneously via a Master-9 (A.M.P.I, Israel). Tones produced by the Arduino were in the form of buzzer-like square waves.

Threat-suppression conditioning consisted of a 2-min habituation period followed by a delivery of 4 tone CS’s (continuous, 1000 Hz, 70 dB spl, 20 s + green LED) then 4 shock foot shock (2 s, 0.6 mA)). The CS and foot shock presentations were separated by a pseudo-random interval of (70–120 s). The order CS and foot shock presentations were reversed on day 2 (4 shocks followed by 4 tones at a pseudo-random interval). Threat-promotion conditioning consisted of the same habituation period and then 4 CS’s co-terminating with a foot shock which was delivered in the same pseudo-random interval. Tone alone controls received 4 CS presentations at the same intervals as threat-suppression conditioned mice but did not receive any foot shocks. The recall session for threat-suppression conditioned mice and tone alone controls took place in the original conditioning context (metal bar floors with no added odor) and consisted of 10 presentations of CS in the absence of foot shock at 1-min intervals. The retrieval session for threat-promotion conditioned mice took place in a new context (plastic floor with peppermint odor) and consisted of the same 10 CS presentations in the absence of foot shock. Freezing during the 20 s CS presentation and the 20 s period prior to CS presentation (preCS) was measured using ANY-maze video tracking system (version 4.99z). ANY-maze “freeze score” was re-termed for this paper to “movement score” since greater freeze score values corresponded to greater movement. This re-terming was done to avoid potential misinterpretation of this value. The criterion used to define freezing onset was a movement score below a threshold 60 for at least 2 s and terminated when movement score rose above a threshold of 70. The freezing threshold movement score of 60 was subtracted from the movement traces in Figures S4I–S4X so that movement score values below zero represent freezing time points while values above zero represent non-freezing time points.

Local infusion of drug during conditioned threat-suppression

Mice were implanted bilaterally with cannulas 1.25 mm above the dTS (see coordinates above). After 1 week of recovery from surgery, an internal guide was inserted that extended 1.25 mm beyond the cannula tip into the dTS and 0.2 μL infusions of vehicle (30% DMSO in saline), a combination SCH23390 (3 mg/mL) + sulpiride (0.3 mg/mL) dissolved in 30% DMSO, or TTX (5 ng/ml) dissolved in saline were performed using a Hamilton syringe and a syringe infusion pump (Harvard Apparatus Model 11, Holliston, MA, USA) at a rate of 0.1 μL/min. Infusions were performed one hemisphere at a time and after each infusion, the internal guide was left in place for 2 min to allow drug diffusion. 15 min after drug infusion, mice underwent conditioned threat suppression as described above. This was repeated for 3 days of conditioning. On the recall day, no drugs were infused and freezing and movement responses during preCS and CS periods were analyzed.

Optofluid drug infusion procedure

D1:cre mice were injected with GCaMP6f in the dTS and optofluidic fibers were implanted 0.1 mm above injection site. After 4–6 weeks of viral expression mice were threat-suppression conditioned for 3 days. Then, on day 4, 6, and 8 mice were infused with 0.2 μL of saline, TTX (5 ng/mL in saline) and a combination SCH23390 (3 mg/ml) + sulpiride (0.3 mg/mL) dissolved in 30% DMSO respectively at a rate of 0.1 μL per minute 15 min prior to drug training session. The session consisted of four 2 s 0.6 mA shocks at 1-min intervals followed by ten 20 s threat suppression cues (continuous, 1000 Hz, 70 dB, 20 s + green LED) delivered at 1-min intervals. To avoid effects due to drug diffusion to other regions, only the first tone is presented in Figure 7, and data for the subsequent tones are in the supplementary raw data. Calcium traces for threat-suppression cue and shock in all photometry experiments, including optofluidic drug infusions, were averaged and waveform analysis was used to determine times when the 95% confidence interval did not contain a Z score of 0 and where the averaged traces were significantly different from each other.91 This approach provides within-subject analysis of behavior and photometry results and decreases the variability caused by differences in learning or GCamP6f expression between mice.

Shock or tone calcium response assessment under anesthesia

The following day after conditioning recall, mice expressing GCaMP6f and optical fibers implanted in the dTS were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane. Mice were then placed on a stereotaxic frame and anesthesia was maintained at 2% isoflurane. For shock delivery, mouse hind paws were lightly clamped with smooth alligator clips and attached to a Coulbourne shock generator. Five shocks (1 s, 0.6 mA) were delivered at 1-min intervals. For tone delivery, a speaker was positioned near the mouse’s head and five tones (continuous square wave 1000 Hz, 70 dB SPL, 20 s) were played at 1-min intervals. GCaMP responses were recorded and averaged together. After completion of the experiment, mice were euthanized for histological validation.

Photometry data acquisition

A commercial fiber photometry apparatus (Doric Lenses) was used for recording calcium signals. The system consisted of a console connected to a computer, photometric multipliers tubes (PMTs), a four-channel programmable LED driver, and two LEDs at 405 and 465 nm connected to fluorescence dichroic mini cubes via 0.22NA 200 μm optical fibers. We measured bulk fluorescence from the dTS using a single optical fiber for both delivery of excitation light and collection of emitted fluorescence. To measure GCaMP6f fluorescent changes reflecting neural activity we used 465 nm excitation. Meanwhile 405 nm excitation was used to measure changes reflecting artifacts such as autofluorescence, movement, and bleaching. Files were analyzed using MATLAB (MathWorks).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical methods

Data were plotted with Prism 9 (GraphPad) or MATLAB and analyzed statistically with Prism 9 (GraphPad). Parametric tests (t tests; ANOVAs followed by Tukey, Dunnett’s or Holm-Sidak post hoc tests) were used in cases where data met assumptions of normality and equal variance. In cases where data did not conform to these assumptions, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank or Mann-Whitney rank sum tests; Friedman’s repeated-measures ANOVA on ranks followed by Student-Newman-Keuls post hoc tests) were used. The violin plots are displayed as median ± quartiles and line graphs are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data in the text are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance is denoted in figures as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, with the specific test used for each comparison indicated in the corresponding figure legend. Detailed statistical analysis can be seen is Table S2.

Analysis and quantification of photometry data

To facilitate the comparison of fiber photometry traces across multiple animals, we applied a multi-step process. First, the raw 465 nm and 405 nm channel data were individually converted into normalized change of fluorescence (ΔF/F) traces using the formula:F(t)−F0(t)F0(t)where F(t) is the original signal and F0(t) is a control trace generated by fitting the signal using least mean squares method. The ΔF/F trace from the 405 nm channel was then subtracted from the ΔF/F trace from the 465 nm channel to correct for artifacts of movement, bleaching, and autofluorescence. Next, standardized z-scores were calculated using the expression:Z(t)=f(t)−μσwhere μ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation of the ΔF/F trace. The z-scores were normalized around a specific time window. For CS delivery, the selected time window began 20 s prior to CS onset and ended 20 s after CS offset. For foot shock and threat recall termination, the selected time window begins 5 s prior to event onset and ended 10 s after shock onset. The average Z score for the preCS or pre-event period was then subtracted from all points within the time window and the resultant traces were then averaged together across animals. AUCs for each trace were computed by numerically integrating the curve using the trapezoidal method. To determine if any observed sex differences in the statistical tests were of sufficient power, a standard threshold of 0.8 was used.

To determine time periods of significance during CS or shock presentation, bootstrapped confidence intervals (bCI) were computed for each trace based on a previously published method(1), and bars were plotted above traces to illustrate periods where z-scores were or significantly different between traces or significantly different from 0 and either above 0.5 or below −0.5. Briefly, waveforms were randomly resampled with replacement (1000 iterations) to obtain bootstrapped means. 95% confidence interval limits were derived based on the percentiles of the sampled distribution and expanded by a factor of n/(n−1) to counter small sample narrowness bias. Periods where confidence interval limits did not contain 0 (baseline) for at least 1/3 s were considered significant transients.91
