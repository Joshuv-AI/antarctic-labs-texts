Corrosion fatigue life prediction of multiple austenitic stainless steel grades in high-temperature, high-pressure water environments using a GA-optimized neural network
Authors: Harsh Kumar Bhardwaj, Mukul Shukla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Predicting the corrosion fatigue life of austenitic stainless steel (AusSS) in high-temperature, high-pressure water environments remains a complex task due to the intricate interaction of thermal, mechanical, and environmental factors, along with the scarcity of comprehensive experimental data. Traditional empirical fatigue models often fail to capture these multifactorial effects, limiting their predictive accuracy. This study presents a novel machine learning framework using a Genetic Algorithm Optimized Neural Network (GAONN) to enhance corrosion fatigue life prediction. The proposed model is applied across five AusSS grades, 316, 316LN, 316NG, 304, and 308L, offering generalized predictions across different alloy compositions and operational scenarios. Key input parameters include environmental factors such as temperature, pressure, and dissolved oxygen (DO), mechanical properties like strain rate and amplitude, and metallurgical attributes such as stacking fault energy (SFE). The GAONN model demonstrates excellent predictive accuracy on the test set, achieving an R2 value of 94.4%, a mean-squared error of 0.014, a root mean-squared error of 0.120, a mean absolute error of 0.105, and a mean absolute percentage error of 3.6%. These results represent a significant improvement over classical corrosion fatigue life models. Additionally, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis identifies temperature, pressure, and strain amplitude as primary contributors to corrosion fatigue life, in agreement with metallurgical theory. This work establishes GAONN as a transparent, accurate, and scalable tool for assessing corrosion fatigue life in next-generation nuclear energy systems.

Introduction

As global energy demands continue to rise and the need for carbon emission reduction intensifies, nuclear power has emerged as a crucial component of sustainable energy solutions. Among various reactor types, the pressurized water reactor (PWR) dominates the nuclear industry, accounting for nearly 70% of operational reactors worldwide1. Compared to a boiling water reactor (BWR), a PWR operates under higher pressure and uses dissolved hydrogen in the coolant, leading to distinct corrosion fatigue mechanisms such as hydrogen-induced cracking and oxide film degradation. These unique environmental conditions make PWR a particularly relevant and scientifically compelling focus for investigating corrosion fatigue behavior and structural integrity of reactor materials. A key material used in these reactors is AusSS, which is essential for critical components such as primary coolant pipelines, reactor internals, steam generator tubing, heat exchangers, and fuel cladding2. The widespread use of AusSS is attributed to its superior mechanical properties and resistance to corrosion. However, during prolonged operation, these components experience cyclic loading due to processes such as plant shutdowns, restarts, thermal fluctuations, and flow-induced vibrations, which can lead to structural failure3. Furthermore, next-generation nuclear reactors introduce additional challenges, particularly regarding corrosion fatigue exacerbated by liquid metal corrosion4. Addressing these concerns necessitates the development of accurate predictive models to assess fatigue behavior under corrosive operating conditions, ensuring long-term structural integrity and reactor longevity.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that high-temperature, high-pressure water environments significantly degrade the fatigue performance of AusSS. This degradation is driven by the combined effects of cyclic loading and metal corrosion, influenced by several key factors such as strain amplitude, strain rate, load ratio, temperature, pH, and the concentrations of DO and dissolved hydrogen (DH). Extensive research has been conducted to understand the deformation behavior and fatigue failure of AusSS under these conditions. Chopra et al.5,6 investigated fatigue resistance in light water reactor (LWR) coolant environments and found a significant reduction in fatigue life compared to air. Their findings indicate that lower DO levels accelerate fatigue crack initiation and propagation, particularly when temperature and strain amplitude exceed critical thresholds and strain rate drops below a minimum limit. Similarly, Hong et al.7 observed that lower strain rates contribute to increased cyclic stress hardening, higher bulk dislocation density, and an inverse relationship between striation spacing and strain rate, all of which exacerbate fatigue degradation. The interaction between mechanical strain and corrosive environments further promotes environmentally assisted cracking, a primary degradation mechanism in reactor materials.

Further studies have explored that both stabilized and non-stabilized stainless steels exhibit fatigue degradation in PWR environments, with nuclear-grade alloys such as 316NG and titanium-stabilized steels displaying distinct fatigue characteristics compared to conventional 304 and 316 stainless steel8. Zhang et al.9 examined the corrosion fatigue behavior of 316LN stainless steel in 325°C water and found that fatigue life decreases linearly with decreasing strain rate due to increased environmental damage. Their crack morphology analysis revealed micro-crack formation and hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity at low strain rates, whereas high strain rates resulted in mechanical shear fracture and crack branching. The presence of a thin oxygen penetration layer near the crack tip indicated early-stage oxide film formation, which influences crack propagation. Kamaya10 further established that crack initiation in PWR environments is governed by strain localization, dislocation interactions, and oxide film formation, with fatigue life in PWR water reduced to approximately 30–40% of that in air when the strain range exceeded 0.8%. This reduction was primarily attributed to the acceleration of crack growth. Cho et al.11 investigated the low-cycle fatigue behavior of 316LN stainless steel in 310°C deaerated water and reported primary hardening across all conditions due to increased dislocation density and dynamic strain aging. Their study also highlighted hydrogen-induced cracking within the dislocation structure and fatigue surface, further reducing fatigue life, particularly at lower strain rates.

To quantify these degradation mechanisms, several classical empirical models5,8,12 based on fracture mechanics have been developed to predict the corrosion fatigue life of AusSS. However, accurately estimating fatigue life in high-temperature, high-pressure water environments remains a challenge due to the complex interplay of multiple influencing factors. These complexities render single-variable models inadequate. Traditional fatigue strain versus life (ε-N) curves, typically derived under fixed environmental conditions, often lead to inaccurate predictions and require frequent recalibration with new experimental data. Moreover, the scarcity of corrosion fatigue data limits the ability to capture real-world variability, while the high cost and time-consuming nature of fatigue testing further impede the development of reliable predictive models. In response to these limitations, ML techniques offer a promising alternative. ML models are well-suited to capture complex, nonlinear relationships among multiple variables and can perform effectively even with limited datasets. The growing availability of ML tools across various programming languages, along with increasingly user-friendly interfaces, has further enhanced their accessibility and applicability in this domain.

With the continued advancement of data-driven algorithms, their application in predicting material corrosion and fatigue behavior has shown considerable promise. In numerous cases, particularly those involving neural network models, these approaches have demonstrated higher accuracy and reliability compared to the classical empirical models13. Kamrunnahar and Urquidi-Macdonald14,15 highlight the effectiveness of artificial neural networks (ANN) in corrosion science, showcasing their capability to analyze complex datasets and predict corrosion behavior with greater efficiency than classical empirical models. Their findings suggest that ANN can accurately simulate both general and localized corrosion, making it a dependable tool for estimating corrosion rates in diverse industrial settings. Kim et al.16 employed the ANN to assess the oxidation resistance of Ni-based superalloys by examining weight changes during cyclic oxidation tests. The ANN model demonstrated superior accuracy in predicting mass gains compared to the response surface methodology. Additionally, it effectively analyzed the influence of individual and interacting alloying elements, facilitating the optimization of superalloy compositions for improved high-temperature oxidation resistance.

Feng et al.17 developed a deep convolutional neural network model to predict corrosion fatigue life, integrating data augmentation through Borderline Synthetic Minority Oversampling and factor analysis with eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) to enhance accuracy. By minimizing the need for extensive fatigue testing, this approach improves the reliability assessment of engineering components and contributes to the digitalization and intelligent analysis of fatigue behavior in offshore platform welded joints. Beyond life prediction, ML has also shown considerable potential in fatigue crack growth (FCG) analysis, further highlighting the broader utility of data-driven approaches in structural health monitoring. For instance, Falaakh et al.18 developed an ML-based approach to predict and analyze the corrosion FCG rate of AusSS in the high-temperature water environment. Among the six supervised ML models evaluated, the categorical boosting model demonstrated the highest accuracy, surpassing classical empirical models. Using the SHAP method, the study uncovered hidden patterns, nonlinearities, and feature interactions, enhancing the understanding of corrosion FCG behavior. Wang et al.19 investigated the corrosion FCG rate of 316SS and 304SS stainless steel in simulated high-temperature, high-pressure PWR environments. Various ML models, including Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), were utilized for data mining and modeling. XGBoost outperformed the other models across all evaluation metrics, demonstrating greater flexibility and predictive accuracy compared to empirical equations. The SHAP analysis further validated the model’s reliability, aligning predictions with theoretical stress corrosion behavior and highlighting key influencing factors such as cold working, temperature, and stress intensity.

Feng et al.20 employed ML models combined with symbol regression (SR) to improve corrosion fatigue life prediction for T91 steel and 316L stainless steel in lead–bismuth eutectic (LBE) environments. The integration of SR enhanced the performance of all ML models, with the ANN achieving the greatest performance boost. Furthermore, the trained ANN model was successfully transferred to predict 316L SS fatigue life with a 50% reduction in training data, showcasing its efficiency in model training and deployment. In a subsequent study, Feng et al.21 introduced a physics-guided neural network (PGNN) to model the LBE-induced corrosion behavior of ferritic/martensitic steels. By embedding oxide layer growth physics into the network structure and applying a sparse neural architecture aligned with empirical laws, the PGNN achieved superior predictive performance and physical interpretability compared to conventional ML models. When integrated with a particle swarm optimization algorithm, the PGNN further enabled the design of optimized F/M steel compositions with experimentally validated improvements in LBE corrosion resistance. Similarly, Zhang et al.22 developed a two-stage equation for hydrogen-assisted FCG using Balanced Iterative Reducing and Clustering Using Hierarchies clustering and SR. By incorporating key variables and validating the model under various conditions, it demonstrated superior accuracy compared to six existing crack propagation models. The study enhances fatigue life prediction in hydrogen environments, contributing to safer pipeline design and operation. Jiang et al.23 developed a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN)-based model to improve the prediction of corrosion fatigue life for 316 AusSS in high-temperature, high-pressure water environments. By incorporating key environmental factors, loading conditions, and SFE, the model enables direct fatigue life prediction. Comparative analysis with experimental data demonstrated a significant accuracy improvement, reducing the prediction error factor from 5 to 3.

This study addresses key research gaps in the corrosion fatigue life prediction of AusSS under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions simulating PWR environments. While prior studies have primarily focused on isolated effects of strain rate, temperature, and DO, the complex synergistic interactions among these variables remain insufficiently captured. Existing empirical and ML models are often developed under controlled environments, limiting their applicability to actual reactor settings, and there is a noticeable scarcity of high-resolution, long-term experimental data for model training and validation. While neural networks offer promise in capturing complex fatigue behavior, their “black box” nature often impedes interpretability, hindering a deeper mechanistic understanding of damage evolution. The generalizability of these models across different AusSS grades, including stabilized and non-stabilized variants, has also received limited attention, as has their applicability to advanced reactor coolants such as sodium or lead. Furthermore, long-term fatigue life prediction remains difficult due to frequent model recalibration requirements and the absence of in-situ validation within operational reactor settings.

To address the identified research gaps, this study proposes an ML-based framework to accurately predict the corrosion fatigue life of AusSS under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions representative of simulated PWR environments. The framework explores a range of conventional ML algorithms alongside optimized neural network architectures, which are rigorously evaluated using established performance metrics to identify the most effective predictive models. The structure of the paper is organized as follows: (1) A review of classical empirical fatigue life prediction models to establish a baseline for comparison (Section “Review of existing life prediction methods of corrosion fatigue”); (2) Compilation and preprocessing of corrosion fatigue life data for various AusSS grades, accompanied by an overview of the theoretical foundations of the ML models employed (Section “Modeling techniques”); (3) A comprehensive performance evaluation of all ML models, benchmarked against classical empirical approaches to assess predictive accuracy and robustness, followed by SHAP-based interpretability analysis of the optimal model to elucidate the influence of individual features (Section “Results and discussion”). (4) Conclusions and key findings of the study (Section “Conclusions”).

Modeling techniques

Data preparation and preprocessing

This study utilizes a curated dataset comprising 88 corrosion fatigue life data points for five distinct grades of AusSS, 316, 316LN, 316NG, 304, and 308L, sourced from well-established literature5–12,27,28. Although relatively limited in size, the dataset is highly diverse, encompassing a wide range of material compositions and experimental conditions representative of PWR environments. Due to the resource-intensive nature of generating corrosion fatigue data under such stringent environments, acquiring large-scale datasets remains a key challenge in this domain. Each data point corresponds to controlled uniaxial, strain-controlled fatigue tests conducted under tension–compression loading with a load ratio of -1, enabling consistent evaluation of key mechanical factors such as strain amplitude and strain rate. The environmental variables, including water temperature, pressure, and DO concentration, were meticulously controlled to ensure data reliability and relevance. As shown in Table 2, the dataset includes seven input parameters in addition to SFE, categorized into two groups: loading and environmental factors. The output parameter is fatigue life, defined as the number of cycles to failure. The loading factors include \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\upvarepsilon }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upvarepsilon }_{\text{a}}$$\end{document}, while the environmental factors encompass water temperature, pressure, pH value, and concentrations of DO and DH. Although pH and DH concentrations are potentially influential factors, they were excluded from this study due to a significant proportion of missing values, as shown in Table 2. Specifically, both parameters exhibited more than 50% missing values, which presents a challenge for reliable data-driven modeling. To investigate the impact of imputing missing values, we conducted a supplementary analysis using mean imputation for the DH and pH columns. Mean imputation was selected for its simplicity, computational efficiency, and ability to maintain dataset structure without introducing outlier values31. It helps preserve the central tendency of a variable, thereby minimizing distortion during model training. To evaluate its impact, an XGBoost regressor32 was applied to three scenarios: (i) inclusion of all input features without any imputation (i.e., with missing values present), (ii) application of mean imputation to the DH and pH columns, and (iii) exclusion of these two feature columns. Interestingly, the model achieved an R2 value of 84.8% in both the first and second cases, indicating that mean imputation did not significantly alter performance. However, when the DH and pH columns were entirely removed, the model’s accuracy improved to 86.1%, suggesting that imputing highly sparse features may dilute model fidelity in small datasets. These findings reinforce the decision to exclude features with excessive missingness, as also supported by recent studies that caution against imputing features with low data coverage due to risks of bias and variance inflation33.

ML algorithms

ML algorithms are powerful computational tools designed to analyze data, identify underlying patterns, and generate predictions or informed decisions based on learned relationships. These algorithms are broadly classified into two primary categories: supervised and unsupervised learning. In the context of corrosion fatigue life prediction, supervised learning is predominantly employed due to its reliance on labeled datasets, which enable models to learn and predict outcomes for unseen data with high accuracy. Common supervised learning algorithms utilized in fatigue life analysis include Linear Regression35, Decision Tree (DT)36, RF37, and XGBoost38. Additionally, ANNs are widely adopted for their exceptional capability to model complex, nonlinear relationships through a flexible architecture inspired by the human brain, consisting of interconnected processing units known as neurons39.

In this context, the present study introduces a comprehensive predictive framework for estimating the fatigue life of materials subjected to the elevated temperature and pressure conditions characteristic of PWR environments. The framework incorporates a set of conventional ML models, namely Elastic Net Regression (ENR)40, GPR41, and SVR42, alongside ANNs augmented by metaheuristic optimization techniques, including Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)43, Genetic Algorithm (GA)44, and Simulated Annealing (SA)45. These methods were strategically selected for their unique strengths in modeling high-dimensional, complex datasets typical of fatigue life studies. To ensure a fair and robust comparison, hyperparameter optimization was conducted using both grid search with k-fold cross-validation46 and randomized grid search47. Further methodological details are provided in Section “ML models”.

ENR combines the regularization techniques of Lasso (L1) and Ridge (L2) regression to manage multicollinearity and facilitate variable selection effectively40. This makes it particularly suitable for scenarios where the number of predictors exceeds the number of observations. The principal hyperparameters for ENR are the mixing parameter, which controls the balance between L1 and L2 penalties, and the regularization parameter, which regulates the strength of the overall penalty. These parameters are typically optimized through cross-validation to mitigate overfitting and enhance interpretability.

GPR, a non-parametric and probabilistic regression technique rooted in Bayesian inference41, models distributions over functions, offering both predictions and associated uncertainty estimates. It is particularly effective in capturing intricate nonlinear relationships within small to medium-sized datasets. The performance of GPR heavily depends on the choice of covariance function (kernel), such as the Radial Basis Function (rbf), along with key parameters like the length scale and signal variance, which define the smoothness and variability of the function. These parameters are usually optimized through maximum likelihood estimation or cross-validation.

SVR adapts the foundational principles of Support Vector Machines to regression tasks by identifying a function that approximates the target outputs within a specified error margin42. Key hyperparameters include the regularization parameter (C), which balances model complexity and training error; the kernel type (e.g., linear, polynomial, rbf); the kernel coefficient, which governs the flexibility of the model; and the ε parameter itself. Grid search combined with cross-validation is employed to fine-tune these hyperparameters and enhance the model’s generalization capabilities.

In the pursuit of enhancing the performance of ANNs, metaheuristic optimization algorithms have gained significant traction due to their proficiency in navigating high-dimensional, non-convex search spaces. One such algorithm, ACO, draws inspiration from the collective foraging behavior observed in real ant colonies, where simple agents cooperate via indirect communication through pheromone trails. Within the context of ANN optimization, ACO is leveraged to identify optimal configurations of the network, including connection weights, layer structures, and other hyperparameters, which are often difficult to fine-tune using traditional gradient-based methods alone43. ACO operates by simulating a population of artificial ants that construct solutions iteratively, guided by the accumulated pheromone intensity and problem-specific heuristics. Critical algorithmic parameters such as the number of ants and the number of iterations must be carefully calibrated. These parameters govern the balance between exploration (global search for diverse solutions) and exploitation (refinement of promising areas in the search space), which is vital to prevent premature convergence and ensure robust optimization performance.

GA, rooted in the principles of natural selection and evolutionary biology, is widely employed to optimize ANNs configurations by iteratively evolving a population of candidate solutions44. Each individual in the population encodes a potential solution, such as a set of network weights, architectural parameters, or training hyperparameters that undergo evolutionary operations like selection, crossover (recombination), and mutation across successive generations. These operations enable the algorithm to explore and exploit the solution space effectively. Several key hyperparameters highly influence the performance and convergence behavior of GA. These include the population size, which determines the breadth of search and genetic diversity; the crossover rate, which governs the probability of combining genetic material from parent solutions; and the mutation rate, responsible for introducing random variations and preventing premature convergence. Proper calibration of these parameters is essential to maintain a balance between exploration (searching new regions of the solution space) and exploitation (refining high-quality solutions).

SA is a stochastic optimization technique inspired by the annealing process in metallurgy, where a material is heated and then gradually cooled to attain a state of minimal internal energy45. In the context of optimizing ANNs, SA is utilized to fine-tune weights and biases by exploring the solution space in a controlled probabilistic manner. Unlike deterministic algorithms, SA allows for the acceptance of worse solutions with a probability governed by a temperature-dependent acceptance function, which enables the algorithm to escape local minima and explore a broader region of the solution landscape. The algorithm’s performance is highly sensitive to several hyperparameters, including the initial temperature, which dictates the extent of early-stage exploration; the cooling schedule or cooling rate, which controls the rate at which the temperature decreases; and the stopping criterion, which defines when the search process terminates, either after a fixed number of iterations or once the system reaches a temperature threshold. Effective tuning of these parameters is critical to achieving a proper balance between exploration (accepting suboptimal moves to traverse the solution space) and exploitation (refining high-quality solutions).

By integrating these diverse ML and optimization techniques, each rigorously tuned for performance, the developed fatigue life prediction framework offers a robust and accurate tool for modeling material degradation under extreme operational conditions. This comprehensive approach effectively captures the complex interactions governing corrosion fatigue, thereby enhancing predictive reliability and supporting more informed structural integrity assessments.

Although numerous studies have highlighted the predictive capabilities of ML models, they often overlook the underlying information these models capture, which is essential for gaining deeper insights into the problem. Due to their complexity, ML models are frequently regarded as “black boxes,” making their internal decision-making processes difficult to interpret. However, recent advancements in ML explainability techniques have enabled a better understanding of how these models generate predictions. One of the most widely adopted methods is the SHAP technique48, introduced by Lloyd Shapley, which has gained significant recognition for its ability to provide insights into feature contributions. Results and discussion

Performance evaluation of the classical models

In this study, classical empirical models, namely the Langer equation, ANL model, and IMR model, are initially employed to predict the fatigue life of AusSS under high-temperature, high-pressure simulated PWR environments. These models serve as foundational benchmarks to evaluate their ability to capture the underlying fatigue behavior and to provide a comparative baseline against more advanced ML-based predictive methods. Consistent with the methodology detailed in Section “Data preparation and preprocessing”, the same training and test data subsets are employed across all models to ensure a fair and consistent evaluation of predictive performance.

Figure 3 presents parity plots for all three classical models, with experimental fatigue life values plotted against model predictions on a logarithmic scale. The plots include factor-of-two and three error bands to assess the deviation between predicted and actual fatigue lives visually. Among the three models, the Langer equation, which serves as a foundational model derived from fatigue tests in air, shows moderate performance. With R2 values of 0.580 for the training set and 0.603 for the test set, it exhibits notable scatter, especially outside the factor-of-two error bounds25. The Langer equation, as presented in Eq. 2, is based solely on a strain-life (ε–N) relationship derived from fatigue data obtained under air or inert testing conditions24, typically using monotonic, strain-controlled fatigue data. Its formulation incorporates only mechanical parameters such as strain amplitude and fatigue life, but it omits key environmental variables such as temperature49, pressure50, strain rate28, and concentration of DO51, all of which are well-established contributors to corrosion fatigue degradation in nuclear reactor environments. As demonstrated by the SHAP analysis in Section “SHAP analysis”, pressure and temperature emerged as two of the most influential variables affecting fatigue life under simulated PWR conditions. However, the Langer equation lacks any mathematical terms to represent these factors. This omission makes it fundamentally unsuitable for capturing environmentally driven degradation mechanisms such as increased solubility of corrosive species, pressure-enhanced hydrogen diffusion, and thermally accelerated stress corrosion cracking, which are critical to accurate fatigue life prediction in PWR systems.Fig. 3Experimental data and predictions of (a) Langer equation, (b) ANL model, and (c) IMR model.

Experimental data and predictions of (a) Langer equation, (b) ANL model, and (c) IMR model.

To address these limitations, the ANL model builds on the Langer framework by integrating environmental variables into its fatigue life estimations, including temperature, strain rate, and DO concentration (Eq. 4)25. This enhancement results in improved predictive performance, with R2 values of 0.814 and 0.644, and a lower MSE of 0.058 and 0.092 for training and test sets, respectively. Despite its improved accuracy, the model shows indications of overfitting, reflected in the performance disparity between the training and test sets. Nevertheless, the ANL model more effectively captures the corrosion-fatigue interactions critical to simulated PWR environments, where temperature fluctuations, chemical concentrations, and varying flow conditions significantly impact structural integrity. Similarly, the IMR model exhibits predictive performance comparable to the ANL model, with identical R2 and MSE values across both training and test datasets. This similarity is attributed to their shared empirical foundations and use of comparable datasets. The IMR approach employs a base fatigue curve for air conditions, supplemented by a Fen correction factor that explicitly accounts for the effects of strain rate, temperature, and DO concentration (Eq. 11)12. However, the IMR model also shows signs of overfitting, as evidenced by the discrepancy between training and test performance.

Although the ANL and IMR models demonstrate enhanced predictive accuracy compared to the Langer model, they reveal notable limitations when applied to simulated PWR environments. Their reliance on extensive corrosion-fatigue testing across varied environmental conditions demands significant experimental efforts and extended development timelines, hindering scalability for practical engineering use. While the inclusion of environmental correction factors allows for a more realistic representation of fatigue degradation in high-temperature, high-pressure aqueous environments, their accuracy remains fundamentally restricted by empirical formulations. These observations underscore the broader limitations of classical empirical models in capturing the complex, multivariate fatigue mechanisms prevalent in simulated PWR settings, where temperature gradients, variable strain rates, seawater chemistry, and dynamic loading interact synergistically. This emphasizes the necessity for advanced ML approaches, which leverage data-driven insights to predict fatigue behavior with greater flexibility and precision in challenging simulated PWR environments.

ML models

Optimization of hyperparameters

ML algorithms typically include a variety of hyperparameters that influence how the model learns52. While most ML libraries provide default values for these hyperparameters, often yielding satisfactory results, tuning them can sometimes lead to improved model performance32,34,53. However, given the potentially large number of hyperparameters, exhaustive tuning can be time-consuming. To manage this, the present study focused on optimizing only the most critical hyperparameters for each algorithm while retaining default settings for the rest as specified by the ML libraries. To enhance model generalization and reduce overfitting risks, a grid search approach was employed in combination with fivefold cross-validation. In this setup, the training data was split into five equal parts; the model was trained on four parts and validated on the remaining one. This process was repeated five times, with each partition serving once as the validation set. The final performance metric was derived by averaging the results across all folds, providing a robust estimate of the model’s generalization capability. It is important to distinguish cross-validation from regularization techniques such as dropout and L2 regularization. While dropout mitigates overfitting by randomly deactivating neurons during training, and L2 regularization penalizes large weight magnitudes to control model complexity, cross-validation does not alter the training process. Instead, it serves solely as an evaluation mechanism to assess the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data54.

In addition to these conventional ML models, this study also investigated three neural network variants enhanced using metaheuristic optimization techniques: the Ant Colony Optimized Neural Network (ACONN), Genetic Algorithm Optimized Neural Network (GAONN), and Simulated Annealing Optimized Neural Network (SAONN). Training neural networks is inherently challenging due to issues like local minima, overfitting, and poor generalization55. These challenges often stem from the rapid convergence of gradient descent and the randomness of initial weight and bias assignments56. To mitigate these problems, each neural network was fine-tuned using the Keras Tuner framework. Parameters such as the number of hidden layers, neurons per layer, activation functions, learning rate, and momentum term (β_1) were optimized with the goal of minimizing prediction error and improving generalization. All networks were trained on data subsets with random initialization of weights and biases, up to 100 epochs, using MSE as the loss function to ensure consistent cross-validation outcomes. A batch size of 42 was selected to balance computational efficiency and stable gradient updates. Although training and validation losses were tracked for all models, detailed convergence behavior is illustrated only for the GAONN model (Fig. 4). The GAONN training curve shows a steep decline in both training and validation losses during the early epochs, followed by a stable plateau, indicating efficient learning and successful convergence. Additionally, the close correspondence between training and validation losses suggests that GAONN generalized well with minimal signs of overfitting.Fig. 4Training versus validation loss curve for the GAONN model.

Training versus validation loss curve for the GAONN model.

The ACONN and SAONN models were designed with significantly simpler architectures compared to the GAONN, reflecting trade-offs between computational efficiency and modeling capacity. The ACONN architecture consisted of a single hidden layer comprising 41 neurons, employing the ReLU activation function. It was trained with a relatively high learning rate (0.02) and a β_1 value of 0.6, which promoted faster convergence but constrained the model’s ability to capture complex, nonlinear interactions. Similarly, the SAONN model also featured a single hidden layer, albeit with 52 neurons, and utilized the tanh activation function to encourage smoother and more centered activations. It was trained with a moderate learning rate (0.01) and a β_1 value of 0.7, resulting in reasonable generalization performance. However, its shallow structure limited its ability to model intricate synergistic behaviors among input variables.

In contrast, the GAONN architecture was deliberately optimized for greater depth, featuring seven hidden layers with 70 neurons each. This deeper structure was designed to facilitate hierarchical feature extraction and effectively capture complex relationships among mechanical loading, environmental conditions, and microstructural characteristics. A very low learning rate (0.0006) was used during training to ensure stable convergence across the highly non-convex optimization landscape, enabling the model to learn subtle temporal and spatial patterns associated with fatigue behavior over prolonged training cycles. The depth of the network allowed for progressive abstraction, where successive layers learned increasingly complex representations. This is consistent with established findings in deep learning literature57, which emphasizes the advantage of deep architectures in modeling highly nonlinear systems. In GAONN, each additional layer enhanced the network’s ability to model the intricate, coupled degradation mechanisms critical to accurate fatigue life prediction under high-temperature, high-pressure reactor conditions.

Overall, the results underscore the importance of aligning hyperparameter configurations with the model’s complexity and optimization methodology. While simpler linear models benefited from light regularization and fewer tuning parameters, neural network performance was highly dependent on architectural depth, activation functions, and the selected metaheuristic optimization strategy.

Additionally, in the case of ACONN, the algorithm was configured with a relatively small number of ants (n_ants = 10) and iterations (n_iterations = 5), prioritizing broad exploration of the solution space over intensive local search efforts32,43. This configuration helps mitigate the risk of early convergence to suboptimal solutions, making it particularly effective for tackling complex, high-dimensional optimization problems. For GAONN, the GA employed a population of 20 individuals over 10 generations. This setup was selected to maintain genetic diversity throughout the evolutionary process while reducing the likelihood of premature convergence32,44. The moderate population size enables a wide-ranging exploration of the hyperparameter space, and the limited number of generations ensures computational efficiency and minimizes the risk of overfitting early solutions. The SAONN model adopted a simulated annealing approach, initiating the process with a high initial temperature of 100 and a gradual cooling rate of 0.95 across 50 iterations32,45. This configuration encourages extensive exploration during the early stages by allowing the occasional acceptance of suboptimal solutions, facilitating escape from local optima. As the temperature decreases, the algorithm transitions toward exploitation, progressively refining the solution.

Evaluation of ML models

The conventional ML models (ENR, GPR, and SVR), along with the metaheuristically optimized neural network models (ACONN, GAONN, and SAONN), were all trained on the same dataset used for evaluating the classical empirical models described in Section “Performance evaluation of the classical models”. Each model was configured with its respective optimized hyperparameters, as detailed in Section “Optimization of hyperparameters”, to ensure a consistent and unbiased performance comparison. The predictive capability of each model was assessed using an independent hold-out test dataset. Figure 5 presents a comparative overview of the predicted versus experimentally measured fatigue life values for both the training and test data subsets across all six models. The performance of these models is assessed using two criteria: the prediction accuracy and the distribution of predicted values relative to the parity line. A low degree of scatter around the parity line indicates a model’s strong capability to learn and generalize the complex, nonlinear patterns inherent in the fatigue life data34. Among the evaluated models, the SVR model demonstrated the weakest predictive performance, yielding R2 values of 0.862 for the training set and 0.833 for the test set, along with a relatively high MSE of approximately 0.043. As depicted in Fig. 5(c), the SVR predictions show considerable dispersion, particularly across the test dataset, with a notable number of data points falling outside the factor-of-two error bounds. This level of scatter highlights SVR’s limited capability to capture the complex, nonlinear, and multivariate characteristics of corrosion fatigue behavior under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions representative of PWR environments. While SVR can model moderately nonlinear systems, it struggles with the intricate dependencies introduced by variable loading conditions, environmental degradation, and microstructural evolution. Furthermore, its sensitivity to hyperparameters and inability to adaptively model threshold effects or rare-event phenomena reduce its generalization ability when exposed to noisy, high-dimensional fatigue data.

In contrast, the GAONN model significantly outperformed all other models, achieving the highest predictive accuracy with R2 values of 0.958 for the training set and 0.944 for the test set, along with the lowest MSE of 0.013 and 0.014 for the training and test sets, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(e), GAONN predictions are densely concentrated along the parity line, with nearly all data points residing within the factor-of-two error bounds, indicative of the model’s high fidelity and excellent generalization capability. The superior performance of GAONN is attributed to its evolutionary optimization framework, in which a GA is used to explore the neural network’s hyperparameter space, including the number and size of hidden layers, activation functions, learning rate, and momentum terms44. Unlike gradient descent-based training approaches that are often prone to local minima, GA performs global optimization through biologically inspired mechanisms such as selection, crossover, and mutation. This allows GAONN to discover network configurations that are well-suited to capturing the inherently nonlinear and coupled relationships among mechanical loading variables (e.g., strain amplitude, strain rate), environmental factors (e.g., temperature, pressure, dissolved oxygen), and intrinsic material descriptors such as SFE. These input parameters collectively govern critical degradation phenomena such as dynamic strain aging, oxide-induced crack tip shielding, and hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity, mechanisms known to exhibit nonlinear thresholds and synergistic effects in corrosion fatigue. By effectively learning these complex patterns, GAONN not only delivers accurate predictions but also maintains robustness in the presence of experimental scatter, rare failure modes, and material variability, which are common challenges in corrosion fatigue life estimation.

The results of this study highlight the increasing potential of ML-based frameworks for accurately predicting corrosion fatigue life under simulated PWR conditions, corroborating recent literature that emphasizes their ability to capture complex, multivariate degradation phenomena20,23. These environments are governed by intricate synergistic interactions between cyclic mechanical loading and aggressive aqueous conditions, leading to highly nonlinear and interdependent damage mechanisms10,11,27,51,58,59. In contrast, classical empirical models, such as the Langer equation, ANL model, and IMR formulation, are constrained by rigid, deterministic structures that lack the flexibility to adapt to diverse operational and environmental inputs. As a result, their predictive accuracy diminishes when applied to the multifactorial degradation scenarios typical of PWR systems. These limitations reinforce the need for advanced, data-driven modeling approaches, particularly those leveraging evolutionary optimization techniques, to enhance predictive reliability and support informed decision-making in structural integrity assessments.
