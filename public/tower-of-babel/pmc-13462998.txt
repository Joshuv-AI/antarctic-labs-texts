Transforming ceramic residues into circular economy solutions in geotechnical engineering
Authors: Messaouda Bencheikh, Assia Aidoud, Ghania Boukhatem, Mehmet Serkan Kırgız, C Ramesh Kannan, Krishnaraj Ramaswamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating ecological concerns related to ceramic residue disposal have encouraged their valorization in geotechnical engineering as a sustainable alternative. This study evaluates the effectiveness of ceramic waste calcined at 900 °C (CW), used alone or with 2% ordinary Portland cement (OPC), for improving low-bearing-capacity clay soils. CW was incorporated from 3 to 18%, and its effects were assessed using methylene blue value (MBV), Atterberg limits (LL, PL, PI), compaction (MDD), unconfined compressive strength (UCS), California Bearing Ratio (CBR), and compressibility (Cc, Cs). Response surface methodology (RSM), supported by ANOVA and regression modeling, was used for optimization and prediction. Results showed significant improvements: MBV decreased by 59.19% at 18% CW; MDD increased by 5.57% and up to 7.55% with OPC. The liquid limit decreased by 23.83% at 9% CW, while the plastic limit dropped to 21.9% at 15% CW and to 20.45% with 12% CW + 2% OPC (− 37.71%). The lowest PI was obtained at 9% CW, reaching 24.06%, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 28.44% compared with the untreated clay. The CBR increased by 351.67% with OPC. UCS improved by 321.71% at 9% CW and reached 698.76% with 9% CW + 2% OPC after 56 days. Compressibility parameters were reduced, with Cs and Cc decreasing by 73.56 and 59.49% at 9% CW, and by 79.58 and 72.46% with OPC. The developed models showed high predictive accuracy (R² ranging from 0.77 to 0.98, p < 0.0001). These findings confirm that calcined ceramic waste, particularly combined with OPC, is an effective and sustainable solution for soil stabilization, supporting circular economy practices.Keywords: Calcined ceramic waste (CW), California bearing ratio (CBR), Geotechnical properties, Ordinary Portland cement (OPC), Response surface methodology (RSM), Soil stabilizationSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

In civil engineering and construction practice, soil remains a fundamental medium for establishing structural foundations1,2. The suitability of a project site is largely dictated by the bearing capacity of its subsurface strata, with preference given to soils capable of sustaining heavy loads3. Yet, it is important to recognize that natural soils are not universally fit for construction purposes1. Accelerated urban expansion has intensified the demand for infrastructure worldwide4, compelling engineers to confront challenging ground conditions where conventional soils prove inadequate. Such problematic soils- whether expansive or collapsible- often exhibit diminished load-bearing capacity, leading to structural distress in foundations and deterioration of pavements5. Expansive soils, in particular, undergo pronounced volumetric changes with moisture fluctuations, swelling upon hydration and shrinking when desiccated. This cyclic swell-shrink behavior has been documented as a major source of damage to civil infrastructure6. Countries such as Australia, the United States, Canada, India, South Africa, and China have reported widespread occurrences of expansive soils7. The differential movements induced by these soils can severely compromise infrastructure integrity, raising pavement surfaces and destabilizing clay subgrades under seasonal moisture variations7. The destructive impact of expansive soils on built environments has been extensively chronicled in global literature6,8–10. To mitigate these adverse effects, soil stabilization techniques are employed to enhance the engineering properties of expansive soils11. As defined by Oyediran and Kalejaye12, stabilization encompasses chemical, mechanical, and biological interventions that improve soil performance for engineering applications. This process modifies the soil’s three phases- solid particles, pore water, and void air- yielding durable characteristics suitable for long-term use. Techniques range from particle binding through hydration to waterproofing strategies that reduce susceptibility to moisture13. Among these, physicochemical modification of clay-rich soils is often favored for its durability14.

Parallel to geotechnical challenges, the escalating generation of municipal solid waste has necessitated integrated management systems15. Industrial production, while resource-intensive, also offers opportunities for recycling and sustainable development16. In particular, construction and demolition debris- responsible for nearly 75% of global pollution-has emerged as a critical environmental concern17,18. Recycling and reuse are increasingly promoted as pathways to sustainable growth19, with construction waste valorization gaining recognition as a viable solution20–23. Waste-derived powders such as quarry dust6, marble dust24–28, aeolian sand29, polyvinyl chloride30, barite powder31, pyroclastic dust32, and brick powder33 have demonstrated effectiveness in stabilizing expansive soils. Ceramic waste (CW), generated in vast quantities during tile polishing, represents another promising stabilizer. Global accumulation of ceramic residues is estimated to exceed 22 billion tons34, with landfilling remaining the dominant disposal method despite its environmental hazards. In Algeria alone, nearly 6 million tons of ceramic materials are consumed annually, producing 15–30% waste35. The absence of standardized protocols for incorporating CW into soil-based structures perpetuates reliance on landfilling36,37. Given that quartz and silicate minerals- key constituents of ceramics- form the bulk of Earth’s crust38, recycling CW offers a sustainable alternative to waste management. Fired at 700–1000 °C, ceramic residues are rich in silica, alumina, and iron oxides, comprising nearly 90% of their composition39. Studies have shown that CW additions can reduce permeability, increase strength, and lower compressibility in soils40–43. For instance, Balegh et al.35 reported significant improvements in mechanical properties with CW dosages up to 30%, while Oruabena and Elechi44 demonstrated enhanced compaction and CBR values in Nigerian soils with optimal CW content. Similarly, Deboucha et al.42 highlighted the synergistic benefits of CW and Portland cement (OPC) in reducing settlement and boosting unconfined compressive strength. Given the inherent variability of soils and the limitations of conventional trial-and-error stabilization methods45–47, advanced optimization strategies are increasingly adopted. Response surface methodology (RSM), a statistical modeling approach, enables systematic evaluation of multiple stabilizers to identify optimal blends48–52. Applications of RSM in soil stabilization have yielded promising results, such as Olgun’s optimization of lime, rice husk ash, and fiber mixtures53, and Boukhatem et al.’s valorization of marble powder and lime in Algerian clays24.

Building on this framework, the present study investigates the potential of ceramic waste calcined at 900 °C, used independently or in combination with 2% OPC, to enhance the geotechnical properties of low-plasticity clay from El Kherba (Mila, Algeria). CW dosages ranging from 3% to 18% were evaluated through tests on Atterberg limits, compaction, UCS, CBR, and compressibility. The findings revealed substantial improvements in dry density, UCS (up to 698.76% after 56 days), and CBR (up to 351.67%), alongside reductions in plasticity and swelling potential. Employing RSM, regression models with high predictive accuracy (R² values ranging from 0.77 to 0.98, p < 0.0001) were developed, underscoring CW- alone or blended with OPC- as a sustainable and cost-effective stabilizer for expansive soils and a viable pathway for industrial waste valorization.

Materials and methods

Clayey soil

On August 7, 2020, two earthquakes measuring 4.9 and 4.5 on the Richter scale hit the province of Mila in eastern Algeria. These seismic events caused indirect damage to more than 2,000 homes, either partially destroyed or collapsed, within a 10-kilometer radius of the epicenter. The El Kherba neighborhood was among the most severely affected areas in terms of structural damage. This study aims to conduct a geotechnical evaluation of the soil in the impacted zone, which has since been abandoned by its inhabitants. On the basis of these results, appropriate technical solutions are proposed to enhance the geo-mechanical behavior of soil. The soil samples were collected from a depth of 1.5 m below the ground surface and transported to the laboratory for analysis. The soil was carefully spread in a clean plastic container and placed in a well-ventilated area to air dry. Once dried, visible impurities such as insects, small branches, leaves, and other organic debris were meticulously removed via tweezers. Finally, the soil was sieved through a 5 mm mesh to facilitate further analysis. In accordance with the relevant French requirements, a battery of tests was carried out to evaluate the physical characteristics and expansion potential of the soil. Calcined ceramic waste powder (CW)

The calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) was sourced from a porcelain manufacturing plant in Guelma, Algeria. It was thoroughly washed with warm water to remove residual impurities, including surface contaminants such as oils, and then air-dried. Subsequent calcination at 900 °C ensured the complete removal of volatile and organic fractions, resulting in a stable mineral powder. The material was then dried under the sun. Once completely dry, the waste was ground using a disc mill to reduce it into finer fragments.

The ground ceramic was subsequently subjected to calcination at 900 °C for two hours, which induced physicochemical transformations that enhanced its properties, including mechanical strength, durability, and chemical stability, thereby increasing its reactivity. Depending on the mineralogical composition of the ceramic waste, calcination at temperatures above 700 °C may promote the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) into calcium oxide (CaO)58,59. However, the presence of calcite was not directly confirmed in the present study, and this transformation is therefore discussed as a potential mechanism reported in the literature for calcite-bearing ceramic materials. Although this process consumes energy, the use of CW helps reduce reliance on conventional hydraulic binders (cement/lime), whose carbon footprint and production energy intensity are significantly higher. Thus, the overall balance aligns with a circular economy approach by valorizing a waste material that, in its raw (“as-received”) form, exhibits insufficient mechanical performance for demanding geotechnical applications58,59. The resulting powder was then sieved through an 80 μm mesh to achieve a fine particle size. To maintain its quality, the ceramic powder was carefully stored in airtight containers before and after use to prevent contamination from moisture and other atmospheric elements. Many researchers have reported that the specific gravity (CW) ranges between 2.27 t/m3 and 2.82 t/m360. The specific gravity (CW) used in this study was determined to be 2.59 t/m3. The elemental analysis serves as a preliminary characterization to confirm the presence of key components required for stabilizing reactions. These findings highlight the physicochemical transformations induced by calcination.

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC)

In this study, we used a cement of the type Portland cement CEM I 42.5 R, which is delivered by the company BISKRIA CIMENT and complies with the Algerian standard NA442-201361 inspired by the European standard EN197-162. Its density is 3.15 t/m3, which is intended for high-performance concrete and rapid short-term resistance.

Experimental procedures and mixture design

The method used to prepare the mixtures (clay + CW, clay + CW + 2% OPC) is simple but requires appropriate laboratory equipment, including trays, small shovels, an oven, an electronic balance, sieves, etc. For each test and to ensure optimal homogeneity, a quantity of sieved clay with various particle sizes, depending on specific requirements, is first manually mixed in a stainless-steel tray under ambient temperature conditions (22–31 °C). In the first phase, increasing amounts of CW, ranging from 3% to 18% with 3% increments relative to the dry soil mass, are gradually added. These proportions were chosen for their economic advantages in soil treatment. In the second phase, the same quantities of CW are mixed with 2% OPC, which is also based on the dry soil mass. The prepared soil samples are then subjected to a systematic experimental procedure, including specific gravity tests, compaction tests, unconfined compressive strength (UCS) tests at different curing times (7, 14, 28, and 56 days), and oedometer tests. For each experimental condition, a minimum of three specimens were prepared to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results, and the average values were reported. The cylindrical specimens used for UCS tests were prepared with standard dimensions of 7.5 cm in diameter and 15 cm in height, in accordance with relevant standards. All mixtures were compacted at their OMC and MDD, as determined from the Proctor test, using standard compaction energy. The moisture content was carefully controlled during mixing, and distilled water was added to achieve the target water content. All plotted experimental data in the figures represent the mean values of at least three independent measurements (n≥3), with a typical standard deviation remaining within ± 5% for compaction and CBR tests, and within ± 8% for UCS testing, reflecting a low and acceptable geotechnical variability.

After preparation, the specimens intended for curing (UCS tests) were immediately wrapped in plastic film and stored under airtight conditions to prevent moisture loss. They were then kept under controlled laboratory conditions at a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C for curing periods of 7, 14, 28, and 56 days. The time interval between mixing and compaction/testing did not exceed 30 min to avoid premature reactions. The CBR and compaction tests were conducted immediately after sample preparation. This standardized protocol ensures consistency in experimental conditions and improves the reproducibility of the results.

Experimental design via the box–Behnken approach (BBD)

Response surface methodology (RSM) is a statistical and mathematical procedure used for optimizing and evolving problems where outcome variables are influenced by multiple influencing variables29,30,49,51,52,63–66. In this study, mixture formulation (% natural clay, 2% CW, and 2% OPC) was carried out via the Box–Behnken design (BBD) approach within the framework of RSM. Two statistical models were employed to analyze various data obtained from experiments conducted according to the BBD model, facilitated by Design Expert 13 software. The first experiment focused on natural clay with poor geotechnical properties and incorporated CW and OPC. The CW content was set between 0% and 18%, with an increase of 3%, while the OPC content was fixed at either 0% or 2%. Although the mixture components (clay, CW, and OPC) sum to 100% by mass, the statistical analysis treats CW content, OPC content, and curing time as independent factors within the Box–Behnken design framework. The clay fraction is not considered an independent variable and is implicitly determined by the selected levels of CW and OPC. This strategy avoids multicollinearity, as only the controlled factors are included in the regression models. Overall, this approach is consistent with RSM-based geotechnical optimization studies that rely on fixed-factor experimental designs rather than mixture regression models.

This experiment aimed to optimize the MDD and the CBR index. The second experiment aimed to optimize the UCS on the basis of the percentage of CW and the amount of OPC. The levels of CW and OPC were consistent with those of the first experiment (0% to 18% for CW with a step of 3%, and 0% or 2% for OPC). Furthermore, the curing periods considered for this second experiment were 7, 14, 28, and 56 days. Table 3 presents the complete dataset used in the model.

Results and discussion

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on the methylene blue value (MBV)

The corresponding results for untreated clay and clay treated with CWare illustrated in Fig. 3.

Influence of the CW percentage on the methylene blue value (MBV).

The methylene blue adsorption (MBV) test is a straightforward and reliable technique used to characterize the presence and behavior of clay minerals in soils69. It serves to identify whether clay minerals are active and potentially detrimental, as active clays exhibit swelling depending on their moisture content70. The MBV value is determined on the basis of the total quantity of methylene blue solution adsorbed. This method is widely applied in geotechnical engineering practice69,71–74. Clay particles with higher specific surface areas and stronger negative charges display greater cation exchange capacities, resulting in increased adsorption potential74. The MBV test in this study was performed in accordance with the French standard NF P 94 − 06875, following the procedures described in74. A progressive decrease in the MBV is observed with increasing percentage of CW incorporated into the clay, amounting to 26.47%, 38.73%, 46.93%, 50.98%, 59.19% and 59.19% for CW contents of 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, 15% and 18%, respectively. Beyond 15%, the MBV tends to stabilize. This reduction indicates a decrease in the content of active clay minerals in the soil. Indeed, CW acts as an inert material with a low specific surface area and a limited cation exchange capacity. Its incorporation dilutes the concentration of reactive clay particles in the mixture. As the CW content increases, more fine clay particles are replaced by less active CW particles, logically resulting in a lower amount of adsorbed CW. These findings are consistent with the results reported by El-Habacha et al.76, Laoufi and Bahar77, El-habacha et al.78, and Ait Hmeid79.

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on consistency limits

Figure 4 shows the evolution of PL, LL, and PI as a function of CW addition, either alone or combined with 2% cement (OPC). The natural clay has a high LL of 66.45%. In accordance with the AFNOR NF P 94 − 05180 standard, Atterberg limit tests were performed to ascertain the limits of consistency (LL, PL and PI) of the values of the soils and their mixtures. The incorporation of CW alone slightly reduces the LL, reaching 50.61% at 9% CW (a 23.84% decrease), before increasing beyond this threshold. When 2% OPC is added, the LL initially increases for low CW contents (3% and 6% CW + 2% OPC) before gradually decreasing and stabilizing at 60.84% with 18% CW + 2% OPC. This behavior is likely influenced by the cement’s reaction with water, altering the mixture’s plasticity. Additionally, increasing the CW content led to a gradual decline in the PL, reaching its lowest value at 15% CW, where the PL decreased from 32.83% (natural clay) to 21.9%, reflecting a 33.29% reduction. The presence of 2% OPC amplifies this decrease, with the PL decreasing to 20.45% at 12% CW, representing a 37.71% reduction. This trend is attributed to the formation of a porous and cohesive matrix due to cement hydration, which promotes particle aggregation and further reduces plasticity. The combined variation in LL and PL affects the PI. For CW-treated clay without OPC, the PI decreases progressively with increasing CW content and reaches 24.06% at 9% CW, whereas it reaches 33.62% for untreated clay, indicating a 28.44% reduction. For mixtures containing 2% OPC, the PI values are 49.66%, 50.15%, 38.66%, 41.71%, 36.04%, and 34.45% for 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, 15%, and 18% CW, respectively. Although the PI values of the OPC-treated mixtures remain higher than those obtained with CW alone, a general reduction in PI is observed at higher CW contents, reaching 34.45% at 18% CW + 2% OPC, which is close to the value of the untreated clay. The decrease in PI enhances soil workability, making it more friable and easier to manipulate.

Influence of the CW percentage on the Atterberg limits.

The addition of CW alone tends to reduce LL, PL, and PI, thereby improving the workability of the soil. This effect can be attributed to the greater specific surface area of CW (ρs = 2.59 t/m3) than that of clay (ρs = 2.17 t/m3 ), which promotes increased water adsorption. However, the water adsorbed on CWs does not act as an effective lubricant for clay particles, reducing their plasticity. Additionally, since CW is an inert material, it does not impart any plasticity to the mixture. Moreover, a lower PI indicates a reduced shrink-swell behavior for the treated soil81. The incorporation of CW with 2% cement (OPC) modifies the plasticity behavior of the mixtures through the interaction between CW and OPC, which, in the presence of water, forms a porous and resistant matrix. Although the PI values remain relatively high at low CW contents, increasing the CW content progressively reduces PI, reaching 34.45% at 18% CW + 2% OPC. This behavior indicates an improvement in the consistency characteristics of the treated soil at higher CW dosages, thus reducing soil plasticity. These effects are attributed mainly to cation exchange and the reduction in the diffuse double layer thickness, whereas the flocculation effect is relatively weak82. Consequently, ion exchange could promote mineral particle agglomeration and PI reduction83,84. Moreover, these interpretations should be considered as hypotheses based on the existing literature82–84. In addition, the observed improvements can also be explained by a filler effect, better particle packing (compaction), and the dilution of active clay minerals. These mechanisms are supported by the decrease in MBV and the increase in dry density.

Several researchers have observed similar variations, such as Chen and Idusuyi85, who stabilized lateritic soil by adding ceramic waste in proportions ranging from 0 to 30%. They reported that this addition resulted in a decrease in the LL, PL, and PI. Similarly, Saya Abdullah and Anoosheh86 investigated the influence of ceramic dust on the mechanical behavior of clayey soil from Erbil, northern Iraq, by incorporating two particle size fractions (passing through No. 40 and No. 10 sieves) at contents of 0%, 5%, and 10%. Their findings indicated that increasing the ceramic dust proportion led to reductions in the liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index. Similarly, H. Venkateswarlu et al.87 examined the impact of quarry dust on black cotton soil and reported decreases in both the liquid and plastic limits, irrespective of the amount of quarry dust added. These observations align with results obtained for fine soils of moderate plasticity, particularly in research focusing on the effect of dune sands86. Numerous other researchers, such as Indraratna88, Boukhatem et al.29,30, Jha & Sivapullaiah82, Anggraini et al.59, Abiola et al.1, Attoh Okine89, Sivapullaiah et al.90, and Bozbey et al.91, have reported similar findings.

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on compaction characteristics

The OMC and MDD results obtained from Proctor compaction tests on natural clay, clay mixed with CW, and clay mixed with CW and 2% OPC are presented in Fig. 5.

Influence of the CW percentage on the maximum dry density (MDD).

The Proctor test is essential for soils used in the road construction sector92. This method involves compacting a given volume of soil samples at varying water contents while maintaining constant compaction energy. In accordance with the NF P 94 − 09393 standard, the soil samples were mixed with predetermined quantities of admixtures to prepare the samples. This process aims to determine the MDD and the corresponding OMC94. The analysis of Fig. 5 highlights a progressive increase in the MDD with increasing percentage of CW, both in the presence and absence of OPC. The MDD of the natural clay is the lowest among all the values, with a value of 1.51 t/m³, indicating that the natural clay has a less dense and compact structure. The addition of CW alone led to a gradual increase in the MDD by 1.72%, 1.85%, 3.31%, 3.58%, 5.24%, and 5.57%, corresponding to 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, 15%, and 18% CW, respectively, reaching a maximum at 18% CW. This trend suggests that CWs improve the compaction of soil by optimizing its granular arrangement. When 2% OPC is added along with the CW, the MDD increases even further, showing a more pronounced improvement. For each corresponding percentage of CW, the addition of 2% OPC consistently results in a higher MDD than that of samples containing only CW, with increases of 2.12%, 3.64%, 4.77%, 6.43%, 6.95%, and 7.55%, respectively. These results demonstrate that OPC plays a key role in enhancing the compactness of the stabilized soil. This increase occurred because CW is a dense material with a density of 2.59 t/m3, which is greater than that of clay (2.17 t/m3). The addition of CW to clay increases the proportion of dense material in the mixture, leading to an increase in the MDD. The effect of CW on the MDD is more significant at lower percentages, as the addition of a small amount of dense material has a greater impact on the average density of the mixture. The added OPC reacts in the presence of water and forms a porous and strong matrix that binds the clay and CW particles. This means that the effect of % OPC on the MDD is generally more significant than that of CW alone, as it contributes to the formation of a denser and more compact structure. Chen and Idusuyi85 stabilized a lateritic soil by adding ceramic waste in proportions ranging from 0 to 30%. They reported that this addition led to a decrease in the OMC, whereas the MDD increased. Similarly, Saya Abdullah and Anoosheh84 reported the same results when treating clay from Erbil in northern Iraq, with ceramic dust particles passing through sieve No. 40 and sieve No. 10 in proportions of 0%, 5%, and 10%. Venkateswarlu et al.87 studied the effect of quarry dust on black cotton soil. The MDD was achieved with 10% quarry dust, whereas the OMC decreased with increasing quarry dust content. These findings are consistent with the conclusions of other researchers, such as Boukhatem et al.29,30 and Md Isa et al.95. Sabat and Bose96 also reported the same trend.

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on the immediate california bearing ratio (CBR) index

Figure 6 shows the influence of the CW percentage on the CBR index. For the CBR tests, a hydraulic press equipped with a torque ring is used to apply a maximum force of 60 kN with a displacement speed of 1.27 mm/min35. The immediate CBR index is determined immediately after compaction35.

Influence of the CW percentage on the CBR index.

As shown in Fig. 6, the addition of (CW) alone to clay leads to a significant increase in the immediate CBR index. Specifically, the CBR increases from 7.132% for natural clay to 12.52%, 26.21%, and 25.83%, representing increases of 75.54%, 267,50%, and 262.24% for CW contents of 3%, 6%, and 9%, respectively. Beyond 9% CW, a reverse trend is observed, with the CBR gradually decreasing compared with the peak value at 9% CW. This reduction reaches 47%, 66.45%, and 82.55% for CW additions of 12%, 15%, and 18%, respectively. Despite the decline, the CBR values remain significantly higher than those of untreated clay, increasing by 91.90% and 21.59% at CW contents of 12% and 15%, respectively. At a CW content of 3%, the CBR increases, indicating a significant improvement in the soil’s bearing capacity. This enhancement can be attributed to the filler effect of the CW42, which reduces the voids between clay particles, thereby increasing the density and cohesion of the mixture. At 6% CW, the CBR continues to rise, reflecting a remarkable improvement. This increase is likely due to pozzolanic reactions between the CW and the clay, which lead to the formation of cementitious compounds that reinforce the soil structure. The maximum immediate CBR value was obtained at 6% CW (26.21%). At 9% CW, the CBR remained very close to the maximum value (25.83%), indicating that both dosages provide substantial improvement in bearing capacity. This suggests that an excess of CW may limit improvement by increasing the proportion of nonreactive particles, thereby reducing the overall effectiveness of the mixture. As the CW content increases from 9% to 15%, the CBR begins to decrease. This behavior suggests that excessive CW acts as a destabilizing agent, creating zones of weakness and reducing the soil’s load-bearing capacity. These findings indicate that an optimal CW dosage of 6% maximizes the immediate CBR index, whereas 9% CW still provides comparable bearing-capacity improvement and was found to be more advantageous when considering the overall geotechnical performance of the stabilized soil, whereas excessive incorporation beyond this threshold leads to performance degradation. This trend, characterized by a peak between 6% and 9% CW followed by a decline, reflects the complex evolution of compaction parameters and plasticity reduction. The initial increase in CBR is closely linked to the improvement of granular arrangement (increase in MDD) and the reduction of the active clay fraction (decrease in PI and MBV). However, beyond 9%, although the density continues to rise, the excess ceramic fines act as unbonded particles that disrupt the cohesion of the clay skeleton. This decline suggests that excessive CW increases the proportion of non-reactive phases, creating zones of lower structural strength.These results are consistent with those reported by Balegh et al.35, who reported that the immediate CBR index continuously increases within the ceramic powder content range of 0–15%, resulting in a 10% increase in the bearing capacity. Furthermore, Deboucha et al.42 recommended that the optimal CW content should not exceed 15%, as adding 15% CW without cement results in a reduction in the CBR index and an increase in soil permeability. These observations are also supported by the findings presented in44,97. The immediate CBR values obtained for the same CW percentages combined with 2% OPC are presented in Fig. 6. In general, the results follow the same increasing trend observed when treating clay with CW alone but with significantly higher improvement rates. Specifically, the CBRs increase by 127.86%, 216.29%, 351.67%, 324.14%, 182.16%, and 69.63% for CW contents of 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, 15%, and 18% combined with 2% OPC, respectively. The maximum CBR value is recorded for a combination of 9% CW and 2% OPC. However, even for higher CW contents beyond 9%, the CBR values remain substantially elevated. This finding indicates that while high dosages of CW (≥ 15%) alone may reduce effectiveness, the incorporation of 2% OPC ensures continued and notable improvement in CBR performance, even at elevated CW levels, without the risk of performance degradation. This significant increase in the immediate CBR index across the various CW + 2% OPC dosages is attributed primarily to the synergistic effect of the two materials. The pozzolanic reaction of the CW, which is rich in silica (SiO2) and alumina (Al2O3), reacts with the calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂] released during OPC hydration to form calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium aluminate hydrate (C-A-H) compounds. These cementitious products increase soil cohesion and density by filling voids and strengthening the soil matrix. Moreover, the rapid hydration of OPC produces early-stage hydration products that contribute to the immediate stabilization of the soil, thereby significantly increasing its load-bearing capacity. Figure 7 shows the variation in applied pressure during the CBR test as a function of penetration for both untreated clay and samples treated with various percentages of CW.

Influence of the CW percentage on pressure as a function of penetration.

The pressure increases with increasing penetration across all the CW-treated samples. This trend indicates that CW-treated soils exhibit greater mechanical resistance, as highlighted by Mishra and Mishra98. The lowest resistance is recorded for natural clay, confirming its limited capacity to withstand applied pressure. For CW contents of 3%, 6%, and 9%, a moderate increase in pressure is observed, suggesting a gradual improvement in the mechanical strength with increasing CW content. Conversely, at higher concentrations of 12%, 15%, and 18%, the applied pressure slightly decreases compared with that of the 9% mixture but still remains higher than that of the untreated clay. The increasing pressure trend observed during the CBR tests of the CW-stabilized mixtures can be attributed to the greater specific gravity of the treated composites (2.59 t/m³) than that of the native soil (2.17 t/m³) and the pozzolanic reactivity of the CW, which contains active mineral phases. In the presence of water, these phases react with soil compounds to form cementitious products, especially calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H), which increase the rigidity and strength of the soil matrix. The curve corresponding to 9% CW shows the highest pressure at any given penetration depth, indicating optimal mechanical reinforcement at this dosage. These findings are in strong agreement with those reported in various studies29,30,99 and in the context of treating expansive clays with various types of industrial waste100,101.

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on the unconfined compressive strength (UCS)

Figure 8 shows the evolution of UCS for both natural clay and clay samples treated with varying percentages of CW for different curing periods (7, 14, 28, and 56 days). UCSis an important test for soil improvement, as reported by Sariosseiri and Balasingam. The UCS of the clayey soil was assessed through an unconfined compression test conducted on cylindrical samples, both before and after treatment. This evaluation aimed to analyze the effects of CW alone, as well as the combined effect of CW with 2% OPC and the influence of curing time (7, 14, 28, and 56 days) on the stabilized soil. The UCS of the untreated clay remains relatively low, approximately 146.59 kPa. With the incorporation of CW, the UCS gradually increases102.

Influence of the CW percentage on the unconfined compressive strength (UCS).

At a low dosage of 3% CW, the UCS remains comparable to that of natural clay during the early curing stages but notably increases by 238.60% after 56 days. A similar trend is observed with 6% CW, where the UCS increases significantly, reaching a 330.23% increase at 56 days. However, the treatment effect at 3% and 6% CW remains suboptimal, likely because of insufficient interaction between the CW and the clay matrix. At these levels, CW may act merely as a filler material without significantly increasing the strength. At 9% CW, a marked improvement in UCS was observed as early as 7 days, with a moderate increase of 32.56%, and reached a peak of 321.71% after 56 days of curing. This suggests that 9% CW promotes beneficial stabilization reactions. However, beyond this optimal content (i.e., at 12%, 15%, and 18% CW), the UCS begins to decrease. This reduction may be attributed to the formation of microcracks, possibly caused by a thermal expansion mismatch between the CW particles and the clay matrix. Such conditions may weaken interparticle bonding and reduce overall cohesion. In addition, the observed decrease is also related to a discontinuity in the cemented/clay matrix due to an excess of non-reactive ceramic fines. This excess creates weak interfaces within the compacted structure, acting as precursors for microcrack development under loading, which is consistent with the drop in UCS values observed beyond the optimum threshold. These findings highlight the strong influence of the curing time: longer curing durations lead to higher UCS values, which are particularly noticeable after 28 and 56 days. This improvement is likely due to progressive hardening reactions between silica (SiO2) from CWs and calcium (CaO) present in the soil, resulting in the formation of cementitious compounds such as calcium silicate hydrates (C-S-H), which significantly increase the strength of the soil. It should be noted that the interpretation of these improvements in terms of pozzolanic reactions is based on indirect evidence, including the chemical composition of CW and the observed mechanical trends. No direct microstructural analyses, such as XRD, FTIR, TG/DTA, or SEM, were conducted on the treated soils in this study. Therefore, these mechanisms should be considered as plausible hypotheses consistent with the literature, rather than experimentally confirmed phenomena.These results are consistent with the findings of Chand & Subbarao103, who reported that extended curing times contribute to the development of ultimate compressive strength. According to Daniyal and Shakeel104, CW constitutes approximately 54% of total construction waste. Owing to its high content of silica, alumina, iron oxide, and calcium oxide, ceramic waste is considered a promising material for improving subgrades in pavement construction Valdés et al.105. Additionally, Muthu Kumarand and Tamilarasan106 studied the effect of marble waste on soil behavior by adding 0% to 25% marble powder to clay in 5% increments. They reported that a maximum UCS of 215 kN/m² was achieved at a 15% marble powder content. At this level, the expansive soil transformed into a low-plasticity, silt-like material with improved strength properties. The effects of adding 2% OPC to the same percentages of CW at different curing periods (7, 14, 28, and 56 days) on UCS are illustrated in Fig. 9.

Influence of the CW percentage and 2% OPC percentage on the unconfined compressive strength (UCS).

A significant improvement in UCS is observed compared with that of the CW alone, especially after 28 and 56 days, highlighting the beneficial role of OPC in enhancing the hardening process of the mixture107. At 3% CW + 2% OPC, a 376.44% increase in UCS was recorded at 56 days. For 6% CW + 2% OPC, a consistent improvement is observed across all curing periods, reaching a 393.34% increase at 56 days. The maximum strength is achieved at 9% CW + 2% OPC, with a remarkable gain of 698.76%, indicating that this combination is optimal. Unlike CW alone, higher CW contents (up to 15–18%) combined with 2% OPC remain effective because of the cement’s binding effect, which promotes the development of mechanical strength. The addition of 2% OPC significantly enhances the efficiency of CW as a soil stabilizer through the formation of hydration products (mainly C–S–H and C–A–H gels), which improve particle bonding and strengthen the soil matrix. The lower UCS values observed for CW-only mixtures at high replacement levels are attributed to the excess of non-reactive ceramic fines, which may disrupt the continuity of the clay matrix and create weak interfaces within the compacted structure42. In conclusion, the combination of CW with 2% OPC represents a much more efficient stabilization solution than does CW alone, offering a promising alternative for improving clayey soils via recycled materials. These results are consistent with those reported by Deboucha et al.42, who demonstrated that under wet and submerged conditions, the CW–OPC–soil mixture led to a UCS increase of approximately 182%. Similarly, Deboucha et al.42 reported that adding 1.5% to 2% OPC with 5% CW improved the UCS of stabilized soils by approximately 72.3% to 176% under unsaturated conditions and up to 2 MPa under saturated conditions. Comparable findings were also reported by Muthu Kumar and Tamilarasan106.

Influence of calcined ceramic waste powder (CW) on the compressibility properties of clay soil

The compressibility characteristic Cs is one of the most common methods used to calculate the degree of expansiveness with reference to the volume change in the behavior of soil94 and the Cc. The aim of this experiment is to examine the consolidation behavior of clay soil samples under incremental vertical loads. This is done while enabling vertical drainage and confining the samples within a rigid enclosure29,30. This test was conducted on both the natural clay and the samples treated with CW at proportions of 3%, 6%, and 9%, either alone or combined with 2% OPC. The procedure followed the NF P 94 − 091108 standard, and compaction was performed at the OMC. The results concerning the compressibility characteristics (Cc and Cs) are presented in Fig. 10.

Influence of CW and 2% OPC on the swelling index (Cs) and compressibility (Cc).

The values of Cs and Cc are relatively high for natural clay, reaching 0.1229 and 0.1543, respectively. As the CW content increases progressively, a gradual decrease in Cs is observed by 16.68%, 41.33%, and 73.56% for CW contents of 3%, 6%, and 9%, respectively. When 2% OPC is incorporated alongside the CW, this reduction becomes even more significant, reaching 65.42%, 70.63%, and 79.58%, respectively. Cc shows a similar decreasing trend to that of Cs, with a steady and marked reduction as the percentage of CW increases, both when used alone and when combined with 2% OPC. The observed reductions in Cc are 8.49%, 37.91%, and 59.49% for 3%, 6%, and 9% CW, respectively. The reduction effect is further amplified with the addition of 2% OPC, reaching 33.90%, 38.76%, and 72.46%, corresponding to the respective CW percentages. The addition of CW led to a significant reduction in the compressibility indices (Cs and Cc), indicating an improvement in the stability of the treated soil. This decrease can be attributed to the interactions between the clay particles and the components of the CW, which reduce the water retention capacity and promote better soil structure. The incorporation of 2% OPC further enhances this reduction by promoting pozzolanic reactions94 and contributing to the formation of cementitious compounds that stabilize the clay matrix. This process increases soil cohesion and results in the development of a more rigid structure, thereby further reducing compressibility and swelling potential. These findings suggest that the combination of CW and OPC offers an effective solution for stabilizing clay soils, enhancing their mechanical performance and improving their resistance to volumetric changes. The optimal concentration for minimizing compressibility indices (Cs and Cc) was 9% CW combined with 2% OPC. This combination results in a swelling reduction of 79.58% for Cs and 72.46% for Cc, indicating a significant improvement in the mechanical behavior of the treated clay. The mixture of 9% CW + 2% OPC provided the best performance, reflecting a decrease in the expansive reactivity of the soil. This is attributed to better particle arrangement and a reduction in pore spaces through air compression and grain deformation. Boukhatem et al.29,30, Dumpa et al.109, Anand et al.110, and Zubair et al.111 used various waste materials for the treatment of expansive clays and reported similar findings. Furthermore, Koyuncu et al.112 demonstrated that adding up to 40% CW in the construction sector reduced the swelling pressure and potential by approximately 86% and 57%, respectively112. These results are consistent with those reported in the literature113,114 and by Balegh35, who reported that stabilization occurs when the ceramic powder content exceeds 15%. Therefore, the compressibility of ceramic powder particles is considered a critical factor compared with the compressibility of the composite skeleton in geotechnical calculations.

It is important to note that the results presented in this study are closely dependent on the selected experimental conditions. In particular, they are associated with the type of soil investigated (El Kherba soil), the use of a single source of CW calcined at 900 °C, and a constant dosage of 2% of OPC. Although the findings indicate a significant improvement in the geotechnical properties, this behavior remains strongly influenced by the mineralogical nature of the soil and the applied treatment conditions. Therefore, applying these results to other contexts, including different soil types, ceramic waste sources, or binder formulations, requires further investigation. Additional studies are thus needed to better understand the influence of these parameters on the behavior of stabilized soils.

Development of the regression model

When multiple regression analysis is used for a design matrix and responses, the reduced quadratic model is considered the best fit for the independent variables and responses59. In addition, by examining the prediction equations derived from the experimental data, the parameter estimations and trends can be clarified117. Key responses, including the MDD (Eq. 2), CBR (Eq. 3), and UCS (Eq. 4), can be estimated via the provided equations, which were created via the actual values of the factors (% clay, % CW, and % OPC). In the present study, it should be clarified that the regression equations presented in Eq. (2) to (4) are based on the coded form of the factors. In the coded model, the independent variables are dimensionless and range from − 1 to + 1, whereas in the actual (real) model, they are expressed in percentage (%). Therefore, the use of coded variables ensures model consistency and avoids obtaining non-physical values that could result from directly substituting percentage values into the Eqs. (2, 3 and 4)117.

The equations reflect the type of relationship between the input variables and their interaction effects on the MDD, CBR, and UCS. In Eqs. (2,3 and 4), the regression coefficients represent the magnitude and direction of the effect of each factor, as well as their interactions, on the response variables. Positive coefficients indicate a synergistic effect and denotes a directly proportional relationship, whereas negative coefficients reflect an antagonistic influence and an inverse relationship24,29,30,49 in relation to the responses (MDD, CBR, and UCS)24,29,30,117. The models were developed using least-squares regression based on experimental data obtained from the Box–Behnken design. The statistical significance of each coefficient was evaluated using (ANOVA), and only significant or near-significant terms were retained in the reduced quadratic models. The standard errors associated with the coefficients were monitored during model fitting to ensure the stability and reliability of the regression.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of ceramic waste (CW), an abundant byproduct of the construction sector in Algeria and worldwide, as a sustainable stabilizing agent for expansive clay soils. Experimental investigations were conducted on clayey soil from El Kherba (Mila, Algeria), in which CW was calcined at 900 °C for 2 h, with or without 2% ordinary Portland cement (OPC). Ceramic waste (CW) was incorporated at proportions ranging from 3% to 18% (by dry weight) to assess its influence on key geotechnical properties, namely, the methylene blue value (MBV), Atterberg limits (LL, PL, PI), compaction characteristics (MDD), unconfined compressive strength (UCS), California bearing ratio (CBR), and compressibility parameters (Cs, Cc). These responses were optimized via response surface methodology (RSM). The results indicate that CW, particularly when combined with a small percentage of OPC, significantly enhances soil strength and compressibility while reducing plasticity, thereby offering an effective and eco-friendly solution for soil stabilization in road and embankment applications.

Incorporating CW into expansive clay notably decreases clay mineral activity, with the MBV reduced by up to 59.19% at 15% CW. This improvement results from the low specific surface area and limited cation exchange capacity of CW, which limits adsorption and dilutes the active clay fraction, confirming its effectiveness in soil stabilization.

Incorporating CW into expansive clay notably decreases clay mineral activity, with the MBV reduced by up to 59.19% at 15% CW. This improvement results from the low specific surface area and limited cation exchange capacity of CW, which limits adsorption and dilutes the active clay fraction, confirming its effectiveness in soil stabilization.

The addition of (CW), alone or with 2% OPC, enhances the consistency of clayey soils by reducing the plasticity parameters. The LL decreased by up to 23.84%, the PL decreased by 37.71%, and the PI decreased by 28.44%. These reductions improve soil workability and mitigate the shrink–swell potential, mainly because of the inert nature and surface characteristics of CWs combined with the cementitious effect of OPC. Overall, the use of a CW–OPC blend represents an effective stabilizing strategy for clay soils.

Quadratic prediction models for important geotechnical parameters, including the MDD, CBR index, and UCS, were developed via statistical analysis via response surface methodology (RSM) based on a Box–Behnken design. The models achieved prediction accuracies of 98.02%, 85.45%, and 77.48%, respectively, demonstrating their reliability in estimating soil behavior. RSM effectively optimizes the mechanical properties of clay soils by relating operating variables to geotechnical responses. This approach offers robust results while minimizing the number of experimental tests, making it both cost-effective and efficient for improving the performance of low-capacity soils.

The use of CW in geotechnical engineering represents an innovative and environmentally friendly approach for improving expansive soils, while promoting the valorization of industrial by-products and sustainable construction practices. In this study, applying this treatment to the specific El Kherba clay soil (Mila), combined with a fixed 2% dosage of OPC and a calcination temperature of 900 °C, led to enhanced mechanical performance while reducing treatment costs. However, it is important to emphasize that these results, as well as the optimization achieved through RSM methodology, are strictly limited to the tested experimental conditions. Although this approach effectively aligns technical efficiency with economic and environmental sustainability, extending these findings to other soil types, different sources of ceramic waste, or alternative calcination conditions requires further investigation to confirm their broader applicability.
