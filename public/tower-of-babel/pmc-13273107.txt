Pay first, treat later: upfront payment requirements as a non-clinical barrier to timely emergency care in fragile and resource-constrained health systems
Authors: Ahmed Omar Siyad, Abdukadir Mohamed Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timely access to emergency care is essential for preventing avoidable illness, disability, and death. Yet in many low- and middle-income countries, patients and families may still be asked to make consultation fees, registration payments, deposits, or other forms of financial clearance before urgent evaluation or treatment proceeds. Using Somalia as an illustrative case while drawing broader lessons for comparable settings, this commentary examines how weak public financing, limited financial risk protection, facility dependence on user fees, and administrative payment processes may delay emergency assessment, stabilization, diagnostics, referral, and admission. It argues that payment-related delay should be understood as a health-system, governance, and equity problem rather than only a household financing problem. Because direct Somali bedside measurement remains limited, the paper frames this pathway as a policy-relevant systems hypothesis rather than a directly quantified patient-level effect. It concludes by proposing a practical reform agenda centered on triage-first and stabilize-first policies, feasible emergency waiver and reimbursement mechanisms, separation of billing from urgent clinical triage, phased financing reforms, and stronger regulation of payment practices that interfere with time-critical care in Somalia and comparable fragile settings.

Introduction

Emergency care is a core function of effective health systems because many acute conditions are highly time sensitive. Trauma, sepsis, acute respiratory compromise, obstetric emergencies, severe infections, shock, and other life-threatening presentations require rapid recognition, early stabilization, and prompt access to diagnostics and definitive treatment. Delays during the first minutes or hours of illness can worsen physiological instability and increase preventable morbidity and mortality [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

In many low- and middle-income countries, emergency care is delivered within financing environments marked by low public investment, limited insurance coverage, and persistent reliance on out-of-pocket spending at the point of service. In such settings, hospitals may depend on user fees to sustain daily operations, purchase consumables, and maintain staffing or diagnostic capacity. What appears at the bedside as a payment request can therefore reflect deeper weaknesses in financing, risk pooling, and governance [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Somalia illustrates these constraints. National policy documents report per capita health expenditure of approximately US$13, with out-of-pocket payments accounting for 46% of total health expenditure, and identify lack of financial access as a major barrier to care seeking. Recent Somalia-specific emergency care evidence further shows limited emergency and critical care readiness, with user fees reported as a common barrier to readiness, particularly in third-level hospitals and private-for-profit facilities [[10], [11], [12]].

The organization of care in Somalia makes this concern especially relevant. World Health Organization (WHO) reports that the private sector provides about 80% of curative care services in Somalia, that there is no national health insurance scheme, and that patients usually pay cash to access for-profit private facilities. A qualitative study from Mogadishu similarly found that ability to pay shapes access to care and that fees for registration, consultation, drugs, and medical supplies contribute to inequitable access. Although Somalia is the illustrative case, similar fee-based arrangements may affect emergency care in other fragile and resource-constrained health systems [13,14].

The central contribution of this commentary is not simply to restate that healthcare in Somalia is costly. Rather, it proposes that upfront payment requirements in emergency settings may operate as non-clinical barriers to timely assessment and stabilization, potentially inserting an administrative checkpoint into the pathway of acute care. Published Somali evidence documents financial exclusion, private-sector dependence, cash payment at the point of care, and user fees as barriers to access and emergency readiness; however, it has not directly quantified whether emergency patients are asked to pay before triage, how much time is lost before first assessment or stabilization, or how payment demands affect diagnostics, admission, or referral. This commentary therefore synthesizes the pathway linking financing constraints to payment-related delay and outlines practical reforms that could reduce avoidable harm while remaining realistic for Somalia and comparable settings [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

Discussion

Why payment requirements become embedded in emergency care

Upfront payment practices are rarely explained by a single factor. In many facilities they emerge from the interaction of weak public financing, inadequate pooled funding, irregular supply chains, limited emergency preparedness, and pressure to recover costs quickly. When hospitals lack predictable reimbursement for emergency services, registration fees, deposits, and payment verification can become normalized operational tools rather than exceptional measures [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

This creates a difficult ethical and managerial tension. Facilities need revenue to function, yet emergency care is the clinical area in which delay is least acceptable. When cost recovery mechanisms are placed ahead of stabilization, the burden of system underfunding is effectively transferred to acutely ill patients and their families at the moment of greatest vulnerability.

Stages of payment-related delay

Payment-related delay can occur at multiple points along the emergency care pathway, not only before the patient reaches a hospital. A clearer staged framework helps explain why the consequences may be severe even when the absolute delay seems short.

First, there may be a pre-facility delay. Families who anticipate high costs may postpone seeking care, attempt home management, borrow money, sell assets, or search for cheaper alternatives before deciding to travel. Transport itself may be delayed if funds are needed for fuel, ambulance use, or referral transfer.

Second, there may be an arrival or registration delay. At the facility entrance, consultation fees, registration charges, card fees, or proof of payment may be requested before full clinical review begins. In overcrowded or under-resourced settings, these procedures can become informal gatekeeping steps.

Third, there may be a triage and stabilization delay. Even when patients reach emergency units, payment verification or demands for deposits may delay or complicate timely access to airway support, oxygen delivery, fluid resuscitation, blood products, analgesia, or other immediate stabilization measures. In time-sensitive illness, such administrative delay may have clinically important consequences.

Fourth, there may be a diagnostic and treatment delay. Financial clearance may be required before laboratory tests, imaging, essential medicines, or procedures proceed. This can slow the transition from initial suspicion to definitive management.

Fifth, there may be a referral, admission, or escalation delay. Patients may remain in emergency areas while families search for funds for inpatient beds, surgery, critical care transfer, or interfacility referral. As a result, disease progression can continue before definitive care is secured.

Clinical, household, and equity consequences

The consequences of these delays extend beyond longer waiting times. Clinically, deterioration may continue before treatment begins, increasing the risk of complications, organ dysfunction, disability, and death. Delayed treatment can also increase the eventual intensity and cost of care, resulting in longer admissions and more resource-intensive late-stage management [4,5,15].

At the household level, direct payment requirements can produce acute financial stress as relatives leave the bedside to secure funds or guarantors. This may disrupt communication with clinicians and deepen emotional distress during emergencies. At a broader level, catastrophic expenditure and distress financing can worsen poverty and widen inequities, especially for poor, rural, displaced, and otherwise vulnerable households [8,9].

The public health implications are also substantial. When communities come to expect that emergency treatment depends on immediate ability to pay, trust in health services can erode. Over time, this may discourage early care seeking and reinforce geographic, social, and economic inequities in access to lifesaving services.

Policy and health system implications

Addressing payment-related delay requires action at policy, facility, and system levels. The most immediate priority is to establish a triage-first and stabilize-first norm in which emergency assessment and lifesaving stabilization are not contingent on prior payment. This principle should be reflected in facility protocols, managerial guidance, and, where possible, national policy or regulatory standards.

A reasonable concern is that triage-first and stabilize-first policies could become unfunded mandates if facilities are expected to provide emergency care without reimbursement. The response is not to ignore facility costs, but to make emergency-first care financially feasible through ring-fenced stabilization funds, emergency waiver systems, reimbursement for uncompensated stabilization, cross-subsidization arrangements, charitable or social support pathways, and phased expansion of insurance or pooled financing for emergency services. Where additional funding is limited, the first protected package should be narrow and time-critical, focusing on triage, immediate stabilization, essential medicines, oxygen, and safe referral before non-urgent billing procedures.

Operational capacity remains important, but it should support rather than dilute the central argument about payment-related delay. In practice, facilities can separate registration and billing from clinical triage, authorize urgent diagnostics and medicines for unstable patients before financial clearance, and audit payment-related delay events as part of quality improvement.

For fragile settings, reforms may need to be phased. Immediate actions can focus on protecting stabilization and referral from payment barriers. Medium-term efforts can expand pooled financing and provider reimbursement. Longer-term reforms should reduce structural dependence on out-of-pocket spending across the health system. Although Somalia is the illustrative case, this staged approach is also relevant to other fragile and resource-constrained health systems where fee-based care may interfere with time-critical emergency pathways.

Conclusion

Upfront payment requirements in emergency settings should be recognized as more than affordability challenges. In fragile and resource-constrained systems, they may function as non-clinical barriers to timely assessment, stabilization, and definitive care, with potential clinical and equity consequences arising from financing and governance weaknesses. This commentary argues that the problem is both a financing issue and an emergency systems issue. The priority is therefore practical action: protect triage and lifesaving stabilization from financial clearance, create feasible waiver or reimbursement mechanisms, separate billing from urgent clinical decisions, and strengthen accountability for time-critical care. Implementation should proceed even while facilities and researchers monitor whether payment checkpoints delay triage, first stabilizing treatment, diagnostics, admission, or referral. Framing emergency care as an essential public good rather than a service contingent on immediate cash payment is central to a more equitable and responsive health system in Somalia and comparable settings.

Dissemination of results

As a commentary, this manuscript did not involve primary data collection from a specific community or facility. Its arguments and recommendations are intended for dissemination through journal publication and for discussion among clinicians, hospital managers, researchers, and policymakers working to strengthen emergency care in Somalia and other African settings.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This article did not involve primary data collection, human participant recruitment, or identifiable patient information. Formal ethics approval and participant consent were therefore not required for this commentary.
