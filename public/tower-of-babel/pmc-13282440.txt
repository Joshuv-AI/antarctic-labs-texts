The launch and diffusion of new patented medicines by therapeutic value
Authors: Wei Zhang, Daphne P Guh, Alexander C T Tam, Paul Grootendorst, Aidan Hollis, Aslam H Anis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence on the launch and diffusion of therapeutically important new medicines is limited. We examined the launch and diffusion of new patented medicines in 14 countries by therapeutic value.MethodsWe analyzed IQVIA MIDAS® quarterly sales data for 422 patented molecules first launched between 2013 and 2021 in any of 14 countries (Australia, Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and the United States) and followed them for at least 2 years. Therapeutic value for each molecule was obtained from rating agencies in Canada, Germany, France, and the United States.ResultsOur main time-to-launch analysis showed that, compared with molecules offering major incremental benefit, those with moderate benefit were launched more quickly in several countries, including Canada, France, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. Molecules with little to no benefit were launched even more rapidly in the Netherlands. However, once launched, market share by volume did not change significantly over the first 5 years regardless of therapeutic value level in each country.ConclusionNew patented medicines with major incremental therapeutic benefit might not always be launched faster than other medicines. Strategies to balance pricing with adoption and diffusion warrant further investigation.Keywords: patented medicines, medicine launch, diffusion, therapeutic value, pharmaceutical markets

Introduction

The Kefauver-Harris Act of 1962 in the United States significantly strengthened drug safety and oversight by requiring pharmaceutical companies to prove a new drug's safety and efficacy before marketing. No doubt this policy generated valuable information on the properties of new drugs. But the enhanced oversight also increased costs. In addition to the cost to pharmaceutical companies of more lengthy and stringent clinical trials, patients had to wait longer for drugs that were found to be therapeutically important. More recently, pharmaceutical companies faced an additional hurdle—the cost of conducting economic appraisals. Most public and some private drug plans require this information to help decide whether to reimburse a new drug. Drug access is further delayed while drug plans review this evidence and engage in price negotiations.1,2 Delays in patient access, however, might be shorter for new drugs that offer high incremental therapeutic value over existing drugs. There are 2 reasons: first, drug plans might be willing to pay higher prices or list more valuable drugs; second, regulators might also expedite the review of both safety and efficacy and economic evidence for high value drugs.3 In this study, we examined whether new patented medicines with high incremental therapeutic value are available to patients, how fast they are available to patients, and whether their use spreads more rapidly than those with little or no incremental value.

Our study contributes to a modest literature. Mulcahy compared the availability and launch timing of new prescription drugs in 2018-2022 in the United States and other Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries.2 However, the analyses did not consider the therapeutic value of the new drugs. Other studies described the therapeutic value of the new drugs approved or marketed in different countries4-9 or compared different therapeutic value classification systems,10-12 but did not compare launch timing or uptake.

Kyle and Williams were the first to analyze the international diffusion of pharmaceuticals by therapeutic value for the period 2000-2013.13 Their findings demonstrated that the US health care system may be “uniquely inefficient” compared to 4 other countries, Australia, Canada, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom, in that it fuels the rapid adoption and diffusion of less important medicines. With new polices implemented after their study period, such as the 21st Century Cures Act in the United States in 2016,14 the “lock” system implemented in the Netherlands in 2015,15 and policy proposals aimed at lowering prices in Canada,16 these earlier findings may no longer hold. Our study built on this research by examining new patented medicines launched in 2013-2021 across 14 countries and by using more comprehensive measures of therapeutic value from independent organizations in Canada, Germany, France, and the United States.

Data and methods

Study design

We studied a retrospective cohort of patented molecules that were sold in 14 countries (Australia, Belgium, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, United Kingdom, United States) from Q1 2013 to Q4 2021. We selected these countries because all are high-income countries and are former or current international price reference countries for Canada.17 To confirm the first sale date in any of the 14 countries (the global first launch) for each patented molecule, we required at least 2 quarters of data with no earlier sales in any country. We then tracked their sales in each country up to Q4 2023, ensuring that each molecule was followed for at least 2 years after its global first launch.

Data sources

We used IQVIA MIDAS quarterly sales data for the period Q3 2012 to Q4 2023, which provide sales values and volumes of individual drug products for the 14 countries. Patented molecules were defined as “new active substances” and “innovative branded products” in the IQVIA MIDAS data. IQVIA MIDAS data have been used in previous studies to investigate sales, prices and launches across countries.13,16,18-20

We obtained therapeutic value information from 4 independent sources: Canada's Patented Medicine Prices Review Board (PMPRB) (breakthrough, substantial improvement, moderate improvement, slight or no improvement),21 Germany's Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care (IQWiG) (major, considerable, minor, non-quantifiable, or no added benefit, or less benefit),22 the independent French medicine bulletin, Prescrire International (bravo, a real advance, offers an advantage, possibly helpful, nothing new, not acceptable),23 and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (breakthrough designation).24 All data were retrieved and reviewed between May 9, 2025 and June 17, 2025.

Therapeutic value

Following the method of Lexchin,12 we first obtained up to 3 independent ratings of the therapeutic value of each new medicine from Canada's PMPRB, Germany's IQWiG, and France's Prescrire International. We grouped the ratings of each organization into 3 categories: “major” (PMPRB: breakthrough or substantial improvement, IQWiG: major added benefit, or Prescrire: bravo or a real advance), “moderate” (moderate improvement, considerable added benefit, or offers an advantage), and “little to no” (the remaining ratings from the 3 organizations) incremental benefit. If more than one rating was available, we assigned the highest rating to the specific molecule. Due to the absence of launch or a delayed review in the respective countries, some molecules did not receive ratings from these 3 organizations. Instead of excluding these molecules, as done in previous studies,8,10,13 we classified them as a separate category, “rating unavailable from the three organizations,” in our main analyses.

To further address the high number of unavailable ratings from PMPRB, IQWiG, and Prescrire International, we defined a variable for FDA breakthrough designation with 3 categories: “breakthrough,” “not breakthrough,” and “missing designation.”24 Unlike the ratings from these 3 organizations, the FDA's breakthrough therapy designation is intended to accelerate drug development and review, and it is granted on the basis of preliminary clinical evidence.24,25 For this reason, we analyzed launch and diffusion separately according to breakthrough therapy designation status. All of the above ratings indicated the relative or incremental therapeutic benefit of a drug compared with existing treatment options.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

We assessed country-level launch outcomes using 3 measures: cumulative probability of launch of new molecules over time since their global first launch, median time-to-launch (the number of years at which 50% of the molecules had been launched in that country), and time to maximum observed cumulative probability of launch during follow-up (the maximum number of molecules launched in that country during the study period). We used the Kaplan–Meier method to analyze time-to-launch for each country and to estimate these launch outcomes by therapeutic benefit rating.

We further used a Cox proportional hazards model to examine whether the hazard of launch differed by therapeutic benefit rating and country. The independent variables of interest included therapeutic benefit rating, country, and their interactions, while adjusting for therapeutic area (level 1 of Anatomical Classification of Pharmaceutical Products provided in IQVIA MIDAS)26 and FDA breakthrough designation. Both the Kaplan–Meier estimator and the Cox proportional hazards model explicitly accounted for censoring by retaining censored molecules in the risk set until the time they were censored (ie, at the end of follow-up).

We assessed diffusion by examining changes in market share among molecules with at least 5 years of follow-up since their first launch in a given country. Market share was calculated as the molecule's sales volume (in standard units) divided by the total sales volume within its anatomical classification level 4 category.26 IQVIA defined the standard unit as a dose of a particular formulation of medicine, such as one tablet, capsule, vial, or 5 mL for liquid. We defined the market as all molecules within the same anatomical classification level 4 category, which represents the most comparable grouping.

We ran separate linear regression models for each country to examine how market share in each therapeutic benefit rating category evolved over the first 5 years following a molecule's launch. The independent variables of interest were indicators for therapeutic benefit rating categories, year (relative to the molecule's launch in that country), and their interactions. The interaction coefficients were of primary interest because they indicate whether diffusion patterns differed by therapeutic benefit rating over the 5-year period within each country. The models adjusted for therapeutic area and FDA breakthrough designation. Based on these regression models, we estimated market share in each of the 5 years following launch for molecules rated as having major, moderate, or little to no incremental benefit.

In a sensitivity analysis, we estimated a Cox proportional hazards model restricted to molecules with at least 5 years of follow-up since their global first launch. We also examined launch and diffusion by FDA breakthrough designation (not breakthrough vs breakthrough) among molecules launched in the United States (ie, US sample). We performed all of our analyses using SAS (r) Proprietary Software 9·4 (TS1M6) (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 4·4·0).

Results

Our cohort included 422 new molecules launched in the 14 countries between 2013 and 2021, of which 128 (30.3%) were first launched in a country other than the United States. A total of 32 (7.6%) molecules were rated as having major incremental benefit, 64 (15.2%) as moderate benefit, 169 (40.0%) as little to no benefit, and 157 (37.2%) had no available ratings from PMPRB, IQWiG, and Prescrire International (Appendix Table S1). Among the 157 molecules without ratings, 26 were designated as breakthrough therapies by the US FDA, whereas 78 were classified as not breakthrough. The number of molecules without ratings has increased in recent years (Appendix Figure S1). Among the 357 molecules launched in the United States for our sensitivity analyses, 108 (30.3%) were designated as breakthrough therapies by the FDA.

Launch

Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 1 and Figures S2-S15) present the cumulative probability of launch for new molecules by country and therapeutic benefit. Across all molecules, launches were most rapid in the United States. Molecules with major or moderate incremental benefit had the slowest and lowest probability of launch in the Netherlands.

For molecules with major incremental benefit, the maximum observed cumulative probability of launch during follow-up was identical to the cumulative probability of launch at Year 5 after global first launch in all countries except Australia, Canada, and Sweden (Table S2). In effect, if these molecules had not been launched within 5 years, they were unlikely to be launched up to 11 years.

All molecules with major incremental benefit were eventually launched in Canada, Germany, and Sweden; whereas only 29% were launched in the Netherlands during the entire study period. All molecules with moderate incremental benefit were launched in Belgium and the United Kingdom.

The Cox proportional hazards model compared the hazard of launch by therapeutic benefit rating and country (Table S3 and Figure 2A). Compared with molecules with major incremental benefit, those with moderate benefit had a higher hazard of launch in Canada (hazard ratio [95% CI] = 1.112 [1.022, 1.210]), France (1.100 [1.025, 1.180]), the Netherlands (1.199 [1.136, 1.267]), Norway (1.193 [1.111, 1281]), Sweden (1.169 [1.100, 1.242]), and the United Kingdom (1.087 [1.033, 1.144]) (Figure 2A). After restricting the sample to molecules with at least 5 years of follow-up since global first launch, the sensitivity analysis showed that those with moderate benefit had a higher hazard of launch only in Canada (1.315 [1.172, 1.475]) and Norway (1.127 [1.029, 1.234]) (Figure S16A). In contrast, molecules with little to no incremental benefit showed a higher hazard of launch than those with major benefit only in the Netherlands, consistent in both the main and sensitivity analyses (Figure 2A and Figure S16A).

Within the US launch sample, molecules without a breakthrough designation showed a higher hazard of launch than breakthrough-designated molecules in Belgium, Japan, the Netherlands, and Spain (Figure 2B and Table S4). However, after restricting the sample to molecules with at least 5 year of follow-up, this pattern was observed only in Japan and the Netherlands (Figure S16B).

Discussion

We compared the launch timing and subsequent diffusion of new molecules that were first sold in any of 14 countries in 2013-2021 by therapeutic benefit rating. We found that the most therapeutically important drugs—those providing major incremental benefits over existing drugs—were not launched more quickly than those with moderate or little to no incremental benefit in some countries, including Canada, France, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. However, once launched, subsequent diffusion remained stable over the first 5 years in each country. The sensitivity analyses restricted to molecules with at least 5 years of follow-up showed similar patterns.

Launch speed was highest in the United States, especially for molecules with major incremental benefit or those with breakthrough designation. Using IQVIA MIDAS data in 2018-2022, Mulcahy also found that most new drugs were sold first in the United States and that the United States was the fastest to launch new drugs among the 27 study countries.2 One possible explanation is the relatively higher US drug prices compared with other comparison countries.27 Furthermore, the 21st Century Cures Act, signed into law in the United States in 2016, may have accelerated the review of novel medical products and thereby increased their availability to patients.14 However, the Inflation Reduction Act of 202228 and on-going policy proposals, such as Most Favored Nation drug pricing,29 intended to lower US drug prices, are expected to delay or prevent the launch of new drugs in the United States,2 a possibility that warrants further investigation.

The Netherlands was an outlier as the country in which launches were slowest and drugs were least likely to launch over the study period. Bussgen and Stargardt also found that the Netherlands was the country with the lowest availability of new pharmaceuticals among the 10 European countries included in our study and the country in which availability decreased the most from 2000 to 2017.20 One possible explanation is the “lock” system implemented in the Netherlands in 2015, in which the Minister of Health has the option to postpone or suspend the reimbursement of expensive new medicines.15

Kyle and Williams were the first to compare drug diffusion by therapeutic value in Australia, Canada, Switzerland and the United Kingdom relative to the United States, using MIDAS sales data in 2000-2013.13 In our study, we included 14 countries and used more recent data. We also categorized therapeutic value into 3 levels based on ratings from 3 organizations in the main analysis and included molecules with unavailable ratings. In contrast, Kyle and Williams classified therapeutic value into 2 categories (more vs less important) based on ratings from France's public health authority and excluded molecules without therapeutic ratings from their analyses. Furthermore, Kyle and Williams used standard units as the diffusion outcome, a measure that can be influenced by market size, population, disease prevalence, and distribution channel structure. To improve comparability across therapeutic value categories and countries, we instead defined the diffusion outcome as market share in standard units. In addition to these methodological improvements, our study also captured some of the impacts of recent policy changes mentioned above, such as the 21st Century Cures Act in the United States and the “lock” system implemented in the Netherlands.

Given these methodological differences, we found that diffusion over the first 5 years did not differ across therapeutic value within each country. These findings are not consistent with those of Kyle and Williams, who concluded that the US health care system may be “uniquely inefficient” in terms of the rapid diffusion of less important drugs. In our study, we identified potential inefficiencies in the Netherlands, where molecules with little to no incremental benefit showed the highest and fastest availability among all molecules.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a high proportion of our study molecules did not have a 3-level therapeutic benefit rating because they had not been launched or because of review delays in the countries corresponding to the 3 rating organizations, PMPRB, IQWiG, and Prescrire International. The rate of missing data is similar to that observed in the study by Vokinger et al.9 Instead of removing these missing data as done by previous studies,8,10,13 we included them a separate category in our main analyses. In addition, we defined FDA breakthrough designation status (which has less missing data) as a separate variable and adjusted for it in the regression models examining the association between therapeutic benefit ratings from the 3 organizations and launch or diffusion outcomes.

Second, therapeutic value assessments and ratings are inconsistent across organizations, as noted in previous studies.8,10-12,16 These ratings consider the specific circumstances of the corresponding countries, for example, the comparator used, the availability of comparable drugs, patient populations, and cultural and societal norms.8,30 In our study, for example, 62 molecules designated as breakthrough therapies by the US FDA were not rated as major incremental benefit by the 3 organizations (PMPRB, IQWiG, or Prescrire International), while 11 molecules were rated in the opposite direction. Despite the differences, the findings on launch and diffusion were qualitatively consistent across the main analyses (based on ratings from the 3 organizations) and the sensitivity analyses (based on FDA breakthrough designation). To address the small number of molecules with major incremental benefit, some studies aggregated therapeutic value into 2 levels by combining major and moderate incremental benefit into one category and comparing it with little to no incremental benefit,7,8,10,13 which may introduce bias by masking differences between the major- and moderate-benefit groups.

Lastly, our study period includes the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced drug launches and diffusion differently across countries.

Conclusion

New patented medicines with major incremental therapeutic benefit might not always be launched faster than other medicines. In particular, medicines with little to no incremental benefit were launched even more quickly than those with major incremental benefit in the Netherlands. However, once launched, medicines across different levels of therapeutic benefit tend to diffuse in a similar pattern over the first 5 years in each country. Our study highlights the need for policies that incorporate therapeutic value into drug market approval, reimbursement, and price regulation. Strategies to balance the pricing, adoption, and diffusion of medicines with high incremental therapeutic value warrant further investigation.
