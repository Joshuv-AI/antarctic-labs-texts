LGALS9 blockade augments vaccine-induced immune responses against prostate cancer
Authors: Bowen Lu, Ning Liu, Wanting Zhao, Haomin Chen, Yingxiang Shao, Haosen Lu, Shanshan Liu, Jie Yang, Yanyan Zheng, Jiage Ding, Dafei Chai, Lijun Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-based vaccination represents a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for prostate cancer; however, its efficacy remains constrained by tumor-induced immune evasion and insufficient activation of antigen-presenting cells. Galectin-9 (LGALS9), an immunoregulatory lectin that contributes to immune suppression, is therefore an attractive target for overcoming tumor-induced immune tolerance.MethodsRecombinant adenoviral vaccines encoding PSMA and LGALS9 (Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9) were generated and validated for antigen expression in vitro and in vivo. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in murine subcutaneous, bone metastatic, and humanized prostate cancer models. Vaccine-induced immune responses were characterized by flow cytometry, ELISA, enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot), immunohistochemistry, EdU proliferation assays, cytotoxic T lymphocyte assays, and cell depletion experiments.ResultsPre-immunization with Ad-LGALS9 significantly potentiated the therapeutic efficacy of the Ad-PSMA vaccine in subcutaneous, bone metastatic, and humanized prostate cancer models, resulting in pronounced tumor growth inhibition and prolonged survival. Mechanistically, LGALS9 targeting enhanced dendritic cell (DC) activation and maturation, upregulating CD80, CD86, major histocompatibility complex-II, and CD40 expression and promoting efficient antigen cross-presentation. This facilitated robust priming and expansion of multifunctional CD8+ T cells producing interferon-γ, interleukin-2, and tumor necrosis factor-α, which mediated potent cytotoxicity against PSMA-expressing tumor cells. Furthermore, LGALS9 immunization induced high-titer neutralizing antibodies that disrupted the LGALS9/TIM-3 inhibitory axis, alleviating T-cell exhaustion. Combined Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination established durable memory CD8+ T-cell responses that conferred protection against tumor rechallenge.ConclusionsTargeting LGALS9 enhances DC-mediated CD8+ T-cell immunity and synergistically augments the therapeutic efficacy of tumor vaccines, representing a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for prostate cancer.

Galectin-9 (LGALS9) regulates immune responses through the TIM-3 pathway and has been implicated in T-cell exhaustion and tumor immune evasion. Adenoviral vectors are effective platforms for inducing robust antigen expression and CD8+ T-cell responses in cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men worldwide.13 Although androgen deprivation therapy and next-generation antiandrogens confer initial clinical benefits, most patients ultimately progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer, for which effective treatments are limited.46 The immunologically “cold” nature of prostate tumors characterized by poor T-cell infiltration, low neoantigen burden, and a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment poses a formidable barrier to current immunotherapies.7 8 Consequently, developing strategies capable of converting prostate tumors into immunologically “hot” lesions represents an urgent unmet need.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), encoded by the FOLH1 gene, is highly expressed in prostate cancer and correlates with tumor aggressiveness.9 10 PSMA contributes to tumor progression through multiple mechanisms, including its folate hydrolase activity that modulates intracellular folate metabolism, promotion of PI3K-AKT signaling to enhance proliferation and survival, and facilitation of angiogenesis via regulation of endothelial cell interactions.1114 Upregulation of PSMA in malignant prostate epithelium and metastatic lesions, coupled with limited presence in normal tissues, highlights PSMA as a tumor-selective antigen and a compelling target for vaccine-based immunotherapy.1517 PSMA-based vaccines have demonstrated the ability to elicit antigen-specific immune responses, yet their in vivo efficacy has remained modest.18 19 This limited response is largely attributed to immune evasion mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME), including defective dendritic cell (DC) activation, T-cell exhaustion, and immunoinhibitory signaling mediated by checkpoint molecules.2024 Overcoming these barriers to potentiate PSMA vaccine-induced immunity remains a major challenge in prostate cancer immunotherapy.

Among critical regulators of immune suppression, galectin-9 (LGALS9) has emerged as a key molecule with context-dependent immunomodulatory functions.25 26 LGALS9 interacts primarily with TIM-3 and has also been reported to engage additional receptors such as CD44, thereby regulating immune tolerance, DC function, and T-cell exhaustion.27 In tumors, LGALS9 frequently contributes to immune escape by driving T-cell dysfunction and apoptosis, while under certain inflammatory contexts it can promote DC activation and enhance antigen presentation.2830 This dual functionality suggests that precise modulation of LGALS9 signaling may reprogram the immune landscape to favor antitumor immunity. Despite its established role in immune regulation, the therapeutic potential of targeting LGALS9 to improve vaccine-induced antitumor responses remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we hypothesized that LGALS9 modulation could enhance the immunogenicity and therapeutic efficacy of PSMA-based vaccination by simultaneously promoting DC activation and alleviating T-cell suppression. To test this, we generated recombinant adenoviral vaccines encoding PSMA and LGALS9 (Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9) and systematically evaluated their antitumor efficacy in multiple prostate cancer models. We demonstrate that Ad-LGALS9 priming robustly enhances Ad-PSMA vaccine efficacy, accompanied by increased DC maturation, improved antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell priming, and induction of neutralizing antibodies that block LGALS9/TIM-3-mediated immunosuppression. This combined Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination elicits potent cytotoxic and memory CD8+ T-cell responses, resulting in durable tumor control in both subcutaneous and bone metastatic models, as well as in humanized mice. Collectively, our findings reveal LGALS9 targeting as a previously unrecognized approach to overcome tumor-induced immune tolerance and potentiate vaccine-induced antitumor immunity, offering a promising framework for next-generation immunotherapies against “cold” tumors such as prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6 wild-type mice (male, 6–8 weeks old) and CD34+ humanized HLA-transgenic NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) (male, 16–18 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University.31 Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (22±2°C), humidity (50±10%), and a 12 hours light/dark cycle. Sterile food and water were provided.

Cell lines

Human HEK-293, HEK-293T and LNCaP cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HEK-293 and HEK-293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher, 12800017) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, FSP500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, C0222). LNCaP cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard conditions. Cells were maintained at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO₂. The murine prostate cancer cell line RM-1 was obtained from ATCC. A stable human PSMA-expressing RM-1 cell line (hPSMA-RM-1) was generated by infection with a lentivirus encoding human PSMA, followed by 2 µg/µL puromycin selection and clonal expansion for subsequent experiments. Both RM-1 and hPSMA-RM-1 cells were grown in DMEM (Thermo, 12800017) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Beyotime, C0222), under standard conditions of 37°C and 5% CO₂.

Plasmid construction and adenovirus generation

To facilitate efficient antigen secretion, a signal peptide sequence32 was fused to the N-terminus of the human PSMA or mouse/human LGALS9 coding sequences. The resulting constructs were synthesized (Sangon Biotech) and cloned into the pCA13 shuttle vector via homologous recombination. For recombinant adenovirus production, pCA13-PSMA or pCA13-LGALS9 were co-transfected with the E1A-deleted adenoviral type 5 pPE3 backbone plasmid pPE3 into HEK-293 cells, which provide the necessary E1 functions in trans. An empty control adenovirus (Ad-Ctrl) was generated by co-transfecting the pCA13 vector lacking a transgene with pPE3 into HEK-293 cells. Viral plaques typically appeared 10–14 days post-transfection. Recombinant adenovirus (designated Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9) were purified by cesium chloride density gradient ultracentrifugation, followed by dialysis against sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Adenovirus titer detection

Viral titers were determined by a 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID₅₀) assay using HEK293 cells. HEK293 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×10⁴ cells per well and cultured overnight to reach approximately 70–80% confluence. Recombinant adenoviruses were serially diluted 10-fold in serum-free DMEM and added to cells (100 µL per well, eight replicate wells per dilution). After incubation for 2 hours at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator, 100 µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to each well. Cells were cultured for 7 days and monitored daily for cytopathic effects by light microscopy. TCID₅₀ values were calculated using the Reed-Muench method and used to estimate plaque-forming unit (PFU) equivalents for dosing purposes. All titrations were performed in three independent experiments to ensure reproducibility.

Adenovirus expression analysis

To verify transgene expression, HEK-293 cells were infected with Ad-PSMA or Ad-LGALS9 at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI=5 PFU/cell). Viral genomic DNA was extracted from culture supernatants using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP304-03) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplification was performed using gene-specific primers for PSMA or LGALS9 to confirm the presence of transgene sequences. The following primers were as follows:

PSMA: Forward, 5’- TTAAGCTTATGTGGAATCTCCTTCACGAAACCGA-3’; Reverse, 5’-TGCTCTAGAGGCTACTTCACTCAAAGTCTCTGCAGCTG-3’. LGALS9: Forward, 5’-TTCGAGTCGACATGGCTCTCTCTTCGTGCCCAG-3’; Reverse, 5’- GATGGATCCCTATGTCTGCACGTGGGTCAGCTG-3’.

To validate protein expression, HEK-293T cells were infected with Ad-Ctrl, Ad-LGALS9, Ad-PSMA, or Ad-LGALS9/PSMA and cultured for 48 hours. Cells were then harvested and intracellularly stained with anti-human APC-PSMA (BioLegend, 342508) or anti-mouse APC-LGALS9 (BioLegend, 136110) antibodies at 4°C for 1 hour in the dark. After washing, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Vaccine treatment in animal models

Subcutaneous tumor model

To establish a subcutaneous prostate tumor model, hPSMA-RM-1 cells (2×105/mouse) suspended in 100 µL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of C57BL/6 mice on day 0. Following tumor inoculation, mice were immunized intramuscularly with adenoviral vaccines. All experimental groups received the same total number of immunizations (five doses) and an equivalent total amount of adenovirus, thereby excluding potential confounding effects due to increased adenoviral exposure. Specifically, mice in the Ad-Ctrl, Ad-LGALS9, and Ad-PSMA single-treatment groups were immunized with 2×10⁸ PFU per dose on days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 (five total doses). To match the total adenoviral load of the combination group, mice in these groups received an additional 2×10⁸ PFU of Ad-Ctrl on day 28. For the combination (Ad-LGALS9/PSMA) group, mice were immunized with Ad-LGALS9 (2×10⁸ PFU) on days 0 and 14, Ad-PSMA (2×10⁸ PFU) on days 7 and 21, followed by a booster immunization on day 28 consisting of Ad-LGALS9 (2×10⁸ PFU) plus Ad-PSMA (2×10⁸ PFU), resulting in a total of five immunizations and an equivalent cumulative adenoviral dose. Tumor growth was assessed by measuring tumor volumes with calipers, calculated as (length×width²/2). At the study endpoint, tumors were excised, weighed, and the tumor inhibition rate was calculated accordingly. Tumor inhibition rate was calculated using the following formula: tumor inhibition (%) = (C − T) / C × 100, where C represents the tumor weight of the control group, and T denotes the tumor weight of each treated mouse.

Bone metastasis model

The bone metastasis model was established by intra-tibial injection of hPSMA-RM-1 cells (5×10⁵ per mouse) suspended in 50% (v/v) growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, 354234) diluted in serum-free RPMI-1640 into anesthetized C57BL/6J mice on day 0. Mice were intramuscularly immunized according to the indicated schedule: mice in the Ad-Ctrl, Ad-LGALS9, and Ad-PSMA single-treatment groups were immunized with 2×10⁸ PFU per dose on days −7, −3, 0, 7 and 14 (five total doses). To match the total adenoviral load of the combination group, mice in these groups received an additional 2×10⁸ PFU of Ad-Ctrl on day 14. For the combination (Ad-LGALS9/PSMA) group, mice were immunized with Ad-LGALS9 (2×10⁸ PFU) on days −7 and 0, Ad-PSMA (2×10⁸ PFU) on days −3 and 7, followed by a booster immunization on day 14 consisting of Ad-LGALS9 (2×10⁸ PFU) plus Ad-PSMA (2×10⁸ PFU), resulting in a total of five immunizations and an equivalent cumulative adenoviral dose. On day 35, mice were euthanized, and the injected femurs were collected for measurement of tumor weight and volume.

Humanized mouse model

For evaluation in a humanized setting, CD34+ HLA-transgenic NSG mice were subcutaneously inoculated with LNCaP cells (5×106 cells/mouse) mixed with 50% (v/v) growth factor-reduced Matrigel on day 0. Mice were intramuscularly immunized with the same adenoviral vaccine regimens (Ad-LGALS9/PSMA group or Ad-Ctrl group), following the identical schedule, dose, and number of administrations as those used in the subcutaneous tumor model. Tumor volumes, weight and inhibition rate were measured.

Tumor rechallenge experiment

To assess long-term immune memory, mice that had achieved complete tumor regression following Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination underwent surgical resection of their primary tumors on day 42 post hPSMA-RM-1 implantation. On the day of rechallenge (day 0), mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 2×10⁵ hPSMA-RM-1 cells on the contralateral flank. Naive, age-matched mice without prior vaccination served as controls. Tumor growth was subsequently monitored to evaluate recall immune responses and protection against tumor recurrence.

Flow cytometry

Spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated in RPMI-1640 medium using sterile 5 mL syringe plungers to obtain single-cell suspensions. Tumor tissues were mechanically homogenized to generate uniform suspensions. All samples were filtered through a 70 µm nylon mesh to remove debris and cell aggregates. Cell suspensions were treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer for 3 min at room temperature to remove erythrocytes, followed by neutralization with RPMI-1640 complete medium, washing two times with PBS, and resuspension for subsequent experiments. Tumor-infiltrating leukocytes (TILs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using 33% Percoll (VicMed, VIC1555), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For surface staining, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4°C for 1 hour in the dark. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A) for 15 min at room temperature, washed with 1×Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend, 421002), and incubated with antibodies diluted in permeabilization buffer at 4°C overnight.

For intracellular cytokine staining, splenocytes from each group were cultured for 72 hours in the presence of recombinant PSMA protein to induce antigen-specific cytokine responses. Before harvest, cells were stimulated for 5 hours with ionomycin (500 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, I9657-1MG), phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (50 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, P1585), and Brefeldin A (5 µg/mL; eBioscience, 00–4506-51) to allow intracellular cytokine accumulation. Cells were then subjected to surface and intracellular staining.

The following antibodies were used for flow cytometric analysis at a dilution of 1:200: anti-mouse FITC-CD11b (BioLegend, 101206), anti-mouse APC-CD11c (BioLegend, 301614), anti-mouse PerCP-Cy5.5-Gr-1 (BioLegend, 108426), anti-mouse APC-NK1.1 (BioLegend, 156506), anti-mouse APC-Cy7-CD45 (BioLegend, 103114), anti-mouse PE-CD3ε (BioLegend, 100308), anti-mouse PerCP-Cy5.5-CD4 (BioLegend, 116012), anti-mouse PerCP-Cy5.5-CD8α (BioLegend, 100734), anti-mouse APC-CD44 (BioLegend, 103012), anti-mouse PE-CD62L (BioLegend, 104408), anti-mouse APC-IFN-γ (BioLegend, 505810), anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488-TNF-α (BioLegend, 506313), anti-mouse PE-IL-2 (BioLegend, 503808), anti-human APC-Cy7-CD45 (BioLegend, 304014), anti-human PerCP-Cy5.5-CD8α (BioLegend, 301032), anti-human APC-CD11c (BioLegend, 301614), anti-human PE-CD103 (BioLegend, 350206), anti-human PE-CD80 (BioLegend, 305208), anti-human FITC-CD86 (BioLegend, 374204), anti-human Pacific Blue-HLA-DR (BioLegend, 307624), anti-human APC-IFN-γ (BioLegend, 506510), anti-human Alexa Fluor 488-TNF-α (BioLegend, 502915), and anti-human PE-IL-2 (BioLegend, 500307). Data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10.8.1).

Cell depletion assay

For T-cell depletion, 500 µg of anti-CD8α monoclonal antibody (mAb; Bio X Cell, BE004-1) or anti-CD4 mAb (Bio X Cell, BE003-1) depleting antibody was administered intraperitoneally 2 days before the first vaccine dose. To ensure continued CD8+ or CD4+ T-cell ablation, additional antibody injections were performed on days 5 and 12 post-initial immunization.

ELISA assay

LGALS9 or PSMA protein levels were measured by ELISA using Mouse Galectin-9 ELISA Kit (EM33RB, Invitrogen) or Human PSMA ELISA Kit (abcam, ab315305). To assess the protein levels of interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-12, IL-10, and IL-4 in culture supernatants, splenocytes were plated in a 24-well culture dish at a density of 1×10⁶ cells/mL and stimulated with 10 µg/mL of PSMA protein at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment for 48 hours. The culture supernatants or serum were then analyzed using an ELISA kit (Invitrogen, BMS606-2, BMS616, BMS614, or BMS613) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Antibody titer determination

Serum IgG titers were quantified by ELISA. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with recombinant LGALS9 or PSMA protein (5 µg/mL) and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Serial dilutions of mouse sera were added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #7076). After substrate development and reaction termination, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Endpoint titers were defined as the highest serum dilution yielding an absorbance at least twofold above that of control wells.

Antibody blocking assay

High-binding ELISA plates were coated with recombinant LGALS9 (5 µg/mL, 100 µL per well) in PBS overnight at 4°C. Plates were washed with phosphate buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Serially diluted sera from adenoviral vaccine-immunized mice were added and incubated for 1 hour, followed by biotinylated TIM-3 (1 µg/mL) for an additional 1 hour. After washing, HRP-conjugated streptavidin was applied for 30 min. Signal was developed with 3,3',5,5'-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Cytotoxicity assay

Splenocytes were stimulated with recombinant PSMA proteins in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 100 U/mL IL-2 (PeproTech, 212–12), and cultured at 37°C for 5 days. These antigen-activated splenocytes were used as effector cells and co-cultured with hPSMA-RM-1 target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 50:1 for 48 hours. After co-culture, cells were collected and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against mouse CD8 and PSMA. Flow cytometry was performed to assess target cell viability, excluding dead cells based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters. The specific cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula: cytotoxicity (%) = ((T − Te) / T) × 100%, where T represents the initial percentage of PSMA+ tumor cells prior to co-culture, and Te indicates the residual percentage of target cells following effector exposure.

Proliferation of CD8+ T cells

Splenocytes were plated into 48-well plates at a density of 1×10⁶ cells per well and stimulated with recombinant PSMA protein (10 µg/mL) for 5 days. The culture medium was partially refreshed every other day to maintain cytokine activity and nutrient supply. On day 5, CD8+ T-cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, C0081L), in combination with PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-mouse CD8α antibody, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Proliferative activity was quantified by flow cytometry.

ELISpot assay

The assay was conducted using the Mouse IFN-γ Single-Color ELISpot kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (ImmunoSpot, SKU: mIFNgp-1M). Splenocytes were isolated from the spleens of treated mice and seeded into an IFN-γ capture plate at 5×105 cells per well. They were then re-stimulated with LGALS9 and PSMA protein (10 µg/mL) in 200 µL of RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 100 U/mL IL-2. After 48 hours of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, the plates were washed and incubated with the IFN-γ detection antibody. The color development was achieved through enzyme-catalyzed substrate precipitation, and IFN-γ-expressing cells were analyzed using an ImmunoSpot S6 Ultimate Reader.

Immunohistochemistry and H&E staining

Tissue samples were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 µm slices according to standard histological procedures. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker. Slides were then cooled to room temperature, blocked with 10% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4°C with a rat anti-mouse CD8α antibody (1:200; eBioscience, 14–0081-82). Following primary antibody incubation, slides were washed and treated with a goat anti-rat HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Zhongshan Biotech, PV9004) for 40 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB chromogenic substrate kit (Zhongshan Biotech, ZLI-9018). CD8+ T-cell infiltration was quantified from immunohistochemically stained tumor sections using ImageJ software (NIH). For each tumor, 5–10 randomly selected high-power fields (HPFs) were analyzed. Positive staining was identified using a consistent intensity threshold applied uniformly across all samples. Quantification was performed in a blinded manner, with investigators unaware of group allocation at the time of analysis. Data are presented as the mean number of CD8+ cells per HPF.

H&E staining was conducted by Wuhan Servicebio Technology according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting sections were evaluated by a pathologist blinded to the treatment groups and were imaged using an Olympus IX73 microscope.

Statistical analyses

All data are expressed as mean±SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance was conducted. Survival data were evaluated using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance was set at *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001.

Results

Adenovirus vaccines efficiently drive PSMA and LGALS9 expression in vitro and in vivo

To evaluate the immunogenic potential of the LGALS9 in enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of a PSMA-based vaccine for prostate cancer, we first constructed recombinant replication-deficient adenoviral vectors encoding PSMA or LGALS9. The expression plasmids pCA13-PSMA and pCA13-LGALS9, each engineered with an N-terminal secretion signal peptide, were co-transfected with the pPE3 adenoviral backbone into HEK-293 cells to produce Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9 (figure 1A). Distinct viral plaques were observed 14 days post-transfection, confirming successful viral packaging (figure 1C). Integration of the PSMA and LGALS9 transgenes was verified by PCR amplification of viral supernatants (figure 1B). To confirm antigen expression in vitro, HEK-293T cells were infected with Ad-PSMA or Ad-LGALS9 and analyzed by flow cytometry. Both vectors mediated robust intracellular expression of their respective transgenes, confirming successful antigen delivery and expression (figure 1D–G). Consistently, ELISA analysis of culture supernatants from transfected 293T cells revealed markedly increased secretion of PSMA and LGALS9 proteins compared with controls (figure 1H,I). To evaluate antigen expression in vivo, mice were intramuscularly immunized with Ad-PSMA, Ad-LGALS9, or the combination Ad-LGALS9/PSMA. Serum collected 3 days post-immunization showed significantly elevated levels of PSMA and LGALS9. Notably, co-administration of Ad-LGALS9/PSMA resulted in antigen secretion levels comparable to those observed with single Ad-PSMA or Ad-LGALS9 immunization, indicating that simultaneous administration does not impair transgene expression or systemic antigen release in vivo (figure 1J,K). These results indicate that Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9 efficiently mediate high-level antigen expression and secretion in vitro and in vivo, thereby providing a robust platform for subsequent immunogenicity and therapeutic evaluation.

Ad-LGALS9 priming enhances the antitumor efficacy and immune infiltration induced by Ad-PSMA vaccination

LGALS9 expression within the TME has been shown to mediate TIM-3-dependent T-cell dysfunction, thereby restricting the efficacy of immunotherapies.3335 To relieve this immune suppression and potentiate PSMA-targeted vaccination, we established an hPSMA-expressing RM-1 prostate cancer cell line and confirmed stable PSMA expression for tumor model generation (online supplemental figure S1). Using this model, we designed a sequential immunization strategy in tumor-bearing mice. Animals were primed with Ad-LGALS9 on day 0, followed by Ad-PSMA vaccination on day 7, a second Ad-LGALS9 boost on day 14, and another Ad-PSMA immunization on day 21. A final combined Ad-LGALS9/PSMA booster was administered on day 28 (figure 2A). Tumor growth was monitored weekly, and tumors were excised and weighed on day 42 post tumor inoculation (figure 2B). Compared with mice receiving Ad-PSMA alone, those immunized with the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA sequential regimen exhibited markedly suppressed tumor growth, significantly reduced tumor weights, and a substantially higher tumor inhibition rate (figure 2C–E). These results indicate that Ad-LGALS9 priming substantially enhances the therapeutic efficacy of the Ad-PSMA vaccine in the subcutaneous prostate tumor model.

To elucidate the immunological basis of this enhancement, we analyzed the composition of tumor-infiltrating immune cells by flow cytometry. Immunophenotypic profiling revealed that Ad-LGALS9/PSMA treatment markedly increased the infiltration of total T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, and DCs within the TME (figure 2F,G and online supplemental figure S2). In contrast, the frequencies and numbers of CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells remained largely unchanged, suggesting that the antitumor enhancement was primarily driven by enhanced CD8+ T-cell responses and DC recruitment. Furthermore, we evaluated the safety profile of the vaccination regimen. No significant changes in body weight or serum cytokine levels were observed among treatment groups (figure 2H,I). Histopathological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys) revealed no apparent pathological abnormalities (figure 2J). Furthermore, hematological and biochemical parameters, including white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), haemoglobin (HGB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE), showed no significant differences between healthy controls and Ad-vaccine-treated mice (online supplemental figure S3). These data demonstrate that Ad-LGALS9 priming effectively alleviates LGALS9-mediated immunosuppression, thereby enhancing DC activation, CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and overall vaccine efficacy, while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

Ad-LGALS9 immunization promotes vaccine-induced DC subset expansion, activation, maturation, and antigen-specific immune responses

DCs, as professional antigen-presenting cells, play a pivotal role in bridging innate and adaptive immunity by initiating naïve T-cell activation.3638 Given their essential contribution to antitumor immune responses, we next investigated how Ad-LGALS9 immunization modulates DC subsets and functional activation following Ad-PSMA vaccination. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-immunized mice exhibited markedly increased frequencies of CD11b+CD11c+, CD8α+CD11c+, and CD103+CD11c+ DC subsets compared with those receiving Ad-PSMA alone (figure 3A–D). Furthermore, DCs from the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA group expressed significantly higher levels of the costimulatory and maturation markers CD80, CD86, and major histocompatibility complex class II (figure 3E–H), indicative of enhanced activation and functional maturation.

Consistent with previous reports that galectin-9 can be neutralized by antibodies and modulated through humoral immune mechanisms,39 40 we detected high titers of antigen-specific anti-LGALS9 antibodies following Ad-LGALS9 or Ad-LGALS9/PSMA immunization (figure 3I), suggesting a potential role for humoral immunity to LGALS9-associated immune regulation. Moreover, in the Ad-LGALS9 group, 1 week after the primary immunization, only low levels of early-stage LGALS9-specific antibodies were detectable, whereas booster immunization triggered rapid antibody maturation and robust increases in high-titer neutralizing antibodies, which were further sustained and amplified by subsequent vaccinations (online supplemental figure S4). Neutralization assays showed that antibodies induced by Ad-LGALS9 or Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination effectively blocked the interaction between LGALS9 and TIM-3 (online supplemental figure S5A). In vitro functional assays revealed that the addition of immune sera from Ad-LGALS9-immunized or Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-immunized mice significantly reduced the frequencies of TIM-3+CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+CD8+ T cells in antigen-stimulated splenocyte cultures (online supplemental figure S5B–E). Ad-LGALS9 or Ad-LGALS9/PSMA exhibited a significant reduction in TIM-3+ T cells in both the spleen and tumor tissues compared with control groups (online supplemental figure S6). These results suggest that vaccination-induced anti-LGALS9 functional neutralizing antibodies effectively interfere with Gal-9/TIM-3 signaling in T cells.

Similarly, Ad-PSMA and Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination induced strong PSMA-specific antibody responses (figure 3J). Furthermore, splenocytes from vaccinated mice produced significantly elevated levels of Th1-type cytokines, including IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, on antigen stimulation, while IL-10 and IL-4 remained unchanged (figure 3K), indicating a Th1-polarized immune response. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Ad-LGALS9 priming enhances the expansion, activation, and maturation of multiple DC subsets, while simultaneously promoting Th1-skewed, antigen-specific immune responses, thereby amplifying the overall immunogenicity and therapeutic efficacy of PSMA-based vaccination.

Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination elicits potent antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell responses that mediate tumor inhibition

Tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are central to effective antitumor immunity.41 To determine whether Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination enhances CD8+ T-cell effector functions, splenocytes from immunized mice were restimulated in vitro with cognate antigens. EdU incorporation assays revealed a significant increase in proliferating CD8+ T cells in the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-vaccinated mice compared with the single-antigen vaccine group (figure 4A,B). enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assays further confirmed a marked elevation in the number of IFN-γ-secreting T cells following Ad-LGALS9/PSMA immunization (figure 4C). Consistent with these findings, cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that CD8+ T cells derived from Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-vaccinated mice exhibited enhanced tumor cell killing capacity against hPSMA-RM-1 target cells compared with Ad-PSMA-immunized controls (figure 4D,E). Flow cytometric analysis of effector cytokine production revealed significantly higher frequencies of TNF-α+, IL-2+, and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA group (figure 4F,G). Similarly, tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells from Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-treated mice exhibited increased intracellular expression of these effector cytokines (figure 4H,I).

Effective CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity often depends on multifunctional CTLs capable of simultaneously producing multiple cytokines.31 Functional profiling revealed that Ad-LGALS9/PSMA co-immunization significantly increased the frequency of dual (IFN-γ+TNF-α+ or IFN-γ+IL-2+) and triple-positive (IFN-γ+IL-2+TNF-α+) CD8+ T cells in both splenocytes and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (figure 4J,K). These results suggest that Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination drives the robust expansion, activation, and functional diversification of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting potent cytotoxic activity and effective tumor inhibition.

CD8+ T cells are essential for Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-mediated tumor rejection and long-term protective immunity

To clarify the role of CD8+ T cells in mediating the antitumor effects of the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccine, in vivo depletion experiments were performed. The efficiency of CD8+ and CD4+ T-cell depletion was validated by flow cytometry, which confirmed effective and sustained reduction of the targeted T-cell populations (online supplemental figure S7). Depletion of CD4+ T cells had minimal impact on tumor control, whereas CD8+ T-cell depletion markedly abrogated the therapeutic efficacy of Ad-LGALS9/PSMA (figure 5A,B). Consistently, loss of CD8+ T cells resulted in reduced frequencies of CD8+ T cells and CD8+CD11c+DCs within spleen and tumor tissues (figure 5C,D), confirming the indispensable role of CD8+ T cells in mediating vaccine-induced tumor rejection. To evaluate the induction of long-term antitumor immunity, we performed tumor rechallenge experiments in mice previously cured by Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination. Remarkably, 40% of vaccinated mice remained completely tumor-free, and the remainder exhibited significantly delayed tumor regrowth compared with naïve controls (figure 5E,F). Moreover, Ad-LGALS9/PSMA-immunized mice demonstrated sustained protection and prolonged survival following rechallenge, with 100% survival maintained through day 70 (figure 5G), indicating the establishment of potent, long-lasting immune memory.

Flow cytometric profiling of splenic T-cell subsets revealed a marked expansion of memory CD8+ T cells, characterized by increased frequencies of both central memory (CD8+CD44+CD62L+) and effector memory (CD8+CD44+CD62L⁻) populations, accompanied by a decline in naïve CD8+ T cells (CD8+CD44⁻CD62L+) (figure 5H-K). Mice receiving Ad-LGALS9/PSMA exhibited a significantly prolonged survival compared with those treated with Ad-PSMA alone (figure 6B). Correspondingly, tumor burden in bone as reflected by tumor volume and weight was markedly reduced in the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA group, resulting in a higher tumor inhibition rate relative to controls (figure 6C–F).

Ad-LGALS9 enhances therapeutic efficacy and immune activation of the PSMA vaccine in humanized prostate cancer model

To further assess the translational potential of the adenoviral vaccine strategy, we generated a recombinant adenoviral vector encoding human LGALS9 (Ad-hLGALS9) and evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination regimen in a humanized prostate cancer model. Humanized mice were subcutaneously inoculated with hPSMA-RM-1 cells on day 0 and immunized intramuscularly with Ad-LGALS9 on day 0, Ad-PSMA on day 7, Ad-LGALS9 on day 14, Ad-PSMA on day 21, followed by a final Ad-LGALS9/PSMA boost on day 28 (figure 7A). Ad-LGALS9/PSMA group markedly inhibited tumor progression, resulting in significantly reduced tumor volume and weight and a corresponding increase in tumor inhibition rate compared with Ad-Ctrl group (figure 7B–D). To delineate the immune mechanisms underlying this enhanced efficacy, we analyzed immune cell populations in the spleen and tumor tissues by flow cytometry. In the spleen, Ad-LGALS9/PSMA immunization led to a pronounced expansion and maturation of DC subsets, including CD8+CD11c+, CD103+CD11c+, CD86+CD11c+, and HLA-DR+CD11c+ populations (figure 7E–I). Similarly, tumor-infiltrating DCs exhibited elevated expression of CD8, CD103, CD86, and HLA-DR, indicative of enhanced antigen presentation and activation potential within the TME (figure 7J–M). These results indicate that Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination effectively suppresses tumor growth in humanized prostate cancer models by promoting DC maturation and activating CD8+ T-cell immune responses.

Discussion

Although prior transcriptomic analyses of human prostate cancer have identified galectin-1 as the most prominently upregulated galectin during disease progression, other galectins, including galectin-9, were reported at comparatively lower levels in bulk tumor tissue in that specific dataset.44 Importantly, tissue-level expression within tumor parenchyma does not fully reflect the immunological relevance of LGALS9 in the TME. LGALS9, expressed by myeloid cells, DCs, and T-cell subsets, acts as an immune checkpoint ligand that suppresses effector T cells and promotes immunosuppressive populations, contributing to T-cell exhaustion and regulatory T cells (Treg) expansion across solid tumors, independent of its expression in tumor cells.27 45 Moreover, LGALS9 exhibits dual and context-dependent roles in cancer, being associated with either tumor progression or, in specific contexts, favorable prognosis, highlighting its complex and highly context-specific biology.46 Therefore, the immunotherapeutic rationale for targeting LGALS9 is based primarily on its functional role in immune regulation rather than its constitutive expression in prostate tumor epithelium. This provides a strong biological justification for vaccine strategies aimed at disrupting LGALS9-mediated immune suppression to enhance antigen-specific antitumor immunity, particularly when combined with PSMA-directed immune responses.

Adenoviral vectors have emerged as powerful platforms for cancer vaccine development due to their superior gene delivery efficiency, intrinsic adjuvanticity, and ability to elicit robust CD8+ T-cell responses.4749 In our study, the adenoviral system enabled high-level expression of both PSMA and LGALS9, resulting in efficient antigen presentation and immune activation. PSMA alone is a weakly immunogenic self-antigen, and previous studies have shown limited efficacy when used as a single-target vaccine.50 51 By contrast, co-expression with LGALS9 markedly enhanced vaccine potency, suggesting that LGALS9 exerts a context-dependent immunostimulatory function within the adenoviral vaccine framework. Ad-LGALS9 vaccination elicited high-titer, neutralizing antibodies that blocked the LGALS9/TIM-3 interaction. Booster immunizations rapidly matured these responses, reducing TIM-3+CD8+ T cells and restoring IFN-γ production in vitro, while in vivo analyses confirmed decreased TIM-3+ T cells in spleen and tumors. Concurrently, Ad-LGALS9 pre-immunization promoted DC maturation and expanded cross-presenting CD8α+CD103+ DC subsets, which are critical for cross-priming naive CD8+ T cells and driving durable cytotoxic response against PSMA-expressing tumors. These results demonstrate that Ad-LGALS9 simultaneously enhances DC-mediated T-cell priming and functional humoral immunity, amplifying the overall efficacy of PSMA-targeted vaccination.

Beyond its effects on antigen-presenting cells, LGALS9 immunization induced high titers of neutralizing antibodies capable of interfering with TIM-3-mediated inhibitory signaling. These antibodies likely alleviate tumor-induced immune suppression by preventing LGALS9/TIM-3 interactions that drive T-cell exhaustion. Unlike passive antibody therapy, vaccination induces polyclonal LGALS9-specific antibodies together with coordinated humoral and cellular immune responses, potentially enabling broader immune modulation. Notably, TIM-3 signaling has also been shown to restrain myeloid cell activation and inflammasome responses,52 suggesting that blockade of the LGALS9/TIM-3 axis by either antibodies or vaccination may contribute to enhanced innate immune activation. Together with enhanced DC activation, this dual immune-programming strategy of Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination strengthens CTL responses while limiting immune escape mechanisms that often restrict the efficacy of therapeutic cancer vaccines. Moreover, this approach may provide more sustained immune modulation compared with repeated antibody administration.

Functionally, Ad-LGALS9/PSMA vaccination generated robust CTL responses characterized by enhanced cytokine polyfunctionality, including IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α secretion. Depletion of CD8+ T cells completely abolished the antitumor efficacy of the vaccine, underscoring the indispensable role of CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in tumor regression. Moreover, the vaccine promoted a marked expansion of effector memory and central memory CD8+ T cells, contributing to long-term immune protection as confirmed by tumor rechallenge experiments. In the bone metastasis model, where the immunosuppressive milieu typically impedes T-cell infiltration, Ad-LGALS9/PSMA overcame these barriers and achieved durable tumor control, highlighting its translational potential for advanced, metastatic prostate cancer. LGALS9 may thus serve as an immune amplifier that bridges innate and adaptive immunity, promoting a more coordinated and sustained antitumor response.

Despite demonstrating robust antitumor immunity and durable protection in a PSMA-expressing prostate cancer model, several limitations warrant consideration. The generalizability of this vaccination strategy to non-prostate malignancies remains unclear. Although PSMA expression has been reported in the neovasculature of other solid tumors and under hypoxic conditions, its immunogenicity across distinct TMEs requires systematic evaluation in additional preclinical models. Moreover, while our data establish a dominant role for CD8+ T cells in mediating therapeutic efficacy, the contributions of other immune components including CD4+ T cells, B cells, innate immune populations, and humoral mechanisms were not comprehensively examined and merit further investigation. Although sustained antitumor protection was observed for up to 10 weeks post-vaccination, longer-term immune durability and memory maintenance were not assessed and will be important for predicting clinical relevance. In addition, as murine tumor models do not fully recapitulate human tumor heterogeneity, clinical studies will ultimately be required to determine safety, immunogenicity, and therapeutic efficacy in patients. Notably, residual tumors observed in a subset of mice suggest potential immune escape or vector-related limitations, such as neutralizing anti-adenoviral antibodies or immune exhaustion following repeated immunization. Future optimization may include heterologous prime-boost strategies, alternative delivery routes, or combination approaches with immune checkpoint inhibitors or cytokine adjuvants to further enhance and sustain antitumor immunity.

In conclusion, this study establishes LGALS9 targeting as an effective approach to enhance prostate cancer vaccine efficacy through complementary mechanisms involving DC activation and the induction of neutralizing antibodies that block LGALS9/TIM-3-mediated immunosuppression. By coordinately strengthening cellular and humoral immune responses, this strategy elicits a robust and durable antitumor effect capable of overcoming immune resistance within the bone metastatic niche. Collectively, these findings support the translational potential of Ad-LGALS9/PSMA as a next-generation immunotherapeutic strategy for advanced prostate cancer.
