Near-real-time interactive N-body model of the left atrium for exploratory arrhythmia induction and ablation simulation
Authors: Bryant Wyatt, Leah Rogers, Mason Bane, Kyla Moor, Philip Alcorn, Gabriella Williams, Kinsey Brawner, Madhur Wyatt, Charles Puelz
Published: 2026

Key Findings ▪This study showcases an interactive, near real-time N-body model capable of simulating the electrophysiology of the left atrium.▪Unlike conventional high-fidelity simulations that require extensive computation time, this model provides an intuitive, interactive environment for exploring the mechanisms of arrhythmias and testing ablation strategies in a live simulation.▪The model allows clinicians and researchers to probe conduction dynamics, test various lesion sets, and visualize arrhythmia transitions dynamically.▪This platform could bridge the gap between static computational models and the dynamic decision-making environment of the clinical electrophysiology laboratory.

Left atrial (LA) arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, remain a major cause of stroke and progressive heart failure. Catheter ablation outcomes depend on an operator’s understanding of arrhythmia drivers and conduction patterns, fueling interest in computational platforms capable of simulating atrial behavior in near real time. However, existing tools for investigating arrhythmia mechanisms often lack the interactivity required for rapid hypothesis testing and conceptual exploration.1 To address this, we developed a phenomenological N-body model.

High-fidelity mechanistic platforms, such as Cardioid, require significant computation time to provide biophysically accurate simulations of cardiac electrophysiology.2 Our N-body approach, which is described in detail in Wyatt et al,3 represents the atrial wall as a system of interacting nodes and connectors. In this discrete approach, activation spreads as a timed signal transport with user-definable conduction velocities and refractory periods, rather than by solving for ionic current flow. This trade-off allows for the simulation of depolarization waves and textbook arrhythmias without numerically solving partial differential equations, making the model a responsive educational and exploratory tool for clinicians, students, and researchers. All computationally intensive portions of the code were parallelized, written in CUDA, and accelerated on NVIDIA graphics processing units, enabling smooth visualization and manipulation of model parameters.4

In our model, arrhythmias can be induced by altering refractory periods and conduction velocities, introducing ectopic foci, or creating structural discontinuities representing fibrosis. 1 method of simulating fibrosis is to ablate nodes to form a nonconducting core, surrounded by border zones with reduced conduction velocity and extended refractory periods. Virtual ablation lesions were applied by modifying node properties to eliminate conductivity within selected regions, allowing near-real-time assessment of atrial electrical behavior in response to different ablation configurations and patterns during live simulations.

We reproduced several clinically observed LA arrhythmias, including microreentrant circuits that emerged spontaneously when conduction heterogeneity or structural discontinuities were introduced; organized atrial flutter patterns that appeared when linear regions, such as isthmuses between pulmonary veins, exhibited reduced conduction velocity and refractory period; and disorganized fibrillatory activity generated by tuning local refractory periods and introducing multiple asynchronous ectopic triggers and reentrant circuits. The model supported virtual ablation experiments, in which targeted lesions reliably terminated arrhythmic activity. For instance, when ablation lines were placed across conduction isthmuses or around ectopic drivers, sinus rhythm was restored. Lesion placements could be manipulated live, allowing for near-real-time exploration of both effective and ineffective ablation strategies. In addition, observing that chaotic activity can arise from key reentrant sites made the arrhythmia more interpretable and enabled users to visually identify the most effective targets for ablation.

Consistent with observations in the idealized model, a patient-specific LA geometry constructed from open-source spatial imaging data exhibited comparable electrophysiological behavior under the same modeling conditions (see Figure 1E). These findings highlight the potential utility of the model as a personalized planning tool capable of capturing not only general arrhythmia mechanisms but also patient-specific ablation targets. The success of this open-source model has led to a collaborative project, supported by a grant from the National Institutes of Health, to create and study patient-specific models derived from computed tomography scan data. We acknowledge that although incorporating patient-specific anatomy is a critical first step, the path to personalized ablation planning will require further integration of complex physiological and electrical data.

This study demonstrates that a near-real-time interactive N-body model can capture LA electrophysiology with sufficient realism to support exploration of mechanisms leading to arrhythmias and ablation strategies. Although conventional high-fidelity simulations often require long computation times and fixed parameter sets, this approach provides an intuitive, interactive environment in which clinicians and researchers can probe conduction dynamics, test lesion sets, or visualize arrhythmia transitions during a live simulation.5 This platform may help bridge the gap between static computational models and the dynamic decision making required in the electrophysiology laboratory.

In conclusion, this near-real-time N-body model represents a novel and practical tool for studying LA arrhythmias and may support advances in personalized ablation planning and electrophysiology training.
