Hidden Talents in Medical Education: Visual Thinkers May Activate Intelligences Through Mind Mapping
Authors: Atiyeh Sadat Sajadi, Ashkan Karimi, Vahid Rahmani, Zohreh Khoshgoftar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis conceptual paper proposes that mind mapping may serve as a tool with the potential to transform students’ invisible clinical thinking into a tangible and assessable “clinical palette”. By visually representing data, analyses, connections, and decision-making processes, mind mapping is proposed to help unveil hidden layers of clinical reasoning while potentially engaging a wide range of intelligences, including spatial, logical, creative, fluid, intrapersonal, and interpersonal. It is hypothesized that this approach may reduce working memory load and facilitate the recognition of previously unperceived patterns, although these proposed cognitive mechanisms have not yet been empirically tested in clinical education settings. Mind mapping is thus presented not merely as a learning technique but as a candidate framework to potentially illuminate overlooked cognitive pathways and help identify latent abilities. In clinical settings, this tool could, in principle, depict the student’s cognitive journey from history-taking to diagnostic hypothesis generation and treatment planning, potentially making subtle and often overlooked aspects of clinical reasoning observable. To operationalize this proposed approach, clinical educators may consider integrating mind mapping into medical training through strategies such as brief 5–10 minute student-generated maps, establishing mind-mapping studios, integrating mind maps with clinical assessment tools, and developing faculty programs focused on teaching the art of visual thinking. As a conceptual perspective, this framework requires empirical validation including controlled and longitudinal studies, before its educational value and comparative advantage over related constructs such as concept mapping and illness scripts can be established.Keywords: medical education, mind mapping, clinical reasoning, multiple intelligences, visual thinking

Introduction

Visual thinking refers to the ability to translate verbal and textual information into symbols, images, structures, and meaningful connections. This mode of thinking requires the simultaneous engagement of multiple intelligences, including spatial, logical, creative, and fluid, and plays a crucial role in training competent medical students, physicians, specialists, and nurses. Students with this capacity can mentally visualize clinical data as a coherent image or algorithmic flow.1

The theoretical rationale for visual thinking in education draws on established cognitive-science frameworks. Cognitive Load Theory posits that working memory has limited capacity, and that instructional formats reducing unnecessary processing demands can free cognitive resources for meaningful learning.2 Dual Coding Theory similarly proposes that information encoded through both verbal and visual channels is processed via partially independent pathways, which may enhance comprehension and retention compared with verbal information alone.3,4 These frameworks provide a plausible theoretical basis for the potential benefits of visual tools such as mind mapping in education; however, their application to medical mind mapping specifically, and to the multi-intelligence claims advanced in this paper, remains largely theoretical and has not yet been directly tested in clinical training contexts.

In the era of artificial intelligence, medical education requires approaches beyond mere information recording and retrieval, approaches that generate “visual insight,” revealing hidden patterns, representing clinical narratives, and exposing talents often overlooked by conventional assessment systems. Implementing mind mapping in clinical contexts, a practical-artistic synthesis of cognition, emotion, and creativity, activates multiple intelligences simultaneously.5 Acting as a “clinical palette,” it enables students to visually externalize their cognitive process from history-taking to hypothesis formulation and treatment planning. The clinical palette thus represents a visual manifestation of clinical reasoning, making analysis, inference, connection-building, and decision-making observable and facilitating the development of students with diverse learning styles and strong visual-analytical skills.6

The Clinical Palette: A New Language for Clinical Understanding

The clinical palette can be defined as a canvas of clinical thought, where students or practitioners trace their cognitive journey from observation to interpretation, from hypothesis formation to decision-making, using lines of logic, shades of inference, and symbols of experience. Within this palette, diverse elements of clinical reasoning, from cognitive schemas to professional intuition, come together, making typically invisible processes visible, reflective, and assessable. Mind mapping serves as a concentrated and powerful mirror of these hidden layers, reducing cognitive load, revealing previously unseen patterns, and highlighting subtle reasoning processes. As such, mind mapping transcends a mere learning technique; it illuminates overlooked cognitive pathways and nurtures latent talents and intelligences.5

It is important to clarify that the clinical palette, as proposed in this conceptual paper, is not intended as a new cognitive tool that stands apart from mind mapping, but rather as a pedagogical lens through which mind-mapping products are interpreted for the purpose of revealing latent learner intelligences. To situate this proposal within existing educational constructs, it is useful to distinguish it from several related frameworks. Concept mapping, originally developed by Novak and Gowin, organizes propositional knowledge hierarchically around a focus question, with an emphasis on cross-links between concepts and evidence-based learning outcomes such as retention and critical thinking.7–9 Cognitive mapping, a term originating in the work of Tolman, refers more broadly to the mental representation individuals construct of spatial or conceptual relationships, and has since been extended to describe internal, largely non-graphical representations of knowledge structures.10–12 Illness scripts and diagnostic schemas, in contrast, describe organized knowledge structures that experienced clinicians retrieve, often unconsciously, to generate and refine diagnostic hypotheses; these are cognitive constructs residing in long-term memory rather than externally drawn artifacts.13,14 Visual Thinking Strategies (VTS) constitute a distinct, evidence-based pedagogical method that uses structured observation of works of art to cultivate visual literacy, tolerance of ambiguity, and diagnostic observation skills.1,15 The clinical palette, as proposed here, differs from these constructs in that it is not a memory structure, an art-observation protocol, or a knowledge-organization system in itself; rather, it is a proposed interpretive framework for using externally drawn mind maps as a window onto otherwise invisible dimensions of learner cognition and intelligence. This distinction remains a theoretical proposition that has not yet been empirically tested, and the added value of the clinical palette relative to standard mind-mapping assessment remains to be demonstrated in future research.

To date, the medical education literature has not examined whether the visual products of mind mapping can be systematically read as indicators of multiple intelligences; this conceptual gap is what the present perspective aims to address.

The Art of Mapping Thought: Mind Mapping in Clinical Practice

Mind mapping functions as the central instrument of this clinical palette. Through its branching structure, use of color, symbols, and hierarchy, it visually represents the flow of clinical reasoning: from data acquisition to scenario generation, integration of findings, prioritization of diagnoses, and formation of management plans. Beyond aesthetic value, artistic principles, composition, contrast, repetition, and visual direction, enhance the brain’s capacity for visual processing and facilitate rapid pattern recognition. By engaging with color, symbols, and intentional layout, students activate deeper cognitive processes, including analytical thinking, association-building, and problem representation.1

These proposed cognitive benefits should be understood as theoretical propositions extrapolated from Cognitive Load Theory and Dual Coding Theory rather than as findings directly demonstrated for clinical mind mapping.2,3,16 Empirical support for mind mapping in medical education to date is limited and comes largely from small-scale or pilot studies; a recent pilot study of small-group mind mapping reported improved learner engagement, but did not establish causal effects on diagnostic accuracy or long-term retention.5 Similarly, existing systematic reviews of related visual methods, such as Visual Thinking Strategies, note methodological limitations including small sample sizes, absence of control groups, and self-selection bias.1 Therefore, while it is plausible that mind mapping may facilitate the visual externalization of clinical reasoning, this remains an area requiring further controlled, longitudinal investigation.

The following illustrative vignettes are hypothetical scenarios constructed for explanatory purposes and do not represent documented case observations, published data, or empirical findings; they are intended solely to help readers conceptualize how the proposed clinical palette might, in principle, reveal latent intelligences. For example, a hypothetical student with average performance in written exams might potentially reveal clinical patterns with remarkable precision when designing visual diagnostic algorithms, illustrating how the integration of spatial-visual and fluid intelligence could, in theory, be reflected in mind-mapping products. A hypothetical resident who participates minimally in verbal discussions might, under this proposed framework, become a “visual analyst” when mapping complex clinical cases, illustrating how linguistic-visual and intrapersonal intelligences could potentially be depicted through clinical relationship mapping. Similarly, a hypothetical trainee who appears slow in theoretical discussions might conceivably exhibit unique problem-solving skills when designing innovative clinical pathways, illustrating a possible activation of creative, fluid, and practical intelligences under this framework. These vignettes require empirical validation through observational or experimental studies before any claim of real-world occurrence can be made.

Drawing on four theoretical frameworks, Gardner’s Multiple Intelligences, Sternberg’s Triarchic Model, the CHC theory, and Goleman’s Emotional Intelligence, it is proposed that mind mapping may act as a multi-channel cognitive and social activator. Under this theoretical framework, mind mapping is hypothesized to enhance information processing while potentially illuminating a diverse set of human intelligences; however, this proposition remains conceptual and awaits empirical testing across each of the domains described below.1,8

Limitations, Boundary Conditions, and Implementation Challenges

Several limitations and boundary conditions warrant discussion before the clinical palette can be considered for broader educational application.

First, this paper does not assume that mind mapping benefits all learners equally or that visual representation is inherently superior to verbal or textual approaches to clinical reasoning. The proposal that mind mapping “reveals” diverse intelligences should not be interpreted as endorsing the broader “learning styles” hypothesis, according to which instruction should be matched to a learner’s presumed preferred modality; this hypothesis has not been supported by rigorous experimental evidence and has been characterized as an educational myth that researchers are urged to stop propagating.17 Rather, mind mapping is proposed here as one of several complementary tools that may make certain forms of reasoning visible for some learners in some contexts, not as a universally superior method.

Second, mind mapping may not be equally effective across all clinical topics or learner populations. A recent systematic review identified practical constraints in implementing mind mapping, including the effort required to construct high-quality maps, variability in short-term critical-thinking gains, and difficulties scaling the method to large class sizes; these constraints are directly relevant to the clinical palette proposal and have not yet been resolved.6 For highly linear, procedural clinical tasks, or for learners who find open-ended visual formats disorienting rather than clarifying, alternative or complementary formats (eg, checklists, algorithms, or structured note-taking) may be more appropriate; the clinical palette should therefore be considered one option within a broader instructional toolkit rather than a universal replacement for other formats.

Third, meaningful implementation faces practical barriers. Faculty require training not only in the mechanics of mind mapping but in how to interpret learners’ maps as indicators of underlying reasoning and intelligence, a skill that has not yet been formally described or validated. Learner variability, including differing levels of comfort with open-ended visual tasks, prior exposure to mapping techniques, and language or drawing proficiency, may affect both map quality and how confidently an educator can infer cognitive or intelligence-related patterns from it. Curricular integration is a further challenge: incorporating brief mapping exercises, studios, or assessment linkages into an already dense clinical curriculum requires institutional buy-in, protected time, and alignment with existing assessment frameworks, none of which is guaranteed.

Fourth, the pilot evidence available to date on mind mapping in medical education suggests short-term engagement benefits, but whether these benefits are sustained requires longer-term observation; existing pilot work explicitly notes that continuous benefits require follow-up beyond the initial intervention period.5 Until such longitudinal and comparative data exist, claims regarding the clinical palette’s educational value should be regarded as hypotheses to be tested rather than established conclusions.

Conclusion and Educational Recommendations

Based on the theoretical framework outlined above, it is proposed, rather than concluded, that mind mapping may facilitate the visualization of certain aspects of students’ clinical reasoning through the conceptual lens of the “clinical palette”, potentially drawing attention to latent talents that might otherwise go unrecognized. By providing a structured and visual platform, mind mapping may support the observation, analysis, and, in principle, the enhancement of learners’ less visible abilities, though this remains to be empirically demonstrated.

To implement this approach, we recommend that clinical educators integrate mind mapping into medical training and adopt the following strategies:

Have students create brief, 5–10 minute mind maps immediately after history-taking, serving as an operational “clinical palette.” This recommendation is grounded in the principle of retrieval practice, whereby the immediate, effortful recall and organization of newly acquired clinical information is thought to strengthen memory consolidation and schema formation more effectively than passive review; recent quasi-experimental evidence in health sciences education supports the retention benefits of immediate retrieval exercises, although this has not yet been tested specifically for mind mapping after history-taking.18

Design and implement mind-mapping studios to produce structured maps from clinical guidelines and to guide targeted feedback and reflective processes. The proposed mechanism draws on structured practice with iterative feedback, a principle broadly supported in skills-based medical education, though direct evidence for studio-based mind-mapping formats specifically remains to be established.

Integrate mind maps with clinical assessment tools, such as Mini-CEX and portfolios, to make various intelligences observable. This recommendation builds on the established role of workplace-based assessment in combining structured observation with reflective feedback to support clinical skill development; recent evidence indicates that Mini-CEX can strengthen clinical reasoning through observation, feedback, and self-reflection, though its combination with mind-mapping products specifically has not yet been studied.19

Develop faculty development programs focused on teaching and guiding the art of visual thinking through mind mapping. Systematic review evidence indicates that structured faculty development programs can improve educators’ knowledge, skills, and professional competence; applying this evidence to visual-thinking instruction specifically is a reasonable extrapolation that has not yet been directly tested.20

Organize group exercises and workshops for analyzing clinical scenarios and mapping decision-making pathways, enabling students to develop problem-solving and critical thinking skills in a visual format. This recommendation is consistent with pilot evidence that small-group mind-mapping activities can promote student engagement in diagnostic curricula, although causal effects on problem-solving or critical-thinking outcomes remain to be demonstrated.5

This paper is intended as a conceptual perspective rather than an empirical report, and none of the frameworks, distinctions, or recommendations proposed above should be regarded as established findings. To translate this vision into empirical evidence, longitudinal studies, randomized controlled trials, and process-oriented research are essential prerequisites, not optional next steps, to identify mechanisms, key components, and influential educational variables, and to determine whether the clinical palette offers measurable value beyond existing approaches such as concept mapping, illness scripts, or standard mind-mapping assessment. Until such evidence accumulates, the clinical palette should be understood as a hypothesis-generating framework intended to stimulate future research and pedagogical experimentation, rather than a validated educational tool ready for widespread adoption. Through future controlled investigation of this tool, educators and researchers may better understand whether and how the qualities of future medical professionals could be reflected within the lines and colors of their mind maps.
