On Bayes factor functions
Saptati Datta, Riana Guha, Rachael Shudde, Valen E. Johnson

Abstract
We describe Bayes factors functions based on the sampling distributions of z , t , χ 2 \chi^{2} , and F statistics, using a class of inverse-moment prior distributions to define alternative hypotheses. These non-local alternative prior distributions are centered on standardized effects, which serve as indices for the Bayes factor function. We compare the conclusions drawn from resulting Bayes factor functions to those drawn from Bayes factors defined using local alternative prior specifications and examine their frequentist operating characteristics. Finally, an application of Bayes factor functions to replicated experimental designs in psychology is provided.

Introduction

Bayes factors offer a potentially valuable alternative to P-values for summarizing the results of hypothesis tests. Unlike classical methods, Bayesian testing procedures quantify the evidence supporting both the null and alternative hypotheses and offer a clear interpretation of the relative probabilities assigned to the data by different hypotheses. However, the effectiveness of Bayes factors depends heavily on the specification of full probability models for both hypotheses, whereas classical tests require only the specification of a null hypothesis and a test statistic.

The need to specify prior densities on parameters that define alternative hypotheses (i.e., alternative prior densities), as well as prior probabilities for the hypotheses themselves, has limited the widespread adoption of Bayesian tests. When testing only two hypotheses, the latter issue is often sidestepped by assigning equal prior probability to each hypothesis or by simply reporting a Bayes factor. Nonetheless, defining alternative prior densities remains a significant challenge, one that entails a number of important substantive issues.

These issues relate to the fundamental definition of hypothesis tests
(
Tendeiro and Kiers 2019
;
van Ravenzwaaij and Wagenmakers 2019
)
. Precise null hypotheses often represent a mathematical approximation to the belief that an actual effect is "small" rather than exactly zero. Conversely, overly dispersed prior distributions, often centered on the null hypotheses, are used to define alternative hypotheses. For instance, in the test of a normal mean, Jeffrey-Zellner-Siow (JZS) priors impose Cauchy densities on a standardized effect even though a standard Cauchy prior assigns one-half prior probability to standardized effects greater than 1.0 in magnitude and 0.3 probability to standardized effects greater than 2
(
Rouder et al. 2009
)
. In many fields, such as the social sciences, medicine, and biology, standardized effects greater than 1.0 are rarely observed.

As a result, Bayes factors may reflect the relative evidence in support of idealized null hypotheses that exclude small effect sizes that are not scientifically important versus overly dispersed alternative hypotheses that assign a high probability to parameter values that are not scientifically plausible.

Related to these concerns, the dispersed priors used to define alternative hypotheses are usually local alternative priors densities that assign their highest probability to parameter values that are consistent with null hypotheses. For instance, the modes of JZS priors
(
Rouder et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
, intrinsic priors
(
Berger and Pericchi 1996
, e.g.,)
, and g-priors
(
Zellner 1986
, e.g.,)
all occur at parameter values that define precise null hypotheses. Such local alternative priors weaken evidence in favor of true null hypotheses because data generated under null hypotheses also support alternative hypotheses. Indeed, local alternative hypotheses typically assign a higher probability to data generated from the null hypothesis than to data generated from the alternative.

To address these issues, we advocate the report of Bayes factor functions (BFFs)
(
Johnson, Pramanik and Shudde 2023
)
. Instead of reflecting the value of a single Bayes factor, BFFs provide a summary of a continuum of Bayes factors obtained by centering alternative prior densities on values that correspond to scientifically plausible standardized effects.

As proposed in
(
Johnson, Pramanik and Shudde 2023
)
, BFFs were defined from the sampling distributions of classical test statistics with alternative hypotheses defined using a non-local moment prior and gamma prior densities.
In this article, we advance the application of BFFs in the following ways:

•

We apply BFF methodology to an expanded class of alternative prior density functions. In addition to other default choices, (e.g., JZS, g-prior, normal-moment), we consider inverse-moment and inverse gamma prior densities.

•

Using a recent result of
Wagenmakers and Grasman 2025
, we compare the expected posterior probabilities obtained using members of these classes of alternative priors.

•

We examine the properties of maxima of BFFs in approximating an “ideal” Bayes factor, which we define as the (unobservable) likelihood ratio between the null and data-generating parameter.

•

We present simulation studies to compare the operating characteristics of Bayesian testing procedures based on the proposed BFFs.

•

We demonstrate that BFFs provide significant advantages when aggregating evidence from replicated experiments or meta-analyses. By employing alternative priors centered on non-centrality parameters corresponding to the same standardized effect, we show that Bayes factors based on data from replicated experiments can be constructed by simply multiplying the Bayes factors from individual experiments. We argue that this construction is not appropriate if the non-centrality parameters for individual experiments are assumed to be independently drawn from priors that are not adjusted in a similar way.

•

We review method-of-moment estimators for dispersion parameters of non-local moment priors in meta-analyses and replicated designs, and derive new method-of-moment estimators for inverse moment priors in this setting.

The BFFs described in this article are defined from the sampling distributions of test statistics. We have adopted this approach for three reasons. First, it simplifies the specification of Bayesian hypothesis tests because the sampling distributions of test statistics under null hypotheses are known, and the sampling distributions of test statistics under alternative hypotheses often depend only on scalar non-centrality parameters. This fact simplifies the specification of priors necessary for a Bayesian hypothesis test. Second, many, if not most, hypothesis tests reported in the social sciences, biological sciences, and medical sciences are based on classical test statistics. Exposing methods to easily construct BFFs using results from these tests promises to significantly expand the application of Bayesian testing methods in these fields. Finally, constructing BFFs from test statistics makes it possible to easily aggregate information from published research studies in systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

BFFs can also be defined using full data likelihood functions and prior densities specified on parameters of arbitrary dimension. In such cases, Bayes factor surfaces can also be used to reflect the variation of Bayes factors as a function of prior hyperparameters
(
Franck and Gramacy 2020
)
, although interpreting these surfaces in common testing scenarios, especially in null hypothesis significance tests, can be challenging. It is also more difficult to aggregate information from Bayes factor curves for replicated experiments. A critical feature of the methodology described below is the expression of Bayes factors as interpretable functions of the discrepancy between null and alternative hypotheses.

2
Construction of BFFs

To illustrate the construction of a BFF based on a test statistic
x
, we assume the following parametric Bayesian framework inspired by null hypothesis significance testing. Specifically, we define the hypotheses as follows:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle x\sim f(\cdot\mid\lambda_{0}),\qquad\lambda_{0}\mbox{ known},

(1)

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle x\sim f(\cdot\mid\lambda),\qquad\lambda\sim g(\cdot\mid\psi).

(2)

Here,
f(\cdot\mid\lambda)
denotes the distribution of the test statistic parameterized by a non-centrality parameter
\lambda
, and
g(\cdot\mid\ \psi)
is a parametric family of prior distributions on
\lambda
under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
, indexed by
\psi
. Given a fixed value of
\psi
, the Bayes factor for the test can be expressed

BF_{10}(x{\,|\,}\psi)=\frac{\int_{\Lambda}f(x\mid\lambda)g(\lambda\mid\psi)d\lambda}{f(x\mid\lambda_{0})},

(4)

where
\Lambda
denotes the parameter space of
\Lambda
.

The Bayes factor function for a fixed value of the test statistic

x
is defined as a function of the hyperparameter
\psi

taking values in a parameter space

\Psi
,

\mbox{BFF}_{x}:\Psi\rightarrow[0,\infty),\quad\psi\mapsto BF_{10}(x\mid\psi),

where
BF_{10}(x{\,|\,}\psi)
is the Bayes factor for the observed value of
x
under the specification indexed by
\psi
.
When the dependence on
x
is understood from context, we refer to this function simply as the BFF.

From the above discussion, if
\psi\mapsto\xi
is a bijection onto its image, it follows that the BFF can be equivalently reparameterized as a function of

\xi
. That is, there exists a function

\mbox{BFF}_{x}^{\xi}:\Xi\rightarrow[0,\infty),\quad\xi\mapsto BF_{10}(x\mid\psi(\xi)),

where
\Xi
denotes the image of the bijection

\psi\mapsto\xi
, and
\psi(\xi)
is the inverse mapping defining
\psi
as a function of
\xi
. A key component of the methodology advocated here is determining parameterizations of
\psi
that yield scientifically interpretable BFFs.

To illustrate these ideas, consider constructing a BFF for a two-sided
z
-test where the
data
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are independently distributed as
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma^{2}
known. The test statistic
z=\sqrt{n}x/\sigma
follows a

N(\sqrt{n}\omega,1)
distribution, where
\omega:=\mu/\sigma
represents the standardized effect. The null hypothesis is
\mu=0
, or
equivalently
\omega=0
. For the alternative hypothesis, instead of specifying a prior on

\mu
, we specify a prior on the non-centrality parameter
\lambda:=\sqrt{n}\omega
.

For illustration, we assume an inverse-moment prior density for
\lambda
. That is, we assume that
\lambda
is drawn from an inverse-moment prior density
{i}(\lambda{\,|\,}\tau,\nu)
of the form
(
Johnson and Rossell 2010
)

i(\lambda{\,|\,}\tau,\nu)=\frac{\tau^{\frac{\nu}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}(\lambda^{2})^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}\right),\ -\infty<\lambda<\infty,\quad\tau,\nu>0.

(5)

The modes of this density occur at

\lambda=\pm\sqrt{2\tau/(\nu+1)}.

(6)

For one-sided tests, the densities
i^{+}(\cdot{\,|\,},\tau,\nu)
and
i^{-}(\cdot{\,|\,},\tau,\nu)

are defined as
2i(\cdot{\,|\,},\tau,\nu)
, but constrained to either the positive or negative real lines, respectively. Fig.
shows several examples of inverse-moment densities. We denote the corresponding distribution function by
\mathcal{I}(\lambda\mid\tau,\nu)
.

Given fixed values of
(\tau,\nu)
, a hypothesis test can then be specified as

H_{0}:z\sim N(0,1),

H_{1}:z\mid\lambda\sim N(\lambda,1),\quad\lambda\mid\tau,\nu\sim\mathcal{I}(\lambda{\,|\,}\tau,\nu).

Under the null hypothesis, the marginal density of
z
is a standard normal density, while under the alternative hypothesis the marginal density of
z
is

m_{1}(z\mid\tau,\nu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{(z-\lambda)^{2}}{2}\right]\frac{\tau^{\frac{\nu}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}(\lambda^{2})^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\,d\lambda.

(7)

Although this one-dimensional integral cannot be evaluated analytically, it can be evaluated numerically in many statistical packages, including R.

We denote the Bayes factor in favor of
H_{1}
by
BF_{10}(z{\,|\,}\tau,\nu)=m_{1}(z\mid\tau,\nu)/m_{0}(z)
. The logarithm of the Bayes factor in favor of the alternative,
\log BF_{10}(z{\,|\,}\tau,\nu)
, is called the weight of evidence, WOE.

Different values of
(\tau,\nu)
correspond to different alternative hypotheses. In defining the BFF for this test, it is helpful to specify
(\tau,\nu)
based on hypothesized prior modes under the alternative. For instance, suppose that a hypothesized prior mode under the alternative hypothesis is
\lambda_{1}=\sqrt{n}\omega_{1}
. Assuming that
\nu
is fixed, then from equation (
6
) the value of
\tau
that places the prior mode at
\lambda_{1}
is

\tau_{\omega_{1},\nu}=\frac{n(\nu+1)\omega_{1}^{2}}{2}.

Thus, the Bayes factor can be expressed as a function of the standardized effect
\omega_{1}
, and a BFF can be defined as the mapping of standardized effects to Bayes factors obtained as
\omega_{1}
is varied over its domain.

Criteria for selecting
\nu
for this particular class of priors are discussed below.

To make this example more concrete, suppose

2.1
Expected posterior probability assessment of prior distributions

Wagenmakers and Grasman 2025
recently exposed the following equality, where
f
and
g
are prior predictive distributions for two models,
{\cal{M}}_{f}
and
{\cal{M}}_{g}
, each assigned equal prior probability:

\int f(x)\frac{f(x)}{f(x)+g(x)}dx=\int g(x)\frac{g(x)}{f(x)+g(x)}dx.

(8)

Assuming prior equipoise, this equality states that the expected posterior probability of
{\cal{M}}_{f}
when
{\cal{M}}_{f}
is true equals that of
{\cal{M}}_{g}
when
{\cal{M}}_{g}
is true. Following Wagenmakers and Grasman, we denote the value of this discrepancy measure by
{\rm D_{EP}}(f\parallel g)
.

We propose using this measure to compare BFFs defined by different alternative prior distributions. Specifically, letting
{\cal M}_{0}
denote the null model
H_{0}
, and
{\cal M}_{1a}
and
{\cal M}_{1b}
denote two alternative models indexed by a common parameter
\xi
, we compare
{\rm D_{EP}}(0\parallel 1a(\xi))
to
{\rm D_{EP}}(0\parallel 1b(\xi))
. Larger values of
{\rm D_{EP}}
imply higher expected posterior probability support of the true model, whether null or alternative.

When comparisons are restricted to non-local priors, the prior mode provides a natural indexing parameter.
However, local alternative priors used in classical null hypothesis significance tests are often centered on the null value, making the mode unsuitable for indexing. Additionally, certain priors–such as the Cauchy–do not possess finite moments and so cannot be indexed by means or standard deviations. To accommodate these cases, we index alternative prior parameters by their interquartile range (IQR) in two-sided tests and by their medians in one-sided tests. This parameterization provides a consistent and interpretable basis for comparing the performance of BFFs across a range of prior distributions.

The
DEP
measure can be used to compare BFFs defined using several testing scenarios based on
t
tests.

2.3
Maxima of BFFs

BFFs provide a convenient summary of evidence in favor of alternative hypotheses defined by prior densities centered on a range of standardized effect sizes. In practice, however, it is tempting to select the maximum value of the BFF curve to summarize evidence in favor of an alternative hypothesis. We examine the sampling properties of this strategy in Section 3, but note that selecting the maximum of the BFF in favor of the alternative hypothesis precludes collection of evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. As previously noted, the maximum of a BFF cannot be less than 0, corresponding to an alternative hypothesis that is close to the null hypothesis. Viewed from a slightly different perspective, the performance of the BFF near small effect sizes reflects that fact that it is not possible to detect arbitrarily small deviations from a null hypothesis based on a finite sample of data. But how small is too small?

To explore this question, we revisit the
z
test described previously, but now with a simple alternative hypothesis,
H_{1}:\lambda=\sqrt{n}\omega
. It follows that the logarithm of
BF_{10}(z)
is

\log BF_{10}(z)=\sqrt{n}\omega z-\frac{n\omega^{2}}{2}.

Under the null hypothesis in which
\omega=0
, the expected weight of evidence is simply

E\left[\log BF_{10}(z)\right]=-\frac{n\omega^{2}}{2}.

Our criterion for choosing the minimum threshold over which the BFF is maximized is to assume that an experiment is designed so that the expected WOE in favor of a true null hypothesis is at least
3
. This threshold lies on the boundary between positive and strong evidence in favor of the null
(
Kass and Raftery 1995
)
. Based on this criterion, we recommend a minimum threshold of
\omega_{\min}=\sqrt{6/n}
.

Adopting this criterion when reporting the maxima of the BFF has several consequences. If both hypotheses are assigned one-half prior probability,
\mbox{WOE}=-3
implies that the posterior probability of the null hypothesis is 0.95. Also under the null hypothesis, the probability of observing evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis is
P(\mbox{WOE}>0)=0.11
, while the probability of observing positive WOE in favor of the alternative hypothesis is
P(\mbox{WOE}>1)=0.05
.

In evaluating the operating characteristics of maxima of BFFs in the next section, we adopt this standard and examine constrained maxima, under the constraint that only priors centered on non-centrality parameters corresponding to standardized effects
\omega>\sqrt{6/n}
are considered.

For the example above with
N=100
and
z=1
, the maximum BFF for
\omega>0.245
is
-1.46
, representing positive WOE for the null hypothesis.

3
Operating characteristics

We now compare the performance of Bayes factor functions (BFFs) based on test statistics and inverse moment priors to other Bayesian testing approaches. Caution must be exercised when making such comparisons, as the Bayesian model that best aligns with the true simulation scenario typically performs the best, and there is no definitive criterion for choosing the simulation model. For this reason, we evaluate the frequentist operating characteristics of Bayesian methods for data generated from simple alternative hypotheses. Our comparison metric is the weight-of-evidence (WOE) provided by each method. We examine the operating characteristics for F and
\chi^{2}
-tests under both null and alternative hypotheses.

3.1
F-tests

Suppose

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{e},

where
\mathbf{X}
, an
n\times p
matrix, has full rank and
\bm{e}\sim N_{n}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
. We test
H_{0}:\bm{\beta}=0
against
H_{1}:\bm{\beta}\neq 0
based on the
F

F_{p,n-p}(\lambda)

f_{p,n-p}(x\mid\lambda)
the corresponding
f

The null hypothesis in this study is that there is no correlation between persistence and conscientiousness measures. To model the sample correlation coefficients, we rescaled Fisher’s z-transformation of the sample correlation coefficient
Fisher 1915
and defined

z_{i}=\frac{\sqrt{n_{i}-3}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+\hat{\rho}_{i}}{1-\hat{\rho}_{i}}\right).

(11)

Here,
\hat{\rho}_{i}
denotes the sample correlation coefficient for study
i
, and
\rho_{i}
denotes the true correlation coefficient. Let
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{20})
.

We make the asymptotic assumption that

\qquad z_{i}{\sim}N\left(\lambda_{i},1\right),

(12)

where

\lambda_{i}=\frac{\sqrt{n_{i}-3}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+\rho_{i}}{1-\rho_{i}}\right)

Let
{\bf z}=\{z_{i}\}
.

Under the null hypothesis, we assume
H_{0}:\mathbf{\rho}=\mathbf{0}
. Under the alternative hypothesis, we define the standardized effect to be

\omega=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\right)

(13)

where
\rho
is the population correlation coefficent,
and assume that
\lambda_{i}
, given
(\tau,\nu)
, are generated independently according to

\lambda_{i}\mid\omega,\nu\sim i(\lambda\mid\tau,\nu),\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\tau_{\omega,\nu,i}=\frac{(n_{i}-3)(\nu+1)\omega}{2}.

(14)

This choice of
\tau_{\omega,\nu,i}
places the modes of the prior distributions on the non-centrality parameters at
\sqrt{n_{i}-3}\,\omega
.
A fixed effect model, in which
\rho_{i}=\rho
for all
i
, is obtained by letting
\nu
become large.

Because the non-centrality parameters
\lambda_{i}
are assumed to be drawn independently from inverse-moment prior densities and the replications of the experiments are also assumed to be conditionally independent, it follows that the Bayes factors based on 20 replications of the experiment, given
\nu
and
\tau
, can be expressed as the product of Bayes factors from the individual experiments. From the construction of Bayes factors in Section
2
, it follows that the combined Bayes factors are

\displaystyle BF_{10}(\mathbf{z}\mid\omega,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\cdots\int\prod_{i=1}^{20}n(z_{i}{\,|\,}\lambda_{i},1)i(\lambda_{i}{\,|\,}\tau_{\omega,\nu,i},\nu)d\lambda_{i}}{\prod_{i=1}^{20}n(z_{i}{\,|\,}0,1)}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{20}BF_{10}(z_{i}|\tau_{\omega,\nu,i},\nu).

(16)

We stress that this expression is not valid if a single non-centrality parameter is assumed to generate all
z_{i}
values. In such a case, the joint marginal density of
\bf z
would not decompose into a product of marginals. Additionally, the assumption of conditional independence among the components of
{\bf z}
is only reasonable if the prior distribution on
{\lambda_{i}}
is properly defined. This means that the mode of the inverse moment prior on
\lambda_{i}
should be adjusted based on the values of
n_{i}
and
\omega
.

5
Discussion

By indexing Bayes factors by the mode of the prior density used to define alternative hypotheses, Bayes factor functions are able to provide useful summaries of evidence in favor of null and alternative hypotheses for priors centered on a range of standardized effects. In this article, we compared inferences based on BFFs based on test statistics to other commonly used Bayesian tests.

Our examination of the operating characteristics of these tests suggest the following:

•

BFFs typically provide more evidence in favor of true null hypotheses when alternative hypotheses stipulate standardized effects greater than
\sqrt{6}/n
in magnitude. For smaller standardized effect sizes, none of the tests considered provided, on average, strong support of true null hypotheses.

•

BFFs provided more evidence in favor of true alternative hypotheses than other Bayesian testing procedures in the simulations performed. In these experiments, the maxima of Bayes factor functions provided accurate estimates of the “ideal” WOE, where the ideal WOE represented the log-likelihood ratio between the data-generating and null parameter values.

•

By indexing Bayes factors according to effect sizes, BFF functions provide a convenient mechanism for combining evidence from test statistics reported in replicated studies.

Future research on BFFs will investigate the application of BFF methodology in meta-analyses in which heterogeneous test statistics are reported, and the extension of BFFs beyond Bayes factors based on test statistics.
