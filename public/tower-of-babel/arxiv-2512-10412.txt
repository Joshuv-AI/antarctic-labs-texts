Vortex atmospheres of traveling vortices: rigorous definition, existence, and topological classification
Kyudong Choi, In-Jee Jeong, Young-Jin Sim

Abstract
. In incompressible and inviscid fluids, the vortex atmosphere refers to the collection of fluid particles outside the support of a traveling vortex that are nevertheless carried along with it. This phenomenon has been recognized since the nineteenth century, e.g., in the classical works of O. Reynolds [Nature, 1876] and O. Lodge [Lond. Edinb. Dubl. Phil. Mag., 1885], yet rigorous mathematical definitions and proofs have remained largely undeveloped, with most subsequent studies relying on thin-core approximations or asymptotic analyses. In this paper, we give a rigorous definition of a vortex atmosphere and establish its existence and uniqueness. We further compare the planar atmosphere surrounding a 2D vortex dipole with the axisymmetric atmosphere surrounding a 3D vortex ring. In particular, we emphasize and prove the topological distinctions observed by W. Hicks [Lond. Edinb. Dubl. Phil. Mag., 1919]: under natural assumptions, every 2D dipole with its atmosphere forms an oval-shaped region, whereas for 3D rings, both spheroidal and toroidal configurations may occur. Our proof is based on showing that each atmosphere can be characterized precisely as a specific superlevel set of its corresponding stream function.



1.
Introduction

A
traveling vortex
is an Euler flow whose vorticity distribution translates rigidly at a constant velocity. The region where the vorticity is nonzero—called the vortex core—maintains its shape while being transported. Typical examples include a three-dimensional axisymmetric vortex ring (e.g., a smoke ring in air or a bubble ring in water) and a counter-rotating symmetric vortex pair (a dipole) in two dimensions. Although classical studies have revealed many properties of the vortex core, they offer little insight into
how the surrounding irrotational fluid is carried along
. In this paper, we revisit this aspect with a precise analysis of the transport structure outside the core.

A key feature of this phenomenon is that the vortex induces a coherent transport of irrotational fluid outside its core. As a vortex moves through a fluid, it may entrain part of the irrotational flow around it. In particular, some fluid particles outside the core revolve around it and are carried along with it; these particles constitute what may be informally viewed as a free-rider region, since they are transported by the vortex without contributing to its momentum. Others, however, fail to match the vortex’s speed and inevitably lag behind.

From this perspective, we may distinguish three regions of fluid with distinct characteristics

1.1.
Underlying mechanisms and main assumptions

Our primary approach is to analyze the streamline configuration in the moving frame. By viewing a traveling vortex as a stationary object in this frame, we describe the corresponding vortex domain as the set of all fluid particles whose trajectories form bounded streamlines, thereby distinguishing those that are genuinely captured and transported by the vortex from those that merely pass by. This characterization allows us to identify each vortex domain as a specific superlevel set of the relative stream function.

Within this framework, we also describe the possible geometric shapes of a given vortex domain. As observed in previous studies, whether the fluid speed at the ring’s center is smaller, equal to, or greater than the ring’s traveling speed determines whether particles near the center remain with the ring or lag behind, and this distinction dictates the resulting configuration of the vortex domain. Our analysis shows that this dependence on the center speed arises from the monotonicity and directional bias of the velocity field in the irrotational region. In this region, the stream function satisfies a homogeneous second-order linear equation, which allows us to determine the signs of certain first and second derivatives. These sign properties yield monotonicity and directional information for the velocity components. Comparing the center speed with the traveling speed then reveals precisely how particles near the center move, thereby enabling a rigorous determination of the geometric shape of the vortex domain.

The relationship between the center speed and the traveling speed–noted as the determinant of the vortex domain geometry–is precisely what distinguishes a two-dimensional vortex
dipole
from a three-dimensional vortex ring. For a 2D dipole, the center speed strictly exceeds twice its translation speed. Consequently, its vortex domain must contain the center and is therefore always an oval-shaped region surrounding the dipole. This phenomenon arises from the structure of the dipole: each of its two vortices induces a flow field that reinforces the motion of its counterpart, producing the strongest superposition at the dipole’s midpoint; see Figure
2
.

In contrast, for a 3D
ring
, although each cross-section in the meridional half-plane resembles the upper piece of a counter-rotating dipole, the ring constitutes a connected vorticity distribution in 3D rather than two localized vortices. Hence, the ring’s center is not directly influenced by the surrounding flow in the same manner. As a result, the possible configurations of vortex domains vary in the three-dimensional ring case, as illustrated in Figure
1
, unlike the rigidly oval structure arising in the two-dimensional dipole in Figure
2
.

2.
Mathematical setting

In this paper, we consider incompressible and inviscid fluids described by the Euler equations of the vorticity form in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mbox{ }\partial_{t}\bm{\omega}+({\bf v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla){\bf v}\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\mbox{ }\nabla\cdot{\bf v}=0,\mbox{ }\nabla\times{\bf v}=\bm{\omega},\\
&\mbox{ }\bm{\omega}|_{t=0}=\bm{\omega}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
\bm{\omega}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the 3D vorticity and
{\bf v}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the 3D velocity that is recovered from its vorticity
\bm{\omega}
by the 3D Biot-Savart law
{\bf v}=\nabla\times(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}\bm{\omega}
.

2.1.
Vortex dipole and vortex ring

One of the well-known solutions of the 3D Euler equations (
2.1
) is an axisymmetric vortex ring without swirl, which steadily propagates along the symmetry axis at a constant speed. Its 2D analogue is a counter-rotating vortex dipole, consisting of a pair of odd-symmetric vortices that are likewise translated at a steady speed in the direction of the symmetry axis. In the sequel, we provide the background and preliminaries needed for a rigorous treatment of steady dipoles and rings.

2.1.1.
Dipole

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the equations
(
2.1
)
are modified as

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mbox{ }\partial_{t}\omega+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\omega=0\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
&\mbox{ }{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}_{2}[\omega],\\
&\mbox{ }\omega|_{t=0}=\omega_{0}\end{aligned}\right.

where
\omega:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the 2D vorticity and
{\bf u}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the 2D velocity given by the 2D Biot-Savart law
{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}_{2}[\omega]=\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{2}})^{-1}\omega
where
\nabla^{\perp}=(\partial_{x_{2}},-\partial_{x_{1}})
.
To model a counter-rotating dipole, we consider a traveling vortex solution to (
2.2
) having the odd-symmetry.

Definition 2.1

(Vortex dipole)
.

A function
\overline{\omega}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
is called a vortex dipole if it is odd-symmetric:

\overline{\omega}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\overline{\omega}(x_{1},-x_{2})\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

non-negative in the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
, and satisfies the following properties:

(i) The set
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\overline{\omega}({\bf x})\neq 0\}
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(up to a set of measure zero). It is called the core of the vortex dipole
\overline{\omega}
.

(ii) It is a traveling solution to (
2.2
) in the sense that, for some constant
W>0
, the function
\omega:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\omega(t,(x_{1},x_{2}))=\overline{\omega}(x_{1}-Wt,x_{2})
solves (
2.2
). The constant

is called the traveling speed of the vortex dipole
\overline{\omega}
.

2.1.2.
Ring

For an axisymmetric fluid without swirl, the 3D vorticity
\bm{\omega}
in (
2.1
) admits its angular component
\omega^{\theta}
only in the usual cylindrical coordinate system

\bm{\omega}=\omega^{\theta}(z,r)\cdot(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0).

Then we can rewrite (

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mbox{ }\partial_{t}\xi+{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\xi\mbox{ }=\mbox{ }0\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\mbox{ }{\bf v}=\mathcal{K}_{3}[\xi],\\
&\mbox{ }\mbox{ }\xi|_{t=0}\mbox{ }=\mbox{ }\xi_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
{\bf v}=\mathcal{K}_{3}[\xi]
denotes the 3D axi-symmetric Biot-Savart law.

Definition 2.2
.

(Vortex ring)
A non-negative function
\overline{\xi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R})
is called a vortex ring if it is axisymmetric and satisfies the following properties:

(i) The set
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\overline{\xi}({\bf x})>0\}
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(up to a set of measure zero). It is called the core of the vortex ring
\overline{\xi}
.

(ii) It is a traveling solution to (
2.3
) in the sense that, for some constant
W>0
, the axisymmetric function
\xi:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\xi(t,(z,r))=\overline{\xi}(z-Wt,r)
solves (
2.3

W
is called the traveling speed of the vortex ring
\overline{\xi}
.

2.2.
Vortex domain and atmosphere

We give a rigorous definition of the vortex atmosphere, a notion that, to our knowledge, has not been formalized before.

Definition 2.3
.

For any vortex ring
\overline{\xi}
(Definition
2.2
) with a traveling speed
W>0
, we say a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the “vortex domain” of
\overline{\xi}
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i) For the 3D flow map
\Phi_{3}(t,\cdot)
generated by the relative vorticity
\xi
given as
\xi(t,(z,r)):=\overline{\xi}(z-Wt,r)
, we have

\Phi_{3}(t,\Omega)=\{(r,\theta,z+Wt)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(r,\theta,z)\in\Omega\}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\geq 0.

(ii) Any proper superset
\widetilde{\Omega}\supsetneq\Omega
, which is open and bounded, fails to satisfy (i).

Moreover, the set
\Omega-\overline{\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\overline{\xi}({\bf x})>0\}}
is called the “vortex atmosphere” of
\,\overline{\xi}
, which is the vortex domain minus the closure of the vortex core.

Definition 2.4
.

For any vortex dipole (Definition
2.1
) with a traveling speed
W>0
, we say a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the “vortex domain” of
\overline{\omega}
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i) For the 2D flow map
\Phi_{2}(t,\cdot)
generated by the vorticity
\omega
given as
\omega(t,(x_{1},x_{2})):=\overline{\omega}(x_{1}-Wt,x_{2})

\Phi_{2}(t,\Omega)=\{(x_{1}+Wt,x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega\}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\geq 0.

(ii) Any proper superset
\widetilde{\Omega}\supsetneq\Omega
, which is open and bounded, fails to satisfy (i).

Moreover, the set
\Omega-\overline{\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\overline{\omega}({\bf x})\neq 0\}}
is called the “vortex atmosphere” of
\,\overline{\omega}
, which is the vortex domain minus the closure of the vortex core.

2.4
, we present both nonempty and empty vortex atmosphere configurations.

Remark 2.5

(Uniqueness)
.

The uniqueness of a vortex domain follows directly from the maximality condition stated in (ii)-Definition
2.3
and (ii)-Definition
2.4

\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
corresponding to a vortex ring
\overline{\xi}

\Phi_{3}(t,\Omega\cup\Omega^{\prime})=\Phi_{3}(t,\Omega)\cup\Phi_{3}(t,\Omega^{\prime})\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\geq 0.

The same conclusion holds for vortex dipoles.

Proposition 2.6

(Existence)

For any vortex ring (Definition

for which one can apply the estimate (1.8) in
[
51
]
:

\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\overline{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|\overline{\xi}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\|r^{2}\overline{\xi}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}<\infty
. For the point
{\bf x}=(r,\theta,z)\in\overline{\Omega_{n}^{\prime}}
in [Case II], there exists the starting point
{\bf x}^{\prime}=(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\overline{\Omega_{n}}
such that

\Phi_{3}(\frac{1}{C_{0}},{\bf x}^{\prime})={\bf x}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})

by (i)-Definition
2.3
. For each
t\in[0,\frac{1}{C_{0}}]
, we have

\left|\Phi_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})-[{\bf x}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})]\right|\leq\sup_{t\geq 0}\|{\bf v}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\cdot(\frac{1}{C_{0}}-t)\leq C_{0}\cdot\frac{1}{C_{0}}=1

and hence

\displaystyle\Phi^{r}_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})+|\Phi^{z}_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\geq r+z+\frac{W}{C_{0}}-2\left|\Phi_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})-[{\bf x}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})]\right|\ \geq r+z-2=r+|z|-2>n-2,

which implies that
\Phi_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime})\in\{(r,z,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:r+|z|>n-2\}.
Thus we get
|{\bf v}(t,\Phi_{3}(t,{\bf x}^{\prime}))|<W/10
for each
t\in[0,\frac{1}{C_{0}}]
, which leads to

(2.5)

\left|{\bf x}^{\prime}-[{\bf x}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})]\right|=\left|{\bf x}^{\prime}-\Phi_{3}(\frac{1}{C_{0}},{\bf x}^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{W}{10C_{0}}

and therefore

z^{\prime}\geq z+\frac{W}{C_{0}}-\left|[{\bf x}^{\prime}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})]\right|\geq z+\frac{W}{C_{0}}-\frac{W}{10C_{0}}>z.

From the initial definition on
{\bf x}=(r,\theta,z)
, the above relation
z^{\prime}>z
implies that
{\bf x}^{\prime}=(r^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},z^{\prime})\notin\overline{\Omega^{\prime}_{n}}
and so
r^{\prime}+|z^{\prime}|\leq n.
However, by our previous observation (
2.5
), we get

r^{\prime}+|z^{\prime}|\geq r+z+\frac{W}{C_{0}}-2\left|{\bf x}^{\prime}-[{\bf x}+(0,0,\frac{W}{C_{0}})]\right|\geq r+z+\frac{4W}{5C_{0}}=r+|z|+\frac{4W}{5C_{0}}>n

which is a contradiction. It means that [Case II] does not occur. It completes the proof of Proposition
2.6
.
∎

By the above proposition(Proposition

), the vortex atmosphere exists and is unique (up to a set of measure zero).

2.3.
Streamline

Instead of treating a vortex ring or a vortex dipole as a traveling solution, we may view it as a stationary solution in the reference frame translating with constant speed
W>0
. In this moving frame, the closure of the vortex domain (core + atmosphere) can be characterized as the set of all bounded streamlines, or equivalently, as the collection of fluid particles whose trajectories remain bounded.

For a vortex ring
\overline{\xi}
and a point
(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, the streamline containing the point is defined as the
image in the
(z,r)
-plane
\Pi:=\{(z,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,r>0\}
of the trajectory map
\Phi_{3}(t,(z^{\prime},r^{\prime}))
in the moving frame containing the point:

\left\{\Phi_{3}(t,(z^{\prime},r^{\prime}))-Wt{\bf e}_{z}\in\Pi\,\,:\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset\Pi,

where we consider
the 3D axisymmetric flow map
\Phi_{3}=(\Phi_{3}^{z},\Phi_{3}^{r})
generated by

\xi(t,(z,r)):=\overline{\xi}(z-Wt,r)
. This streamline is everywhere parallel to the local velocity in the moving frame, which is
\overline{{\bf v}}-W{\bf e}_{z}:={\bf v}(0,\cdot)-W{\bf e}_{z}
, where
{\bf v}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the 3D velocity corresponding to the traveling vortex ring
\overline{\xi}(\cdot-Wt{\bf e}_{z})
with the unit vector
{\bf e}_{z}=(0,0,1)
of the
z
-axis in the usual cylindrical coordinate system. That is, each streamline in the
(z,r)
-plane
\Pi
is contained in some level set

\Gamma_{3}(\gamma^{\prime}):=\{(z,r)\in\Pi:\psi[\overline{\xi}]-Wr^{2}/2=\gamma^{\prime}\}\quad\mbox{for some}\mbox{ }\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}

where
the 3D axisymmetric stream function
\psi=\psi[\overline{\xi}]
satisfies the relation

\overline{{\bf v}}=\overline{v}^{z}{\bf e}_{z}+\overline{v}^{r}{\bf e}_{r},\quad\overline{v}^{z}=\frac{\partial_{r}\psi[\overline{\xi}]}{r},\quad\overline{v}^{r}=\frac{-\partial_{z}\psi[\overline{\xi}]}{r}

with the unit vector
{\bf e}_{r}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)
of the
r
-axis in the cylindrical system (e.g., see
[
36
, Chapter 1]
for a background).

Similarly, for a vortex dipole
\overline{\omega}
, the velocity is given as
\overline{{\bf u}}-(W,0):={\bf u}(0,\cdot)-(W,0)
where
{\bf u}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the 2D velocity corresponding to the traveling vortex dipole
\overline{\omega}(\cdot-(Wt,0))
.
Hence, the following level set

\Gamma_{2}(\gamma^{\prime}):=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]-Wx_{2}=\gamma^{\prime}\},\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}

gives a streamline in the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
, where the 2D stream function
\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
satisfies the relation

\overline{{\bf u}}=\left(\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}],-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]\right)

(e.g., see
[
1
, Chapter 1.2]
for a background).

To characterize (the closure of) a vortex domain as the collection of bounded streamlines, it is crucial to verify the boundedness of a level set containing a certain streamline. To this end, we observe the decays of
\psi[\overline{\xi}]/r^{2}
and
\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]/x_{2}
as
|{\bf x}|\to\infty
in Proposition

below. Its proof will be given in Appendix

.

Proposition 2.7
.

For any axisymmetric
\overline{\xi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
having a compact support, its 3D axisymmetric stream function
\psi[\overline{\xi}]
satisfies

\sup_{|(z,r)|\geq R}\frac{|\psi[\overline{\xi}](z,r)|}{r^{2}}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\mbox{ }R\to\infty

under the cylindrical coordinate system
(r,\theta,z)
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Similarly, for any odd-symmetric

By Proposition

•

For the case of

3.1.
Steiner symmetry

Before we compare a ring with a dipole, we introduce the definition of Steiner symmetry that will be frequently used throughout the remainder of the paper.

Definition 3.1
.

(2D Steiner symmetry about
x_{2}
-axis)
We say a function
\omega:\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}\to[0,\infty)
has the Steiner symmetry if, for each
x_{2}>0
, we have

\omega(x_{1},x_{2})=\omega(-x_{1},x_{2})
for any
x_{1}\geq 0
and that

x_{1}\mapsto\omega(x_{1},x_{2})\mbox{ }\mbox{is non-increasing in}\mbox{ }x_{1}\geq 0.

We say the symmetry is strict if the map is strictly decreasing.
Similarly, we say a set
A\subset\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
has the Steiner symmetry if the characteristic function
\mathbf{1}_{A}:\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}\to[0,\infty)
has the Steiner symmetry.

Definition 3.2
.

(3D Steiner symmetry about
z
-axis)
We say an axisymmetric function
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
has the Steiner symmetry if, for each
r>0
, we have

\xi(z,r)=\xi(-z,r)
for any
z\geq 0
and that

z\mapsto\xi(z,r)\mbox{ }\mbox{is non-increasing in}\mbox{ }z\geq 0.

We say the symmetry is strict if the map is strictly decreasing.
Similarly, we say an axisymmetric set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
has the Steiner symmetry if the characteristic function
\mathbf{1}_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to[0,\infty)
has the Steiner symmetry.

and
3.2
). Many steady vortices that are known to have mathematical stability arise as maximizers of kinetic energy under suitable constraints; hence variational methods have been widely employed in their analysis (see, e.g.,
[

However, not all steady vortices arise from such variational principles. For instance, D. Cao et al.

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\pi r}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos\vartheta\left(r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right)}{\left[r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right]^{3/2}}\textrm{d}\vartheta\right)\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos\vartheta\left[f(\cos\vartheta)-f(-\cos\vartheta)\right]\textrm{d}\vartheta\right)\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}2(\cos\vartheta)^{2}f^{\prime}(s)\textrm{d}\vartheta\right)\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

for some
s=s(\vartheta,r,r^{\prime},z,z^{\prime})\in(-\cos\vartheta,\cos\vartheta)
, where

f(s)=\frac{\left(r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime}s+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right)}{\left[r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}s+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right]^{3/2}}

and

f^{\prime}(s)=r\left(-r^{\prime}D+3r^{\prime}\left(r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime}s+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right)\right)D^{-5/2}\quad\mbox{with}\mbox{ }D=r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}s+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}.

That is, we get

v^{z}(z,r)=\int_{\Pi}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}2(\cos\vartheta)^{2}\left(-r^{\prime}D+3r^{\prime}\left(r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime}s+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\right)\right)D^{-5/2}\textrm{d}\vartheta\right)\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}.

As we have

(r-r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\leq D\leq(r+r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2},

we get

\displaystyle|v^{z}(z,r)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Pi}r^{\prime 2}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(D+r^{\prime 2}+rr^{\prime}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2})D^{-5/2}\textrm{d}\vartheta\right)\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Pi}r^{\prime 2}\frac{(r+r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}{[(r-r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}]^{5/2}}\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}.

As
\lambda\to\infty
, we have
r^{\prime}\to\sqrt{2}
, and by assuming
r<1
, we get

|v^{z}(z,r)|\lesssim\int_{\Pi}\frac{1}{(1+(z-z^{\prime})^{2})^{3/2}}\xi_{\lambda}(z^{\prime},r^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}\lesssim 1

for sufficiently large
\lambda>0
. Since
W\gtrsim\log\lambda
as
\lambda\gg 1
due to Theorem 6.5 and Lemma 6.1 in
[
36
]
, we have

|v^{z}(z,r)|\leq\frac{W}{2}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }0\leq r\leq 1,\,z\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{given that}\mbox{ }\lambda\gg 1.

Step IV. Unbounded streamlines below the vortex core

It remains to show that there exists an unbounded streamline of the form
\{(z,l(z))\in\Pi:z\in\mathbb{R}\}
for some function
l:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)

that is in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and passes under the vortex core
\{\xi_{\lambda}>0\}
. It would imply that the 3D flow map
\Phi_{3}(t,\cdot)
starting below the streamline
\{r<l(z)\}
stays beneath the line (in the moving frame) for all time, i.e.,

\{\Phi_{3}(t,(z,r))-Wt{\bf e}_{z}:t\in\mathbb{R},\,r<l(z)\}\subset\{r<l(z)\},

while such a particle fails to hop on the moving frame
(W,0)
due to the bound of
z
-velocity
v^{z}
in
Step III
, which is
W/2
.

(Claim) If
\lambda\gg 1
is large enough, there exists an unbounded streamline
\{(z,l(z))\in\Pi:z\in\mathbb{R}\}
for some function
l:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)

that is in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, contained in the level set
\Gamma_{3}(\gamma^{\prime})=\{\psi-Wr^{2}/2=\gamma^{\prime}\}
for some
\gamma^{\prime}<0
,
such that

l(z)<\inf\{r>0:\,(z,r)\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\xi_{\lambda}\}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }z\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\lambda\gg 1
be large enough. Then we have

\lim_{r\to 0}\left(\frac{\psi(0,r)}{r^{2}}-\frac{W}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(v^{z}(0,0)-W\right)\leq-\frac{W}{4}\lesssim-\log\lambda<0

due to
Step III
. Then, we fix a small
L\ll 1
such that

\frac{\psi(0,L)}{L^{2}}-\frac{W}{2}<0.

We then put

\phi(z,r):=\psi(z,r)-\frac{W}{2}r^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma^{\prime}:=\phi(0,L)<0.

By
Step III
, we observe that for each
z>0
, the function
\phi(z,r)
decreases strictly in
r\in[0,L]
due to

\partial_{r}\phi(z,r)=\partial_{r}\psi-Wr=r(v^{z}(z,r)-W)<0.

Moreover, for each
z>0
, we have

\phi(z,0)=0>\gamma^{\prime}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\phi(z,L)<\phi(0,L)=\gamma^{\prime}

by
Step II
, and therefore there exists a unique constant
l=l(z)\in(0,L)
such that

\phi(z,l(z))=\gamma^{\prime}.

The same holds for
z<0
, with the relation
l(-z)=l(z)
due to the even symmetry of
\psi.
We put
l(0):=L
, and we obtain a streamline
\mathcal{S}
parametrized by
l:\mathbb{R}\to(0,L]
, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}:=\{(z,l(z))\in\Pi:z\in\mathbb{R}\}

contained in the level set

\Gamma_{3}(\gamma^{\prime})=\{\psi-Wr^{2}/2=\gamma^{\prime}\}.

We note that
\mathcal{S}
pass below the vortex core
{\mathrm{supp}}\,\xi_{\lambda}
since
\lambda\gg 1
and
L\ll 1
.

We can easily show that
l
is continuous. Since
\partial_{r}\phi(z,r)<0
for each
r\in(0,L),\,z\in\mathbb{R}
, we apply the implicit function theorem at each point
(z,l(z))
to verify that the extended streamline
l:\mathbb{R}\to(0,L]
is locally
C^{1}
. One can verify
l\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by the following relation

\psi(z,l(z))-W\frac{l(z)^{2}}{2}=\gamma^{\prime},

which leads to

l^{\prime}(z)=\frac{v^{r}(z,l(z))}{v^{z}(z,l(z))-W}\mbox{ }\Rightarrow\mbox{ }|l^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{2\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{W}<\infty

due to
Step III
and the estimate (1.8) in
[
51
]
:

\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|\xi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\lesssim\lambda^{1/2}.

It completes the proof of
(Claim)
, and so Theorem
3.7
is proved.
∎

4.
Simply-connected vortex domain

In this section, we extend our analysis by incorporating an additional geometric condition beyond Steiner symmetry, namely
simply-connectedness
. In particular, we show that if a vortex core is simply connected, then its vortex domain is also simply connected. This follows from the characterization of a vortex domain as a superlevel set of the associated stream function. Indeed, the examples used in the proofs of Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
are all simply connected, and hence their vortex domains arise precisely as superlevel sets.

Throughout this section, several arguments appear in both two and three dimensions with only minor modifications—for example, Lemmas
4.3
and
4.7
, and Lemmas
4.2
and
4.6
. Since one of the aims of this paper is to provide a careful, side-by-side analysis of the similarities and differences between two-dimensional vortex dipoles and three-dimensional vortex rings, we present the parallel arguments explicitly in order to keep the exposition complete and self-contained.

From now on, particle trajectories are understood in the moving frame.

4.1.
Dipole with simply-connected core

We begin by considering the case of a 2D vortex dipole. We give the main statement below, whose proof will be given at the end of this subsection.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\overline{\omega}
be any vortex dipole (Definition
2.1
) with a traveling speed
W>0
. In the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
, if
\overline{\omega}
has the Steiner symmetry (Definition
3.1
) with a simply-connected vortex core, then the corresponding vortex domain
\Omega
also has the Steiner symmetry and is simply connected in the half plane. In particular, the vortex domain is given by the superlevel set

\Omega=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]({\bf x})-Wx_{2}>0\right\}

in the half plane. Moreover, the vortex domain has an “oval-shape” in the sense that, for some constant
R\in(0,\infty)
and a function
l:[0,R]\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
l>0
in
[0,R)
,
l(R)=0
, and
l\in C([0,R])\cap C^{1}((0,R))
, we have

\Omega=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\in(0,R),\,|x_{1}|<l(x_{2})\right\}.

Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
below will be used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
that follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\overline{\omega}
be any vortex dipole (Definition
2.1
) with a traveling speed
W>0
. In the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
, if
\overline{\omega}
has the Steiner symmetry (Definition
3.1
), then its stream function
\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
has the strict Steiner symmetry in the half plane. Moreover, any point
{\bf y}=(y_{1},y_{2})\in\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}<0,x_{2}>0\}
satisfying

\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}](y_{1},y_{2})-Wy_{2}=:\gamma<0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}](0,s)-Ws>\gamma\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }s\in(-\gamma/W,y_{2})

is not contained in the corresponding vortex domain. See Figure
5
for a simple illustration for this.

Figure 5
.
In Lemma
4.2
, the flow map takes the particle at
{\bf y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
indefinitely away from the origin. For all time, this particle is imprisoned in the level set
\{\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]-Wx_{2}=\gamma\}
and asymptotically approaches the horizontal line
\{x_{2}=-\gamma/W\}
.

Proof.

We first note that the stream function
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
is given by

(4.1)

\mathcal{G}({\bf x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(-\log|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|\right)\overline{\omega}({\bf y})d{\bf y}=\int_{y_{2}>0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\log|{\bf x}-{\bf y}^{*}|-\log|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|\right)\overline{\omega}({\bf y})d{\bf y}

where
{\bf y}^{*}=(y_{1},-y_{2})
and satisfies

(4.2)

\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})=-\mathcal{G}(x_{1},-x_{2})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{G}(x_{1},0)=0

for any
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, due to the odd symmetry of
\overline{\omega}
. Moreover, the even symmetry
\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathcal{G}(-x_{1},x_{2})>0
is obvious, and for fixed
x_{1}>0
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle 2\pi\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{2}>0}(y_{1}-x_{1})\left[\frac{1}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}^{*}|^{2}}\right]\overline{\omega}({\bf y})\textrm{d}{\bf y}=\int_{y_{2}>0}\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}\mbox{ }(y_{1}-x_{1})}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|^{2}|{\bf x}-{\bf y}^{*}|^{2}}\overline{\omega}({\bf y})\textrm{d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}y_{1}}{(y_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2})(y_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+y_{2})^{2})}\overline{\omega}(y_{1}+x_{1},y_{2})\textrm{d}y_{1}\textrm{d}y_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}y_{1}}{(y_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2})(y_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+y_{2})^{2})}\underbrace{\left[\overline{\omega}(y_{1}+x_{1},y_{2})-\overline{\omega}(-y_{1}+x_{1},y_{2})\right]}_{\leq 0}\textrm{d}y_{1}\textrm{d}y_{2}

\displaystyle<0

by the Steiner symmetry of
\overline{\omega}
. It implies that
\mathcal{G}
has the
strict
Steiner symmetry.

Now, we suppose that there exists a point
{\bf y}=(y_{1},y_{2})\in\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}<0,x_{2}>0\}
satisfying

\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}](y_{1},y_{2})-Wy_{2}=:\gamma<0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}](0,s)-Ws>\gamma\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }s\in(-\gamma/W,y_{2}).

We will show that the negativity of
\gamma
prevents the point
{\bf y}
from being contained in the core. To this end, we will construct an unbounded streamline along which the particle initially at
{\bf y}
moves away from the origin in the moving frame indefinitely as
t\to\infty.

For each
x_{2}\in(-\gamma/W,y_{2}]
, we consider the equation

(4.4)

\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})-Wx_{2}=\gamma\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }x_{1}<0.

Since
\mathcal{G}(0,x_{2})-Wx_{2}>\gamma
for each
x_{2}\in(-\gamma/W,y_{2}]
, the
strict
Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
guarantees that the solution of (
4.4
) is uniquely determined by a continuous function
x_{1}=-f(x_{2})
for some
f:(-\gamma/W,y_{2}]\to(0,\infty)
with
f(y_{2})=-y_{1}
, whose differentiability is obvious from the differentiability and the
strict
Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
. We now consider the limit
x_{2}\downarrow-\gamma/W
. Since
\mathcal{G}>0
in
\{x_{2}>0\}
and
\mathcal{G}\to 0
as
|{\bf x}|\to\infty
,
we have
f(x_{2})\to\infty
as
x_{2}\downarrow-\gamma/W
. Otherwise, if
\liminf_{x_{2}\downarrow-\gamma/W}f(x_{2})\in[0,\infty)
, then for some subsequence
s_{n}\to-\gamma/W
, we have

0=\lim_{n\to\infty}Ws_{n}+\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{G}(-f(s_{n}),s_{n})=\mathcal{G}\left(-\liminf_{x_{2}\downarrow-\gamma/W}f(x_{2}),-\gamma/W\right)>0,

which is a contradiction.

We now consider the trajectory of a particle initially at
{\bf y}
. As its streamline must be contained in the level set of
\mathcal{G}-Wx_{2}
, and
the vertical velocity
\overline{u}^{2}=-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{G}
is negative in the left side
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}<0,x_{2}>0\}
(by (
4.3
)) the particle must travel along the curve
(-f(x_{2}),x_{2})
starting from
x_{2}=y_{2}
and heading to
x_{2}\downarrow-\gamma/W
without any standstill; see Figure
5
.
Indeed, if the particle cannot go below than
-\gamma/W+\epsilon
forever for some
\epsilon>0
, then we have a contradiction due to the fact

\sup_{s\in[-\gamma/W+\epsilon,y_{2}]}\overline{u}^{2}(-f(s),s)<0.

Thus, the particle starting from
{\bf y}
escapes and gets indefinitely far away from the origin. It completes the proof.∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\overline{\omega}
be any vortex dipole (Definition
2.1
) with a traveling speed
W>0
. In the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
, if
\overline{\omega}
has the Steiner symmetry (Definition
3.1
) with a simply-connected vortex core
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\overline{\omega}({\bf x})>0\}
, then the superlevel set

\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]({\bf x})-Wx_{2}>0\right\}

also has the Steiner symmetry (Definition
3.1
), is simply connected, and contains the vortex core
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\overline{\omega}({\bf x})>0\}
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.2
says that the stream function
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
has the
strict
Steiner symmetry in the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
. We will establish several observations related to the concavity of
\mathcal{G}
on the
x_{2}
-axis. Knowing that the vortex core is simply connected and has the Steiner symmetry in the half plane
\{x_{2}>0\}
, we can find two constants
0\leq R_{1}<R_{2}<\infty
such that

\{s>0:\overline{\omega}(0,s)>0\}=(R_{1},R_{2}).

\blacktriangleright

(Claim 1) The function
s\mapsto\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)
is strictly increasing
in the intervals
[0,R_{1}]
and
[R_{2},\infty)
, respectively.

Considering the convolution formula (
4.1
), we note that
\mathcal{G}
is infinitely differentiable at the point
{\bf x}\in\overline{\{\overline{\omega}\neq 0\}}^{c}
since the function
\log|\cdot|
in the integrand is infinitely differentiable without any singularity. In particular, for each
{\bf x}\in\overline{\{\overline{\omega}\neq 0\}}^{c}
, we have

(4.5)

-\Delta\mathcal{G}({\bf x})=\overline{\omega}({\bf x})=0.

For each
s\in(0,\infty)\setminus[R_{1},R_{2}]
, the Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
gives
\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}\mathcal{G}(0,s)\leq 0
. Indeed, we obtain the strict inequality
\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}\mathcal{G}(0,s)<0
by observing that, from (
4.3
), we get

\displaystyle 2\pi\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}\mathcal{G}(0,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{2}>0}\partial_{x_{1}}\frac{4x_{2}y_{2}(y_{1}-x_{1})}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|^{2}|{\bf x}-{\bf y}^{*}|^{2}}\Bigg|_{{\bf x}=(0,s)}\overline{\omega}({\bf y})\textrm{d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{2}>0}\frac{4sy_{2}\left[3y_{1}^{4}+2y_{1}^{2}(s^{2}+y_{2}^{2})-(s^{2}-y_{2}^{2})^{2}\right]}{\left[(y_{1}^{2}+(s-y_{2})^{2})(y_{1}^{2}+(s+y_{2})^{2})\right]^{2}}\overline{\omega}({\bf y})\textrm{d}{\bf y}

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}8sy_{2}\underbrace{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{3t^{4}+2t^{2}(s^{2}+y_{2}^{2})-(s^{2}-y_{2}^{2})^{2}}{\left[(t^{2}+(s-y_{2})^{2})(t^{2}+(s+y_{2})^{2})\right]^{2}}\overline{\omega}(t,y_{2})\textrm{d}t\right)}_{(*)}\textrm{d}y_{2}

where, since the numerator
3t^{4}+2t^{2}(s^{2}+y_{2}^{2})-(s^{2}-y_{2}^{2})^{2}
changes its sign only at

t^{2}=-\frac{a}{3}+\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}}{9}+\frac{b^{2}}{3}}=:T>0,\quad a=s^{2}+y_{2}^{2},\mbox{ }b=s^{2}-y_{2}^{2},

we have

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\cdots)\overline{\omega}(t,y_{2})\textrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{T}}(\cdots)\omega(t,y_{2})\textrm{d}t+\int_{\sqrt{T}}^{\infty}(\cdots)\overline{\omega}(t,y_{2})\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\mbox{ }\overline{\omega}(\sqrt{T},y_{2})\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{T}}(\cdots)\textrm{d}t}_{<0}\mbox{ }+\mbox{ }\overline{\omega}(\sqrt{T},y_{2})\underbrace{\int_{\sqrt{T}}^{\infty}(\cdots)\textrm{d}t}_{>0}=\mbox{ }\overline{\omega}(\sqrt{T},y_{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}(\cdots)\textrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mbox{ }\overline{\omega}(\sqrt{T},y_{2})\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{t}{(t^{2}+(s-y_{2})^{2})(t^{2}+(s+y_{2})^{2})}=0

(note that we have
(*)=0
only if
\overline{\omega}(\cdot,y_{2})\equiv 0
, which is impossible). Applying the strict inequality
\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}\mathcal{G}(0,s)<0
to the relation (
4.5
) gives us
\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}\mathcal{G}(0,s)>0
. This completes the proof of
(Claim 1)
.

\blacktriangleright

(Claim 2) The set
\{s>0:\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>0\}
is nonempty and contains the interval
(0,R_{2})
.

We recall that
\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,0)>2W
from Proposition
3.5
and that
\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)
does not decrease in
s\in[0,R_{1}]
from
(Claim 1)
, which imply that
\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>Ws>0
for each
s\in[0,R_{1}]
. Note that the set
\{s>0:\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>0\}
is nonempty in either cases of
R_{1}=0
or
R_{1}>0
. It remains to show

R_{2}\leq R:=\sup\{x_{2}>0:\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>0\mbox{ }\mbox{ }\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }s\in(0,x_{2})\}.

We suppose the contrary, i.e.,
R<R_{2}.
By the continuity of
\mathcal{G},
we have
\mathcal{G}(0,R)-WR=0
and so
R>R_{1}
. In sum, we have
R\in(R_{1},R_{2})=\{s>0:\overline{\omega}(0,s)>0\}
. Since the vortex core is open, there exists a small constant
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
such that
\overline{\omega}(-\varepsilon,R)>0
. Moreover, by the
strict
Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
, we have
\mathcal{G}(-\varepsilon,R)-WR=:\sigma<0
. Since
\mathcal{G}(0,x_{2})-Wx_{2}>0>\sigma
for each
x_{2}\in(0,R)
by the definition of

, Lemma
4.2
implies that the point
(-\varepsilon,R)
cannot be contained in the vortex domain (and hence in the vortex core), which is a contradiction to
\overline{\omega}(-\varepsilon,R)>0
. Thus, we conclude that
R\geq R_{2},
and this completes the proof of
(Claim 2)
.

\blacktriangleright

(Claim 3) We have
\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)<0
for
s\geq R_{2}
.

We observe that the derivative
\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)
is strictly increasing in

by
(Claim 1)
, while

as

as

. This means
\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)<0
for

. It completes the proof of
(Claim 3)
.

\blacktriangleright

(Claim 4) The superlevel set
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\mathcal{G}({\bf x})-Wx_{2}>0\}
is simply connected and has the Steiner symmetry.

We recall
(Claim 2)
and
(Claim 3)
, which are saying that the function
\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws
is positive for each
s\in(0,R_{2})
and decreases strictly in
s>R_{2}
. By the boundedness of the superlevel set
\{\mathcal{G}({\bf x})-Wx_{2}>0\}
(Proposition
2.7
), we have
\{s>0:\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>0\}=(0,R)
for some

. By the Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
, the superlevel set
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0,\,\mathcal{G}({\bf x})-Wx_{2}>0\}
has the Steiner symmetry and is simply connected. It completes the proof of
(Claim 4)
.

It remains to prove that the superlevel set

4.2.
Sadovskii vortex

Before we move toward the case of a 3D vortex ring, we consider a special form of a 2D vortex dipole: a
Sadovskii vortex
. It is a vortex dipole satisfying the Steiner symmetry in the half plane
\{x_{2}>0\}
and, in particular, exhibiting “touching” of the counter-rotating vortex pair in the sense of

\{|{\bf x}|<r\}\subset\overline{\{\overline{\omega}\neq 0\}}\quad\mbox{for some}\mbox{ }r>0.

We refer to
[
28
,
31
]
for backgrounds about this. Knowing that the vortex domain of every dipole has an oval shape in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(Theorem
4.1
), a natural direction to find a Sadovskii vortex has been to construct an odd-symmetric dipole
\overline{\omega}
satisfying

(4.11)

\{\overline{\omega}>0\}=\{\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]-Wx_{2}>0\}\quad\mbox{in the half plane}\mbox{ }\{x_{2}>0\}

(see
[
28
,
31
,
46
]
and the references therein) so that the entire vortex core
\{\overline{\omega}\neq 0\}
coincides with the oval vortex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e., the atmosphere is empty.

In what follows, we will verify that the atmosphere of a Sadovskii vortex is always empty, and vice versa when the core is simply connected in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
7
7

7

Typical examples are Chaplygin–Lamb dipole
[
26
,
49
]
and the Sadovskii patch found by Huang–Tong
[
46
]
.
. This would imply that characterizing a Sadovskii vortex by the relation (
4.11
) is indeed the only possible direction.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\overline{\omega}
be a vortex dipole (Definition
2.1
) has a simply-connected vortex core
\{\overline{\omega}>0\}
in the half plane
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}>0\}
. Then, the corresponding vortex atmosphere is empty if and only if
\overline{\omega}
is a Sadovskii vortex:

\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|{\bf x}|<r\}\subset\overline{\{\overline{\omega}\neq 0\}}\quad\mbox{for some}\mbox{ }r>0.

Proof.

Let
\overline{\omega}
be a vortex dipole having the traveling speed
W>0
. We first recall Theorem
4.1
, which saying that the vortex domain
\Omega
has an oval shape in that, for a constant
R>0
and a continuous
l:[0,R]\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
l>0
in
[0,R)
and
l(R)=0
, we have

\Omega\cap\{x_{2}>0\}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\in(0,R),\,|x_{1}|<l(x_{2})\}.

Hence, it is obvious that, assuming that the vortex core is simply connected in the half plane
\{x_{2}>0\}
, the empty atmosphere ((core)
\equiv
(vortex domain)) always implies that
\overline{\omega}
is a Sadovskii vortex.

For the other direction, we now assume that
\overline{\omega}
is a Sadovskii vortex. It suffices to show that the atmosphere is empty, i.e., the core coincides with the vortex domain
\Omega
. Knowing that the vortex core in the half plane
\{x_{2}>0\}
is simply connected, has the Steiner symmetry, and contains a ball
\{|{\bf x}|<r\}
for some
r>0
, we can find a constant
R^{\prime}\geq r
and a function
\widetilde{l}:[0,R^{\prime})\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
\widetilde{l}>0
in
[0,R^{\prime})
and

\{\overline{\omega}>0\}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\in(0,R^{\prime}),\,|x_{1}|<\widetilde{l}(x_{2})\}

(at this stage, the continuity of
\widetilde{l}
and the end-point value
\widetilde{l}(R^{\prime})
are unknown, but later it will turn out to be true by showing that
R=R^{\prime}
and
l\equiv\widetilde{l}
). We also bring
(Claim 2)
in the proof of Lemma
4.3
in here: the set
\{s>0:\mathcal{G}(0,s)-Ws>0\}=(0,R)
is nonempty and contains the interval
(0,R^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
is the stream function. This implies
R\geq R^{\prime}
.

\blacktriangleright
(Claim 1) We have
l\equiv\widetilde{l}
in the interval
(0,R^{\prime})
.

We clearly have
l\geq\widetilde{l}
, since the vortex domain must contain the vortex core. Suppose there exist
y_{2}\in(0,R^{\prime})
such that
l(y_{2})>\widetilde{l}(y_{2})
, and consider the point

{\bf y}=\left(-\frac{l(y_{2})+\widetilde{l}(y_{2})}{2},y_{2}\right).

Observe that the vortex domain contains
{\bf y}
, but the closure of the core does not. So for all time
t>0
, the particle initially at
{\bf y}
must stay in the vortex domain and, at the same time, not in the core. Since the point
{\bf y}
is in the left side
\{x_{1}<0\}
where the vertical velocity
\overline{u}^{2}=-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{G}
is negative (due to the
strict
Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
), the particle initially at
{\bf y}
must stay in the set

\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<x_{2}\leq y_{2},\,x_{1}\in(-l(x_{2}),-\widetilde{l}(x_{2}))\right\},

where the interval

(-l(x_{2}),-\widetilde{l}(x_{2}))
should be non-empty for each
0<x_{2}\leq y_{2}
.
Hence, the particle must be heading downward for all time without any standstill (see the proof of Lemma
4.3
for a similar argument). It implies that the particle goes down forever to meet
x_{1}
-axis at some point
(-L,0)

at time infinity satisfying the relation

0<\widetilde{l}(0)\leq L\leq l(0).

As every particle trajectory conserves the value of
\mathcal{G}-Wx_{2}
, we obtain the relation

\mathcal{G}(-L,0)-W\cdot 0=\mathcal{G}({\bf y})-Wy_{2}.

Here, a contradiction arises. The right-hand side is positive since
{\bf y}
is contained in the vortex domain
\Omega\cap\{x_{2}>0\}
given as the superlevel set
\{\mathcal{G}-Wx_{2}>0\}\cap\{x_{2}>0\}
(Theorem
4.1
), while the left-hand side is clearly
0
due to the odd-symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
(see (
4.2
) in the proof of Lemma
4.2
). It completes the proof of
(Claim 1)
.

\blacktriangleright
(Claim 2) We have
l(R^{\prime})=0
.

We suppose the contrary, i.e., assume
l(R^{\prime})>0
. From the result of
(Claim 1)
, we have
R>R^{\prime}
. Moreover, we may define
\widetilde{l}(R^{\prime}):=l(R^{\prime})
and consider the extension of
\widetilde{l}
such that
\widetilde{l}\equiv 0
in
(R^{\prime},R)
, since the vortex core is contained in
\{x_{2}<R^{\prime}\}
. Then, in the interval
(R,R^{\prime})
, we have
l>0
and
\widetilde{l}\equiv 0
. Consider any point
{\bf z}
in the nonempty set

\mathcal{A}:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\in(R^{\prime},R),\,x_{1}\in(-l(x_{2}),0)\}.

Since
{\bf z}
is in the vortex domain and not in the closure of the core, the particle initially at
{\bf z}
must be staying in the set
\mathcal{A}
. Here, the reason that the particle cannot cross the
x_{2}
-axis in a finite time is that the horizontal velocity is negative on the line segment
\{x_{1}=0,x_{2}\in[R^{\prime},R)\}
:

(4.12)

\overline{u}^{1}(0,s)=\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}(0,s)<0\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }s\in[R^{\prime},R)

(recall
(Claim 3)
in the proof of Lemma
4.3
). Since
\mathcal{A}
is in the left side
\{x_{1}<0\}
where the vertical velocity
\overline{u}^{2}=-\partial_{x_{1}}\mathcal{G}
is negative (due to the
strict
Steiner symmetry of
\mathcal{G}
), the particle must be heading downward for all time without any standstill, which means it needs to escape from
\mathcal{A}
by crossing the line segment

\mathcal{L}:=\{x_{1}\in[-l(R^{\prime}),0],\,x_{2}=R^{\prime}\}

in a finite time, so a contradiction. This completes the proof of
(Claim 2)
.

From
(Claim 1)
and
(Claim 2)
, we conclude that
R=R^{\prime}
and
l\equiv\widetilde{l}
in the interval
(0,R]
. We may redefine
\widetilde{l}(0)=l(0)
, if necessary. It means the vortex core
\{\overline{\omega}>0\}
coincides with the vortex domain
\Omega\cap\{x_{2}>0\}
, and hence the atmosphere is empty. It completes the proof of Theorem
4.4
. ∎

4.3.
Ring with simply-connected core

Unlike the 2D case (Theorem

(L,R)

5.
Open problems

In this final section, we give some open questions from a mathematical viewpoint.

(1)

Vortex atmosphere for general settings.

The notion of a vortex atmosphere naturally extends far beyond the setting of steadily translating vortices; for instance, it can be considered for co-rotating vortex pairs and even for non-rigid vortex motions. In 1992, Meleshko et al.
[
57
]
investigated the dynamics of vortex atmospheres in various multi-vortex configurations, such as the interaction of two vortex pairs with different circulations (see also
[
56
]
). In particular, they examined vortices exhibiting leapfrogging behavior and observed the existence and evolution of a
leapfrogging atmosphere
.

Given the recent surge of interest in the mathematical analysis of leapfrogging vortices
[
40
,
12
,
37
,
5
,
4
,
62
,
33
,
34
,
18
,
19
,
14
,
60
]
, determining whether a vortex atmosphere exists in a leapfrogging scenario–and, if so, understanding its geometric structure–appears to be a particularly compelling open problem.

(2)

Stability of vortex atmosphere.

Research on the stability of vortex solutions with steady (or rigid) motion is extensive (see, e.g.,
[
64
,
65
,
17
,
11
,
1
,
30
,
47
,
29
,
38
,
3
,
66
,
27
,
41
,
28
,
2
,
21
,
25
]
), and most studies have focused on the stability of the vorticity (or its stream function). See also
[
10
,
48
,
53
]
for shear flow settings.

However, the focus of stability analysis can instead be shifted from the vorticity to the vortex atmosphere. For instance, one may investigate the stability of a given vortex atmosphere by examining the topological or quantitative changes in its streamlines under perturbations. Such an analysis requires a notion of the vortex atmosphere for vortex solutions exhibiting non-rigid motion, as discussed in (1) above.

(3)

Ratio of
|Atmosphere|:|Core|
.

Hicks
[
43
]
calculated the volume of the atmosphere (the liquid carried forward) across a range of ratios between the core radius and ring radius. He pointed out that the thinner the ring is (compared to the ring’s size), the thinner its toroidal-shape atmosphere becomes. However, to our knowledge, the asymptotic rate of
|Atmosphere|:|Core|
remains open.

(4)

Existence of revolved-lemniscate atmosphere.

Although the atmosphere of revolved lemniscate type (Figure
1B
and the
case
\overline{v}^{z}(0,0)=W
in
[Case I]
of Theorem
4.5
)
has been discussed in the literature (e.g.,
[
43
,
35
,
54
]
), its rigorous existence remains open. Unlike the spheroidal and toroidal cases
(Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
), for which Hill’s spherical vortex
and the line-vortex ring serve as benchmark solutions, establishing the existence of
a revolved lemniscate atmosphere appears considerably more challenging, owing to the absence
of a suitable model problem that could serve as an analogue.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.7

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

For a given axisymmetric
\overline{\xi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with a compact support, its 3D axisymmetric stream function
\psi=\psi[\overline{\xi}]
satisfies
|\psi/r|=O(|(z,r)|^{-2})
as

|(z,r)|\to\infty
(see
[
36
, Lemma 1.1]
), which gives

|\psi(z,r)/r^{2}|\leq C_{0}r^{-1}|(z,r)|^{-2}\leq C_{0}r^{-3}
as
|(z,r)|\gg 1
, for some constant
C_{0}>0
. Moreover, as
\psi(z,0)=0
for each
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the mean value theorem tells us that for each
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
,

\frac{\psi(z,r)}{r^{2}}=\frac{(\partial_{r}\psi)(z,r^{\prime})}{r}\quad\mbox{for some}\mbox{ }r^{\prime}=r^{\prime}(z,r)\in(0,r).

For each
(z,r)
satisfying
|z|\gg 1
, we get
|(z,r^{\prime})|\gg 1
,
and hence we apply the relation
|\nabla\psi/r|=O(|(z,r)|^{-3})
as

|(z,r)|\to\infty
(see
[
36
, Lemma 1.1]
) to obtain that

|\psi(z,r)/r^{2}|\leq|\nabla\psi(z,r^{\prime})/r^{\prime}|\leq C_{1}|(z,r^{\prime})|^{-3}\leq C_{1}|z|^{-3}
for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Now, we consider a constant
R\gg 1
large enough and any point
(z,r)
satisfying
|(z,r)|\geq R
. Then we have either
|z|\geq R/\sqrt{2}\gg 1
or
r\geq R/\sqrt{2}\gg 1
. For the former case, we use the relation
|\psi(z,r)/r^{2}|\leq C_{1}|z|^{-3}
, while for the later case, we apply
|\psi(z,r)/r^{2}|\leq C_{0}r^{-3}
. In sum, we get

\left|\frac{\psi(z,r)}{r^{2}}\right|\leq C_{2}R^{-3}\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }R\gg 1

for the constant
C_{2}=2^{3/2}\max\{C_{0},C_{1}\}
, as desired.

Similarly, for a given odd symmetric
\overline{\omega}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and its stream function
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}[\overline{\omega}]
, we borrow the estimate
|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})|\leq C_{3}|x_{2}|^{1/2}
for some constant
C_{3}>0
from
[
1
, Proposition 2.1]
, and we obtain that
|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})/x_{2}|\leq C_{3}|x_{2}|^{-1/2}
if
x_{2}\neq 0
. On the other hand,
since
\mathcal{G}(x_{1},0)=0
for each
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, the mean value theorem once again gives that for each
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}
with
x_{2}\neq 0
,

\frac{\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})}{x_{2}}=\left(\partial_{x_{2}}\mathcal{G}\right)(x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime})\quad\mbox{for some}\mbox{ }x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{2}^{\prime}(x_{1},x_{2})\in(\min\{0,x_{2}\},\max\{0,x_{2}\}).

Applying the boundedness of the support of
\overline{\omega}
to the relation
|\nabla\mathcal{G}({\bf x})|\leq C_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|}|\overline{\omega}({\bf y})|\textrm{d}{\bf y}
for some constant
C_{4}>0
, we have
|\nabla\mathcal{G}({\bf x})|=O(|{\bf x}|^{-1})
as
|{\bf x}|\gg 1
. Hence, for any
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
|x_{1}|\gg 1
, we have
|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})/x_{2}|\leq|\nabla\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime})|\leq C_{5}|(x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime})|^{-1}\leq C_{5}|x_{1}|^{-1}
for some constant
C_{5}>0
. As with the case of the axisymmetric case
\overline{\xi}
, we consider a large enough
R\gg 1
, and suppose a point
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies
|(x_{1},x_{2})|\geq R
. Then we have either
|x_{1}|\geq R/\sqrt{2}\gg 1
or
|x_{2}|\geq R/\sqrt{2}\gg 1
, which leads to
|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})/x_{2}|\leq 2^{1/2}C_{5}R^{-1}
or
|\mathcal{G}(x_{1},x_{2})/x_{2}|\leq 2^{1/4}C_{3}R^{-1/2}
respectively, as desired. It completes the proof.

∎
