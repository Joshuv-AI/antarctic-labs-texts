Analytical and Clinical Performance of the Atellica HEMA 580 Haematology Analyzer: A Comparison With the ADVIA 2120i
Authors: Hyeongyu Lee, Jooyoung Cho, Gilsung Yoo, Juwon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe Atellica HEMA 580 is a novel haematology analyser providing eight‐part leukocyte differential and optical platelet (PLT) counting. We compared its analytical and clinical performance with that of the ADVIA 2120i.MethodsA total of 796 residual whole blood specimens were analysed. Carryover, linearity and precision were assessed. Inter‐instrument comparison was performed for complete blood counts and five‐part differential (5‐Diff) parameters using Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analyses. Additional regression against manual microscopy was conducted for flagged samples, including PLT, reticulocytes and common 5‐Diff parameters.ResultsCarryover was < 1% for all parameters. Linearity was excellent (R 2 ≥ 0.99). Precision was within acceptable limits; coefficient of variant was < 5% for most parameters, except optical PLT, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and nucleated red blood cells under specific conditions. Inter‐instrument comparisons revealed remarkably high correlations (r ≥ 0.9) for most parameters, whereas mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) (r = 0.429) and basophils (r = 0.627) showed weaker concordance. Passing–Bablok regression indicated minimal systematic or proportional bias for most parameters. Bland–Altman analysis showed that > 95% of results were within the 95% limits of agreement, except white blood cells, mean corpuscular volume and PLT (94.7%–94.8%). Comparison with microscopy confirmed good concordance for PLT and reticulocytes; however, lymphocytes and monocytes had proportional and systematic biases.ConclusionThe Atellica HEMA 580 demonstrated reliable analytical performance and strong agreement with the ADVIA 2120i. This supports its suitability as a routine haematology analyser, providing accurate and clinically comparable results.Keywords: ADVIA 2120i, Atellica HEMA 580, complete blood count, Haematology analyser, performance evaluation

Introduction

Automated haematology instruments use the principles of electrical impedance and/or light scattering to measure numerous characteristics of red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC) and platelets (PLT) [1, 2]. The results obtained from these instruments can be used to diagnose many haematological disorders, ranging from iron deficiency anaemia to leukaemia, and facilitate the prognosis of various non‐haematological conditions [3]. Furthermore, automated haematology instruments can help promptly identify patients who require peripheral blood smears and bone marrow tests and those who are likely to have adverse risks.

To meet the increasing healthcare demands of an aging global population and growing emerging markets, modern automated haematology instruments must deliver high throughput, precision and accuracy. In addition, the latest instruments provide novel parameters such as nucleated RBC (NRBC), immature granulocytes (IMG) and reticulocyte indices, which are not available on older instruments [4]. With these advances in technology and equipment, the criteria for evaluating new equipment are constantly being updated in numerous ways [5].

The Atellica HEMA 580 (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) is a recently introduced high‐volume hematology analyzer. While its predecessor, the ADVIA 2120i (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) primarily utilizes peroxidase staining and light scatter for leukocyte differentiation, the Atellica HEMA 580 incorporates fluorescence flow cytometry and impedance variation to provide an expanded eight‐part leukocyte differential count (8‐Diff). The Atellica HEMA 580 enables the quantification of immature cell populations, specifically immature monocytes (IMM), immature lymphocytes (IML) and immature granulocytes (IMG). This shift to fluorescence‐based detection allows for more precise discrimination of cell populations based on nucleic acid content and internal complexity. These extended parameters provide standardized, quantitative data on immature fractions, which may serve as early indicators of hematopoietic stress or inflammation, potentially enhancing clinical interpretation compared with previous‐generation analyzers. The aim of this study was to evaluate the analytical and clinical performance of the Atellica HEMA 580 in comparison with the ADVIA 2120i, which has been widely validated as a routine laboratory instrument [6, 7].

Materials and Methods

Samples

Blood specimens were collected from patients who visited the Wonju Severance Christian Hospital between December 2022 and January 2023. Venous blood samples were collected into vacuum tubes containing K2‐EDTA (BD Vacutainer, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and analysed within one hour of collection. Each blood specimen was collected for routine complete blood count (CBC), and the samples remaining after examination were used for this study. Samples were collected for inter‐instrument comparison and manual microscopy examination.

Initially, 1000 samples were randomly collected for inter‐instrument comparisons. Of the 1000 samples collected, those with a volume < 1 mL or with visible severe haemolysis or agglutination and those suspected of having been stored at room temperature for over 8 h after collection were excluded. Consequently, 796 consecutive routine clinical specimens were analyzed using both the Atellica HEMA 580 and ADVIA 2120i. It should be noted that the optical platelet count (PLTO) was reported only in 126 cases where the Atellica HEMA 580's automated reflex algorithm detected potential red blood cell interference (e.g., microcytosis or fragmentation). This final dataset ensured a comprehensive representation of both normal and abnormal ranges across all clinical parameters. Detailed information regarding the sample sizes and the specific analytical ranges (minimum to maximum values) for each parameter is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Carryover

High‐concentration samples can interfere with subsequent measurements of low‐concentration samples if left in the tubing, needles, or other parts of the instrument. The carryover test was performed according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) H26‐A2 guidelines [8]. High‐concentration (H) and low‐concentration (L) samples were analyzed in four replicates (H1, H2, H3, H4 followed by L1, L2, L3, L4). Carryover was calculated using the formula: Carryover (%) = [(L1–L3) / (H3–L3)] x 100. The fourth measurements (H4 and L4) were performed to confirm measurement stability and were not included in the carryover calculation.

Linearity

Linearity was evaluated using a five‐level dilution panel (S1–S5) prepared by proportionally mixing high‐concentration (S5) and low‐concentration (S1) whole blood specimens, in accordance with the CLSI H26‐A2 guidelines [8]. Intermediate levels were generated using the following mixing ratios: S2 (3:1, S1:S5), S3 (1:1, S1:S5) and S4 (1:3, S1:S5). Each level was measured in duplicate, and the mean values were subjected to linear regression analysis. The coefficient of determination (R [2]), slope and intercept—along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs)—were calculated to assess the analytical linearity.

Precision

Analytical precision was evaluated using both clinical specimens and commercial control materials in accordance with the CLSI EP15‐A3 guidelines [9]. To determine repeatability (within‐run precision), three levels of pooled patient samples (low, normal and high concentrations) were analyzed 10 times in a single run. To evaluate reproducibility (between‐run precision), three levels of commercial control materials (Level 1, 2 and 3) provided by the manufacturer were analyzed in duplicate for five consecutive days. The coefficient of variation (CV%) and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for each parameter. Acceptability criteria were defined based on the manufacturer's allowable CV% specifications for each parameter.

Limit of Blank, Limit of Detection and Limit of Quantification

The analytical sensitivity of the Atellica HEMA 580 was evaluated for WBC and PLT counts by determining the limit of blank (LOB), limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ). For LoB assessment, isotonic saline was measured 60 times, and calculated as: LoB = Meansaline + (1.645 × SDsaline).

For LoD evaluation, low‐concentration specimens were prepared by diluting patient samples with saline to concentrations between the LoB and 4 × LoB. Five such specimens were measured 12 times each (n = 60), and the LoD was calculated as: LoD = LoB + (1.653 × SDlow concentration sample), where 1.653 is the multiplier for 55 degrees of freedom as per CLSI EP17‐A2 [10].

For LoQ assessment, four patient samples with concentrations near the calculated LoD were measured 10 times each. The LoQ was defined as the value at which the coefficients of variation for leukocytes and platelets were 15% and 25%, respectively [8]. All obtained values were compared against the manufacturer's specifications.

Inter‐Instrument Comparison

The ADVIA 2120i and Atellica HEMA 580 results for the 796 whole blood specimens were compared in accordance with the CLSI EP09c guidelines [11]. The HGB, RBC, PLT, WBC and 5‐Diff parameters were compared. The key technical characteristics and measurement principles of both analyzers are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The Atellica HEMA 580 analyzer employs a dual‐methodology approach for platelet (PLT) quantification. By default, PLT counts and volume indices are determined via the impedance measurement principle. However, the system incorporates an automated reflex mechanism to ensure accuracy in the presence of potential interferences. When the analyzer detects red blood cell (RBC) abnormalities that may overlap with the platelet population—such as microcytic RBCs or schistocytes—it automatically performs a remeasurement using optical extinction. In these specific instances, the reported result is PLTO, which provides superior discrimination between small RBCs and platelets.

Manual microscopic reviews were triggered by predefined flags to ensure diagnostic accuracy. For platelet counts, samples with PLT < 100 × 10 [9]/L were reviewed via microscopic estimation at ×1000 magnification across 10 oil‐immersion fields. For reticulocytes, samples with reticulocytes > 1.5% were manually confirmed by counting 1000 red blood cells. Additionally, smears were reviewed when large immature cell populations exceeded 3% (LUC on the ADVIA 2120i and the sum of IMM, IML and IMG on the Atellica HEMA 580).

Peripheral blood smears were prepared and stained with Wright–Giemsa using an automated slide stainer (Aerospray Hematology Pro; ELITechGroup, Puteaux, France). In accordance with CLSI H26‐A2 [8] and H20‐A2 guidelines [12], manual differential counts were performed independently by two experienced laboratory physicians, each conducting a 200‐cell count. Any discrepancies were resolved through joint review and consensus; if unresolved, a third senior laboratory physician adjudicated the final result.

Statistical Analysis

Method comparison was performed using Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis. Systematic and proportional biases were considered present if the 95% CIs for the intercept and slope did not include 0 and 1, respectively [13]. Agreement was evaluated by calculating the mean difference and 95% limits of agreement (LOA; mean ± 1.96 SD) [14]. For the assessment of clinical significance, the observed mean bias for each analyte was compared against the analytical performance specifications provided by the Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia Quality Assurance Programs (RCPAQAP) [15]. Since there is no single global consensus on analyte‐specific criteria for inter‐instrument comparisons, these widely recognized external limits were utilized as the primary reference. Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used to assess the precision and carryover of the Atellica HEMA 580. RStudio (version 2024.4.1.748, Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) was used to analyse the linearity of the Atellica HEMA 580 and the correlation and agreement between the two HAs.

Results

Carryover

The samples analysed with the Atellica HEMA 580 showed no significant carryover. For the four parameters (WBC, RBC, HGB and PLT) analysed, it was within the range declared acceptable by the manufacturer (< 1%). The individual measurand values for high‐ and low‐level samples, along with the calculated carryover percentages, are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

While PLTO demonstrated stable precision, the extended parameters (IMM, IML and IMG) were excluded from the formal precision summary. Due to the predominantly normal range of these parameters in our study population, the near‐zero mean values resulted in statistically unreliable and disproportionately high CV%, which do not accurately reflect the analytical performance of the analyzer.

Determination of LoB, LoD and LoQ

The determined LoB, LoD and LoQ values for WBC and PLT counts are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. For WBC, the LoB was identical to the manufacturer's specification, while the LoD and LoQ values were slightly higher but remained within clinically acceptable limits. For PLT, all obtained sensitivity values (LoB, LoD and LoQ) were lower than the manufacturer's specifications, demonstrating superior analytical sensitivity in our evaluation.

Discussion

HAs are one of the most widely utilised instruments in laboratories, routinely used for screening and assessing the condition of patients in clinical settings. Since the HAs used in clinical laboratories require both high throughput and diagnostic accuracy, it is important to evaluate their capabilities before their introduction. In this study, we evaluated the performance of the Atellica HEMA 580 HA in the measurement of CBC parameters and abnormal results compared with that of the ADVIA 2120i HA.

The Atellica HEMA 580 demonstrated mostly reliable analytical performance, including carryover, linearity and precision. It exhibited a carryover percentage of less than 1%, in accordance with the findings of Schapkaitz et al. [17]. In addition, verification of the LoB, LoD and LoQ demonstrated values comparable to those provided by the manufacturer, indicating that the analyzer maintains adequate analytical sensitivity for detecting low concentrations of WBC and PLT. Specifically, the verified LoQ values in this study—0.12 × 10 [9]/L for WBC and 5.0 × 10 [9]/L for PLT—are comparable to or even superior to those reported in a recent multi‐platform evaluation by Nikler et al. [18], which assessed four widely used hematology analyzers. These results confirm that the Atellica HEMA 580 offers robust analytical performance for the reliable quantification of samples with severe cytopenia, matching the high‐sensitivity standards of established high‐end platforms. The assessment of its within‐run precision showed very good results for all determined parameters. While most parameters yielded consistent results across the low‐, normal‐ and high‐level control groups, PLTO, lymphocyte, monocyte and eosinophil counts exceeded the 5% CV under specific conditions. A high CV value for PLTO has also been reported in other HAs using flow cytometry, which is unlikely to be sufficient to accurately discriminate between PLT and RBC [19]. Additionally, the high CV values observed for lymphocytes, monocytes and eosinophils can be attributed to the fact that the CV values increased as the absolute value of the average decreased [20]. The exclusion of certain novel parameters (IMM, IML and IMG) from the precision analysis was a deliberate decision based on statistical validity. In the absence of enriched abnormal specimens with elevated IMM, IML or IMG counts, a precision study would yield clinically misleading CV% values. Future studies utilizing a dedicated cohort of patients with hematopoietic activation are warranted to fully characterize the precision of these parameters.

The Atellica HEMA 580 demonstrated comparable performance to the ADVIA 2120i regarding overall assessment, with the exception of certain parameters, including MCHC, RDW, MPV and basophils. These findings are consistent with those of a previous investigation of the Atellica HEMA 580, which showed good correlation with the Sysmex XN‐1000 (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) for most parameters except MCHC, basophils and large immature cells [17]. Indeed, a weak correlation for MCHC has been repeatedly observed in comparative studies of various HAs. Woo et al. [21] reported a MCHC correlation coefficient of 0.683 in a comparative study between the Sysmex XN‐1000 and Alinity hq (Abbott Laboratories, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Similarly, a weak correlation for basophils has been reported in other studies comparing HAs [22, 23], likely because of the low proportion of basophils among the 5‐Diff parameters. Åstrand et al. [24] demonstrated that basophil counts measured using the ADVIA 2120i were particularly sensitive to changes in time and storage temperature.

The Passing–Bablok regression showed an overall good agreement between the Atellica HEMA 580 and ADVIA 2120i. However, slopes deviating from 1 were observed for the MCHC, MPV and PCT, and the intercept for MCHC did not include 0, indicating a bias in these parameters. PLT indices are known to vary substantially among HAs. In particular, impedance‐based systems may miscount PLT, thereby influencing MPV and other derived PLT indices [25]. A previous comparative study between the HEMA 580 and Sysmex XN‐1000 reported both proportional and systematic biases in MCHC measurements, in agreement with our observations [17]. Moreover, while the majority of parameters satisfied the clinical acceptability criteria, the mean biases for MPV and basophil (%) did not meet the RCPAQAP limits in our study. However, as highlighted by Nikler et al. [18], these discrepancies should not be interpreted as a lack of analytical accuracy in the Atellica HEMA 580. Instead, such differences likely reflect the inherent technological limitations and methodological variations of the reference analyzer (ADVIA 2120i) rather than a deficiency in the evaluated system. These factors are known to cause statistically significant inter‐analyzer variability, especially for parameters sensitive to differing measurement principles, and do not necessarily preclude the clinical utility of the new analyzer.

Additional Passing–Bablok regression against manual microscopy showed very high concordance for PLT and reticulocytes. In contrast, lymphocytes exhibited both proportional and systematic biases, whereas monocytes showed a systematic bias. This bias in lymphocytes and monocytes can be attributed to the morphological similarity between atypical lymphocytes, normal lymphocytes and monocytes, complicating their manual differentiation under the microscope [26, 27]. Additionally, overestimated monocyte counts in HAs have been occasionally reported, possibly owing to a skewed distribution during slide preparation [28, 29].

In the Bland–Altman analysis, most CBC parameters and 5‐Diff parameters exhibited excellent agreement, with over 95% of the samples falling within the 95% LOA. The WBC, MCV and PLT demonstrated slightly lower levels of agreement, with 94.7%, 94.8% and 94.8% of samples, respectively. This means they did not meet the 95% acceptance threshold, but they did fall within the relatively narrow LOA of ±2%. When the LOA range was sufficiently narrow that it had no significant clinical impact, the two methods could be considered interchangeable, even if the data value within the LOA was less than 95% [30].

In addition to the conventional CBC parameters, the Atellica HEMA 580 provides several extended parameters based on flow cytometry and spectrophotometry, including IMM, IML and IMG. These parameters may provide additional information regarding early hematopoietic activation or inflammatory responses, potentially improving clinical interpretation compared with traditional differential counts. Although the present study primarily focused on conventional CBC parameters, these extended parameters may have potential clinical utility and need further investigation in future studies.

This study has some limitations. First, given the nature of a single‐centre–based study, it is possible that different results may be obtained in other groups or laboratory settings. Second, the limited number of flagged or abnormal samples may not accurately reflect all pathological conditions. In addition, certain platform‐specific parameters, such as LIC, were not fully evaluated due to the low frequency of these findings in the study population, which limited meaningful statistical analysis. Further multicentre studies including a wider range of abnormal samples and additional comparative instruments are warranted to validate and expand these findings.

The Atellica HEMA 580 demonstrated comparable analytical and clinical performance and strong correlation with the ADVIA 2120i. We conclude that the Atellica HEMA 580 is a competent HA that can provide reliable and accurate diagnostic results in clinical laboratories.
