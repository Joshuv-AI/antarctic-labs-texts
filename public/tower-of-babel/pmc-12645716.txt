The mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone enhances cardiovascular recovery upon food intake normalization in obese mice
Authors: M Monmirel-Fauconnier, M Pieronne-Deperrois, B Bonnard, C Frimat, K Linghui, A Ni Dheasmhunaigh, L Nicol, S Morchid, S Renet, A Dumesnil, V Richard, B Fève, F Jaisser, S Boulkroun, P Mulder, A Ouvrard-Pascaud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with obesity exhibit high prevalence of heart failure with preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFpEF or HFmrEF). Recently, the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone demonstrated benefits in this population. Glucagon-like peptide-1 analog aided weight loss also showed benefits. We hypothesized that finerenone would improve cardiovascular parameters when combined with weight loss in previously obese mice.MethodsMice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 24 weeks, and were compared to mice fed a HFD for 16 weeks before returning to a standard diet for a further 8 weeks (rSD), with or without finerenone treatment 1 mg.kg−1.day−1 (rSD + F). Glycaemia and insulin tolerance were assessed, cardiac left ventricle (LV) function was evaluated by ultrasound and invasive hemodynamics, myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) was measured by MRI, and exercise capacity was evaluated on a treadmill.ResultsAfter 16 weeks of HFD, compared to controls, obese mice had hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance, no difference in LV fractional shortening but increased LV filling pressure, impaired LV compliance, and reduced exercise capacity. After 24 weeks, this obesity phenotype was worsened by a reduction in MPR, LV fractional shortening, and cardiac output. After returning to standard diet, compared to the HFD group, body weight decreased in both the rSD and rSD + F groups, with improvements in glycaemia, insulin resistance, LV stroke volume, and LV compliance. In the rSD + F group, compared to rSD group, finerenone further improved LV compliance (34% improvement) (LVEDPVR: Ctl 1.19 ± 0.25, HFD 4.75 ± 0.31***, rSD 3.44 ± 0.39†, rSD + F 2.27 ± 0.23†††,§; ***P < 0.001 vs C, †, †††P < 0.05 and < 0.001 vs H, §P < 0.05 vs HS), as well as LV interstitial collagen fibrosis (-47%), and was necessary to improve LV fractional shortening, cardiac output, LV filling pressure (-30%) (LVEDP: Ctl 2.73 ± 0.17, HFD 4.73 ± 0.34***, rSD 4.53 ± 0.33, rSD + F 3.18 ± 0.26††,§; * vs C,† vs H,§ vs rSD), MPR (+ 28%), and exercise capacity (+ 43%). Benefits of finenerone were likely due, in part, to reduced LV mRNA and protein expression (-49%) of the protein tyrosine-phosphatase PTP-1B, a downstream negative regulator of the insulin receptor, associated with increased phosphorylation level (+ 82%) of AKT kinase.ConclusionIn the mouse model of HFD-induced obesity and heart failure, finerenone improves weight loss-induced recovery of cardiac function.Keywords: Mineralocorticoid receptor, Aldosterone, Finerenone, Heart failure, HFpEF, HFmrEF, Obesity, Weight loss

Introduction

In the epidemiology of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), more than 80% of patients are obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) or overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) [1]. Abdominal obesity is the main trigger of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its associated comorbidities: type-2 diabetes (T2D), adipose and endothelial dysfunctions, chronic kidney disease, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, all of which contribute to increased cardiovascular risk. Nevertheless, several observational studies pointed out an ‘obesity paradox’ suggesting that obese individuals have a better prognosis among patients with chronic heart failure (CHF) [2–5], but this must be qualified. Indeed, a meta-analysis of over 22,800 CHF patients with a mean follow-up of 2.85 years concluded that, while the highest risk of cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization concerned individuals with a low BMI < 20 kg/m2, the lowest risk concerned non-obese overweight subjects (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2), without significant effect of an increase in the degree of obesity [4]. A study from a database of over 88,000 patients hospitalized for myocardial infarction did not fully support the obesity paradox in the long-term prognosis. Indeed, patients without obesity or with low-risk grade I obesity (BMI 30–34.9 kg/m2) had a notably lower 5-year overall mortality compared to patients with moderate-risk grade II obesity (BMI 35–39.9 kg/m2) and high-risk grade III obesity with (BMI > 40 kg/m2) [5]. In HFpEF, abdominal rather than whole-body obesity is the primary issue. This was notably highlighted by the TOPCAT trial (Treatment Of Preserved Cardiac function heart failure with and Aldosterone antagonisT), in which higher all-risk mortality was observed in 2413 patients with abdominal obesity, compared to 897 patients without [6]. Therefore, to limit both the risk and the progression of HF, body weight reduction appears to be an important factor that requires further research.

Studies suggest benefits from bariatric surgery [7, 8]. At 6 months post-surgery in severely obese patients (BMI > 35 kg/m2) diagnosed with HFpEF, bariatric surgery-induced body weight loss was associated with improved New York Heart Association (NHYA) class, from class II-III to class I, and additionally, ultrasound data indicated decreased LV mass and improved mitral annular early diastolic velocity (e’) as a relaxation index (but with no difference in the LV filling pressure E/e’ index, ratio of the of early mitral inflow velocity over e’) [9]. Five years after surgery in severely obese patients, a 22% reduction from a mean pre-operative BMI of 43 kg/m2 was associated with improved left ventricle (LV) remodeling and longitudinal mechanics [10]. On a large scale, a cohort follow-up study of over 47,800 obese patients found a five-fold lower incidence of HF among the 46.6% who underwent bariatric surgery compared to non-operated patients [11]. However, even if the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity now suggests access to bariatric surgery for patients with grade I obesity in case of associated comorbidity, notably arterial hypertension, T2D or chronic kidney disease, such intervention cannot be a systematic recourse, especially in elderly patients over 65 years old. Moreover, even if considered safe, bariatric surgery can present increased post-operative risks of pulmonary edema, hypotension and renal failure [7]. Thus, the effects of weight loss through diet control need to be more widely evaluated regarding their impact in HFpEF patients.

Some studies suggest that even moderate weight loss can be beneficial. Studies in elderly patients (mean age 67 years old) with HFpEF and a mean BMI > 39.4 kg/m2 showed that a 6.9% decrease in body weight resulted in a 1.3-fold increase in peak oxygen consumption during exercise [1], or a 1.2-fold improvement in the 6-min walk distance test of exercise capacity [12], along with an improvement in the LV filling pressure ultrasound E/e’ index [12]. A reduction in LV mass was further reported when a caloric restriction-induced 8% decrease in body weight was combined with 20 weeks of exercise [13]. In addition to diet control, it is now proposed that the use of drugs with metabolic action to facilitate and to amplify weight loss, might improve cardiovascular function in patients with HF.

Recently, the trials on Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity and HFpEF (STEP-HF) [14], with or without diabetes mellitus (STEP-HF-DM) [15] evaluated the benefits of facilitating weight loss during a one-year treatment with a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogue, that stimulates insulin release while inhibiting release of glucagon. Starting at an average BMI of 37 kg/m2 in STEP-HF and STEP-HF-DM studies, the BMI reductions in semaglutide-treated patients compared with placebo were 13.3% vs 2.6% and 9.8% vs 3.4%, respectively. In both trials, semaglutide treatment was associated with improvements in the 6-min walk distance test and in the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire-clinical summary score (KCCQ-CSS), as well as a reduction in hospitalization [7, 15, 16]. In both trials, almost all patients were treated with diuretics, > 79% with β-blocker, > 80% with angiotensin-converting enzyme-inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (in combination or not with the neutral endopeptidase neprilysin). Furthermore, in the STEP-HF-DM, 32.7% of patients received a sodium-glucose transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor (3.6% in STEP-HF) [16] that has been proven to be specifically effective in reducing the risk of cardiovascular mortality in HFpEF [17], but without highlighting here any benefit attributable to the combination with semaglutide [15]. Even more recently, the Summit trial concluded that a one-year treatment with tirzepatide, an incretin agonist of the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1, in obese patients with HFpEF and an average BMI of 38 kg/m2, led to a lower risk of death from cardiovascular causes or worsening HF [18]. In the Summit, STEP-HF and STEP-HF-DM studies, respectively 36%, 34.8% and 32.5% of patients received a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) but the possibility that this may give them an additional advantage in weight loss conditions has not been discussed. For this purpose, our study aims to address the possibility of specific cardiovascular benefits of the new MRA, finerenone, alongside weight loss induced by a return to the standard diet in the mouse model of HFD-induced obesity.

Obesity can lead to the expansion of plasma volume and to increased sodium reabsorption, notably because of increased aldosterone levels following activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [19, 20]. Moreover, obesity induces high leptin levels, as a result of the loss of its anorexic action, an adipokine that stimulates aldosterone synthesis and release by adrenal glands [21]. Previously, post-hoc analysis from the TOPCAT trial that tested the efficacy of spironolactone in HFpEF patients from the Americas, Georgia and Russia, showed reduced rates of cardiovascular death and hospitalization in American patients, who had a higher average BMI and a greater incidence of diabetes mellitus [22]. Recently the Figaro-DKD trial showed that in patients with T2D and chronic kidney disease, finerenone improves cardiovascular outcomes [23], demonstrating an interest in targeting MR in the context of comorbidities increasing the risk of HF, as is the case in the context of obesity and MetS. Thus, studying the action of finerenone combined with weight loss in rodent models of HF secondary to obesity is interesting for the implementation of effective treatments in humans.

In the Zucker fatty fa/fa rat model of MetS, we previously showed that a 3-month finerenone treatment improved diastolic function i.e., LV filling pressure and compliance, and decreased LV weight and collagen content [24]. We also showed that four months after ovariectomy-induced post-menopausal conditions, mice became overweight and developed several criteria of MetS, leading to coronary endothelial dysfunction and HFpEF, the progression of which was drastically limited by a 1-month finerenone treatment, along with improvements in exercise capacity and spontaneous activity [25]. Interestingly, these effects were associated with a 9.6% reduction in body weight and normalization of fasting hyperglycaemia [25].

Our hypothesis is that in obesity and HFpEF, MR blockade and simultaneous weight loss might provide additional benefits in reducing cardiovascular risk and helping to improve quality of life, notably by facilitating physical exercise. Our study aims to test the effect of finerenone alongside weight loss induced by a return to the standard diet in the preclinical mouse model of high-fat diet-induced obesity.

Material and methods

Animals, body weight tracking and finerenone treatment

Experiments conformed to the 2010–63 European Union directive, the national Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 0335402), and the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the US National Institute of Health (No. 85–23). Wild-type mice from the B6D2/F1 (Janvier, France) genetic background were housed 5/cage in a ventilated rack, with ad libitum access to food and water, in a 12 h day/night cycle and a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C. Nine weeks old male mice were fed a high-fat sucrose diet HFD (60% energy from fat) (260HF U8978-19, Safe, France) or maintained on a standard diet (A03, Safe). After 16 weeks, obese mice were divided into 3 groups for a further 8 weeks with either: i) HFD; ii) return to thestandard diet (rSD); iii) return to the standard diet plus Finerenone 1 mg.kg−1.day−1 (rSD + F). Finerenone was mixed in the food, considering a daily consumption of 5 mg.day−1. The weight of each mouse, and the average food consumption per cage were measured weekly.

Body composition

Body composition of fat and lean mass was assessed in vigil mice by echo-MRI (EMR-185, Echo-MRI, USA). Mice who did not spontaneously enter the device and/or experienced incontinance from stress were excluded from the measurements. Calibration was done with canola oil. Parameters were normalized to mice weight (%).

Glycaemia, insulin tolerance test and plasma dosages

Glycaemia was measured after 6 h of fasting, and at several time points after human insulin 0.75 U.kg−1 (Actrapid, Novo-Nordisk, France) intraperitoneal (IP) injection i.e., at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min. A blood drop collected from a tail incision was put on reactive strips (Glu-Test Strips, Nova Biomedicals, UK) and read by glucometer (Stat Strip Xpress-I, Nova Biomedicals, UK). Triglycerides and total cholesterol dosages were measured from plasma collected at the sacrifice, using Catalyst One bio-analyzer (IDEXX, USA). Plasma leptin was dosed using an ELISA test (KMC2281Invitrogen™, USA) using 10 μλ of plasma per mouse as per manufacturer instructions.

Echocardiography

Measurements were taken using Vivid7 ultrasound (GE-Healthcare, France) with a linear 14 mHz probe from mice anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (1.5%). A depilatory agent was applied to mice who were put on a heated examination table equipped with electrocardiogram electrodes. Acquisitions in time-movement mode were done in parasternal short axis view at the LV papillary level. LV wall thicknesses, systolic and diastolic diameters were measured, and FS (%) was calculated as: (diastolic diameter – systolic diameter) / (diastolic diameter × 100). Velocity Time Integral (VTI, cm) was measured in pulse-Doppler mode at the pulmonary artery, enabling calculation of both stroke volume (SV, mL.beat−1, [VTIaortic x Pi x (D2aortic/4]) and cardiac output (CO, mL.min−1, SV x HR).

Invasive hemodynamic and pressure–volume loops

LV hemodynamic and pressure–volume curves were assessed in anesthetized mice (Inactine, 10 mg.kg−1, IP). The carotid artery was cannulated with a pressure–volume catheter (Millar Instruments, USA) to record arterial pressure and heart rate, after which the catheter was advanced into the LV. Pressure–volume loops were obtained at baseline and during loading, by gently occluding the abdominal aorta with a cotton swab. LV end-systolic and end-diastolic pressures were measured, and LV end-systolic and end-diastolic pressure–volume relations were calculated with LabChart software (AD Instruments, New Zealand).

MRI measurement of LV perfusion myocardial perfusion reserve

Myocardial perfusion was evaluated using MRI (Bruker Biospec 4.7 Tesla, France) by arterial spin labeling, as described previously [26]. Coronary reserve was calculated as: ratio of perfusion after coronary dilatation with ATL313 (2 μγ/kg, IP) over basal perfusion.

Exercise capacity on treadmill

Mice were acclimated over two consecutive days to exercise on a treadmill at speed of 15 m.min−1 during 10 min with a 5% slope (3/6 treadmill, Columbus Instruments, Ohio, USA). The next day, mice were subjected to an endurance-exhaustion test with an exclusion criterion set at number of shocks ≥ 6 shocks.min−1 or > 20 total shocks (shock frequency 3 Hz, intensity 6 mA). Speed was gradually increased by 0.6 m.min−1 over 20 min from 0.5 to 12 m.min−1, then by 1.2 m.min−1 over three successive endurance steps of 10 min at 12.6, 13.8, and 15 m.min−1 respectively, before being increased by 0.6 m.min−1 over 30 min from 15.6 to 33 m.min−1. For the stress test, an independent series of mice was used, with a starting speed of 13 m.min−1 for 2 min, before progressively increasing over 1 min intervals, reaching 33 m.min in the 13th minute.

Western-blot (PTP-1B, Akt, phospho-Akt)

LVs were homogenized mechanically (Precellys Evolution) in RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% Cholic acid, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris–HCl ph7.4) with protease inhibitors (Complete ULTRA Tablets Mini EDTA Free Easy Pack, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (Phos-STOP Easy Pack, Roche). Homogenates were centrifugated at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected to determine protein concentration using Pierce BCA protein assay (ThermoFischer Scientific #23,225). Proteins (40 μγ /sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE in resolving gels (Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free Precast Gels 4–20%, Biorad). Revelation of total proteins by the stain-free method was activated under UV light using a Chemidoc imaging system (Biorad). The proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System, Biorad) before blocking for 1 h at room T°C with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin in Tris-buffered saline plus 0,1% Tween-20. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with different primary antibodies (1/1000 anti-PTP1B Cell-Signaling #1718, 1/5000 anti-Akt Cell-Signaling #9272S, 1/1000 anti-phosho-Akt Cell-Signaling #4060S). Membranes were rinsed three times with TBS-T (3 × 10 min) and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1/10000 AffiniPure Goat anti-Rabbit IgG, Jackson-Immunoresearch Lab #111–035-00) at room T°C for 1 h. Proteins were revealed using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Biorad) and imaged using a Chemidoc imaging system (Biorad). Bands were quantified using ImageLab software and normalized to total protein load for each sample.

Histology and immuno-staining (MCSA, fibrosis, macrophages, capillary density)

Hearts were removed before atria and ventricles were separated and weighed. For each mouse, an LV section was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cardiomyocytes count and mean cross-sectional area (MCSA) were obtained from 6 μm cryo-sections of the mid-LV. Sections were fixed in an acetone bath for 10 min and rehydrated with PBS for 10 min before blockage for 10 min with BSA 3%. To reveal endothelial cells, sections were incubated for 1 h with a primary antibody rat anti-mouse CD31 (1:100) (553,371, BD, USA) in 1% BSA-PBS buffer with 0.3% saponin, washed 3 × 5 min with PBS and incubated for 30 min in the dark with the secondary antibody (1:400) (donkey anti-rat Cy3, 712–166-153, Jackson Immunoresearch, USA). Sections were washed again 3 × 5 min with PBS and incubated with FITC-WGA (1:100) for 30 min (Wheat Germ Agglutinin-A488, Invitrogen, USA). Sections were washed as previously, rinsed in H2O and mounted in vectashield. Six images/section were captured (magnification × 40) using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss AxioImager Z1, Germany) and capillary density was expressed as the mean number of capillaries/cardiomyocytes. To detect total CD68+ macrophages and CD206+ M2-like macrophages subpopulation, sections were incubated with the primary antibody rabbit anti-mouse CD206 (1:2500) (Ab64693, Abcam, UK) and secondary antibody donkey anti-rabbit-Cy3 (1:400) (711–605-152, Jackson Immunoresearch), as previously. Endogenous biotin and avidin were blocked for 10 min (Dako Biotin Blocking Kit, USA). Sections were then incubated with a biotinylated primary antibody—rat anti-mouse CD68 (1:200) (130–102-025, Miltenyi, Germany) as previously, before being incubated for 30 min under darkness with secondary reagents SA-Cy5 A647 (1:500) (FP-CA5640, FP-YE5081, Interchim, France). Sections were washed 3 × 5 min in PBS and mounted in Vectashield. Six images/sections were captured (magnification × 20) using a fluorescence AxioImager Z1 microscope, and the mean number of macrophages, M2-like cells, and their relative percentage were determined. To quantify interstitial collagen content, cryo-sections were stained with pico-Sirius Red according to standard protocols, and ten photographs/sections were analysed under a polarized light microscope (Zeiss AxioImager Z1) (magnification × 10). Collagen content was calculated as the percentage of the area of collagen relative to the total image area. Image analysis was performed using Image Pro-Plus software (version 6.3).

Statistics

Data is expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), analyzed with GraphPad-Prism-7.04. Unpaired Student t-test (after D’Agostino-Pearson test for normal distribution) was used to compare obese mice maintained on HFD and control mice. Comparison between obese mice maintained on HFD, and mice returned to the standard diet with or without finerenone treatment, was conducted using One-way ANOVA or Two-way ANOVA (for the insulin tolerance test), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance at P < 0.05.

Results

Sixteen weeks of high-fat-sucrose diet-induced heart failure with preserved ejection fraction

Male mice aged 8 weeks were given a high-fat sucrose (H) diet (60% of calories from fat) ad libitum for 16 weeks, resulting in constant weight gain (Fig. 1A). Echo-MRI measurements evidenced an increase in the body fat percentage, with a reduction in the percentage of lean body mass, confirmed at the time of sacrifice by the increased weight of the bilateral subcutaneous fat pads (Table 1). Obese mice showed increased fasting glycaemia (Fig. 1B and Table 1) associated with decreased insulin sensitivity (Fig. 1C), as well as increased plasma total cholesterol (Table 1). The function of the left ventricle (LV) of the heart was studied by echocardiography. After 16 weeks of HFD, the LV fractional shortening (direct index of the ejection fraction) was not reduced (Table 1) and no heart hypertrophy was evidenced (Table 1). However, after 9 weeks of HFD, a reduction in the LV stroke volume was measured (ml/beat: Ctrl 0.083 ± 0.002; HFD 0.077 ± 0.003*; n = 15/group, *p < 0.05) without change in cardiac output (ml/min: Ctrl 39.9 ± 1.0; HFD 37.4 ± 1.8; n = 15/group, ns), which was still the case after 16 weeks (Table 1). Despite preserved LV fractional shortening, the reduction in stroke volume suggested a gradual impairment of cardiac function. Indeed, LV catheterization after 16 weeks of HFD revealed diastolic dysfunction in obese mice, who presented with no change in LV ejection pressure i.e., LV end-systolic pressure (LVESP) (Fig. 1D), but had increased LV filling pressure i.e., LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) (Fig. 1E). In addition, the load-independent assessment of pressure–volume relations (PVR) indicated that LV systolic contractility (LVESPVR) remained unchanged in mice fed a HFD compared to Ctrl mice (Fig. 1F), whereas the LV diastolic compliance was impaired in obese mice as demonstrated by increased LVEDPVR (Fig. 1G).

MRI measurements of LV basal perfusion (Fig. 1H) and LV maximal perfusion (after intraperitoneal injection of an A2a adenosine receptor agonist) did not show any difference between mice fed a HFD and Ctrl mice, nor did the calculation of the myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR), as the ratio of maximal to basal perfusions (Fig. 1I). However, linked to a reduction in cardiac index (calculated as the ratio of cardiac output / body weight) (Table 1), exercise capacity on a treadmill was reduced in mice fed an HFD compared to Ctrl mice, whether considering the entire duration of the test including a 30-min endurance plateau before a final 30-min acceleration phase (0.6 m.min−1from 15.6 m.min−1 to 33 m.min−1) (Fig. 1J), or only this final phase (reached by 8/9 ~ 88% of controls and 4/9 ~ 44% of obese mice) (Fig. 1K).

Thus, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) was associated with 16 weeks of HFD in male mice.

Improvement in heart function after returning to the standard diet, and additional benefits with concomitant finerenone treatment

The possible improvement in LV function after an 8-week return to the standard diet (rSD group) and the possibility of additional benefits from concomitant finerenone treatment (1 mg/kg/day) (rSD + F group) was then investigated. After 3 weeks of interruption of the HFD diet in both the rSD and the rSD + F groups, mice showed significant weight loss compared to mice maintained on the HFD (Fig. 2A). Afterwards, and until the end of the 24-week protocol, the evolution of their weight-tracking curve was similar to that of Ctrl mice (Fig. 2A). The return to the standard diet induced a reduction in the percentage of body fat, as well as an increase in the percentage of lean body mass (Table 2), which was confirmed by weighing bilateral subcutaneous fat pads after sacrifice (Table 2). The return to the standard diet also induced improvements in fasting glycaemia (Fig. 2B and Table 2), and in insulin sensitivity (Fig. 2C), without additional benefits from finerenone treatment. After 24 weeks of HFD, mice had increased total plasma cholesterol, triglycerides, and leptin, while lower values were measured in mice that benefited from an 8-week return to the standard diet (Table 2).

Cardiac functional evaluation was initiated by echocardiographic analyses, 5 weeks after the return to the standard diet with or without finerenone treatment. After 21 weeks (16 + 5) of HFD, continuously obese mice exhibited a decrease in LV fractional shortening (-8.9%) and cardiac output (-17%) (Table 2). Returning to the standard diet alone was not enough to significantly improve these parameters but required the addition of finerenone treatment (Table 2). At the end of the protocol, after 24 weeks of HFD diet or after the 8 weeks of return to the standard diet with or without finerenone treatment, LV catheterization showed no difference between the groups in LV systolic ejection pressure and LV contractility i.e., LVSEP (Fig. 2D) and LVESPVR (Fig. 2F) respectively. With regards to the diastolic phase, standard diet combined with finerenone treatment was necessary to decrease (-29.8%) the LV filling pressure i.e., LVEDP (Fig. 2E), which was previously measured as increased in animals after 16 weeks of HFD (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, finerenone clearly induced greater improvement (34%) in LV diastolic compliance i.e., LVEDPVR (Fig. 2G) than interrupting the HFD alone.

MRI measurement of LV basal perfusion showed no difference between groups (Fig. 2H). However, compared to Ctrl mice, the ratio of maximal to basal perfusion showed that a prolonged exposure to the HFD induced a reduction in MPR in obese mice (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, MPR was only improved (+ 27.5%) in mice that combined finerenone treatment and return to the standard diet (Fig. 2I). Concerning the exercise capacity on a treadmill, it was of course reduced in obese mice. Interestingly, in mice that were returned to the standard diet without finerenone treatment, despite a cardiac index returned to a control value, but consistent with the MPR data, exercise capacity was not improved (Fig. 2J, K, and Table 2). However, exercise capacity was improved in mice that were returned to the standard diet with concomitant finerenone treatment, whether considering the entire duration of the test (+ 42.9%) (Fig. 2J) or only the final 30-min acceleration phase (+ 69.1%) (N.B.: this final phase of the test was reached by 12/15 ~ 80% of control mice, 10/15 ~ 66% of obese mice, and 14/15 ~ 93% of both groups returned to the standard diet) (Fig. 1K) following the 30-min endurance plateau. Finally, an additional treadmill test limited to a stress test (12 min of acceleration, increasing from from 13 to 33 m.min−1 over one-minute intervals) confirmed the benefit of finerenone in terms of exercise capacity (distance, m: Ctl = 218 ± 32, HFD = 83 ± 38*, rSD = 152 ± 42, rSD + F = 230 ± 27†, n = 8/group, *p < 0.05 vs C, †p < 0.05 vs H).

Myocardial PTP1B expression and Akt phosphorylation are modulated in mice treated with finerenone

We measured the LV myocardial expression of the protein tyrosine phosphatase-1B (PTP1B), which is a MR target [27] and whose activity was proven to participate in negative regulation of the insulin receptor [28]. PTP1B protein expression was higher in mice maintained on HFD (+ 31%) compared to Ctrl mice and decreased in mice when returned to the standard diet combined with finerenone treatment (-48.8%) but not with standard diet alone (Fig. 3A). Moreover, finerenone treatment decreased PTP1B mRNA expression (RT-qPCR of PTP1B / HPRT1, % of Ctl: Ctl = 100 ± 4, HFD = 105 ± 2, rSD = 106 ± 3, rSD + F = 90 ± 3††,§§, n = 10/group, ††p < 0.01 vs H, §§p < 0.01 vs rSD). Because decreased PTP1B activity is involved in the positive regulation of Akt (protein kinase B) [28], downstream of insulin receptor activation, the phosphorylation level of Akt was evaluated, and was shown to be increased only in mice with finerenone treatment (+ 81.7%) but not with standard diet alone (Fig. 3B to 3D).

Adverse interstitial collagen remodeling was greater improved upon return to the standard diet when treated concomitantly with finerenone

After 24 weeks of HFD, in obese mice compared to Ctrl mice, the ratio of heart weight over tibia length (+ 6.5%) as well as the gross weights of the heart (+ 8.0%) and LV (+ 7.9%) were increased (Table 3). This was corroborated by an increase in the mean cross-sectional area of LV cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the number of macrophages was higher in the myocardium of obese mice compared to Ctrl mice (Fig. 4B and C), but no difference was observed in the proportion of M2-like cells among macrophages in any group of the study (Fig. 4D). An 8-week return to the standard diet was not sufficient to reduce the values of these morphological and histological parameters (Fig. 4A to C and Table 3). However, while capillary density was increased in mice maintained on HFD (+ 13%), comparatively lower values were observed in mice that were returned to the standard diet, with or without finerenone treatment (-12% and -14%, respectively) (Fig. 4E). Concerning myocardial fibrosis, a specific effect of finerenone was demonstrated in regards to LV collagen density. Indeed, fibrosis was evidenced after 24 weeks of HFD diet (Fig. 4F) and was more efficiently decreased in mice who received finerenone treatment (-47.4%) compared to untreated mice alongside the 8-week return to the standard diet, watching both the thin and thick fibers, respectively revealed in green (-45%) or red (-91.5%) in polarized light microscopy (Fig. 4G and H).

Discussion

Our study shows, that in the mouse model of HFD-induced MetS and HFpEF, an 8-week return to the standard diet allows incomplete cardiovascular recovery that is clearly ameliorated by concomitant finerenone treatment, with regards to cardiac functional parameters in LV diastole and systole, myocardial perfusion reserve, exercise capacity on a treadmill, and LV fibrosis.

The rationale for the protocol was based on a long period of 16 weeks of a HFD-induced obesity because we wanted HFpEF to be clearly established in adult mice. Of note, ultrasound indicated little reduction in stroke volume (compensated with regard to the fractional shortening) as a clue of LV functional impairment from 9 weeks of HFD. Supporting the idea that HFpEF is a chronic pathology after 16 weeks of HFD, invasive hemodynamic data from our lab show impaired LVEPVR (ie, LV diastolic compliance) already after 12 weeks of HFD in B6D2/F1 female (not shown). Concerning the duration of the return to the standard diet, we chose 8 weeks because starting from an obese status, this allowed us to carry out our measurements after a period of (at least) 3 weeks of stable weight in each group, which reached the weight of control mice.

We first characterized HFpEF in B6D2/F1 mice fed an HFD for 16 weeks, which showed no reduction of the LV fractional shortening (direct index of ejection fraction), but resulted in diastolic dysfunction, evidenced by increased LV filling pressure (LVEDP) and impaired LV compliance (as demonstrated by the higher LVEDPVR). This HFpEF translated to a decrease in exercise capacity during an endurance-exhaustion test on a treadmill. This data mainly confirmed our previous characterization of HFpEF after 16 weeks of a high-fat diet in male mice from FVB/N genetic background [29].

Data in the present study indicated, that from 16 weeks of HFD, obese mice were in a state related to MetS, with increased total plasma cholesterol, and with increased fasting glycaemia and decreased insulin sensitivity as symptoms of T2D comorbidity. Switching to the standard diet after 16 weeks of HFD improved metabolic parameters associated with MetS, as well as insulin sensitivity, which no longer differed from that of Ctrl mice. Concomitant finerenone treatment did not enhance these improvements in global metabolic parameters.

After 24 weeks of HFD, in addition to the persistence of cardiac diastolic dysfunction, heart failure evolved toward the progression of LV systolic dysfunction, highlighted by slightly reduced LV fractional shortening, reduced LV stroke volume, and reduced cardiac output. Moreover, impaired coronary and microvascular function was evidenced by the decrease in myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) in obese mice. The return to the standard diet for 8 weeks (from week 17 to week 24), improved LV compliance but did not improve the LV filling pressure measured invasively, the MPR measured by MRI, nor the systolic parameters evaluated by echocardiography. Remarkably, compared to untreated mice upon diet normalization, concomitant finerenone treatment resulted in better improvement of LV compliance and specific improvements of LV filling pressure, MPR, LV fractional shortening, LV stroke volume, and cardiac output. It can be assumed that a longer return to the standard diet without pharmacological treatment could have further improved the aforementioned parameters, but finerenone was, at least, a clear accelerator of cardiac functional recovery.

Reduced MPR is an indicator of coronary microvascular dysfunction, and is associated with a sixfold increase in the risk of mortality in patients with chest pain, despite normal angiography [30]. In obese male patients with diabetes mellitus and albuminuria but without clinical cardiovascular disease, all treated with an ACE inhibitor and calcium channel blocker, adenosine-stimulated MPR was higher after 6 weeks of treatment with the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist eplerenone, compared to a thiazide diuretic [31]. The benefit of an MR antagonist on MPR was confirmed after 6 months of treatment with spironolactone in diabetic patients [32]. We previously evidenced that finerenone counteracts the reduction in MPR in mice after myocardial infarction, involving improvements in coronary endothelial function and NO bioavailability [33]. Altogether, these studies propose a pivotal role for MR in regulating MPR in different pathophysiological conditions, highlighting the interest in studying the evolution of myocardial perfusion during weight gain, and after weight loss with or without finerenone treatment. Interestingly, in C57Bl/6 mice, MPR was reduced after 24 weeks of a high-fat diet compared to 18 weeks, in the absence of decreased basal perfusion but with increased LV mass [34]. Moreover, no differences in these parameters were previously observed after just 12 weeks on the high-fat diet." [34]. In the present study in B6D2/F1 mice, there was no reduction in MPR after 16 weeks of HFD, nor an increase in LV mass, but a dysfunctional process was ongoing. Indeed, a return to the standard diet for 8 weeks was not enough to prevent a staggered degradation of the MPR, which was, however, limited by concomitant finerenone treatment, thus helping to promote an improvement in exercise capacity on a treadmill.

PTP1B was an interesting target to analyze in weight loss conditions, because its cardiac upregulation was previously demonstrated in the mouse models of HFD-induced obesity [35] and sucrose feeding-induced insulin resistance [36]. PTP1B notably reduces insulin-induced tyrosine phosphorylation of the insulin receptor and of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1), thereby reducing activation of downstream signaling pathways [28]. Interestingly, in rats fed a high-fat diet, compared to controls, intravenous infusion of insulin failed to increase the myocardial ratio of phospho-Akt/Akt [37] downstream to the activation of the insulin receptor. We previously showed that constitutive PTP1B deletion in mice prevented aging-associated cardiac dysfunction [38], and improved LV perfusion and diastolic compliance after myocardial infarction [39]. Importantly, it was reported that in obese mice, PTP1B deletion improved heart function and improved the contractility and relaxation parameters of isolated cardiomyocytes [35]. In mice fed a high-fat diet resulting in obesity and insulin resistance, we reported that PTP1B pharmacological inhibition improved LV compliance as well as insulin-NO-mediated relaxation of isolated coronary arteries [40].

Several publications highlighted relationships between PTP1B, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone-system (RAAS), and insulin actions in the heart and vessels. In isolated rat aortas, co-incubation with angiotensin-II antagonized the relaxation induced by insulin, while the addition of PTP1B inhibition restored it, involving eNOS activation and increased NO production downstream of the enhanced insulin receptor response [, 41]. On the other hand, in diabetic rats, 14-day pretreatment with the MR antagonist eplerenone resulted in cardio-protection immediately after ischemia/reperfusion injury, with improvements in LV functional parameters, oxidative stress, apoptosis and inflammatory cells infiltration [42]. This cardio-protection was dependent on the phosphoinositol-3 kinase (PI3-K)/Akt pathway and increased Akt phosphorylation ratio, since co-treatment with wortmanin, a PI3-K inhibitor, prevented the benefits of eplerenone [42]. Nevertheless, whether this was due to PTP1B inhibition upon MRA treatment was not assessed. Interestingly, chromatin immunoprecipitation by using a specific anti-MR antibody prior to DNA sequencing (ChIP-Seq), after aldosterone stimulation of a human renal cell line, made it possible to identify the PTPN1 gene of PTP1B, ranked seventh among genes with the highest scores in 974 MR genomic targets [27]. In addition, 24-h stimulation of these cells with 10 nM aldosterone resulted in a 1.3-fold induction of PTP1B mRNA compared to vehicle [27]. Taken together this data indicates that PTPN1 gene is a direct target of the aldo/MR.

We thus measured PTP1B expression in the heart, from continuously obese mice or after a return to the standard diet with or without finerenone treatment. Twenty-four weeks of HFD resulted in a 1.3-fold induction of PTP1B in the myocardium, likely involving MR activation, since the combination of finerenone treatment and the return to standard diet was necessary to reduce it, as well as this combination was necessary to increase Akt phosphorylated ratio. Therefore, part of the mechanistic beneficial effect of finerenone treatment might include benefits from a reduction in PTP1B activity, possibly enhancing insulin action in the cardiovascular system.

In regards to heart weight and average cardiomyocyte size, an 8-week return to the standard diet after 16 weeks of the HFD was not enough to prevent the development of LV hypertrophy. In all groups with increased LV weight, a higher number of macrophages infiltrated the myocardium, compared to Ctrl mice. However, no change was observed in the proportion of M2-like macrophages in any group. In contrast, compared to persistently obese mice, finerenone decreased the LV collagen content in a more pronounced manner than by returning to the standard diet alone. Analysis of images under polarized light microscopy showed that finerenone reduced the amount of green collagen fibers forming thin fibers composed of loosely packed thin fibrils (which would represent mainly type III collagen fibers), and reduced the amount of orange-red collagen fibers composed of closely packed thick fibrils and/or well bridged fibrils (which would represent mainly type I collagen fibers) [43]. Our results are consistent with historical data from the Aldo-salt rat model of cardiac overload, in which a sub-hypotensive dose of MR antagonist reduced LV fibrosis without change in LV hypertrophy [44, 45]. Thus, the results demonstrate that finerenone specifically limits the development of fibrosis in the context of heart remodeling, which contributes to the improvement of heart function.

One can hypothesize that a longer return to the standard diet could have contributed to improvements in all the aforementioned parameters, even independently of finerenone treatment. However, our results already concern a rodent model with a weight loss phase followed by a stable period of body weight of at least 3 weeks and in any case, even if the real advantage of finerenone is the acceleration of cardiovascular recovery, it is nonetheless a significant benefit for therapeutic care. On the other hand, it can also be considered that despite the physiological benefits of maintained weight loss, the data from the present study illustrates that a detrimental memory after prolonged period of obesity includes cardiovascular disturbances. This legacy memory, which should be controlled to prevent further complications, has been described as integrating several characteristics i.e., maintained oxidative stress, non-enzymatic glycation of proteins, epigenetic changes, and chronic inflammation [46]. Although it deserves more investigations, we can speculate that finerenone might have participated in restricting the memory trace of previous obesity. For example, as a contributing mechanism to restrain such detrimental memory, we previously reported the rapid action of a 7-day finerenone treatment in reducing oxidative stress in the Zucker fa/fa rats model of MetS, as evidenced by a reduction in LV reactive oxygen species content and improved plasma nitrite levels [24].

As a precaution, we had to consider the risk of confounding effect that could have resulted from a variation of food intake between finerenone treated or untreated group during the return to the standard diet. All mice back to the standard diet were fed the same diet, containing 0.28% sodium. Therefore, even if aldosterone is known to regulate sodium appetite, we did not expect a major effect in terms of food consumption due to MR blockade. By weighing the quantity of food consumed per week per cage (no individual values), we did not measure any difference between the rSD and rSD + F groups. This was globally confirmed by mixed-effect statistical analysis of weight tracking curves. During the study of weight reduction from week 17 to 24, there was no difference between the groups rSD and rSD + F, except at the end of week 18 (after 2 weeks of return to the standard diet). Moreover, compared to continuously obese mice in the HFD group, the weight reduction was already significant in the rSD + F group at the end of week 17 and not yet in the rSD group (after which the reduction was significant for both groups until the end of the protocol). It would require further investigation to know if these observations should be taken into account in relation to an accelerated cardiovascular recovery thanks to the pharmacological treatment, but this does not detract from the benefit of the finerenone.

Recently, in the FINEARTS trial (Finenone-MinerAlocorticoid Receptor antagonist Tolerability Study-HF) in patients with HFpEF or with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), treatment with finerenone in addition to usual therapy resulted in a lower rate of the primary composite endpoint of total worsening of HF events and cardiovascular mortality [47]. In FINEARTS, in the finerenone and placebo groups, only 13.1% and 14.1% of patients were treated with an SGLT-2 inhibitor respectively (and only 2.6% and 2.9% with a GLP-1 analogue), highlighting that the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist contribution addressed an unmet need in these patients. Furthermore, patients treated or not with SGLT-2 inhibitor had similar results with regard to the primary endpoint. Weight loss was not taken into consideration in the results of the FINEARTS study, which was mainly conducted in overweight individuals or individuals with grade I obesity (average BMI of 30 ± 6 kg/m2, including over 40% of type-2 diabetics), with 45.3% and 49.4% of adverse events occurring in patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 in the finerenone and placebo groups, respectively. The possible cumulative benefits of weight loss, whether assisted by drug therapy or not, and treatment with finerenone should be the subject of in-depth studies in patients. Indeed, our preclinical data suggest that finerenone enhances, and specifically benefits, the dynamics of cardiovascular improvements provided by weight reduction in obese individuals with HFpEF or HFmrEF.
