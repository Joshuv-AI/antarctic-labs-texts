OsSSID6 Negatively Regulates Salt Stress Tolerance in Rice (Oryza Sativa L.) via Metabolic Pathways and ROS Scavenging
Authors: Ziyi Wang, Xiaofei Zan, Hao Chen, Jiali Zhu, Xiulin Zhao, Mingfei Chen, Yijuan Kong, Juan Yang, Xiaomei Jia, Xiaoying Ye, Chuanqiang Liu, Rongjun Chen, Jianqing Zhu, Jun Zhu, Lihua Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil salinization is becoming a huge threat to reducing productivity of rice (Oryza sativa L.) around the world. Previous studies have found that some Domain of unknown function (DUF) proteins play an essential role in the growth and development of plants. The DUF936 family is reported to respond to abiotic stresses, but the specific molecular mechanisms of its members remain elusive. In this study, OsSSID6 (Salt-Stress Induced DUF936 protein) is found at the cell membrane and the protein’s expression could be affected by several abiotic stresses. The CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines increased salt tolerance in rice, whereas the overexpression lines showed more sensitivity. And meanwhile the similar changes of ROS-scavenging capacity were observed both in knockout and overexpression lines. Transcriptome analysis identified that the expression of genes linked to multiple metabolic pathways, including phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, and stress response, was significantly up-regulated in KO lines. Our findings reveal that OsSSID6 gene modulates rice salt stress tolerance by orchestrating a network of metabolic pathways, including those involved in the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging system, phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis and stress response-related mechanism. sThese results provide important information for engineering salt-tolerant crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00868-3.Keywords: Rice, Salt stress, DUF, ROS, Transcriptome analysis

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is among the principal food crops worldwide, particularly in Asia (Cantrell and Reeves 2002). However, in recent years, soil salinization has become one of the important factors threatening global crop yield (Zelm et al. 2020). Globally, more than 1 billion hectares of land are affected by salinization, which will get worse as climate change intensifies (Pabuayon et al. 2020). Salinization leads to the deposition for excessive soluble salts, such as Ca2+, Na+, Cl−, etc(Zhang et al. 2022). Plants primarily absorb water from the soil through their roots to sustain growth. However, the accumulation of excessive salts reduces the soil water potential around the roots, which limits water supply to the plants and decreasing the uptake of both water and nutrients. This leads to a decline in plant cell turgor pressure inhibits cell elongation, and ultimately results in impaired plant growth, reduced plant height (Liu et al. 2022; Machado and Serralheiro 2017; Kumar et al. 2020). The water deficit induced by salt stress severely affects individual growth and development. For instance, seed germination depends onenzymes like α-amylase, which break down starch stored in the endosperm into metabolizable sugars to provide energy for the developing embryo and radicle. During this period, a decrease in soil water potential hinders seed water absorption, thereby delaying germination. Existing studies has shown that salt-sensitive genotypes exhibit lower α-amylase activity compared to salt-tolerant genotypes (Orlovsky et al. 2016). The reduced enzyme activity significantly inhibits sugar transport, thereby constraining embryo growth and development (Hasanuzzaman et al. 2021). In addition, the water deficit in plants under salt stress also impairs root development, hinders nutrient uptake, and causes leaf curling and yellowing, ultimately reducing crop yield (Orlovsky et al. 2016; Gong 2021a, b).

The accumulation of salt in the soil also triggers diverse physiological and molecular changes (Zelm et al. 2020; Gong 2021a, b). The initial response primary stresses such as osmotic stress and ion toxicity. Under salt stress, plants accumulate soluble osmoprotectants to counteract osmotic stress, such as proline, soluble sugars, and polyamines (Munns 2002; Apse and Blumwald 2002; Kosar et al. 2019; Pommerrenig et al. 2007). These osmoprotectants share a common characteristic: even at high concentrations, they lower the cytoplasmic osmotic potential without disrupting metabolism and help stabilize proteins, thereby reducing cellular damage. As a key osmoprotectant, Proline enhances salt tolerance in rice (Mansour and Ali 2017). This is achieved through the upregulation of biosynthetic genes like OsP5CS, which promotes proline accumulation (Kotchoni et al. 2010; Sripinyowanich et al. 2013). Meanwhile, the ion homeostasis is critical for maintaining cell membrane stability and supporting growth. However, excessive Na⁺ enters plant root cells via non-selective cation channels (NSCCs) (Demidchik and Maathuis 2007). This accumulation of Na⁺ not only causes toxicity to cellular structures but also disrupts the uptake of other essential cations, leading to nutritional imbalances (Rahman et al. 2016).

Salt stress further induces oxidative stress (Zelm et al. 2020; Gong 2021a, b), which stems from the toxic accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These molecules damage membranes and disrupt physiological processes. When present in excess, ROS react with cellular components, causing oxidative damage such as cell membrane injury, physiological dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and protein denaturation (Hasanuzzaman et al. 2021; Chen and Hoehenwarter 2015; Singh et al. 2015). Among these, lipid peroxidation is a key indicator of oxidative damage (Farmer and Mueller 2013). Severe lipid oxidation significantly reduces membrane fluidity, disrupts membrane integrity, and impairs the weaken function of cell membranes, thereby exposing intracellular structures to further destruction (Møller et al. 2007). Enhancing salt tolerance in plants relies, to a certain extent, on strengthening their capacity to maintain ROS homeostasis and mitigate oxidative stress. This is accomplished mainly through the upregulating the activity of both enzymatic(Murphy Michael et al. 2011) and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems (Hanin et al. 2016). Boosting the activity of ROS-scavenging enzymes improves plant tolerance to salt stress (Bose et al. 2013; Mittova et al. 2000; Zhang et al. 2013a, b). For instance, overexpressing OsNCED1 or OsAPX2 increases peroxidase (POD) activity, which enhancing resistance of rice to drought and salt stress. As positive regulators of salt tolerance, SAPK1 and SAPK2 synergistically act during rice germination and seedling stages by stimulating ascorbic acid production and elevating the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), thereby promoting ROS detoxification following salt stress (Lou et al. 2018a, b). In addition, plant responses to salt stress involve multiple pathways—ranging from the activation of Ca²⁺ channels that initiate stress signaling, to physiological-level synthesis of protective compounds (Park et al. 2016).

The Domains of Unknown Function (DUF) protein family, as a large family, has two main characteristics: relatively conserved amino acid sequence and unknown domain function (Lv et al. 2023). Since the initial identification of DUF1 and DUF2 in 1988 (Schultz et al. 1998; Romling and Simm 2009; Bateman et al. 2010; Einspahr and Weiss 2013), later renamed the GGDEF and EAL domains after their functions were elucidated—the number of identified DUF families has continued to grow. The Pfam database (version 35.0) currently includes 19,632 protein families, 4795 of which are DUFs (Lv et al. 2023). Although DUFs are widely distributed in nature, certain families, such as DUF579, DUF640, DUF86 and DUF642, are found specifically in plants.

The functions of most DUFs remain unknown, while some have been verified to participate in diverse biological processes, such as developmental regulation, substance synthesis, and hormone perception. A number of DUFs have been identified to be involved in growth and substance synthesis functions, for example, the RUS1, RUS2, RUS4, DUF266, DUF231 and DUF1218 of Arabidopsis thaliana are reported to regulate the development of plant. Similarly, S22B, BC10 (Zhou et al. 2009) and OsRMC could affect the root growth and pollen fertility of rice. Several DUFs, such as OsSGL (Cui et al. 2016), OsSIDP366, OsSIDP361 and OsDSR2, contribute to abiotic tress responses in rice. Although significant research has been conducted on DUFs, the full scope of their biological functions remains to be explored.

In a previous study, nine members of rice DUF936 family have been identified and analyzed preliminarily. Among them, OsDUF936.3, OsDUF936.5, and OsDUF936.6 exhibited significantly upregulated expression under salt stress, whereas only overexpression of OsDUF936.6 in E. coli markedly enhanced salt tolerance (Li et al. 2018). In this study, we investigated that OsSSID6 negatively regulates salt tolerance in rice seedlings by transgenic, biochemical and molecular approaches, RNA-seq and RT-qPCR were used to reveal its regulatory network. This study contributes to elucidate the mechanism of OsSSID6 in salt tolerance preliminary, and offer a potential gene associated with salt tolerance breeding in rice. Although substantial research has been conducted on the molecular mechanisms of salinity response and tolerance in rice, these processes remain incompletely understood—particularly regarding the DUF protein family. A comprehensive analysis of the molecular functions of OsSSID6 will enable us to ascertain its role in conferring salt tolerance to plants. Such research holds dual significance: it will provide novel and valuable insights for the engineering of salt-tolerant crops, and simultaneously advance our knowledge of the intricate salt stress signaling pathways in rice, particularly through in-depth investigation of the DUF protein family.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material, Growth Condition and Salt Stress Treatment

The sequence of OsSSID6 (Gene ID: LOC_Os09g34110) was cloned from rice (Oryza sativa L. subsp. japonica cv. Nipponbare). The knockout (KO) mutants and overexpression (OE) lines used in this study were generated in Nipponbare. The seeds underwent a 30-minute soak in 2% (v/v) NaClO, followed by rinsing with sterile water and subsequent germination. Then, the rice plants were cultivated in a growth chamber using a modified Yosida medium having a period that consists of 16 h of illumination and 8 h of darkness. The nutrient solution used for culture and stress treatment was replaced every 3 days. The modified nutrient solution formulation is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

To determine the salt tolerance of transgenic plants, seedlings from wild type (WT), knockout (KO) and overexpression (OE) lines which had reached 2 weeks of age were transferred into nutrient solution with 200 mM NaCl and maintained there for 5 days, allowed by a 10-day recovery period. The salt stress tolerance was evaluated considering the survival rate of seedlings. The survival rate was derived from the ratio of surviving plants to total treated plants. Meanwhile, to simulate salt stress in the field, we conducted a soil experiment. The seedlings were cultured in pots for 2 weeks under normal conditions and then irrigated with a 200 mM NaCl solution, which was replenished every 3 days. The salt stress tolerance was evaluated considering the fresh weight under salt stress for 10 days.

Besides, in order to observe the germination of seeds and seedling growth under salt stress, nutrient solution with different salt concentrations, from 0 to 200 mM, were used for treatments, respectively.

Plasmid Construction and Generation of Transgenic Plants

The KO lines were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Zhang et al. 2019). To get the OE lines, the coding sequence of OsSSID6 was amplified form Nipponbare and inserted into the pU1300 vector, which recombinant plasmid was introduced into callus of Nipponbare by Agrobacterium tumefacien-mediated transformation. For tissue expression pattern analysis, the 1.5 kb promoter sequence of OsSSID6 was ligated upstream of the GUS reporter gene in pCAMBIA1305 vector, and OsSSID6pro: GUS plants were obtained. RT-qPCR and target sequence amplification were used to identify transgenic plants, and Table S1 lists the primers used for vector construction and detection.

RNA Extraction and Real-Time qPCR Analysis

2-week-old seedlings of WT were subjected to 24 h stress treatments at 4℃, 42℃, 50 µM ABA, 20% (w/v) PEG and 150 mM NaCl, respectively. And samples were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 24 h after treatment initiation, allowed by expression analysis. Seedlings from WT and transgenic lines which had reached 2 weeks of age were treated with 200mM NaCl, and sampled at 0 h (control) and 8 h (NaCl) for expression analysis of genes related to stress response.

Total RNA extraction from all samples was performed using Trizol reagent (Nanjing Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcriptase (Nanjing Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and was used for RT-qPCR, using a SYBR GreenPCR Master Mix kit (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), to analyze the transcriptional accumulation of target genes. Ubiquitin gene (Os01g0328400) was used as the internal reference gene for rice. The relative quantification technique was applied to quantify the relative expression levels as described (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Table S2 lists all primers used for expression analysis.

Histochemical Detection of GUS Activity and Subcellular Localization of OsSSID6

Tissues from OsSSID6pro: GUS transgenic seedlings were collected and stained in accordance with the previously described method (Wu et al. 2003). The images were captured using a ZEISS stereo microscope. The coding sequence of OsSSID6 without the TGA terminator was cloned from Nipponbare cDNA and fused with GFP using pCAMBIA2300-GFP vector. The 35 S: OsSSID6-GFP and 35 S: GFP plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and co-infected 4-weeks-old tobacco leaves with membrane marker (OsPIP1;3). After 48 h, the subcellular localization was examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Physiological Measurements and Staining

Seedlings aged 2 weeks were subjected to 200 mM NaCl treatment for 0 or 48 h. Subsequently, approximately 0.2 g of shoots were homogenized with 100 mM phosphate buffer solution (pH = 7.0) and centrifuged at 8000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), and the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) and proline (Pro) were analyzed using the supernatant, as described in the previous study (Chen et al. 2015). For determination of O2− and H2O2 after salt stress, the leaves were incubated in 3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) or Nitro Blue Tetrazolium (NBT) solution for 12 h, then decolorized with absolute ethanol until colorless and the photos were taken by ZEISS stereo microscope.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from three-leaf stage seedlings of WT, KO and OE lines, whether they had been treated with 200 mM NaCl for 0–24 h, with three biological replicates per sample. Majorbio. Inc. (Shanghai, China) executed RNA purification, reverse transcription, library construction, sequencing and standard RNA-seq analysis. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using Goatools, and KEGG enrichment analysis was implemented using Python’s scipy software at the same time.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis of the experimental data was carried out by means of GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The statistical difference was elucidated by t-test analysis of variance, with P < 0.05 (*) and P < 0.01 (**) being significant. Three technical replicates were completed for each sample. Data are shown as means ± SD.

Results

GUS Activity Assay and Subcellular Localization of OsSSID6

In previous work, we preliminarily analyzed the expression pattern of OsSSID6 in 15 tissues by real-time PCR and demonstrated that its expression is influenced by several abiotic stress (Li et al. 2018). To further validate the spatial expression profile of OsSSID6, transgenic plants harboring the OsSSID6pro: GUS construct were developed, and β-glucuronidase (GUS) activity assays were performed on a variety of tissues, such as anthers, internodes, young stems, leaf sheaths, spikelet hulls, and seeds. The results showed that the expression of OsSSID6 could be tissue specific (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted to detect the expression pattern of the gene in 2-week-old WT seedlings subjected to multiple abiotic stresses for 24 h. The expression level of the gene was altered to varying degrees under these stress conditions (Figure S1). In parallel, seedlings were separately imposed different abiotic stress treatments for 12 h. Compared with the normal condition, the root staining changed in different degrees. After treatment with 4℃, 50 mM ABA or 20% (w/v) PEG, the staining of root was deepened, which was shallow to a certain extent after 200 mM NaCl or 42℃ treatment (Fig. 1B) This findings suggested that OsSSID6 can response to multiple abiotic stress.

To determine the subcellular localization of OsSSID6, a recombinant OsSSID6-GFP fusion proteins was employed. The results of transient expression showed that the green fluorescent signal of fusion protein was predominantly found at the cell membrane and overlapped with the membrane marker (Fig. 1C).

Knockout of OsSSID6 Confers Rice Salt Stress Tolerance

In the background of Nipponbare, the knockout and OE lines were generated to study its biological function. Three independent OsSSID6-overexpressing (OE-1, OE-2, OE-3) lines were confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure S2A). Three homozygous knockout mutants (KO-1, KO-2, KO-3) had different number of base deletions or insertions respectively, leading to premature termination of protein translation (Figure S2B).

The performance of transgenic plants under salt stress was evaluated at different developmental stages. First, germination assays were conducted under 150 mM and 200 mM NaCl treatments and both WT and transgenic seeds showed varying degrees of inhibition. However, KO lines exhibited higher germination rates and less inhibition compared to WT, while OE lines showed the opposite trend (Fig. 2A). Next, the 2-day-old WT and transgenic seedlings of uniform growth were used for exploring the growth of seedlings in nutrient solution with 0 or 150 mM NaCl, maintained for 5 days. In the control group, there was no obvious difference (Fig. 2B, C). However, seedling growth was suppressed in all lines. The OE lines were most inhibited, showing shorter seedling height and more severe root development retardation. In contrast, KO lines, especially KO-3, performed better than WT (Fig. 2D, E).

Furthermore, two-week-old seedlings were subjected to a nutrient solution with 200 mM NaCl for 5 days, followed by a 10-day recovery period. The survival rate of OE-1, OE-2 and OE-3 were 33%, 31% and 32%, respectively, and all lower than the WT of 42%. In contrast, KO-1, KO-2 and KO-3 exhibited higher survival rates of 58%, 67%, and 57%, respectively (Fig. 2F-I). To further verify this phenotype, a soil-based salt stress assay was performed. After treatment, KO lines retained more green tissue compared to WT, whereas OE lines showed more severe salt injury, including increased wilting (Figure S3). Additionally, following 10 days of 200 mM NaCl treatment, OE lines had lower fresh weight than WT, while KO lines displayed higher fresh weight (Fig. 2J). Collectively, these results suggest OsSSID6 acts as a negative regulator of rice salt tolerance at both germination and seedling stage.

OsSSID6 Affects Salt Tolerance of Rice by Regulating Physiological Mechanism and ROS Scavenging System

The potential physiological mechanism underlying improved salt tolerance was investigated by examining changes in stress-relevant parameters, including MDA and proline accumulation. Under normal condition, no significant differences were observed in MDA or proline levels between genotypes. But upon exposure to salt stress for 2 days, the OE lines exhibited a higher MDA content compared to the WT, while it was lower in KO lines (Fig. 3A). Proline content showed the opposite trend: it was lower in OE lines and higher in KO lines relative to WT under salt stress (Fig. 3B). Consistent with this, the expression of OsP5CS2, a key gene in proline biosynthesis, was upregulated in KO lines and downregulated in OE lines (Fig. 3C), suggesting enhanced osmotic adjustment capacity in the knockout genotype.

During stress, by-products are produced, which are harmful to plants, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) is one of them. Therefore, to detect the accumulation of O₂⁻ and H₂O₂ in WT and transgenic plants subjected to salt stress, NBT and DAB staining were used. The control group exhibited no significant differences. Following a 2-day exposure to 200 mM NaCl, however, the staining results showed that the OE lines were both accumulated more O2− and H2O2 compared with WT, which were characterized by the appearance of a greater number of blue-purple spots and more severe browning on the surface of leaf, but these symptoms were less severe on the leaf surface of knockout lines (Fig. 3D, E). In addition, we detected the enzyme activities of ROS-scavenging system, including POD, SOD and CAT. Significant differences were absent in the control group. After applying salt stress, these enzyme activities were increased to varying degrees. Compared with WT, the enhancement of enzyme activities in OE lines was smaller, while that in knockout lines was significant (Fig. 3F). This result could be mutually confirmed with the staining results.

Knockout of OsSSID6 Confers Rice Oxidative Stress Tolerance

The enhanced activity of ROS clearance system in knockout lines suggested that knockout of OsSSID6 might improve the oxidative stress tolerance of rice. To confirm this speculation, we used methyl viologen (MV) to simulate oxidative stress. WT and transgenic line seeds were immersed in nutrient solutions containing 0 or 10 µM MV, and germination was counted. Compared with WT, the OE lines were more inhibited by MV, as indicated by the lower germination rate, while that of knockout lines was higher (Fig. 4A). In a subsequent experiment, 2-day-old seedlings of WT and transgenic seedlings were grown for 5 days in nutrient solution with 0 or 30 µM MV. Under MV treatment, the growth of seedlings was inhibited to a certain extent, but the OE lines had a lower seedling height than the WT, while the knockout lines were less affected (Fig. 4B-E).

In addition, we observed the growth state of 2-week-old of WT and transgenic seedlings at higher MV concentrations. After 10 days of treatment with 50 µM MV, KO lines showed no severe wilting, while both WT and OE lines exhibited wilting symptoms, with the latter being more severely affected (Fig. 4F). Transcriptome Analysis of OsSSID6 Transgenic Lines in Response To Salt Stress

To investigate the underlying mechanism by which OsSSID6 regulates salt tolerance in rice, we performed the RNA-seq analysis in OE, KO and WT under normal and salt stress conditions, using a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 (up-regulated) or FC ≤ 0.5 (down-regulated). A total of 36,474 genes were detected in this study. 12,431 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) affected by salt stress were identified in WT. Besides, 13,058 and 11,105 DEGs of OE and KO lines were identified after salt stress (Fig. 5A). We found that, after treatment, 743 genes were specifically up-regulated in KO, and 1,806 genes were specifically down-regulated in OE lines, respectively (Fig. 5B). By conducting KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analysis (Fig. 5C), the 743 genes from KO lines were mainly concentrated in “Zeatin biosynthesis.” and “Starch and sucrose metabolism”, while the specifically down-regulated genes from OE lines were enriched in “Zeatin biosynthesis” and “Phenylalanine metabolism”. Then, we made a comparison of the above results and showed that 79 genes were overlapping. Subsequent analysis demonstrated that the genes were primarily concentrated in pathways such as “Flavonoid biosynthesis” and “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis,” which are relevant to plant responses to abiotic stress. Phenylpropanoid pathway could form many polyphenols, which are generally considered as non-enzymatic compounds for antioxidant, and the up-regulation of related genes in this pathway may explain the enhanced antioxidant capacity of KO lines to some extent.

Discussion

Knockout of OsSSID6 Enhances Salt Tolerance of Rice

Salinity stress is one of most challenging abiotic stresses, as it can cause yield loss when crops encounter during their life (Ahmad et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2021). A variety of physiological and molecular changes are triggered by salt stress, which hampers plant growth through the inhibition of photosynthesis, leading to reduced resources and suppressed cell division and expansion (Zhou et al. 2024). A comprehensive comprehending of the mechanisms underlying salt tolerance is crucial to deal with this and cultivate salt-tolerant rice varieties.

Investigating salt stress-related genes is an essential means for elucidating rice salt tolerance, and lots of genes have been documented, such as OsGS1;1, OsGS2 (James et al. 2018) OsNAC6 (Nakashima et al. 2007) and etc. The whole-genome sequencing of rice (Oryza sativa L.) has made it possible to characterize all DUF family of rice, but only a few of them have been identified about their biological functions. In our study, OsSSID6 encodes a protein located at cell membrane and knockout mutants had a higher tolerance to salt stress. As expected, overexpression of OsSSID6 decreased rice tolerance to salt stress, which was corroborated by various stress phenotypes, such as leaf chlorosis, low survival rate at high salt concentration. These findings indicate that OsSSID6 could work as negative regulator of rice response to salt stress.

Salt Tolerance of Knockout Lines is Attributed to ROS Homeostasis

The change of stress resistance may involve comprehensive changes in some aspects and among them, the ROS signal in addressing abiotic stress have been explored extensively (Miller et al. 2008; Carvalho et al. 2013). Intracellular ROS remains at a low level under optimal growth conditions. Under salt stress, due to the disturbance of cellular water potential (Hussain et al. 2018; Mittler et al. 2004), ROS homeostasis is disrupted and accompanied by damage to the cell and even individuals, generally. The enhancement of salt stress tolerance depends to some extent on the ability to maintain ROS homeostasis and cope with oxidative stress (Murphy et al. 2011), which is mainly achieved by increasing the activity of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems (Hanin et al. 2016), such as POD, SOD, CAT and APX, etc. These are important physiological indicators of salt tolerance in plants (Zhang et al. 2013a, b; Baoxiang et al. 2021; Lou et al. 2018a, b). In this study, the DAB and NBT staining revealed that levels of H2O2 and O2− were different in KO and OE lines under salt stress. The low level of them in KO lines may due to the stronger enzyme activity of scavenging system. SOD catalyzes dismutation of O2− into H2O2 and O2. CAT plays the role of breaking down H2O2 in cell and POD conducts substrate catalytic reactions at the expense of H2O2 (Scandalios 2002). Our results showed that the enzyme activities were increased but decreased in KO and OE lines respectively, and RNA-seq showed that genes associated with peroxidase synthesis were up-regulated in KO lines. Based on these analyses, the reduction in ROS accumulation in KO lines may be due to the enhancement of scavenging capacity, while the OE lines may be due to the low enzyme activity in vivo, thereby reducing the ability to convert H2O2, O2− into non-toxic H2O and O2 to protect cells.

Oxidative stress is a complex chemical and physiological phenomenon accompanied by almost all abiotic stresses in plants and develops along with the excessive production and accumulation of ROS (Demidchik 2015). It causes reversible or irreversible modification of substances, such as proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and etc (Farmer and Mueller 2013). Among them, lipid oxidation (also known as lipid peroxidation) is widely considered as a “hallmark” of oxidative stress (Tsikas 2017) and terminal products of it are corrosive substances, such as MDA, which can reflect the degree of cell membrane damage under stress to some extent. BnBBX22.A07 overexpression promotes salt tolerance in Arabidopsis and B. napus and MDA exhibited no obvious increased in the salt-treated OE lines (Zhang et al. 2024). Lower MDA content showed that the cell membrane was less severe in KO lines. Os4BGlu12 has been reported to be involved in remodeling damaged cell walls, and it may play a role in defense responses and the degradation of cell wall-derived oligosaccharides (Opassiri et al. 2010) and RNA-seq showed its expression was enhanced in KO lines when exposing to stress. We speculated cell wall remodeling may have occurred, thus reducing the extent of membrane damage.

As a promoter of intracellular oxidative stress, methyl viologen (MV) exerts phytotoxic effects on plants by transferring electrons from photosystem I to O2 and leading to the generation of O2− in chloroplasts (Kerchev et al. 2015). Currently, most studies on plant oxidative stress use MV for simulation. For example, expression of AtGR2 (Wang et al. 2019) or swpa4 (Kim et al. 2021) could enhance tolerance to methyl viologen-mediated oxidative stress in tobacco and Arabidopsis thaliana, respectively. In rice, the similar performance was observed for overexpression of OsGRAS23 (Xu et al. 2015). In this study, although WT and all transgenic lines were damaged after MV treatment, the KO lines still showed better performance than others.

Multi-type Metabolic Regulatory Pathways Affected by OsSSID6 Under Salt Stress

Under salt stress, disruption of plant cell homeostasis usually causes osmotic stress, which involves a variety of transient biophysical changes, such as reduced cell turgor pressure and plasma membrane contraction (Park et al. 2016). To alleviate these adverse effects, plants rely on activating synthesis of osmolytes, such as proline (Sripinyowanich et al. 2013; Hur et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2021), polyols and sugars. Metabolism of starch and sucrose is one way to salt stress response. starch is not only the main energy source, but also participates in stress response mechanisms (Liu et al. 2020; Deng et al. 2020). αAmy is a key enzyme that hydrolyzes starch into sugars to increase plant stress resistance, for example, inhibition of OsMYBS2 could induce the expression of αAmy3, thus enhancing rice’s ability to tolerate osmotic stress (Chen et al. 2019a, b, c). Trehalose, as an intermediate product of starch and sucrose metabolism, plays a role in reducing damage (Samadi et al. 2019). For instance, overexpression of OsTPP3 could enhance trehalose synthesis and improve salt tolerance (Jiang et al. 2019). In this study, KO lines showed increased proline accumulation under salt stress, RNA-seq and RT-qPCR results showed that related genes, such as RAmy3A, RAmy3D, trehalose-6-phosphate synthases and phosphatases, were both up-regulated in KO lines, and the opposite was true in OE lines.

To elucidate gene regulation networks in plants, RNA-seq analysis has been utilized. Our transcriptomic sequencing and KEGG pathway analyses indicated that OsSSID6 expression significantly up- or down-regulated the expression of many genes including arginine and proline metabolism, Plant hormone signaling, Photosynthesis, Biotin metabolism, Starch and sugar metabolism, CoA biosynthesis, Pantothenate and MAPK signaling pathway, linoleic acid metabolism and so on. Among them, the “secondary metabolism” accounts for a large part. Specialized metabolites, which have traditionally been termed “secondary metabolites,” play roles in multiple plant processes, with the phenylpropanoid pathway being the most extensively investigated (Jiang et al. 2016, 2020), generating over 8,000 metabolites that facilitate plant–environment interactions (Dong and Lin 2021). The initial catalytic reaction involves three enzymes, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamic 4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarine-CoA ligase (4CL) (Fraser and Chapple 2011). As the key rate-limiting enzyme, the enhancement of PAL(Li et al. 2024) activity is associated with increase of product and stress tolerance. The downstream of phenylpropanoid pathway involves different branches, and flavonoid metabolism is one of them. Salt stress is thought to induce biosynthesis of flavonoids (Kim et al. 2017). At high salt concentrations, the expression of NtCHS is activated and promotes the accumulation of flavonols such as rutin in tobacco (Chen et al. 2019a, b, c). Overexpression of CHS, DFR and other flavonoid structural genes could enhance the tolerance of rice and tobacco to salt stress, by increasing the levels of flavonol and anthocyanin glycosides, reducing the accumulation of ROS (Cui et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2019a, b, c). In addition to decreasing oxidative damage caused by accumulation of ROS (Nakabayashi and Saito 2015), they could also function as signaling molecules to activate defense-related signaling pathways and regulatory mechanisms, thereby improving stress tolerance (Agati et al. 2011; Landi et al. 2015). Previous studies have highlighted the key role of these pathways in plant abiotic stress response (Zhan et al. 2019). Our study showed that the specific DEGs were enriched in “Flavonoid biosynthesis”, “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, and the related genes involved in were up-regulated in KO lines, such as OsPAL1, OsC4H2, Os4CL5 and CYP75B3, indicating the significance of the phenylpropanoid pathway in OsSSID6-mediated salt tolerance.

What’s more, the molecular mechanism by which OsSSID6 regulates salt tolerance through the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway remains to be further elucidated. Subsequent research will focus on: (1) verifying the differential accumulation of flavonoid compounds between OE and KO lines under salt stress, and (2) conducting protein-protein interaction assays within related pathways. These experiments are expected to provide deeper insights into the molecular basis of OsSSID6-mediated salt tolerance regulation in rice.

Conclusion

In this study, in light of our results, we put forward a hypothetical regulatory model illustrating the role of OsSSID6 in rice salt tolerance. (Fig. 7). We found that salt sensitivity in rice is partly attributable to the salt-induced activation of OsSSID6. We observed that overexpression of OsSSID6 induced by salt stress downregulated the expression of stress resistance genes (Figs. 6), whereas knockout of OsSSID6 exerted positive effects on salt tolerance. Consequently, the activity of OsSSID6 must be tightly regulated. These discoveries provide critical insights for the engineering of salt-tolerant crops.
