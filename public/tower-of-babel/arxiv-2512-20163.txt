Population Protocols Revisited: Parity and Beyond
Leszek Gąsieniec, Tytus Grodzicki, Tomasz Jurdziński, Jakub Kowalski, Grzegorz Stachowiak ∗

Abstract
The population protocol model is used to study the power of pairwise interactions between simple, indistinguishable entities, referred to as agents . In this model, each agent is equipped with limited storage, represented by a single state drawn from a predefined state space. In the probabilistic variant of population protocols adopted here, a random scheduler selects an interacting pair of agents uniformly at random at each step of the protocol. In this variant, in addition to efficient state utilisation , one is also concerned with the stabilisation time , determined by the number of interactions required to reach the final configuration, divided by the population size n n . We say that a protocol is time-efficient if it stabilises in polylogarithmic (in n n ) time, and space-efficient if it utilises polylogarithmic state space. We also say that a protocol is stable if it stabilises with the correct answer with probability 1, and silent if agents do not change their states after stabilisation. For nearly two decades, population protocols have been extensively studied, yielding efficient solutions for central problems in distributed computing, including leader election , and majority computation – a predicate type in Presburger Arithmetic closely tied to population protocols. Surprisingly, no protocols have achieved both time- and space-efficiency for congruency predicates, such as parity computation, which are complementary in this arithmetic framework. This gap highlights a significant challenge in the field. To address this gap, we explore the parity problem , where agents are tasked with computing the parity of the given sub-population size. Then we extend the solution for parity to compute congruences modulo an arbitrary m m . Previous research on efficient population protocols has focused on protocols that minimise both stabilisation time and state utilisation for specific problems. In contrast, this work slightly relaxes this expectation, permitting protocols to place less emphasis on full optimisation and more on universality, robustness, and probabilistic guarantees. This allows us to propose a novel computing paradigm that integrates population weights (or simply weights ), a robust clocking mechanism, and efficient anomaly detection coupled with a switching mechanism (which ensures slow but always correct solutions). This paradigm facilitates universal design of efficient multistage stable population protocols. Specifically, the first efficient parity and congruence protocols introduced here use both O ⁡ ( log 3 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{3}n) states and achieve silent stabilisation in O ⁡ ( log 3 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{3}n) time. We conclude by discussing the impact of implicit conversion between unary and binary representations enabled by the weight system, with applications to other problems, including the computation and representation of (sub-)population sizes. All algorithmic solutions presented in this paper are stable, silent, and guarantee stabilisation time with high probability (whp) defined as 1 − n − η , 1-n^{-\eta}, for a constant η > 0 . \eta>0.

Introduction

The model of population protocols originates from the seminal paper
[
5
]

between simple, indistinguishable entities referred to as

When two agents engage in a direct interaction, their states change according to the predefined

The weakest assumptions in population protocols concern the fixed (constant-size) operational

of agents, with the population size

It is assumed that a protocol starts in the predefined

In the standard

In the probabilistic variant, in addition to efficient

All algorithmic solutions presented in this paper are

for a constant

In this paper, the rules of a transition function are denoted as
A+B\rightarrow A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}

Encoding a state of an agent can be seen as labeling it by a number represented in a binary form.
Encoding compound states can be achieved by utilising specified positions in binary representation for each substate set.

1.1
Related Work and Results

Population protocol studies primarily follow two main research avenues. The first focuses on understanding the behavior of large populations driven by simple interaction rules. The second treats population protocols as computational systems, investigating their computational power based on factors such as the type of scheduler and the memory available to individual agents.
This study refers to the latter, focusing on designing efficient population protocols with robust probabilistic guarantees.

The original population protocol model
[
5
]
assumes that the number of states and the transition function are constant, i.e., independent of the population size
n
.
However, for fundamental problems such as
leader election

[
20
]
,
majority computation

[
1
]
, and the evaluation of various functions and predicates
[
8
]
, it has been demonstrated that stable, fixed-state protocols operating in sublinear time are infeasible.
This limitation has motivated the study of population protocols in which the number of states is permitted to grow with
n
, enabling such problems to be solved efficiently, i.e., in polylogarithmic time.

Prior work on efficient population protocols has emphasized minimizing both stabilisation time and state usage for specific problems.
In contrast, this study adopts a broader approach, prioritizing universality, robustness, and probabilistic guarantees over full optimisation. We introduce a novel computing paradigm that incorporates population weights (or simply weights), a robust clocking mechanism, and efficient anomaly detection paired with a switching mechanism to ensure slow but consistently correct solutions.
This new paradigm supports the universal design of efficient multistage protocols, which
are stable (always correct), silent (agents don’t alter their states after stabilisation), and ensure stabilisation in time
O(\text{polylog }n)
with high probability.

In the remainder of this section, we present an overview of both established and emerging concepts that underpin the new paradigm and its associated algorithmic solutions. We also situate our contributions within the broader research context.

Further Context

In the population protocol model, integer values are represented in unary format, where each value corresponds to the cardinality of a subpopulation of agents in a designated state.
This representation makes addition straightforward, i.e., fast and stable.
While it is well understood that fixed-state Monte Carlo population protocols can simulate a limited variant of
register machines
(recognising problems in LOGSPACE)
[
6
]
,
the efficient and stable computation of basic operations such as subtraction (multiplication and division) remains significantly more challenging.
The primary obstacle in subtraction lies in the need for effective pairing of agents from two subpopulations, which may require linear time when their cardinalities are comparable or equal.
A similar challenge arises in majority protocols, where it is addressed by amplifying the difference (bias) between the cardinalities to a magnitude that population protocols can process efficiently
[
6
]
.
In the same paper, the authors propose a method for efficient Monte Carlo type subtraction by iteratively generating powers of two, combined with simple addition and more sophisticated majority protocols.
This approach serves as a prototype for our
system of weights
, which
introduces an implicit binary representation of integer values, supported by corresponding operations.

The Parity Problem

Recall that in the
parity problem
, agents are tasked with computing the parity of a given sub-population size. In this work, we present a stable population protocol that solves the parity computation problem in polylogarithmic time.
A common strategy for designing such protocols involves running a fast Monte Carlo algorithm that solves the problem with high probability, complemented by a slower, stable backup when needed. This approach has proven effective for problems such as majority computation and leader election, as discussed in the introduction. We adopt the same methodology in our construction. Notably, our protocol ensures both stabilisation and silence.

We begin (Section
2.1
) by presenting a simple protocol that computes the parity of a given subpopulation
X
of agents, using a fixed number of states. It always stabilises in time
O(n\log n)
, both in expectation and with high probability. To our knowledge, it represents the current state-of-the-art for stable parity computation and serves as the backup in our overall protocol design.

3
Efficient Protocols with Anomaly Detection

In this section, we develop methods for constructing efficient population protocols that silently stabilise with certainty.
Specifically, assuming the existence of two protocols:
a slow, state-efficient protocol that stabilises silently in polynomial time with probability 1, and
a fast Monte Carlo type protocol possibly composed of subprotocols, we show how to integrate these two to obtain a protocol that is both efficient in time and guaranteed to stabilise silently.

Monte Carlo protocols typically aim to compute the desired solution with high probability, but the correctness of the solution remains uncertain after execution. Existing mechanisms for detecting fast protocol failures and transitioning to a consistently stabilising protocol have been developed on an ad hoc basis.
In contrast, we propose a novel universal anomaly-detection mechanism that effectively integrates multiple Monte Carlo subprotocols with a reliable, though slower, always-correct solution. Specifically, this mechanism identifies efficiently scenarios necessitating a shift to a slower, always-stabilising protocol and facilitates the creation of complex population protocols by sequentially combining simpler ones.
Additionally, the constructed protocols are silent.

Our efficient (polylogarithmic time and space) protocols converge to the correct solution with high probability or signal potential errors via an efficient anomaly-detection mechanism.
Such signal is treated as a failure of the fast protocol, even if it does not always indicate an actual error. Our universal strategy is to first run a fast Monte Carlo protocol that either stabilises with high probability or, upon detecting an anomaly, switches to a slower, always-stabilising fallback.

3.1
Clock Protocol

Central to our design is the clock protocol, which acts as a clocking device and a command counter, tracking algorithmic progress and synchronizing all agents to the same point in time. In rare cases of desynchronisation, it signals an anomaly.
Each main (e.g., parity) protocol is built by sequentially composing subprotocols, each spanning one or more rounds.
The clock counts down
R=\text{poly}\log(n)
rounds of the main protocol, each taking
O(\log n)
time. To measure a round’s duration, a leader-maintained interaction counter tracks
c\log n
interactions, triggering the transition to the next round.

Components of the clock protocol

•

Each agent maintains a round number
r
.

•

Leaders maintain an auxiliary counter
s
to count interactions within a round. After
c\cdot LOG
interactions, they increment
r

r=R
.

•

If two agents with round numbers
r

r-1

r-1
adopts
r
.

•

If two agents’ round numbers differ by more than 1, a clock anomaly is signaled.

.

For any constant

C\log n

r
whp.

Proof.

Each agent experiences on average
2C\log n

whp, for sufficiently large

∎

In the clock protocol, we choose

Lemma 4

The expected time for the clock to terminate after
R

O(R\log n)

Proof.

Let us consider a modified clock protocol in which anomaly detection is replaced by a new rule.
This rule specifies that when two agents interact in rounds

r^{\prime}<r

O(R\log n)

∎

In this work, we focus on fast population protocols, specifically Monte Carlo protocols with anomaly detection (MC+ protocols). These protocols either silently stabilise on the correct computational output whp or trigger an anomaly detection signal. To maintain order in protocol analysis, we distinguish two types of anomalies:

•

, that arises when agents whose rounds differ by more than 1 interact,

•

, which involves the detection of irregularities specific to the protocol itself.

To capture the desired properties, we introduce the definition of MC+ protocols.

MC+ protocol

•

Is active for a specified number of rounds, meaning its operations are limited to
R
consecutive rounds.
Note that if there are no anomalies, this means reaching a silent configuration.

•

There is a negligible probability that a protocol anomaly signal will be triggered during the protocol’s execution or within an expected time of
O(n^{a})
following its completion. Such an anomaly signal would stem from a state inconsistency, potentially indicating erroneous behavior in the protocol.

•

The absence of such a signal, together with the lack of clock anomaly, indicates a successful execution of the task.

Theorem 2
.

If a fast MC+ protocol and a polynomial-time stable protocol exist for the same problem, they can be combined to yield a protocol that is both fast (in expected running time) and stable (with high probability). Moreover, if the polynomial-time stable protocol is silent, the resulting combined protocol is also silent.

Proof.

Assume that the fast protocol completes its computations in

If not successful, it reports a protocol anomaly in at most

average time with negligible probability less than

•

The leader in each of the MC+ protocols needs a fixed number of states except for the clock component, which requires
O(\log^{3}n)
states.
Non-leaders need
O(\log^{2}n)
states in the clock component. Those that belong to the weights use
O(\log n)
states in the global component to determine the weight number and a constant number of states in the local component. Non-leaders that are not in the weights have a constant number of states in the global component and a logarithmic number in the local component.

By Theorem
2
, the fast MC+ parity protocol can be combined with a slow parity protocol, resulting in an efficient, stable parity protocol.
This protocol is silent because the slow parity protocol is silent.
The total number of rounds of the MC+ parity protocol is
R=O(\log^{2}n)
.
By Lemma
2
, the slow parity protocol requires polynomial time. By Theorem
2
, the expected running time of the fast stable parity protocol is
O(\log^{3}n)
.
The total number of states in the combined parity protocol is equal to the sum of their numbers of states, i.e.,
O(\log^{3}n)
.

∎

4
The Congruence problem

In this section, we show how our solution to the parity problem naturally extends to deciding congruences modulo any integer
m
.
The main challenge is constructing a polynomial-time silent protocol for the general congruence problem. We chose not to adapt the succinct congruence protocol from Czerner et al.
[
17
]
, as it remains unclear whether this protocol is silent.
Our fast congruence protocol follows the same structure as in the parity case.

4.1
Slow, Stable and Silent Congruence Protocol

In this subsection, we address the
m
-congruence problem
, which involves determining whether the size of a distinguished subpopulation
X

m>1

We now outline a slow but always stabilising protocol for the congruence problem modulo any integer
m\geq 2
. We use the fact that using the binary representation of
m
, one can define a sequence
m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
such that
m_{0}=1,m_{k}=m
and
k=O(\log m)
. This sequence satisfies for each
i

m_{i+1}=2m_{i}
or
m_{i+1}=m_{i}+1
and will be the sequence of possible agent masses. At the beginning of the protocol, all agents from the set

have mass
m_{0}=1
, and all other agents have mass
0

If
m_{i+1}=2m_{i}
and

m_{i}
leads to one of them acquiring mass
m_{i+1}
and the other one acquiring mass
0

, such an interaction reduces the mass of both agents to zero. If, in turn,
m_{i+1}=m_{i}+1

m_{i}
and
1
leads to a result analogous to the one described before. A limitation of this approach is that, during execution, no agents with mass
1
may be available when needed for the second interaction type. Therefore, we introduce another type of interaction, in which
m_{i+1}=m_{i}+1
and an agent with mass
m_{i}
interacts with an agent with mass
m_{j}\neq 1,j\leq i
. In this case, the first agent acquires mass
m_{i+1}
, and the second mass
m_{j}-1
.
If
m_{j}-1=m_{j-1}
, this interaction does not produce a mass outside the allowed set of agent masses. However, if
m_{j}=2m_{j-1}
, we must include mass
m_{j}-1
in the allowed set, preserving a total of
O(\log m)
distinct masses. To handle these extra masses, we introduce a rule: when an agent with mass
m_{j}-1
interacts with a mass
0

m_{j-1}
and
m_{j-1}-1

The protocol described above reduces the total mass of agents from
|X|
to mass

\mu\equiv_{m}|X|
. To inform all mass
0

0

0
receive a special state

t

Conclusion

In this work, we propose a new computing paradigm for population protocols, enabling the design of the first efficient, stable, and silent protocol for the parity and congruence problem.
This new computing paradigm integrates several tools, including population weights, clocking mechanism, and efficient anomaly detection switching control to slow but always correct solutions. The new paradigm facilitates universal design of efficient multistage stable population protocols, enabling, e.g., efficient computation of more general congruencies.
The power of the system of weights lies in the implicit conversion between unary and binary representations.
In fact, due to this conversion, our parity protocol could also be amended to compute and report in a binary format (where, e.g., the consecutive bits of any integer value are stored in dedicated
O(\log n)
agents) any subpopulation or even the whole population size.

This raises a natural question: is binary representation of integers, both for input and output, better suited for designing efficient and stable population protocols?
Although an efficient, stable multiplication protocol using binary representation is conceivable, achieving the same with unary representation is far more challenging, yet feasible (details omitted in this version). The primary obstacle is constructing a slow but stable protocol. Although recent years have seen remarkable progress in fast population protocols, advances in slow (polynomial time) yet stable protocols have been mainly limited to succinct protocols
[
17
]
. Our work indicates that further research is needed in this direction.
