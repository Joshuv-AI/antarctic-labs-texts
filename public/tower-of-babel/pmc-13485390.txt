Diagnosis-Specific Clinical and Longitudinal Predictors of Suicide Attempts in Early-Onset Mood Disorders
Authors: Yaerim Nam, Jaegwon Jeong, Sojeong Kim, Soohyun Park, Ji Won Yeom, Chul-Hyun Cho, Heon-Jeong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective To identify diagnosis-specific clinical and longitudinal predictors of suicide attempts in early-onset mood disorders, comparing major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar I disorder (BD-I), and bipolar II disorder (BD-II) within a single cohort to inform targeted risk stratification. Methods We analyzed 430 patients aged under 35 years (a proxy for early-onset) with MDD, BD-I, or BD-II from a prospective South Korean cohort. Diagnoses were based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) and the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview. Lifetime and follow-up suicide attempts and suicidal ideation (Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation, SSI-beck) were assessed using structured interviews and standardized scales. Logistic regression examined predictors of lifetime suicide attempts, and Cox proportional hazards models evaluated baseline predictors of time to first suicide attempt over approximately 60 weeks of follow-up. Results At baseline, 40.5% of patients reported a lifetime suicide attempt, and 62 patients attempted suicide during follow-up. In the total patients, earlier age at onset, longer untreated duration, alcohol use problems, psychiatric family history, and higher SSI-beck scores were associated with lifetime suicide attempts. Diagnosis-specific analyses showed distinct patterns. Prospectively, depressive severity and SSI-beck scores predicted first suicide attempts, with alcohol use and impulsivity additionally important in MDD. Conclusion Suicidal ideation and depressive severity are key transdiagnostic predictors of suicide attempts in early-onset mood disorders, while alcohol use, impulsivity, psychiatric family history, and illness burden mark diagnosis-specific risk profiles guiding tailored monitoring and interventions.Keywords: Mood disorders, Suicidal ideation, Suicide attempt, Major depressive disorder, Bipolar disorder, Cox proportional hazards Model

INTRODUCTION

Suicide is a major public health concern worldwide, accounting for an estimated 727,000 deaths annually, and South Korea continues to report the highest suicide rate among OECD countries. Suicide fatalities are strongly associated with psychiatric illness (over 90%), with mood disorders—including major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD)—representing the largest diagnostic group [1,2]. While MDD, BD-I, and BD-II all confer elevated risk of suicide attempts, their clinical features and underlying risk profiles diverge [3,4]. Individuals who develop mood disorders at a younger age—typically before early to mid-adulthood—tend to show a more severe course, including a higher likelihood of suicidal behavior in both MDD and BD [5,6].

Previous research in MDD and BD has shown that younger age, early-onset illness, comorbid substance use, prior suicide attempts, and recent psychiatric hospitalization or discharge are key predictors of suicidal behavior [1]. However, most of these studies have been cross-sectional in design, limiting insight into longitudinal trajectories of suicide risk and diagnosis-specific predictors of suicide attempts. Furthermore, while the impact of environmental factors such as childhood abuse and trauma on suicide risk is well established, there remains a critical need to focus on clinical predictors, such as symptom severity, treatment latency, and specific comorbidities, that are directly observable and modifiable in routine psychiatric practice [7].

Importantly, it remains unclear whether these clinically observable predictors operate similarly across MDD, BD-I, and BD-II, or whether some factors function as transdiagnostic risk markers while others modify risk in a diagnosis-specific manner. Because much of the existing evidence comes from studies conducted in separate samples or with pooled bipolar subtypes, a head-to-head comparison within a single prospective cohort using harmonized assessments can clarify whether apparent between-diagnosis differences reflect true diagnostic heterogeneity rather than methodological variation across studies.

Nevertheless, most prior studies have either examined these diagnoses separately or grouped bipolar subtypes together, and few have directly compared MDD, BD-I, and BD-II within a single prospective cohort to identify diagnosis-specific risk factors [8-10]. Longitudinal comparisons of clinical predictors across these three diagnostic groups are even more limited. A clearer understanding of these distinct clinical pathways is essential for developing targeted and diagnosis-informed prevention strategies. Such evidence can move beyond pooled estimates by clarifying which clinical domains warrant uniform vigilance across diagnoses versus which domains may be prioritized differently by diagnosis when planning monitoring and early intervention in early-onset mood disorders.

Against this background, the present study sought to investigate and compare longitudinal clinical predictors of suicide attempts across MDD, BD-I, and BD-II within a young, early-onset cohort drawn from the same prospective study. By differentiating clinical risk factors both within and between diagnostic groups, we aimed to provide practical implications for risk stratification and early intervention in everyday clinical settings.

METHODS

Participants

Data were derived from the Mood Disorder Cohort Research Consortium, a multicenter prospective study conducted at eight hospitals in South Korea (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03088657) [11]. We included participants who were under 35 years of age, met Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria for MDD, BD-I, or BD-II [12], and had at least one follow-up visit. We used age <35 years at enrollment as a pragmatic proxy for early-onset; this operational definition differs from conventional onset-based definitions (e.g., age at first episode), and we use the term “early-onset” in this sense throughout. In total, 430 patients met these criteria. During follow-up, 13 patients experienced diagnostic conversion within the mood disorder spectrum (MDD → BD-I, n=4; MDD → BD-II, n=4; BD-II → BD-I, n=5). For all analyses, patients were classified according to their final DSM-5 diagnosis, resulting in 146 patients with MDD, 130 with BD-I, and 154 with BD-II. Baseline predictors were measured at enrollment, before any diagnostic conversion during follow-up. Thus, subgroup analyses by final diagnosis should be interpreted with the understanding that baseline features in converters may represent early manifestations of the eventual diagnosis. Diagnoses were established by board-certified psychiatrists and confirmed using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview [13]. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB No. 2015AN0239) and the ethics committees of all participating hospitals and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Basic demographic variables were collected through structured clinical interviews, along with clinical history such as age at the first mood episode, age at first psychiatric consultation, number of past psychiatric hospitalizations, lifetime alcohol use problems, and family history of psychiatric disorder. Family history of psychiatric disorder was assessed at baseline by clinician interview based on self-report of any psychiatric disorder in first-degree relatives (yes/no). Lifetime alcohol use problems were assessed similarly as any history of problematic alcohol use (yes/no). Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics across diagnostic groups are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Lifetime and prospective suicide attempts were assessed via structured interviews at baseline and follow-up visits.

Suicidal ideation and hopelessness were measured using the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI-beck) [14,15] and Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) [16,17], respectively. Depressive symptoms were assessed with Korean version of the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (K-MADRS) [18,19]. Mood disorder vulnerability was evaluated using the Korean version of the Mood Disorder Questionnaire (K-MDQ) [20,21], and past hypomanic symptoms were assessed with the Hypomania Checklist-32 (HCL-32) [22]. Impulsivity was measured using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS) [23,24]. Lifestyle irregularity was evaluated using the Biological Rhythms Interview of Assessment in Neuropsychiatry (BRIAN) [25,26].

Statistical analysis

Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To examine baseline associations between each predictor and lifetime suicide attempts, we fitted binary logistic regression models with lifetime suicide attempt status as the dependent variable. In the primary models, each clinical variable of interest was entered separately while adjusting for sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, years of education, employment status, and marital status). This analytic strategy was chosen to mitigate multicollinearity among clinical symptom measures (e.g., depressive severity, suicidal ideation, hopelessness, impulsivity), which can obscure the distinct contribution of individual predictors when entered simultaneously. Conceptually, MADRS captures overall depressive symptom burden, BHS reflects a trait-like cognitive style of pessimism/hopelessness, and SSI-beck indexes the current intensity and specificity of suicidal thinking (e.g., desire, intent, and planning). Although these domains are interrelated, they may contribute differently to suicidal behavior: depressive burden may increase vulnerability, hopelessness may shape negative future expectations, and suicidal ideation may more directly reflect proximal risk for acting on self-destructive thoughts. Accordingly, we examined each clinical domain in separate covariate-adjusted models and then evaluated their relative independence in fully adjusted models. Covariate-adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values from these single-predictor models are reported for the total cohort in Table 1 and for diagnosis-specific analyses in Table 2.

In addition to these covariate-adjusted single-predictor models, we conducted full multivariable logistic regression models that included all sociodemographic and clinical variables simultaneously as an exploratory analysis of robustness. For longitudinal analyses, Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate the time to the first suicide attempt over a mean follow-up period of approximately 60 weeks [27]. Analyses focused on the first suicide attempt to characterize incident risk and support clinically interpretable comparisons across diagnostic groups. Recurrent suicide attempts were observed in 14 participants during follow-up (MDD, n=1; BD-I, n=6; BD-II, n=7). Given the very limited number of recurrent events within each diagnostic group relative to the planned subgroup analyses and number of covariates, we did not perform diagnosis-specific recurrent-event survival models, in order to preserve model stability and interpretability. The time origin was the baseline assessment. For participants who attempted suicide during follow-up, the event time was defined as the date of the first reported attempt; participants without a suicide attempt were right-censored at the date of their last clinical visit. The proportional hazards assumption was examined for each predictor using statistical tests and graphical diagnostics based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals, and no meaningful violations of this assumption were observed; detailed diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary Tables 1-12. Multivariable Cox models were constructed for the total cohort and separately for each diagnostic group (MDD, BD-I, and BD-II), including sociodemographic variables and clinical risk factors previously implicated in suicidal behavior. Key results from the Cox models for the total cohort and the diagnosis-specific analyses are summarized in Tables 3 and 4, respectively, and full sets of hazard ratios (HRs) for the diagnosis-specific Cox models are provided in Supplementary Tables 10-12. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Results are reported as ORs or HRs with 95% CIs, and statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p<0.05.

RESULTS

Of the 430 participants with early-onset mood disorders, 174 (40.5%) reported at least one lifetime suicide attempt. Baseline comparisons showed that individuals with a history of suicide attempts tended to have an earlier age at first mood episode, a longer duration of untreated illness, and higher rates of alcohol use problems and family history of psychiatric disorder than those without attempts. They also exhibited more severe depressive symptoms, greater hopelessness, higher impulsivity, and stronger suicidal ideation (Table 1). Baseline sociodemographic characteristics were generally comparable across diagnostic groups, with the exception of years of education, which was slightly lower in MDD than in BD-I and BD-II. Detailed sociodemographic and clinical descriptive statistics by diagnosis are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

In logistic regression models adjusted for sociodemographic factors, several clinical variables were associated with lifetime suicide attempts in the total cohort. Earlier onset, longer untreated duration, alcohol use problems, family history of psychiatric disorder, and higher suicidal ideation scores emerged as significant predictors (Table 1). When a full model including all sociodemographic and clinical variables was examined, suicidal ideation (SSI-beck) remained a robust independent correlate of lifetime suicide attempts, even after accounting for depressive severity, hopelessness, and impulsivity. Alcohol use problems and family history showed similar directions of association across analyses, although subgroup estimates—particularly in BD-I—were sometimes imprecise.

Diagnostic subgroup analyses revealed distinct risk profiles (Table 2). For transparency, all predictors were tested in the diagnosis-specific models; only statistically significant predictors are displayed in Table 2 for readability, and full model results (including non-significant estimates) are provided in Supplementary Tables 3-6. In MDD, lifetime suicide attempts were most strongly related to longer untreated illness, history of psychiatric hospitalization, alcohol use problems, and higher scores on K-MDQ, BHS, and SSI-beck. In BD-I, the direction of associations suggested higher odds of lifetime attempts in relation to earlier psychiatric contact, family history of psychiatric disorder, higher HCL-32 scores, and suicidal ideation; however, the estimate for family history was imprecise, reflecting sparse events. In BD-II, prior psychiatric hospitalization and higher suicidal ideation were the principal cross-sectional predictors. More detailed baseline comparisons between patients with and without a lifetime history of suicide attempts within each diagnostic group are provided in Supplementary Tables 7-9.

During follow-up, 62 participants made at least one suicide attempt. Recurrent suicide attempts were observed in 14 participants (MDD, n=1; BD-I, n=6; BD-II, n=7). In Cox proportional hazards models for the total cohort, baseline depressive severity (MADRS) and suicidal ideation (SSI-beck) predicted shorter time to a first suicide attempt (Table 3). Full Cox model results for MDD, BD-I, and BD-II, including all covariates, are presented in Supplementary Tables 10-12.

Again, diagnosis-specific Cox models highlighted different longitudinal patterns (Table 4); as in Table 2, only statistically significant predictors are displayed for readability, with full model results provided in Supplementary Tables 10-12. In MDD, future suicide attempts were associated with alcohol use problems, higher depressive symptoms, impulsivity (BIS), and suicidal ideation at baseline, while hopelessness showed a borderline inverse association that should be interpreted cautiously. In BD-I, younger age, earlier illness onset, and suicidal ideation were associated with time to first attempt, whereas several subgroup estimates—most notably for family history—were extremely imprecise with wide CIs, consistent with sparse-event instability; therefore, interpretation should emphasize directionality rather than effect-size magnitude. In addition, some categorical predictors in the diagnosis-specific full models yielded non-estimable (NE) coefficients, consistent with (quasi-)complete separation due to sparse cell counts; these estimates are reported for completeness and should not be over-interpreted. In BD-II, depressive severity and suicidal ideation were the main longitudinal predictors.

Taken together, suicidal ideation and depressive severity showed consistent associations with suicide attempts across diagnoses. Other factors (e.g., alcohol use problems, family history, hospitalization history, and trait measures) showed more diagnosis-dependent patterns, with some BD-I estimates remaining imprecise due to sparse events.

DISCUSSION

This prospective cohort study examined both cross-sectional and longitudinal risk factors for suicide attempts in early-onset mood disorders across three diagnostic groups (MDD, BD-I, and BD-II). Consistent with prior research, suicidal ideation and depressive symptom severity emerged as robust predictors of suicide attempts across diagnoses [28,29]. Importantly, suicidal ideation remained a powerful predictor even after controlling for related constructs such as hopelessness, impulsivity, and depressive severity in fully adjusted models, highlighting its central role in suicide risk assessment in young mood disorder patients.

Our findings support the notion that although many symptom-based clinical measures are closely interrelated, suicidal ideation retains independent predictive value [29,30]. The observation that SSI-beck remained significant in the full logistic regression model—despite adjustment for overlapping clinical factors—suggests that the intensity of suicidal thinking reflects a distinct risk dimension not fully captured by overall depressive burden or emotional dysregulation. Clinically, this underscores the need to treat suicidal ideation not merely as a secondary symptom of depression but as a primary, dynamic risk marker that warrants direct and repeated assessment.

Diagnosis-specific patterns further refine this picture. In MDD, suicide attempts were closely linked to comorbid alcohol use problems, prior psychiatric hospitalization, and higher impulsivity prospectively, in addition to depressive severity and suicidal ideation. This is in line with previous work in unipolar depression indicating that behavioral and psychosocial factors—particularly comorbid substance use—play an important role in the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts [1,30,31]. The persistent impact of alcohol use in both diagnosis-specific and fully adjusted models suggests that it functions not only as a marker of illness severity, but also as a distinct behavioral driver that may lower inhibition and facilitate acting on suicidal thoughts.

In BD-I, psychiatric family history showed directionally positive associations with suicide attempts in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. This direction is broadly consistent with prior work indicating that familial loading, including family history related to suicide and other psychiatric conditions, is clinically relevant to suicidal behavior in bipolar disorder [32,33]. However, in the present sample the corresponding OR and HR estimates were highly imprecise with very wide CIs, indicating sparse-event instability; therefore, these results should be interpreted as supporting the direction of association rather than providing a precise estimate of effect size. Taken together, our findings are compatible with the existing consensus that familial vulnerability may be clinically relevant in BD-I, but the prognostic strength of this association requires replication in larger cohorts with more events. The association with hypomanic traits may likewise be compatible with trait-level vulnerability implicated in prior work, although this interpretation should also be considered provisional given limited precision in subgroup estimates.

By contrast, the risk profile in BD-II was more restricted in our sample, with suicide attempts primarily associated with depressive severity and suicidal ideation, with prior hospitalization emerging as a cross-sectional indicator of illness burden. We did not observe significant associations with impulsivity or alcohol use in BD-II. Given the modest number of events in this subgroup and the substantial literature identifying comorbid substance use disorders as important risk factors for suicidal behavior in bipolar disorder, this lack of association should be interpreted with caution and is likely to reflect limited statistical power rather than evidence of a true absence of risk [34,35]. The contribution of hospitalization history in BD-II is nonetheless consistent with the interpretation that suicidality in this group may be tied to illness chronicity, treatment resistance, and episodes severe enough to require inpatient care [1,4].

Taken together, these diagnosis-specific patterns indicate that MDD, BD-I, and BD-II share common transdiagnostic risk factors—particularly suicidal ideation and depressive severity—yet differ in the relative salience of behavioral, familial/trait-related, and illness-burden indicators. Broadly, MDD showed more consistent signals for behavioral factors (e.g., alcohol use problems, impulsivity), whereas BD-II findings were more consistently aligned with depressive burden and hospitalization history. In BD-I, familial/trait-related factors showed directionally positive associations that are consistent with prior literature, but subgroup estimates were less precise and should be viewed as provisional. Recognizing these distinct clinical pathways may help clinicians move beyond a uniform approach to suicide risk and tailor assessments according to diagnostic context.

From a longitudinal perspective, suicidal ideation again emerged as the most consistent and potent predictor of future suicide attempts across all diagnostic groups, underscoring the importance of repeated and ongoing assessment of suicidal thoughts rather than reliance on a single baseline evaluation [28-30]. In this context, focusing on time to the first attempt helps clarify when incident suicidal behavior is most likely to occur during follow-up. Clinically, baseline markers associated with a shorter time to first attempt may support earlier safety planning and closer monitoring in the weeks to months after assessment, while recurrent suicidal behavior remains an important topic for future studies. Accordingly, our findings are most informative for identifying patients at risk for an initial near-term attempt and for guiding early monitoring after assessment or care transitions; they should not be interpreted as predictors of recurrent suicidal behavior, which may involve partially distinct mechanisms. Depressive severity also contributed to prospective risk, supporting the view that effective mood stabilization and sustained control of depressive symptoms are central components of suicide prevention in mood disorders. At the same time, the precision of some HR estimates—particularly in BD-I—was clearly influenced by the relatively small number of incident attempts, and these diagnosis-specific findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and replicated in larger cohorts.

Previous cohort studies in major affective disorders have provided important evidence on risk and protective factors for suicidal behavior across MDD and BD. Our study extends this literature by focusing specifically on early-onset mood disorders and by directly comparing MDD, BD-I, and BD-II within a single prospective cohort [36]. By combining standardized symptom scales with a structured measure of suicidal ideation (SSI-beck), we were able to delineate both shared and diagnosis-specific clinical predictors of suicide attempts, and to show that suicidal ideation remains a central, transdiagnostic marker even after accounting for overlapping symptom dimensions. These results offer clinically applicable cues for diagnosis-informed risk stratification in young patients with mood disorders.

These findings carry several practical clinical implications. First, routine and structured assessment of suicidal ideation should be integrated into standard care for young patients with mood disorders, for example by reassessing suicidal ideation at every visit during periods of clinical change (e.g., symptom worsening, medication changes, sleep disruption, or increased substance use) and at regular intervals even when symptoms appear stable. In inpatient settings, repeated assessment of suicidal ideation should be paired with documentation of changes in intent, planning, and access to means as clinical status fluctuates, and incorporated into discharge planning.

Second, systematic screening for alcohol use problems—particularly in MDD—may help identify patients at elevated risk in whom targeted psychoeducation, motivational interviewing, or referral for addiction-focused interventions could mitigate behavioral drivers of suicide attempts.

Third, eliciting and documenting family history of psychiatric disorder (limited to first-degree relatives in this study) is an important component of assessment across mood disorders. In BD-I, prior literature supports the clinical relevance of familial/genetic vulnerability, and our results were directionally consistent with this view, although effect estimates were imprecise; thus, family history should be considered a contextual risk feature rather than a standalone predictor. Clinically, incorporating family history into the initial risk formulation may help guide the threshold for heightened vigilance during high-risk periods, such as rapid symptom shifts, treatment transitions, or early follow-up after discharge.

Fourth, a history of psychiatric hospitalization, reflecting prior episode severity and illness burden, showed cross-sectional associations with lifetime suicide attempts particularly in MDD and BD-II. In practice, hospitalization history can be incorporated as contextual information when planning follow-up intensity, especially around periods of rapid symptom worsening or treatment transitions. For example, following inpatient care or emergency presentations for suicidality, earlier post-discharge contact and a shorter interval to the first outpatient follow-up visit may be considered, with step-down to longer intervals as risk stabilizes.

At a systems level, these results support the development of diagnosis-informed risk stratification approaches that combine transdiagnostic indicators (suicidal ideation, depressive severity) with diagnosis-relevant modifiers (e.g., alcohol use problems and impulsivity in MDD, contextual familial factors in BD-I, and hospitalization history as an illness-burden indicator in BD-II). In practice, this could translate into structured intake and follow-up templates that systematically capture suicidal ideation, key behavioral risk markers, family history, and indicators of illness burden, triggering stepped-up monitoring or safety planning when multiple risk domains are present. Incorporating frequent, structured assessment of suicidal ideation into routine clinical workflows—alongside careful evaluation of behavioral risk markers and contextual familial vulnerability—may improve early detection of high-risk individuals in early-onset mood disorders and support more personalized prevention strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations deserve mention. First, despite the prospective design, the mean follow-up period of about 60 weeks may have been insufficient to capture late-onset suicide attempts, so our findings mainly reflect short- to medium-term risk. Second, although the cohort included three diagnostic groups, the number of suicide attempts within each subgroup—particularly during follow-up—was relatively small, leading to wide CIs for some OR/HR estimates (e.g., in BDI) and increasing the likelihood of unstable estimates driven by sparse events. In some diagnosis-specific multivariable models, sparse cell counts also resulted in (quasi-)complete separation and NE coefficients for certain categorical predictors; therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously with emphasis on directionality rather than effect-size magnitude. Third, our survival analyses modelled time to the first suicide attempt only; subsequent attempts were not incorporated into the Cox models. Recurrent suicide attempts were observed in 14 participants during follow-up (MDD, n=1; BD-I, n=6; BD-II, n=7), but the number of recurrent events within each diagnostic group was limited relative to the number of covariates and planned subgroup analyses. Therefore, the present findings do not address risk factors for recurrent suicidal behavior. Fourth, lifetime and follow-up suicide attempts were assessed by clinician interview based on patient report, which may have been affected by recall bias or underreporting, and alcohol use problems and family history were assessed by patient report, and several symptom/trait domains (e.g., impulsivity) relied on self-report scales rather than objective indices. We also did not incorporate childhood trauma or social support into the present analyses, which limits the contextual interpretation of our findings. Finally, because the study was conducted in a naturalistic treatment setting without control over specific pharmacological or psychosocial interventions, treatment variability may have influenced symptom trajectories and suicide risk, and the findings may not fully generalize beyond early-onset patients treated in tertiary care settings.

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study identified both shared and diagnosis-specific predictors of suicide attempts in early-onset mood disorders. Across MDD, BD-I, and BD-II, suicidal ideation and depressive symptom severity were consistent transdiagnostic predictors of lifetime and prospective suicide attempts, underscoring the need for routine, structured, and repeated assessment of suicidal thoughts in young mood disorder patients. At the same time, diagnosis-specific patterns suggested clinically actionable modifiers: in MDD, alcohol use problems and impulsivity point to the importance of systematic screening and targeted management of disinhibitory behaviors alongside mood treatment; in BD-I, directionally positive but imprecise associations with familial psychiatric loading and hypomanic traits suggest that careful enquiry about family history and temperament may be clinically informative, while requiring replication in larger samples; and in BD-II, the links with depressive burden and hospitalization history highlight the need for sustained follow-up during depressive worsening and enhanced support around severe episodes and care transitions. Earlier age at onset and delayed psychiatric engagement also emerged as correlates of suicide attempts in some analyses, emphasizing the value of early recognition and timely intervention. Overall, these results support diagnosis-informed, individualized risk assessment that can be translated into more frequent monitoring during symptom change and earlier post-discharge follow-up when multiple risk domains co-occur.
