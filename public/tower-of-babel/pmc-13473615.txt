Visible-light-driven photocatalytic reduction of Cr(VI) over EDTA–TiO2 through surface complexation and ligand-to-metal charge transfer
Authors: Mst. Farhana Afrin, Monir Uzzaman, Mai Furukawa, Ikki Tateishi, Hideyuki Katsumata, Satoshi Kaneco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The energy band gap of commercial TiO2 (P25, ~ 3.04 eV) is slightly narrower than that of pure TiO2 (~ 3.2 eV), yet remains inactive under visible light. In contrast, ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) introduced by surface complexation with organic ligands can significantly enhance light absorption and effectively narrow the band gap. Here, electron-rich ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) serves as an electron donor and coordinates with TiO2 via carboxylate linkers. As a result, the EDTA–TiO2 complex exhibits efficient visible-light-driven photoreduction of toxic Cr(VI) to non-toxic Cr(III). Individually, the optimal loading of EDTA was 200 ppm, which further decreased to 100 ppm upon the introduction of methanol (MeOH, 20 vol%) as a hole scavenger and/or electron donor. Upon visible light illumination, electrons from the HOMO of EDTA move towards the conduction band (CB) of TiO2 through the LMCT process, enabling visible-light absorption and driving the efficient reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(III). The characteristic FT-IR bands at 1410 and 1677 cm− 1 confirm EDTA−TiO2 complex formation, which is further supported by the calculated adsorption energy (Eads = −18.83 kcal/mol). This study provides mechanistic insight into the adsorption mode of EDTA through carboxylate coordination and elucidates the role of LMCT in enhancing light-harvesting efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49772-w.

Introduction

The escalating global crisis environmental deterioration have intensified the development of semiconductor-based heterojunction photocatalysts as advanced functional materials1,2. These systems facilitate efficient charge separation and directional migration of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, thereby significantly enhancing solar energy conversion and redox efficiency3. This synergistic interfacial engineering enables superior performance in photocatalytic heavy metal reduction and organic pollutant degradation4. Nevertheless, challenges including interfacial recombination losses and limited visible-light absorption continue to constrain the overall efficiency and practical scalability of heterojunction systems.

Extensive research over the past decades has demonstrated the considerable promise of TiO2 nanoparticles in environmental remediation and solar energy applications5–7. It’s widely regarded as an effective photocatalyst because of its chemical stability, low toxicity, and strong redox activity toward both organic and inorganic contaminants in aqueous systems. Pure TiO2 predominantly absorbs UV light due to its wide band gap (~ 3.20 eV), rendering it ineffective in visible light absorption (400–700 nm). In a controlled mixture of anatase and rutile phases of TiO2 (Degussa P25), the band gap was slightly narrowed (3.04 eV) but remained inactive under visible light. To address the intrinsic ultraviolet limitation of TiO2, numerous modification strategies have been developed to enable visible-light-driven activity, among which dye sensitization8,9 and elemental doping are the most widely investigated10. Visible-light activity in TiO2 can further arise from interfacial charge-transfer complex formation with surface-bound species, even when both components are individually inactive in the visible spectrum11.

Photoexcitation with energy exceeding the TiO2 band gap generates electron-hole pairs that migrate to the catalyst surface, where they drive oxidation and reduction reactions. Excited states involving charge transfer are broadly categorized as metal-to-ligand charge transfer, ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT), intra-ligand charge transfer, and ligand-to-ligand charge transfer transitions12,13. Research on LMCT excited states has emerged, as select transition-metal complexes have been shown to possess chemical and photochemical robustness, excited state lifetimes, and the capacity to participate in productive photochemical transformations14–17. Recently, it has been recognized that LMCT excitation promotes electron density from coordinated ligands into metal-centered acceptor orbitals, generating oxidized ligand character and unlocking previously inaccessible photochemical reactivity18,19. LMCT transitions at organic-inorganic interfaces extend light absorption into the visible region and facilitate efficient photoinduced charge separation and migration20–22. Surface coordination of organic ligands (carboxylate-based ligands, catechol, salicylic acid, and hydroxy-containing ligands) with TiO2 triggers LMCT, effectively narrowing the band gap and enhancing visible-light photoactivity23–26.

Carboxylate-based multidentate ligands, exemplified by EDTA, bind to wide-band-gap semiconductors such as TiO2 through stable coordination interactions, providing effective interfacial anchoring that supports charge transfer activity. Surface anchoring occurs through deprotonation of EDTA carboxyl groups (–COOH), which coordinate with Lewis-acidic Ti4+ sites on the TiO2 surface to form a robust surface complex27,28. EDTA binds to TiO2 primarily through σ-type coordination bonds, wherein electron density from carboxylate oxygen lone pairs is donated to Ti4+ centers, while the ligand π system remains electronically decoupled from the oxide29,30. This carboxylate-anchoring model is fundamental in establishing a stable interfacial linkage that enables efficient LMCT under visible light irradiation. The LMCT transition occurs when photoexcited electrons are promoted from the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of EDTA, predominantly associated with carboxylate functionalities, into the TiO2 CB via vacant Ti4+ 3d orbitals14.

These photoexcited electrons are subsequently utilized to reduce highly toxic and carcinogenic environmental contaminant hexavalent chromium Cr(VI) into Cr(III)31–33. Cr(VI) is a hazardous heavy metal pollutant that poses severe risks to human health and ecosystems due to its carcinogenicity, persistence, and resistance to biodegradation34,35. It is primarily released through industrial activities such as leather tanning, electroplating, metal finishing, and pigment production. Due to its high solubility and mobility in aqueous environments, Cr(VI) readily contaminates soils, surface waters, and groundwater, leading to widespread environmental and public health concerns36–40. Visible-light excitation of the EDTA–TiO2 surface complex drives LMCT, generating CB electrons that selectively reduce toxic Cr(VI) to environmentally benign Cr(III) species11. Under strongly acidic conditions, Cr(VI) predominantly exists as dichromate (Cr2O72–), which possesses a high reduction potential and is thermodynamically favorable for reductive transformation41. EDTA further drives the reaction equilibrium forward by forming surface–coordinated chromium complexes and subsequently chelating Cr(III), thereby stabilizing the reduced Cr(III) species and suppressing re-oxidation42,43.

DFT calculations confirm that EDTA forms a stable and strongly bound complex with TiO2, driven by favorable binding energy/adsorption energy44. The decrease in the HOMO–LUMO gap and the localization of HOMO on EDTA and LUMO on TiO2 indicate efficient interfacial charge transfer under photoexcitation29. Notably, effective charge transfer absorption emerges despite the intrinsically wide band gap characteristic of TiO2.

Accordingly, advancing semiconductor-assisted photocatalysis requires strategies that promote charge carrier separation and expand TiO2 photoactivity into the visible light range45. This study identifies molecular design strategies that promote the formation of interfacial complexes exhibiting low-energy LMCT transitions capable of driving photochemical transformations. Collectively, these findings establish a new design principle for interfacial visible light absorbers that enable direct photoinduced charge separation for photoenergy conversion applications.

Experimental details

Synthesis of EDTA–TiO2 complex

In this study, commercial TiO2 nanoparticles (P25, Degussa; anatase/rutile = 75/25, average particle size ≈ 25 nm, specific surface area ≈ 50 m2 g− 1) were used. EDTA–2Na was generally added directly to the reaction system as a hole scavenger. However, for comparison and characterization purposes, an EDTA–TiO2 surface complex was prepared via a one-pot method. Briefly, 100 mg of TiO2 was dispersed in 10 mL of an aqueous EDTA–2Na (100 ppm) solution and stirred for 3 h at room temperature to ensure complete surface complexation (Fig. 1). The suspension was then heated at 100 °C to evaporate the solvent and the resulting carboxylate-anchored EDTA–TiO2 complex was obtained as a pale white powder after drying and gentle grinding.

Schematic presentation of the carboxylate-anchor model for the EDTA–TiO2 surface complex.

Photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction

A Pyrex reactor vessel with an internal volume of 55 mL was used for the photocatalytic reduction of Cr(VI) ions in aqueous medium under controlled conditions. A 20 mg TiO2 (P25) powder was dispersed in 30 mL of an aqueous solution initially containing 30 ppm Cr(VI). A varied amount of EDTA–2Na and MeOH was added as hole scavengers and uniformly dispersed in the suspension. To establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium, the mixture was stirred continuously in the dark for 30 min, then illuminated with visible light (450 nm). A UV cutoff filter (≥ 400 nm) was used throughout the irradiation to exclude ultraviolet wavelengths and allow only visible light to interact with the sample, thereby reducing unwanted photochemical reactions. During irradiation, 2 mL suspension was collected every 10-min interval, centrifuged for 5 min at 10,000 rpm, and analyzed using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (AS ONE Corp., ASV11D, Japan) with the 1,5–diphenyl carbazide (DPC) colorimetric method. Specifically, 300 µL of the Cr(VI) suspension, 500 µL of sulfuric acid (2 M), and 200 µL of DPC solution (10 g/L) were transferred into a 25 mL volumetric flask, diluted with distilled water, and left to stand for 10 min to complete complex formation. Finally, the absorbance was recorded at 540 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Characterization

X–ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded by Rigaku RINT Ultima–IV diffractometer, employing Cu–Kα radiation in a 2θ range of 10–80°. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR spectrometer, fitted with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory. The surface morphology was assessed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JEOL JEM–1400 Flash). Optical properties were recorded by UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) (JASCO V–750 UV–vis spectrophotometer). Electron-hole recombination properties were recorded by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Shimadzu RF-5300PC).

Electrochemical measurement

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) based Nyquist plots, Mott–Schottky (M-S) analysis, and transient photocurrent response (TPR) measurements were conducted using an ALS Electrochemical Analyzer (Model 614 F, BAS). A three-electrode system with a platinum wire as the counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and 0.2 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution as the electrolyte. The working electrode was prepared by dispersing 2.0 mg of the sample in a mixed solvent of 40 µL 2-propanol and 80 µL Nafion, followed by sonication and drop-casting onto a fluorine-doped tin oxide glass substrate. The potential of the Ag/AgCl reference electrode was taken as 0.196 V vs. NHE at 25 °C.

Computational calculation

Initially, the TiO2 cluster model [Ti21O40(OH)4] was constructed based on the X-ray crystallographic data. Subsequently, the carboxylate group of the EDTA ligand was anchored to two adjacent titanium atoms of the dominant TiO2 facet in a bidentate bridging mode. To maintain the proper geometry, the titanium and oxygen atoms (except the OH group) of the [Ti21O40(OH)4] cluster were frozen. Density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were employed using the B3LYP hybrid functionals with the 6–311 g(d, p) basis set46. Free energy, adsorption energy, frontier molecular orbital (FMO) features (HOMO: Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital, LUMO: Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital), and density of state (DOS) of pristine TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 were calculated using the Gaussian 09 software package47.

Result and discussion

Structural analysis (XRD and FT-IR)

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) are used to elucidate the crystalline structure and phase purity, surface functional groups and chemical bonding, respectively.

The XRD pattern of P25 TiO2 confirms its biphasic nature, consisting of anatase and rutile phases (Fig. 2a). Diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 25.28°, 36.95°, 37.80°, 38.58°, 48.05°, 53.89°, 55.06°, 62.69°, 68.76°, 70.31°, 74.03°, 75.03° and 76.02° correspond to the strong diffraction anatase phase (A), whereas the peaks at 27.45°, 36.09°, 39.19°, 41.23°, 44.05°, 54.32°, 56.62°, 62.74°, 64.04°, 65.48°, 69.79°, 72.41°, 74.41°, 76.51°, and 79.82° can be attributed to the rutile phase (R), respectively48,49. The XRD reflections are consistent with the characteristic planes of anatase and rutile TiO2 as referenced by standard XRD pattern of JCPDS No. 21-1272/76 confirming the presence of both phase in P2548,50. Similar but slightly lowered diffraction peaks were observed in EDTA–TiO2, indicating reduced crystallinity due to surface coordination of EDTA without altering the TiO2 crystal structure. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the broad absorption features centered at ~ 800 and 1630 cm− 1 correspond to Ti–O stretching vibrations and the H–O–H bending mode of adsorbed water molecules, respectively, observed in pure TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2. The appearance of vibrational bands at 1410 and 1677 cm− 1 solely in EDTA–TiO2 confirms successful formation of the EDTA–TiO2 complex, which is assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching modes of coordinated carboxylate groups, respectively. Additionally, the symmetric and asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations observed in the 2860–2945 cm− 1 region further verifies the presence of EDTA moieties on the TiO2 surface49. An enlarged FT-IR spectra has presented in Fig. S1. XPS survey spectra confirm the presence of C 1s along with Ti 2p and O 1s (Fig. S2) for EDTA–TiO2 complex.

SEM analysis

The surface morphology of pristine TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 was analyzed through scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM image of pristine TiO2 (Fig. 3a) reveals a highly agglomerated morphology composed of irregularly shaped nanoparticles, resulting in a compact and densely packed structure. Such severe agglomeration is commonly attributed to the high surface energy of TiO2 nanoparticles, which promotes particle–particle interaction and limits the accessibility of active surface sites. In contrast, EDTA–TiO2 (Fig. 3b) exhibits a comparatively looser and more porous agglomerated architecture, with improved particle dispersion and a roughened surface texture. This morphological evolution can be attributed to the surface coordination of EDTA molecules, which act as a chelating and capping agent during synthesis, suppressing excessive particle growth and aggregation. The presence of EDTA at the TiO2 surface introduces steric hindrance and interparticle spacing, leading to a more open structure. The reduced agglomeration and increased surface roughness of EDTA–TiO2 are expected to enhance surface area, reactant adsorption, and interfacial charge transfer, which are favorable for photocatalytic reactions.

DRS, Tauc plot, and PL analysis

Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), Tauc plot analysis, and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy are employed to assess the light-harvesting ability, optical energy bandgap, and charge-carrier recombination dynamics, respectively. The DRS spectra (Fig. 4a) reveal a noticeable red shift of the absorption edge for EDTA–TiO2 compared to pristine TiO2, indicating enhanced light-harvesting ability. This improvement arises from the surface coordination of EDTA molecules with TiO2 through the carboxylate group, forming a surface Ti–O–C bond (Fig. 1), which induces ligand-to-metal charge transfer transitions from EDTA to Ti4+ centres. Consequently, EDTA–TiO2 absorbs photons at longer wavelengths, leading to increased generation of photoexcited charge carriers relative to bare TiO2.

Tauc plots (Fig. 4b) reveal a reduced bandgap from 3.04 eV (TiO2) to 2.88 eV (EDTA–TiO2), confirming EDTA–induced electronic modulation51. The bandgap narrowing originates from surface-induced shallow energy states near the conduction band formed by LMCT interactions, and facilitates electronic excitation at lower photon energies. This lowers the excitation threshold and enhances photo-response efficiency under visible light irradiation.

Fig. 4(a) UV-vis DRS spectra, (b) Tauc plot, (c) PL spectra, and (d) band structure of pure TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 complex.

(a) UV-vis DRS spectra, (b) Tauc plot, (c) PL spectra, and (d) band structure of pure TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 complex.

PL spectral analysis was employed under 330 nm excitation energy (Fig. 4c). PL spectra exhibit markedly lower emission intensity for EDTA–TiO2, indicating suppressed electron–hole recombination. This effect can be attributed to the electron-donating and hole-scavenging nature of EDTA, which promotes rapid interfacial charge transfer and prolongs charge carrier lifetimes. The reduced PL intensity therefore confirms enhanced charge separation and decreased e−‒h⁺ pair recombination rate, directly contributing to the superior photocatalytic performance of EDTA–TiO2 52.

The flat band potentials (Ef) were measured from Mott-Schottky (M-S) plots. From the M-S intercept curves (Fig. S3a, b), the Ef of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 is −0.72 V and − 0.67 V (vs. Ag/AgCl, sat. KCl at pH = 6.8), respectively. Further, the values were converted to – 0.12 and – 0.07 V (vs. RHE) employing the Nernst equation (E (vs. RHE) = E (vs. Ag/AgCl) + 0.059 × pH + E0 (vs. Ag/AgCl)). The positive M-S slope confirms the n-type semi-conductivity, and their conduction band (CB) is located above the 0.2 V of Ef 53. Therefore, the estimated CB values of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 are – 0.32 and – 0.27 V, respectively. Further, the valence band (VB) edge position was calculated using the band edge position equation (Eg = EVB ‒ ECB). Thus, the calculated VB position of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 is + 2.72 and + 2.61 V, respectively (Fig. 5d). This favorable band alignment promotes visible-light activity while maintaining sufficient redox potential and improved charge separation, thereby enhancing the photocatalytic performance of EDTA–TiO2 compared to pure TiO2.

EIS and TPR analysis

Figure 5a, b compares the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and photoelectrochemical response of pristine TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2. EIS provides charge carrier dynamics, including electron-hole separation and recombination rates, which directly affect photocatalytic efficiency. The Nyquist plots exhibit depressed semicircles associated with non-ideal interfacial charge transfer in the photocatalytic system and were fitted using an equivalent circuit comprising solution resistance (Rs) in series with charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and a constant phase element (CPE). In Fig. 5a, EDTA–TiO2 displays a substantially smaller arc radius than TiO2, reflecting a decrease in charge-transfer resistance at the electrode–electrolyte interface54. This reduction suggests that surface coordination of EDTA alters the interfacial electronic environment, facilitating more efficient electron transport and mitigating charge recombination.

Additionally, the transient photocurrent response (TPR, Fig. 5b) measured under illumination indicates how well the photoinduced electrons and holes separate and move. Under periodic light irradiation, EDTA–TiO2 generates a stronger and consistently reproducible photocurrent compared with pristine TiO2, indicating improved separation and mobility of photogenerated charge carriers55. This enhanced photocurrent correlates with improved photocatalytic reduction of Cr(VI) through the ligand-to-metal charge transfer mechanism facilitated by the carboxy-anchor model with EDTA.

DFT analysis

Gibb’s free energy was calculated to predict the reaction spontaneity and thermodynamic stability of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 complex. The negative free energy value for each catalyst indicates the spontaneous and exothermic reaction possibility. The individual free energy of EDTA and TiO2 was − 1102.10 and − 21155.85 Hartree, which further significantly increased for the EDTA–TiO2 complex (−22257.98 Hartree), indicating the complex formation and a thermodynamically stable nature. The calculated adsorption energy of EDTA–TiO2 is −18.83 kcal/mol, confirming the tight binding between the ligand and semiconductor surface through the interfacial bonding and effective electronic coupling56,57.

Frontier molecular orbital features were calculated to investigate the reaction kinetics, chemical reactivity, electronic transition, and optical absorption properties. A smaller HOMO-LUMO energy gap is responsible for kinetic instability, higher chemical reactivity by easier transition of electron from HOMO to LUMO, and optical absorption over a wide wavelength58. The calculated band gap of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 complex was 2.99 and 2.50 eV, respectively (Figs. 6a–d). In the TiO2, the charge-density distribution is mainly localized around surface oxygen (2p) atoms, reflecting the intrinsic ionic character of Ti–O bonding (Figs. 6a, b). Upon EDTA adsorption, pronounced charge redistribution is observed at the EDTA–TiO2 interface, with electron accumulation and depletion regions extending across surface Ti and coordinating O/N atoms of EDTA. This feature confirms the formation of strong surface coordination rather than weak physisorption. The HOMO is predominantly distributed over the EDTA, while the LUMO is mainly situated on the TiO2 surface. This spatial separation of the frontier orbitals suggests efficient charge transfer from the EDTA ligand (donor) to TiO2 (acceptor) upon photoexcitation via LMCT, which can facilitate visible-light-driven photocatalytic activity59.

(a-d) Molecular orbitals, (e) DOS plot, and calculated (f) UV-vis spectra of TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 complex.

The DOS analysis (Fig. 6e) shows that EDTA modification introduces additional electronic states near the valence band edge of TiO2, resulting in an upward shift of the valence band maximum and a slight band-gap narrowing. These EDTA–derived states mainly influence the valence band, while the conduction band remains dominated by Ti 3d orbitals. The modified band structure enhances visible-light absorption and promotes hole-driven reactions, thereby contributing to improved photocatalytic performance.

The UV–vis spectra derived from TD-DFT calculation reveal a clear difference in the optical response of pristine TiO2 and EDTA–modified TiO2. TiO2 shows an excitation energy of 2.31 eV (538 nm), with a low oscillator strength (0.0001), which further contributed to weak visible-light absorption, and its excitations involve multiple transitions such as H→L (24.5%), H→L + 1(40.5%), H→L + 4 (15.0%), and H-1→L + 3 (6.0%), suggesting the contribution of mixed orbitals (Table S1). In contrast, upon EDTA incorporation, the excitation energy decreases to 1.96 eV (633 nm) with an increase in oscillator strength (0.0004), implying enhanced optical activity, which is further confirmed by the red-shifted absorption spectra (Fig. 6f). The dominant H→L (95.0%) transition confirms a strong ligand-to-metal charge transfer from EDTA to the cluster TiO2. This electron transfer process reduces the bandgap and strengthens visible-light absorption, suggesting enhanced charge carrier separation and superior photocatalytic performance of the EDTA–TiO2 complex. These findings indicate good consistency between experimental (Fig. 4) and theoretical results. Most stable optimized structures of the TiO2 cluster and the EDTA–TiO2 complex are presented in Fig. S4.

Photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction

The photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction activities of pure TiO2 and EDTA–TiO2 have been evaluated under various conditions (Fig. 7) using 20 mg of photocatalyst and 30 ppm of Cr(VI) aqueous solution. No apparent Cr(VI) reduction was observed in photolysis and catalysis experiments, which reveals the stable nature of Cr(VI). Under visible-light irradiation, pristine TiO2 exhibited only ~ 5% Cr(VI) removal after 60 min irradiation, which is mainly attributed to surface adsorption rather than true photocatalytic reduction due to its wide band gap and fast charge recombination (Fig. 7a). The addition of 20 vol % of MeOH moderately enhances Cr(VI) reduction (46.9%) by acting as a sacrificial hole scavenger, which suppresses electron–hole recombination and increases the availability of conduction band electrons. However, the introduction of 100 ppm EDTA in the TiO2 surface significantly improves Cr(VI) reduction (59.5%) owing to surface chelation and ligand-to-metal charge transfer, which also contributed to enhancing visible-light absorption and interfacial electron transfer. A combined system employing 20 vol % MeOH and a reduced EDTA concentration (100 ppm) achieved 99.3% Cr(VI) reduction within 60 min irradiation, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect between surface complexation and efficient hole scavenging. The apparent rate constants (Fig. 7b) follow the order: TiO2 < TiO2 (MeOH) < TiO2 (EDTA) < TiO2 (EDTA + MeOH), consistent with the observed kinetic trends. The highest rate constant (0.0996 min− 1) for the combined system (EDTA + MeOH) is ascribed to enhanced charge separation, prolonged electron lifetime, and accelerated Cr(VI) reduction at the TiO2 surface. For comparison, 20 mg of the EDTA–TiO2 complex (containing 100 ppm EDTA) with 20 vol % MeOH was employed under the same reaction conditions, and 99.1% of Cr(VI) was reduced after 60 min of visible-light irradiation (Fig. S5).

(a) Photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction efficiency of different photocatalysts and (b) their corresponding rate constant after 60 min of visible light irradiation (450 nm).

Further, active-species trapping experiments were performed to elucidate the dominant reactive species in Cr(VI) reduction with TiO2 (Fig. 8) in presence of optimized amount of EDTA and MEOH. In the absence of scavengers, nearly complete Cr(VI) reduction (99.3%) was achieved, while the addition of K2S2O4 (h+ scavenger) and t-BuOH (•OH scavenger) caused only minor changes (97.0% and 99.6%, respectively), indicating that holes and hydroxyl radicals play a minor role. In contrast, the presence of ammonium oxalate (AO), an electron scavenger, dramatically suppressed the reduction efficiency to 25.5%, confirming that photogenerated electrons are the key active species responsible for Cr(VI) reduction. The slight decrease (98.7%) observed with p-benzoquinone (p-BQ) suggests a minor contribution from superoxide radicals, but electron-driven reduction dominates the reaction pathway.

Reaction condition optimization

The photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction process was systematically optimized by prioritizing the reaction pH, followed by the concentrations of sacrificial agents (EDTA and MeOH), initial Cr(VI) concentration, and catalyst dosage. As these parameters critically influence Cr(VI) speciation, charge-carrier separation, adsorption behavior, and the availability of active sites.

The effect of solution pH on Cr(VI) reduction was examined at varied pH (2 ~ 6), where 0.14 M HNO3 and 0.1 M NaOH aqueous solutions were used to adjust the reaction pH. At an uncontrolled pH (2.45) and 20 mg of TiO2 with combined (EDTA + MeOH) condition, 99.3% of a 30 ppm Cr(VI) was reduced after 60 min of irradiation (450 nm). The photoreduction ability increased from 99.3 to 99.7% with the decrease in pH from 2.45 to 2.0 and yielded the highest rate constants 0.0996 to 0.1196 min− 1, respectively (Fig. S6a, b). This behavior arises from the favorable electrostatic attraction between positively charged TiO2 surfaces and dominant anionic Cr(VI) species (HCrO4⁻), as well as the more positive redox potential of Cr(VI)/Cr(III) under acidic conditions. The DPC reacts selectively with Cr(VI), where DPC is oxidized to 1,5-diphenylcarbazone and Cr(VI) is reduced to Cr(III). The resulting Cr(III) forms a stable reddish-violet complex with DPCA, exhibiting a characteristic absorption peak at ~ 540 nm that allows quantitative analysis by UV–Vis’s spectrophotometry41.

In contrast, neutral to mildly acidic conditions (pH 4–6) significantly suppress the reduction kinetics due to electrostatic repulsion, reduced Cr(VI) adsorption, and less favorable thermodynamics for electron transfer.

EDTA and MeOH were introduced as sacrificial hole scavengers to enhance visible-light-driven photocatalytic Cr(VI) reduction. Herein, EDTA acts as an efficient sacrificial hole scavenger, rapidly consuming valence-band holes and effectively suppressing electron–hole recombination, thereby prolonging the lifetime of photogenerated conduction-band electrons. To achieve maximum Cr(VI) reduction efficiency, the concentrations of these additives were systematically optimized. Pure TiO2 exhibits negligible Cr(VI) reduction (k = 0.0012 min− 1) (Fig. 7b), whereas increasing the EDTA concentration to 100 ppm moderately enhances the photoreduction (59.5%) ability and corresponding rate constant (k = 0.0169 min− 1) (Fig. S7a, b). While further increases to 200 and 300 ppm led to a pronounced acceleration in Cr(VI) reduction (99.2 and 99.4%, respectively) and their rate constant (k = 0.080 and 0.0879 min− 1, respectively), resulting from enhanced surface complexation, ligand-to-metal charge transfer, and improved electron availability. Further, MeOH was introduced as a sacrificial agent, whereby it reacts with photogenerated holes or ·OH radicals to form reactive intermediates (·CH2OH), which indirectly enhance the availability of electrons for Cr(VI) reduction (Fig. S7c, d). In the absence of MeOH, Cr(VI) reduction is sluggish (k = 0.0012 min− 1), whereas increasing MeOH content (20 vol %) progressively enhances the reduction ability (46.9%) and rate constant (k = 0.0105 min− 1). This improvement arises from the role of MeOH as a sacrificial hole scavenger, which prolongs electron lifetime and facilitates Cr(VI) reduction. However, the overall enhancement remains moderate due to the limited visible-light activation of TiO2 in the absence of EDTA.

Although higher EDTA concentrations resulted in near-complete reduction, the improvement beyond 200 ppm was marginal, while the associated cost increased significantly. Therefore, a combined system employing EDTA and MeOH was adopted to balance the photocatalytic efficiency and economic feasibility. When used individually, 100 ppm EDTA and 20 vol % MeOH achieved 59.5% and 46.9% Cr(VI) reduction (Fig. S8a, b), respectively, highlighting their limited effectiveness as standalone sacrificial agents under visible light. In contrast, the combined addition of MeOH and EDTA resulted in a pronounced enhancement in reduction efficiency, indicating a synergistic effect between the two-hole scavengers. Specifically, the presence of 5 vol % MeOH with 100 ppm EDTA increased the Cr(VI) reduction efficiency to 87.5%, which further improved to 98.8% and 99.1% upon increasing the MeOH content to 10 and 15 vol %, respectively. A marginal increase to 99.3% was observed at 20 vol % MeOH, suggesting that near-saturation of charge-scavenging efficiency was achieved. This trend confirms that the cooperative suppression of charge recombination by EDTA and MeOH maximizes electron availability for Cr(VI) reduction, while excessive MeOH provides diminishing returns. A similar trend was observed for the apparent rate constants, which increased with MeOH concentration in the presence of EDTA and approached saturation at higher MeOH contents.

The effect of catalyst dosage on Cr(VI) reduction was evaluated using a fixed composition of 20 vol % MeOH and 100 ppm EDTA under visible-light irradiation (Figs. S9a, b). At a low TiO2 loading of 5 mg, only 70.4% Cr(VI) reduction with a slower rate constant of 0.0226 min− 1 was achieved, reflecting the limited availability of active sites and insufficient light utilization. Increasing the catalyst dose to 10 mg significantly enhanced the reduction efficiency to 97.4% (k = 0.0879 min− 1), indicating improved light harvesting and charge-carrier generation. A further increase to 15 and 20 mg resulted in marginal improvements, reaching 98.9% (k = 0.0951 min− 1) and 99.3% (k = 0.0996 min− 1) reduction, respectively, suggesting that near-optimal photocatalytic activity was attained. However, at a higher loading of 25 mg, the reduction efficiency and rate constant slightly decreased to 98.2% and 0.0926 min− 1, which can be attributed to increased light scattering and particle agglomeration that hinder effective photon penetration and charge utilization.

The influence of the initial Cr(VI) concentration on photocatalytic reduction was investigated using a fixed catalyst dose of 20 mg TiO2 in the presence of 20 vol % MeOH and 100 ppm EDTA under visible light irradiation. At an initial concentration of 20 ppm, 99.7% Cr(VI) reduction with a 0.115 min− 1 rate constant was achieved within 40 min, with the majority of the reduction occurring during the first 20 min, indicating rapid reaction kinetics at low pollutant loading (Fig. S9c, d). Increasing the Cr(VI) concentration to 30 ppm resulted in 99.3% reduction with a 0.0996 min− 1 rate constant within 60 min, while further increases to 40 and 50 ppm led to slightly decreased efficiencies of 98.0% (k = 0.0823 min− 1) and 96.2% (k = 0.0623 min− 1), respectively, over the same irradiation period. This gradual decline in performance at higher concentrations can be attributed to increased electron consumption and partial saturation of surface-active sites, which slow the reduction kinetics despite prolonged irradiation.

Stability and reusability test

The stability and reusability of the photocatalyst were examined over four successive Cr(VI) reduction cycles using 20 mg EDTA–TiO2 complex containing 100 ppm of EDTA in the presence of 20 vol% MeOH under visible-light irradiation. After each cycle, surface-adsorbed Cr(OH)3 was removed by treatment with dilute nitric acid (HNO3), followed by thorough washing with deionized water and drying in a vacuum oven at 60 °C. In the first cycle, 99.3% Cr(VI) reduction was achieved within 60 min, demonstrating excellent initial activity. The reduction efficiency slightly decreased to 98.5%, 97.8%, and 97.2% in the second, third, and fourth cycles, respectively, while maintaining high overall performance (Fig. 9a). The minor decline in activity can be attributed to partial surface fouling or loss of active sites during repeated use, confirming the good stability and reusability of the photocatalyst. After the fourth cycle, the collected photocatalyst was subjected to SEM and FT-IR analysis, and compared with fresh and used EDTA–TiO2 complex and no significant changes were observed (Fig. 9b–d). These findings collectively confirm the strong durability and robust reusability of EDTA–TiO2, highlighting its promising role for practical and sustainable applications in the remediation of chromium-contaminated water.

Proposed mechanism

The visible-light-driven reduction of Cr(VI) over TiO2 originates from an interfacial LMCT process enabled by EDTA surface coordination. Through its carboxylate groups (–COO–), EDTA selectively binds to surface Ti⁴⁺ sites, forming Ti–O–EDTA interfacial complexes that introduce new electronic states at the TiO2 interface. This interfacial electronic restructuring extends the photo-response of TiO2 into the visible region despite its intrinsically wide band gap. Upon photoexcitation, electrons are transferred from EDTA–derived states into the TiO2 CB, where they subsequently reduce Cr(VI) to Cr(III) (Fig. 10). Concurrently, oxidized EDTA species (EDTA·⁺) can undergo further electron donation, sustaining the reduction process. In addition, methanol is readily oxidized by VB’s holes to generate reactive hydroxymethyl radicals, which further contribute electrons to the reduction process. According to DFT analysis, the EDTA–TiO2 system exhibits strong thermodynamic stability, as evidenced by negative free energy and adsorption energy values. The reduced band gap and LMCT pathways highlight the role of EDTA in improving the electronic and photocatalytic properties of TiO2.

Conclusion

In summary, a simultaneous in situ photodeposition method and evaporation method were used for the EDTA–TiO2 complexation. FT-IR vibration (1411 cm− 1), and DFT-derived sufficient negative adsorption energy (Eads = −18.83 kcal/mol) confirm the formation of EDTA–TiO2 complex. FMO and TD-DFT calculations also revealed a sufficient electron transfer from the HOMO of adsorbed EDTA to CB of TiO2.The superior performance of the EDTA–TiO2 complex for improved Cr(VI) reduction was mainly achieved through the LMCT process. This work extends the LMCT concept and demonstrates an effective strategy to activate visible-light-inactive semiconductors via surface complexation, thereby broadening their photocatalytic applicability.
