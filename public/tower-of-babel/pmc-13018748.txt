Effects of a probiotic fermented dairy product on hippocampal metabolites, structure and function: an 8-week randomised, placebo-controlled trial in healthy women
Authors: Wolfgang Marx, Chao Suo, Thusharika Dissanayaka, Suzan Maleki, Liam Nguyen, Nikolaj Travica, Mohammadreza Mohebbi, Mojtaba Lotfaliany, Murat Yucel, Amelia J McGuinness, Michael Berk, Hajara Aslam, Felice N Jacka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFermented foods are a promising yet underexplored intervention for influencing brain function and mental health through the gut–brain axis.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of a dairy product fermented with probiotic bacteria on aspects of brain structure and function.DesignIn a triple-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial, 40 healthy women aged 18–55 years were randomised to consume either 130 g per day of a fermented probiotic yoghurt or a placebo for 8 weeks. The primary outcome was the between-group differential change from baseline to week 8 in left hippocampal metabolites, measured using magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Secondary outcomes included changes in brain structure and function, faecal microbiome composition and functional potential, mental health, gastrointestinal symptoms, memory and blood markers of oxidative stress and inflammation.ResultsThere was a between-group difference in the change in average left hippocampal glutathione concentration (mean difference in change: −0.49; 95% CI −0.95 to −0.04), as well as brain volume in the hippocampus and nucleus accumbens, although these results did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons. There were between-group differences in the change in average functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and left frontal pole. There was also a significant between-group change in gut microbiome beta diversity. There were no differences in other secondary measures.ConclusionThis study provides preliminary evidence that a probiotic fermented dairy product can modulate hippocampal-related outcomes.Trial registration numberACTRN12622000622707.

Introduction

The microbiome–gut–brain axis—characterised as the interplay between the gut microbiome and the brain—has emerged as potentially important in mental health and cognitive function.1 The gut microbiome’s influence on brain health is supported by preclinical studies showing modulation of key pathways related to the hippocampus, a brain region central to learning, memory, mood and stress regulation.2 Animal studies with germ-free mice suggest that the gut microbiome can modulate hippocampal concentrations of 5-hydroxytryptamine and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid as well as hippocampal neurogenesis.3 4 Preclinical studies have demonstrated that interventions with probiotics and prebiotics beneficially modulate several mechanisms influencing the hippocampus, including increasing brain-derived neurotrophic factor concentrations, reducing oxidative stress, suppressing hippocampal apoptosis and reducing deposition of amyloid-beta plaques within the hippocampus.5 Recent probiotic supplementation clinical trials also report significant changes in brain imaging outcomes.68

In contrast to the standalone probiotic and prebiotic interventions evaluated in prior studies,68 fermented foods, such as yoghurt and fermented vegetables, contain a combination of these probiotic and prebiotic bioactive components in addition to biogenic compounds that are produced during the fermentation process.9 10 It is theorised that due to this combination of microbiome-related components, fermented foods may exert additional benefits on psychological and cognitive functioning when compared with isolated interventions.9 One prior study, for example, examined the impact of consumption of a fermented dairy product with added probiotic bacteria in 12 healthy women for 4 weeks, compared with a non-fermented dairy product or a group that received no intervention and showed that the fermented dairy intervention modulated activity of brain regions (insula, periaqueductal grey) involved in emotion and sensation processing.11 However, given the limited studies that have evaluated the impact of fermented food interventions on the brain, further studies are required to replicate these results and to explore other dimensions of brain function that are relevant to mental and cognitive outcomes.

Thus, our study aimed to extend the current knowledge by evaluating the possible effect of a fermented probiotic yoghurt on multiple brain parameters, with a specific focus on the hippocampus, as well as related blood, clinical and gut microbiome endpoints, using a randomised, placebo-controlled trial design.

Methods

Trial design

This trial was prospectively registered with the Australia New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12622000622707). The Deakin University Human Research and Ethics Committee (2022–018) and the Monash University Human Research and Ethics Committee (2022-32337-77961) approved this study. The study was led by researchers at the Food & Mood Centre at Deakin University in Geelong and conducted at the BrainPark, The Turner Institute for Brain and Mental Health, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia.

This was an 8-week, triple-blinded, placebo-controlled, parallel trial. Eligible participants completed two in-person study visits: one at baseline and one at the week 8 follow-up. The study duration of 8 weeks was based on prior probiotic intervention studies that have reported significant changes in brain imaging outcomes within this time frame and prior meta-analyses that suggest that studies >4 weeks may be more efficacious.81113 Participants were randomly allocated to the intervention or placebo product and required to consume the assigned product each morning for the 8-week study duration. During the 8-week trial period, participants were contacted two times via phone, text or email to assess adverse events and study adherence.

Participants

Participants were recruited via online social media advertisements and physical flyers between 16 June 2022 and 21 December 2022. Prior to participant enrolment, a prescreening interview was completed by telephone to determine eligibility. Potentially eligible participants provided consent via the online REDCap eConsent form.

We recruited only women to reduce interindividual variability and increase internal validity, based on preclinical studies suggesting sex-specific differences in microbiome–brain interactions. For example, Clarke et al3 reported sex-dependent effects of the microbiome on hippocampal serotonergic signalling, a key mechanism relevant to our primary outcome. This approach is consistent with early-phase mechanistic trials that prioritise signal detection under controlled conditions.

Further eligibility criteria included: aged 18–55 years who were: considered healthy and with an absence of a diagnosis of cognitive impairment, major neurological disorder, any diagnosed psychiatric disorders, absence of mood disturbance (Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)−2 score <3)14 or major gastrointestinal disorders; deemed safe and comfortable to undergo an MRI; willing to maintain their habitual diet and level of exercise, to comply with study procedures and to provide voluntary informed consent; and English-speaking or non-English-speaking participants who could ensure external interpreter for the onsite clinical visits and for the phone follow-up timepoints.

Exclusion criteria included the following: use of probiotics or antibiotics during the previous 2 weeks; recently commenced a new supplement known to influence gut/brain health (eg, dietary fibre); reported consuming fermented dairy on a regular basis; allergy to dairy or lactose intolerance or allergy/intolerance to any study components; acute illness or recovering from acute illness; pregnancy or breastfeeding; and/or participation in another study using an investigational product.

Participants were asked to maintain their normal exercise/physical activity levels and dietary intake. They were also instructed to list and maintain any eligible dietary supplements or prescribed medications. Additionally, participants were provided $A100 for completing the study to cover out-of-pocket expenses associated with participation in the trial.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Intervention

The intervention was a 130 g of fermented probiotic yoghurt, consumed each day, that was fermented with >3 billion Colony-Forming Units (CFU) of Bifidobacterium. lactis (BB-12) and >100 billion CFU of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus. The placebo was also dairy-based but was not fermented with bacterial cultures or subjected to any fermentation processes. It included the following ingredients to match the appearance, texture and flavour of the interventional yoghurt: milk, water, milk solids, cream, thickener (1422, Pectin), gelatine, natural flavour and acidity regulator (330). All study products were provided to participants free of charge. Participants completed a study product log to record missed doses, which was submitted to the research staff at the final time point.

Outcomes

Imaging measurements

Brain imaging was conducted at baseline and week 8 using a Siemens Skyra 3 Tesla MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil at the Monash Biomedical Imaging Facility, Monash University. The imaging protocol included T1-weighted structural MRI, resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI), task-based fMRI and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS).

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

The prespecified primary outcome was the difference in hippocampal glutathione (GSH), N-acetyl aspartic acid (NAA), choline (Cho), creatine (Cr), myo-inositol and glutamate/glutamine concentrations between groups. MRS data were analysed using LCModel software, applying standard quality control thresholds (Cramer-Rao lower bound <20%, Signal-to-Noise Ratio>10) and corrected for partial volume effects using tissue concentration estimates.

Structural and functional MRI processing

T1-weighted structural MRI data were analysed for hippocampal and cortical thickness using FMRIB Software Library‘s (FSL) FIRST and FreeSurfer pipelines, with total intracranial volume adjustments. Functional connectivity analyses were performed using the CONN toolbox, while DTI data were processed using MRtrix and FSL, including motion correction and diffusion metrics extraction. Task-based fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12, modelling activation contrasts for memory encoding and retrieval phases.

Additional details on MRI acquisition parameters, preprocessing steps and analytic pipelines are provided in online supplemental materials.

Clinical, psychological and cognitive assessments

Dietary intake was assessed at baseline using the 80-item Dietary Questionnaire for Epidemiological Studies (V.3.2).15 Depressive symptoms were measured at baseline and week 8 using the PHQ-9,16 a validated 9-item tool (score range: 0–27), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Well-being was assessed with the WHO Well-Being Index (WHO-5),17 and gut symptomology was measured using the Visual Analogue Scale for Irritable Bowel Syndrome.18

Hippocampal-dependent memory function was evaluated at baseline and week 8 using the Paired Associates Learning task (Cantab, UK), a computerised assessment of spatial memory. Full details of assessment tools are available in online supplemental materials.

Biospecimen analysis

Non-fasted serum and plasma blood samples were collected at baseline and at the 8-week follow-up by a trained phlebotomist and changes in blood markers of oxidative stress (eg, serum total antioxidant capacity) and inflammation (eg, plasma high-sensitivity C reactive protein) were measured by an independent lab (Melbourne Pathology, Australia).

Participants were provided with self-administered faecal collection kits and instructed to collect samples at home at baseline and follow-up. Samples were returned via prepaid express post using kits that included a DNA stabiliser to preserve microbial integrity during transport. On receipt at Microba (Brisbane, Australia), samples were immediately stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using the DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT Kit (Qiagen) with mechanical lysis using PowerBead Pro beads. Libraries were constructed with the Illumina DNA Prep (M) Tagmentation Kit (Illumina, 20018705) and IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Index Sets A-D (Illumina 20027213–16), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq6000 (Illumina). Quantification of gene and pathway abundances was performed using the Microba Gene and Pathway Profiler. Detailed gut microbiome methods are in online supplemental materials.

Power calculation

Based on previous work,19 an R2 of 0.3, a common SD of 0.15 for each group and a drop-out rate of 15%, a total sample of 40 was estimated to achieve 80% power to detect a meaningful difference in change in mean levels of the primary outcome. This a priori power calculation was based on an omnibus Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) framework designed to assess overall group differences. This power analysis was conducted for the primary outcome only and did not account for multiple comparisons corrections across the secondary outcomes. As such, the study may be underpowered to detect small to moderate effects on these secondary outcomes after correction for multiple comparisons, and these results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Participants were randomised after providing consent and prior to the baseline visit in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or placebo group using a block randomisation procedure containing four observations per block. Allocation sequence was generated by a statistician separate from the research team and procedures. An unblinded research assistant conducted the group assignments. The outcome assessors and all other study investigators, including the statistician, remained blinded to group allocation until the completion of data analysis for the primary outcome.

Statistical methods

A modified intention-to-treat analysis that included only participants who completed the baseline visit and received the study intervention was performed. For brain imaging outcomes, participants with poor quality imaging data (unable to be analysed) were excluded from the analysis.

For the primary and most secondary outcomes, we used generalised estimating equations (GEE) models to estimate between-group differential changes from baseline to the 8-week follow-up. The GEE models included the group allocation, nominal time point and the interaction between intervention group and time point as fixed effects. The two-way interaction between group allocation and nominal time points estimated the between-group differential change from baseline at week 8.

For voxel-based or vertex-based analyses, including functional MRI and whole-brain FreeSurfer analysis, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) model was employed with nominal time and group as the main factors as well as their interaction (time×group).

To evaluate between-group differences in gut microbiome alpha diversity, we used Shannon index and richness metrics. Low-prevalence taxa present in fewer than 5% of samples were filtered out. Between-group differences in gut microbiome beta diversity were calculated on complete cases. Centred log-ratio (CLR) transformations were carried out before conducting beta-diversity and differential abundance statistical tests (species, genus, family and phylum), given the compositional and non-normal nature of microbiome relative abundance data. We created CLR-transformed component-wise change scores (baseline minus week 8) and used permutational analysis of variance with the adonis2 function from the vegan package38 with 999 permutations. The R2 statistic is reported, representing the proportion of variance explained by the grouping factor (ie, intervention or placebo group) in the model.

Post-hoc Pearson’s correlation analyses were conducted between outcomes where a significant intervention effect was observed.

Multiple imputation was conducted for all outcomes with missing follow-up data. The imputation model included baseline covariates to improve imputation precision and 50 imputations were specified, assuming data were missing at random.

For imaging outcomes at a voxel/vertex level, a cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied with an initial threshold of p<0.0010.20 The threshold for significant imaging findings was defined as an FDR-corrected p value <0.05. For all other outcomes, family-wise Benjamini-Hochberg corrections were applied to control for multiple comparisons, and both adjusted p values (q-values) and unadjusted p values are presented in the results tables.

Figures were developed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0. Gut microbiome outcomes were analysed using the lme4 R package.21 All other statistical analyses were conducted using Stata V.14.22

Results

A total of 102 potentially eligible participants were screened, with 43 meeting eligibility criteria (figure 1). Three eligible participants did not complete the baseline visit, leaving 40 participants to be enrolled and eligible for our modified intention to treat analysis. Baseline data

The sample comprised 40 adults (mean age 32±12 years), with 18 participants allocated to the intervention group and 22 to the placebo group. Most participants (98%) had completed postsecondary education. The majority identified as Oceanian (70%), followed by South-East Asian (25%), with smaller representation from North-East Asian (3%) and Southern and Eastern European (3%) backgrounds. Baseline characteristics were generally similar between groups (table 1); however, there were higher levels of dietary supplement use within the intervention group compared with the placebo group. The most reported dietary supplements were multivitamins (27%) and vitamin D (27%). The most reported medication was the contraceptive pill (44%). Baseline dietary intake was similar between groups across all macronutrients (online supplemental table 1).

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

There was a significant between-group differential change from baseline to week 8 in glutathione concentration at the left hippocampus (mean difference in change: −0.49; 95% CI −0.95 to −0.04; table 2), with negative values indicating greater increases in the intervention group. Glutathione concentration at the left hippocampus increased in the intervention group, with minimal change in the placebo group (figure 2). However, this did not survive multiple comparisons testing of all the metabolites of interest (ie, NAA, Cr, Choline, Myo-inositol and glutamate/glutamine). There were no significant differences in any other measured metabolite and no significant difference when calculated as Cr ratio (online supplemental table 2).

Functional connectivity

Seed analysis on the left hippocampus found a significant between-group differential change from baseline to week 8 in functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and left frontal pole (pFDR-corrected <0.001, k=188, T=5.86, (−22, 42, 12)), which remained significant after FDR correction (mean difference in change: −0.21; 95% CI −0.31 to −0.11; figure 3). Functional connectivity between these two regions increased in the intervention group, with inverse changes of a similar magnitude in the placebo group.

Brain structure outcomes

There was a significant between-group difference in change from baseline to week 8 in left hippocampal volume (mean difference: −126.84; 95% CI −225.46 to −28.22; figure 4), with an increase in the intervention group and an inverse decrease in the placebo group. A similar between-group difference was observed in the right nucleus accumbens (mean difference: 27.78; 95% CI 0.92 to 54.63; figure 4) but favouring the placebo group rather than the intervention group. The change in left hippocampal volume remained significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons within the hippocampal region (ie, left and right hippocampus); however, both the left hippocampal and right nucleus accumbens differences were no longer significant after multiple comparison adjustments were applied across all bilateral brain regions (table 3).

No significant intervention effect was observed for DTI-related outcomes (eg, fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity) at the whole brain level or region of interest level after multiple comparison correction, including the two tracts of interests that connect to the hippocampus (eg, Fornix and hippocampal part of cingulum; online supplemental table 3). Further analysis showed no significant differences in changes for thickness at the whole brain level or other subcortical regions after multiple comparison correction.

Task-based fMRI

At baseline, brain activation during the memory encoding phase was evident in various cortical regions and bilateral hippocampi (online supplemental figure 1). A comparable activation pattern was observed during the memory retrieval phase (online supplemental figure 2). However, we did not find any time by group interaction on the behavioural outcomes over 8 weeks, that is, success rate (mean difference in change: −0.05; 95% CI −0.19 to 0.08, online supplemental figure S3). Further whole brain imaging analysis showed no significant time by group interactions for either memory encoding phase or memory retrieving phase.

Gut microbiome outcomes

We did not observe statistically significant between-group differences in the changes in average species-level richness or Shannon index at week 8 (online supplemental figure S4; online supplemental table S4). However, we did observe a statistically significant between group difference in the change in beta diversity (Aitchison distances; r2=0.038, p=0.010), indicating that 3.8% of the shift in overall community structure between groups may be attributed to the intervention (online supplemental figure S5; online supplemental table S5). We also observed statistically significant between-group differences in the change of 13 bacterial species (CLR-transformed abundances; p<0.05) (online supplemental table S6). Notably, the species Bifidobacterium animalis and Streptococcus thermophilus (both present in the interventional product) increased in the intervention group, with minimal change in the placebo group (online supplemental figure S5). Bifidobacterium animalis was the only species to approach statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons (online supplemental table S6). There were no between-group differences in the change in MetaCyc group alpha diversity (online supplemental figure S7) or the changes in any functional MetaCyc pathway (data not shown).

Correlation analysis of MRI outcomes

There was an overall moderate positive correlation between left hippocampal volume and change in functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and frontal pole (r=0.325, p=0.047; figure 5). There was also a moderate positive correlation between change in glutathione concentrations in the left hippocampus and change in functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and frontal pole (r=0.394, p=0.019; figure 6). There was a moderate positive correlation between the change in Bifidobacterium animalis and the change in functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and frontal pole (r=0.38, p=0.026; online supplemental figure S7).

Participant adherence and adverse events

More than 90% of the intervention/placebo was consumed by 90% of the participants. Three adverse events were self-reported during the intervention period. One participant within the placebo group reported mild bloating or flatulence at two timepoints and one participant within the intervention group reported mild nightmares. Furthermore, baseline MRI scans identified an abnormal brain growth in one participant within the placebo group that was referred to the participant’s doctor for follow-up.

Discussion

Our study suggests significant between-group differences in hippocampus-related functional connectivity between those who received an 8-week fermented probiotic yoghurt intervention versus a placebo yoghurt. Furthermore, we found some evidence for increases in hippocampal volume and glutathione concentrations in those receiving the intervention product; however, these did not survive multiple comparison testing. These results are consistent with prior probiotic interventions that have investigated brain imaging outcomes, whereby the most consistently reported significant effect has been on functional connectivity endpoints, in comparison to limited evidence for other endpoints such as brain region volume.8 11 12 23

To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide preliminary evidence that fermented probiotic yoghurt interventions may increase glutathione concentrations within the hippocampus. Glutathione is one of the primary antioxidants responsible for mitigating oxidative stress and, as such, these results have particular relevance to neuropsychiatric disorders.24 However, our results need to be considered preliminary as they were not maintained after correction for multiple comparisons. Hence, further studies should investigate the effect of fermented dairy interventions on glutathione status in larger samples. Our study also provides preliminary, though inconsistent, data regarding the effect of fermented probiotic yoghurt on brain volume endpoints. The between-group differential change in left hippocampal volume suggests a favourable effect of fermented probiotic yoghurt on a brain region implicated in several major psychiatric disorders including depression and schizophrenia.25 26 An unexpected finding was the increase in right nucleus accumbens volume observed in the placebo group. As this result did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and was not hypothesised a priori, it should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the measurement of the nucleus accumbens has been shown to have only moderate test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient ~0.70–0.80),27 which increases the likelihood that this result may be due to measurement variability rather than a true structural change. Replication in larger samples with greater statistical power is required to determine the reliability and importance of this effect.

The reported change in functional connectivity between the left hippocampus and the frontal pole may have application to populations with neurological conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, age-related cognitive decline and traumatic brain injury, where reduced functional connectivity between these areas compared with healthy controls has been documented.2830 Our results are consistent with the findings of two previous probiotic interventions that demonstrated changes in frontal pole connectivity; however, these prior studies reported that change was between regions such as the frontal medial cortex (decreased) and occipital polar cortex (increased), respectively, rather than with the hippocampus as reported in our study.7 12

Despite the observed preliminary neurobiological changes in hippocampal connectivity and glutathione levels, it is important to emphasise that no significant improvements were detected in clinical symptomatology or cognitive performance across the 8-week intervention. This suggests that while the intervention may influence brain-related pathways, these effects did not translate into measurable psychological or behavioural outcomes. This is likely due to the recruitment of a healthy participant population with low baseline levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, which minimised the likelihood of separation of outcomes between groups. This is in line with subgroup analyses included in prior meta-analyses of probiotic interventions, which reported that improvements in mood were generally driven by studies in people with a clinical diagnosis of depression or greater symptom severity, with no significant results observed in healthy individuals.31 This suggests that future studies should focus on clinical populations or those with elevated depressive symptoms to evaluate the possible procognitive, neurostructural and antidepressant effects of probiotic interventions. Alternatively, study design methods that induce participant stressors may also increase the separation of outcomes between groups of healthy participants, as has been demonstrated in prior probiotic intervention studies using cold pressor or a social exclusion paradigm stressor.32 33 Similarly, the observed between-group difference in gut microbiome beta diversity (Aitchison distances, R²=0.038, p=0.010) suggests that approximately 3.8% of the shift in microbial community structure was attributable to the intervention. While this effect size is consistent with prior microbiota-targeted interventions, the clinical relevance of these results is unclear in the absence of changes in microbial metabolic pathways or alpha diversity. Future studies are needed to clarify whether the observed changes in microbial composition translate to clinically meaningful biological or psychiatric outcomes.

The results of our trial should be viewed within the context of the following limitations. While our sample size is concordant with recent studies with similar study designs,12 23 34 larger trials are required to confirm these results. This is especially important for outcomes such as changes in left hippocampal volume and functional connectivity, where baseline between-group differences were observed. Similarly, the differential changes in volume in the left hippocampus versus the nucleus accumbens may reflect sample size limitations, and the loss of statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons indicates that larger studies are needed to confirm these preliminary findings. Furthermore, the null oxidative stress results may be due to many participants not being fasted at time of collection, and, therefore, may have been influenced by dietary antioxidant intake. Fasting was not always feasible in this study due to MRI scheduling constraints, with some appointments occurring in the afternoon. Future studies that focus on biomarkers should consider standardising fasting status prior to blood collection to reduce variability and improve sensitivity.

Our study recruited healthy individuals only. While this allows us to investigate the role of fermented dairy products within the context of the general population, further studies that investigate these same outcomes within clinical populations such as those with mental or cognitive disorders or elevated symptoms may provide meaningful insight into the mechanisms of action in disease conditions. To our knowledge, there is only one study by Schaub et al,8 which investigated a 4-week probiotic supplementation on brain imaging outcomes in people with major depressive disorder, reporting decreased putamen activation in response to neutral faces after the probiotic intervention as well as improvements in depressive symptoms. However, further studies are required to replicate this and to expand to other mental and cognitive disorders. Furthermore, we included only women to reduce biological heterogeneity, based on evidence that microbiome–brain interactions, including those affecting the hippocampus, may differ by sex.3 Future studies should include male participants to evaluate sex-specific responses and to improve generalisability across populations.

Finally, a shortcoming common within the probiotic-microbiome literature is the limited understanding of strain-specific mechanisms of action. We used a probiotic yoghurt that included the common yoghurt cultures Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus as well as added Bifidobacterium animalis subspecies lactis (BB-12), a probiotic strain with substantial prior literature.35 However, it is currently unknown whether certain strains or fermented food formulations may provide different treatment effects compared with others. Moreover, there is no clarity on whether the results we observed may be attributable to the probiotic bacteria contained within the fermented yoghurt product, the fermentation products produced or indeed something specific to fermented dairy in general. More research is needed to understand the biochemical mechanisms potentially responsible for the outcomes we observed.

Our study also had several strengths. First, our study used a placebo control that was matched to the intervention by volume, taste, appearance, texture and aroma. This is a notable strength given the difficulty in effectively blinding food interventions where matching placebos are typically unavailable.36 Our study was also longer in duration compared with many recent probiotic studies that investigated brain imaging outcomes over a 4-week time frame.6 8 23 34 The use of an 8-week timeframe allowed us to investigate brain imaging outcomes over a time course that has been previously reported to be more likely to demonstrate changes in psychiatric outcomes.13 Finally, our study expands the currently limited literature regarding fermented dairy interventions, informing additional microbiome-targeted interventions beyond probiotic and prebiotic supplementation.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that an 8-week fermented probiotic yoghurt intervention may modulate hippocampal functional connectivity in healthy women; we also provide preliminary evidence of beneficial effects on GSH levels and volume. Further studies are required to replicate these results in larger samples and within clinical populations where hippocampal-related pathology is relevant, such as in mental illness and cognitive impairment.
