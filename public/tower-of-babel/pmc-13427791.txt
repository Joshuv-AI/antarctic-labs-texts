Should Metastatic Site Inform First-Line Therapy in Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma?
Authors: Michael A Liu, Kyle Miyazaki, Karie Runcie, Hanna Cash, Lingbin Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrontline systemic therapy for metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has evolved to include doublet immunotherapy (IO/IO) and immunotherapy–tyrosine kinase inhibitor combinations (IO/TKI), with selection typically guided by histology and risk stratification. However, optimal regimen selection remains undefined owing to limited head-to-head data. Subanalyses of landmark clinical trials and emerging real-world multicenter study data suggest differential treatment benefit derived by metastatic site, particularly for bone metastases. Evidence suggests that bone metastases promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and may preferentially upregulate genes encoding angiogenic factors, compared with other sites. Such biology-driven patterns provide a rationale for the hypothesis that IO/TKI may confer greater activity in bone-predominant disease. Liver metastases may also exhibit similar site-specific patterns due to harboring an immunosuppressive and vascular environment, but RCC-specific data remain scarce. This overview summarizes the mechanistic rationale, clinical evidence, and practical implications of tailoring treatment by disease site, highlighting critical gaps as well as future research priorities.

Key Points

In this report, we examined how metastatic sites may influence the choice of initial treatment in metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma.Bone metastases harbor biological features that are hypothesized to favor targeted therapy over immunotherapy; this hypothesis is supported by preliminary and exploratory signals from clinical trial subgroup analyses and recent real-world data.Clinicians should consider metastatic site of RCC in addition to disease characteristics and patient preferences when selecting a frontline therapy option for patients.

Introduction

In the last decade, the treatment paradigm for patients with metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has improved significantly with the advent of doublet immunotherapy (IO/IO) and immunotherapy combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitor (IO/TKI) regimens. Since 2018, the positive results of the CheckMate 214 trial established the IO/IO regimen of ipilimumab combined with nivolumab in intermediate-to-poor-risk clear cell RCCs [1]. Subsequent landmark trials established the efficacy of frontline IO/TKI regimens, including cabozantinib and nivolumab, lenvatinib and pembrolizumab, and axitinib and pembrolizumab. Current international guidelines, including from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), and European Association of Urology (EAU), support use of either IO/IO or IO/TKI in the frontline setting for metastatic renal cell carcinoma [2]. However, owing to lack of head-to-head comparisons, no consensus exists on optimal therapy selection. An emerging body of evidence now suggests that metastatic site may be informative in guiding selection of frontline therapy, driven initially by exploratory subgroup analyses in landmark trials and now corroborated by newer real-world multicenter studies. Tumor biology at different metastatic sites may exhibit differential interactions with the mechanisms of treatments with immunotherapy and TKIs. Therefore, this overview synthesizes the mechanistic rationale, clinical evidence, and practical implications of using metastatic site to guide the treatment of clear cell metastatic RCC.

Current Framework for Frontline Therapy Selection

International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) risk stratification, which categorizes patients into favorable, intermediate, and poor risk on the basis of six clinical and laboratory values, is the most commonly used risk tool to derive patient benefit from therapy and is incorporated into the NCCN guidelines [3]. Historically, favorable-risk patients were directed toward TKI-based regimens. While prognostic, its role in predicting treatment is increasingly debated, specifically in terms of which patients benefit most from IO/IO versus IO/TKI, as long-term survival follow-up data now suggest that favorable-risk patients may also benefit from IO/IO [4]. This evolving landscape has led to a growing emphasis on other clinical factors, including disease burden, performance status, comorbidities, and the presence of sarcomatoid differentiation. Sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation is the most well-established biological predictor favoring response to IO/IO over VEGFR inhibitor therapy, based on a post hoc subset analysis of the CheckMate 214 study as well as meta-analyses demonstrating durable responses in this rare population [5, 6]. However, no validated predictive biomarker exists to guide treatment in the majority of patients who have non-sarcomatoid disease. While factors such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutation burden, and gene expression profiling have been explored, they have not demonstrated sufficient predictive clinical utility to be used routinely in practice [7, 8].

Rationale for Site-Specific Stratification

A key limitation is that the major combination therapy trials were compared against sunitinib rather than each other, making cross-trial hazard ratio comparisons challenging. Network meta-analyses have attempted to bridge this gap but are limited owing to the heterogeneity of populations and varying follow-up durations [9]. Therefore, the hypothesis that metastatic site may drive differential treatment response from the combination regimens holds appeal. Metastatic site of disease is already used as a prognostic marker in patients with RCC, where patients with bone or liver disease are associated with poorer prognosis and higher disease burden or symptom morbidity [10]. Experts from the 2025 EAU Annual Congress called for updated risk stratification models that moved beyond IMDC criteria, noting that the presence of bone or liver metastases in patients with metastatic RCC is associated with differing tumor microenvironments that may influence immunotherapy response [2]. With the accumulation of data from contemporary subanalyses of clinical trials and real-world clinical studies, evolving evidence suggests that the site of metastatic involvement may also have a predictive role in the selection of first-line therapy.

Biologic Basis for Site-Specific Treatment Response

The biological heterogeneity across metastatic sites among patients with RCC has been analyzed at the genomic, transcriptomic, and microenvironmental levels. Gulati et al. demonstrated that metastatic sites have unique molecular profiles distinct from the primary tumor, which can be reflected in differences in immunogenicity, angiogenic signaling, and stromal architecture [11]. Bone metastases in particular have unique biological features that may impact treatment response. First, unique immunosuppressive microenvironments result from the bone marrow niche enriched in regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and osteoclast-activating factors, which have been shown to attenuate response to immunotherapy and promote resistance [12]. Concurrently, genomic analyses demonstrate elevated angiogenesis gene signatures promoting upregulation of growth factors, including VEGF, PDGF, and TGF-α, compared with the profiles of other metastatic sites. The hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling pathway, the central molecular driver in clear cell RCC pathogenesis, may serve as an important mediator in these processes [13]. Furthermore, bone metastases display an angiogenic/stromal molecular subtype, and the microenvironment is made up of increased endothelial cells and monocytic lineages [11]. This biological framework supports the rationale for the differential sensitivity to systemic treatments, where the bone-immunosuppressive environment may hinder the efficacy of immunotherapy-based regimens, while the angiogenic and stromal features enhance susceptibility to TKI-based regimens. This paradigm has been further supported through clinical data from the BIONIKK trial, a prospective biomarker-guided trial in patients with metastatic RCC, which demonstrates that patients with a highly angiogenic phenotype responded more strongly to TKIs, while those with an immune-high phenotype responded preferentially to immunotherapy [14]. Additional prospective evidence was demonstrated in the OPTIC RCC trial, a phase II multicenter study that prospectively assigned patients with angiogenic gene expression signatures to cabozantinib and nivolumab and those with immune-enriched profiles to ipilimumab and nivolumab. Among those with angiogenic tumors, an objective response rate was observed in 76% of patients, and all patients experienced some reduction in tumor burden [15].

Liver metastases may also exhibit a unique immunosuppressive environment through different pathways, including recruitment of immunosuppressive macrophages and Kupffer cell-mediated immunosuppression, induction of antigen-specific T cell apoptosis in hepatic microenvironments, and high expression of immune checkpoint ligands [16]. These features, alongside the high vascular density and reliance on angiogenesis for liver metastatic lesions [17], provide a concurrent rationale for the preferential response to TKIs in patients with liver lesions, analogous but distinct from patients with bone metastases. In contrast, patients with lung metastases are thought to be more immunogenic, in part due to a higher proportion of PD-L1+ antigen-presenting cells and immune cell infiltration, as has been shown in patients with breast cancer [18]. However, data are scarcer regarding immune profiling, specifically in patients with RCC with lung metastatic disease.

An important complementary perspective is that certain inherent biological properties of a tumor may increase the likelihood of metastasis to specific disease sites. Specific molecular subtypes of clear cell RCC show preferential organotropism, or a tendency to spread to particular organs, driven by tumor-intrinsic properties such as angiogenic signaling, immune evasion capacity, and stromal composition [11, 14, 15]. Potentially, tumors with these properties may be more predisposed to metastasize to the bones or liver. This concept is exemplified by the glandular or indolent metastatic pattern seen in RCC, which has been associated with more favorable disease biology and behavior. If metastatic site of disease partially reflects underlying tumor molecular subtype, then differential treatment responses by site may also reflect the intrinsic biology of the seeding tumor.

Clinical Evidence from Trial Subgroup Analyses and Real-World Studies

The first signals of metastatic site-specific differential outcomes came from exploratory subgroup analyses from the landmark randomized clinical trials, although these trials were not prospectively designed with these specific hypotheses. CheckMate 214 established IO/IO as the preferred treatment compared with sunitinib in metastatic clear cell RCC owing to a demonstration of strong overall survival benefit. However, subgroup analyses suggested that this benefit applied mainly to patients with lung (hazard ratio [HR] 0.77, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.64–0.93) or liver (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54–1.00) metastases (Table 1). Patients with RCC with bone metastases did not demonstrate a statistically significant overall survival advantage (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.61–1.11), and progression-free survival appeared to mildly favor sunitinib (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.76–1.51) [4] in this subgroup. In contrast, the subgroup analyses for patients with bone-metastatic disease in trials evaluating patients receiving IO/TKI showed a consistent and very strong survival benefit. In CheckMate 9ER, patients with bone metastases who received nivolumab and cabozantinib exhibited superior survival compared with those who received sunitinib (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.45–0.95) [19]. Similarly, the CLEAR trial reported a numerically favorable overall survival trend in patients with bone metastases who received lenvatinib and pembrolizumab (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.44–1.02) and a significant progression-free survival benefit (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.33–0.77) [20, 21]. It is important to acknowledge the limitations of these analyses, as subgroup analyses can be subject to imbalanced patient baseline characteristics, increased chance of a type 1 error due to multiple comparisons, and varying degrees of the magnitude of bone metastases. While these trials were not powered to detect this level of granularity, these preliminary findings suggested the possibility for a differential benefit of IO/IO and IO/TKI on the basis of predominance of bone metastases.

Recent real-world multicenter retrospective studies within the last year have corroborated these exploratory trial findings. A newly published multicenter analysis of patients with bone-predominant metastatic RCC showed a significantly longer median overall survival advantage for patients treated with TKIs compared with patients receiving immunotherapy (41.3 months versus 19.3 months) [22]. Additionally, a separate new cohort study across seven French centers showed an absolute difference in the time to next treatment of 17.9 months compared with 8.5 months for IO/TKI versus IO/IO in patients with bone-metastatic RCC, although this was not statistically significant, in the setting of limited power and sample size [23]. Further supporting evidence was recently published in a large multicenter retrospective analysis of 579 patients with intermediate or poor risk metastatic RCC, which compared IO/IO with IO/TKI therapy, stratified simultaneously by metastatic site. The authors found that patients with bone and liver metastases had significantly greater benefit from IO/TKI than IO/IO, while those with lung, brain, and lymph node metastases had equivalent benefits from either regimen [24]. This study provided the most granular site-specific comparisons to date. These site-specific differences appear to extend to the refractory setting in patients with metastatic RCC, where a multicenter study compared second-line nivolumab with TKIs [25]. No significant differences in overall survival were observed between patients receiving nivolumab compared with TKIs, but patients with bone metastases had significantly higher overall response rates to TKI therapy. Patients treated with second-line nivolumab had an overall response rate of only 4.2% compared with 29.2% for the TKI group. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed that the bone lesions exhibited significantly higher VEGFR2 expression than their corresponding primary tumors, again supporting the biological basis for the improved sensitivity to TKIs in this subgroup. This study raises the hypothesis that bone-metastatic disease may confer a durable state of immunotherapy resistance across multiple treatment lines.

The majority of site-specific data have focused on bone metastases, but liver metastases may represent an emerging signal. These lesions are associated with a poorer prognosis, and the immunosuppressive environment may attenuate the effects of immunotherapy. The retrospective analysis by Toyoda et al. [24] did suggest that liver metastases are a second site where IO/TKI performs superior to IO/IO. However, other analyses, including data from CheckMate 214 [4], do not suggest a diminished efficacy of IO/IO in this setting, and retrospective data showed that second-line nivolumab maintained a strong overall response rate of 50.0%, much higher than that reported for bone metastases [25]. This suggests that the degree of immunosuppression in liver metastases may be distinct from that in bone disease, but this remains speculative. Whether liver metastases should be used to guide clinical decision-making in the same way as bone metastases remains uncertain owing to the mixed clinical evidence for a differential response to IO/TKIs. Notably, in clinical practice, liver metastases are often regarded as a marker of aggressive and high-burden visceral disease; thus, oncologists may favor IO/TKI regimens owing to the more rapid response kinetics, and this may also bias some of the real-world datasets. Further prospective data are needed to clarify whether liver metastases alone should independently guide frontline treatment decisions.

Clinical Implications

The site of metastatic disease should not be used in isolation when deciding between IO/IO versus IO/TKI, and other factors such as IMDC risk score, volume of disease, disease histology, comorbidities and side effect profiles, frailty, and patient preferences should still be considered. However, the accumulation of biological and recent clinical data suggests that patients with bone metastases from RCC may benefit more strongly and have more durable remissions from IO/TKI than IO/IO. The choice of IO/TKI backbone may also hold impact, for example, cabozantinib’s additional inhibition of MET and AXL may influence osteoclast and osteoblast function [26], and it has been hypothesized that this multi-kinase profile confers additional activity in bone metastases [24], although this requires further evaluation. Response kinetics should also be considered, as patients with rapid progression in weight-bearing regions or spinal disease tend to carry high morbidity and mortality burden, and TKI-based regimens tend to act faster. The delayed response kinetics of IO/IO may be particularly consequential in patients who have impending risk of skeletal events such as pathological fractures or cord compression. In addition to initiation of systemic treatment, multidisciplinary management involving radiation oncology, orthopedics, palliative care, and physical therapy may be essential to comanaging these patients [27]. Patients with bone metastases should be considered for prophylactic or palliative radiotherapy, which may improve survival or symptom burden [28, 29]. Orthopedic surgery evaluation should be considered in those with weight-bearing bones that meet criteria for prophylactic fixation [30]. Additionally, bone-modifying agent initiation should be considered concurrently with systemic therapy to reduce the incidence of skeletal-related events [31]. Finally, early palliative care input is important and has demonstrated improvements in quality of life, functional status, and goal-concordant decision-making [32].

Limitations and Future Directions

Several methodological limitations must be considered in the context of the observational data supporting metastatic site-guided treatment selection. These data are inherently subject to confounding owing to factors such as selection bias of the treatment regimens used, differences of time period and follow-up durations, heterogeneity of imaging review sources, and the differences in criteria for bone metastases. While the collective data reported here across multiple multicenter studies are promising, future prospective studies with prespecified metastatic-site stratification are necessary to confirm findings. Additionally, several crucial questions remain unanswered. First, it is unclear whether any particular IO/TKI regimen may confer additional benefit to bone metastatic sites, for example, whether cabozantinib’s unique multi-kinase profile, inhibiting MET and AXL in addition to VEGFR, provides additional activity to these sites [33]. Notably, the KEYNOTE-426 trial of axitinib and pembrolizumab establishing one of the four approved IO/TKI regimens did not report metastatic site subgroup data in a granular fashion as with the other trials; thus, data are limited on this regimen [34]. Second, the minimum threshold of bone disease to impact decision-making to include TKI’s is not well established. Third, it is uncertain whether these findings can be extrapolated to patients with non-clear cell histologies given the majority of patients in these studies have clear cell RCC. Lastly, it is uncertain whether biomarkers such as VEGFR expression profiles and angiogenesis gene signatures play a predictive role in guiding personalized treatment decisions in metastatic RCC.

Conclusions

Current evidence remains limited by the observational and retrospective nature of the summarized data. However, the consistency of the new observational studies with prior landmark trial subgroup analyses, supported by data from biological and genomic profiling, supports the hypothesis of differential treatment outcomes for metastatic clear cell RCC based on metastatic site of disease. As real-world datasets and future prospective trials evolve to incorporate site-based stratification, the evidence base will continue to mature and guide future care. In the interim, consideration of disease site, especially the presence of bone metastases, when selecting frontline therapy for clear cell metastatic RCC may help to inform treatment options in this patient population.
