Rapid in situ range testing for acoustic telemetry in Leizhou Bay, South China Sea
Authors: Shaoliang Lyu, Hagai Nsobi Lauden, Lifei Wang, Kun Lin, Guobao Chen, Jianyu Dong, Ning Chen, Xuefeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acoustic telemetry is widely used to study the movement and habitat use of marine animals, but its effectiveness depends greatly on the appropriate receiver spacing and the local environmental context. In practice, when time or logistical resources are limited, short-term range tests, if carefully planned and interpreted, can provide rapid and valuable deployment guidance. Here, we conducted a 24-h in situ range test in a subtropical bay as a practical case study and reference workflow for planning receiver deployment before animal tracking studies. Two transmitters with different power outputs were deployed, and a generalized additive mixed model was applied to assess how environmental factors influenced detection proportion. The results showed that the effective detection range (50% detection proportion) was 170.4 m for the high-power transmitter and 114.3 m for the low-power transmitter. Mean detection proportion at nighttime was significantly higher than at daytime for both transmitter types. Distance to transmitter and water temperature significantly affected both transmitter types, while other factors, such as hour of day, wind, water depth, dissolved oxygen, background noise, and tilt angle, showed transmitter-specific effects. This study was not intended to develop universal predictive models from the 24-h dataset, but rather to determine appropriate receiver spacing and demonstrate how environmental variables can be incorporated to interpret rapid range test results under local conditions. The workflow provides a replicable and efficient approach for short-term range testing and data interpretation, offering practical guidance for optimizing acoustic telemetry deployment in similar environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23724-2.

Introduction

Globally, the decline of marine fisheries resources has not been alleviated1, and coastal ecosystems are facing unprecedented challenges from multiple stressors2,3. In our efforts to conserve marine biodiversity, sustain fisheries, and support coastal livelihoods, effective management of marine organisms and their habitats has become crucial4. Consequently, obtaining detailed and accurate information on the movement patterns and habitat use of marine organisms is fundamental for developing appropriate management strategies, such as establishing protected areas5. However, existing monitoring methods (e.g., fishing gear, underwater diving) capture only momentary snapshots of dynamic ecosystems6. Likewise, conventional mark-recapture methods provide only limited endpoint data (i.e., time and location at initial tagging or subsequent recapture)7. While valuable, these methods fall short of delivering the continuous, high-resolution data needed for advanced coastal ecosystem management.

Fortunately, acoustic telemetry, also known as ultrasonic telemetry, offers a solution by enabling continuous tracking of a wide range of marine animals from migratory species to sedentary species within their natural habitats (e.g., tunas8,9, salmons10,11, snappers12,13, seabreams14,15, flatfish16,17, lobsters18,19, crabs20,21, squids22,23, octopuses24,25, sea urchins26, sea stars27, and abalones28). In acoustic telemetry systems, transmitters attached to animals emit coded acoustic signals that are detected by underwater receivers29. To maximize receiver performance, determining the appropriate spacing between receivers is a critical first step in array design30. Detection range varies across locations due to complex and heterogeneous marine environments, and it is therefore widely acknowledged that conducting in situ range testing before using this system for animal tracking is an essential part of pre-deployment planning31. In practice, when time or resources are limited, short-term range tests are often used to rapidly estimate the effective detection range under representative field conditions. While longer-term range tests may yield more precise estimates, short-term range tests are often sufficient and preferable for providing practical deployment guidance without delaying subsequent tracking studies. Importantly, even such quick assessments require careful planning, systematic data collection, and contextual interpretation to ensure meaningful results.

A receiver′s ability to detect transmitter signals is influenced by numerous factors, such as equipment specifications (e.g., transmitter power output, with high-power transmitters generally achieving greater detection ranges than low-power transmitters) and environmental conditions (e.g., water temperature, salinity, currents, tides, wind, substrate, background noise)32–34. This is particularly relevant in bay ecosystems, which act as interfaces between marine and terrestrial environments, exhibit high environmental variability, and often serve as spawning and nursery grounds for many marine animals. In addition to determining the appropriate receiver spacing, optimizing deployment requires integrating both equipment-related and environmental considerations to maximize detection performance35. Such knowledge enables researchers to strategically plan deployments according to local conditions, thereby improving detection efficiency and data quality. For example, choosing an appropriate transmitter power output and increasing the number of receivers in areas strongly influenced by environmental factors can enhance overall performance. Neglecting these factors risks misinterpreting detection data, leading to inaccurate animal behavioral inferences in the subsequent tracking studies and potentially misguided management decisions36.

Despite their importance, practical field-based examples that jointly evaluate receiver spacing and key influencing factors remain scarce, particularly in Chinese coastal waters. This study addresses that gap by presenting a rapid in situ range test in a subtropical bay as a practical case study and reference workflow for pre-deployment planning. Specifically, we compare transmitters with two power outputs and analyze environmental data to explain variability in detection proportion during a 24-h deployment. Rather than aiming to develop universally applicable predictive models from this single short-term dataset, our goal is to determine appropriate receiver spacing and demonstrate how environmental information can be incorporated to interpret rapid range test results under local conditions. Such rapid assessments, while limited in duration, can still provide timely, practical guidance for receiver deployment and serve as methodological references for similar studies.

Materials and methods

Study area and rationale for 24-h test duration

Leizhou Bay (Fig. 1), a semi-enclosed bay located on the eastern coast of Leizhou Peninsula in China, covers an area of approximately 900 km2 with water depths ranging from 8 to 28 m37. It is bordered to the north by Donghai Island, to the east by Naozhou Island, and opens towards the southeast. The bay experiences irregular semi-diurnal tides with strong tidal currents and serves both as a nursery for many juvenile fishery species and as habitat for the world′s second-largest population of the endangered Indo‐Pacific humpback dolphins (Sousa chinensis)38,39. In recent years, the expansion of land-based pond and cage aquaculture, together with the establishment of heavy industrial complexes (e.g., steel and chemical production) on Donghai Island, has increased ecological pressures40. The seabed substrate in the study area consists mainly of fine sand and silt. The range test was conducted over a full 24-h period to capture an entire diel cycle, providing a rapid yet representative assessment of detection performance under both day and night conditions. This duration was chosen to balance representativeness with operational feasibility: longer tests could yield slightly more precise estimates, but would delay subsequent tracking studies and increase logistical costs.

Equipment and deployment

Two range test transmitters with different power outputs and eight receivers were deployed near the mouth of Leizhou Bay, where the average water depth is 12 m. The transmitters were Innovasea (formerly Vemco) V13-1H (high power, 152 dB re 1 μPa at 1 m) and V13-1L (low power, 147 dB re 1 μPa at 1 m), hereafter referred to as V13H and V13L, respectively. Both transmitters were programmed with a fixed delay of 10 s (i.e., each transmitter emitted a signal every 10 s). The short delay was selected to maximize the number of transmissions within the 24-h test. Although the delay interval was the same, V13H and V13L were activated at different times to stagger emission cycles and minimize the risk of simultaneous transmissions or signal collisions.

V13H and V13L were spaced 0.2 m apart vertically and secured with cable ties to a nylon mooring rope so that they were suspended 7 m above the seabed. The rope was anchored to the seabed with a concrete block and held vertically by a surface buoy. Eight Innovasea VR2Tx receivers were deployed using the same anchoring method, each fixed 7 m above the seabed for consistency with transmitter depth. This mid-water placement was chosen to reduce the influence of seabed reflections and surface wave interference. The receivers were positioned along a straight transect extending from the transmitters at 50-m intervals, starting at 50 m and ending at 400 m (Fig. 1). The 400-m limit was chosen based on a preliminary range test at the same site, which showed detections beyond this distance were rare. All equipment was deployed on 29 April 2018 and retrieved on 30 April 2018.

Environmental variables monitoring

During the 24-h test, environmental measurements were taken from an anchored research vessel (engine off) positioned approximately 300 m from the deployment site. Given the relatively small spatial extent of the study area and its uniform seabed topography, measurements taken at the vessel location were assumed to represent conditions across all receiver positions.

Environmental variables were recorded hourly using dedicated instruments (Table 1). Specifically, water temperature, salinity, pH, and dissolved oxygen at deployment depth (7 m above the seabed) were measured with a YSI Pro Plus multiparameter meter. Although VR2Tx receivers record internal temperature, YSI measurements were used to ensure standardized accuracy and comparability. Wind speed and direction were measured using a handheld anemometer. Water depth, representing tidal variation, was measured using a Speedtech SM-5A depth meter. According to the manufacturer′s manual (www.innovasea.com), background noise represents the mean broadband acoustic energy level at the receiver′s operating frequency, measured by the VR2Tx internal hydrophone and preamplifier. It was quantified in millivolts (mV) as the root mean square voltage of the signal during periods without valid detections, obtained from the receiver′s internal noise diagnostics. Higher values indicate stronger underwater acoustic energy at that frequency band, which may arise from waves, rain, vessel activity, or biological sounds. Tilt angle refers to the angle between the receiver′s transducer axis and the vertical direction, as measured by the VR2Tx internal tilt sensor, and is defined relative to the vertical axis (0° = upright, 90° = horizontal). Tilt angle values may vary over time due to water currents acting on the mooring rope.

Statistical analysis

Detection proportion, range, and diel variation

Analyses were conducted using data from 06:00 on 29 April to 05:59 on 30 April, representing a complete diel cycle. For analysis, daytime was defined as 06:00–17:59 and nighttime as 18:00–05:59.

Detection range was defined as the distance at which a given proportion of transmissions was detected31, with the effective detection range being taken as the distance corresponding to a 50% detection proportion42. Hourly detection proportion for each receiver was calculated as the number of observed detections divided by the expected number (i.e., observed detections/360)43, based on the 10 s fixed transmission delay.

One-way ANOVA was used to compare differences in detection proportion among receivers for high and low-power transmitters; when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied instead. Additionally, paired-sample t tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests when assumptions were violated) were used to compare differences in detection proportion between high and low-power transmitters, as well as between daytime and nighttime7. To evaluate whether diel detectability could be partly explained by ambient conditions, paired-sample t tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests when assumptions were violated) were used to compare differences in background noise between daytime and nighttime. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Because detection events are binary (detected or not detected), a logistic regression model was fitted to predict detection proportion at different distances, and to estimate appropriate receiver spacing31. Model fit was examined by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, where P > 0.05 indicates an adequate fit44.

Generalized additive mixed model

A generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) was used to estimate the influence of environmental variables, temporal variable (hour of day), and spatial variable (distance to the transmitter) on detection proportion (response variable)45.

Prior to modeling, multicollinearity among variables was checked using variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF > 3 were excluded (VIF < 3 indicating no collinearity) to reduce redundancy and improve model stability46. The final model was a full model, in which all explanatory variables retained after the collinearity check were included simultaneously. This approach was chosen to allow direct comparison of the relative effects of all predictors. Receiver ID was included as a random effect to account for non-independence among receivers47. A beta distribution with logit link was applied to accommodate proportional data bounded between 0 and 1. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R (R Core Team) within the RStudio environment.

Results

Detection proportion and range

Detection proportion decreased significantly with increasing distance from the transmitters (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.05), regardless of the transmitter′s power output (Fig. 2). Across most distances, the high-power transmitter (V13H) maintained a significantly higher detection proportion than the low-power transmitter (V13L) (Paired-sample t-test, P < 0.05). Interestingly, at close range (< 76.2 m), the detection proportion for V13L showed a slight advantage over V13H, but this reversed at 76.2 m, beyond which V13H consistently outperformed V13L. Diel variation

Over the 24-h cycle, the mean detection proportion at nighttime was significantly higher than at daytime (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05), regardless of the transmitter′s power output (Fig. 3). For V13H, the mean detection proportion decreased from 42.6% at nighttime to 34.6% at daytime. For V13L, the mean detection proportion decreased from 28.1% at nighttime to 18.4% at daytime. Salinity and pH (VIF > 3) were excluded from the analysis due to collinearity, with salinity positively correlated with temperature and pH negatively correlated with dissolved oxygen. No collinearity was detected among the remaining variables. The explanatory variables that significantly affected detection proportion differed between high and low-power output transmitters. Specifically, for V13H, all variables except tilt angle and dissolved oxygen had significant effects on detection proportion (GAMM, P < 0.05) (Table 2). Hour of day was the most influential predictor, with a higher detection proportion observed at nighttime (Fig. 5). The second most significant variable was wind direction, where a higher detection proportion occurred near 0° (i.e., northerly winds). The third key variable was distance to transmitter, with detection proportion decreasing nonlinearly as distance increased. Water temperature and wind speed were also significant, with the detection proportion increasing when the temperature was above 26.2℃ and when the wind speed was greater than 4.0 m/s. Discussion

Acoustic telemetry is a commonly used technology for tracking marine animals, and understanding detection proportion and its key drivers is essential for designing and implementing effective tracking studies31. While acoustic telemetry has been applied in several studies on fish movement in China5,48, this is, to our knowledge, the first to focus specifically on an in situ range test and its environmental drivers. Our study provides a practical workflow to guide the implementation of an in situ range test.

The primary objective of a range test is to determine the detection range, which in turn informs the reasonable deployment distance between receivers and serves as a basis for subsequent animal tracking research31. While a 50% detection proportion is often used as the “effective detection range”, this threshold is not absolute and can be adjusted according to study objectives41,42. If researchers aim to increase detection probability, above 50% of detection proportion can be selected to set the distance between receivers. Conversely, if the aim is to extend the detection range, below 50% of detection proportion may be chosen. In our study, the effective detection range (50% detection proportion) was 170.4 m for V13H (152 dB) and 114.3 m for V13L (147 dB), reflecting a difference of 56.1 m. This difference (approximately 11.2 m per dB) may help inform transmitter power selection, but it is likely context-dependent across sites and conditions.

In theory, detection proportion of the high-power transmitter should be higher than low-power transmitter due to the greater underwater transmission capacity of high-power49. Our results support this, as the mean detection proportion for V13H was significantly higher than V13L. However, within 76.2 m, the detection proportion for V13H was lower than V13L. This may be attributed to a phenomenon called Close Proximity Detection Interference (CPDI)35. When the transmitter and receiver are too close (e.g. < 76.2 m), strong reflections may cause signal overlap or collisions, which can interfere with the receiver′s ability to detect the actual signal and the detection proportion can be low. A previous study has shown that the extent affected by CPDI increases with the transmitter power output and depth50. Therefore, when tracking sedentary animals, careful consideration of transmitter power is needed, as CPDI can reduce detection at close range, whereas it is less problematic for migratory animals45. Generally, the higher battery life required for high-power output transmitters means that they are typically larger in size49. We suggest that low-power output transmitters are preferred for small sedentary animals, while high-power output transmitters are preferred for large migratory animals.

GAMM results revealed that distance to transmitter and water temperature were significant predictors for both V13H and V13L, likely due to the propagation properties of sound underwater, where acoustic signals nonlinearly attenuate with increasing distance36, while temperature influences signal refraction and absorption via changes in water density51. Tilt angle was not significant for both V13H and V13L. According to the manufacturer′s description, if the tilt angle becomes too extreme, the receivers’ view may be obstructed, potentially leading to detection failure52. In our study, the tilt angle of receivers ranged from 4 to 29°, likely because the fluctuations in this angle did not significantly affect the receivers′ detection.

Mean detection proportion at nighttime (18:00–5:59) was significantly higher than that at daytime (6:00–17:59), contrary to several multi-day studies43,47,53. A likely explanation is reduced nighttime vessel activity in our study area. This interpretation is supported by our day-night comparison of background noise, which showed significantly lower levels at nighttime (314.2 mV) than at daytime (342.7 mV). While background noise includes physical (e.g., water flow)52, biological (e.g., biotic activity)53, and anthropogenic (e.g., boat traffic) sources, boat traffic likely contributed most to this diel difference. Notably, the phenomenon of zero detections for several hours mainly occurred during the daytime, and it is highly likely that the higher noise levels during the day interfered with signal transmission, though equipment malfunction can not be entirely ruled out.

Tidal depth changes can alter propagation by modifying water column height and current patterns54. Our study area experiences irregular semi-diurnal tides, making tidal changes more likely to affect detection proportion. Wind direction effects may be explained by partial sheltering from land and islands: detections were lowest during southerly winds, which face fewer natural barriers. Furthermore, detection proportion increased slightly with wind speed for V13H, although higher wind speeds are generally expected to reduce detection rates by increasing surface turbulence and ambient noise31, our results indicated the opposite pattern. This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively narrow wind speed range (2.1–7.2 m/s) observed during the study, which may not have included sufficiently high values to produce the anticipated negative effect. Prior work shows that microbubbles (e.g., from macrophytes or high total gas saturation) can alter the transmission of acoustic signals43. In our study, a negative association between dissolved oxygen and detectability was observed only for the low-power output transmitter and within a narrow dissolved oxygen range (7.89–8.18 mg/L), which may be consistent with such processes. Given the 24-h window and limited variation, we regard this finding as exploratory rather than causal. Confirming this will require longer, multi-season deployments with direct measurements of gas supersaturation and models that include transmitter power × environment interactions.

We support the call to conduct range tests before all acoustic telemetry studies31. Even a 24-h test can provide valuable, site-specific insights into optimal receiver spacing and identify key influencing factors. Nevertheless, relationships between detection proportion and influencing factors derived from such short-term data should be interpreted with caution. For studies aiming to establish broadly applicable relationships, longer-term monitoring covering multiple diel cycles and seasons is recommended, ideally incorporating additional variables (e.g., data from automatic oceanographic buoys)55 and developing predictive models that integrate multiple factors. Such efforts would help correct detection bias and enhance the reliability of animal movement inferences56.

This study was a short, site-specific range test, and thus has several limitations. First, observations cover a single 24-h window at one location, so environmental gradients were narrow (e.g., limited ranges of wind speed and dissolved oxygen). Consequently, the findings are not broadly generalizable and should be interpreted with caution. Second, the diel contrast is inferred partly from a proxy for background noise; vessel traffic intensity was not measured directly, and other physical or biological sources may have contributed. Periods of zero detections occurred mainly during the daytime; while elevated background noise is a plausible driver, equipment malfunction can not be entirely excluded. Third, the short-range reduction in detections for the high-power transmitter is consistent with close proximity detection interference (CPDI), but CPDI was not tested experimentally in this study and should therefore be viewed as suggestive rather than causal. Finally, the GAMM results are primarily explanatory for this dataset; there was no external validation, and estimates may differ under broader seasons, sites, or transmitter-power × environment combinations. Longer, multi-season deployments with direct measurements of anthropogenic noise and expanded environmental ranges are needed to assess transferability.

Conclusion

A well-designed 24-h in situ range test can provide rapid, site-specific guidance for acoustic telemetry deployment. In Leizhou Bay, the effective detection range (50% detection proportion) was 170.4 m for the high-power transmitter (V13H, 152 dB) and 114.3 m for the low-power transmitter (V13L, 147 dB). Detection performance varied with distance to the transmitter and water temperature for both transmitters, and diel patterns showed higher nighttime detectability. Several predictors exhibited transmitter-specific effects, and the short-range reduction in detections for the high-power transmitter is consistent with close proximity detection interference (CPDI). The workflow presented in this study offers a clear and replicable framework for selecting receiver spacing and interpreting detection data under local conditions.

When time and logistical resources are constrained, a short-term range test can be sufficient to guide deployment decisions. Nonetheless, to improve transferability and predictive utility, future work should: (1) extend sampling across multiple diel cycles and seasons; (2) directly quantify anthropogenic noise (e.g., vessel traffic) and dissolved gas supersaturation; and (3) develop and externally validate predictive models that incorporate power × environment interactions with additional oceanographic data streams.
