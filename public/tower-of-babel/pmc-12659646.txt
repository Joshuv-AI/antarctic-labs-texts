Crural stitch technique for liver retraction in laparoscopic upper gastrointestinal surgery: a retrospective case series
Authors: Oussama Shibly, Maher Salloum, Georges Sakhat, Philippe Attieh, Rawan Abdallah, Khaled Al Jamal, Mustafa Allouch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective liver retraction is essential for laparoscopic upper gastrointestinal (GI) surgeries. Traditional methods often require additional trocars, specialized instruments, and assistant support, which can increase procedural cost, operative time, and risk of liver trauma. This study presents a simple, cost-effective, and safe technique for liver retraction using a crural stitch without the need for additional incisions or equipment.MethodsThis retrospective single-surgeon, single-center case series included 1,956 patients who underwent various laparoscopic upper GI procedures at Nini Hospital, Lebanon, between 2011 and 2022. The liver retraction technique involved a 0-silk suture placed through the right anterior crura, exteriorized through a 2 mm incision using an ENDOCLOSE device, and secured externally with a clamp. Patient records were reviewed to assess the incidence of complications specifically related to the retraction method.ResultsThe technique was successfully used in all patients regardless of liver size or steatosis status. No complications, including hepatic injury or infection, were attributed to the liver retraction method across the spectrum of procedures, including sleeve gastrectomy, gastric bypass, hiatal hernia repair, myotomy for achalasia, and others.ConclusionThe described crural stitch technique provides a safe, reproducible, and atraumatic alternative for liver retraction in laparoscopic upper GI surgeries. It eliminates the need for extra ports and instruments, reduces operating costs, and minimizes complications. Further prospective, multi-center studies are warranted to validate these findings and determine broader applicability.Keywords: Laparoscopic surgery, Liver retraction, Crural stitch technique, Upper gastrointestinal surgery, Minimally invasive surgery

Background

It has long been documented that fewer laparoscopic incisions are associated with improved post-operative pain, less ileus, less ventral herniations and faster recovery times [1]. One element of most laparoscopic upper gastrointestinal surgeries which often requires an additional incision is liver retraction, as an additional trocar needs to be inserted to allow adequate retraction and exposure of the operative site, especially when operating on the stomach. This is especially pertinent in patients with large left lobes, such as patients with hepatic steatosis and those with a high body mass index [1]. For these reasons, several groups have described multiple techniques of liver retraction over the years, all while aiming to reduce iatrogenic trauma to the liver, provide adequate exposure, operative times and reduce the number of ports. Several methods have been developed to achieve this goal, such as intracorporeal retractors, graspers, tape, and even nasogastric tubes. Another method that has been documented is the use of sutures to suspend the left lobe of the liver [1]. Several methods of suturing have been described, although not extensively, but good outcomes have been reported. We describe a technique involving a right crural stitch, exteriorized without the need of an additional trocar, through a 2 mm incision, with the suture held outside the abdomen with a Kelly clamp.

Methods

This is a single-center, single-surgeon retrospective case series conducted at Nini Hospital in Tripoli, Lebanon. The study includes 1956 patients treated from 2011 to 2022. The sampled patients underwent a variety of laparoscopic procedures, with selected patients undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, laparoscopic bypass surgery, laparoscopic hiatal hernia repairs, laparoscopic surgery for achalasia, as well as laparoscopic insertion and removal of gastric bands.

All patients provided their informed consent for their de-identified data to be included in a study.

The medical database of our center was used to identify patients who had undergone laparoscopic surgery for which the crural stitch method was used.

The patients were categorized according to the type of surgery they underwent and whether complications occurred. With all included patients, the technique presented in this article was used to retract the liver, irrespective of the liver size and/steatosis status.

Potential hepatic injury was assessed postoperatively through routine clinical evaluation and laboratory monitoring, including liver function tests (LFTs), conducted during follow-up visits. All patients were assessed in clinic two weeks postoperatively for wound and clinical status, and most were followed for an average duration of two months. No patients showed clinical or biochemical signs of hepatic injury attributable to the liver retraction technique. A subset of patients, particularly those who underwent bariatric procedures, had longer follow-up as part of their routine postoperative care; however, for consistency, outcomes in this study were reported based on the standardized two-month follow-up period.

In a few cases with a markedly enlarged or redundant left lobe, additional retraction maneuvers were necessary. These were still performed using the same crural stitch technique and did not require conversion to mechanical retraction. These patients were included to demonstrate the adaptability of the method. No subgroup analysis was conducted due to the small number of such cases and the absence of complications in this group.

Surgical technique

The following suture technique offers versatility and adjustability, allowing for effective liver retraction which is crucial for proper visualization and safe completion of surgical procedure. Additionally, in well trained hands, it requires less than 2 minutes to achieve adequate retraction.

After gaining access to the upper abdomen, a full-length 0-silk suture on a straight or curved needle is introduced through one of the working ports. Under direct laparoscopic vision, the left lateral segment of the liver (segment II–III) is gently elevated (Fig. 1) using the atraumatic tip of a grasper to expose the right diaphragmatic crus. A single stitch is then placed through the anterior aspect of the right crus (Fig. 2), approximately 2–3 cm below the esophageal hiatus, taking care not to include any liver tissue or phrenic vessels.

Once the stitch is secured on the crura, the needle is cut off intracorporeally. An ENDOCLOSE suture-passing device is introduced percutaneously through a small 2 mm skin puncture, just below the xiphoid process or slightly to the right, and is used to grasp and exteriorize both ends of the silk suture (Fig. 3). The threads are then pulled upward gently to lift the left lobe of the liver, thereby providing wide exposure of the gastroesophageal junction and the upper stomach (Fig. 4). This method provides a stable and adjustable retraction without the need for an additional trocar or mechanical retractor. At the end of the operation, the suture are released, withdrawn through the same puncture site, and discarded. The 2 mm puncture is small enough do not require closure or suturing.

Except for occasional incidental needle pricks to liver, the Glisson’s capsule was never breached by the threads even in cases of massive steatosis. In a few cases, additional liver retraction was needed in redundant lateral lobes.

Results

Demographics

A total of 1956 patients from the lead surgeon’s practice were included for analysis. Patients were not categorized based on gender, age or ethnicity.

Complications

All included patients were followed up in clinics at 2 weeks post-op for wound assessment and the mean post-operative follow-up period was 2 months depending on the procedure (Table 1). We found that zero patients suffered from any significant complication related to the described liver retraction procedure.

Discussion

Retraction of the left lobe of the liver is necessary for laparoscopic bariatric surgeries in order to view and operate on the esophagus, stomach, and other tissues that are located beneath the liver. The traditional method of retracting the liver involves making a second subxiphoid incision and port, as well as using a mechanical retractor that is handled by an assistant. Since the launch of laparoscopic bariatric surgery, medical professionals have worked to reduce the level of invasiveness associated with these procedures while also increasing their efficiency. Without the need for an additional incision, trocar, or assistance, the suture-based approach outlined herein provides an effective and unexpensive means for liver retraction during a range of upper abdominal surgeries including patients with morbid obesity. As mentioned, mechanical retractors such as the Nathanson, fan-shaped, or snake retractors are used for traditional liver retraction during laparoscopic surgeries. A number of substitute techniques for laparoscopic liver retraction have been reported. These include liver suspension with silicone disks, a Penrose drain and J-shaped retractor or stapler, a Jackson-Pratt drain with sutures, a retractor hook with a bulldog clamp, cyanoacrylate glue, and suture-based techniques involving gauze or liver lobe piercing [2–5]. The main drawback of these methods is that they lengthen the duration of the procedure and call for supplementary supplies and equipment [6].

Emerging evidence suggests that using mechanical retractors is linked to increases in hepatic enzyme levels and slower recovery compared to non-mechanical liver retraction methods [3, 7–11]. To the support this statement, a retrospective study conducted by Ertekin et al., compared the long surgical gauze technique with the traditional Nathanson retractor for liver retraction in laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy performed between January and December 2023. Patients were divided into two groups based on the retraction method used. Key outcomes included postoperative pain (VAS scores), analgesic use, liver enzyme levels (AST, ALT), CRP, and recovery markers. The long surgical gauze technique group experienced significantly lower pain scores, required less analgesia (p < 0.001), and had lower postoperative AST, ALT, and CRP levels, suggesting reduced hepatic stress. Additionally, these patients achieved earlier mobilization, aligning with Enhanced Recovery After Surgery principles [12]. Furthermore, another retrospective study compared a double-sling suture liver retraction method with the liver puncture technique in 189 patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrectomy for gastric adenocarcinoma. The DSS group showed significantly shorter operation and liver retraction times, along with lower postoperative liver enzyme elevations, despite having higher American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status scores. No retraction-related complications occurred in either group. The findings suggest that the DSS method is a rapid, safe, and effective alternative for liver retraction in upper abdominal laparoscopic surgery [13].

A rare but reported complication is hepatic infarction along with gas formation in liver parenchyma due to medial retraction of the left lobe of the liver while performing laparoscopic gastrectomy, with only one case reported in the literature [14].

Furthermore, vascular injury to the liver has been linked to compression of the left lateral segment of the liver, especially from below. Incidence of congestion, parenchymal rupture, and liver necrosis have been documented following mechanical liver retraction, regardless of the rarity of severe liver damage [14–16]. Innovative methods, such as hepatic left lateral lobe inversion and suture suspension were proven to reduce injury and ischemia compared to mechanical retractors [17].

In comparison to conventional liver retraction using mechanical retractors (e.g., Nathanson or fan retractors), the crural stitch technique demonstrated clear advantages in our series. Traditionally, such retractors necessitate the use of an additional subxiphoid trocar, bringing the average number of ports to four in laparoscopic upper GI procedures, whereas all procedures in our study were successfully completed with only three trocars. This reduction in trocar number translates into smaller total incision burden, potentially lowering postoperative pain, wound complications, and port-site herniation risks. Furthermore, none of the 1,956 patients in this series experienced hepatic injury, infection, or device-related complications, contrasting with previous reports describing retractor-associated transaminitis, hepatic congestion, or parenchymal tears. Therefore, our findings underline that the crural stitch method not only simplifies the operative setup and eliminates the need for an additional assistant or specialized device, but also appears to reduce postoperative complications related to retraction trauma.

Several suture-based liver retraction techniques have also been described in the literature, such as the Dima Stitch introduced by Dima Havaleshko and the various suspension methods reviewed by Palaivleu et al. for single-incision laparoscopic surgery. Although these techniques also aim to minimize port use, they often involve transfixing the liver parenchyma or using intermediary materials—such as pledgets, tapes, or silicone disks—to suspend the liver [18]. Such methods carry an inherent risk of minor bleeding, parenchymal injury, or transient increases in hepatic enzymes [19]. In contrast, our crural stitch technique is entirely extrahepatic and non-penetrating. The suture is anchored to the right diaphragmatic crus rather than through the liver tissue, eliminating the risk of ischemia or structural damage while maintaining stable and adjustable retraction.

Additionally, the crural stitch method requires only a standard 0-silk suture and a 2 mm skin puncture using an ENDOCLOSE device—both readily available in any laparoscopic setup. No intracorporeal knot tying, specialized suspension devices, or assistant support are necessary. This makes the technique easily reproducible, cost-neutral, and adaptable across various upper GI procedures, regardless of patient body habitus or liver size. The simplicity, safety, and consistency of this method distinguish it from other suture-based techniques and mechanical retractors alike, offering a practical and atraumatic solution for liver retraction in minimally invasive upper GI surgery.

Considering the above-mentioned complication profile, there is a need for safe, reproducible and reliable methods of liver retraction. The crural stitch method presented in this article presents one such method, as seen by the absence a technique related complication in our practice.

Future research should focus on prospective comparative or randomized trials evaluating this method against conventional mechanical retractors, including objective outcomes such as postoperative pain scores, incisional hernia rates, and operative efficiency. Furthermore, future prospective studies should also include validated patient-reported outcome measures to assess health-related quality of life and the perceived benefits of reduced port numbers, as these factors represent an important dimension of minimally invasive surgery.

Conclusion

The described technique shown in this study, is a safe and simple alternative option for liver retraction during laparoscopic upper gastrointestinal surgeries. It comes at low additional cost with virtually no significant complication, with effective exposure. This study has its own limitations, mainly that it is a single-center, single-surgeon series, with the technique being performed by a surgeon with decades of experience at the time the technique was first employed. As such, further high-level studies should be performed to accurately demonstrate the efficacy and safety profile of this technique.
