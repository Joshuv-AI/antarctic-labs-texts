Mapping methods and tools for measuring patient experience in oncology settings: a scoping review
Authors: Julia Charlotte Kowalska, Dawid Storman, Kamila Michalska, Mateusz Świerz, Małgorzata Maraj, Joanna Zając, Alicja Domagała
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePatient experience (PX) is crucial for evaluating healthcare quality in oncology, encompassing comprehensive interactions from diagnosis through survivorship. However, the field lacks consensus on PX measurement and its conceptualization. The aim of this scoping review was to systematically map methods and tools used to measure PX in original studies, while providing detailed characteristics of each tool.MethodsGuided by the Arksey and O'Malley framework, this review involved a systematic search across six databases up to May 2025.ResultsData from 109 original studies, identifying 43 unique tools, were extracted and synthesized narratively. Survey-based questionnaires predominated, with national cancer PX surveys being most frequently used (44%). Despite this, significant fragmentation was evident, with most tools used in limited studies (1–2). 63% of tools lacked explicit theoretical foundations, and psychometric reporting was imprecise. Consistently measured domains included information/communication, care coordination, and respect/dignity.ConclusionsThe measurement of PX in oncology is diverse but inconsistent, marked by a lack of standardization, theoretical ambiguity, and insufficient psychometric validation. Therefore, it is highly challenging to transfer such results into impactful clinical interventions for patients. Addressing these gaps through theory-driven tool development, rigorous validation, and culturally appropriate adaptations, especially for cancer survivorship and diverse global contexts, is essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-026-10797-6.

Introduction

The measurement of patient experience (PX) has become increasingly vital in healthcare, particularly within the complex and often emotionally charged environment of oncology [1]. This emphasis stems from a growing recognition that PX, encompassing pre-consultation interactions, hospitalization, and discharge planning, serves as a crucial indicator of healthcare quality beyond mere clinical outcomes [2]. This approach offers a multi-faceted perspective on the effectiveness and humanization of care, directly influencing treatment adherence and overall well-being [3]. This patient-centered approach, which conceptualizes PX as the cumulative effect of all interactions an individual has with the healthcare system, including aspects from customer experience, is critical for understanding and predicting patient intentions and behaviors following care [4]. Within oncology, this is particularly salient given the often-protracted treatment trajectories and the profound impact of cancer on individuals' lives, making the evaluation of patient-reported experiences indispensable for refining care delivery and optimizing patient satisfaction [5].

The main issue for researchers remains the lack of consensus on precise definition of PX, which manifests in numerous competing conceptualizations often mistaken for related terms such as “patient satisfaction” or “patient outcomes,” thereby complicating differentiation, impeding study replication, and resulting in measurement tools with divergent underlying concepts [6, 7]. PX, often captured through Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREM), refers to the sum of all interactions shaped by an organization’s culture that influence patient perceptions across the care continuum, encompassing objective aspects like waiting times, communication, respect, coordination, and emotional support, as defined by the Beryl Institute and echoed in oncology-specific frameworks [8, 9]. In contrast, patient satisfaction evaluates the degree to which care meets individual expectations, desires, or prior experiences, often through subjective ratings that can be influenced by unmet needs or external factors, adopting a more top-down approach in traditional surveys [10]. Patient outcomes, meanwhile, focus on the impacts of disease and treatment on health status, including symptoms, functioning, quality of life, and well-being—typically assessed via Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROM)—distinct from the process-oriented nature of experiences [3, 11]. Despite the clear distinctions, current PREMs often conflate these concepts, leading to generic and static instruments that fail to capture the dynamic and multi-faceted nature of evolving healthcare landscapes [12, 14]. Consequently, it leads to inconsistent and non-replicable measurement in this specific field.

Despite the recognized importance of PX in oncology care, the field exhibits a highly diverse and fragmented array of measurement approaches and instruments. These encompass a mix of standardized PREM, generic satisfaction surveys, author-constructed questionnaires, electronic PROMs adapted for experience domains, and qualitative methods like interviews or focus groups [11, 13–15]. Such heterogeneity stems from inconsistent conceptualizations, variable coverage of key domains (e.g., communication quality, care coordination, emotional and informational support, access to services, and involvement of family caregivers), and frequent tailoring to specific cancer types, treatment modalities, or care phases—from diagnosis through survivorship [16]. This landscape not only complicates the replication of research findings and cross-country comparisons but also features numerous tools lacking robust psychometric properties, including suboptimal reliability, construct validity, content validity, responsiveness, and interpretability for quality improvement [17]. For instance, many instruments suffer from high literacy demands, double-barreled questions, or insufficient testing across diverse populations, limiting their actionability in clinical practice [18, 19].

The primary aim of this scoping review is to systematically map the methods and tools used to measure PX in oncology care settings and therefore to identify and characterize them, including their conceptual frameworks, psychometric properties, and applicability across diverse patient populations and care contexts. Ultimately, these insights are poised to foster scholarly and policy dialogues on harmonizing patient experience definitions—distinguishing them from satisfaction or outcomes—and establishing uniform measurement protocols to enhance comparability, evidence synthesis, and patient-centered oncology care globally.

Methods

Scoping review framework

This scoping review is guided by the methodological framework developed by Arksey and O'Malley [20], as further refined by Levac et al. [21] and Peters et al. [22] The reporting of this review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines (PRISMA-ScR) [23]. This framework provides a systematic approach to comprehensively map the existing literature, identify key concepts, and highlight research gaps without undertaking a quality appraisal of included studies.

Protocol and registration

A detailed protocol for this scoping review was developed prior to commencing the review process. This protocol outlines the specific objectives, research questions, search strategy, eligibility criteria, and planned methods for data extraction and synthesis. The protocol was registered on Open Science Framework: 10.17605/OSF.IO/FU7RZ, ensuring transparency and reproducibility of the review. This research received no external funding.

Defining research questions

Specifically, this review addresses the question how PX is measured in the context of oncological care. The question is analyzed on the following aspects: method of measurement, frequency of usage in original studies, conceptual foundations, definitions, domains measured, clinical settings, type of study design and study populations.

Information sources

A systematic and comprehensive search was conducted up to 08 May 2025 across 6 electronic databases including: EMBASE, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and PsycINFO.

Additionally, hand-searching and reference check were performed. Search strategies for each database are available in Appendix 1.

Study selection process

All identified records were imported into a Rayyan reference management software [24] and duplicates were removed. The remaining unique records underwent a two-phase screening process:

1. Title and abstract screening: titles and abstracts were independently screened by pairs of independent reviewers (JK&MS, KM&DS). Records deemed irrelevant were excluded. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (AD). 2. Full-text screening: full-text articles were assessed for eligibility by pairs of independent reviewers (JK&MS, KM&DS, JZ&MM), with conflicts resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (AD). Reasons for exclusions at this stage were recorded.

Calibration exercises were performed at each stage of the process, until reaching at least 80% compatibility.

Given the substantial volume of identified studies, the authors decided to divide the review into two complementary scoping reviews: the current one concentrating on formally named and established tools, and a parallel review examining the prevalent author-developed questionnaires that characterize much of the field.

Data extraction

Data from the included studies were independently extracted by pairs of co-authors (JK and JZ&MS) using a pre-designed data extraction form in Excel format. Potential conflicts were resolved through discussion or by a third adjudicator (AD). The extraction form was piloted on a subset of studies and refined as necessary to ensure comprehensive and consistent data collection. We extracted the following data: name of tool used in the study, theoretical framework (behind tool development), definition of patient experience (conceptualization); metrics of tool (e.g. reliability, validity scores, if available); authors and year of studies using the particular tool; country of population; sample size; study design (quantitative/qualitative/mixed); cancer type (breast/colon/urological/prostate/myeloma/colorectal/AML/lung/other, specify/mixed/not specified/any/various); setting type (inpatient/outpatient/mixed); development stage (usage/usage + modification/development).

Data synthesis and reporting

A narrative synthesis was conducted, summarizing findings from the included studies. Qualitative data, such as definitions of patient experience and themes from qualitative studies, were synthesized thematically to identify overarching concepts and patterns. Findings were presented in a narrative format, supplemented by tables to summarize key characteristics and findings, directly addressing the review’s research questions.

Reported domains of each instrument were inductively grouped into higher-order thematic categories. For each tool, coverage of each theme was independently assessed based on the explicit presence, scope, and prominence of relevant domains. Themes were classified as fully covered when addressed by a dedicated multi-item domain, partially covered when addressed by single or peripheral items, and not covered when absent (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Thematic coverage of patient experience domains across identified measurement tools. Tool usage was highly uneven. While a small number (23%) of instruments were used repeatedly, 77% of all identified tools were used in only one study, indicating substantial fragmentation in PX measurement.

The studies were predominantly published from the early 2000 s onwards, underscoring the rising prioritization of PX as a quality indicator in oncology and cancer survivorship care. Geographically, the research was concentrated in high-income countries, notably the United Kingdom (dominant with NCPES studies), United States (e.g., Press Ganey surveys, CAHPS-based), Canada (e.g., Your Voice Matters Survey, Ambulatory Oncology Patient Satisfaction Survey), and Western and Northern Europe (e.g., Norway’s Consumer-Perspective Evaluation Questionnaire, Germany’s Cologne Patient Questionnaire, Sweden’s National Cancer Patient Questionnaire, Switzerland’s SCAPE). This geographical concentration highlights a potential gap in research from lower- and middle-income countries, where healthcare access and patient experiences may differ significantly. Detailed origins of each study are described in Appendix 2.

What methods and tools are used to measure PX in oncological care? How frequently are they used?

NCPES emerged as the most prevalent instrument, utilized in 38 (35%) studies predominantly from the United Kingdom (e.g., England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland), often involving large-scale secondary analyses of national data, such as Abel et al. [26, 27], Alessy et al. [28] and Brookes et al. [1]. This was followed by a modest number of tools employed moderately often (e.g., Your Voice Matters Survey, Picker Patient Experience Questionnaire) and a substantial proportion (many single-use tools like Prostate Care Questionnaire for Patients, Sarcoma Assessment Measure) applied in only one study each.

Regarding type of study designs, most studies employed quantitative cross-sectional designs, often analyzing secondary data from large-scale national or regional surveys. Ninety-three of the included studies (86%) relied exclusively on quantitative methods. Mixed-methods designs were used less frequently (10; 9%), typically combining surveys with interviews or open-ended responses. Qualitative-only studies were rare (6; 5%) and were mainly used in the development or refinement of measurement tools.

What are the theoretical or conceptual foundations of these tools?

Across the 43 identified PX measurement tools, explicit theoretical or conceptual foundations were uncommonly reported within the included studies. Most instruments were developed pragmatically for service evaluation and quality improvement, with limited reference to formal theory. Nevertheless, patterns emerged regarding both the theoretical grounding of tools and how PX was conceptualized and defined.

Overall, 27 tools (63%) did not explicitly report a formal theoretical framework, instead drawing implicitly on patient-centered care principles, experiential reporting approaches, and prior survey instruments. In contrast, 16 tools (37%) reported an identifiable theoretical or conceptual foundation, although the depth and clarity of theoretical integration varied substantially (Table 1). Most instruments appeared to be used pragmatically for service evaluation, where the primary focus of the publishing authors was on clinical results rather than conceptual or theoretical transparency. Therefore, the identified "theoretical ambiguity" may reflect a systemic reporting gap in oncology literature rather than a universal absence of theory in tool design.

Several tools referenced more than one conceptual influence, most commonly combining patient-centered care principles with process-of-care frameworks. Tools grounded in the Donabedian framework were primarily used to link care processes with outcomes such as satisfaction or intention to recommend, rather than to operationalize patient experience as an outcome in its own right. Only a small number of instruments were explicitly theory-driven at the design stage, including tools based on shared decision-making theory, person-centered coordinated care, or health system responsiveness. These tools typically articulated a clearer conceptual distinction between patient experience, satisfaction, and outcomes (Appendix 2).

How is patient experience defined by authors?

Across the included studies, patient experience was most defined implicitly, rather than through explicit theoretical statements. The dominant operational definition used in the majority of tools conceptualized patient experience as a concept of self-perceived reported experiences of cancer care across the care pathway.

In 4 tools only (3%), patient experience was framed within specific conceptual models, including shared decision-making theory (collaboRATE, consideRATE) or health system responsiveness (HSRQ). However, even in these cases, formal definitions of patient experience were often assumed rather than explicitly articulated.

What domains of PX are measured?

Despite heterogeneity in structure and terminology, there was strong convergence in the domains of patient experience assessed across tools. Most frequently measured domains were: information and communication (36 tools, 84%), coordination and continuity of care (31 tools, 72%) and respect, dignity, interpersonal care (28 tools, 65%). Other repetitive dimensions concerned areas such as involvement in decision-making, emotional and psychosocial support, access and timeliness, physical environment/comfort, practical/logistical support, and overall ratings.

National surveys typically organized items into pathway-based sections, whereas some cancer-specific and generic PREMs used multi-item psychometric domains. The majority (98%) of tools have not identified domains through psychometric testing. While the majority of tools have proven content validity (84%), other measurements are rarely reported even among adapted tools. This suggests a significant reporting bias, where researchers may be utilizing established or adapted tools without detailing their psychometric performance in the specific study context. Consequently, it remains unclear whether these tools lack robust psychometric properties entirely or if publishing authors are simply omitting this critical methodological information, highlighting a need for more transparent validation reporting in future oncology PX research. Only NCPES was adapted or translated for use in different national or cultural contexts. Adaptation processes commonly involved translation, contextual modification, and pilot testing. However, formal cross-cultural validation was inconsistently reported, and equivalence between versions was rarely examined empirically. The exception among national ones is SCAPE based on NCPES, but extending its methodological value with full psychometric testing. Therefore, we also decided to include a summary of psychometric characteristics for each tool in Appendix 2.

Themes represent higher-order groupings of reported domains. Coverage was classified as full (2), partial (1), or absent (0).

In which clinical settings are these tools used?

Patient experience tools were implemented across a range of oncology settings. Among the included studies, Fifty-two (48%) focused on patient experience measurement in outpatient oncology settings. Thirty-three studies (30%) were conducted in mixed settings (inpatient and outpatient), while Eleven (10%) were carried out exclusively in inpatient settings. In Thirteen studies (12%) the clinical setting was classified as other. Measurement most commonly occurred during active treatment or shortly after treatment completion, with limited focus on long-term survivorship.

Which types of cancer diagnosis are most represented in those measurement tools?

Cancer populations varied across studies. The majority focused on mixed cancer populations (53%), reflecting the dominance of national surveys like NCPES. Among diagnosis-specific studies, breast cancer (13%), prostate and other urological cancers (11%), colorectal cancer (8%), and lung cancer (6%) were most frequently represented, while rare cancers (3%) and hematological malignancies (4%) were comparatively underrepresented. While underrepresentation of rare diseases is adequate to morbidity, the number of studies specific for most prevalent cancer types is alarming.

Discussion

The results of this comprehensive scoping review, encompassing 109 empirical studies and identifying 43 unique patient experience measurement tools in oncology care, reveal a dynamic yet fragmented landscape with most of the identified tools being used in only a limited number of studies, underscoring a lack of standardization in the field. While the high proportion of tools used only once (77%) underscores a lack of standardization, this fragmentation requires a nuanced interpretation. This high rate of single-use tools can be partially reconciled by the clinical necessity for instruments that capture the unique trajectories and granular needs of specific cancer subpopulations, which often require more tailored measurement approaches than generic tools provide. While the underrepresentation of rare diseases is expected, the relative scarcity of studies for the most prevalent cancer types is concerning and suggests that while researchers are developing specialized tools, these instruments have yet to achieve widespread adoption. To bridge the gap between fragmentation and specialization, a modular approach to PREM development is recommended; in this framework, a set of core, consistent domains—such as communication, coordination, and respect—is used to ensure comparability, while tumor-specific modules are appended to address granular clinical needs unique to specific diagnoses.

A critical observation is the frequent absence of explicit theoretical or conceptual foundations for many tools. The findings of this review align with previous scoping efforts in the field of PREMs in cancer care, consistently identifying heterogeneity and challenges in measurement. Standardization of PREMs at a national or regional level, exemplified by the widespread use of the NCPES in the United Kingdom, would significantly help unify this body of research and enhance the comparability of results across different healthcare systems. Furthermore, establishing consistent domains—such as the frequently measured areas of information, communication, and care coordination—would provide a universal language for patient experience regardless of the specific tool applied. This consistency is a prerequisite for translating research findings into impactful clinical interventions that can be evaluated and compared across different global settings. Nevertheless, the additional challenge relates to the predominance of PREMs developed and validated in highly educated cancer populations from high-income countries. Although several instruments provide valuable standardization, their applicability across culturally diverse and lower-resource settings may be limited without appropriate linguistic, cultural, and health-literacy adaptation. Future PREM development and validation efforts should prioritize inclusivity and cross-cultural applicability.

For instance, the most recent scoping review in the field by Shao et al. [17] noted that the field is in its early stages and lacks uniform development, highlighting the need for further investigation into the effectiveness and validity of existing assessment tools, and emphasizing the scarcity of high-quality, cancer-specific instruments. Similar observations regarding the diverse and often inconsistent landscape of PX measurement have been echoed by other researchers. Mollica et al. [29] identified research gaps in PX of cancer care, emphasizing the necessity of in-depth literature examination to define these gaps, acknowledging the varied definitions and approaches to measuring this concept. They explicitly differentiate "patient experience" from "patient satisfaction," noting that experience captures a more specific measurement of patient care processes from the patient’s perspective. This aligns with our finding that where definitions of PX are present, they correctly distinguish it from patient satisfaction or outcomes [30]. LaVela and Gallan [7] further elaborated on the complexities and ambiguities in measuring patient experience, often due to a lack of a common definition and its conflation with related terms like satisfaction and engagement.

The broader literature in cancer survivorship also reflects the fragmentation and methodological considerations in measurement. While some recent reviews focus on patient-reported outcome measures in cancer survivors, such as Singhal et al. [31] work on PROM implementation or Mols et al.’s [32] insights from population-based PRO registries, they often implicitly or explicitly contrast with the distinct, yet equally challenging, landscape of patient-reported experience measures. Tarver et al.’s [33] scoping review on survivorship care plans among minority, rural, and low-income populations highlights persistent disparities and unmet needs in specific groups, further validating our observation regarding geographical and demographic gaps in PX research. It is essential to acknowledge that the capacity and feasibility of implementing PREMs in low-income countries differ greatly from high-income settings due to significant variations in healthcare infrastructure, financial resources, and digital access. Addressing this requires the development of culturally appropriate and resource-efficient measurement protocols tailored to these specific global contexts, ensuring that the patient-centered ideal is not limited by the technical or literacy demands of tools designed for Western healthcare systems. These reviews, while often centered on outcomes or specific interventions, consistently touch upon elements of PX, implicitly battling with the same conceptual and measurement inconsistencies identified in our broader review of PREMs. The continued call for improved quality and consistent guidelines for PREM development and implementation by Giannopoulos et al. [34] remains highly relevant across the entire cancer care continuum, including survivorship.

Regarding the four categories of instruments identified, this review found that while national surveys provide powerful population-level monitoring, they often report at the item level and may lack the psychometric depth found in more specialized scales. Cancer-specific PREMs offer the thematic coverage necessary for meaningful clinical interventions, often including domains like symptom management or holistic support that generic tools may overlook. Generic PX instruments remain useful for cross-specialty benchmarking, but for oncology-specific quality improvement, researchers should prioritize cancer-specific PREMs to capture the distinct, multi-faceted nature of the cancer journey from diagnosis through survivorship. By prioritizing theory-driven tool development and rigorous validation, the field can move away from ad-hoc measurement toward a standardized framework that remains sensitive to the specific needs of cancer survivors and diverse patient populations. Ultimately, the goal is to establish uniform protocols that foster global dialogue and improve the quality of oncology care worldwide.

Strengths and limitations of the review

This scoping review offers a comprehensive mapping of patient experience (PX) measurement in oncology, providing an exhaustive inventory of 43 unique tools across 109 empirical studies. A primary strength of this work is its inclusive methodological approach, which imposed no restrictions on publication dates, languages, or study designs, allowing for the synthesis of quantitative surveys, validated scales, and mixed-methods applications. By identifying a significantly larger volume of studies and tools compared to previous review [17], which identified 24 studies and 11 tools—this review provides a more nuanced understanding of the assessment landscape. Furthermore, the deliberate bifurcation of this research into two parallel reviews—one for formally named tools and another for author-developed questionnaires—allows for a deeper analysis of established instruments versus ad-hoc measurement practices. Such a detailed overview is essential for researchers to pinpoint methodological gaps and for clinicians to select contextually appropriate instruments to drive standardization across healthcare systems.

Despite these strengths, this review has several limitations inherent to the scoping review methodology. First, consistent with the Arksey and O'Malley framework [20] as refined by Levac et al. [21], this review did not undertake a critical appraisal of the methodological quality or risk of bias of the included studies. Therefore, while it maps the landscape of PX measurement, it does not evaluate the robustness of the evidence generated by the tools or studies identified. This means that while we identify the tools available, we do not assess their internal validity or the quality of the studies that used them. Second, this specific review deliberately focused on formally named and established measurement tools, purposefully excluding a vast body of author-developed questionnaires, which are addressed in a parallel complementary review. While this bifurcation provides a more detailed analysis of formal tools, it means that this particular review alone does not offer a complete picture of all ad-hoc or less formalized instruments used in the field. Finally, the findings are limited by the quality of reporting in the original studies as many authors omit details regarding theoretical frameworks and psychometric validation, which may obscure the true technical maturity of the identified tools.

Gaps in measurement approaches

Based on the findings, several critical gaps exist in the current measurement approaches for patient experience in oncology. The geographical concentration of research, primarily in high-income countries, points to a substantial gap in understanding patient experiences in lower- and middle-income countries. These regions often face unique healthcare challenges and cultural contexts, necessitating tailored measurement instruments and research efforts.

There is also an apparent gap in the explicit theoretical grounding of many PX tools. A more consistent and explicit application of theoretical frameworks would enhance the clarity, consistency, and interpretability of findings across different studies and contexts [17, 35].

Furthermore, while mixed cancer populations are frequently studied, the underrepresentation of specific cancer types, particularly rare cancers and hematological malignancies, indicates a need for more tailored instruments that capture the unique experiences associated with these conditions. Similarly, the predominant focus on active treatment phases, with limited attention to long-term survivorship, highlights a gap in understanding the evolving patient experience across the entire care continuum.

Finally, the variability in psychometric reporting necessitates a concerted effort towards rigorous and transparent validation processes for all PREMs. This includes comprehensive assessments of reliability, various forms of validity, and responsiveness to change like among PROMs [36]. The readability and comprehensiveness of these tools also need consideration to ensure their utility for all patients [34].

Implications for practice and research

For clinical practice and healthcare policy, the findings underscore the importance of discerning between different types of patient experience measures. Clinicians and policymakers should critically evaluate the psychometric properties and conceptual clarity of tools before implementation, striving to select instruments that are fit-for-purpose and aligned with specific quality improvement objectives. Recognizing the distinct nature of patient experience from satisfaction and outcomes, as elucidated in this review, is crucial for developing targeted interventions that genuinely enhance patient-centered care. The utility of national surveys for broad monitoring should be complemented by the development and application of more specific, validated tools to address granular aspects of care [37, 38].

This review identifies clear priorities for advancing the field of future research, including the following recommendations:

Conclusion

In conclusion, while the landscape of patient experience measurement in oncology is rich and diverse, it faces challenges related to fragmentation, theoretical ambiguity, and insufficient psychometric validation. Addressing these gaps is crucial for the development of more robust, conceptually clear, and contextually appropriate Patient-Reported Experience Measures. Such advancements will facilitate more precise evaluations of healthcare quality, inform evidence-based interventions to enhance patient-centered care, and ultimately contribute to improved patient outcomes and experiences in oncology worldwide. The insights from this review provide a roadmap for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers to collectively advance the science and practice of measuring patient experience in this critical area of healthcare.
