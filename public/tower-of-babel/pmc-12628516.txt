Volumetric evaluation of the maxillary sinus using cone-beam computed tomography
Authors: Sumeyye Celik Ozsoy, Serkan Bahrilli, Fatma Altiparmak, Hatice Yilmaz, Ibrahim Burak Yuksel, Muhammet Emin Arslan, Ali Altindag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVolumetric evaluation of the maxillary sinus is a crucial step in preoperative planning for various dental and maxillofacial procedures. Multiple anatomical and demographic factors, such as age, sex, and nasal septum deviation, are known to influence sinus morphology. While previous studies have examined these factors individually, a comprehensive analysis integrating their collective impact on sinus volume is still needed. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides a valuable tool for accurate three-dimensional assessment, enabling clinicians to identify anatomical variations and plan for safer surgical interventions.AimThis study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the volumetric changes of the maxillary sinus using CBCT and to determine the independent and collective influence of age, sex, nasal septum deviation, and tooth loss on sinus volume.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on CBCT scans of 120 patients (mean age: 33.6 ± 16.8 years; 60 males, 60 females). The scans were standardized and evaluated by an experienced oral radiologist on a calibrated monitor. Maxillary sinus volumes were measured using 3D Slicer software, with specific attention to the field of view (FOV) and voxel size of the scans. Associations between sinus volume and demographic or anatomical factors were assessed using correlation and regression analyses.ResultsThe mean sinus volumes were 14.26 cm³ (SD: 5.66) on the right and 14.44 cm³ (SD: 5.40) on the left side (p = 0.414). Age was negatively correlated with sinus volume (r = − 0.31, p < 0.01), and males had significantly greater volumes than females (p < 0.05). Nasal septal deviation was a significant independent predictor in regression analysis (β = +1257.0, p = 0.030). The final model explained 17.9% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.150).ConclusionMaxillary sinus volume is significantly influenced by age, sex, and nasal septal deviation, with males exhibiting larger volumes and age showing a negative correlation. These findings underscore the importance of accurate CBCT-based volumetric assessment for personalized surgical planning, particularly in cases involving nasal septum deviation.Keywords: Cone-beam computed tomography, Maxillary sinus, Nasal septal deviation, Paranasal sinuses, Tooth loss

Introduction

Paranasal sinuses are air-filled cavities situated within the craniofacial bones adjacent to the nasal cavity, which encompass the maxillary, frontal, sphenoid, and ethmoidal sinuses. These structures play vital roles in various physiological functions and exhibit significant anatomical variability, which can complicate diagnosis and treatment [1]. Understanding the developmental patterns of these sinuses is crucial, as their final morphology can undergo continuous changes into adulthood. Research indicates that the volume and configuration of the paranasal sinuses can alter considerably with age, which has important implications for clinical practice, particularly in surgical planning and diagnosis [2]. A recent systematic review examined the volumetric changes experienced by the paranasal sinuses over time, suggesting that clinical awareness of these alterations is vital as life expectancy increases and the need for interventions in this anatomical region grows [2]. The morphological development of the paranasal sinuses is influenced by various factors, including genetic and environmental conditions [3]. An in-depth understanding of these developmental patterns aids clinicians in anticipating potential complications associated with sinus pathologies [2, 3].

Among imaging modalities, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has emerged as a prominent diagnostic tool, particularly in oral and maxillofacial radiology. CBCT provides accurate and reliable 3D images, offering superior reliability and reproducibility compared to conventional 2D imaging methods, which makes it a preferred choice for pre-surgical planning in both otolaryngology and maxillofacial surgery [4, 5]. It has gained traction among dental practitioners and otolaryngologists due to its ability to provide superior detail in visualizing paranasal sinus anatomy while exposing patients to lower radiation doses compared to traditional computed tomography [6]. The application of CBCT extends beyond skeletal imaging, allowing for precise evaluation of soft tissue lesions and anatomical variations, making it invaluable for practitioners in planning surgical interventions [7]. Furthermore, continuous technological advancements and increased availability of CBCT systems have solidified its role as a standard imaging tool in the diagnosis and management of sinus conditions, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes [8].

The maxillary sinuses, the largest of the paranasal sinuses, are paired cavities situated within the maxillary bones [9, 10]. Their morphology is characterized by a pyramid shape, with boundaries that include the orbital floor superiorly and the lateral wall of the nasal cavity medially. The close anatomical relationship between the maxillary sinus and the roots of the posterior teeth makes it a critical area of interest in dental practice [11, 12].

Previous studies have investigated the impact of factors such as age, sex, and nasal septum deviation on sinus volume; however, a limited number have comprehensively evaluated the combined influence of these demographic and anatomical factors on maxillary sinus morphology within a single patient cohort. It has been noted that anatomical variations like nasal septum deviation are significantly correlated with the incidence of maxillary sinus pathologies, such as mucous retention cysts [12–14]. Such a holistic understanding is crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment planning for sinus-related conditions. The present study aims to investigate the volumetric characteristics of the maxillary sinus and determine the combined influences of age, sex, nasal septum deviation, and tooth loss, thereby providing a more integrated understanding of these relationships [15].

Methods

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University, Faculty of Dentistry (Decision No: 2022/160, dated July 28, 2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Power analysis

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for a t-test comparing the means of two independent groups. The analysis assumed a one-tailed test, a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.95. The results indicated that a total of 70 subjects (35 per group) are required to achieve the desired statistical power under these assumptions.

A total of 240 CBCT scans from 120 patients (right and left maxillary sinuses) who presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology for various diagnostic purposes between 2020 and 2022 were evaluated retrospectively. The patient cohort was selected based on a power analysis that indicated a minimum of 70 subjects were required for sufficient statistical power. The study was conducted on a patient sample that included 60 males and 60 females.

Imaging protocol and data collection

The data for this study were obtained from the image archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology and included CBCT and panoramic images of 120 patients aged 16 to 65 years. All CBCT scans were acquired using a NewTom Giano HR scanner (Verona, Italy) operating at 90 kVp and 6 mA, with a FOV of 10 × 10 cm and a voxel size of 0.3 mm. Chronological age was calculated by subtracting the birth date from the date of image acquisition. Gender and age data were recorded for all participants.

All CBCT scans were exported in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format and imported into the 3D Slicer software version 5.6.2 (Surgical Planning Lab, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA). All CBCT scans were exported in DICOM format and imported into the 3D Slicer software version 5.6.2 (Surgical Planning Lab, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA). Image evaluation and volumetric measurements were performed by a single, board-certified oral and maxillofacial radiologist with 10 years of experience (I.B.Y.) The radiologist was blinded to the patient’s demographic information during the measurement process. A standardized protocol was used to view the images on a calibrated, high-resolution diagnostic monitor (Eizo Radi Force MX242W; resolution 1920 × 1200; Eizo Corporation, Ishikawa, Japan) in a dimly lit room.

The intra-observer reliability for maxillary sinus volume measurements was assessed by repeating the measurements on 20 randomly selected CBCT scans after a three-week interval. The repeated measurements demonstrated high intra-observer reliability, with no significant variation observed between sessions (p > 0.05). The Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.91 further supported the robustness and reproducibility of the measurements.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Before analysis, all data were checked for completeness and consistency. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. As the data were found to be non-normally distributed, non-parametric tests were chosen for further analysis. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values) were calculated for all variables. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired comparisons between right and left sinus volumes. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare sinus volumes between sexes and between patients with and without nasal septal deviation. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to evaluate relationships between age, number of missing teeth, and sinus volume. Finally, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent predictors of mean sinus volume. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2) and the adjusted R2. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The intra-observer reliability for maxillary sinus volume measurements was assessed by repeating the measurements on a randomly selected subset of 20 scans after a three-week interval. The paired-samples t-test showed no significant difference between the two sets (p > 0.05), and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.91, indicating excellent measurement consistency.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive volumetric analysis of the maxillary sinus using CBCT, with a specific focus on identifying the independent factors that influence its size. While foundational studies have established key principles, such as the bilateral symmetry of the maxillary sinuses [16, 17], our research advances the field by using a robust multiple linear regression model to concurrently assess the predictive power of multiple demographic and anatomical factors within a single patient cohort. This integrative statistical approach, combined with standardized CBCT-based segmentation in a balanced male–female sample, represents a methodological refinement compared with prior univariate analyses, offering a clearer understanding of independent predictors of sinus morphology and their clinical implications for surgical planning. In our sample of 120 patients, no significant difference was observed between the right and left sinus volumes. This finding not only corroborates previous research but also aligns with the latest evidence from systematic reviews on maxillary sinus morphology [18, 19]. This consistent bilateral symmetry is crucial for predictable surgical planning in restorative procedures and sinus augmentation, as it allows for the application of similar volumetric considerations to both sides [20].

In terms of dental status, our analysis revealed a nuanced relationship with maxillary sinus volume. While a comparison of sinus volumes between the more edentulous side and the contralateral side showed no significant difference, a statistically significant negative correlation was found between the overall number of missing teeth and sinus volume. This finding suggests that a systemic increase in tooth loss across the dentition may contribute to a gradual decrease in sinus volume, even if a direct comparison of a single edentulous area does not show a pronounced effect. These results align with some studies that argue prolonged edentulism may influence alveolar ridge resorption without drastically altering sinus pneumatization [21]. Conversely, other investigations, such as Schriber et al. [22], have also reported a minimal impact of tooth loss on sinus dimensions. The influence of dental status on sinus volume continues to be a subject of debate in the literature, with studies such as the recent work by Itokawa et al. (2025) reporting a significant decrease in sinus volume with tooth loss, a finding that offers new insights into the relationship between these factors [23].

Age and gender emerged as significant determinants in our study. A significant negative correlation between age and sinus volume was observed on both sides, suggesting that sinus volume tends to decrease with advancing age. This finding is consistent with other CBCT studies that have documented age-related structural changes in the maxillary sinus [24, 25]. In addition, our data demonstrated that males possess significantly larger sinus volumes than females. Similar gender-based differences have been reported in recent literature [25], reinforcing the ability of CBCT to effectively capture such demographic variations.

The impact of anatomical variations such as nasal septal deviation on sinus volume was also analyzed. Although our univariate analysis indicated that patients with nasal septal deviation had slightly higher sinus volumes compared to those without, these differences were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, our robust multiple regression analysis identified nasal septal deviation as an independent predictor of increased sinus volume. This finding is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that nasal septal deviation may play a subtle yet significant role in altering sinus anatomy, a relationship that may be masked in simpler group comparisons. This observation is in line with recent literature which reports a mixed evidence regarding the influence of nasal septal deviation on the maxillary sinus [26–29]. These findings are particularly relevant for clinicians planning surgical interventions and implant placements, where a comprehensive understanding of normative and anatomical variations, including the presence of nasal septal deviation, is critical.

Overall, our regression model, which explained approximately 15–18% of the variance in sinus volume, underscores that demographic factors (age and gender) and anatomical variations (nasal septal deviation) are more critical determinants of maxillary sinus size than dental status. The limited impact of tooth loss on sinus volume resonates with previous studies indicating that the process of sinus pneumatization is not a straightforward consequence of dental extractions or edentulism [21, 22]. The use of CBCT in this study provided high-resolution, three-dimensional data that facilitated a comprehensive evaluation, reaffirming its status as the preferred imaging modality for such assessments [16].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias, as the CBCT scans were originally obtained for various diagnostic purposes unrelated to sinus evaluation. Additionally, as a single-center study, the findings may have limited generalizability to broader, more diverse populations. A significant limitation is the low R2 ≈ 0.15 of our regression model, which indicates that while age, gender, and nasal septal deviation are significant predictors, they explain only a small proportion of the total variance in sinus volume. This suggests that other factors not included in this study, such as the presence of concha bullosa, hypertrophied turbinates, or other local anatomical variations, may also play a crucial role. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents any conclusions regarding causality between the observed variables. Future studies should include multicenter data, broader age ranges, and longitudinal designs to better assess changes in sinus volume over time and across diverse populations.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study offer important insights for clinicians performing surgical procedures in the posterior maxilla. Our data confirm that age and sex are fundamental factors influencing maxillary sinus volume, which clinicians should account for during patient assessment. The study’s most notable finding is the identification of nasal septal deviation as an independent predictor of increased sinus volume, a relationship that highlights the importance of a comprehensive anatomical evaluation. Using CBCT, clinicians can predict age-related changes, account for sex-based differences, and identify the subtle yet significant role of nasal septal deviation in affecting sinus anatomy. This detailed pre-surgical assessment is crucial for tailoring treatment plans, minimizing complications, and ensuring predictable outcomes in procedures such as sinus augmentation and implant placement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive volumetric analysis of the maxillary sinus, identifying key factors that independently influence its volume. Our data suggest that maxillary sinus volume is predominantly affected by demographic (age, sex) and anatomical (nasal septal deviation) factors, rather than dental status alone. A significant finding is that while the total number of missing teeth correlates with sinus volume, it is not an independent predictor in our multivariate model, which had a limited explanatory power (R2 ≈ 0.15). This highlights the complex, multifactorial nature of maxillary sinus morphology, suggesting that other unmeasured variables also play a significant role.

The clinical implications of these findings are significant. A comprehensive pre-surgical assessment employing CBCT imaging is vital to ensure a safe and predictable surgical outcome. This is especially important for maintaining the integrity of the Schneiderian membrane, securing implant stability, and reducing risks related to the posterior superior alveolar artery. By taking into account these critical predictors—age, sex, and the presence of nasal septal deviation—clinicians can improve diagnostic precision and enhance surgical planning in the posterior maxilla.
