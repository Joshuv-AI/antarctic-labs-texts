Exploring the pharmacokinetic interactions between cisplatin and Triphala through in-vitro, in-vivo, and in-silico approaches – A pilot study to bridge modern medicine with Ayurveda
Authors: T.K. Girija Kumari, Kamatchi Sundara Saravanan, Lakshmi M. Sundar, Gouri Nair, G.N.S. Hemasree, Ganesan Rajalekshmi Saraswathy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerbal drugs are often used alongside conventional cancer therapies to mitigate the adverse effects and enhance therapeutic efficacy. Triphala, a traditional herbal formulation used in Ayurveda, is known for its anticancer and CYP2E1 inhibitory properties. Cisplatin, although highly effective in cancer treatment, is known to cause significant hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity which are mediated by toxic metabolites formed through CYP2E1 metabolism.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to explore the pharmacokinetic drug interactions between Triphala and cisplatin.MethodsAt the outset, the pharmacokinetic interactions between Triphala and cisplatin were confirmed by in-vitro experiments using HepG2 and 786-0 cell lines based on Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) generation and cell viability assay, and in-vivo study using Sprague Dawley rats based on serum cisplatin concentration. Later, the literature derived phytoconstituents were subjected to in-silico studies to investigate their molecular interactions with the metabolizing enzyme CYP2E1.ResultsIn-vitro studies revealed a reduction in ROS generation and enhanced cell viability, indicating that the metabolism of cisplatin is hindered by Triphala, which was attributed to its CYP2E1 inhibitory potential. In-vivo studies showed a marginal increase in cisplatin concentrations in rat serum that received the combination of Triphala and cisplatin, supporting the in-vitro findings. In-silico studies revealed that Triphala constituents, especially gallic acid and 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid, stably bind and potentially inhibit CYP2E1 via key interactions with THR303.ConclusionThe results suggest that Triphala may interact with cisplatin by inhibiting the CYP2E1 enzyme, potentially affecting the metabolism and toxicity of cisplatin. These findings highlight the need for further research to evaluate the clinical implications of using Triphala alongside cisplatin-based cancer therapies.

Introduction

Triphala is composed of three myrobalan fruits namely, Amlaki (Emblica officinalis Gaertn.), Haritaki (Terminalia chebula Retz.), and Bibhitaki (Terminalia bellirica Roxb.). This tridoshic rasayana promotes rejuvenation and longevity in all individuals irrespective of their ages and their Vata, Pitta, and Kapha constitutions. Apart from general health benefits, it is also used in the treatment of several disorders of the gastrointestinal and cardiovascular systems [1]. This formulation is also mentioned in Ayurvedic classical literature for its bronchodilator and antispasmodic properties. Cumulative scientific evidence suggest that the formulation possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antibacterial, antimutagenic, adaptogenic, hypoglycemic, antineoplastic, nephroprotective, hepatoprotective, chemoprotective, and radioprotective properties [[2], [3], [4]].

Triphala is claimed to exhibit anticancer potential against breast, prostate and pancreatic cancer [5]. Network pharmacology study carried out by Chandran U et al., [6] represented the interactions of Triphala constituents with molecular targets associated with cancer offering novel insights on the relationships amongst constituents, targets and potential applications in various cancer pathways. Further, to substantiate the anticancer property, this formulation is reported to decrease the levels of NF-ƙB and phospho-Akt, p56, and enhance apoptosis mediated through MAPK/ERK, mTOR, and RAS signalling [7].

Though several Ayurvedic and indigenous medications are claimed to possess anticancer properties, chemotherapy remains the cornerstone for the management of cancer. Among chemotherapeutic agents, cisplatin is highly versatile, demonstrating efficacy against various malignancies, including lung, ovarian, testicular, bladder, and head and neck cancers [8]. Despite its effectiveness, cisplatin has several limitations owing to its toxicities, especially the hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity. The formation of a cisplatin-glutathione-conjugate is an essential component of the nephrotoxicity [9]. The metabolism of cisplatin by the enzyme CYP2E1 is reported to result in renal and hepatotoxicity [10,11]. The mechanism of nephrotoxicity induced by cisplatin is attributed to the generation of H2O2 post cisplatin-CYP2E1 interaction. Herein, the released H2O2 disrupts heme protein to heme and iron. Subsequently, as a chain reaction, the iron is involved in Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) cascade by releasing OH· and a number of other oxidants. Further, the oxidative stress mediated via CYP2E1 contributes to hepatotoxicity as well [10]. It is also reported that cisplatin enhances renal CYP2E1 activity and inhibition of this enzyme with 4 methyl pyrazole significantly ameliorated cisplatin induced cell death in mice and human kidney cells [12].

Currently, in clinical settings where pluralistic medicine is practiced, herbal drugs are concomitantly prescribed alongside various cancer therapies, for instance, literature supports the clinical benefits of ginger in preventing chemotherapy-related nausea and vomiting, and Indian frankincense (Boswellia serrata) for alleviating radiation-induced brain edema [13]. However, hitherto, there is no scientific research available either to delineate the therapeutic/adverse outcomes of such combinations or unearth potential interactions between the same.

Given the fact that Triphala is being reported to possess anticancer potential as well as inhibitory potential on CYP450 enzymes [14] and considering the mechanism of cisplatin induced toxicities mediated via CYP2E1, this study focuses on exploring the pharmacokinetic drug interactions between cisplatin and Triphala through a series of in-vitro, in-vivo and in-silico experimental approaches. The outcomes of the study may provide scientific insights to synthesize evidence on the potential harms or benefits of this combination being used in the clinical setting.

Methodology

The study is parsed into three phases:

Phase I: In-vitro analysis: The influence of Triphala on cisplatin metabolism mediated via CYP2E1 was explored in Hep G2 and 786-0 cell lines.

Phase II: In-vivo analysis: The pharmacokinetic interaction between Triphala and cisplatin was explored in Sprague Dawley rats.

Phase III: In-silico analysis: The constituents of Triphala were retrieved from published literature and subjected to molecular docking and molecular dynamics studies to explore their molecular interactions against the metabolizing enzyme CYP2E1.

Phase I: In-vitro analysis

Rat liver microsomes (Invitrogen, Product No.: RTMCPL; Lot No.: RT063-C) were incubated with cisplatin in the presence and absence of Triphala [15]. The resulting metabolites were then exposed to Hep G2 and 786-0 cell lines to assess ROS generation and cell viability. The aforesaid cell lines were selected to assess the hepatic metabolism (Hep G2) followed by potential renal toxicity (786-0), providing a physiologically relevant system to study Triphala's influence on CYP-mediated bioactivation and subsequent cytotoxic outcomes.

Microsome preparation

20 mg/ml rat liver microsomes were procured and diluted 10-folds to obtain 2 mg/ml. From this stock, 100 μl (200 μg) of microsomes were used for each reaction.

Treatment groups

32 mg/ml of Triphala stock was prepared, and two-fold serial dilutions were made with assay buffer to obtain test concentrations ranging from 1.56 to 100 μg/ml [16]. Similarly, cisplatin was also diluted to obtain 100 μM using assay buffer. Cell culture

Hep G2 (Passage 24) and 786-0 (Passage 18) cell lines were procured from National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune. The monolayer cell culture was trypsinized and cultured at 37°C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere in the medium containing 10 % FBS. The cell count was adjusted to 5 × 105 cells/ml using respective media.

Triphala-cisplatin CYP metabolism assay

Based on the grouping protocol, cisplatin (100 μM) alone, cisplatin (100 μM) + CYP2E1 inhibitor Diethyl dithiocarbamate (DDC), and cisplatin (100 μM) + different concentrations of Triphala were mixed with 25 μl of NADPH (SRL; Cat. no., 99197; Lot no.: 9289582) regeneration system and made up to 400 μl using phosphate buffer. The entire mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Then 100 μl of microsomes (2 mg/ml) were added and allowed for 5 min at 37 °C. Later, 500 μl of 20 % TCA was added to the mixture to stop the reaction. The contents were then centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 5 min to obtain the supernatants containing the drug metabolites.

Further, 100 μl of the diluted cell suspension of Hep G2 and 786-0 cells (50,000 cells/well) was added separately to each well of a 96 well microtiter plate. After 24 h, when a partial monolayer was formed, the supernatant was discarded and washed with medium. Later, 100 μl of supernatant containing the drug metabolites collected in earlier step was added on to the partial monolayer in microtiter plates. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in 5 % CO2 atmosphere. These samples were used for carrying out ROS analysis and cell viability assay in Hep G2 and 786-0 cell lines.

ROS analysis

The treatment samples from the CYP assay were removed from the plates and washed once with 1X PBS to which 100 μl of cell permeate, 25 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate solution was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 atmosphere. After incubation, the cells were washed with FBS and the fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at an excitation and emission maxima of 485/535 nm [18]. The experiment was run in triplicate.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using MTT assay [19]. After the incubation period of the test solutions for CYP assay, the wells were discarded and 100 μl of MTT (5mg/10 ml of MTT in PBS) was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 atmosphere. The supernatant layer was removed and 100 μl of DMSO was added and the plates were gently shaken to solubilize the formed formazan. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 590 nm. The percentage growth inhibition was calculated using the following formula.

Percentage Inhibition = ((Absorbance of Control – Absorbance of Sample)/Absorbance of Control) x 100.

Phase II: In-vivo analysis

The in-vivo pharmacokinetic effects of Triphala-cisplatin combination were carried out in 8-week-old Sprague Dawley rats weighing 220–250 gm. The animals were divided into two groups (n = 3), wherein group 1 was treated with a single dose of cisplatin alone (5 mg/kg b.w i.p), and group 2 was treated with a single dose of cisplatin (5 mg/kg b.w i.p) in combination with Triphala (500 mg/kg, p.o.). Following the exposure of the respective treatments, cisplatin concentration in blood was estimated. Blood samples were withdrawn from tail vein at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24 h post cisplatin/Triphala extract administration (Fig. 1). Later, cisplatin concentration in plasma was quantified using ICP-MS (Agilent 7800), that atomizes the sample and creates atomic and small polyatomic ions, which are then detected [20]. Literature derived constituents of Triphala.

Herein, these constituents were subjected to molecular docking studies against the crystal structure of human CYP2E1 (PDB ID: 3E4E) [35] using the co-crystallized ligand as a reference for binding site validation. Molecular docking studies was carried out using Glide tool (Schrödinger Release 2021-3: Glide, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2021).

Compounds demonstrating favourable docking scores and exhibiting binding interactions similar to the co-crystallized ligand were shortlisted for further analysis. These selected compounds were then evaluated for their pharmacokinetic properties using QikProp [36] to assess ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) profiles. Only those compounds that complied with Lipinski's Rule of Five indicating drug-likeness were retained for subsequent analysis.

Finally, the shortlisted compounds were subjected to Molecular Mechanics-Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations to estimate the binding free energy, thereby refining the selection based on predicted binding affinity [37]. Those compounds with better binding free energy were subjected to molecular dynamics simulation study.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed with the Desmond package [Schrödinger Release 2021-2: Desmond Molecular Dynamics System, D. E. Shaw Research, New York, NY, 2021] to analyze the stability of the drug–protein complex for 100 ns. The simulation system was built using an orthorhombic boundary box with the dimensions of 10 Å × 10 Å × 10 Å, and filled with the TIP3P water model. The OPLS3e force field was used to equilibrate at 300 K and 1 bar of pressure. The structural and dynamic stability of the complex during simulation has been investigated by RMSD, RMSF, and RoG calculations, which describe the conformational behavior and compactness of the protein-ligand complex.

Results

In-vitro analysis

The interaction between Triphala and cisplatin mediated via CYP2E1 inhibitory potential of Triphaa was explored in Hep G2 and 786-0 cell lines in terms of ROS analysis and cell viability assay.

In the case of Hep G2 cell lines, cisplatin alone showed ROS Fold Change (FC) of 2.74, and with CYP2E1 inhibitor DDC it exhibited a ROS FC as low as 0.65, indicating that CYP2E1 inhibitor effectively inhibited the metabolism of cisplatin. Similarly, Triphala in combination with cisplatin, displayed a concentration dependent decrease in the ROS FC. The ROS FC was 2.68 and 1.53 with cisplatin in combination with 1.56 and 100 μg/ml of Triphala respectively, which is less than the ROS FC in cisplatin alone group. Hence, it is evident that Triphala significantly inhibited the metabolism of cisplatin in a concentration dependent manner (Fig. 2).Fig. 2ROS Fold Change in Hep G2 cell lines.Fig. 2

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulation was performed to validate and measure the stability of the chemical constituent-target complex. Constituents such as gallic acid and 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid with the same interaction (THR303) as that of co-crystallized ligand were subjected to molecular dynamics. The CYP2E1 and constituent complex were immersed in an orthorhombic box of TIP3P. The solvated system was neutralized by adding Na+ and Cl− counterions; then, the system was subjected to molecular dynamics simulation for 100 ns. Further, RMSF, RMSD, and RoG of the drug-target complex were scrutinized using molecular dynamics.

The protein RMSD for the co-crystallized ligand (4-methylpyrazole) ranged between 1.00 Å and 2.25 Å over the 100 ns simulation. In contrast, the ligand RMSD exhibited higher fluctuations, varying from 1.5 Å to 12 Å throughout the trajectory. Discussion

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in leveraging traditional system of medicine as an alternative or complementary source for the management of various disorders including cancer [38]. Triphala, often considered as Panacea, is one such crucial Ayurvedic formulations which is known for its potential anticancer properties [39]. Triphala is also claimed for its CYP450 inhibitory potential [14]. Considering its role in altering the metabolic profiles and anticancer potential, the present study explores the pharmacokinetic interactions between Triphala and cisplatin.

Triphala's influence on cisplatin metabolism was studied using cultured human liver (Hep G2) and kidney cells (786-0). The co-administration of Triphala and cisplatin led to a significant reduction in ROS FC and was found to be concentration dependent. This suggest that Triphala inhibits CYP2E1 there by hindering the metabolism of cisplatin, thus reducing the formation of cisplatin-metabolite followed by a decrease in the generation of ROS. This is also evident from the cell viability assay, as the viability increased with the increase in the concentration of Triphala co-administered with cisplatin, indicating the fact that the amount of metabolite formed was inversely proportional to the concentration of co-administered Triphala. Thus, Triphala inhibits CYP2E1, thereby impending cisplatin metabolism leading to a reduction in the formation of cisplatin metabolite, concurrently reducing ROS generation followed by reduced cell toxicity.

The in-vivo pharmacokinetic analysis of cisplatin co-administered with Triphala in rats provided evidence on the cisplatin metabolism. The results indicated that concomitant administration of Triphala with cisplatin resulted in a marginal increase in the peak plasma concentration of cisplatin and also prolonged its half-life, as compared to the cisplatin alone administered animals. This finding is in line with the fact that Triphala inhibit CYP2E1 that is involved in the metabolism of cisplatin, thereby increasing the concentration in the blood. Moreover, the AUC, that represents the total drug in blood over a period of time was also marginally elevated in group 2 treated with cisplatin-Triphala combination compared to the group 1 treated with cisplatin alone.

Molecular docking studies of Triphala's constituents against CYP2E1 in complex with the inhibitor 4-methylpyrazole was carried out. THR303 resides in the well-conserved I-helix of CYP2E1, distal to the heme. This position enables its hydroxyl group to partake in hydrogen bonding, which constitutes part of the proton transfer network required for activation of molecular oxygen and for generation of the catalytically active iron-oxo species. Furthermore, 4-methylpyrazole chelates to the heme iron and forms hydrogen bonds with THR303. This interaction is critical for the correct orientation of small substrates/inhibitors in the active site [35]. Constituents with docking scores lower than −5.0 and key interactions, particularly with THR303, included gallic acid and 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid, which were subsequently subjected to molecular dynamics studies.

The RMSD analysis revealed that the 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid-protein complex exhibited greater stability compared to both the 4-methylpyrazole-protein and gallic acid-protein complexes. However, a discrepancy appeared between the docking and molecular dynamics results for 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid. While docking initially prophesied a key interaction with THR303, this contact was not observed in the protein–ligand interaction histogram from the molecular dynamics simulation. Instead, new interactions, notably with ARG100 and HIS370, were formed during the simulation indicating a potential reorientation of the ligand or a shift in its binding pose. In contrast, gallic acid maintained a stable hydrogen bond with THR303 throughout the simulation, confirming the interaction predicted by docking.

In line with our in silico results, there were previous research studies exemplifying the stronger inhibitory potential of Triphala constituents such as gallic acid, ellagic acid against the CYP isoforms - CYP1A2, CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6 [16]. In addition, Devasena et al. [40] reported the inhibitory potential of chebulic acid, ellagic acid, epicatechin and ascorbic acid against CYP2E1. Nontakham et al. [16] explored the pharmacokinetic interactions between Triphala and CYP-probes phenacetin and midazolam in rats. The core focus of this study was to assess the inhibitory potential of Triphala on CYP1A and CYP3A and potential clinical consequences of combining the aforesaid drugs with this herbal formulation. The research findings forecasted potential toxicities when Triphala is combined with drugs metabolized by CYP1A2 and/or CYP3A4, thus, highlighting the need to take necessary precautions in clinical practice. While our findings emphasise the beneficial combination of triphala with cisplatin, Nontakham et al.’s study warns of reduced drug metabolism pertinent to CYP1A2 and/or CYP3A4, resulting in potential toxicities. Herein, our research revealed the inhibition of toxic metabolite generation from cisplatin via CYP2E1, suggesting a protective role of Triphala in alleviating cisplatin-induced toxicity. However, the in-vivo pharmacokinetic analysis was based on a single dose. Chronic repeated-dose studies would be necessary to understand the long-term interaction effects between Triphala and cisplatin. The docked ligands demonstrated stable binding within the enzyme's catalytic pocket, primarily through hydrogen bonding with THR303 [41]. Given that the co-crystallized ligand is a known CYP2E1 inhibitor, the identified constituents demonstrating comparable interactions, may contribute to CYP2E1 inhibitory potential of Triphala.

A comprehensive bioavailability/bioequivalence (BA/BE) analysis was not carried out due to resource and data constraints, which is a key limitation of this study. Both clinical pharmacology and translational perspectives of BA/BE assessment was beyond the scope of the present work. Hence, an in-depth study plan for such analysis will be incorporated in future investigations.

Conclusion

This study revealed a potential pharmacokinetic interaction between Triphala, a traditional polyherbal formulation, and cisplatin, a widely used chemotherapeutic agent. The in-vitro and in-vivo experimental evidences delineated Triphala's potential to alter the metabolism of cisplatin and thereby reduce generation of ROS and associated oxidative stress. Furthermore, molecular docking and dynamics studies revealed that Triphala constituents, particularly gallic acid and 3,5-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzoic acid exhibited stable binding and potential inhibitory activity against CYP2E1, primarily through key interactions with THR303. Consequently, Triphala may mitigate cisplatin-induced toxicities by inhibiting CYP2E1-mediated metabolite formation, emphasizing its therapeutic potential in cisplatin-based chemotherapy. Further, to completely elucidate the clinical significance of this interaction, an in-depth exploratory research involving clinical trials is warranted.
