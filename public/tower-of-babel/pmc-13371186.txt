Effect of oral semaglutide on cardiometabolic risk factors in overweight and obese individuals with or without diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Niloofar Seighali, Mohammad Sadra Gholami-Chahkand, Mandana Ebrahimzade, Farzin Tahmasbi Arashlow, Seyede Parmis Maroufi, Parnian Rahnama, Mohammad Hassan Matin, Alireza Khodayari Javazm, Pouya Ebrahimi, Khurram Nasir, Rosy Thachil, Tor Biering Sørensen, Kaveh Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is linked to cardiometabolic risk, and semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, has emerged as a promising option for weight and metabolic control. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effects of oral semaglutide, compared with placebo, on cardiometabolic risk factors in overweight and obese adults with or without diabetes.Materials and methodsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis, following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched through 25 August 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCT) with overweight and obese populations, with or without diabetes, were included. The treatment and control groups received oral semaglutide and placebo, respectively. Outcomes were cardiometabolic factors and adverse events at baseline and follow-up. We pooled data using a random-effects model to estimate changes in outcomes by reporting Estimated Treatment Difference (ETD) as mean difference. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on dosage and follow-up duration.ResultsThirteen RCTs involving 26,284 participants met the criteria (52% received semaglutide). Female ratio of the semaglutide group was 30.1% and for the placebo group weas 33.3%. Compared with placebo, semaglutide showed significant reductions in body weight (ETD − 2.85 kg; 95% CI [− 4.03 to − 1.66]; p < 0.001), BMI (ETD − 0.66 kg/m²; 95% CI [− 0.95 to − 0.36]; p < 0.001), waist circumference (ETD − 1.79 cm; 95% CI [− 2.63 to − 0.95]; p < 0.001), HbA1c (ETD − 0.94%; 95% CI [− 1.13 to − 0.76]; p < 0.001), fasting plasma glucose (ETD − 26.91 mg/dL; 95% CI [− 33.56 to − 20.26]; p < 0.001), systolic blood pressure (ETD − 2.71 mmHg; 95% CI [− 3.70 to − 1.72]; p < 0.001), and diastolic blood pressure (ETD − 0.77 mmHg; 95% CI [− 1.38 to − 0.17]; p = 0.01) over 6 months. Benefits were consistent across different follow-up durations and doses. Gastrointestinal symptoms remained the most common adverse events (OR for GI issues = 2.69; nausea = 3.13; vomiting = 6.25; all p < 0.001). Semaglutide also showed a protective effect on cardiovascular events (OR = 0.64; p = 0.006).ConclusionOral semaglutide demonstrates meaningful improvements in cardiometabolic outcomes and a favorable safety profile, supporting its use as a non-invasive therapy for obesity and related metabolic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-026-01149-5.Keywords: Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Semaglutide, Obesity, Cardiometabolic risk factors, Body weight

Introduction

Obesity is considered a major public health challenge, affecting over 650 million people and increasing across all regions and age groups [1]. Cardiometabolic risk factors, including insulin resistance, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), dyslipidemia, hypertension, and chronic inflammation, are strongly associated with obesity, all of which contribute to elevated cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. Although lifestyle modifications are fundamental to obesity treatment, their long-term effectiveness remains limited, and most individuals require additional pharmacological intervention for meaningful weight reduction as there is genetic component to obesity [4, 5].

Pharmacological treatment of obesity has primarily focused on central appetite regulation and caloric expenditure [6]; thereafter, therapeutic options effective in improving impaired lipid profiles and glycemic control have gained interest for ameliorating secondary complications of obesity and improving cardiovascular outcomes. As a less toxic and inexpensive alternative, plant-based remedies such as Corchorus Olitorius have been explored to evaluate their safety and efficacy on glycemic control, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, and oxidative stress biomarkers in rodent models of obesity and T2DM. These studies have demonstrated significant reductions in blood glucose, body weight, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), along with increases in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). However, these preclinical findings have shown limited translation into clinical settings [7].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), including exenatide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, and semaglutide, have emerged as effective treatments for both obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). They help improve glycemic control and durable weight reduction by enhancing insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon release, and slowing gastric emptying [8]. Additionally, results from multiple cardiovascular outcome trials have demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and enhance overall cardiometabolic health [9–11].

Semaglutide is a long-acting GLP-1 RAs approved for the management of T2DM and obesity, and is the only drug in this class available in both injectable and oral formulations [12, 13]. Injectable Semaglutide is often associated with adherence challenges, whereas its oral formulation offers greater convenience and may enhance compliance [14, 15]. Moreover, growing evidence suggests that oral semaglutide may favorably influence additional cardiometabolic risk factors such as blood pressure, glycemic control, and body weight, with mostly dose-related gastrointestinal side effects [12, 16].

Despite these findings, the overall impact of oral Semaglutide on cardiometabolic risk factors in obese patients, both with and without T2DM, remains uncertain. Although some studies have shown strong improvements in glycemic control, and body weight outcomes, others have found limited or inconsistent results compared with subcutaneous administration [17–20]. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effects of oral semaglutide on cardiometabolic risk factors and adverse events in obese patients.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [21]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the following registration code: CRD420251124286. As this study synthesizes data from previously published reports, Ethics approval was not required.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted on 25 August 2025 across three databases: Embase, PubMed, and Scopus. The search focused on identifying studies evaluating the effect of oral semaglutide on cardiometabolic risk factors and adverse events in overweight/obese individuals (with or without T2DM). We used search strings combining MeSH/Emtree and free-text terms for the following keywords: Core keywords included: “semaglutide”, “oral semaglutide”, “Rybelsus”, “Wegovy”, “obesity”, “overweight”, “cardiometabolic risk factors”, “body weight”, “body mass index”, “waist circumference”, “diabetes mellitus”, “blood glucose”, “HbA1c”, “hypertension”, and “cardiovascular outcomes”. The complete search strategy is provided in Supplemental material. Table S2. To increase sensitivity, no limitation was implemented at the search stage.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies recognized with PICO criteria included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that examined the effect of oral semaglutide intervention (I) compared with placebo (C), in an overweight or obese population (P), and reported outcomes on cardiometabolic risk factors (O). Studies were excluded if they compared oral semaglutide with non-placebo group, had a non-randomized/observational design, were review articles, case reports, duplicate publications, commentary studies, or letters without extractable data.

Screening and study selection

Bibliographic records from all databases were imported into Rayyan and de-duplicated by 2 authors (N.S. and M.E.) [22]. Four reviewers (S.P.M., F.T., M.M., and P.R.) independently screened titles and abstracts, and assessed full texts of studies based on the pre-specified eligibility criteria, documenting reasons for exclusion. Particular attention was given to identifying overlapping populations or duplicate trial reports; in such cases, only the most complete dataset was included to avoid double counting. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus and adjudication by two different authors (N.S. and M.S.Gh.).

Risk of bias assessment (quality assessment)

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was used to evaluate the quality of RCTs [23] by 2 investigators (A.K. and P.R.), and studies were classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Any disagreements were resolved by the third author (N.S.).

Data extraction and outcomes

Two authors (A.K. and M.E.) independently extracted data using Microsoft Excel (Redmond, USA) with a pre-piloted template. The extracted data included study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, country, sample size per arm), intervention regimen (dose, dose-escalation schedule, follow-up duration), baseline characteristics of participants (mean age, mean BMI, gender percentage), and clinical outcomes. Graphical data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer version 4.5, a validated tool commonly used in systematic reviews for extracting numerical data from published figures. Previous studies have demonstrated high accuracy and reliability of this tool (Drevon, D., Fursa, S. R., & Malcolm, A. L. (2017). Intercoder Reliability and Validity of WebPlotDigitizer in Extracting Graphed Data. Behavior Modification, 41(2), 323–339). The Excel spreadsheet was then rechecked by a third author (N.S.). Since many heterogeneous data points were reported across the included studies, they were divided into two analysis windows: early (≤ 6 months) and longer-term (> 6 months). Our primary outcomes of interest were changes from baseline in HbA1c, BMI, body weight (in kilograms and %), and waist circumference. Secondary outcomes included systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and adverse events.

Data synthesis (statistical analysis)

All analyses for this systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using Stata statistical software, version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A random-effects model was applied in all analyses, utilizing the REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood) method for weighting and accounting for the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among studies. For continuous data, the Estimated Treatment Difference (ETD), representing the absolute Mean Difference (MD), with 95% CI of the ETD was used as the effect estimate. For events, Log RR (95% CI) was calculated, and for proportions, Log OR (95% CI) was used as the effect size. Statistical heterogeneity between studies was assessed with Cochran’s Q test and expressed as the I² statistic, where lower values indicate low heterogeneity (I² <25%), moderate (I² = 50%), or high (I² > 75%); a P < 0.10 from the Q test was considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. The statistical significance of the overall pooled effect was evaluated using the Z-test, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 deemed significant. The combined effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI of the pooled estimates did not overlap the null line (zero for ETD, Log RR, and Log OR).

Subgroup analysis

To examine potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed when enough data were available (defined as a minimum of two studies per subgroup category). Differences between subgroups were evaluated using the Qb test. For continuous outcomes, the subgroup analysis was divided by oral semaglutide dose. For proportion-based outcomes, subgroup analysis was conducted based on both the semaglutide dose and the follow-up duration.

Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated across all outcomes [24]. A visual examination of funnel plot symmetry was conducted, and formal statistical tests for small-study effects were performed using Egger’s regression asymmetry test and Begg’s rank correlation test. A p-value exceeding 0.10 for both tests was considered to indicate no significant publication bias.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A systematic search across online databases retrieved 4,636 records after duplicate removal. Through title and abstract screening, 30 studies were identified for full-text screening. Finally, a total of 13 RCTs involving 26,284 participants, published between 2019 and 2025, were eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis. (Fig. 1)

PRISMA flow diagram

The demographic characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. All 13 RCTs involved patients with overweight (BMI > 25) or obesity (BMI > 30) who were randomized to take oral semaglutide or placebo, regardless of diabetes affection [12, 25–36]. The total number of participants was 26,284, of whom 52.3% received oral semaglutide and 47.7% received a placebo. The weighted mean age of the semaglutide and placebo groups was 63.74 ± 10.42 and 65.31 ± 9.86, respectively. Female ratio of the semaglutide group was 30.1% and for the placebo group weas 33.3%. The weighted baseline BMI (mean ± sd) in semaglutide and placebo group were 31.67 ± 6.11 and 31.91 ± 6.25, respectively. The duration of diabetes among studies involving the diabetic population (n = 10) varied from 3 to 15.5 years, with a mean weighted estimate of 14.4 years. The durations of follow-up widely ranged between 12 and 208 weeks, with a weighted mean of 165.2 weeks. The semaglutide doses varied from 2.5 mg to 50 mg once daily across studies.

Primary outcomes

Body weight

Body weight changes (% and kg) from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

The random-effects meta-analyses of weight changes showed pooled ETDs of − 3.09% (95% CI: −4.24 to − 1.95; p < 0.001) and − 2.85 kg (95% CI: −4.03 to − 1.66; p < 0.001), indicating significant reductions in body weight by oral semaglutide, irrespective of the dose, compared to placebo at 6 months (Fig. 2. A and 2. B, respectively). For follow-up periods beyond 6 months, the pooled ETDs of − 7.07% (95% CI: −11.75 to − 2.38; p < 0.001) and − 5.70 kg (95% CI: −8.43 to − 2.96; p < 0.001) were estimated, suggesting significant and sustained reductions in body weight, though greater in magnitude compared to the first 6 months (Figure S2 and S3, respectively). The subgroup analyses showed significantly greater reductions observed at higher doses in all timeframes (Figure S4-7).

Weight loss ≥ 5% and ≥ 10% of body weight during the follow-up

Our analysis showed pooled log RRs of 1.27 (95% CI: 0.98 to 1.57; p < 0.001) and 1.78 (95% CI: 1.55 to 2.02; p < 0.001) for achieving weight loss ≥ 5% and ≥ 10% of body weight (Fig. 2 C and 2. D, respectively). The corresponding RR were 3.56 (95% CI: 2.66 to 4.80) and 5.93 (95% CI: 4.71 to 7.54), indicating that patients receiving oral semaglutide were significantly more likely to achieve weight loss ≥ 5% and ≥ 10% compared with those receiving placebo, during all follow-up periods (Figure S8 and S9, respectively).

BMI changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

The random-effects meta-analyses of BMI changes demonstrated a significant reduction in BMI by semaglutide compared to placebo at 6 months with a pooled ETD of − 0.66 kg/m² (95% CI: −0.95 to − 0.36; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3. A) that were further enhanced beyond 6 months of treatment with pooled ETD of − 2.27 kg/m² (95% CI: −3.48 to − 1.06; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3. B). Among the three analyzed doses of semaglutide, 7<–≤14 mg/day showed the greatest reduction in BMI, whereas doses ≤ 3 mg did not significantly reduce BMI at 6 months (Figure S10). The dose-dependent relationship also persisted beyond 6 months, as the greatest reduction was associated with doses of > 14 mg (Figure S11).

Waist circumference changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

A significant decrease in waist circumference was shown by semaglutide at 6 months with a pooled ETD of − 1.79 cm (95% CI: −2.63 to − 0.95; p < 0.001), whereas beyond 6 months of treatment, semaglutide significantly decreased this measure even more profoundly (pooled ETD − 6.06 cm (95% CI: −9.19 to − 2.93; p < 0.001)). (Fig. 4. A and 4. B). Substantial heterogeneity existed among studies. Subgroup analysis revealed a dose-dependent trend with significant between-dose differences. However, all doses except doses under 3 mg significantly decreased waist circumference at 6 months (Figure S12). The dose-dependent trend with significant between-dose differences was also consistent in long-term (Figure S13).

HbA1c

HbA1c changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

The random-effects meta-analysis of HbA1c changes at 6 months resulted in a pooled estimated treatment difference (ETD) of − 0.94% (95% CI: −1.13 to − 0.76; p < 0.001), indicating a significant HbA1c reduction in the oral semaglutide group, regardless of the dose, compared to placebo at 6 months (Figure S64.A). A significant and sustained HbA1c reduction was also observed beyond 6 months of treatment (pooled ETD − 0.53% (95% CI: −0.72 to − 0.34; p < 0.001)) (Figure S64.B), although less pronounced compared to the first 6 months. Further subgroup analysis by dose showed a dose-dependent trend in HbA1c reduction with significantly greater reductions at higher doses in 6 months, which did not reveal significant differences after 6 months (Figures S14 and S15).

HbA1c reaching the targets of ≤ 6.5% and ≤ 7% during the follow-up

The meta-analyses yielded pooled log risk ratios (log RR) of 1.61 (95% CI: 1.18 to 2.03; p < 0.001) and 1.25 (95% CI: 0.93 to 1.56; p < 0.001) for achieving HbA1c of ≤ 6.5% and 7%, respectively (Figure S64. C and S64. D, respectively). The corresponding risk ratios (RR) were estimated to be respectively 5.00 (95% CI: 3.25 to 7.61) and 3.49 (95% CI: 2.54 to 4.76), indicating that patients receiving oral semaglutide were significantly more likely to achieve HbA1c targets compared with those receiving placebo, during all follow-up periods. Subgroup analyses in both HbA1c targets of 6.5% and 7% revealed no statistically significant difference (Figure S16 and S17).

Secondary outcomes

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

Oral semaglutide significantly decreased SBP at 6 months (pooled ETD − 2.71 mmHg; 95% CI − 3.70 to − 1.72; p < 0.001) with no heterogeneity, whereas a slightly greater decrease in SBP was found beyond 6 months of semaglutide treatment (pooled ETD − 2.79 mmHg; 95% CI − 4.39 to − 1.19; p < 0.001) (Figure S18 and S19, respectively). No statistically significant differences were retrieved among dose categories in subgroup analysis, although a dose-dependent trend could be observed in SBP reductions at 6 months (Figure S20). Semaglutide doses ≤ 7 mg/day failed to decrease SBP significantly after 6 months of treatment, yet there were no statistically significant between-dose differences similar to the first 6 months (Figure S21).

Diastolic blood pressure (DBP) changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

A modest yet statistically significant decrease in DBP was shown by semaglutide at 6 months (pooled ETD − 0.77 mmHg (95% CI: −1.38 to − 0.17; p = 0.01) with negligible heterogeneity (Figure S22). On the contrary, DBP was not significantly reduced by semaglutide after 6 months of treatment (pooled ETD − 0.69 mmHg; 95% CI − 1.45 to 0.07; p = 0.08) (Figure S23). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant between-dose difference, with the 7<–≤14 mg/day group being the only subgroup associated with a statistically significant reduction in DBP at 6 months, whereas it was not significant beyond 6 months (Figure S24 and S25, respectively).

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) changes from baseline at 6 months and beyond 6 months

Oral semaglutide significantly reduced FPG at 6 months with a remarkable pooled ETD of − 26.91 mg/dL (95% CI: −33.56 to − 20.26; p < 0.001) (Figure S26). Although the FPG reduction remained statistically significant beyond 6 months, the magnitude was approximately halved compared to the first 6 months of treatment (pooled ETD − 12.39 mg/dL (95% CI: −19.20 to − 5.57; p < 0.001) (Figure S27). Subgroup analysis showed progressively greater FPG reductions associated with higher doses of semaglutide in 6 months (Figure S28 and S29).

Safety outcomes

Based on number of “participants” experienced each adverse event (AE); the most frequently reported AE were gastrointestinal related, with nausea as the most common AE which 24.8% of participants in semaglutide group experienced, following by diarrhea (13.7%), and vomiting (13.0%) in comparison with the placebo group (9.7%, 8.7%, 3.2%, respectively).

The meta-analysis of adverse events based on the number of “events” from the on-treatment observation period demonstrated that oral semaglutide was associated with significantly increased risks of gastrointestinal(GI)-related issues including remarkably higher risk of vomiting (odds ratio (OR) = 6.25; 95% CI [4.17, 9.23]; p < 0.001) followed by nausea (OR = 3.13; 95% CI [2.18, 4.48]; p < 0.001), overall GI disorders (OR 2.69; 95% CI [2.43, 2.98]; p < 0.001), and dyspepsia (OR = 1.86; 95% CI [1.26, 2.72]; p = 0.002), ordered by their OR. Although patients in the semaglutide group experienced a protective effect for cardiovascular events by semaglutide (OR = 0.64; 95% CI [0.47, 0.88]; p = 0.006). Regarding other GI-related issues, showed no statistically significant trends compared to placebo. On the other hand, the risks of nasopharyngitis (OR 0.46 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.60; p < 0.001)), fatal adverse events (OR 0.42 (95% CI: 0.26, 0.70; p < 0.001)), and serious adverse events (OR 0.52 (95% CI: 0.44, 0.60; p < 0.001)) were significantly reduced by semaglutide compared to placebo. (Table S3 and Figure S30-S43)

Publication bias

According to visual inspection of the funnel plots and quantitative measurements using Egger’s test, there was no evidence of publication bias at 6 months in primary or secondary outcomes, except for weight (kg) and FPG changes, with Egger’s test p-value 0.048 and 0.034 (Figure S44 and S50, respectively). The publication bias seemed to be consistently at higher risks regarding long-term outcomes as seen in BMI, HBA1c, weight (% and kg), and DBP changes beyond 6 months with Egger’s test p-value < 0.05 (Figure S59, S59, S47, S45, and S63, respectively). This indicates potential publication bias with the presence of a small study effect.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 RCTs demonstrated the significant cardiometabolic benefits of oral daily semaglutide in over 26,000 participants suffering from overweight or obesity, with or without diabetes. Six months of treatment with oral semaglutide once daily was associated with significant reductions in multiple metabolic outcomes, including HbA1c, body weight, BMI, SBP, DBP, FPG, and waist circumference. These benefits declared dose-dependent trends with greater reductions achieved at higher doses during the first 6 months. The long-term durability of semaglutide’s cardiometabolic benefits was confirmed by sustained significant improvements in all outcomes except for DBP.

The cardiovascular protective effect of oral semaglutide has been established in our study, supporting the macrovascular benefits as a class effect of semaglutide in consistent with previous studies [27]. The Semaglutide Cardiovascular Outcomes Trial (SOUL) by McGuire et al. [39] confirmed the benefit of a maximally tolerated dose of oral semaglutide (14 mg daily) versus placebo among high-risk Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) patients with a 14% relative risk reduction in Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE). These data are consistent with data from prior large trials of the injectable formulation, SELECT (subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg in obesity without diabetes) reported an HR of 0.80 [40] and SUSTAIN 6 (subcutaneous semaglutide 0.5 mg /1.0 mg in T2DM) [41] which showed reduced MACE.

Regarding equivalent metabolic effectiveness, semaglutide often has higher effectiveness among the class of GLP-1 RAs. In a detailed meta-analysis performed by Karimi et al.[42]. Semaglutide was compared to other injectable products, and semaglutide produced statistically lower HbA1c than Liraglutide (Hedges’ g: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.94; p < 0.001), and demonstrated significantly better effectiveness against Dulaglutide in HbA1c lowering (Hedges’ g: 3.72; 95% CI: 0.02 to 7.41; p = 0.05). This enhanced metabolic effectiveness, with a convenient oral route of administration and known CV protection, leads oral semaglutide to be a highly effective non-injectable option for patients who need high levels of cardiometabolic risk management.

In addition to being compared to other GLP-1 RAs, oral semaglutide has strong metabolic advantages compared to other established classes of oral antihyperglycemic agents (AHAs). When pooled against active comparators, such as SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., Empagliflozin) [43], Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 (DDP-4) inhibitors (e.g., Sitagliptin) [44–46], and the injectable Liraglutide [47]. Several meta-analyses consistently conclude that oral semaglutide has a better metabolic effect [48–50], so that Jingxin Li et al. [51] showed treatment with oral semaglutide was associated with a Mean Difference (MD) of -0.33% (95% CI: -0.46% to -0.21%) for HbA1c and − 1.52 kg (95% CI: -2.17 kg to -0.88 kg) for weight loss, confirming overall metabolic advantage. This metabolic advantage extends to cardiovascular risk factors as well, as Avgerinos et al.[48] reported oral semaglutide had a beneficial effect on reducing systolic blood pressure, even compared to other AHAs.

In terms of DDP-4 inhibitors, the clinical distinction is enormous. This is primarily because, in general, DDP-4 inhibitors are weight neutral [45, 52]. In the PIONEER 7 trial by Pieber et al.[53], oral semaglutide with flexible dosing was shown to be significantly better than fixed-dose sitagliptin, with much higher proportions of people reaching the HbA1c goal of < 7% (OR: 4.40) and significantly greater weight-loss outcomes (ETD: -1.9 kg) over 52 weeks [45]. On the other hand, while oral semaglutide was able to establish a metabolic benefit over the SGLT2 agent empagliflozin, both the semaglutide and SGLT2 class offer cardioprotection, but with different mechanisms [43].

The extensive metabolic benefits of semaglutide stem from its role as a long-acting version of native GLP-1, which is highly resistant to the metabolic inactivation of DPP-4 [54]. Glycemic control is accomplished through GLP-1’s known glucose-dependent mechanism, which potentiates insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells while simultaneously inhibiting inappropriate glucagon secretion from alpha cells, thereby inhibiting hepatic glucose production [54, 55]. The combination of these effects contributes to the highly significant and durable reductions in HbA1c and FPG observed [54].

The substantial and clinically meaningful reductions in body mass observed with oral semaglutide occur primarily through its action in the central nervous system (CNS) in pathways that control hunger and satiety [56]. GLP-1 receptors are present in important brain centers, especially the hypothalamus and brainstem [54, 56]. Moreover, activation of central GLP-1 receptors decreases ingestive behavior via increased satiety signaling, which translates into decreased energy consumption and ultimately weight loss [57]. Additionally, semaglutide also helps this process peripherally by delaying gastric emptying, which reinforces physical fullness and limits calorie intake [58].

Not only does semaglutide have anti-inflammatory properties, but it also confers important hemodynamic effects [49, 51]. Semaglutide promotes optimal endothelial function through the increased secretion of nitric oxide (NO), a well-known vasodilator [59]. The drug also has effects on fluid regulation and natriuresis, likely through an increase in atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) levels, resulting in the notable and consistent decrease in systolic blood pressure that has been demonstrated in clinical trials [12, 27, 36].

Our results demonstrated that oral semaglutide can be an effective non-invasive therapeutic choice for overweight/obese patients as cardiometabolic protection. It can be used alongside dietary modification and physical activity by amplifying both weight loss and cardio-metabolic improvements with accessibility, better adherence, and patient preference compared to injectable form [14, 15].

The current meta-analysis possesses strengths that contribute to the robustness of our findings and their potential implications for the field. We comprehensively searched databases and included all the relevant studies, which ensured that our analysis was comprehensive and not limited to specific regions or countries. We also included randomized controlled trials with a placebo-controlled group to ensure a high level of evidence. The results of our studies should be interpreted in the context of some limitations. The results obtained from studies showed heterogeneity, which we assessed by the source by random-effect model analysis and subgroup analysis. As the population of most of the included studies was diabetic except for 3 of them, analyzing outcomes for diabetic and non-diabetic populations separately was not available in which is an important point. Also, the use of digitization tools may have introduced minor extraction errors. We suggest future studies assess oral semaglutide effects on the cardiometabolic risk of overweight/obese patients without diabetes. Also, Comparative studies with other pharmacologic and lifestyle interventions, as well as real-world data on cost-effectiveness and adherence, are essential to help clinicians’ decisions.

Conclusion

Our results showed that oral semaglutide can be an effective non-invasive therapeutic choice for overweight/obese patients with or without diabetes, as a cardiometabolic protection. Weight loss, decreasing BMI, glycemic control and blood pressure control are challenges for overweight and obese patients which clinicians can consider oral semaglutide in regimen to achieve these therapeutic endpoints. However, precise caution is warranted due to limitations in the available evidence. Further high-quality studies are required to confirm its long-term efficacy and safety before definitive clinical recommendations can be established.
