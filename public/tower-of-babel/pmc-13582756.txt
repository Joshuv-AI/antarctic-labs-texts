A conserved miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 regulatory axis enhances type I IFN antiviral responses in teleost fish
Authors: Jiamei Liu, Huazhi Chen, Wenxing Li, Yinnan Mu, Xinhua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type I interferon (IFN) signaling plays an essential role in restricting viral infection. Accumulating evidence indicates that microRNAs (miRNAs) serve as key regulators of the type I IFN signaling in mammals. However, whether the miRNA-mediated regulation is involved in type I IFN signaling in teleost fish remains unknown. In large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea, Lc), we found that miR-7132a induced by poly(I: C) or type I IFN LcIFNi promotes type I IFN responses in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, miR-7132a directly targets the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1 and inhibits LcSOCS1 expression by reducing its mRNA stability and repressing its protein translation. LcSOCS1 promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination at K382 residue of LcSTAT2 via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, leading to proteasomal degradation of LcSTAT2. By reducing LcSOCS1-mediated degradation of LcSTAT2, miR-7132a promotes LcSTAT2-dependent type I IFN antiviral responses. Notably, miR-7132a-mediated inhibition of SOCS1 is also present in other teleost species. Collectively, these findings reveal a novel positive regulatory axis by which miR-7132a promotes type I IFN antiviral responses by suppressing SOCS1-mediated STAT2 degradation. Thus, this study provides valuable insights into miRNA-mediated regulatory axis of type I IFN responses in teleost fish.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06378-9.

Introduction

The type I interferon (IFN) signaling pathway plays a vital role in antiviral immunity in host cells, and genetic defects in this pathway render individuals unable to effectively control viral infections [1, 2]. The type I IFN induced by viruses binds to IFN-α/β receptors (IFNAR), causing the receptors to dimerize and activate Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and tyrosine kinase 2 (Tyk2) [3]. JAK1 and Tyk2 then phosphorylate the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) and STAT2. Activated STAT1 and STAT2 form heterodimers through their SRC homology 2 (SH2) domain-phosphotyrosine interactions [3]. The heterodimers bind to IFN regulatory factor 9 (IRF9) to form the IFN-stimulated gene factor 3 (ISGF3) complex [4, 5]. The ISGF3 complex translocates into the nucleus and binds to IFN-stimulated response elements (ISRE), leading to the transcription of numerous IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) to inhibit virus infection [4, 5]. To date, although the mechanisms that activate the type I IFN pathway to clear viral infections have been well described, the precise mechanisms that fine-tune this pathway remain poorly understood.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a family of small non-coding RNAs that bind to the 3’UTR of target mRNAs through their seed sequences, resulting in mRNA degradation or translational repression, and play critical roles in type I IFN antiviral responses. For example, miR-373 is upregulated during hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection and negatively regulates the type I IFN signaling pathway by targeting JAK1 and IRF9 in primary human hepatocytes [6]. MiR-208b and miR-499a dampen type I IFN signaling to negatively regulate antiviral immune responses in HCV-infected hepatocytes by directly down-regulating the expression of IFNAR1 [7]. In Sendai virus- and vesicular stomatitis virus-infected macrophages, miR-466i could directly target IFNα expression to inhibit host antiviral innate immune responses [8]. These studies demonstrate that miRNAs play crucial roles as regulatory factors in modulating the antiviral responses mediated by the type I IFN signaling pathway in mammals. Nevertheless, studies on the miRNA-mediated regulation of antiviral immunity via the type I IFN pathway in other vertebrates, such as teleost fish, remain limited.

Suppressors of Cytokine Signaling 1 (SOCS1) is a potent negative regulator of the type I IFN pathway, ensuring the generation of an appropriate immune response to viral infections [9]. SOCS1 contains a variable N-terminal region, a central SH2 domain, a conserved C-terminal SOCS box domain, and an N-terminal extended SH2 subdomain (ESS) [9]. SOCS1 recruits Elongin B/C via its SOCS box domain to form an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that tags target proteins for proteasome-mediated degradation, such as JAK1 [10], Tyk2 [11], and STAT1 (Unpublished data). To date, mechanisms by which SOCS1 mediates the regulation of STAT2 in the type I IFN pathway remain unclear.

Aquaculture provides a rich source of protein for humans, yet viral diseases in farmed fish, which progress rapidly, cause high mortality and lack effective treatments, remain a major obstacle to sustainable aquaculture [12]. Therefore, understanding fish antiviral immune mechanisms is essential for developing effective prevention strategies and therapeutics against viral diseases in aquaculture. In large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea), our previous studies revealed the crystal structure of LcIFNd and LcIFNi [13, 14], the negative regulatory role of LcSOCS3 in antiviral responses [15], and the mechanism by which LcSOCS1 negatively regulates type I IFN antiviral responses via degrading LcSTAT1 (Unpublished data). In addition, we have obtained genome-wide miRNA transcriptomic data of L. crocea, and functionally characterized several key miRNAs involved in host immune regulation [16–18]. However, whether the miRNA-mediated regulatory axis contributes to antiviral immunity in fish has not been explored.

Here, we found that miR-7132a induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi positively regulates type I IFN antiviral responses in L. crocea. Mechanistically, miR-7132a inhibits LcSOCS1 expression by reducing its mRNA stability and repressing its protein translation via directly targeting the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1. LcSOCS1 interacts with LcSTAT2 and promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination at K382 residue of LcSTAT2 for proteasomal degradation via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Further, miR-7132a weakens the inhibitory effect of LcSOCS1 on the LcSTAT2-mediated antiviral immune responses. The miR-7132a-mediated inhibition of SOCS1 is also present in other fish species. These findings indicate that the miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 regulatory axis plays a crucial role in sustaining type I IFN-mediated antiviral immune responses, offering valuable insights into the regulatory axis of fish antiviral immunity.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

Large yellow croaker head kidney (LYCK) cells (RRID: CVCL_HP90), previously established by us [19], were cultured in medium 199 (M199; Gibco, cat#C11150500BT) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, cat#A5670701) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, cat#15140122) at 28 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, cat#11995073) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Epithelioma papulosum cyprini (EPC) cells (ATCC CRL-2872) were maintained in M199 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 28 °C and 5% CO2. Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV; ATCC VR-1390) was propagated in EPC cells at 28 °C and harvested when more than 80% virus-induced cytopathic effect appeared.

Reagents, miRNA oligoribonucleotides, and antibodies

Poly(I: C) (cat#P9882) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich. The recombinant LcIFNi was expressed and purified according to established protocols [14]. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; cat#PWL064), proteasome inhibitor MG132 (cat#MB5137-1), autophagy inhibitor NH4Cl (cat#MB2697-1), 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; cat#MA0128) were purchased from MeilunBio. Actinomycin D (cat#W10655) was purchased from YuanyeBio. MiRNA mimic and miRNA inhibitor were synthesized by Tsingke, and the sequences of the miRNAs are listed in Table S1.

Rabbit antibodies against Flag tag (cat#20543-1-AP), Myc tag (cat#16286-1-AP), HA tag (cat#51064-2-AP), and β-Tubulin (cat#10068-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech. Mouse antibodies against Myc tag (cat#60003-2-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech. HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies (cat#SA00001-1), HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (cat#SA00001-2), CoraLite647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (cat#SA00014-9), CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies (cat#SA00013-1), and rabbit IgG antibodies (cat#30000-0-AP), mouse anti-IgG (cat#B900620) antibodies, and mouse anti-Ago2 (cat#67934-1-Ig) antibodies were obtained from Proteintech. Mouse anti-Flag Beads (cat#L-1011), mouse anti-Myc Beads (cat#L-1010), and mouse anti-HA Beads (cat#L-1009) were purchased from Biolinkedin. Rabbit antibodies against LcSOCS1, LcSTAT2, and LcMxA were custom-made by GL Biochem.

Bioinformatic analysis

Potential regulatory miRNAs for LcSOCS1 were predicted via PITA [20] and miRanda algorithms [21] using miRNA transcriptomic data from the large yellow croaker (Accession numbers: SRR21397128-SRR21397139) [16]. The predicted miRNAs were ranked based on their targeting efficacy. Precursor miR-7132a (pre-miR-7132a) sequences and location information were obtained from the miRBase database and whole-genome data of various teleost fish species (Table S2). A multiple sequence alignment of pre-miR-7132a and the miR-7132a binding site in the 3’UTR of SOCS1 from these teleost fish species was performed using DNAMAN8 software [22]. Additionally, the docking model of the LcSTAT2-LcSOCS1 protein sequences was generated by AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/?golgi=truever), and PyMOLWin software [23] was used to visualize the model. The ubiquitination sites in the DBD domain of STAT2 were predicted by MusiteDeep [24]. A multiple sequence alignment of STAT2 protein from multiple vertebrate species was performed using DNAMAN8 software.

Plasmid construction

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms by which miR-7132a directly targets the 3’UTR of SOCS1 in teleost fish, these plasmids were constructed as follows: The 3’-UTR of LcSOCS1 mRNA (Accession number: XM_027281353.1) was amplified via PCR using specific primers. The resulting PCR products were digested and ligated into the pmirGLO plasmid (Promega), designated as pmirGLO-LcSOCS1-3’UTR-WT. A mutant within the miR-7132a binding sites in the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1 was constructed and designated as pmirGLO-LcSOCS1-3’UTR-MUT. Myc-tagged expression plasmids for LcSOCS1 were constructed by inserting its CDS and 3’UTR sequences into the pcDNA3.1+/N-Myc expression vector (GenScript) with Myc tag, designated as LcSOCS1-3’UTR-Myc. The wild-type reporter vectors and mutant reporter vectors within the miR-7132a binding site in the SOCS1 3’UTR of brown-marbled grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus) and European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax) were synthesized by Tsingke. Primer sequences are supplied in Table S1.

To investigate the mechanisms by which SOCS1 promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination at K382 of STAT2 for proteasomal degradation via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, these plasmids were constructed as following: The ORF of LcSTAT2 (GenBank accession: XM_010747091.3) was amplified by reverse transcription (RT)-PCR using specific primers and inserted into pCMV-FLAG-MAT-Tag-1 expression vector (Sigma) tagged with Flag tag, designated as LcSTAT2-Flag. Similarly, LcSTAT2 mutants lacking the ND, CCD, DBD, SH2, and TAD domains (designated as LcSTAT2-△ND-Flag, LcSTAT2-△CCD-Flag, LcSTAT2-△DBD-Flag, LcSTAT2-SH2-Flag, and LcSTAT2-TAD-Flag, respectively) and two LcSTAT2 single-residue mutants (LcSTAT2-K380R-Flag and LcSTAT2-K382R-Flag) were also constructed. Various expression vectors of LcSOCS1 were constructed in previous studies, including the wild-type expression vector of LcSOCS1 (LcSOCS1-Myc), LcSOCS1 mutants lacking the KIR, ESS, SH2, and SOCS-BOX domains (designated as LcSOCS1-△KIR-Myc, LcSOCS1-△ESS-Myc, LcSOCS1-△SH2-Myc, and LcSOCS1-△SOCS-BOX-Myc, respectively), and a mutant within the elongin B/C binding site in LcSOCS1 (L213C217/P213P217, namely LcSOCS1LC−PP-Myc) (Unpublished data). The construction of the LcMxA-promoter reporter plasmid has been described previously [14]. Primer sequences are supplied in Table S1.

Additional plasmids, including pRL-TK, promoter-ISRE, pRK5-HA-Ubiquitin-WT, pRK5-HA-Ubiquitin-K48, pRK5-HA-Ubiquitin-K63, and pRK5-HA-Ubiquitin-K48R were obtained from Addgene. All recombinant plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All plasmids were extracted using the MaxPure Plasmid EF HC kit (Magen).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

RT-qPCR analysis was carried out as previously described [25]. Total RNAs were extracted from treated cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, cat#15596026CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA underwent reverse transcription into first-strand cDNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, cat#R312-02) with stem-loop primers or oligo(dT)20 primers. RT-qPCR analysis was performed by using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, cat#Q711-02) on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). β-Actin (Accession number: XM_027284923.1) and U6 (Accession number: XR_003462831.1) served as endogenous controls for mRNA and miRNA normalization, respectively. The relative expression levels were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. RT-qPCR primers are listed in Table S1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were carried out as previously described [26]. LYCK cells (2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) in 24-well plates were transfected with the indicated plasmids and miRNA oligonucleotides using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Transfection Reagent (Yeasen, cat#40802ES03). After 48 h, the cells were twice washed with ice-cold PBS, and lysates were prepared by incubation with (1×) Passive Lysis Buffer for 10 min. The cell lysate was centrifuged, and luciferase activity was assessed via a GloMax Discover (Promega) according to the dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, cat#E1910) protocol. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase to renilla luciferase activity.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays

RIP assays were performed using the BeyoRIP™ RIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, cat#P1801S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. LYCK cells (1 × 107 cells/dish) were transfected with 100 nM miR-7132a mimic or NC mimic using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Transfection Reagent. After 48 h, the cells were incubated with protein A/G beads, followed by incubation with mouse anti-IgG or mouse anti-Ago2 antibodies. Total RNAs were separated from the immunoprecipitates using TRIzol reagent, after which the enrichment levels of LcSOCS1 were detected via RT-qPCR.

RNA pulldown assays

RNA pulldown assays were performed according to established protocols [17]. Biotin-modified NC mimic and miR-7132a mimic were synthesized by Tsingke. LYCK (1 × 107 cells/dish) were transfected with 100 nM biotin-modified miR-7132a mimic or NC mimic using Hieff Trans® Liposomal Transfection Reagent. After 48 h, the biotinylated miRNA-mRNA complexes were isolated using streptavidin agarose (Bersinbio, cat#Bes5102). Total RNA was separated from the streptavidin agarose using TRIzol reagent, after which the enrichment levels of LcSOCS1 were detected via RT-qPCR.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and Immunoblotting assays

Co-IP and immunoblotting assays were performed according to established protocols [27]. Briefly, HEK-293T cells in 10-cm dishes (1 × 107 cells/dish) were transfected with the plasmids as shown in the figures using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, cat#C0533). After 48 h, the cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, cat#P0013). For the IP assays, protein complexes were precipitated using anti-Flag Beads, anti-Myc Beads or anti-HA Beads at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed five times with IP lysis buffer, and then eluted with SDS-PAGE loading buffer. For immunoblotting, denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, cat#ISEQ00010). The membranes were blocked with a protein-free blocking buffer, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-Flag (1: 10,000), rabbit anti-Myc (1: 10,000), rabbit anti-HA (1: 10,000) and rabbit anti-β-Tubulin (1: 10,000). The membranes were further probed with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (1: 10,000). Signal detection was performed using an Azure Imager C500 system (Azure Biosystems).

Indirect immunofluorescence

Indirect immunofluorescence was performed according to established protocols [28]. Briefly, HEK-293T cells in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) were co-transfected with LcSOCS1-Myc and LcSTAT2-Flag using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent. After 24 h, the cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon, cat#E672002) at 4 °C overnight, and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (Sangon, cat#A110694) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following permeabilization, the cells were incubated with 5% BSA diluted in PBS for 1 h. The cells were labeled with mouse anti-Myc and rabbit anti-Flag antibodies (1: 200) at 4 °C for 30 min. Then, the cells were labeled with CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies (1: 200) and CoraLite647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (1: 200) for 30 min, and then stained with 2 µg/mL DAPI for 10 min. Finally, stained cells were observed under a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica).

Viral plaque assays

The plaque formation assays were carried out as previously described [15]. In brief, EPC cells (2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were plated in 24-well plates and transfected with the indicated plasmids and miRNA oligoribonucleotides using FishTrans (MeiSenTe, cat# FT2020-1). After 48 h, the cells were infected with SVCV (MOI = 0.001) and incubated at 28 °C for 2 h, after which the supernatants were substituted with M199 medium containing 1.2% methylcellulose, 2% FBS, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Following a 72-hour incubation period, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet to visualize plaques. Viral titers were calculated via the formula: PFUs/mL = 10ᵃ × b × (1000/c), where a represents the dilution factor of the virus-containing supernatant, b is the number of plaques formed, and c is the volume of the virus-containing supernatant used for infection.

In vivo assays

Large yellow croakers weighing 20 ± 0.18 g were obtained from a mariculture farm in Ningde, Fujian, China, and acclimatized for one week in aquaria with water at 2% salinity and 22 °C as approved by the College of Marine Sciences at Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. Fish were intraperitoneally injected with NC mimic or miR-7132a mimic at a dose of 1 nM/g [29]. After 24 h, fish were intraperitoneally injected with LcIFNi at 12 µg/g [30] or an equal volume of PBS. Twenty-four hours later, the spleen and head kidney of five fish from each group were collected for total RNA and protein extraction.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 was utilized to analyze and calculate data collected from three biological replicates. The figures present data as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). The significance test of data in figures was conducted using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. Significance levels were set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01.

Results

MiR-7132a positively regulates type I IFN responses in L. crocea

To screen the functional miRNA targeting LcSOCS1, we carried out a systematic candidate screening at the early stage of this study. Combined with large yellow croaker miRNA omics data and bioinformatics prediction, three candidate miRNAs (miR-7132a, miR-30a, and miR-181a) were predicted to interact with the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1 (Fig. S1A). Among them, miR-7132a obtained the optimal binding score in bioinformatics prediction (Fig. S1A). Dual-luciferase reporter detection and poly(I: C) or type I IFN stimulation assays were then performed to examine their targeting relationship and immune response characteristics. The results showed that only miR-7132a significantly suppressed LcSOCS1 3’UTR activity and exhibited specific upregulated expression upon poly(I: C) or type I IFN stimulation, whereas miR-30a and miR-181a had no obvious regulatory effect and upregulated expression pattern (Fig. S1B-S1D). Based on these data, we selected miR-7132a as the key molecule for subsequent functional and mechanistic analyses.

Then, we examined the effects of synthetic miR-7132a mimic and inhibitor on miR-7132a expression. We found that miR-7132a mimic significantly increased endogenous miR-7132a expression levels, while miR-7132a inhibitor significantly decreased endogenous miR-7132a expression levels under unstimulated and poly(I: C)- or LcIFNi-stimulated conditions (Fig. S2, Fig. 1A and G). The expression of antiviral genes including LcMxA, LcPKR, and LcViperin could be significantly induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi (Fig. S3). Subsequently, the regulatory role of miR-7132a in antiviral gene expression and type I IFN pathway signaling was investigated. We found that miR-7132a overexpression significantly promoted the expression of antiviral genes (LcMxA, LcPKR, and LcViperin), and the activity of ISRE promoter and LcMxA promoter induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi (Fig. 1B and F). Conversely, miR-7132a knockdown significantly inhibited the expression of antiviral genes, and the activity of ISRE promoter and LcMxA promoter induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi (Fig. 1H and L). Additionally, miR-7132a overexpression significantly enhanced LcMxA protein expression induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi, whereas miR-7132a knockdown markedly attenuated LcMxA protein expression (Fig. 1M and P). MiR-7132a inhibits LcSOCS1 expression by destabilizing its mRNA and repressing its protein translation via directly targeting the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1

We found that LcSOCS1, a negative molecule in the type I IFN pathway, was induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi (Fig. S4), and the expression trend of LcSOCS1 was negatively correlated with that of miR-7132a (Fig. 2A and B). Therefore, we speculated that LcSOCS1 might be a potential target of miR-7132a. Bioinformatics analysis showed that miR-7132a exhibited potential binding affinity to the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1, with 12 complementary base pairs (Fig. 2C). To validate the regulatory relationship between miR-7132a and LcSOCS1, the pmirGLO luciferase vector containing the 3’UTR sequences of LcSOCS1 (pmirGLO-LcSOCS1 3’UTR-WT) was constructed. We found that miR-7132a mimic significantly inhibited the luciferase activity of pmirGLO-LcSOCS1 3’UTR-WT in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 2D and E). RIP assays showed that significant enrichment of the endogenous LcSOCS1 mRNA was detected in Ago2 immunoprecipitates upon miR-7132a overexpression (Fig. 2F). RNA pull-down assays demonstrated that endogenous LcSOCS1 mRNA was abundantly pulled down by biotinylated miR-7132a mimic (Fig. 2G). A mutant (pmirGLO-LcSOCS1 3’UTR-MUT) was constructed, in which seven consecutive base pairs within the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1 were altered. To determine the function of miR-7132a in regulating the expression of LcSOCS1, we found that miR-7132a mimic inhibited the mRNA levels of LcSOCS1 in a dose-gradient manner, and significantly shortened the half-life of endogenous LcSOCS1 mRNA (Fig. 2J and K). MiR-7132a mimic significantly inhibited the protein levels of LcSOCS1 in a dose-gradient manner (Fig. 2L and M), and the suppressive effect of miR-7132a on LcSOCS1 protein levels was markedly more pronounced than that on its mRNA levels (Fig. 2J). Further, we investigated the effect of miR-7132a on LcSOCS1 expression upon stimulation with poly(I: C) or LcIFNi. We found that miR-7132a overexpression significantly suppressed the mRNA levels of LcSOCS1 induced by poly(I: C) or LcIFNi, whereas miR-7132a knockdown significantly promoted the mRNA levels of LcSOCS1 (Fig. 2N and O). These results indicate that miR-7132a inhibits LcSOCS1 expression by destabilizing its mRNA and repressing protein translation via directly targeting the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1.

LcSOCS1 interacts with the DBD domain of LcSTAT2 through its SH2 domain in the cytoplasm

To further investigate how miR-7132a positively regulates type I IFN pathway by targeting LcSOCS1, the regulatory mechanisms of LcSOCS1 on the type I IFN pathway need to be clarified. The bioinformatic analysis predicted that LcSTAT2 might be a target protein of LcSOCS1 (Table S3). To validate this prediction, we first investigated the interaction between LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2. Molecular docking analysis indicated a potential interaction between LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2 (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, subcellular localization results showed that LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2 colocalized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3B). Reciprocal Co-IP assays confirmed a direct interaction between endogenous LcSOCS1 and endogenous LcSTAT2 (Fig. 3C and D). To determine the interaction between LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2 depending on which domains, a series of domain deletants of LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2 were constructed (Fig. 3E and F). We found that the interaction was abolished by the LcSOCS1 deletant lacking the SH2 domain, or by the LcSTAT2 deletant lacking the DBD domain (Fig. 3G and H). These results indicate that LcSOCS1 interacts with the DBD domain of LcSTAT2 through its SH2 domain in the cytoplasm.

LcSOCS1-induced proteasomal degradation of LcSTAT2 is dependent on its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity

To investigate the functional consequences of the interaction between LcSOCS1 and LcSTAT2, we determined the effect of LcSOCS1 on endogenous LcSTAT2 expression. The results showed that increasing LcSOCS1 transfection resulted in a dose-dependent downregulation of endogenous LcSTAT2 protein levels (Fig. 4A and B), suggesting that LcSOCS1 may facilitate the degradation of endogenous LcSTAT2. This was further confirmed through cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays, which demonstrated that the half-life of endogenous LcSTAT2 was markedly shortened in cells overexpressing LcSOCS1 (Fig. 4C and D). To investigate the mechanism underlying LcSOCS1-induced endogenous LcSTAT2 degradation, the co-transfected cells were treated with various inhibitors, including the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the lysosomal inhibitor NH4Cl. As shown in Fig. 4E and F, MG132 reversed LcSOCS1-induced decreases in endogenous LcSTAT2 protein levels, but not NH4Cl. These results indicate that LcSOCS1 promotes LcSTAT2 degradation via the proteasome pathway.

Since ubiquitination is an important process during proteasome-dependent degradation, we further examined whether LcSOCS1-induced degradation of LcSTAT2 was due to ubiquitination. Indeed, our results showed that LcSTAT2 was remarkably more labeled with polyubiquitin in the LcSOCS1 overexpression group than in the control group (Fig. 4G). SOCS1 typically binds to Elongin B/C through the SOCS-BOX domain to co-mediate ubiquitination [10, 11, 31]. We then investigated whether the LcSOCS1-mediated LcSTAT2 ubiquitination also requires the involvement of Elongin B/C. First, we examined the conservation of the Elongin B/C binding sites (L213 and C217 in SOCS1) across species (L. crocea, C. carpio, D. rerio, X. laevis, G. gallus, O. cuniculus, M. musculus, and H. sapiens), and found that these two residues are highly conserved (Fig. S5). To investigate the roles of L213 and C217 in LcSOCS1, we constructed an LcSOCS1 mutant plasmid (LcSOCS1LC−PP-Myc) by mutating these two residues to proline. Using wild-type LcSOCS1-Myc as a control, we observed that the mutant LcSOCS1LC−PP-Myc failed to bind Elongin B/C and significantly reduced the ubiquitination level of LcSTAT2 (Fig. 4H and J). These results indicate that LcSOCS1-induced proteasomal degradation of LcSTAT2 is dependent on its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity.

LcSOCS1 promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2 at conserved Lys382

To investigate whether the type of LcSOCS1-mediated ubiquitination of LcSTAT2 is K48-linked or K63-linked, we used ubiquitin mutants in which only K48 or K63 was retained, while all other lysine residues were exchanged to arginine residues. We found that LcSOCS1 notably enhanced K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2, but had no influence on K63-linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 5A). Additionally, polyubiquitination was markedly reduced when lysine 48 was mutated (K48R) (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that LcSTAT2 degradation mediated by LcSOCS1 depends on K48-linked polyubiquitination.

To determine which lysine residues of LcSTAT2 are essential for the LcSOCS1-mediated K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2, we first examined the LcSOCS1-mediated K48-linked polyubiquitination on domain deletants of LcSTAT2. We found that LcSOCS1 had no effect on the K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2 mutant lacking DBD domain, but LcSOCS1 promoted the K48-linked polyubiquitination of other LcSTAT2 domain mutants (Fig. 5C). This suggested that the DBD domain of LcSTAT2 is necessary for LcSOCS1-mediated degradation and ubiquitination of LcSTAT2. Two lysine residues (K380 and K382) in the DBD domain of LcSTAT2 were predicted to be ubiquitination sites by MusiteDeep (Table S2). To explore which lysine residue is critical for LcSOCS1-mediated LcSTAT2 ubiquitination in the DBD domain, we mutated them from K to R. We found that LcSOCS1 failed to promote the K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2 K382R mutant, and promoted the K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2 K380R mutant (Fig. 5D). Notably, K382 in LcSTAT2 exhibited high conservation across vertebrate species (Fig. 5E), suggesting that this site is functionally conserved in negative regulation. Collectively, these results indicate that LcSOCS1-induced proteasomal degradation of LcSTAT2 is dependent on K48-linked polyubiquitination of LcSTAT2 at conserved K382.

MiR-7132a attenuates the inhibition of LcSOCS1 on LcSTAT2-mediated type I IFN antiviral responses

Given that miR-7132a negatively regulates post-transcriptional levels of LcSOCS1, and LcSOCS1 targets LcSTAT2 for Ub-proteasomal degradation, we further investigated whether miR-7132a regulates LcSTAT2-mediated type I IFN antiviral responses via targeting LcSOCS1. We found that LcSOCS1 notably promoted the degradation of LcSTAT2, whereas co-transfection of miR-7132a mimic exhibited a significantly reduced promotion effect (Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, miR-7132a overexpression significantly attenuated the inhibition of LcSOCS1 on LcSTAT2-induced expression of antiviral genes (Fig. 6C and E). Luciferase reporter assays showed that miR-7132a overexpression remarkably weakened the suppression of LcSOCS1 on LcSTAT2-induced ISRE and LcMxA promoter activities (Fig. 6F and G). Additionally, miR-7132a overexpression obviously attenuated the inhibition of LcSOCS1 on LcSTAT2-induced LcMxA protein expression (Fig. 6H and I). Given that there was no available viral infection cell model for large yellow croaker, we used a SVCV-infected EPC cell model to investigate the role of miR-7132a/LcSOCS1/LcSTAT2 axis in the cellular antiviral capacity. Upon SVCV infection, miR-7132a overexpression significantly mitigated the inhibition of LcSOCS1 on the antiviral function of LcSTAT2 in terms of CPEs, viral titer, and viral gene expression (Fig. 6J and L). Collectively, these results indicate that miR-7132a attenuates the inhibition of LcSOCS1 on LcSTAT2-mediated type I IFN antiviral responses.

MiR-7132a-mediated inhibition of SOCS1 is present in other fish species

We aligned sequences of precursor miR-7132a, and miR-7132a-binding sites in the 3’UTR of SOCS1 from different teleost fish to test the universality of our findings. We found that mature miR-7132a showed conservation in other fish species (Fig. 7A). The miR-7132a-binding sites in the 3’UTR of SOCS1 also displayed conservation in other fish species (Fig. 7B). We hypothesized that inhibition of SOCS1 by miR-7132a might be present in other fish species. To test this hypothesis, the regulatory relationships between miR-7132a and SOCS1 were validated in other fish species, such as E. fuscoguttatus and D. labrax. Luciferase assays revealed that miR-7132a suppressed luciferase activity of the wild form of SOCS1 3’UTR luciferase plasmid in both fish species, but it had no effect on mutated forms (Fig. 7C and D). Immunoblotting assays demonstrated that miR-7132a significantly inhibited SOCS1 protein levels in a dose-dependent manner in both fish species (Fig. 7E and H). Our in vitro results indicate that the miR-7132a-mediated inhibition of SOCS1 also exists in E. fuscoguttatus and D. labrax.

MiR-7132a promotes type I IFN responses in vivo

To explore the in vivo regulatory role of miR-7132a, we injected miR-7132a mimic into large yellow croakers, followed by the injection of LcIFNi protein to induce type I IFN responses. Analyses of key immune tissues (spleen and head kidney) showed that miR-7132a overexpression significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of LcSOCS1 under LcIFNi stimulation, which in turn upregulated the mRNA levels of LcSTAT2 and downstream ISGs (LcMxA, LcPKR, and LcViperin) (Fig. S6, Fig. 8A–E and J–N). Meanwhile, miR-7132a overexpression significantly suppressed the protein levels of LcSOCS1 under LcIFNi stimulation, and enhanced the protein levels of LcSTAT2 and LcMxA (Fig. 8F, I, O and R). These results indicate that miR-7132a enhances type I IFN responses in large yellow croaker.

Discussion

This study reveals that miR-7132a positively regulates type I IFN antiviral responses via attenuating the inhibition of SOCS1 on STAT2 degradation. These findings reveal a novel regulatory mechanism underlying the miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 axis and provide a molecular basis for regulating antiviral immunity in teleost fish, offering potential targets for the development of disease-resistant strains and targeted immunomodulatory therapeutics in fish culture.

MiR-7132a was first discovered in Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar), and it is related to embryonic development [32, 33]. We have discovered that miR-7132a is a miRNA specific to teleost fish (Fig. 7A), and that it may play an indispensable regulatory role in teleost fish. To date, whether miR-7132a regulates antiviral immune responses and what its target genes might be remain unclear. In this study, we confirmed that miR-7132a positively regulates type I IFN antiviral responses by targeting SOCS1. This finding has broadened our understanding of the regulatory role of miR-7132a. Furthermore, evidence is rapidly growing that miRNAs are evolutionarily conserved in mammals, and the present study revealed that miR-7132a also targets the 3’UTR of SOCS1 in other fish species, such as E. fuscoguttatus and D. labrax, which indicates that miRNAs in fish species may also possess evolutionarily conserved regulatory systems. Whether the repressive regulatory axis between miR-7132a and SOCS1 is widely present in teleosts remains to be further validated. Additionally, given that miRNAs typically target different genes to participate in various physiological processes [34], we hypothesize that miR-7132a might have other target genes. Thus, further investigation into other regulatory mechanisms of miR-7132a is needed in the future.

Although type I IFN signaling is an essential component of the innate immune system, its excessive activation can provoke pathological inflammation, leading to severe outcomes [35]. Therefore, tight regulation of type I IFN signaling is vital for preserving immune homeostasis [35]. SOCS1 is a vital negative regulatory factor in the type I IFN pathway, and promotes the proteasomal degradation of multiple target proteins, such as JAK1 [10], Tyk2 [11], and STAT1 (Unpublished data). In addition to these target proteins, the present study found that STAT2 is a novel target of SOCS1, which promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination at K382 of STAT2, leading to its proteasomal degradation via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. We hypothesize that to prevent the excessive activation of the type I IFN pathway signaling, host cells utilize SOCS1 to degrade as many of the signaling molecules in the type I IFN pathway as possible. To ensure that the type I IFN pathway produces enough antiviral ISGs in response to viral infections, we found that the negative regulatory function of SOCS1 was attenuated by miR-7132a in the early stage of the type I IFN antiviral response. Notably, this regulatory pattern allows miR-7132a to play a dual role in maintaining type I IFN immune homeostasis in teleost fish. Specifically, during viral infection and subsequent type I IFN activation, miR-7132a is rapidly upregulated to inhibit SOCS1 expression at the post-transcriptional level. By blocking SOCS1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of STAT2, miR-7132a strongly enhances type I IFN signaling and promotes the transcription of antiviral ISGs, thereby accelerating pathogen clearance in the early stage of infection. Conversely, once the infection is controlled, excessive type I IFN signaling may potentially cause immune-mediated tissue damage [35]. In the late stage of IFN induction, as miR-7132a expression decreases, its inhibitory effect on SOCS1 gradually diminishes. This adjustment restores SOCS1 activity, thereby avoiding excessive STAT2-dependent IFN signaling and preventing tissue damage caused by uncontrolled inflammatory responses. The dual function of the miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 axis reflects an evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanism in teleost fish, which balances effective antiviral immunity with protection against type I IFN-mediated tissue damage. This balance is especially important for teleost fish, which are continuously exposed to various pathogens in their aquatic environment. Collectively, the miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 regulatory axis in the type I IFN pathway provides novel insights into the precise regulation of the type I IFN signaling.

While our study provides valuable insights into the function and mechanism of miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 axis in regulating type I IFN signaling, the objective limitations should be acknowledged. Targeted gene knockout experiments, such as miR-7132a knockout via CRISPR/Cas9, should also be conducted to further clarify the impact of miR-7132a deficiency on the type I IFN signaling pathway and antiviral responses. Additionally, investigating the potential of miR-7132a as a therapeutic target in aquaculture presents a promising avenue. Strategies such as the development of miR-7132a-specific mimic or the generation of miR-7132a-overexpressing fish lines may offer valuable antiviral immunity in commercially important teleost species.

In summary, our study reveals a novel regulatory axis of miR-7132a/SOCS1/STAT2 in regulating type I IFN antiviral responses in teleost fish. We found that miR-7132a induced by type I IFN inhibits LcSOCS1 expression by reducing its mRNAstability and repressing its protein translation via directly targeting the 3’UTR of LcSOCS1, and attenuates SOCS1-mediated degradation of STAT2, thus positively regulates type I IFN antiviral responses (Fig. 9). Additionally, the ability of miR-7132a to target the 3’UTR of SOCS1 is also confirmed in other fish species. Collectively, our findings not only advance our understanding of miR-7132a-mediated regulatory axis, but also provide insights into the miRNA-mediated precise regulatory mechanisms of the type I IFN antiviral responses.
