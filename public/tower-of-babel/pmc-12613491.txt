Genetic parameters estimation and optimization of genomic selection in mud crab (Scylla paramamosain): a case study for growth-related traits
Authors: Xiyi Zhou, Zhenying Hong, Wenxiao Cui, Yin Zhang, Mhd Ikhwanuddin, Shaopan Ye, Hongyu Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenomic selection (GS) has become a preferred method for genetic improvement in aquaculture species. However, its application in mud crab (Scylla paramamosain) remains limited. Given that the prediction accuracy of GS varies across species and is influenced by several factors, including statistical models, marker density, and the size of the reference population, a systematic investigation of GS in mud crab is necessary. To explore the impact of these factors on GS and optimize the application strategies of GS in mud crab breeding, a total of 508 mud crabs were genotyped by the 40 K liquid SNP array (named “Xiexin No. 1”) to estimate genetic parameters of growth traits and systematically investigate the impact of prediction models, SNP density, and reference population size on GS.ResultsResults showed that the genomic heritability for body weight (BW), carapace length (CL), carapace width (CW), and body height (BH) was 0.860 ± 0.043, 0.554 ± 0.081, 0.521 ± 0.083, and 0.597 ± 0.077, respectively. Different models (GBLUP, rrBLUP, BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, and BayesR) exhibited similar prediction accuracies for four growth-related traits, with ranges of 0.510–0.515, 0.569–0.574, 0.567–0.570, and 0.543–0.548 for BW, CL, CW, and BH, respectively, but GBLUP showed advantages in computational efficiency. Notably, the accuracy of GS for four growth-related traits would be further improved as the SNP density increased but began to plateau after 10 K SNPs. When SNP density increased from 0.5 K to 33 K, the average prediction accuracies of six models for BW, CL, CW, and BH were improved by 6.22%, 4.20%, 4.40%, and 5.23%, respectively. Moreover, the prediction accuracy of GS was also improved as the number of reference individuals increased. When the reference population size expanded from 30 to 400, the average prediction accuracies of six models for BW, CL, CW, and BH increased by 8.66%, 3.99%, 4.97% and 4.56%, respectively. In addtion, the prediction unbiasedness of GBLUP, BayesA, BayesB, and BayesC were close to 1, only when the reference population size was more than 150.ConclusionsTaken together, the reference population comprising at least 150 samples genotyped with over 10 K SNPs is the minimum standard for implementing GS of growth-related traits in mud crab. Our results would provide valuable insights and practical recommendations on implementing GS in mud crab genetic breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12241-1.Keywords: S. paramamosain, Genomic selection, Prediction accuracy, Growth-related traits

Background

The mud crab (Scylla paramamosain) is one of the commercially important crustacean species in aquaculture, widely distributed along the southeastern coastal regions of China [1, 2]. In China, mud crab has a farming history of over a century due to its high nutritional value and fast growth rate [3]. Notably, the artificial farming production of mud crab in China reached 157 thousand tons in 2023 [4]. However, most mud crab farming still relies on wild-caught seed crabs. Currently, the quantity and quality of wild seedlings and parent crabs have declined rapidly due to overexploitation of wild populations and environmental pollution, posing significant challenges to the sustainable development of mud crab farming [5, 6]. Furthermore, the genetic improvement of mud crabs is still in its infancy, primarily relying on individual selection. Although individual selection is a practical and widely used method for aquatic species, its relatively low efficiency is insufficient to meet the current demands [7]. Therefore, it is necessary to implement a modern genetic evaluation method to accelerate the genetic improvement of the mud crab.

With the development of high-throughput genotyping technology, genomic selection (GS), also called genomic prediction (GP), has been widely implemented to accelerate the genetic improvement of economically important traits [8, 9]. GS is a form of marker-assisted selection that uses genome-wide markers to predict the genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) for the desired trait based on the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and markers [8]. GS offers significant advantages over traditional breeding methods, including improved selection accuracy, reduced breeding costs and generation intervals, and enhanced prediction of breeding values, particularly for low heritability or difficult-to-measure traits [10, 11], such as disease resistance traits [10], feed efficiency [12], and meat quality [13, 14]. In addition, GS can reduce the inbreeding rate per generation by avoiding the co-selection of relatives through a more accurate estimation of Mendelian sampling variation [15–17].

Nowadays, GS has become the preferred method in genetic improvement and is widely used in high-value aquatic species [18], such as body length of common carp [19], growth traits and resistance to sea lice of Atlantic salmon [20, 21], resistance to infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus and bacterial cold water disease of rainbow trout [22, 23], and resistance against Vibrio parahaemolyticus of penaeid shrimp [24]. Compared with traditional breeding methods based on pedigree, GS achieves significantly higher prediction accuracy, with improvements of 24% for growth-related traits and 22% for disease resistance-related traits [7, 25]. As for mud crab, based on the high-resolution chromosome-level genome [26], the first 40 K liquid SNP array (“Xiexin No. 1” SNP array) has been developed, achieving the prediction accuracy of GBLUP ranging from 0.535 to 0.585 for growth-related traits, which provides an efficient and reliable genotyping platform for GS implementation in mud crab [27].

Although the prediction accuracy of GBLUP in mud crabs was at an upper level compared with previous studies [28], the accuracy of GS can be further enhanced by optimizing factors such as statistical models, marker density, and reference population size. Heretofore, various statistical models have been developed for GS, mainly categorized into best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) method, Bayesian methods, and machine learning. However, no single model consistently outperforms others across traits and scenarios. In rainbow trout, the highest prediction accuracy for resistance against Piscirickettsia salmonis was obtained by BayesC among different GS models, including GBLUP, ssGBLUP, BayesC, and Bayesian Lasso [29]. In fish, the Bayesian threshold model yielded 2.5% and 1.2% higher accuracies than the linear model for ordered categorical traits [30]. In Atlantic salmon, Bayesian and GBLUP methods yielded similar prediction accuracies for the sea-lice resistance trait [31]. In addition, high marker density generally would improve prediction accuracy by explaining more genetic variation [32, 33]. However, several studies have clearly shown that prediction accuracy is difficult to improve meaningfully after marker density reaches a certain threshold [31, 34, 35]. Therefore, while ensuring prediction accuracy, it is feasible to minimize the SNP panels density as much as possible to reduce the genotyping costs in practical applications of GS. Besides, the reference population size also plays a significant role in the accuracy and efficiency of genomic selection. A larger reference population leads to more accurate prediction, which is vital for the effectiveness of GS breeding programs [36, 37]. Although the impacts of these factors have been widely investigated in other species [38–40], their impact varies across species. Given the limited GS research on mud crabs, it is essential to explore the influence of these key factors on GS accuracy to develop an efficient and low-cost application strategy.

In this study, to optimize the application strategy of GS in mud crab, we evaluated genetic parameters of growth-related traits in mud crab and systematically investigated the impact of statistical models, reference population size, and SNP marker density on the accuracy of GS. Our results will provide valuable insights and practical recommendations for implementing GS in mud crabs, which will help address current technical bottlenecks of genetic breeding and accelerate the progress of genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Population, phenotypes, and genotypes

The experimental population of mud crab in this study was caught from the natural sea at crablet stage I and cultured in a pond in Shantou, Guangdong Province, with artificial feeding of Potamocorbula rubomuscula and low-value fish. After three months of growth, a total of 508 samples were collected randomly for growth-related traits measurement and muscle sampling. The growth-related traits, including body weight (BW), carapace length (CL), carapace width (CW), and body height (BH), were measured by electronic scales (0.1 g) and digital calipers (0.1 mm). In addition, muscle tissues were taken from each sample and immediately immersed in 75% ethanol and then stored at −20 °C for DNA extraction. The total genomic DNA of each sample was extracted using the TIANamp Marine Animals DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech Beijing Co. Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Further, genotyping was performed using the “Xiexin No. 1” 40 K SNP array that contains probes for 40,352 core SNPs [27]. To obtain the high-quality genotype data, SNPs were filtered using PLINK software (v 1.9) with the following criteria: SNPs with MAF lower than 0.05 or more than 10% missing genotypes were excluded, and samples with call rate lower than 90% were removed. The remaining missing genotypes were imputed using Beagle (v 5.4) default [41]. Finally, a total of 506 samples remained for further analysis, which contained 32,621 SNPs across 49 chromosomes evenly (see Additional file 1: Figure S1).

Genomic evaluation

To investigate the impact of the statistical models, SNP marker density, and reference population size on genomic prediction, 3 scenarios (denoted S1-S3) for genomic prediction were considered. Scenario 1 (S1) investigated the impact of statistical models on genomic prediction. In this scenario, a total of 32,621 SNPs and 506 samples were used to perform genomic prediction based on 6 different statistical models. Scenario 2 (S2) evaluated the effect of SNP marker density on the prediction accuracy across six statistical models. Eight subsets of SNP markers with different numbers (0.5 K, 0.8 K, 1 K, 3 K, 5 K, 10 K, 20 K, and 30 K) were randomly selected from the total SNP dataset. Each subset was constructed hierarchically, with each larger subset containing all the SNPs from the preceding smaller subset. To ensure statistical reliability, the above process was replicated five times, and average prediction accuracies were reported. Scenario 3 (S3) explored the impact of reference population size on genomic prediction across six GS methods. A validation population of constant size (100 samples) was randomly selected from 506 samples. Ten subsets of the reference population, consisting of different sample sizes (30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400), were randomly selected from the remaining samples. Each subset was constructed hierarchically, including all samples from the preceding smaller subsets. To ensure statistical reliability, the above process was replicated 50 times, and the average prediction accuracies were reported.

Predictive ability evaluation

To evaluate the prediction accuracy of genomic selection, the corrected phenotypic values (PHE) were obtained by correcting the original phenotypic data for the fixed effects of gender and sampling date using the “lm” function in R. The predictive ability of GS was measured by computing the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the genomic estimated breeding values (GEBV) and the PHE, i.e., . In addition, the regression coefficient of PHE on GEBV was also computed to measure the unbiasedness in the GEBV, i.e., . To reduce the sampling error, the 5-fold cross-validation (CV) and leave-set-out (LSO) strategies were used in this study. The 5-fold CV was used to assess the predictive ability of GS in S1 and S2. In brief, the data set was randomly divided into five mutually exclusive subsets with approximately the same number of samples, in which four subsets were used as the reference population and the fifth subset was used as a validation population. Then, the cross-validation process was repeated five times to ensure that each subset was validated once. Finally, the average accuracy values and the bias of genomic prediction for the five replicates of five-fold cross-validation were reported [49, 52]. The LSO strategy was adopted to assess the predictive ability of GS in S3 [53]. In brief, 100 samples were selected as a validation population first. Then, a different number of samples were randomly selected from the remaining samples as a reference population for genomic prediction. Finally, this process was repeated 50 times, and the average accuracy values and the bias of genomic prediction were reported.

Results

Descriptive statistics of growth-related traits and genetic parameter estimations

The descriptive statistics and narrow-sense heritability of growth-related traits were presented in Table 1. The mean (SD) values of BW, CL, CW, and BH were 71.60 ± 40.66 g, 48.98 ± 8.61 mm, 72.79 ± 12.37 mm, and 28.63 ± 5.01 mm, respectively. Among these traits, BW exhibited the highest coefficient of variation (CV) at 56.79%, followed by CL (17.58%) and BH (17.51%), and the lowest was CW (17.00%). Notably, the narrow-sense heritability was 0.860 ± 0.043, 0.597 ± 0.077, 0.554 ± 0.081, and 0.521 ± 0.083 for BW, BH, CL, and CW, respectively. Furthermore, these traits exhibited high genetic correlations (0.939 ~ 0.985) and phenotypic correlations (0.947 ~ 0.988) with each other (Table 2). Among them, the genetic and phenotypic correlation between CL and CW was the highest, with values of 0.988 ± 0.005 and 0.985. In addition, genetic correlations were generally higher than phenotypic correlations among growth traits, except for the trait correlations between CW and BH. In general, these growth-related traits of mud crabs have high variability, heritability, and genetic correlations, which indicate a good potential for genetic improvement.

The prediction accuracy of genomic selection with different statistical models

To investigate the impact of statistical models on the accuracy of genomic prediction, six statistical models were used to predict the GEBV for four growth-related traits, including GBLUP, rrBLUP, BayesA, BayesB, BayesC, and BayesR. Despite the different prior assumptions regarding marker effects among the six GS models, they exhibited similar genomic prediction performance (Figure 1), without significant difference (p >0.05, see Additional file 1: Table S1). The prediction accuracy ranges for body weight, carapace length, carapace width, and body height were 0.510–0.515.510.515, 0.569–0.574.569.574, 0.567–0.570.567.570, and 0.543–0.548.543.548, respectively. Notably, Bayesian methods showed slightly higher prediction accuracy than BLUP methods for BW, CL, and CW, whereas BLUP methods outperformed Bayesian methods for BH. In addition, the unbiasedness of genomic prediction for the four traits was close to 1, ranging from 1.003 to 1.101 (see Additional file 1: Figure S2).

The prediction accuracy of genomic selection with different SNP marker densities

To investigate the influence of SNP marker density on the accuracy of genomic prediction, eight subsets of SNP markers with different numbers (0.5 K, 0.8 K, 1 K, 3 K, 5 K, 10 K, 20 K, and 30 K) were randomly selected from the total SNP dataset (33K) and used for genomic prediction based on six statistical models. Regardless of SNP density, six statistical models showed similar genomic predictive accuracy for four growth-related traits in the mud crab (Figure 2). Notably, the prediction accuracy increased rapidly as the SNP number increased from 0.5 K to 3 K, followed by a moderate increase from 3 K to 10 K, and then plateaued beyond 10 K. When performed GS using the lowest SNP density (0.5 K), the mean prediction accuracies of six models were 0.482, 0.548, 0.545, and 0.516 for BW, CL, CW, and BH, respectively. When SNP density increased from 0.5 K to 33 K, the mean prediction accuracies of six models increased to 0.512, 0.571, 0.569, and 0.543 for BW, CL, CW, and BH, with improvements of 6.22%, 4.20%, 4.40%, and 5.23%, respectively. In addition, irrespective of the SNP number, prediction unbiasedness for four traits using different GS models remained close to 1, ranging from 0.945 to 1.241, except for BayesR using SNP densities below 3 K (see Additional file 1: Figure S3).

Prediction accuracy for four growth-related traits of mud crab using different statistical models based on different SNP densities

The prediction accuracy of genomic selection with different reference population sizes

To investigate the effect of the reference population size on the accuracy of genomic prediction, ten different sizes of reference populations (30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 samples) were randomly selected from 506 samples and used for genomic prediction based on six statistical models. Generally, the prediction accuracy of four growth-related traits improved as the number of samples in the reference population increased (Figure 3). When the number of samples in the reference population was 30, the mean prediction accuracies were 0.462, 0.552, 0.543, and 0.526 for BW, CL, CW, and BH, respectively. When the number of samples in the reference population increased from 30 to 400, the mean prediction accuracies improved to 0.502, 0.574, 0.570, and 0.550 for BW, CL, CW, and BH, with relative improvements of 8.66%, 3.99%, 4.97% and 4.56%, respectively. Except for the BayesR model, which exhibited noticeable deviation (ranging from 1.037 to 2.200), the prediction unbiasedness of other models remained close to 1 across different traits and reference population sizes, ranging from 0.921 to 1.429 (see Additional file 1: Figure S4).

Discussion

Genomic selection is expected to speed up genetic progress in animal breeding programs. To implement GS in mud crab breeding, it is crucial to evaluate the performance of GS in predicting GEBV, as well as assess various statistical models, marker densities, and reference population size to create appropriate strategies for an effective breeding program in mud crab. In this study, we estimated the genetic parameters, performed GS using different statistical models, marker densities, and reference population size, and compared the prediction accuracy among all scenarios on four growth-related traits in mud crab.

Genetic parameters of four growth-related traits

Genomic heritability refers to the proportion of phenotypic variation that can be attributed to genetic factors, which is a key parameter for evaluating the feasibility of genetic improvement through appropriate selection strategies [38, 54, 55]. Several studies have reported a positive correlation between prediction accuracy and genomic heritability of the trait [49, 56, 57]. However, the anticipated positive correlation between heritability and prediction accuracy was not consistently observed in the current study. Specifically, although BW exhibited the highest heritability among the four traits analyzed, its prediction accuracy ranged from 0.510 ± 0.016 to 0.515 ± 0.014, which was comparable to or even lower than that of the other three traits with lower heritability. This inconsistency may be attributed to measurement bias in the phenotypic values of BW caused by physical abnormalities, such as lack of chela, walking legs, or swimming legs, which are commonly encountered in cultured mud crabs, may lead to underestimation of true BW values and reduced prediction reliability. To mitigate such errors, future studies should consider using more stable and easily measurable body size traits as covariates to correct BW measurements or as proxies for indirect selection. Moreover, the genetic architecture of BW may involve limited number of large-effect QTLs that are not effectively captured by the SNP markers [58]. Additionally, phenotypic values of traits are influenced by genetic factors, environmental conditions, and genotype-by-environment interactions [59], for which a higher degree of environmental variance is also a potential reason to attenuate the prediction accuracy for BW, despite its high heritability.

Furthermore, our results revealed that both phenotypic and genetic correlations among the four traits were highly positive, indicating that these traits are likely controlled by common genes or QTLs. The high correlation also implies that direct selection on one trait could simultaneously result in indirect selection on the other three traits [55, 60]. Given the strong correlations between BW and the other three traits, including CL, CW, and BH, incorporating these morphometric traits into selection programs may help enhance the accuracy and efficiency of genetic improvement for BW. Notably, with the exception of the traits correlation between CW and BH, the genetic correlations for other trait pairs were consistently higher than phenotypic correlations, indicating that underlying genetic relationships may not be fully reflected at the phenotypic level due to environmental or non-genetic factors [61].

Effect of statistical models on GS

Differences in prediction accuracy among different statistical models can be attributed to their underlying assumptions of marker effect variances. The early statistical models, such as GBLUP and rrBLUP, assume the equal contribution of all markers to the genetic variance. In contrast, Bayesian models allow for different marker variances, prioritizing certain markers over others for trait modeling [38]. According to previous studies, when traits are controlled by a few QTLs with major effects, Bayesian models generally outperform GBLUP in prediction accuracy. Conversely, when traits are influenced by numerous QTLs with small effects, BLUP models generate similar or even superior accuracy compared to Bayesian models [49, 62]. In this study, BLUP models and Bayesian models showed similar prediction accuracy for four growth-related traits, ranging from 0.510 ± 0.016 to 0.572 ± 0.011, and 0.512 ± 0.014 to 0.574 ± 0.012, respectively. At the same time, prediction unbiasedness for the four traits was stable irrespective of GS models, suggesting that these four growth-related traits may be highly polygenic traits controlled by numerous QTLs with small effects. Additionally, large-scale genomic evaluations inevitably entail heavy computational demands [63]. As expected, BLUP methods cost less time than Bayesian counterparts (see Additional file 1: Table S2), since Bayesian models rely on extensive MCMC sampling through Markov chains and Gibbs sampling to estimate the posterior marginal distributions of model parameters [8]. However, increasing model complexity results in huge computational costs but does not necessarily lead to higher predictive accuracy [64]. Thus, GBLUP provided a more practical and resource-efficient choice for genomic prediction in growth-related traits of mud crab, when considering both prediction accuracy and computational efficiency.

Effect of marker density on GS

An increase in marker density generally improves prediction accuracy. Our results showed that the prediction accuracy of four growth-related traits increased with the rise in SNP density, which could be explained by the increasing number of markers in LD with the QTLs [53]. However, when SNP density exceeded a certain threshold (10 K), the prediction accuracy plateaued, showing only slight improvements or even declines. Similar patterns have been reported in other studies, although the specific threshold at which the plateau occurs might be different [39, 62, 65]. For instance, in Pacific white shrimp, prediction accuracy for growth traits increased rapidly as marker density rose from 0.05 K to 3.2 K, but stabilized with further increases up to 23 K [39]. In Atlantic salmon, the prediction accuracy of growth traits was improved by approximately 20 % with increased marker density from 0.5 K to 5 K, but showed minimal gain or no improvement with further increases to 112 K [20]. The different thresholds of the plateau are mainly influenced by the LD between markers and QTLs.

Theoretically, QTLs or chromosome segments contributing to phenotypic variation are typically in LD with at least one marker locus in the genome. In populations with strong LD, the number of independent segments is relatively small, for which fewer markers are sufficient to capture all genetic variance [8, 66]. In this study, the prediction accuracy of six statistical models using 10 K SNP dataset reached at least 98.25% of that achieved with the 33 K SNP dataset, which suggested that the 10 K density marker can achieve ideal predictive accuracy for growth-related traits of mud crab. Comparative analysis of the 10 K and 33 K SNP datasets revealed different distributions of physical distance between adjacent SNPs (see Additional file 1:Figure S5). In the 10 K dataset, inter-SNP distances were predominantly between 60–80 kb, accounting for 41.77% of the total, while in the 33 K dataset, distances were concentrated within 20–40 kb, comprising 67.14% of observations. However, the extent of LD between adjacent SNPs was similar in both datasets. Specifically, the majority of SNP pairs exhibited low LD, with R-squared estimates below 0.05, representing 70.12% and 66.59% of the total in the 10 K and 33 K datasets, respectively. These findings support the feasibility of adopting 10 K SNP-based low-density chips as a cost-effective strategy for mud crab breeding programs.

Effect of reference population size on GS

The size of the reference population used to predict GEBV is an important factor influencing genomic prediction accuracy. Results in this study showed that the prediction accuracy improved with the expansion of reference population size, which is consistent with findings of previous studies in dairy cattle [67], wheat [68], alfalfa [38], etc. An increase in the number of samples in the reference population can reduce both bias and variance of estimates of marker effects, resulting in enhanced prediction accuracy [69]. Moreover, a larger reference population may increase the genetic relationships between samples in the reference and validation populations, further contributing to the improvement of prediction accuracy [69, 70]. Notably, BayesR model exhibited considerable bias, ranging from 1.393 to 2.200, when the reference population contained fewer than 150 individuals. BayesR offers greater flexibility to handle polygenic traits and complex genetic architectures by assuming that SNP effects follow a mixture of four normal distributions [51, 71]. However, it is challenging to fit such a complex prior distribution effectively and accurately with limited reference populations.

In practice, it is a significant challenge for a single breeding organization to obtain a sufficiently large reference population, primarily due to the substantial time and financial resources required. A promising alternative is to combine multiple populations of the same breed or related breeds to construct a joint reference population, by which more information is available for the estimation of marker effects, resulting in more accurate predicted breeding values [72]. The advantages of joint reference populations with similar genetic backgrounds have been demonstrated in various species, such as Atlantic salmon [73], dairy cattle [74, 75], pig [76, 77], etc. However, as the reference population size increases, higher marker density generally contributes to greater prediction accuracy [78]. Especially, when a joint reference population is constructed by populations with obvious differentiation, a higher SNP density is required to achieve optimal prediction accuracy. For instance, in a combined Yorkshire pig population, imputed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data consistently outperformed 80 K SNP chip data in GBLUP, likely because WGS provides stronger LD between markers and causal variants [79]. Given the low level of genetic differentiation in mud crab populations across different regions [5, 80, 81], a joint reference population based on multiple populations across different geographic regions is a cost-effective strategy to improve prediction accuracy and selection efficiency.

Conclusions

In conclusion, four growth-related traits of mud crabs exhibited high phenotypic variability, heritability, and genetic correlations. Notably, although BLUP and Bayesian models showed similar prediction accuracies, GBLUP had advantages in computational efficiency. Moreover, increasing SNP marker density and reference population size can improve the prediction accuracy of GS in mud crab. Taken together, the reference population comprising at least 150 samples genotyped with over 10 K SNPs, is the minimum standard for implementing GS of growth-related traits in mud crab. Our results would provide valuable insights and practical recommendations on implementing GS in mud crab genetic breeding programs.
