Effect of tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing on antibacterial-antifungal activity and postoperative pain intensity after non-surgical endodontic retreatment: randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: İlke Menteş, Meltem Sümbüllü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and antifungal efficacy, as well as postoperative pain levels, following non-surgical retreatment of single-rooted teeth using tricalcium silicate- or calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressings.Materials and methodsForty-nine patients diagnosed with apical periodontitis were randomly assigned to two groups: tricalcium silicate (n = 25) and calcium hydroxide (n = 24). Microbiological samples were obtained before instrumentation (S1), after chemo-mechanical preparation (S2), and 10 days post-medication (S3). Total bacterial load and Candida albicans levels were quantified using qPCR. Postoperative pain was assessed on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10.ResultsNo significant differences were found between the groups in demographic variables. The calcium hydroxide group showed a significantly greater reduction in total bacterial load in S3 samples. No significant differences were observed between groups regarding C. albicans levels or postoperative pain at any time point. However, a significant reduction in pain was noted on day 1 compared to baseline in the calcium hydroxide group.ConclusionsBoth materials were effective in reducing microbial load; however, calcium hydroxide demonstrated superior antibacterial efficacy. Antifungal activity and postoperative pain outcomes were comparable between the groups.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at Clinicaltrials.gov on 15/01/2025 (NCT06779370).Keywords: Apical periodontitis, Candida albicans, Calcium hydroxide, Tricalcium silicate, Intracanal dressing, Postoperative pain, Retreatment, Total bacterial load

Introduction

Retreatment is a widely performed procedure in modern dentistry. It is carried out to restore the periapical tissues in cases where root canal treatment has not been successfully performed or when a previously treated root canal becomes reinfected due to apical or coronal leakage [1]. The presence of resistant microbial flora can be a significant factor in the prognosis of endodontically treated teeth. The application of root canal disinfection protocols is crucial for eliminating microbial flora in teeth with apical periodontitis [2]. Effective cleaning of root canals cannot be achieved solely through chemomechanical preparation. Therefore, the use of an intracanal dressing, which provides disinfection and a physical barrier, is advantageous in the treatment process [3]. The use of calcium hydroxide in endodontic treatment has been supported by various scientific studies demonstrating its antibacterial efficacy within root canals [4, 5]. However, studies have indicated that calcium hydroxide may not reliably eliminate total bacteria after medication [6, 7].

Fungal species are more commonly associated with secondary or persistent infections following treatment [8]. The most frequently isolated species belong to the Candida genus. Studies have shown that C. albicans exhibits resistance to Ca(OH)₂ [9, 10]. In response, considerable research efforts in recent years have focused on developing alternative strategies against C. albicans [11, 12]. Accordingly, the use of intracanal dressings may play a crucial role in eliminating microorganisms from the root canal system during retreatment procedures [13].

Postoperative pain following endodontic treatment typically arises from mechanical, chemical, or microbial factors that induce inflammation in the periapical region. This pain commonly occurs within the first 48 h after treatment [14, 15]. A thorough understanding of the variables contributing to postoperative pain enables clinicians to select appropriate techniques and materials to minimize the incidence of postoperative pain. One of the most important causes of postoperative pain is microorganisms that cannot be eliminated from the root canal.

Due to their biocompatibility and sealing properties, calcium silicate-based materials derived from biologically active bioceramics are widely used in procedures, such as apical barrier formation in teeth with open apices, vital pulp therapy, endodontic surgery, and root canal filling [16–18]. The widespread acceptance of bioceramic-based materials has led to the development of Bio-C Temp (Bio-C Temp, Angelus, Londrina, PR, Brazil) as an intracanal dressing. While there are in vitro studies on the antibacterial efficacy of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressing [19, 20], no in vivo clinical studies have investigated the antibacterial and antifungal activity or postoperative pain intensity of tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing in teeth with apical periodontitis. The research question was systematically structured according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) criteria: Is the application of tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing effective in antibacterial and antifungal activity and postoperative pain levels after nonsurgical endodontic retreatment? In this PICO model, population refers to individuals with single-rooted teeth requiring root canal retreatment with mild pain, intervention refers to tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing compared to calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressing, and outcome refers to antibacterial and antifungal activity and postoperative pain levels. The null hypothesis tested was: “Tricalcium silicate- and calcium hydroxide-based root canal dressings would not have a statistically significant effect on total bacterial load, C. albicans, and postoperative pain in single-rooted teeth with apical periodontitis requiring retreatment.”

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This randomized, single-blind clinical trial was conducted according to the CONSORT 2025 statement [21]. The ethical approval for this study was granted on 29.03.2024 by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Atatürk University with the decision numbered B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/25. The recruitment period started on June 12, 2024, and was completed on January 10, 2025. The study protocol was registered in the clinical trial database (www.clinicaltrials.gov) under the registration number NCT06779370.

Sample size and patient selection

G*Power analysis was used to determine the minimum required sample size. For the evaluation of antibacterial efficacy, the study by Karataş et al. [22] was used as a reference, and based on an effect size of d = 1.1692, with 95% test power and a 95% confidence interval, 17 participants per group were determined. For the evaluation of antifungal efficacy, the study by Alghofaily et al. [23] was referenced, and based on an effect size of d = 2.4005, with 95% test power and a 95% confidence interval, six participants per group were determined. For the comparison of postoperative pain levels, the study by Karataş et al. [24] was referenced and based on an effect size of d = 0.7575, with 0.80 test power and a 95% confidence interval (alpha error 0.05), 23 participants per group were determined.

To increase the statistical power of the study and compensate for potential patient loss, 25 patients were included in each group, and the patients were blinded to group allocation, and the patients were blinded to the groups.

Inclusion criteria

Participants in this study were selected based on the following criteria: patients aged between 18 and 65 years; single-rooted, single canal incisors and premolars diagnosed with apical periodontitis and with a history of previous root canal treatment; teeth exhibiting a Periapical Index (PAI) score of two or higher; patients with a pain score below 40 on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS); teeth with a pocket depth of less than three millimeters, patients who had not used antibiotics within the last month; patients who had not used analgesics within the last 72 h; and teeth with a minimum of two years since the completion of root canal treatment. Finally, the participants were required to be systemically healthy, that is, reporting no systemic diseases.

Exclusion criteria

Participants had Schilder canal curvatures greater than 25°; presented with generalized periodontitis; had teeth exhibiting swelling, tenderness on palpation, sinus tract, or fistula.

Treatment protocol

After providing detailed information about the study, each participant read and signed a written informed consent form. The randomization procedure was carried out using the online platform www.randomizer.org, which generates random numbers for assigning participants to experimental groups. Patient allocation followed the sequential order determined by this tool. The randomization process and its implementation were managed by the secretary.

For patients meeting the inclusion criteria, the pain scale was assessed before anesthesia through necessary clinical evaluations. Local anesthesia was administered using 4% articaine hydrochloride with 1:100,000 adrenaline (Ultracaine DS Forte; Aventis, Istanbul, Turkey) (Ultracaine DS Forte; Aventis, Istanbul, Turkey), followed by rubber dam isolation. All treatment steps were performed under 6x magnification dental loupes. The tooth surface was disinfected for 30 s with a cotton pellet soaked in 30% hydrogen peroxide, followed by a 30-second disinfection with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite. The disinfection process was further enhanced by using 5% sodium thiosulfate.

The access cavity was prepared with a sterile, high-speed, round diamond bur. After accessing the pulp chamber, sterile teflon tape was placed over the canal orifice, and the pre-access disinfection procedures were repeated. Subsequently, three sterile paper points were used to collect the initial microbiological samples (S0) from the cavity. For a tooth to be included in the study, S0 had to test negative. All teeth included in the study met these criteria.

The coronal gutta-percha was removed using reciprocating files (VDW, Germany) with sterile saline irrigation. The working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (Dentsply Sirona, Switzerland) and a 15 K file (Mani Inc.; Utsunomiya, Tochigi, Japan). The length was established as soon as the “over” signal was observed on the apex locator, and the procedure was performed 0.5 mm short of this length. The working length was then confirmed using radiography. A Reciproc Silver endodontic motor (VDW, Munich, Germany) was programmed to the “RECIPROC ALL” mode for root canal instrumentation. The previously completed root canal treatment was removed using single-file system Reciproc 25 (VDW, Munich, Germany).

After gutta-percha removal, a baseline microbiological sample (S1) was collected from the root canal using size 25, sterile paper points, which were placed at the working length of 1 min each to absorb the canal contents. Special care was taken to prevent contact between the paper points and the cavity walls. The collected samples were transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and stored at −20 °C.

Root canal shaping was completed using R25 and R40 reciprocating files (VDW, Germany) at the working lengths to finalize the canal preparation. After each file usage, the canal was irrigated with 2 mL of 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) using a close ended side-vented needle (Endo-Top, Cerkamed, Stalowa Wola, Poland). The final irrigation protocol included 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Imicryl, Konya, Turkey) and 5 mL of 17% EDTA (Imicryl, Konya, Turkey), both activated three times for 20 s each using passive ultrasonic activation (PUI). To neutralize the effects of sodium hypochlorite, the canal was irrigated with 5 mL of 5% sodium thiosulfate.

Following irrigation procedure, a second microbiological sample (S2) was collected using three sterile paper points, each of which was left in the canal for 1 min. The collected samples were transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing sterile PBS and stored at −20 °C. After the shaping and sampling procedures were completed, an intracanal dressing was placed inside the root canal. Two groups were planned based on the type of intracanal dressing used as follows:

Calcium Hydroxide-Based Intracanal Dressing: After the treatment protocol was completed, the calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressing, Calcicur (Voco, Germany), was placed inside the root canal, 1 mm short of the working length, using an applicator tip.

Calcium Hydroxide-Based Intracanal Dressing: After the treatment protocol was completed, the calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressing, Calcicur (Voco, Germany), was placed inside the root canal, 1 mm short of the working length, using an applicator tip.

Tricalcium Silicate-Based Intracanal Dressing: After the treatment protocol was completed, the tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing, Bio-C Temp (Angelus, Brazil), was placed inside the root canal, 1 mm short of the working length, using an applicator tip.

Tricalcium Silicate-Based Intracanal Dressing: After the treatment protocol was completed, the tricalcium silicate-based intracanal dressing, Bio-C Temp (Angelus, Brazil), was placed inside the root canal, 1 mm short of the working length, using an applicator tip.

The canal orifices were temporarily closed using a sterile teflon pallet and the coronal cavities were restored with Cavit G (3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). Radiographs were taken to confirm the presence of the dressing placed in the canal.

Each patient received a VAS for pain assessment and was instructed to record their pain levels daily on a scale from 0 to 100 for the next 10 days. With the values recorded on the VAS as the basis, pain levels were categorized into four groups [25]: no pain (0–4 mm), mild pain (5–44 mm), moderate pain (45–74 mm), and severe pain (75–100 mm). The patients were scheduled for a second appointment 10 days later. In the second appointment, the crown of each tooth was disinfected following the previously described protocol, and the temporary restoration was removed aseptically under rubber dam isolation. The intracanal dressing was eliminated using 5 mL of saline activated by PUI and mechanically removed with the master apical file.

To collect the final microbiological sample (S3), three sterile paper points were placed at the working length and left inside the canal as previously described. After sampling, the root canals were filled with cold lateral condensation technique using guta percha cones and sealer (Sealapex, Sybron Kerr, Brea, CA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

The collected paper points were immediately placed into Eppendorf tubes containing 1 mL of PBS and stored at −20 °C for qPCR analysis. The frozen samples were then thawed and analyzed for bacterial load using universal 16 S rRNA primers and for fungal (C. albicans) presence using specific primers in a Real-Time PCR system. DNA isolation was performed using the Yeast DNA Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA), following the manufacturers’ protocols. The DNA concentrations were adjusted using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO spectrophotometer, and the microbial load was quantified via Real-Time PCR using the Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen, Germany).

Postoperative pain evaluation

The intensity of pain after root canal treatment was assessed on the 1 st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 10th days using the VAS. This scale was thoroughly explained to the patients prior to the treatment, and each patient was provided with a form to record their pain levels. On the specified days, the patients were asked to select the number that best represented their pain intensity and note it on the form, along with any analgesic use. These forms were collected during the follow-up appointment on the 10th day after the treatment. Additionally, the patients were contacted by phone on the specified dates to remind them to assess their pain intensity using the scale. The patients with whom contact could not be established were excluded from the study.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the study data was performed using the SPSS 20.0 statistical package program (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Chi-square test was used to examine the distribution of categorical variables between the groups.

The normality and homogeneity of the numerical data were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Levene’s test. Since the numerical data did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons.

For within-group comparisons of total bacterial load, C. albicans presence, and postoperative pain levels, Wilcoxon test and Friedman test were applied, respectively.

A 95% confidence interval (P = 0.05) was used for all the statistical analyses.

Discussion

Additional microbial reduction can be achieved by using an interappointment intracanal medication, which supplements the effects of chemo-mechanical procedures [26]. Ca(OH)2 is the most commonly used intracanal medication and its antimicrobial efficacy is corroborated by in vitro studies [27]. However, dentine alters the biocidal activity of calcium hydroxide questioning its benefit in root canal treatment [28]. Bio-C Temp is a calcium silicate–based bioceramic material specifically designed for use as an intracanal medicament. According to the manufacturer, this dressing provides Ca²⁺ ion release and maintains an alkaline environment, both of which contribute to its antibacterial potential [19]. Although there are in vitro studies evaluating the antibacterial efficacy of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressings [19, 20, 29, 30], no clinical studies have examined the antibacterial-antifungal activity and postoperative pain effects of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressing in teeth with apical periodontitis.

In the present study, we evaluated the effects of a calcium silicate–based intracanal dressing and a calcium hydroxide–based intracanal dressing on total bacterial load, C. albicans, and postoperative pain in single-rooted teeth with apical periodontitis requiring retreatment. Intragroup analysis revealed that both calcium silicate– and calcium hydroxide–based root canal dressings significantly reduced the total bacterial load and the amount of C. albicans before and after treatment. Furthermore, a statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups when the total bacterial load levels were compared after medication placement (S3). Based on these results, the null hypothesis was rejected.

There was a significant difference in the total bacterial load between S2 and S3 samples in the patients treated with both calcium hydroxide- and calcium silicate-based root canal dressings. Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the antimicrobial efficacy of Ca(OH)₂ in root canal systems. Martinho et al. [31] reported that calcium hydroxide exhibited strong antimicrobial activity when left in the canal for seven days, while Shuping et al. [26] observed a 92.5% reduction in bacterial load following its use. Similarly, in vitro investigations have evaluated the antimicrobial effect of tricalcium silicate-based dressing. Balto et al. [20] demonstrated a 75.25% reduction in the proportion of dead bacteria after two weeks of application against Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Patri et al. [30] confirmed the antimicrobial activity of Bio-C Temp on days 1, 3, and 7 using spectrophotometric analysis. These findings are consistent with our study.

Comparative analysis revealed that the reduction in bacterial load was greater in the calcium hydroxide group. Previous in vitro studies has suggested that calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressing exhibit superior antibacterial and antibiofilm properties compared to tricalcium silicate-based dressing [19, 29, 32]. Another study showed that calcium silicate-based intracanal medicament has lower antibacterial activity compared to conventional calcium hydroxide-based medicaments [33]. An alkaline pH is essential for the biological action of intracanal medications, supporting the mineralization process and eliminating microorganisms involved in endodontic infections. Tricalcium silicate–based medicaments release fewer Ca²⁺ and OH⁻ ions during the hydration reaction [19, 34, 35], which results in lower pH levels within the first 24 h compared with calcium hydroxide–based medicaments. Siquera et al. [36] showed that Bio-C Temp failed to reach the minimum recommended pH, with its highest value being 8.56 after 24 h. In contrast, Campello et al. [37] reported that Bio-C Temp exhibited antibacterial efficacy comparable to calcium hydroxide pastes, a finding that differs from previous reports and from the results of the present study. These discrepancies may be attributed to methodological differences, including the use of extracted teeth and mixed bacterial cultures from the subgingival crevice.

Calcium hydroxide–based medicaments are formulated with aqueous vehicles, whereas tricalcium silicate–based medicaments contain a viscous vehicle, polyethylene glycol, which regulates ion release and slows the diffusion of hydroxyl ions into tissues [38]. Viscous carriers dissolve less than aqueous carriers due to their higher molecular weight [39]. While aqueous solutions like water and saline promote rapid ion release, viscous carriers like propylene glycol cause calcium and hydroxyl ions to be released into the environment over a longer period [40]. This difference in vehicle may influence antimicrobial performance. Capitanio et al. [41] found that, at 24 h, Bio-C Temp exhibited significantly lower calcium release and pH than Ultracal XS, although no difference was observed at 7 days. Therefore, the differences in bacterial load reduction observed in the present study may be attributed to variations in hydroxyl ion release and pH dynamics determined by the specific properties of the vehicles.

A statistically significant difference in C. albicans levels was found between the S2 and S3 samples within each group. Mejía et al. [42] demonstrated that Ca(OH)₂ exhibited antimicrobial and antifungal activity against E. faecalis and C. albicans. Similarly, Delgado et al. [43] showed that calcium hydroxide has a significant antifungal effect in their study. Both dressings used in our study exerted their antimicrobial and antifungal effects by releasing hydroxyl ions, leading to an increase in pH.

An important virulence factor of C. albicans is its ability to transition between blastospore and hyphal forms. This characteristic allows it to invade host tissues and evade phagocytosis by macrophages [44]. C. albicans can survive in a wide pH range, including highly alkaline environments, which enables it to cause persistent infections. It can also adhere to dentin surfaces and aggregate with bacteria [45]. The resistant biofilm formed by C. albicans provides protection against starvation, alkaline environments, mechanical forces, and various antimicrobial chemicals [46]. Additionally, C. albicans contains PacC (pH-responsive transcription factor), which increases its resistance by controlling various characteristics such as pigmentation, nitrogen source utilization, penicillin synthesis, and growth in alkaline pH [47]. In the present study, some S3 samples showed that C. albicans was not completely eradicated. This result could be attributed to the resistance of C. albicans. There was no significant difference in C. albicans levels between the groups at S3 stage (p >0.05). Similar results might have been obtained due to the resistant microflora present in teeth requiring retreatment and the limited effectiveness of the existing dressings.

In the present study, the effects of calcium hydroxide and tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressings on postoperative pain were evaluated, and no significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of postoperative pain and analgesic intake. There are no studies in the literature evaluating the effect of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressings on postoperative pain. Therefore, our study will be compared with similar studies. According to the intergroup analysis, in the calcium hydroxide-based intracanal dressing group, the postoperative pain levels gradually decreased from day 1 to day 10, with a statistically significant difference between the preoperative pain levels and postoperative day 1. The studies by Gündoğar et al. [48], Pak & White [49], and Topçuoğlu et al. [50] reported that the incidence and intensity of postoperative pain were highest within the first 24 h but significantly decreased after the 48th hour. Considering the microbiological etiology of pain after endodontic treatment [44], the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-modifying properties of calcium hydroxide have been suggested to contribute to its analgesic effects [51, 52]. However, it has also been reported that this effect does not always occur [53]. This finding is consistent with the results of our study and can be explained by the resistance of certain microorganisms to Ca(OH)₂ and its insufficient penetration into the dentin tubules.

No studies have examined the effects of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressings on postoperative pain. Therefore, the current study was compared with similar studies. Tricalcium silicate-based materials are biocompatible and chemically stable. Particularly, their ability to induce minimal inflammatory responses in cases of periapical extrusion is one of the reasons for their preference [54]. Additionally, calcium silicates are believed to activate fewer nociceptors responsible for the release of calcitonin gene-related peptides, which may prevent neurogenic inflammation and pain [55]. Bioceramic pastes have shown lower IL-6 and TNF-α expression, demonstrating reduced inflammatory activity [56].In our study, the decreased pain levels in the postoperative period associated with the tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressing may be related to the material’s low inflammatory potential and biocompatible properties.

To assess pain, various scales are used to make this subjective experience as objective as possible, allowing the patient to express the intensity and nature of pain using words or numbers. Research has shown that the VAS is one of the most commonly used measurement methods and is considered reliable and valid for pain assessment [25]. Based on these studies, the VAS was used in the current study.

Molecular methods, such as real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), have the advantage of assessing a broader microbial population than culture methods. This method can detect not only bacteria that can be cultured but also those that are alive but exhibit low metabolic activity, which often go undetected in culture methods. Several studies in the literature have used the qPCR technique to identify microorganisms within the root canal system [22, 57, 58]. Considering these studies, we employed qPCR in the present study.

One limitation of the present study is that a premixed dressing containing 45% calcium hydroxide (Calcicur) was used instead of pure calcium hydroxide powder. Factors that determine the effectiveness of calcium hydroxide dressings include the method of preparation, radiopacity level, and method of application to the root canal [27, 59]. Additionally, it is challenging to ensure homogeneous placement of calcium hydroxide throughout the canal. The consistency of carriers combined with calcium hydroxide can influence clinical ease of use and may hinder its uniform distribution within the canal system [60], which means that differences in the application method of dressings could impact the results. Since the tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressing we used was in an injectable form, we chose an injectable calcium hydroxide-containing dressing to ensure that the application method of the dressings remained consistent. Another limitation of the present study is that, although the patients were blinded to the type of intracanal dressing used for the treatment, the operator could not be blinded during the trial due to the nature of the procedure; however, to minimize potential bias, all statistical analyses were performed by an independent examiner who was blinded to the group allocation. Further studies are needed to better understand the periapical healing potential and impact on clinical outcomes of tricalcium silicate–based intracanal dressing.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, it was observed that the tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressing was effective in reducing the total bacterial load and C. albicans levels. However, it should be noted that the calcium hydroxide-based dressing provided a greater reduction in total bacterial load. Both dressings exhibited similar antifungal effects and postoperative pain levels. Further clinical studies are required to explore the effects of tricalcium silicate-based root canal dressings.
