Consumer product-related fractures among children younger than 18 years old treated in United States emergency departments, 1990–2023
Authors: Jennifer Yu, Jaahnavi Badeti, Thitphalak Chounthirath, Gary A Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to analyze the characteristics and trends of consumer product-related fractures among children < 18 years old treated in United States (US) emergency departments (EDs).MethodsNational Electronic Injury Surveillance System data for 1990–2023 were analyzed and national fracture estimates were calculated. US Census Bureau data were used to calculate annual fracture rates.ResultsAn estimated 24,071,446 children < 18 years old were treated for nonfatal consumer product-related fractures in US EDs during 1990–2023, and 7.8% were admitted to the hospital. The average annual rate of pediatric fractures was 983.8 per 100,000 US children < 18 years old, and overall, the rate decreased by 26.8% during the study. Males accounted for 65.0% of fractures and were more likely than females to be admitted (OR: 1.094, 95% CI: 1.091–1.098). However, the difference in the rate of fractures between males and females narrowed during the study. The number of fractures peaked at age 11 years among females compared with 13 years among males. Fractures occurred most frequently in the home (38.7%), followed by a place of sports or recreation (32.7%) and school (19.5%). The most common injury mechanism was fell off/rolled off (36.4%), followed by play and/or sport (25.0%). Most (71.4%) fractures involved the upper extremity. Children < 6 years old had the highest hospital admission rate from 2009 to 2023 and were twice as likely to be admitted (OR: 1.986, 95% CI: 1.979–1.992) than older children during the study. Most (70.6%) fractures were associated with sports and recreation activities and equipment. Fractures involving home furnishings, fixtures, and accessories (OR: 1.221, 95% CI: 1.215–1.227) or the injury mechanism of fell off/rolled off (OR: 1.435, 95% CI: 1.430–1.439) were more likely to be associated with hospital admission than fractures associated with other products and activities or injury mechanisms, respectively.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate secular trends of consumer product-related fractures among US children, locations where fractures occurred, injury mechanisms, and associated consumer products and activities. The characteristics and trends of pediatric fractures identified in this study can help inform future research and prevention of these common injuries.Keywords: Orthopedic injury, Fracture, Trauma, Pediatric, Adolescent

Background

Fractures are common injuries among children with a reported annual incidence of 12 to 36 per 1,000 children [1–4]. It is estimated that fractures account for 10–15% of childhood injuries [3, 5], and approximately 42% of boys and 27% of girls will experience a fracture by age 16 years [3]. There were an estimated 11.5 fracture-related emergency department (ED) visits per 1,000 United States (US) children from 2010 to 2015 with a hospital admission rate of 5% [6]. Another study reported a similar ED visit rate of 9.5 per 1,000 US children in 2010 [7]. Orthopedic surgery for fractures and musculoskeletal injuries has been reported as the second most common type of pediatric in-patient surgery [7, 8]. Although most children fully recover, there may be important physical, psychological, and social sequelae, including effects on school attendance and family members [9–12]. In addition, pediatric musculoskeletal trauma was associated with an estimated $3.5 billion in health care costs in the US in 2012 [6, 13].

Previous research on the epidemiology of pediatric fractures has often been limited in scope, being restricted to a specific type of fracture [14–20], injury mechanism [21], sport or recreational activity [20, 22, 23], single institution [20, 22, 24], inpatient population [25, 26], subset of the pediatric age group [4, 19, 22, 24, 27–31], or single-year period [7, 16, 25, 26]. Other studies have included children and adults [14] or non-US populations [4, 15, 22, 24, 27–29, 32–34]. We are aware of only two comprehensive national studies of the epidemiology of pediatric fractures treated in US EDs, including one that analyzed data from the Nationwide Emergency Department Sample for 2010–2015 [6] and another that used data from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) for 2010 [7]. To our knowledge, previous investigations have not evaluated the secular trends of fractures among US children, locations where fractures occurred, injury mechanisms, and associated consumer products and activities.

The objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the characteristics and trends of consumer product-related fractures among children < 18 years old treated in US EDs over more than three decades (1990–2023) using nationally representative data from the NEISS.

Methods

Data sources and case selection criteria

We analyzed data for patients < 18 years old treated in EDs from January 1, 1990 through December 31, 2023 for nonfatal fractures (NEISS diagnosis code 57) associated with consumer products or sports and recreational activities and related equipment. Data were obtained from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS), which is managed by the United States Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC). The NEISS includes data collected from approximately 100 US hospital emergency departments (EDs), which represent a stratified probability sample of the more than 5,000 US hospitals with at least six inpatient beds and a 24-hour emergency department [35]. Trained NEISS coders at each participating hospital enter data from hospital patient medical records into the NEISS database using established coding guidelines [36]. The NEISS does not collect data for injuries associated with motor vehicles, aircraft, boats, firearms, medical devices, or other products that do not fall under the jurisdiction of the CPSC; occupational injuries are also not included in the NEISS database [36]. July 1 st intercensal and postcensal population estimates were obtained from the US Census Bureau to calculate annual rates of fracture-related ED visits, including age group-specific and sex-specific rates [37–40]. This study was determined to be exempt by the institutional review board of the authors’ institution.

Study variables

Variables included in this study were year of ED visit, patient age, sex, body region of fracture, disposition from the ED, location where the fracture occurred, injury mechanism, and associated consumer products and activities. Age was categorized as < 6 years old (young children), 6–12 years old (children), and 13–17 years old (teenagers). Based on NEISS variable categories, body region was grouped as (1) head or neck, (2) upper extremity, (3) trunk, (4) lower extremity, or (5) other. Disposition from the ED was grouped into (1) treated and released, (2) admitted (including treated and transferred to another hospital, treated and admitted within the same facility, and held for < 24 h observation), or (3) left against medical advice. Also based on NEISS variable categories, the location where the fracture occurred was grouped as (1) home (NEISS categories: home, apartment/condominium, and mobile/manufactured home), (2) school, (3) street, (4) place of sports or recreation, or (5) other (NEISS categories: public place, industrial place, and farm).

The injury mechanism associated with the fracture was determined by searching for keywords and phrases in the brief narrative that describes the circumstances of the injury event for each case and was categorized as (1) fell off/rolled off, (2) struck/struck by, (3) during play and/or sport, (4) jump/climb/slide, (5) slip or trip, or (6) other (including dropped, twist, pushed or tossed, dragged, stepped on object, and unspecified mechanism). If there was a sequence of actions leading to the fracture, the mechanism most proximal to the injury was selected; for example, if a child climbed up a tree and fell, then ‘fell off’ was selected as the mechanism.

Consumer products and activities associated with fractures were categorized based on NEISS product codes and were grouped into the major categories (1) sports/recreation and equipment, (2) home structures and construction materials, (3) home furnishings, fixtures, and accessories, and (4) other, and each of these categories had subcategories based on those used in the CPSC annual report, e.g., the sports/recreation and equipment category had the subcategories of football, bicycles, basketball, etc [41]. For the 55,400 unweighted cases with two reported product codes, only one product code was chosen based on the following hierarchical filters. First, if a case included the product code for “rugs or carpets, not specified” (NEISS code 676), then the other product code was chosen. Second, in the remaining cases, if a case included the product code for “floors or flooring materials” (NEISS code 1807), then the other product code was chosen. Third, after the two previous filters were applied, among remaining cases, a product code was randomly selected for cases with both product codes falling under the same major product or activity category. If a case had both a sports/recreation-related code and a non-sports/recreation-related code, the sports/recreation-related code was chosen. After these filters were applied, any remaining cases were assigned one of the two product codes using random selection.

Statistical analysis

SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc. Cary, NC) software was used for statistical analyses. National estimates were calculated using the PROC SURVEYFREQ procedure in SAS by applying the sample weight, cluster, and stratum variables provided in the NEISS database, which account for the complex probability sampling design in addition to a sampling change in 1997. All data reported in this article are national estimates unless otherwise noted as unweighted cases. According to CPSC guidelines, estimates were considered potentially unstable if the estimated number of fractures was < 1,200, the number of unweighted fractures was < 20, or the coefficient of variation was >33% [42]. All national estimates reported in this article are stable estimates unless stated otherwise. Simple or piecewise linear regression was performed to determine the statistical significance of linear temporal trends by evaluating whether the null hypothesis of slope = 0 could be rejected at a significance level of α = 0.05 using Student’s t-test. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the magnitude of association between selected risk factors and hospital admission.

Results

An estimated 24,071,446 children < 18 years old were treated for nonfatal consumer product-related fractures in US EDs during 1990–2023, corresponding to an estimated annual average of 707,984 fractures (Table 1). Males accounted for 65.0% of fractures and the proportion of males increased with increasing age group. The number of fractures peaked at age 11 years among females compared with 13 years among males (data not shown). There was a seasonal decline in the number of fractures during the cold-weather months of November through March (ranging from 6.1% to 7.8% per month) with the highest number of fractures occurring in May (10.3%) and September (11.0%) (data not shown). The most common location for fracture occurrence was in the home (38.7%), followed by a place of sports or recreation (32.7%) and school (19.5%). The most common injury mechanism was fell off/rolled off (36.4%), followed by play and/or sport (25.0%). The upper extremity was the most common body region involved (71.4%), followed by lower extremity (21.7%) and head and neck (5.1%); this relative ranking of body regions was seen across all age groups. The lower arm was the part of the upper extremity most frequently involved, accounting for 18.4% of all fractures, followed by wrist (15.8%) and finger (15.3%) (Table 2).

Most (92.2%) children who experienced fractures were treated and released from the ED and 7.8% were admitted (Table 1). The proportion of fractures that were treated and released increased with increasing age group, while the proportion of admissions decreased. Fractures to the head and neck were four times more likely (OR: 4.044, 95% CI: 4.026–4.062) to be associated with admission than fractures in non-head and neck body regions.

Most (70.6%) fractures were associated with sports and recreation, followed by home structures and construction materials (12.8%) and home furnishings, fixtures, and accessories (9.9%) (Table 1). Sports and recreation was the leading category related to fractures across all age groups, and the proportion of fractures associated with this category increased with increasing age group. Fractures related to home furnishings, fixtures, and accessories were more likely to be associated with admission (OR: 1.221, 95% CI: 1.215–1.227) than those associated with other products and activities. Males were more likely than females to be admitted (OR: 1.094, 95% CI: 1.091–1.098). In addition, fell off/rolled off was more likely than other injury mechanisms to be associated with a hospital admission (OR: 1.435, 95% CI: 1.430–1.439).

Children younger than six years old

Fractures among < 6-year-olds involved males in 55.2% of cases and nearly three-fourths (73.9%) of fractures in this age group occurred at home (Table I). The most common injury mechanism was fell off/rolled off (54.0%), followed by jump/climb/slide (16.0%). Most fractures involved the upper extremity (70.5%), including 21.7% to the lower arm and 11.9% to the elbow (Tables 1 and 2). Among children < 6 years old, 7.3% of fractures were associated with the head and neck region (Table 1).

Most (87.5%) children < 6 years old with fractures were treated and released from the ED and 12.4% were admitted to the hospital (Table 1). Children < 6 years old were twice as likely to be admitted (OR: 1.986, 95% CI: 1.979–1.992) than older children. Sports and recreation accounted for 37.0% of fractures, followed by home furnishings, fixtures, and accessories (30.9%). Specific products and activities associated with fractures in this age group included beds (13.5%), monkey bars or other playground climbing equipment (7.9%), stairs and steps (6.7%), divans or studio couches (5.7%), trampolines (5.6%), and slides or sliding boards (5.0%) (Table 3). Among children < 6 years old who experienced a fracture involving the head region (not including the neck), fractures associated with floors/flooring materials were more likely to be admitted (OR = 1.088, 95% CI: 1.059–1.119), than those associated with non-floors/flooring products.

Children 6–12 years old

Among fractures involving 6–12-year-olds, males accounted for 59.2%, and 36.2% occurred at home, followed by a place of sports or recreation (33.1%) and school (20.7%) (Table 1). Fell off/rolled off was the injury mechanism associated with 40.8% of fractures in this age group, followed by play and/or sport (22.4%). Most fractures involved the upper extremity (76.6%), including to the lower arm (22.9%), wrist (19.8%), and finger (15.4%) (Tables 1 and 2).

Most (92.9%) 6–12-year-olds with fractures were treated and released and 7.1% were admitted. Sports and recreation accounted for 76.2% of fractures, followed by home structures and construction materials (10.8%) (Table 1). The most common specific products and activities associated with fractures in this age group were bicycles (10.2%), football (7.7%), and monkey bars or other playground climbing equipment (7.0%) (Table 3).

Adolescents 13–17 years old

Among 13–17-year-olds with fractures, more than three-fourths (77.8%) were males and 43.5% sustained a fracture at a place of sports or recreation, followed by school (25.3%) and home (21.3%). Adolescents commonly experienced fractures during play and/or sport (37.9%). The upper extremity (65.3%), lower extremity (25.8%), and head and neck (6.1%) were the body regions most involved in this age group (Table 1). More specifically, the finger was involved in 19.5% of fractures, followed by wrist (13.7%) and lower arm (10.7%) (Table 2).

Most (93.8%) adolescents who sustained fractures were treated and released and 6.1% were admitted to the hospital. Sports and recreation accounted for most (82.2%) fractures in this age group, followed by home structures and construction materials (11.0%) (Table 1). The most common specific products and activities associated with fractures in this age group were football (16.0%), basketball (14.2%), and bicycles (6.6%) (Table 3).

Trends

The average annual rate of pediatric fractures treated in US EDs was 983.8 per 100,000 US children < 18 years old during the study period. The fracture rate decreased non-linearly by 4.2% from 1,018.1 per 100,000 US children < 18 years old in 1990 to 975.5 in 1997, followed by a 21.6% increase to 1,185.8 in 2004 (P = 0.04), and then a 37.2% non-linear decrease to 744.8 in 2023, including a transient dip in 2020 and 2021. Overall, there was a 26.8% decrease during the study years (Fig. 1). The fracture rate among males was greater than that among females throughout the study period; however, the difference between the sex-specific rates narrowed over that period and followed the overall general trend.

Discussion

An estimated >24 million children < 18 years old were treated for nonfatal consumer product-related fractures in US EDs during 1990–2023, which corresponds to an average of 1,940 fractures per day or one fracture every 45 s nationally. Previous reports have indicated that pediatric fractures were increasing [3, 43] or decreasing [6], but our 34-year study period revealed that trends have varied over time, with an initial decrease from 1990 to 1997, then an increase from 1997 to 2004, followed by a decrease from 2004 to 2023, resulting in an overall 27% decrease during the study years. The reason that these reported trends are different may be attributable to the use of different databases and our focus on consumer product-related fractures. It is likely that several factors influenced these trends, including changes in product and environmental safety, sports safety rules and equipment, ED care-seeking behavior, and physical activity among children, including sports participation.

Consistent with prior reports [3, 7], older children had higher fracture rates than younger children. This may be attributable to developing mobility during the younger years, followed by increasing participation in activities with higher potential for substantial kinetic energy transfer, such as sports, during the later childhood and teenage years. However, the difference between age group-specific fracture rates narrowed substantially during the study years, with rates for 13–17-year-olds and 6–12-year-olds decreasing faster than those for children < 6 years old following the peak in fracture rates in 2004. There were also changes in the location where fractures occurred during the study period, specifically, the home was the location with the highest rate of fractures from 1990 until 2010, when sports and recreational locations replaced the home environment as the site with the highest rate of fractures.

Although there was an overall decrease in the rate of fractures among children < 18 years old treated in US EDs from 1990 to 2023, the proportion of those ED visits that were admitted to the hospital increased from 2.8% in 1990 to 3.1% in 2023. The decision to admit a child to the hospital following a fracture may be influenced by many factors, including fracture type and severity, circumstances of the injury, available resources in the home and access to medical follow-up care, comorbidities, and changes in medical standards of care over time. For example, there has been a trend of increased operative management of pediatric long bone fractures since the 1990 s [44–47]. The overall pediatric fracture admission rate decreased by 1.8% during the study period, which reflected overall decreases among 6–12-year-olds and 13–17-year-olds and an overall increase of 39% among children < 6 years old. Children < 6 years old became the age group with the highest fracture admission rate starting in 2009 and continuing through 2023, and children < 6 years old were twice as likely to be admitted to the hospital than older children. In addition, fell off/rolled off was 1.4 times more likely than other injury mechanisms to be associated with a hospital admission. These findings suggest that additional research and fracture prevention efforts be directed towards falls in the home among children < 6 years old.

Prevention of fractures associated with sports and recreational locations also merits additional attention, especially among children 6–12 years old and 13–17 years old, representing 33% and 44% of the fractures in these age groups, respectively. Sports and recreational locations replaced the home environment as the site with the highest rate of fractures from 2010 to 2023. The rate of hospital admissions associated with fractures that occurred at a place of sports or recreation increased overall by 103% during the study period. Fracture prevention strategies include product and environmental design, and rules and guidelines with accompanying education for participants and supervising adults; these strategies should be adapted for the specific sport or recreational activity. For example, our findings showed that playground climbing equipment was an important source of fractures among < 6-year-olds and 6–12-year-olds. The magnitude of the energy transfer associated with falls from a climbing apparatus is determined by the fall height and the cushioning properties of the impact surface [48]. Both of these factors are addressed by ASTM voluntary safety standards and US Consumer Product Safety Commission guidelines [49–51]. However, these safety policies focus on prevention of head injuries and not forearm fractures, which are the most common type of fracture associated with playground climbing equipment. Modifications of the safety standards have been proposed that would focus on forearm fracture prevention [48, 52, 53]. Consistently maintaining appropriate surfacing beneath playground equipment and limiting maximum equipment height to 1.5 to 2.5 m could help minimize injuries [48, 52]. In addition, safety education of children and supervising adults is important. These same prevention approaches can be adapted to other sports and recreational activities.

On March 13, 2020, a national emergency associated with the COVID-19 pandemic was declared in the US. Stay-at-home restrictions were implemented and school closures and cancellation of sports events followed. ED visit volumes decreased and fractures and other types of child injury were affected, such as child poisoning [54–56]. In this study, there was a transient decrease in the rate of fractures at school and places of sports or recreation (but not at home) in 2020 and 2021, followed by a rate rebound in subsequent years when COVID restrictions were lifted and children and their families returned to their normal activities. The fracture rate among children < 6 years old did not demonstrate the decline in 2020 and 2021 that was seen among 6–12-year-olds and 13–17-year-olds, likely because young children typically spend most of their time in and around the home and therefore were less affected by the stay-at-home restrictions. Hospital admission rates were also affected, with a transient decrease in 2020 seen for fractures at school and places of sports or recreation, but there was a transient increase observed for fractures occurring in the home.

Biological and behavioral/societal differences, including risk-taking and more aggressive behaviors, and gender-related activities may help explain why males had higher fracture rates than females [32]. However, the difference between the sex-specific fracture rates narrowed during the latter years of the study, with rates for males declining faster than those for females since the peak in the fracture rate in 2004. Males also experienced higher hospital admission rates than females throughout the study period. The male predominance of fractures has been reported in previous studies [3, 6, 7]. In addition, the number of fractures peaked at a younger age among females than males in this study, which has also been previously reported and is thought to be associated with diminished bone density during the pubertal growth spurt, which females experience before males [57, 58].

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. This study underestimates the true number of fractures because (1) some fractures are treated in non-ED medical settings, such as urgent care facilities or private physician offices, and (2) the NEISS does not collect data about occupational injuries or injuries associated with motor vehicles, aircraft, boats, firearms, medical devices, or other products that do not fall under the jurisdiction of the CPSC. The NEISS lacks variables providing information about fractures, such as the specific bone fractured, part of the bone fractured, Salter-Harris classification, or other details; although case narratives may include additional information, this information can be inconsistent or incomplete, which limited further analysis. The NEISS does not allow for longitudinal follow-up of patients to determine long-term medical outcome. In addition, the NEISS does not include personal identifiers; therefore, we were unable to determine if individuals were included in the dataset more than once. Because NEISS data only represent ED visits in the US, our findings may not reflect the corresponding epidemiology of pediatric fractures outside the US. Despite these limitations, the NEISS provides a valid stratified probability sample of consumer product-related fractures treated in US EDs that allows for calculating national estimates, which are useful for investigating the characteristics and trends of this common type of child injury.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate secular trends of consumer product-related fractures among US children, locations where fractures occurred, injury mechanisms, and associated consumer products and activities. The characteristics and trends of pediatric fractures identified in this study can help inform future research and prevention of these common injuries.
