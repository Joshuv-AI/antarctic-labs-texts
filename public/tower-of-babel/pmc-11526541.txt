18F-FDG PET/CT for predicting inferior vena cava wall invasion in patients of renal cell carcinoma with the presence of inferior vena cava tumor thrombus
Authors: Anhui Zhu, Xiaoyan Hou, Na Guo, Weifang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPreoperative evaluation of inferior vena cava (IVC) wall invasion is very important to improve outcomes of patients with renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and may allow surgical urologists to treat the IVC more effectively. The objective of this study was to evaluate preoperative 18F-FDG PET/CT in patients with RCC and IVC tumor thrombus (IVCTT) for the diagnosis of IVC wall invasion.MethodsThis retrospective case-control study evaluated 68 patients with RCC with level I-IV tumor thrombus. According to the histopathologic examination result, the patients were divided into IVC wall invasion group and non-invasion group. The 18F-FDG PET/CT features between two groups were analyzed. Furthermore, a logistic regression model was used to determine if there was an association between PET/CT features and IVC wall invasion.ResultsSixty-eight patients were evaluated, and 55.9% (38/68) had IVC wall invasion. Compared with non-invasion group, invasion group had higher SUVmax of RCC, higher SURmax (tumor to tumor thrombus ratio, Tu/Th), higher IVCTT coronal diameter, and longer IVCTT craniocaudal extent (all p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis showed that SURmax (Tu/Th) (OR 8.760 [95%CI, 1.019–75.310]; p = 0.048) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT (OR 1.143 [95%CI, 1.029–1.269]; p = 0.028) were predictors of IVC wall invasion. A model combining SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT achieved an AUC of 0.855 (95%CI, 0.757–0.954). The specificity and sensitivity for assessing IVC wall invasion was 92.1% and 76.7%, respectively.ConclusionsIncreases in SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT are associated with a higher probability of IVC wall invasion. Preoperative 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging may be used to assess IVC wall invasion.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common malignant tumor of urinary system. RCC has a tendency to invade into the renal vein or inferior vena cava (IVC) [1]. Inferior vena cava tumor thrombus (IVCTT) is seen in 4–10% of locally aggressive RCC cases [2]. Patients with IVC wall invasion have a poor prognosis after radical nephrectomy and tumor thrombectomy [3]. Segmental IVC resection or IVC wall resection is generally performed in patients with IVC wall invasion [4, 5]. The 5-year survival rate in patients without IVC wall resection was only 26%, but reached 57% after the IVC wall resection [6]. Therefore, preoperative evaluation of IVC wall invasion is very important to improve outcomes of patients with RCC, and may allow surgical urologists to treat the IVC more effectively [7].

According to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM system of renal cancer, T3c disease means IVCTT filling above the diaphragm or invading into the IVC wall [8]. 18F-FDG PET/CT is commonly performed to stage and restage for patients with RCC [9, 10]. Ferda et al. and Mizuno et al. supported that 18F-FDG PET/CT is an effective tool to assess the prognosis of patients with RCC [11, 12]. A new 18F-FDG PET/CT study of patients with RCC showed that SUVmax of RCC and SUVmax of IVCTT has not achieved a valuable result [13].

To our knowledge, the use of SURmax (tumor to tumor thrombus ratio, Tu/Th) and IVCTT maximum coronal diameter to diagnose IVC wall invasion in RCC patients has not been studied.The aim of this study was to evaluate preoperative 18F-FDG PET/CT for the diagnosis of IVC vascular wall invasion, and provided imaging evidence for clinical treatment decision.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional review board and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patients

The clinical and imaging data of sixty-eight patients who underwent radical nephrectomy and IVC thrombectomy between January 2012 and December 2021 were identified by a computerized search in our institutional radiological information system (RIS). The inclusion criteria included (a) patients were pathology-proven RCC with IVC level I-IV tumor thrombus, and (b) patients underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging within 2 weeks preoperatively. Patients were excluded if they had level 0 IVCTT, had postoperative recurrence, or had neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy at the time of examination. The nuclear grading was performed using the Fuhrman system [14]. The classification of IVCTT was based on the Mayo Clinic standard [15]. The patients were divided into two groups with and without IVC wall invasion according to the histopathologic examination result. This study cohort and exclusion criteria are listed in Fig. 1.

Imaging examination

A 52-ring Biograph Integrated PET/CT system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) was used for examinations. The field of view (FOV) was 58 cm, and pixel size of the PET images reconstructed for fusion was 3 mm with a matrix size of 128 × 128. The technical parameters used for CT imaging: pitch, 1.5; gantry rotation speed, 0.5 s/rotation; tube voltage, 120 kV; modulated tube current, 30–200 mA.

Patients were fasting 6–8 h before examination and received an intravenous injection of 3.7–4.4 MBq/kg 18F-FDG. About 1 h later, CT scans were performed from parietal to proximal femur. PET images were performed for the same longitudinal coverage in a three-dimensional (3D) acquisition mode with 3 min per bed. CT images were fused with the PET data, and used to generate a map for attenuation correction, then generating reconstructed images for review on a PACS workstation (Medex system Workstation, Medex Tecknologies Private Limited).

Image analysis

Each 18F-FDG PET/CT study was separately read by a 22 and 29 years of board-certified experience in nuclear oncology, respectively. The findings of the reviewers were compared, and discrepancies agreed upon by consensus. Reviews were performed in the absence of clinical data (including CT, MRI, and pathologic results) and information from other PET/CT scans.

A suspected malignant lesion means a positive accumulation of focal 18F-FDG higher than that of the adjacent tissue. IVCTT was diagnosed when the FDG uptake in the venous lumen was higher than that of the adjacent aorta. The FDG uptake in the venous lumen was nearly equal or lower than that of the adjacent vein or aorta was interpreted as venous bland thrombus (VBT) [16, 17]. Two nuclear medicine radiologists determined the IVCTT level.

A semi-quantitative measurement (in millimeters) of the RCC and IVCTT included the maximum diameter of primary tumor, the craniocaudal extent of IVCTT which was measured the vertical distance from its top to the bottom, and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT. In the coronal fused PET/CT image, the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT was measured perpendicular to the IVC at the place where FDG uptake increased. The SUVmax and SURmax (Tu/Th) were used to quantify FDG uptake in the primary tumor and IVCTT. The SUVmax of primary tumor divided by the SUVmax of IVCTT was SURmax (Tu/Th). SURmax (Tu/Th) indicated the difference of FDG uptake between the primary tumor and IVCTT.

Statistical analysis

Skewed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), and Mann-Whitney U test was used for analysis between two groups. Distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and two-sample independent t-test was used for analysis between two groups. Demographic variables were expressed as frequency and proportions, and chi-square test was used for analysis between two groups. The univariable associations of PET/CT features with the probability of IVC wall invasion between two groups were evaluated using binary logistic regression analysis. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was also performed to determine the best variables, and the optimal cut point was selected according to the Youden method. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Two-tailed p < 0 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Sixty-eight patients ranged in age from 39 to 82 years. There were 15 women aged 48–80 years (mean age, 60 years ± 10 [standard deviation]) and 53 men aged 39–82 years (mean age, 63 years ± 8 [standard deviation]). The pathological type of RCC included clear RCC (n = 51), type 2 papillary RCC (n = 16), and other type (n = 1).

Of the 68 patients, 55.88% (38/68) had IVC wall invasion. Thirty-eight patients with IVC wall invasion underwent IVC segmental resection (n = 15) and the involved IVC wall resection (n = 23). For the remaining 30 patients, IVC wall invasion was not detected at the time of surgery, and the final histopathology reports for these patients confirmed the absence of IVC wall invasion.

The clinical IVCTT grade was level I in 5 patients, level II in 33 patients, level III in 18 patients, and level IV in 12 patients. Among the 68 patients, 25% (17/68) had VBT. There was no difference in age, sex, side of tumor, RCC Fuhrman grade, IVCTT level, VBT, or metastasis stage between two groups. There were apparent difference in RCC pathological type between two groups (p = 0.008). Patient characteristics are listed in Table 1.

The logistic regression model of multivariate analysis showed that two features were predictors of IVC wall invasion: SURmax (Tu/Th) (OR 13.793 [95%CI, 1.763–107.933]; p = 0.012), and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT (OR 1.181 [95%CI, 1.081–1.290]; p < 0.001). Taken 1.3 as the best cut-off value of SURmax (Tu/Th), the sensitivity, the specificity, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 47.4%, 86.7%, and 0.694, respectively. Taken 34.5 mm as the best cut-off value of the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT, the sensitivity, the specificity, and the AUC was 65.8%, 86.7%, and 0.806, respectively. Combining these two features, we found that the sensitivity, the specificity, and the AUC for assessing IVC wall invasion was 92.1%, 76.7%, and 0.855 (95%CI, 0.757–0.954), respectively (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Discussion

IVC infiltrated by IVCTT is a risk factor for poor prognosis [6]. Segmental IVC resection or IVC wall resection is generally performed in patients with IVC wall invasion [4, 5]. Therefore, preoperative evaluation of IVC wall invasion is very important to the urologic surgeon for choosing more effective operative strategies.

One study showed that 18F-FDG PET/CT can effectively detect IVCTT with the accuracy of 100% (41/41), but combining SUVmax of RCC with SUVmax of IVCTT had not achieved a valuable result for the diagnosis of IVC wall invasion [13]. In our study, 55.9% (38/68) of the patients had IVC wall invasion. PET/CT detected all the IVCTT with the accuracy of 100% (68/68). Although SUVmax of IVCTT were not different (median SUVmax: 5.6 vs. 4.7, respectively; p = 0.19), the SUVmax of RCC, the SURmax (Tu/Th), the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT, and the craniocaudal extent of IVCTT in patients with IVC wall invasion were significantly higher than that of patients without IVC wall invasion (all p < 0.05). By the multivariate analysis, our data demonstrated that the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT (OR 1.181 [95%CI, 1.081–1.290]; p < 0.001) and SURmax (Tu/Th) (OR 13.793 [95%CI, 1.763–107.9]; p = 0.012) was independent predictive factor for IVC wall invasion. Combining these two features, the AUC was 0.855, which was better than that of SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT alone. Furthermore, increases in SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT were associated with a higher probability of IVC wall invasion, which had not been reported in previous studies. The higher SURmax (Tu/Th) indicated that FDG uptake in primary tumor was higher than that of VTT. Studies have shown that FDG uptake of high-grade RCC is significantly higher than that of low-grade patients [10], and high-grade patients have worse prognosis [18]. Although there was no significant difference in Fuhrman grade of primary tumors in this study, the primary tumors in the invasive group tended to be of higher grade. Among the 38 cases in the invasion group, 13 cases were Fuhrman grade III, 7 cases were grade IV, 52.6% (20/38) were high grade. In the non-invasive group, there were 7 cases of Fuhrman grade III and 2 cases of Fuhrman Grade IV, and the proportion of high grade was only 30.0% (9/30). Therefore, when the FDG uptake of the primary tumor is significantly higher than that of IVCTT, it may indicate that the primary tumor is of a higher grade and therefore has a tendency to invade IVC wall. This study showed the higher SURmax (Tu/Th), not higher SUVmax value of IVCTT only is important in predicting IVC wall invasion.The reason may be related to the heterogeneous composition of primary tumor and tumor thrombus. Due to the small sample size in this study, the sample needs to be expanded for further study.

Alayed et al. found that patients with IVC wall invasion in MRI images had larger IVC diameters (41 ± 9 vs. 19 ± 6 mm; p = 0.003), and craniocaudal extent of IVCTT (87 ± 34 vs. 51 ± 31 mm; p = 0.024) [19]. Sheth et al. also found that the arteries feeding the tumor thrombus from the adjacent organ might predict IVC wall invasion in the early arterial phase in MDCT image [20]. It is well known that 18F-FDG PET/CT has limitations in showing of IVC wall invasion because of poor soft-tissue resolution and cannot precisely measure the maximum coronal IVC diameter in PET/CT images. So, we measured the maximum coronal IVCTT diameter instead of measuring the maximum coronal IVC diameter due to PET/CT can display the FDG uptake extent of IVCTT. In the present study, the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT (36.3 ± 7.8 vs. 26.9 ± 7.9 mm; p < 0.001) and craniocaudal extent of IVCTT([median, 74.5 mm] vs. [median,48.0 mm]; p = 0.001) were significantly larger in patients with wall invasion than those without wall invasion.

Liu et al. assessed IVC wall invasion using CT, MRI, and contrast-enhanced ultrasound) in patients with RCC, and found that the maximum coronal IVC diameter and VBT were independent risk factors for IVC wall invasion [21]. In our study, 17 cases with VBT verified by surgery, 16 cases were detected by PET/CT and 1 cases missed due to its small size. However, our study showed that VBT was not a predictor of IVC wall invasion.

Previous studies have found that the histological subtype of RCC can significantly influence its behavior, including the tendency to invade the IVC wall. The more common subtypes of RCC are clear cell carcinoma (70%), types 1 and 2 papillary carcinoma (15%). Studies have shown that clear cell carcinoma is more likely to extend into the IVC compared to other subtypes [22]. The incidence of IVC wall invasion in type 1 papillary carcinoma is relatively lower, reflecting its generally less aggressive nature. However, type 2 papillary carcinoma is generally more aggressive and has a higher propensity for vascular invasion, including IVC wall invasion [23, 24]. According to a multi-institutional study by Faba et al., there was no significant difference in histological type of RCC between 793 cases with IVC wall invasion and 230 cases without IVC wall invasion [18]. However, our data demonstrated that there were significant differences in histological type of RCC between two groups. In our study, the types of RCC in the group of 38 cases with IVC wall invasion and the group of 30 cases without IVC wall invasion included clear cell RCC 23(60.5%) and 28(93.3%), respectively; type 2 papillary RCC 14 (36.8%) and 2(6.7% ), respectively; others 1(2.7%) and 0, respectively. There are some differences between this study and Faba’s report. The reasons for this are, on the one hand, the small sample size of this group; on the other hand, the differences in RCC subtypes. All papillary RCC in our group were type 2. In contrast, almost all papillary RCC in Faba’s study were type 1, with 41 (5.3%) and 20 (8.8%) in the IVC wall invasion group and no IVC wall invasion group, respectively, and only one case of type 2 papillary RCC in the invasion group. Since the low proportion of type 2 papillary RCC in patients without IVC wall invasion in our series, no further study was conducted on the correlation between the histological types and IVC wall invasion, and further confirmatory research with a larger sample size is needed.

There were some limitations to our study. First, data collection was retrospective, and some missing data were identified. Second, the study population underwent surgery performed by several urologic surgeons using different techniques over a long period. Third, our sample size of type 2 papillary was small and further studies are needed to confirm our results. Finally, enhanced CT was not performed in this study, which needs to be further improved. Despite these limitations, this study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to measure the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT and SURmax (Tu/Th) by 18F-FDG PET/CT and we found a stronger association between the two features with IVC wall invasion.

Conclusions

SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of inferior vena cava tumor thrombus (IVCTT) was predictors of IVC wall invasion. Increases in SURmax (Tu/Th) and the maximum coronal diameter of IVCTT were associated with a higher probability of IVC wall invasion. Preoperative 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging may be used to assess IVC wall invasion.
