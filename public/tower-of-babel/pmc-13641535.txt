Cross-Species Comparison between Mouse Kidney Multi-Omics and Human Genome-Wide Association Studies Highlights Molecular Features Associated with Oxalate Nephropathy
Authors: Ryosuke Chaya, Kazumi Taguchi, Yutaka Hashimoto, Yuya Shirai, Ryuya Edahiro, Noah Sasa, Kenichi Yamamoto, Yoshihiro Tomofuji, Shinichi Namba, Kyuto Sonehara, Rei Unno, Shuzo Hamamoto, Atsushi Okada, John Muroski, Yoichi Kato, Ileana M. Cristea, Yukinori Okada, Takahiro Yasui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a condition that has a significant effect on the quality of life because of its severe pain, and it also poses a risk to life expectancy through the potential for urinary tract infections and irreversible kidney injury. According to recent epidemiologic studies, the lifetime prevalence of urolithiasis is estimated to be 10%–15% in men and 7% in women in Europe, the United States, and Japan.1–4 In addition, urolithiasis is a persistent disease characterized by a high recurrence rate, with up to 50% of patients experiencing recurrence within a decade.5,6 The pathophysiology of urolithiasis is complex, influenced by both genetic and acquired factors.7

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) has been used as a genetic approach to elucidate the pathogenesis of urolithiasis. According to twin studies, the heritability of urolithiasis is estimated to be 57% in men and 46% in women. This high heritability underscores the importance of approaching urolithiasis research from a genetic perspective.8,9 In a recent large-scale urolithiasis GWAS, Tanikawa et al. identified 14 significant loci, including nine novel loci, in a urolithiasis GWAS involving 198,769 individuals (11,130 cases and 187,639 controls).10 Urolithiasis has occasionally been included as one of the target traits in a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS). In this study, we used data from a large-scale PheWAS performed by Sakaue et al. using BioBank Japan (BBJ), which included urolithiasis as one of the analyzed phenotypes.11

Another way to investigate the pathogenesis of urolithiasis is molecular biologic approaches, such as animal models and human specimens. Our research team developed mouse models of hyperoxaluric nephrocalcinosis in the kidney and identified molecules associated with urolithiasis.12–15 Gene expression in human renal papilla specimens, the site of urinary stone formation, revealed that FABP4 is associated with urolithiasis.16 GWAS and molecular biology approaches have advanced urolithiasis research but remain limited in identifying causal molecules and distinguishing cause from consequence, highlighting the need for integrative multi-omics and cross-species analyses.

In this research, we conducted a cross-species multi-omics analysis combining kidneys from a hyperoxaluric mouse model and urolithiasis GWAS data. We aimed to prioritize candidate molecules that may play functional roles in the mouse kidney and look for genetic associations in human GWAS data. The relevance of these candidate molecules was further assessed using additional urolithiasis-associated datasets. Analyzing the transcriptome, whole-cell (WC) proteome, and phosphoproteome in the kidneys of the hyperoxaluric nephrocalcinosis mouse model identified potential links to urolithiasis. GWAS summary statistics for urolithiasis from large-scale trans-ethnic meta-GWAS from BBJ, UK Biobank, and FinnGen (nTotal=198,769) were used for gene enrichment analysis using Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA).17 Incorporating data from mouse models and human studies, our study prioritized cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. Using publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data, we explored the cell types from which the molecules identified in the above analyses originated. Finally, we assessed their potential involvement in human urolithiasis using gene-expression data from human renal papillae of patients with urolithiasis and colocalization and Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses between GWAS and kidney expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data.

Methods

Animal Procedures

A hyperoxaluric mouse model was generated by intraperitoneal injection of glyoxylate (100 mg/kg).12 Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 week old) were divided into five groups (n=4): control, day 1, day 3, day 6 (1, 3, or 6 days of treatment), and day 12 (6-day treatment plus 6-day washout).

Histology

The kidneys were fixed in 4% formalin, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin for histologic analysis. Evaluation of calcium oxalate crystals within the kidneys was conducted using Pizzolato staining.

Protein Quantification Using Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Proteins from mouse kidney tissues were precipitated with methanol–chloroform. The extracted proteins were digested with trypsin and analyzed as peptides for WC proteomics and phosphoproteomics. For phosphoproteomic analysis, phosphopeptides were enriched with TiO2 resin and used for subsequent quantification. Peptides were analyzed by nanoflow liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry.

Multi-Omics Analysis of Murine Kidney RNA-Seq, WC Proteome, and Phosphoproteome

To evaluate molecular changes quantified by RNA-seq and proteomics analyses, differential expression analysis was performed across three time points (day 1, day 3, and day 6) in the model mouse kidney. To characterize the biologic functions highlighted by these differentially expressed molecules, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using gene ontology (GO) Biological Process annotations. Furthermore, for phosphoproteome data, kinase set enrichment analysis was used to identify upstream kinases that may be responsible for the observed phosphorylation changes, using kinase-substrate relationship data from PhosphoSitePlus.18 Differentially expressed genes, proteins, and peptides were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05.

GWAS Summary Analysis

We analyzed publicly available GWAS summary data on urolithiasis to quantify human genetic risk at the molecular and cell type levels. The GWAS summary used in this study was a meta-analysis of urolithiasis GWAS results from a Japanese cohort (BBJ) and European cohorts (UK Biobank and FinnGen) and corresponds to the data used in the PheWAS study by Sakaue et al.11 To identify genes associated with urolithiasis, we performed gene enrichment analysis using MAGMA.17 Genes were considered significant at an FDR <0.05 in MAGMA analyses. To assess the enrichment of genetic disease risk across organs identified by GWAS, we use FUnctional Mapping and Annotation of Genetic Association (FUMA)19 and partitioned linkage disequilibrium score regression (partitioned LDSC).20,21 To investigate which biologic functions are enriched in the human urolithiasis GWAS, we performed gene set enrichment analysis using MAGMA based on GO biological process annotations. To evaluate cell type enrichment, we employed both linkage disequilibrium score regression specifically expressed genes22 and single-cell disease relevance score (scDRS)23 using publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from mouse kidneys24 and human kidneys as references. The mouse dataset was obtained from the study conducted by Miao et al., and the human dataset was derived from the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP).

Integration of Human and Mouse Data

Human gene IDs were converted to mouse gene IDs using an ortholog reference to integrate human and mouse data. Ortholog reference data were obtained from Mouse Gene Informatics.25 The number of concurrently significant molecules or gene sets in both analyses was assessed using the Fisher exact test. The significance threshold for this assessment was set at an FDR <0.05. We define molecules that are significant (FDR <0.05) in both mouse and human data as cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules.

Cell Type Deconvolution Analysis

Cell type deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data from the hyperoxaluric mouse model was performed using CIBERSORTx26 to evaluate whether glyoxylate administration altered cellular composition and to assess the impact of these changes on transcriptomic profiles. We used a mouse single-cell RNA-seq dataset, the same one used in scDRS analysis, as the reference.

Renal Papillae Microarray Analysis

Gene expression analysis of renal papillae, the site of stone formation in patients with urolithiasis, was conducted using microarray data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (GSE73680).27 From this dataset, we extracted data from patients with urolithiasis only and performed differential expression analysis between renal papillae with Randall plaque and those without.

Colocalization Analysis

We conducted a colocalization analysis to assess whether effective variants shared between urolithiasis GWAS and eQTL summary statistics were present within the gene loci corresponding to cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. In this study, we refer to single-nucleotide polymorphisms as variants. The coloc.abf function in the R package coloc28 was used for analysis, computing the posterior probabilities of four hypotheses: H1 (no GWAS showing effects), H2 (effects present in one GWAS only), H3 (effects present in both GWAS without colocalization), and H4 (effects present in both GWAS with colocalization).28 An H4 posterior probability >75% was defined as indicative of colocalization.

MR Analysis

To investigate whether kidney eQTLs exert a causal effect on urolithiasis, we performed MR analysis on the variants of genes corresponding to cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. The analysis was conducted using the TwoSampleMR package29 in R. Variants with P ≤ 5.0×10−8 in the eQTL dataset were selected as instrumental variables.

Statistical Analysis

The Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for multiple testing correction, with a significance level set at FDR <0.05.

Additional details on methods are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Results

Three Modalities of Multi-Omics Analysis of the Kidney in a Hyperoxaluric Mouse Model

To comprehensively capture molecules functioning in the kidneys where calcium oxalate stones are formed, we conducted a multi-omics analysis using RNA-seq, WC proteome, and phosphoproteome on the kidneys of the hyperoxaluric mouse model. We confirmed the presence of calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys (Figure 1A). Blood and urine biochemical test results are also presented in Supplemental Table 1. These results confirmed that the hyperoxaluric mouse model was successfully established.

In the images of the corticomedullary junction and medulla, the tubular lumen of the renal tubules and loop of Henle was visible throughout most regions. Calcium oxalate crystals were found in the tubular lumen of the medullary and corticomedullary junction and medulla in the glyoxylate 6-day group (the day 6 group). (B) Scatter plot of variability and significance depicting the differentially expressed molecules in RNA-seq (left), WC proteome (middle), and phosphoproteome (right), with the x axis representing the SD and the y axis representing the negative logarithm of the P value. The plots highlight the differentially expressed molecules. (C) Left: the correlation coefficients for the expression profiles across each modality are illustrated. Right: Fisher exact test was conducted to assess the degree of overlap between differentially expressed molecules for each modality. FDR, false discovery rate; WC, whole-cell.

After quantifying RNA, proteins, and phosphorylated peptides extracted from the kidneys of this mouse model, we conducted differential expression analysis. The analyses of variance demonstrated that 1173 of 10,828, 342 of 6969, and 516 of 7217 molecules were significantly differentially expressed in the RNA-seq, WC proteome, and phosphoproteome, respectively (Figure 1B). The expression profiles measured for each modality are shown in Supplemental Figure 1A. We analyzed the quantitative connections between molecules quantified by these three methods. The proteomic quantification of proteins and phosphorylated peptides involves annotating them with the original genes before transcription (see Supplemental Methods: proteomics data analysis). Molecules quantified across three modalities are shown in Supplemental Figure 1B, and the normalized expression levels for each pair of modalities are presented as scatter plots in Supplemental Figure 1C. The Pearson correlation coefficients were 0.48 between RNA-seq and the WC proteome, 0.13 between RNA-seq and the phosphoproteome, and 0.03 between the WC proteome and the phosphoproteome (Figure 1C). In Supplemental Figure 1D, the correlation was also assessed using linear regression. We also evaluated the shared differentially expressed molecules between the two modalities using the Fisher exact test. The odds ratios (ORs) were 4.4 for RNA-seq versus WC proteome, 2.3 for RNA-seq versus phosphoproteome, and 2.4 for WC proteome versus phosphoproteome, with corresponding P = 9.7×10−25, 6.9×10−11, and 7.0×10−9, respectively (Figure 1C). The observed trend of a higher correlation between RNA-seq and the WC proteome, alongside a lower correlation between the phosphoproteome and other modalities, as seen in our analysis of mouse kidneys, is consistent with previous reports. The low correlation between the phosphoproteome and the other modalities suggests that the phosphoproteome captures distinct biologic processes.

Subsequently, a gene set enrichment analysis using GO biological process was conducted to ascertain the functional roles of the molecules identified through differential expression analysis.30 The RNA-seq enrichment was mainly associated with inflammatory and injury-related processes along with metabolic processes in monocarboxylic acid and reactive oxygen species. In contrast, WC proteome enrichment predominantly reflected cellular energy and lipid metabolism among organic/oxoacid, fatty acid oxidation, lipid catabolism, and purine- and ATP-related pathways (Supplemental Figure 2, A and B). To further characterize the phosphopeptides identified in the phosphoproteome, we performed kinase set enrichment analysis to determine which downstream phosphorylation targets were altered (Supplemental Figure 2C). This analysis revealed enhanced phosphorylation downstream of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR; OR=3.8, P = 0.0047). Previous studies have shown that mTOR can promote nephrocalcinosis by inhibiting autophagy in renal tubules.31 However, mTOR does not emerge as a significant locus in the subsequent GWAS analyses.

Gene-, Pathway-, and Organ-Level Analysis of Urolithiasis GWAS Summary Statistics

We analyzed publicly available GWAS summary statistics to elucidate the genetic background of urolithiasis. GWAS summary statistics for urolithiasis were obtained through a meta-analysis of Japanese GWAS (BBJ) and European GWAS (UK BioBank and FinnGen).32–34 In the BBJ GWAS and European GWAS, there were 20 and ten loci, respectively (Supplemental Figure 3A). Owing to the increased statistical power of cross-population meta-analysis, 33 loci were identified (Figure 2A).

In the gene enrichment analysis, MAGMA17 evaluated P values for 40,649 genes, of which 343 were identified as significantly associated genes. Regarding gene set–level associations, we performed MAGMA-based gene set enrichment analysis using GO biological process annotations to identify biologic functions enriched in the urolithiasis GWAS (Supplemental Figure 3B). This analysis identified 97 GO terms that were significantly associated with urolithiasis after multiple testing correction. Examination of the top-ranked terms revealed prominent enrichment for immune-related processes as well as for signaling pathways such as phospholipase C/protein kinase C signaling. In addition, renal urate salt excretion and renal system processes were also enriched, suggesting that both immune regulation and kidney-specific biologic functions are genetically associated with urolithiasis.

To assess which organs showed enrichment of genetic risk for urolithiasis, we performed FUMA19 and partitioned LDSC20 analyses. Using the GENE2FUNC function in FUMA, we evaluated the top 50 genes identified by gene enrichment analysis (Figure 2B).1 The results showed that many kidney-specific genes were among the enriched genes. In the partitioned LDSC results, the genetic risk was also enriched in kidney-associated regions (Figure 2C). These results suggest that urolithiasis is a disease with a strong genetic connection to the kidneys.

Cross-Species Identification of Urolithiasis-Associated Molecules and Biologic Processes

Building on the mouse multi-omics analyses and the human GWAS results presented above, we next performed a cross-species integrative analysis to identify shared urolithiasis-associated molecules and biologic processes at both the gene and gene set levels. We conducted an analysis to identify biologic similarities between the kidney multi-omics results from mouse models and the GWAS results for urolithiasis. A total of 46 molecules, including 31 genes identified through RNA-seq, two proteins from the WC proteome, and 21 peptides from the phosphoproteome, were found to be significantly differentially expressed in mice and were identified as human significant genes from gene enrichment analysis with MAGMA (Table 1 and Supplemental Table 2). We define these molecules as cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. To confirm that the identification of these 46 molecules was not incidental, we demonstrated that genes with low P values in both human and mouse data were significantly enriched. Specifically, enrichment was evaluated by comparing the observed overlap with that expected under a null model in which genes were randomly distributed across datasets using the Fisher exact test. A significant overlap between the molecules identified by human MAGMA analysis and those differentially expressed in mouse analysis was observed in the RNA-seq (OR=1.9, P = 2.5×10−3) and phosphoproteome (OR=1.8, P = 0.04) datasets (Figure 3A). WC proteome analysis showed no significant overlap with human data (OR=0.27, P = 0.04).

Building on the molecule-level overlap, we next evaluated cross-species concordance at the functional level using gene set enrichment analysis. Comparison of enriched GO biological process terms revealed four terms that were significantly enriched in both mouse and human analyses: regulation of peptide secretion, amide transport, ossification, and tissue morphogenesis (Supplemental Table 3). To quantify the overlap of enriched GO biological process terms between analyses, we applied the Fisher exact test (Figure 3B). This analysis demonstrated highly significant overlap between RNA-seq data from the mouse kidney and human GWAS results (OR=3.7, P = 3.2×10−25), as well as between the WC proteome and human GWAS results (OR=2.5, P = 3.5×10−4). In addition, a strong concordance was observed between mouse RNA-seq and WC proteome enrichment results (OR=23.6, P = 3.4×10−36). Following this, we visualized the assignment of the 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules to enriched GO biological process terms using a heatmap (Figure 3C). Specifically, the heatmap displays GO terms ranked among the top 15 by P value in the mouse kidney RNA-seq and human MAGMA analyses, as well as the four GO terms that were significantly enriched in both species. This visualization illustrates where the cross-species–prioritized molecules are positioned within these biologically relevant processes. Overall, 34 of the 46 molecules were assigned to at least one of the displayed GO terms. No GO terms reached statistical significance in the phosphoproteome.

Cell Type Enrichment Analysis Highlights Important Cell Populations in Urolithiasis

To identify kidney cell types potentially associated with urolithiasis, we performed cell type enrichment analyses using publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from mouse kidneys24 as a reference. Based on this dataset, cells were classified into proximal tubular cells, distal tubular cells, collecting duct intercalated cells (ICs), collecting duct principal cell (PC), stromal cells, immune cells, and others (Figure 4A). We calculated pseudo-bulk expression profiles and tau scores from the single-cell RNA-seq data to define cell type–specific genes, with genes showing tau ≥0.8 considered cell type–specific.35,36 These cell type–specific gene sets were then used to assess enrichment in the hyperoxaluric mouse RNA-seq data and the human urolithiasis GWAS results.

The Fisher exact test was used to evaluate which cell type–specific genes were more abundant in the differentially expressed genes of the mouse RNA-seq data. Genes expressed in specific cell types other than collecting duct ICs were enriched among the differentially expressed genes in the mouse RNA-seq data (Figure 4B). The P values for proximal tubular cells, immune cells, stroma cells, distal tubule cells, and collecting duct PCs were 8.0×10−61, 2.0×10−32, 9.5×10−17, 5.3×10−9, and 4.6×10−7, respectively. Next, to assess changes in cell composition following glyoxylate administration, we performed cell type deconvolution analysis on the mouse bulk RNA-seq data using CIBERSORTx with the mouse single-cell RNA-seq dataset as a reference. This analysis revealed that, as the duration of glyoxylate administration increased, the proportion of proximal tubular cells decreased, while the proportions of other cell types tended to increase (Figure 4C).

According to calculations using scDRS,23 the P values for collecting duct PCs, immune system cells, stroma cells, distal tubular cells, collecting duct ICs, and proximal tubular cells were 3.0×10−3, 4.0×10−3, 0.071, 0.40, 0.48, and 0.58, respectively (Figure 4D). Even after multiple testing corrections, collecting duct PCs and immune system cells remained significantly enriched. As this study aimed to integrate mouse experimental data with human GWAS results using the same single-cell reference, we employed the mouse single-cell dataset described above. To complement these analyses and directly link GWAS findings with human kidney biology, we further incorporated human single-cell datasets. We also attempted cell type enrichment analyses of human GWAS data using the KPMP human kidney single-cell RNA-seq dataset (Supplemental Figure 4, A and B) with linkage disequilibrium score regression specifically expressed gene and scDRS; however, neither approach revealed significant enrichment in any specific cell type (Supplemental Figure 4, C and D). These results indicate that the essential cell types for urolithiasis in both mice and humans are immune cells and collecting duct PCs, which accounted for six of 31 genes identified through cross-species analysis (Table 1).

Follow-Up Analysis of Cross-Species Urolithiasis-Related Molecules Using Human Renal Papilla Gene Expression and Kidney eQTL Data

As shown in Table 1, we identified 46 molecules potentially related to urolithiasis. To assess their relevance to human disease, we performed follow-up analysis using gene expression data from human renal papillae and colocalization and MR analysis using kidney eQTL data.

The renal papilla is located near the openings of the collecting ducts into the urinary tract, where a kidney stone initially forms.37 To assess whether the 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules identified in our integrative analysis are also transcriptionally altered in the renal papilla, we analyzed a microarray-based transcriptome dataset27 comparing renal papillae with and without stones from patients with nephrolithiasis. Stones formed at this site are known as Randall's plaques. In the differential expression analysis, eight out of the 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules exhibited P < 0.05, and considering multiple testing, SHROOM3 (log-fold change=0.43, P = 4.0×10−4) and CRYAB (log-fold change=1.3, P = 1.0×10−3) exhibited FDR values <0.05 (Figure 5A). CRYAB and SHROOM3 showed consistent upregulation in mouse and human gene expression data. We conducted a permutation test using random shuffling of gene labels to demonstrate that these results were not obtained by chance (Supplemental Figure 5; see Supplemental Methods: permutation analysis). The empirical P value for observing two genes from collecting duct PCs or proximal tubular cells with FDR <0.05 was 0.0066, all of which were significant.

We performed colocalization analysis to examine whether urolithiasis GWAS signals and kidney eQTL signals overlapped within the chromosomal regions of the 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. We used the publicly available kidney eQTL dataset from NephQTL2.38 This dataset includes eQTL data from both the tubular and glomerular regions. Notably, GLUD1, UMOD, SLC34A1, TCEA3, and H1-0 demonstrated H4 posterior probabilities from tubular eQTL data exceeding 0.75, with values of 0.99, 0.98, 0.91, 0.90, and 0.87, respectively (Figure 5B). For glomerular eQTL, only H1-0 showed a significant H4 posterior probability (0.87). In this analysis, it was expected that molecules derived from proximal and distal tubules in the cell type enrichment analysis would colocalize with tubular eQTL data. Because UMOD is derived from distal tubules and SLC34A1 and TCEA3 are derived from proximal tubules, these molecules can be considered anatomically validated.

Furthermore, we investigated whether kidney eQTLs within the chromosomal regions of the 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules were associated with urolithiasis in the human GWAS summary statistics. Consistent with the colocalization findings, MR analysis indicated that genetically predicted expression of the genes in the tubular region; UMOD (log OR: −0.50, P = 2.6×10−22), GLUD1 (log OR: −0.22, P = 1.6×10−10), TCEA3 (log OR: −0.091, P = 5.2×10−5), and H1-0 (log OR: −0.086, P = 9.54×10−6) as well as H1-0 (log OR: −0.061, P = 9.1×10−6) in the glomerular region (Figure 5C).

Together, these analyses demonstrate that several cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules, including CRYAB, SHROOM3, UMOD, GLUD1, SLC34A1, TCEA3, and H1-0, are consistently supported by human renal papilla gene expression, kidney eQTL colocalization, and MR analyses.

Discussion

Although expression-based molecular data from a hyperoxaluric mouse model or genetic association data from GWAS alone are insufficient to reveal the pathophysiology of urolithiasis, we aimed to explore converging evidence from both sources to propose candidate molecules potentially involved in disease mechanisms. Multi-omics analysis of mouse kidneys revealed numerous differentially expressed molecules. Gene set enrichment analysis using GO biological process revealed distinct biologic themes underlying nephrocalcinosis in hyperoxaluric mice. GO biological process enrichment highlighted inflammatory and injury-related responses, along with extensive metabolic reprogramming, including alterations in small-molecule, lipid, and energy metabolism. These findings are consistent with the notion that calcium oxalate deposition induces local tissue injury and inflammation, accompanied by adaptive metabolic changes in renal tubular cells.13,39

Gene enrichment analysis of human urolithiasis GWAS identified 343 significant genes, providing a gene-level framework for disease susceptibility. Subsequent MAGMA-based gene set enrichment analysis further revealed that these genes are clustered within specific GO Biological Process categories. Interestingly, the appearance of immune-related pathways such as T-cell lineage commitment and neutrophil-mediated responses in the gene set enrichment results of a GWAS—a study design that is inherently independent of acute acquired factors—highlights a potential genetic predisposition of immune regulation in urolithiasis susceptibility. Moreover, this finding is consistent with previous reports linking urolithiasis to immune-related mechanisms.40

Cross-species analyses have been frequently employed in previous studies and have sometimes yielded meaningful results.41 In this study, we identified 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules that showed differential expression in a hyperoxaluric mouse model and were supported by GWAS data. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed four GO biological process terms that were significantly enriched in both human GWAS and the mouse model: regulation of peptide secretion, amide transport, ossification, and tissue morphogenesis. Notably, the shared enrichment of ossification—a process directly related to calcium mineralization—is particularly intriguing in the context of urolithiasis, a disease characterized by pathologic crystal formation. These shared gene set terms also provide functional context for interpreting the cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules. For example, CASR and SLC34A1, both assigned to the ossification category, are well known to play key roles in calcium homeostasis: pathogenic variants in CASR cause Bartter syndrome42–44 with nephrocalcinosis, whereas dysfunction of SLC34A1 has been implicated in infantile hypercalcemia.45 Together, these observations suggest that cross-species–enriched biologic processes help us to interpret cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules.

Cell type enrichment analyses using single-cell RNA-seq data of the mouse kidney suggested that immune cells and collecting duct PCs may be involved in urolithiasis, based on enrichment signals observed in both mouse and human datasets. Several studies have suggested the involvement of immune cells, including macrophages, in urolithiasis.46–48 However, when we performed cell type enrichment analyses using human kidney single-cell RNA-seq data as a reference, no significant enrichment was observed.

Possible explanations for this discrepancy include differences in cell type composition between the reference datasets. The mouse data were derived from the whole kidney, whereas the human samples were obtained by cold-cup biopsy. Proximal tubule-derived transcripts typically dominate bulk kidney RNA49; however, the biopsy samples may deviate from this composition because of the anatomic specification. Therefore, to enable consistent comparisons across species, we primarily relied on mouse single-cell data for cell type enrichment analyses.

In a follow-up analysis using gene expression data from renal papillae samples of urolithiasis patients, SHROOM3 and CRYAB were found to be significantly upregulated in regions with Randall plaque compared with adjacent stone-free regions. Because Randall plaques typically form near the openings of the collecting ducts on the papillary surface, cells derived from the collecting ducts are anatomically positioned to influence plaque formation. Because collecting duct PCs were highlighted in both human and mouse data by the cell type enrichment analysis, molecules derived from these cells are likely to be differentially expressed around Randall plaques. SHROOM3 and CRYAB are predominantly expressed in collecting duct PCs. SHROOM3 is a gene implicated in CKD and is known to be involved in cellular morphology.50 It is conceivable that alterations in cell adhesion mediated by SHROOM3 could affect molecular reabsorption, potentially linking it to urolithiasis.

Both colocalization and MR analyses consistently identified UMOD, GLUD1, TCEA3, and H1-0 as significantly associated with urolithiasis, supporting their strong involvement in disease pathogenesis. UMOD encodes a glycoprotein secreted from the thick ascending limb of Henle loop into the tubular lumen and has been implicated not only in urolithiasis but also in renal function and vascular calcification suppression.51 In our analysis, UMOD was classified as distal tubule–specific, consistent with our categorization of the thick ascending limb as a distal nephron compartment (see Supplemental Methods: processing of single-cell RNA-seq reference datasets). The role of GLUD1 in the kidney with respect to urolithiasis remains largely unclear. However, GLUD1 was assigned to two of the four GO biological process terms that were commonly enriched across human and mouse results—regulation of peptide secretion and amide transport—suggesting indirect involvement in kidney stone formation. Notably, GLUD1 encodes glutamate dehydrogenase, a central enzyme in glutamate metabolism linking amino acid turnover to cellular energy and nitrogen balance. In renal clear cell carcinoma, GLUD1 downregulation has been shown to activate the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, suggesting that altered GLUD1 activity in renal cells may modulate intracellular signaling and metabolic adaptation under conditions of oxalate-induced stress.52 This interpretation is further supported by our phosphoproteome-based kinase set enrichment analysis, which highlighted mTOR. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that higher urinary L-glutamate levels are associated with lower calcium oxalate crystal concentrations, suggesting a potential protective role for glutamate in the urinary microenvironment.53 Taken together, these observations suggest that GLUD1 may indirectly influence susceptibility to urolithiasis by modulating amino acid metabolism and peptide handling.

This study has limitations. First, the hyperoxaluric mouse model has not been shown to accurately reflect the pathogenesis of human urolithiasis. This model may more closely represent pathologic conditions associated with severe hyperoxaluria, such as primary hyperoxaluria, rather than idiopathic calcium oxalate stone disease. Differentially expressed molecules identified by the mouse kidney multi-omics analysis may partly reflect changes in cell composition in response to glyoxylate, as indicated by cell type deconvolution. Second, the use of a European linkage disequilibrium reference panel in cross-population meta-GWAS analyses may have affected the interpretation of association signals in Japanese cohorts. Third, cross-species comparisons are typically restricted to molecules that have orthologs, potentially excluding species-specific or rapidly evolving loci. Fourth, the gene ID conversion from mouse to human for multi-omics integration may have introduced mapping inaccuracies or excluded nonconserved genes. Details regarding the ortholog mapping procedure, as well as the types of genes that could not be aligned between humans and mice, are provided in Supplemental Methods: Integration of human and mouse data and in Supplemental Data 1. Fifth, cell type enrichment analysis was based on single-cell RNA-seq data from healthy mouse kidneys, which may not reflect the cellular state in the hyperoxaluric model or align directly with human kidney cell types. Finally, differential expression and colocalization analyses cannot establish causal relationships. The results of MR analyses should be interpreted cautiously and do not constitute definitive proof of causality.

In conclusion, this cross-species analysis integrating human urolithiasis GWAS and multi-omics profiling of a hyperoxaluric mouse model prioritized 46 cross-species urolithiasis-related molecules with potential relevance to urolithiasis. These molecules, supported by genetic association signals and expression-based evidence, may contribute to a better understanding of disease mechanisms and serve as a resource for future biomarker or therapeutic target discovery.
