Development and validation of a prediction model for postoperative delirium in elderly spinal patients: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: XiuQin Lu, Yongcheng Liu, Shusen Jiang, Yongwen Lai, Jiaxing Huang, Chunying Zhu, ChaoSheng Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Previous studies have predominantly focused on risk prediction models for delirium in patients undergoing cardiac or orthopedic joint surgeries, with limited reports on prediction models for postoperative delirium (POD) following spinal surgeries. This study first investigates independent risk factors for POD in elderly spinal surgery patients. Based on these factors, it subsequently constructs a nomogram prediction model, the accuracy and applicability of which are rigorously validated. MethodsThe data of 846 patients who underwent surgical treatment in the Department of Spinal Surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University from January 2019 to September 2023 were retrospectively collected. The cases were randomly divided into a training set (592 cases) and a validation set (254 cases) using a 7:3 randomization method, where the training set was used for model development and the validation set for model evaluation. Binary Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent risk factors for POD and to construct a predictive model and a score-risk prediction value reference table. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Internal validation was conducted using the validation set.ResultsThe results of this study found that 144 patients developed POD, with an incidence rate of 17.0%. Multivariate regression analysis revealed that patient age, history of smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, preoperative psychiatric disorders, anesthesia method, and postoperative hemoglobin levels were independent risk factors for POD in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery. Based on these factors, a nomogram model and a scoring-risk prediction table were developed. The total score of the model ranges from 0 to 350, corresponding to predicted risks ranging from less than 1% to over 90%. The model’s performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for the training cohort was 0.81 (95% CI = 0.76–0.86), and for the validation cohort, it was 0.86 (95% CI = 0.80–0.93). Additionally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test results showed good calibration, with p-values of 0.685 for the training cohort and 0.706 for the validation cohort, indicating favorable model consistency. Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the net benefit within the intervention probability threshold range was 8% to 68% for the training set and 7% to 73% for the validation set, suggesting that the model has high practical value in clinical decision-making.ConclusionsThe patient’s age, smoking history, alcohol use history, diabetes history, preoperative psychiatric disorders history, anesthesia method, and postoperative hemoglobin levels are identified as independent risk factors for the occurrence of postoperative delirium in patients undergoing such surgeries. Simultaneously, the nomogram prediction model established based on these factors demonstrates good risk prediction accuracy for postoperative delirium and excellent clinical applicability.Keywords: Spinal surgery, Elderly patients, Postoperative delirium, Predictive modeling, Columnar graphs

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common complication in elderly patients undergoing surgery. It is mainly characterized by acute cognitive impairment after surgery, usually accompanied by disturbances in the patient’s orientation, attention, memory, and motor ability, as well as disruptions in the sleep-wake cycle, thinking, perception, and activity level [1, 2]. Regarding spinal surgery, different literatures report that the incidence of postoperative delirium ranges from 0.49% to 21% [3]. POD not only increases the difficulty of nursing care, prolongs the length of hospital stay and increases hospitalization costs, but may even develop into long-term cognitive impairment and the risk of dementia, bringing a huge burden to families and society [4].

With the continuous increase in the proportion of the aging population, the volume of spinal surgeries in elderly patients has shown a significant upward trend [3]. Early identification and effective prevention of POD are critical for improving patient outcomes. However, current research on POD risk in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery remains limited. This study analyzes and screens relevant risk factors for POD in spinal surgery patients and establishes a predictive model to provide clinical reference.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study retrospectively analyzed the data of 846 patients who underwent surgical treatment at the Department of Spine Surgery, the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University, from January 2019 to September 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (Ethical Review Opinion No.: 2023YJSLL-150).Inclusion criteria: ① Age ≥ 65 years;② Primary diagnosis of a spine-related disease;③ Underwent cervical, thoracic, or lumbar spine surgery.Exclusion criteria:① Patients who underwent surgery under local anesthesia;② Patients with multiple injuries or those who underwent concomitant surgeries at other sites;③ Patients whose surgeries were canceled due to various reasons;④ Patients who were lost to follow-up or had incomplete medical records.

Based on the data extracted from the patient’s hospital medical record inquiry system, the content includes three aspects.① General preoperative conditions: Age, gender, BMI index, history of alcohol consumption, history of smoking, history of preoperative cognitive impairment (Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, traumatic brain injury, etc.), mental disorders (anxiety, depression, schizophrenia, etc.), ASA classification, past disease history (diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease(COPD), cerebral hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, etc.), past medication history (antihypertensive drugs, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular drugs, hypoglycemic and lipid-lowering drugs, cortisol drugs, etc.), preoperative disease duration, as well as preoperative mental status and walking ability.② Preoperative laboratory examination indicators: Biochemical indicators such as platelet count, red blood cell count, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, albumin, serum creatinine, and C-reactive protein.③ Perioperative related indicators: Time from admission to surgery, surgical site, surgical grade, duration of surgery, duration of anesthesia, anesthesia method (endotracheal general anesthesia, neuraxial anesthesia), anesthesia maintenance (total intravenous anesthesia, intravenous-inhalation combined anesthesia), intraoperative blood loss, whether blood products were transfused, indwelling urinary catheter, postoperative hemoglobin level, and postoperative recovery (transfer to post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), transfer to intensive care unit (ICU) for monitoring).The original variables include various data types, requiring counting and classification of the variables, as well as analysis of the variables to detect missing values and outliers. In our study, there were no missing values for qualitative data, so no processing was needed; for quantitative data, missing values were imputed using the median [5].

The diagnostic criteria for delirium are based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), published by the American Psychiatric Association in 2013 [6]. Based on the query of progress notes during the patient’s hospitalization through the electronic medical record system, the exact evidence of postoperative delirium in patients is identified.The specific determination methods include: ① There are clear in-hospital progress notes describing the acute abnormal cognitive function in patients after surgery, and the symptoms meet the diagnostic criteria for delirium in the aforementioned DSM-5; ② There are consultation records from the department of neurology, neurosurgery, or psychiatry/psychology after surgery, with explicit consultation opinions considering the diagnosis of postoperative delirium; ③ For cases where the diagnosis of postoperative delirium cannot be confirmed, the determination shall be made after discussion by neurologists with an intermediate professional title or above and at least 3 years of practice experience.Patients included in the study were divided into two groups based on the occurrence of POD: the POD group (positive group)and the non-POD group (negative group). According to the definition of this study, a history of preoperative cognitive impairment includes Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, traumatic brain injury, and other related conditions. Mental disorders include anxiety, depression, schizophrenia, and other such illnesses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software and R software. Measurement data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the t-test. Measurement data with a skewed distribution were presented as median and interquartile range, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test. First, SPSS 26.0 software was used to set a fixed random seed (1234). Second, a random grouping variable was generated, specifically by creating a Bernoulli-distributed random number sequence to achieve a 7:3 division ratio. Finally, the training set and validation set were selected based on the grouping variable. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate Logistic regression analysis of the training set were included in the multivariate Logistic regression analysis to screen for independent risk factors for postoperative delirium (POD). The rms package in R was used to establish a POD nomogram model and a score-risk prediction table. Model validation was conducted by plotting a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate its predictive value for POD. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the consistency of the model. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to evaluate the application value of the model in actual clinical decision-making. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Validation and clinical applicability of the POD prediction model

In this study, the ROC curve was used to evaluate the performance of the model. The AUC value in the training set was 0.81 (95%CI = 0.76–0.86), and the AUC value in the validation set was 0.86 (95%CI = 0.80–0.93). The model showed good discriminative ability in both groups (Fig. 3 A, B). Additionally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to objectively evaluate the calibration of the curve. The calibration curves of the training set (P = 0.685) and the validation set (P = 0.706) both indicated good calibration of the model (Fig. 3 C, D). Finally, the threshold probability interval of the net benefit was calculated and the POD decision curve was plotted to explore the clinical application range of the nomogram. The results showed that the threshold probability interval of the maximum net benefit for predicting POD using the nomogram developed in this study was 8% to 68% in the training set and 7% to 73% in the validation set (Fig.4A, B).

Discussion

In our study, a total of 846 patients were included, among whom 144 developed POD, with an incidence rate of 17.0%. This incidence rate is slightly higher compared with previous studies [2, 7], which may be related to the fact that we adopted several assessment methods for defining delirium in this study, resulting in a relatively wider inclusion of positive patients. Meanwhile, some surgeries for spinal degenerative diseases are mainly performed on elderly patients, especially very elderly ones, so the average age of the elderly patients included in our study is relatively older.This also suggests that in clinical practice, we should attach sufficient importance to such elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery, and actively take targeted measures to effectively prevent and reduce the occurrence of postoperative delirium.

The results of this study show that patient age, smoking history, drinking history, history of diabetes, preoperative history of psychiatric diseases, anesthesia method, and postoperative hemoglobin level are independent risk factors for POD in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery.

Age has been confirmed as a risk factor for POD by most researchers [8, 9], and this study also shows that age ≥ 65 years is an independent risk factor for POD in patients undergoing spinal surgery. At present, the exact mechanism underlying the relationship between advanced age and POD remains unclear. Elderly patients often have preoperative frailty [10], with decreased activity of the central nervous system and reduced ability of self-regulation of cerebral blood flow. When cerebral hypoperfusion occurs during surgery, it is easy to cause damage to brain function, resulting in impairment of the blood-brain barrier, imbalance of neurotransmitters, and aggravation of central inflammatory response in patients. These changes further affect the structure and functional state of the central nervous system, leading to postoperative cognitive dysfunction in patients.

A study by Chen et al showed that the smoking rate among POD patients was 54%, and the risk of POD in smoking patients was 4.018 times higher than that in non-smokers [11]. Similarly, the results of this study are comparable: in the training set, the smoking rate among POD patients was 41.0%, and the risk of POD in smoking patients was 5.65 times higher than that in non-smokers, with a statistically significant difference. Studies have indicated that cognitive impairment usually does not occur under smoking conditions, but rather more during exposure to nicotine. Smoking can cause desensitization of acetylcholine receptors [12] leading to their functional imbalance and inducing POD.On the other hand, elderly spinal patients are often complicated with chronic pain. Long-term chronic pain can cause patients to develop anxiety/depression symptoms, and in such a state, smokers are more likely to be dependent on nicotine [12] making smoking more common among patients with chronic pain diseases, which seems to form a positive cycle effect.

In the 2020 Dementia Prevention Guidelines published in The Lancet[13], excessive alcohol consumption has been identified as a risk factor for cognitive decline. Similarly, in this study, it was shown that patients' history of alcohol consumption is an independent risk factor for POD in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery, and the risk of POD in patients with a drinking history is 3.14 times higher than that in non-drinkers, with a statistically significant difference. Memory loss, personality changes, and subcortical brain damage induced by alcohol consumption may be related to the occurrence of POD [14].A study by Flórez et al found that the association between excessive alcohol consumption and cognitive impairment may be mediated by glutamatergic excitotoxicity (induced by upregulation of N-methyl-D-receptors), leading to a series of neurotoxic effects[15]. However, this study only included information on whether patients had a past or current history of alcohol consumption, without recording the frequency and specific dosage of alcohol intake, and did not further explore the relationship between alcohol intake and POD. Therefore, in future studies, the connection and mechanism between the two can be investigated in depth.

In this study, a history of diabetes in patients is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of POD after spinal surgery. As a chronic metabolic disease, studies have shown that the risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in diabetic patients is 49%, and at the same time, the risk of MCI progressing to dementia is also significantly increased [16]. The results of this study also show that the incidence of POD in elderly diabetic patients after spinal surgery is significantly increased. Diabetic cognitive impairment (DCI) may be related to nerve damage, abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, blood-brain barrier (BBB), calcium homeostasis imbalance, intestinal flora imbalance [17, 18].

With the acceleration of the pace of life and the increase in social pressure, the prevalence of psychiatric diseases such as anxiety and depression is on the rise [19, 20],. Anxiety and depression often coexist as comorbidities, which not only affect patients' mental health but also are risk factors for patients to develop POD [21]. This is consistent with the report by Elsamadicy et al that depression is an independent risk factor for POD in elderly patients undergoing spinal decompression and fusion, and it is speculated that this is closely related to the abnormalities in neurotransmitters and metabolism in the brain caused by a history of psychiatric diseases [22].The results of this study also suggest that a history of anxiety, depression, or other psychiatric disorders in elderly patients is one of the independent risk factors for POD after spinal surgery, which is consistent with the findings of previous related studies [23]. The specific mechanism by which such psychiatric disorders increase the risk of POD is not yet fully clear. Studies have shown that the monoamine neurotransmitter dysfunction in patients with depression is not an isolated event, but rather the result of a multi-system interactive imbalance (glial activation - epigenetics - neuroplasticity), which not only affects emotional regulation but may also be involved in the regulation of cognitive function. Clinically, sufficient attention should be paid to elderly patients with a history of such psychiatric disorders. We should not only carefully distinguish between these psychiatric diseases and POD but also actively take targeted measures to effectively prevent and treat the occurrence of postoperative delirium.

Previous studies have shown that the impact of anesthesia methods on POD outcomes remains controversial, with inconsistent results across different studies. Some studies suggest that the incidence of POD is higher with general anesthesia compared to regional block [24]. However, other studies have indicated that regional anesthesia and general anesthesia are similarly effective in preventing POD. For example, in the latest evidence-based and consensus guidelines on POD in adult patients issued by the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine [25], seven studies met the inclusion criteria (6 randomized controlled trials and 1 observational study), among which 6 out of the 7 studies showed no difference in the incidence of POD between regional anesthesia and general anesthesia.However, the results of this study suggest that the incidence of POD in elderly patients who underwent intubated general anesthesia was 6.68 times higher than that in elderly patients who received non-intubated general anesthesia, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups. We analyze the main reasons as follows: In spinal surgery, non-intubated general anesthesia is mostly used in some simple minimally invasive surgeries. Compared with open surgeries, minimally invasive surgeries are less traumatic and allow patients to recover more quickly. Moreover, in such surgeries, patients usually need to stay awake to cooperate with the surgical process, so the dosage of general anesthetics or sedatives used during the operation is relatively small, resulting in less intervention on patients. Therefore, this reduces the impact of surgical anesthesia on patients' POD to a certain extent. As for whether the anesthesia method has a direct impact on patients' POD, more multi-center, large-sample clinical studies are needed in the future to clarify this.

A retrospective cohort analysis conducted by Gold et al over a 3-year period also showed that lower postoperative hemoglobin levels were significantly associated with POD [7]. The results of this study also confirm this finding. In this study, compared with the non-POD group, the POD group had lower postoperative hemoglobin levels, greater intraoperative blood loss, and a higher proportion of patients with preoperative anemia.There is a high correlation among these three factors during the perioperative period: postoperative hemoglobin level is directly causally related to preoperative anemia and intraoperative blood loss. Intraoperative blood loss increases the probability of perioperative hypotension, leading to insufficient cerebral perfusion. Postoperative low hemoglobin level further imbalances the supply and demand of cerebral oxygen, and elderly patients have poor tolerance to hypoxia, thus increasing the probability of inducing POD. Of course, some studies [26, 27] also suggest that perioperative blood transfusion itself may have an impact on postoperative delirium, and some non-standard blood transfusion treatments may interfere with the conclusion that hemoglobin level is related to POD. This is because patients with more severe anemia are more likely to receive more packed red blood cells, and surgeons, anticipating a large amount of bleeding in patients, usually prepare autologous blood transfusion devices or stored autologous blood transfusion, which to a certain extent reduces intraoperative blood loss and the incidence of postoperative anemia.

The limitations of this study are as follows: firstly, this is a single-center, retrospective study. Although it has been conducted in strict accordance with the experimental design process, due to the limitation of sample size and the inevitable interference of confounding factors, it may have an impact on the research results.Secondly, this study is a retrospective analysis. During the data collection process, previous studies did not adopt a unified and standardized definition of delirium. Therefore, we could only use the most commonly used clinical diagnostic methods to define the occurrence of postoperative delirium. Although we invited experts from the department of neurology to participate in the analysis, there is still a possibility of overestimating or underestimating the incidence of postoperative delirium, which may introduce bias into the results. Hence, it is necessary to establish more different prediction models and conduct multi-center, prospective clinical studies to further verify and improve this research.

In summary, the nomogram prediction model established in this study has good accuracy in predicting the risk of POD and excellent clinical applicability, which can provide reference strategies for the clinical prevention and treatment of POD in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery.
