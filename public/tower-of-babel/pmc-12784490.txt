How can we make postnatal information resources more accessible to women experiencing challenges accessing healthcare? Report of a co-production project
Authors: J. MacLellan, C. Byrne, E. Bray, A. Caza, A. Martin, M. Knight, C. Pope
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is a serious and persistent gap in perinatal morbidity and mortality rates for women from minoritised groups and for those living in deprived areas in the UK. If symptoms can be recognised in time this enables them to be treated more effectively. There are numerous information resources of varying quality detailing symptoms of potential postnatal serious symptoms that inform women’s decisions to seek care. However, cross cultural understanding, linguistic constraints and multiple challenges that act in a mutually reinforcing way can impact women’s access to information in a format they can understand and act upon. Women continue to report unmet needs for information and support in the postnatal period, but there is no guidance for health care professionals on how to provide information to better care for women experiencing intersecting challenges in access to health care.MethodsThe BEAMS project partnered with charities who support women with lived experience of challenges accessing healthcare. With the additional support and insight of two peer researchers we used arts-based, participatory, trauma-informed methods to work with 60 women of lived experience through interactive workshops. We reviewed current postnatal information resources used in the NHS, and explored what worked well in transferring the health message to the reader and what did not. Together we co-wrote scripts (checked for clinical accuracy) to share our message and worked with a narrative artist to create example resources that illustrates accessible information design.ResultsTogether we co-created two checklists to guide the design and delivery of accessible information. We applied the information design checklist to the creation of posters and animations in multiple languages using postnatal serious symptoms as the topic. We also used the information delivery checklist to engage with multiple professionals, organisations and charities to disseminate and deliver the resources.ConclusionWorking in a collaborative way requires time, flexibility and openness from everyone involved. It may feel unstructured or challenging at times, and produce unexpected findings, but this equitable way of working is respectful, develops valuable partnerships and authentic outputs.

Background

In the UK, the leading causes of maternal death within a year of giving birth include cardiovascular disease, sepsis, postpartum haemorrhage, neurological conditions, thromboembolic disease, and suicide [1]. Many of these deaths are preventable if symptoms are recognised and acted upon in time. National data [1–3] show that women facing multiple intersecting challenges such as poverty, homelessness, refugee or migrant status, social service involvement, and English language needs, are disproportionately affected. This is because their challenges act in a mutually reinforcing way to amplify the adverse impacts on the woman’s experience [17].

The ‘three delays model’[4] highlights that adverse outcomes often result from delays in deciding to seek, access, or receive quality care. The first delay—deciding to seek care—is a complex action influenced by health literacy and the ability to navigate complex healthcare systems [5, 6]. Although resources describing postnatal serious symptoms exist, their accessibility and understandability are inconsistent. Current health information design guidelines emphasise clarity [7] and accessibility [8] but digital poverty, language barriers, and low health literacy limit women’s ability to act on health information [9, 10]. Yet, no specific evidence-based guidance exists to support healthcare professionals designing accessible health information for women facing such challenges [7, 9]. Addressing this gap is a national safety priority [12].

The Better Outcomes Postnatally for Mums (BEAMS) project was a UK National Institute of Health Research funded project (NIHR 204956) that emerged from engagement with women who experienced barriers to care during the project design. They reported that postnatal information was often inaccessible: “she just gave me a leaflet without checking I could read.” Consultations with charities and health professionals confirmed that current resources are rarely trauma-informed or appropriate for women facing social or linguistic disadvantage.

BEAMS co-produced evidence-based guidance on health information design and delivery for groups experiencing healthcare access challenges. Using postnatal serious symptoms as an exemplar, the project developed two practical checklists to guide the creation and dissemination of accessible, user-centred health information.

Methods

Study design

We used co-production, a values driven approach based on the principle that those experiencing a problem are best placed to design the solutions. It combines professionals’ expertise with the lived expertise of service users to develop sustainable solutions [13]. We used a Participatory Action Research (PAR) design [14, 15], that involved participants and researchers working together to understand a situation and take action to change it for the better. PAR uses different methodologies, and we followed the framework of the Institute of Health Improvements’ Always Events™ that has been successfully used in the UK NHS [16]. Always Events™ are defined as those aspects of the care experience that should always occur when service users or their family members interact with health care professionals or the health care delivery system. The fundamental premise of the Always Events™ methodology is co-production, with service users identifying the event that is key to improving their experience of care. It serves as a practical approach for co-designing and implementing reliable solutions. The research team comprised of a midwife researcher (JM), a co-production artist (CB), and two peer researchers from the communities we worked with (EB, AC). We all brought different expertise and approaches to the BEAMS project. The midwife researcher contributed clinical and academic insight, while the co-production artist and peer researchers offered grounded community perspectives. Aware of the different types of power within our team, we used ongoing reflexive practice, shared facilitation, and participant-led consensus decision-making to minimise imbalance. Our positionalities shaped how we interpreted discussions and guided the collaborative process. Throughout the project we remained committed to centring women’s voices and creating a safe, respectful space for collaboration.

Participants and sampling

We worked with two peer researchers who work in outreach roles at our partner charities (EB, AC), to support recruitment of 60 women with challenges accessing healthcare to ensure the outputs benefit women most at risk of serious illness in the maternity system [17]. We engaged with the Lived Experience Teams (LET) of six partner charities, who support women experiencing different challenges, to invite 40 women for the co-design workshops. To include the voices of young mothers and Black British mothers that had not been represented in the workshops, we engaged with a further two charities. The 60 participants were directly involved in data collection, analysis, co-production of findings, creation of the outputs and dissemination of results as advocated in the PAR approach and Always Events™ toolkit.

Recruitment

We used the Birth Companions charity definition of multiple disadvantage to categorise the range of experiences reflecting challenges accessing healthcare among our proposed participants [18]. This includes poverty, homelessness, experience of domestic violence, refugee or migrant status, social service involvement, English language needs, pregnancy below the age of 20, substance misuse, mental ill health, being involved in the criminal justice system or additional learning/support needs. During engagement with our charity partners and peer researchers at the beginning of the project, the ‘multiple intersecting disadvantage’ terminology was felt by the group to be stigmatising. This was changed to ‘experience of challenges accessing healthcare’ by group consensus and used on all documentation and communications regarding the project.

Participants were recruited through trusted channels of engagement (the partner charities) who shared the project participant information sheet and answered any questions prior to the workshop/consultation session in collaboration with the project team. The workshop facilitator read out the consent form, making it clear that people could leave at any time and could participate in any way and at any depth that was comfortable for them. Interpreters worked one to one with women who needed them, and supported women to achieve informed consent and participate in the workshop activities. Verbal consent was recorded from each participant in their preferred language.

Data collection

We used interactive, multi-layered, arts-based methods to develop an innovative inclusive, social, and creative participatory space between women with lived experience, a narrative artist (CB) and the project lead researcher (JM) [20]. The use of arts-based approaches acknowledges that life experiences are multi-sensory, multifaceted, and complex [21]. This approach is recommended to support engagement of participants with varied levels of literacy [17, 22, 23]. Our methods were flexible as we adapted to different ways of working and were guided by the non-linear, iterative process of design thinking [24].

Co-design workshops

We conducted four co-design workshops across England with 40 participants who had lived experience of challenges accessing healthcare to ask what works in the design and presentation of post-natal health information, using collage to share ideas in accessible ways. The workshops were designed and facilitated using trauma-informed principles to ensure the activities, communication, and decision-making processes prioritised emotional safety, choice, and empowerment for all participants [25]. At the beginning of each workshop the facilitator (JM) would agree working principles with the group that included respect for each other and their contribution, confidentiality, and that sharing of personal experience was not required. Participants were made aware of a designated safe space away from the workshop with an assigned person for support. Support was available from representatives of the charity at each engagement to provide immediate support and follow-up with participants after the event. Charities were paid for their recruitment and wrap around support. Participants were reimbursed for their time and travel expenses, with refreshments and childcare provided [19].

We showed participants current postnatal resources used in the NHS and asked what worked well in sharing the message and what did not work well. The women were encouraged to cut out sections from the resources and record their impressions. The project narrative artist shared a variety of pre-drawn representations of postnatal serious symptoms with speech bubbles, stickers, pens and a large piece of blank paper for women to use. A researcher supported each table and asked prompt questions. We worked with interpreters in each of the workshops (Arabic, Bengali, Tigrinya, Punjabi, and Somali languages). Some of the women described their experiences and what would have helped them, others shared ‘what good looks like’, with others marking edits and ideas against existing resources or pictures.

The second activity explored where and how the participants preferred to access health information. We worked through a ‘circle of trust’ exercise to identify trusted sources of information and trusted individuals within the woman’s network. We built in time after each session for the research team to debrief and discuss together about our trauma informed approach and support of cultural safety in the workshop. This helped us develop a shared understanding of these concepts and more inclusive ways of working. We made minor adjustments, including briefing the interpreters about the wrap around support available to participants so they could reinforce this information with the woman they were working with, and increasing the involvement of the peer researchers in the facilitation activities during the workshop [25].

Data analysis

We co-analysed the collages with the participants during each workshop through individual and group reflections, to allow the sharing of interpretations of their work. The facilitator took notes of these reflections and discussed these, alongside detailed review of all the collages, with the two peer researchers at the end of the four workshops. Together we iteratively constructed themes from the work, and summarised their characteristics taking a consensus approach to create the checklist of accessible design principles [26]. We applied these principles to stories shared by the women to create a draft script for a postnatal symptoms animation and summary poster. We refined the language, imagery and format of the resources with feedback from two further groups of women. They were invited based on experiences that we had not heard during the workshops – mothers who had their children before the age of 18, or who were care leavers themselves and/or who were from the Black British community. These draft animations were shared with our advisory panel, comprised of professionals from clinical practice, education, NHS England and participants with lived experience. The lived experience participants advocated for the imagery in the resources to be representative of real life when a more abstract approach was proposed by the professional members of the panel.

Additional clinical review of the resources was sought beyond the research team and advisory panel through the Consultant Midwives group at the Royal College of Midwives, a team of Health Visitors at the Institute of Health Visitors, Obstetricians and General Practitioners at two additional conference events. This resulted in some small changes to the signposting guidance to aid clarity and two of the images to ensure compliance with the UNICEF baby friendly feeding initiative guidance [27]. The final drafts of the animations and posters were then submitted for PIF Tick assessment, an independent quality assurance trust mark for health information [28]. The resources were translated into 11 languages to reflect the principal languages of our research site maternity populations (Arabic, Bengali, Kurdish Sorani, Mandarin, Pashto, Polish, Romanian, Somali, Tetum, Tigrinya, Urdu).

Analysis of the circles of trust diagrams enabled the construction of information delivery modalities. We synthesised the information from each workshop to create a list of the top five most trusted sources of information in each circle (most trusted, less well trusted to least trusted) and held a prioritising event with our peer researchers and a small group of six women who had participated in the workshops. We employed a consensus methodology to facilitate structured group agreement on source of information priorities, using iterative discussion and ranking to ensure alignment between participants and reframed them into recommendations for delivery of accessible information. Roll out of the resources was designed to be evaluated by the two peer researchers visiting the maternity units, community clinics and local charities to see if the resources had been disseminated according to the BEAMS guidance. They would act as ‘secret shoppers’, to look for the BEAMS resources and have informal conversations about their use/reception in practice.

Results

The participants

To ensure we were working with the women we said we would, we recorded participant ethnicity and factors associated with challenges accessing healthcare. We worked as a team to design two individual level activities that would record this information in a meaningful, inclusive and affirming way.

Creating the information design checklist

The co-design workshop analysis emphasised the importance of simple messaging, culturally appropriate imagery, and the need for advocacy and rights messaging within the resources. The participants shared that having access to information that they could understand helped them feel more in control and better able to advocate for themselves. They described how hearing about real experiences of other women that look and sound like them builds trust, and how talking and sharing is beneficial. Continuity was described as the key to trust with a reminder to health professionals to not assume that women are supported by family. We collated these principles into a checklist for resource design (Fig. 4).

Creating the information delivery checklist

The information delivery activity showed networks of trust among the women, identifying who they would approach for information about their health. The NHS information website was well trusted by those women who were able to access it. Those with English as an additional language or who had recently arrived in the UK reported relying on their family, friends and the charity that was supporting them. This highlights how cross-cultural understanding of health and health systems needs to be considered during healthcare professional consultations. Some women were well integrated into their community and mentioned midwives, GPs, nursery and school teachers as trusted sources of health information but family and friends remained in the inner circle.

Key points for information delivery are detailed in the checklist in Fig. 7. These points included involving friends and family in information sharing, encouraging maternity services to collaborate with charity partners to share key messages and providing resources to groups that support new mothers. It was important to share information in multiple formats as people absorb information in different ways. The women requested animations, audio files and physical resources summarising the main messages that they could take home and share with their families. Through this process, the group agreed on the component of information delivery that should consistently occur to ensure women are reached. We named this our ‘Always Event’™. The women emphasised that information should be shared and reinforced in a conversation with a health professional regardless of whether they had previously had a child. It would offer an opportunity for the woman to ask questions, and reinforce the importance of the messaging.

Testing of the delivery guide in practice

We followed the ADKAR (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) change model to support adoption of the resources into practice in our 4 partner NHS sites and explore their use of the BEAMS information delivery guide [29]. We launched our resources at a multi-disciplinary, interactive workshop with health care professionals and women with lived experience who had attended the original workshops. We creatively illustrated the value of the postnatal exemplars through a co-created drama performance to show stakeholders why change in information presentation and delivery is necessary and how it can make a difference (ADKAR steps Awareness, Desire, Knowledge). We had proposed to engage with the maternity safety champion of each of our NHS sites to roll out the exemplar according to our reaching women information delivery checklist. In discussion with our sites, we found the postnatal lead midwife to be the key person in the clinical team to approve and cascade the resources in collaboration with their local patient experience teams.

The NHS sites hosted the resources on their website, in their resource library for health care professionals and as a resource for women in their digital records. There was particular interest in the multi-lingual resources. In one site, they incorporated the postnatal and mental health animations into their postnatal discharge pack that women were asked to watch before they went home. In another site, the health visiting and midwifery team worked together to support women with complex needs. They shared the resources among their team to discuss with women postnatally. One site trialled the provision of flyers of the one-page summary resource in different languages in their maternity clinic foyer advice point. Another site gave out the one-page summary resources during multi-language antenatal classes and placed posters on their maternity information board. The resources have taken time to implement in practice, with each site using the resources in different ways. The local teams sent photographic evidence of the resource distribution back to the research team. Each NHS site wished to host the animations within their Trust websites and so the viewing metrics for the animations were only available for the three months from launch to transfer of the resources to the NHS sites. Close partnership with a charity supporting Somali speaking women resulted in over 1500 views of the Somali language animations, highlighting the value of community champions.

Two NHS trusts have taken the BEAMS method and guidance to create their own information resources. Engaging with their local communities to choose the topic of information, its design and to contribute to the voiceovers on the animations holds hidden value in the empowerment and health advocacy of the women in their communities.

Discussion

The BEAMS project was a co-produced, participatory project that aimed to co-create principles of accessible health information design and delivery to reach women most at risk of poor health and experiences in maternity care. Working with two peer researchers and 60 women with lived experience of challenges accessing healthcare, we co-created checklists for professionals detailing accessible information design and delivery principles. Using these checklists, and postnatal serious symptoms as an exemplar, we co-created resources in multiple formats and languages for distribution through the maternity service.

While guidance exists for designing health information according to principles of readability and access for people who are visually impaired, there is no evidence-based guidance specifically tailored for the needs of people facing challenges accessing healthcare and who are most at risk of poor outcomes in maternity services [30, 31]. Checklists are widely recognized tools that condense complex processes into reliable, repeatable steps, reduce omissions, and support performance improvement [32, 33]. When carefully designed and validated, they offer legitimacy, usability, and support the implementation of best practice [34, 35]. Designing such checklists requires systematic processes that was followed in the BEAMS project: clarifying the context, ensuring accurate and inclusive content, using supportive visuals, and validating with the target audience [36]. Their legitimacy stems not only from expert consensus and evidence review, but also from meaningful involvement of those with lived experience. Addressing equality and diversity in lived experience involvement is critical for the relevance and acceptability of the work. When individuals see themselves represented in resources and research, it fosters trust, belonging, and greater engagement [37].

Reducing health inequity requires working with those most affected, especially those less likely to have been included in research previously [38]. Not including people most affected by health inequity in research further increases their marginalisation. We found co-production (through the Always Events™ methodology) to offer a framework for the BEAMS project team to work with target populations in increasingly involved and dynamic ways [39]. Co-production is organic, evolving and is shaped and negotiated by all participants [40]. It is a process, not a single event that is designed to be iterative, allowing researchers to return to the community to get feedback and further refine the design. Of central importance are relationships, trust and equality. In the BEAMS project we found the process took time; to develop collaboration skills among participants and researchers, but also time and energy to engage with women who have different experiences and discover how best to work together [41]. Working in this way with people who may not be used to having their voice heard must be done safely to minimise the risk of harm to participants [25]. To maximise safety and trust, our study worked through community organisations [42]. They contacted and supported the members of their lived experience teams, while working with the researchers to ensure all engagements were trauma-informed with wrap-around support. This co-learning was extremely valuable as the research team learned to work in more flexible and nuanced ways to engage in a trustworthy and authentic relationship with the participating women. We found that there needs to be reflexivity in the research team to create a safe, supportive space that can foster peer support and knowledge exchange.

Participants were referred to as “lived experience experts” in recognition of their wide-ranging perspectives and contribution to the work [43]. This inclusive term refers to the knowledge and understanding a person gets from living through something. However, the team remained conscious of the risk of defining individuals solely by their experiences. The risk that certain experiences of interest legitimise the person’s place in the room is in contrast to them moving forward in their life and representing how they can be shaped, rather than defined by their experience [44]. Instead, the focus was on their informed perspectives and ideas. Some of the women commented on how this approach made them visible as women rather than someone who has suffered trauma, it made them feel at ease, listened to and respected.

Arts-based methods played a key role in creating a safe, inclusive space and have been used successfully in other work with vulnerable populations [45]. These techniques allowed participants to engage with difficult postnatal experiences indirectly, and at their own pace. A relationship of trust is fundamental to engagement with arts-based methods which depends on the cultural safety and support felt during the process. We define cultural safety as creating an environment where individuals feel secure and respected in their cultural identities, going beyond cultural competence to actively address power imbalance and promote inclusivity [46]. Early consultation with people of lived experience and collective agreement on the objectives and procedures of the research are the key to ethical research practice and require additional time in project planning [47]. Participants voiced strong support for these methods, appreciating the multiple ways they could contribute without pressure to share personal trauma. In some cases, the task offered an opportunity for the women to share their stories together, in others they remained silent. In all cases, the women engaged well with the task and shared how they were tired of telling their story and wanted to enable change. This aligns with trauma-informed practice principles of empowerment, voice, and choice [48].

Importantly, participants were actively involved not just in the development but also in the dissemination of the resources. The women chose to give voice to the animations by recording the voiceovers, speaking directly in the dissemination of the work to other women and to practitioners. Avoiding the simplification of their input to de-identified quotes is an important component of active involvement as ongoing marginalisation in the dissemination can negate the benefits of collaborative knowledge transfer [49]. Other examples of meaningful participation included participants joining the advisory group following their experience in the workshop or expressing interest in taking part in the drama production for the launch workshop at the end of the study [50]. This shows the impact of authentic co-production.

The BEAMS project has the potential to inform and influence national policy by providing evidence-based guidance on how to design and deliver accessible information for minoritised groups. By highlighting the link between communication barriers and preventable maternal deaths, BEAMS supports policy initiatives aimed at reducing health inequity and improving maternity safety. The co-produced checklists and co-production model offer practical, transferable tools that can be integrated into national and international policy frameworks, extending beyond maternity care to enhance knowledge transfer, timely care-seeking, and health outcomes.

Strengths/Limitations

The project created a community of motivated lived experience members, passionate about improving support for other mothers in taking care of themselves. It gathered a network of professionals interested in working in this way to share experiences and support each other. It provided a template for best practice in co-production and tools for immediate use in maternity services that can be applied to future quality improvement projects.

While it can be affirming and supportive to share personal experiences, we had advertised BEAMS participation as contributing from lived experience and not to talk about lived experiences to ensure psychological safety of all participants. We learned that skills were needed among the research team to manage the sharing of personal experiences in some of the sessions. We ensured a member of the recruiting charity was available to support participants after the session and we offered counselling support. While no participants accessed this support, they described feeling safe and cared for because it had been considered.

Sustaining participant engagement beyond the co-production workshop, particularly for providing feedback on developing resources and prioritising checklist findings, required considerable administrative capacity and co-ordination from the research team. Consultations sometimes had to be rescheduled or repeated as people were unable to join, or the agenda was not met as conversations explored different topics to those prioritised by the research team. This led to some uncomfortable conversations about payment rates in relation to participation time but provided team learning about the clear setting of objectives and expectations with all participants in future engagements.

We experienced significant challenges in the translation of the resources into different languages. Some translations used formal rather than conversational speech and some symptoms were translated erroneously which could result in serious consequences and distrust of the resources. We paid for additional and independent proof reading of each translation, and had them checked by participants from our research as a final quality check. This took time, and was expensive in some cases, but greatly appreciated by the communities who valued access to the information in visual, audio and written format in their own language.

Long term, we anticipate the postnatal serious symptoms exemplar will support women seek help early for their postnatal symptoms. This will positively impact the experiences and outcomes for women and their families, healthcare professionals and financially for the NHS. However, due to institutional processes and differences our sites adopted parts of the resources they felt most useful and reported delivering them in different ways. This meant our planned evaluation was not feasible and did not take place. Instead, we relied upon reports from the midwives at each implementation site to share examples of how they were using the resources in practice, which was less structured than we had planned.

Conclusion

The BEAMS project highlights the importance of co-production, trauma-informed methods, and inclusive checklist development in the creation of accessible health information. It offers an example of how to use arts-based methods in a participatory study design to work with women experiencing challenges accessing healthcare who may not usually engage in research. The practical focus of using lived experience to reach and support other women who may be experiencing challenges was described by the women as ‘giving them a voice for change’. The co-learning of the research team and the participants who delivered the project, created connections for the future and commitment to co-production as a robust methodology to impact health inequities. The evidence-based checklists and exemplar resources are practical outputs with immediate application in practice.
