Vaccine hesitancy and attitudes toward newborn heel-prick screening among religious officials: A mixed-methods study, Erzurum, Türkiye
Authors: Esra Daharli, Ertuğrul Çoruh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine hesitancy has risen globally and in Türkiye. Religious officials often act as community opinion leaders; their views may shape acceptance of vaccination and newborn bloodspot screening (NBS). We conducted a cross-sectional study among Muslim religious officials in Erzurum, Türkiye (n = 200). A 47-item questionnaire captured sociodemographics, health-service use/satisfaction, attitudes to childhood/adult vaccines and newborn bloodspot screening, and the 21-item Turkish Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (higher scores = higher hesitancy). Open-ended item elicited qualitative comments. Quantitative comparisons used t-tests/one-way ANOVA (α = 0.05). Qualitative data underwent inductive thematic analysis (Braun–Clarke); two independent coders achieved Cohen’s κ = 0.80. Mean vaccine hesitancy score was 62.9 ± 11.4. Scores did not differ by sociodemographics (p > .05). Lower satisfaction with health services was associated with higher hesitancy (overall p = .004). Hesitancy varied by attitudes toward adult vaccination (p = .003), childhood vaccination (p = .002), and NBS support (p = .004) — with lower scores among supporters. Only 19% were aware of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) fatwa on vaccination; awareness was not associated with hesitancy (p > .05). Thematic analysis (n = 40 textual responses) yielded five themes: safety/adverse-effect concerns (27.5%); information & service-process experience (22.5%); perceived benefit/effectiveness (bipolar) (20.0%); privacy/biomaterial (NBS) (17.5%); and fertility/genetic-harm beliefs (12.5%).Among religious officials, vaccine hesitancy appeared to cluster around safety and institutional trust, reinforced by privacy/biomaterial, fertility/genetic, and effectiveness narratives. Interventions should prioritize content transparency (including halal and safety information), process transparency for NBS (storage/secondary use/disposal policies), co-delivery of messages by physicians and religious officials, proactive risk communication, and brief, targeted training modules.

Introduction

Vaccines are among the most effective and cost-effective interventions for preventing infectious diseases. Achieving high vaccination coverage is essential to establishing population immunity and curbing transmission. However, in recent years in recent years vaccine hesitancy is increasingly recognized as one of the primary drivers of suboptimal vaccination coverage worldwide, influencing both vaccine delay and refusal.—mirroring global trends – vaccine hesitancy (and, at times, refusal) has risen in Türkiye as well. This trend threatens not only individual health but also public health.1

In recent years, vaccine hesitancy has been conceptualized using frameworks such as the 5C model, which includes confidence (trust in vaccines and health systems), complacency (low perceived disease risk), convenience (access-related factors), calculation (engagement in extensive information searching), and context (sociocultural influences). Vaccine hesitancy is widely recognized as a multidimensional construct. Alongside factors such as limited knowledge, fear of adverse effects, conspiracy narratives, and distrust in the health system, religious beliefs also shape vaccination decisions.2 In Muslim communities in particular, halal/haram(religious compliance at every stage) concerns about vaccine constituents, notions of predestination (qadar), and perceptions of “interference with nature” can contribute to refusal or hesitation.3

Similarly, another procedure performed in the neonatal period, such as the heel prick test (neonatal blood sample screening, NBS), may be hesitation possible for reasons similar to vaccine hesitancy, and attitudes toward such public health screening procedures may also be influenced by religious beliefs.attitudes toward public health screening procedures — such as the newborn heel-prick test (newborn bloodspot screening, NBS) — may also be shaped by religious beliefs. Inadequate public understanding of the test’s purpose and benefits, or perceptions that the procedure is religiously objectionable, can negatively affect the effectiveness and uptake of NBS programs.4

Religious officials — particularly in rural and conservative settings — serve as influential community opinion leaders. Within mosque-centered social networks, these trusted figures are key sources of information; their attitudes toward vaccination and health services can directly shape the views and behaviors of large segments of the public.2,5 Within this framework, religious beliefs and actors can be situated particularly within the “confidence” and “context” dimensions, as they shape trust in health interventions and provide normative guidance.Nevertheless, in Türkiye, empirical studies examining religious officials’ knowledge and attitudes toward vaccination and newborn screening remain scarce.

This study aimed to examine the attitudes of Muslim religious officials in Türkiye (imams, muezzins, preachers) toward vaccine hesitancy and their perceptions of newborn health practices such as the newborn heel-prick test (newborn bloodspot screening, NBS). Using a questionnaire administered to 200 religious officials, we assessed the extent to which religious orientations shape decision-making in these domains. The findings are intended to inform public health policy and to guide the development of targeted health-communication strategies that engage religious leaders.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting. We conducted a cross-sectional study in Erzurum Province (eastern Türkiye) to examine religious officials’ attitudes toward vaccine hesitancy and the newborn heel-prick test (newborn bloodspot screening, NBS).

Study population and sampling. The target population comprised Muslim religious officials actively serving in the province — including imams, muezzins, preachers (male and female), and official Qur’an course instructors. The sample size was calculated using Epi Info based on an estimated population of approximately 1000 religious officials in Erzurum Province. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an expected prevalence of 50%, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be approximately 230 participants. Due to feasibility constraints, 200 participants were included.

Questionnaire and measures

A structured questionnaire was used for data collection. The instrument comprised 47 items in total: six sociodemographic items (age, sex, marital status, having children, years in service, education); items on health-care utilization frequency, most frequently accessed facility, satisfaction with health services, source of health information, awareness of primary health-care services, and adult vaccination status; eight items eliciting views on the newborn heel-prick test (newborn bloodspot screening, NBS); items on childhood immunization services; 11 Likert-type items assessing attitudes toward vaccination — including perceived vaccine safety, potential conflict with religious beliefs, concerns about vaccine constituents, trust in the health system and in the pharmaceutical industry, perceived benefit/efficacy, broader religious concerns, perceived protection against infectious diseases, and concerns about the heel-prick procedure; one item assessing awareness of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) fatwa on vaccination; and the 21-item Vaccine Hesitancy Scale validated in Türkiye by Sarıgül (2020)6 (original Cronbach’s α = 0.905). The scale has no established cutoff; higher scores indicate greater hesitancy. The questionnaire was administered face to face.

Qualitative item and analysis

At the end of the questionnaire, open-ended questions invited respondents to share positive or negative views regarding vaccination and the heel-prick test. Forty participants provided textual responses. Using an inductive thematic content analysis, statements were iteratively reviewed, similar responses were coded and grouped, and five themes were identified. Representative quotations are presented in the Results to illustrate each theme. In qualitative research, sample size is not determined by statistical power calculations but by the principle of data saturation, where no new themes emerge from the data. In this study, data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively, and thematic saturation was considered to be reached within the available responses, as no new codes or themes emerged in the later stages of analysis.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews. Participants were informed about the study aims; voluntary participation and anonymity were emphasized, and written informed consent was obtained.

Ethics approval

The study received ethics approval from the Erzurum University of Health Sciences, Faculty of Medicine Scientific Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 2024/12–25). Institutional permission was also obtained from the Erzurum Provincial Mufti Office under the Presidency of Religious Affairs.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v27 with a two-tailed α = 0.05. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro – Wilk test and visual inspection (histograms, Q–Q plots); homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median, and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons used independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA with Tukey/Bonferroni post-hoc tests where appropriate. Associations between variables were examined using Pearson and Spearman correlations.

Open-ended questions were placed at the end of the survey; 40 participants provided textual responses. Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) theme development, (4) theme review, (5) definition and naming, and (6) reporting. Coding was conducted independently by two researchers; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Cohen’s κ = 0.80 indicated strong inter-coder agreement. Coding was performed in NVivo/Excel; the analytic unit was the participant statement, and multiple coding was permitted. Themes are presented with frequencies (n, %) and representative quotations. Quotations were anonymized as K1, K2, … . The research team reached consensus on five themes and approved the final analysis.

Ordinal variables were initially analyzed using their original categorical structure to preserve response variability. Where appropriate, additional analyses were conducted by collapsing conceptually similar categories.

Results

A total of 200 participants were included; 40 of them provided qualitative responses in addition to completing the quantitative survey. The mean vaccine hesitancy score was 62.9 ± 11.4. Participants’ mean age was 36.5 ± 10.4 y; 50% were women, 70% were married, and 67.6% had children. The mean length of service as a religious official was 12.7 ± 9.5 y, and 82% held a university degree. No significant associations were observed between sociodemographic characteristics and vaccine hesitancy scores (p > .05). Approximately half of the sample reported fewer than five health-care visits per year, and state/city hospitals were the most commonly accessed facilities.

Vaccine hesitancy scores did not differ by the number or type of health-care visits (p > .05). Although overall satisfaction with health services was high, participants who felt services “need improvement” or reported being dissatisfied had significantly higher hesitancy scores (p = .004). Most participants obtained health information primarily from a physician, and scores did not vary by information source (p > .05).

Regarding newborn-related services, participants reported the greatest awareness of childhood vaccination and the newborn heel-prick (disease) screening. Only 36.5% supported heel-prick blood collection and 31.0% supported routine childhood immunizations; vaccine hesitancy scores differed significantly across opinion categories for both domains (p = .002 and p = .004, respectively). Adult vaccinations were largely not received or were unfamiliar, and hesitancy scores varied by attitudes toward adult immunization (p = .003).

Only 19% of participants were aware of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) international fatwa concerning vaccination, and vaccine hesitancy scores did not differ by fatwa awareness (p > .05). When concerns about vaccines were probed, the most frequently endorsed driver was distrust of the pharmaceutical industry; this was followed by fears that vaccines may cause various diseases, the influence of prominent community leaders, the impact of social media, and worries about infertility. Qualitative findings

Five themes emerged. The most prevalent theme was safety/adverse-effect concerns (n = 11; 27.5%), followed by privacy and biomaterial (heel-prick data) concerns (n = 7; 17.5%); fertility/genetic-harm beliefs (n = 5; 12.5%); perceived benefit/effectiveness–a bipolar pattern of supportive vs. unnecessary/ineffective views (n = 8; 20.0%); and information and service-process experience (n = 9; 22.5%). Beyond their descriptive presentation, these themes collectively point to underlying mechanisms shaping vaccine hesitancy. Safety concerns, fertility-related beliefs, and biomaterial anxieties reflect heightened risk perceptions, while references to conflicting expert opinions and uncertainty about health information suggest broader issues of trust in health systems and authorities. These patterns can be interpreted within the 5C model, particularly the dimensions of confidence and calculation, as well as the Health Belief Model, where perceived barriers and benefits play a central role in shaping health-related decisions. Taken together, the findings suggest that vaccine hesitancy in this context is not solely driven by lack of knowledge, but rather emerges from the interaction of perceived risks, trust dynamics, and sociocultural interpretations of medical interventions.

Limitation study

The estimated population size and the slightly smaller-than-required sample may have affected the statistical power of the study.

Although the fact that the study was conducted in a single region limits generalization, it should also be considered that the selected province is a region where employees from across the country work on a rotational basis.

Given that participants were religious officials, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias or perceived expectations related to their professional roles, potentially leading to underreporting or overreporting of certain attitudes.

The study assessed attitudes rather than actual vaccination behaviors, and therefore findings should be interpreted as perceptions rather than direct indicators of vaccination uptake.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined the views of religious officials in Türkiye regarding vaccination services and newborn heel-prick (newborn bloodspot screening) procedures. Although prior literature reports generally supportive attitudes toward preventive health within faith communities, recent diversification of information sources, fluctuations in institutional trust, and the influence of online discourse have been associated with increasingly heterogeneous attitudes. In some contexts, this has made vaccine hesitancy more visible among religious groups; importantly, the official positions of religious institutions and the personal beliefs of individuals do not necessarily align.7,8 Vaccination hesitancy and heel prick blood sampling for newborn screening tests are observed alongside similar attitudes. Therefore, in this study, attitudes toward heel prick blood sampling, which is performed concurrently with vaccination services, were evaluated together. In our sample, the mean vaccine hesitancy score was 62.9 ± 11.4, which exceeded means reported for the Turkish general population (49.74 ± 15.6) and for nurses (46.82 ± 12.43) in prior studies.9,10 Cross-study comparisons warrant caution given differences in sampling frames and study periods; nevertheless, the pattern suggests that hesitancy may be comparatively elevated among religious officials. Because these actors often function as community opinion leaders, their attitudes may indirectly shape broader community decisions and thus carry particular public health relevance.11,12 Furthermore, the absence of significant differences across sociodemographic strata in our data implies that intervention efforts may be best designed to reach the wider body of religious officials, rather than narrowly defined subgroups.

Although most participants reported obtaining health information from health professionals, vaccine hesitancy scores did not differ by information source. The often-debated influence of social media did not appear to be a stand-alone driver13; this may reflect unmet informational needs during clinical encounters, time constraints, and limited use of persuasion/skills-based communication. Consistent with this interpretation, lower satisfaction with health services was associated with higher hesitancy scores, suggesting that the service experience may operate as a contextual determinant of attitudes.4

The relatively low proportion of participants supporting routine childhood vaccination (31.0%), compared with higher support for newborn heel-prick screening (36.5%), should be interpreted within the broader context of vaccine hesitancy trends in Türkiye. National reports indicate that although childhood vaccination coverage remains generally high at the population level, increasing pockets of hesitancy have been documented in specific subgroups. The observed pattern in this study may therefore reflect attitudinal variation rather than actual vaccination behavior. In addition, the higher acceptance of newborn screening compared with vaccination may be explained by differences in perceived risk, invasiveness, and familiarity of the procedures.

It should also be acknowledged that the findings are based on self-reported attitudes and may be subject to social desirability bias and response bias. Participants may have underreported hesitancy toward certain interventions or, conversely, expressed stronger opinions due to the professional and sociocultural context of religious authority roles. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as indicative of perceived attitudes rather than direct measures of vaccination behavior.

Vaccine hesitancy scores were lower among participants who supported or had received childhood immunizations and the newborn heel-prick procedure. This pattern suggests that supportive orientations toward routine services align with reduced hesitancy. For community leaders such as religious officials, fostering pro-acceptance attitudes may facilitate wider reach and strengthen community uptake efforts.3,11,14 In this context, establishing health-ambassador programs with selected religious officials — developed through partnerships between public health services and faith institutions — appears a promising, context-sensitive option.7 Vaccination coverage is a critical determinant of population-level protection against vaccine-preventable diseases. When coverage falls below the threshold required for herd immunity, the risk of outbreaks increases, particularly among vulnerable groups such as infants, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Herd immunity plays a crucial role in interrupting transmission chains and preventing the reemergence of controlled or eliminated infectious diseases. In recent years, declines or stagnation in vaccination uptake have been associated with localized outbreaks of measles and other vaccine-preventable diseases in various settings, highlighting the public health importance of maintaining high immunization coverage.

The Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) issues fatwas intended to harmonize positions across Muslim communities, including on the use of health services. In the context of vaccination, the OIC fatwa generally permits and encourages the use of vaccines, emphasizing the protection of life and public health as a core principle of Islamic jurisprudence. In our study, however, awareness of the OIC fatwa on vaccination was low and was not associated with vaccine hesitancy. This low level of awareness may be attributed to limited dissemination of the fatwa beyond formal religious or institutional channels, as well as the fact that such transnational religious rulings may not be routinely communicated to local religious officials or the general public in daily practice. Furthermore, the lack of association with vaccine hesitancy suggests that abstract or distant religious directives may have limited influence compared to interpersonal communication, local religious authority figures, and culturally embedded beliefs. Despite the normative emphasis on adherence to collective religious rulings, both the limited awareness of such guidance and its apparent lack of influence on attitudes suggest that top-down religious endorsements alone may be insufficient to shape decisions. In Türkiye, where religious services are provided by the state through the Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) — which endorses vaccination and preventive care — our findings similarly indicate that institutional statements may not translate into behavioral alignment at the individual level. These patterns point to a need for more localized, bidirectional, and relationship-centered communication strategies that engage religious officials and communities in context-specific dialogue.8

Our findings indicate that vaccine hesitancy among religious officials is rooted primarily in safety and institutional trust dimensions. The dominant qualitative theme was safety/adverse-effect concerns (27.5%); on the quantitative side, endorsements of “may cause various diseases” (11.6%) and “not sufficiently protective” (10.3%) aligned with this concern. Lower hesitancy scores among those who supported vaccination and the heel-prick/NBS procedure (p = .002 and p = .004, respectively) suggest that a favorable risk – benefit appraisal attenuates hesitancy. This pattern accords with the safety–effectiveness axes emphasized in the vaccine hesitancy literature.2,15,16

The information and service-process experience theme (22.5%) was consistent with the quantitative pattern whereby lower satisfaction with health services was associated with higher hesitancy scores (overall p = .004). Endorsements of “communication/media” (9.0%), “influence of prominent leaders” (9.4%), “distrust of health-care workers” (8.1%), and “distrust of the health system” (7.7%) suggest that not only what is communicated but also by whom and how it is conveyed is decisive. This underscores the importance, within clinical encounters, of informational sufficiency, effective/skills-based persuasive communication, and message delivery by trusted sources.16–18

The privacy and biomaterial (heel-prick) theme (17.5%) reflected uncertainties about storage and secondary use of samples and narratives about “use abroad for other purposes.” Although not directly quantified in the survey, this concern was indirectly supported by indicators of institutional trust and the association with service satisfaction. These patterns align with reports in the newborn screening literature highlighting privacy and specimen-retention anxieties. Clearly specifying, within informed consent, the duration of storage, access permissions/secondary uses, and disposal policies may help strengthen trust.16,19,20

The fertility/genetic-harm belief theme (12.5%) was mirrored quantitatively by endorsement of “may affect fertility” (9.0%). This belief set reflects a value- and faith-based frame that cannot be reduced to information deficits alone. Accordingly, combining clear halal content disclosures with evidence-based counter-narratives provides a faith-sensitive communication approach.8

The perceived benefit/effectiveness theme (20.0%) displayed a bipolar pattern: one subset viewed the practices as beneficial, whereas another considered them unnecessary/ineffective. Lower hesitancy scores among supporters (childhood vaccination p = .002; heel-prick p = .004) corroborate the directional role of perceived benefit. Presenting local disease-burden data alongside concrete benefit narratives—i.e., combining numbers with stories — may enhance persuasive impact.18,21

Taken together, the findings suggest that content transparency (regarding halal status and safety), process transparency (clear policies on storage/use of heel-prick specimens), joint messaging from trusted sources (co-delivery by physicians and religious officials), and proactive risk communication are actionable strategies to reduce hesitancy among religious officials.8,18

Limitation study

The estimated population size and the slightly smaller-than-required sample may have affected the statistical power of the study.

Although the fact that the study was conducted in a single region limits generalization, it should also be considered that the selected province is a region where employees from across the country work on a rotational basis.

Given that participants were religious officials, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias or perceived expectations related to their professional roles, potentially leading to underreporting or overreporting of certain attitudes.

The study assessed attitudes rather than actual vaccination behaviors, and therefore findings should be interpreted as perceptions rather than direct indicators of vaccination uptake.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study demonstrated that vaccine hesitancy is a dominant attitude among Muslim religious leaders. Hesitancy was associated with low satisfaction with healthcare services and was more pronounced among those who did not receive/were unaware of adult vaccinations and those who did not support childhood vaccinations or heel prick testing. Qualitative findings showed that the core of the hesitancy lay in themes of safety/side effects and information-process/institutional trust rather than explicitly religious concerns, and was reinforced by narratives of privacy/biomaterial, fertility/genetic harm, and perceived efficacy. Despite limitations such as cross-sectional design, convenient sampling, and reliance on self-report, the combined quantitative and qualitative evidence obtained among religious leaders supports positioning interventions along axes of safety, trust, and transparency.
