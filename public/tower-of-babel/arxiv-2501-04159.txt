Dual Numbers for Arbitrary Order Automatic Differentiation
F. Peñuñuri, K. B. Cantún-Avila, R. Peón-Escalante

Abstract
Dual numbers are a well-known tool for computing derivatives of functions. While the theoretical framework for calculating derivatives of arbitrary order is well established, practical implementations remain less developed. One notable implementation is available in the Julia programming language where dual numbers are designed to be nested, enabling the computation of derivatives to arbitrary order. However, this approach has a significant drawback as it struggles with scalability for high-order derivatives. The nested structure quickly consumes memory, making it challenging to compute derivatives of higher orders. In this study, we introduce DNAOAD, a Fortran-based implementation of automatic differentiation capable of handling derivatives of arbitrary order using dual numbers. This implementation employs a direct approach to represent dual numbers without relying on recursive or nested structures. As a result, DNAOAD facilitates the efficient computation of derivatives of very high orders while addressing the memory limitations of existing methods.

Introduction

Differentiation is a fundamental process that is widely applied in numerous
fields of science and engineering. It can be carried out symbolically
using specialized software, such as Maxima, Maple, or Mathematica, or
numerically through finite-difference methods. Another approach is
Automatic Differentiation (AD)
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, which enables the numerical computation of derivatives
with the same level of accuracy and precision as symbolic methods, but
without requiring symbolic computations.

Automatic Differentiation (AD) is a systematic algorithmic technique for
efficiently applying the chain rule to compute derivatives. Although there is extensive research on this topic, most studies focus on the
computation of first- and second-order derivatives for real-valued
functions
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. ADOL-C
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
is an interesting implementation of this
technique, which takes advantage of operator overloading to enable differentiation.
Although ADOL-C supports higher-order derivatives for some functions, a
considerable number, including asin, acos, atan, and exponentiation,
lack full support for differentiation across all derivative orders.

Notable AD implementations that support the computation of derivatives of arbitrary order include the PyTorch library in Python
[
14
]

and the ForwardDiff package in Julia
[
15
]
. Even when these
libraries do not natively support automatic differentiation for
complex-valued functions, the complex case can be managed by separately
computing the derivatives of the real and imaginary components of the
functions involved.

PyTorch is a versatile and widely used Python library for AD and deep
learning applications. One of its key strengths is its ability to compute
higher-order derivatives using a dynamic computation graph. This graph
tracks operations at runtime and facilitates automatic computation of gradients. However, when dealing with higher-order differentiation—especially
when calculating derivatives of complex operations or multiple
gradients—PyTorch retains the entire computational graph in memory. While
this is necessary to compute successive gradients, it significantly
increases memory consumption. For instance, calculating higher-order
derivatives can cause the computational graph to rapidly expand due to
the accumulation of intermediate values. Consequently, PyTorch’s memory
usage may quickly exceed the system’s available memory, often resulting
in a
std::bad_alloc
error, which occurs when memory allocation
requests surpass the system’s capacity. In our numerical experiment,
performed on a 12th generation Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-12650H CPU @ 4.70 GHz (base
400 MHz) with 15 GB of RAM, running Debian GNU/Linux 12, this error
occurred when calculating the 12th-order derivative of the function

f(x)=\sin(x)\,\exp(-x^{2})
, composed with itself five times.

In contrast, ForwardDiff, implemented in Julia, computes derivatives
using dual numbers and elegantly handles higher-order derivatives through
their recursive nesting. For simplicity, we refer to a structure resulting
from nesting these numbers
n
times as a dual number of order
n
or a
multidual number of order
n

[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Despite the elegance of this approach, challenges emerge when
computing higher-order derivatives with ForwardDiff. With each successive
derivative, ForwardDiff generates a new Dual type that encapsulates all
previously computed derivative information. This recursive nesting
increases complexity, as each successive derivative introduces additional
layers of dual numbers. For higher-order derivatives, this nesting becomes
particularly problematic, leading to excessive memory consumption and
ultimately causing stack overflows. In our numerical experiments, a
StackOverflowError occurred when attempting to compute the 15th-order
derivative of the previously mentioned function.

In this study, we present a Fortran implementation of dual numbers to
arbitrary order without relying on a recursive or nested structure. This
approach theoretically enables the computation of derivatives of any
order, effectively overcoming the limitations of the nested dual number
method. While practical constraints, such as computational resources,
will ultimately limit the achievable order of derivatives, our method
significantly extends the range of computable derivatives beyond what is
feasible with the nested approach. It is important to note, however,
that as the derivative order increases, the risk of precision loss grows.
Despite depending on the specific function being differentiated, our
numerical experiments show that derivatives of orders around 100 may
require quadruple precision for accurate computation. For even
higher-order derivatives, further increases in precision will likely be
necessary.

Our Fortran implementation uses complex numbers as the components of
dual numbers. Although an implementation with real numbers may be
necessary for efficiency reasons, we chose to use complex numbers for
greater generality. It is important to note the lack of a consistent
convention regarding the branches chosen for complex functions across
different programming environments. For example, when computing

\arcsin(1.1)
, the result obtained from SBCL (version 2.2.9.debian),
Octave (version 7.3.0), and Wolfram Mathematica (version 12.0) is

(1.5708,-0.44357)
. In contrast, the same computation using Python’s
cmath library yields
(1.5708,0.44357)
, a result consistent with that
produced by modern versions of Fortran. We have elected to adopt the
latter convention, aligning with the Fortran implementation, to avoid
the necessity of developing custom functions to select a specific
branch. The decision regarding which branch to standardize will
ultimately rest with the Fortran standards committee
(ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22/WG5).

5
Conclusions

This study addresses the challenges of computing higher-order derivatives
with dual numbers by introducing DNAOAD, a Fortran-based implementation
designed for arbitrary-order automatic differentiation. Most existing
dual-number implementations are limited to lower-order derivatives, with
only one notable approach—Julia’s ForwardDiff—supporting arbitrary-order differentiation through nested dual numbers. However, no prior work has
demonstrated a method for arbitrary-order differentiation with dual numbers
that avoids reliance on nested structures, which are prone to significant
memory consumption and stack overflow errors. DNAOAD bridges this gap by
directly implementing dual numbers to arbitrary orders, eliminating the
need for recursive or nested frameworks. This direct and innovative
approach significantly reduces computational overhead, enabling the
efficient and reliable computation of derivatives at very high orders,
and providing a scalable and practical solution to the limitations of
existing methods.

Through numerical experiments, DNAOAD demonstrated its capability to compute derivatives at very high orders with remarkable accuracy, though extremely high orders may require enhanced numerical precision, such as quadruple precision. The implementation also provides a considerable number of useful functions for various applications, including the calculation of gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians, as well as higher-order derivatives of complex functions. These capabilities enhance DNAOAD’s applicability to a broad range of fields, such as optimization, physics, and engineering, where precise differentiation is essential.

In comparison with existing tools, such as PyTorch and ForwardDiff—which excel in low-order differentiation but face scalability issues due to memory overheads—DNAOAD provides a scalable and efficient alternative. Its robustness was demonstrated in experiments involving deeply nested functions, highlighting its potential for tackling complex computational tasks. This implementation represents a significant advancement in practical automatic differentiation by combining theoretical rigor with computational efficiency. Future work may focus on extending DNAOAD to other programming environments and further optimizing its performance for large-scale applications.

The Fortran source code, which contains the data supporting the findings
of this study, is openly available on GitHub at

https://github.com/fpenunuri/DNAOAD
.
