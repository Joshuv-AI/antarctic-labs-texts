Metal Nanoparticles as Sensitizers in Energy-Based Therapies for Lung Cancer: From Mechanistic Insights to Clinical Translation
Authors: Xiuying Jin, Yue Li, Ruixue Zhang, Yang Yang, Anna Han, Xiangyu Jin, Songnan Zhang, Chunhua Quan, Zhenhua Lin, Xing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. Energy-based modalities, including radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and radiofrequency ablation, are now integral to multidisciplinary treatment regimens for this disease. However, the clinical efficacy of these approaches is severely constrained by intrinsic tumor hypoxia, the robust DNA repair machinery of cancer cells, and the off-target toxicity inflicted upon adjacent normal tissues. To address these bottlenecks, research has demonstrated that metallic nanoparticles (MNPs), by virtue of their unique physicochemical properties, can effectively enhance radiation energy deposition, modulate the tumor microenvironment, and achieve targeted delivery, thereby emerging as highly promising sensitizers for energy-based therapies. For instance, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) can achieve a radiation sensitization enhancement factor ranging from 1.2 to over 2.0 in vitro by increasing local dose deposition and generating secondary electrons, an effect that is dependent on nanoparticle size and concentration. Notably, this strategy has progressed to clinical validation, with silica-based gadolinium nanoparticles (AGuIX) being particularly prominent; these have completed Phase I/II clinical trials for the treatment of various malignancies, including lung cancer and brain metastases, confirming the translational potential of this technology. Here, we systematically review the advances in the application of MNPs in energy-based therapies for lung cancer and provide an in-depth analysis of their multimodal sensitization mechanisms, including the generation of ROS, sensitization of DNA damage, and induction of immunogenic cell death. On this basis, we further summarize the key translational barriers currently faced. Namely, we identify the in vivo safety of nanoparticles, the lack of standardized evaluation systems, and bottlenecks in large-scale production as key barriers, and we offer an outlook on future directions that integrate smart responsive nanosensitizers with clinical personalized therapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies and the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Histologically, it is classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of cases and SCLC for approximately 15%.1 According to GLOBOCAN 2022 statistics, there were an estimated 2.48 million new cases and 1.23 million deaths from lung cancer globally, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide.2

Within the framework of modern multidisciplinary treatment, energy-based local targeted therapies, such as radiation therapy, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and photodynamic therapy (PDT) play a critical role across different stages of disease owing to their precision and minimally invasive nature.3 For instance, radiotherapy is applicable in nearly all stages of lung cancer: stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) can deliver precise high-dose radiation for patients with early-stage inoperable NSCLC, achieving local control rates of 85% to 95%; for patients with locally advanced disease, concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains the standard of care; and when the disease progresses to an advanced stage, palliative radiotherapy can effectively alleviate symptoms from bone and brain metastases, thereby improving quality of life.4,5 RFA is mainly used for local control in oligometastatic stage IV lung cancer, particularly in elderly and frail patients, and offers 5-year local control rates of 60–75% for peripheral lung tumors ≤3 cm in diameter, providing an alternative for patients who are ineligible for other local therapies.6 PDT, by contrast, has distinct advantages in the management of central airway lesions and airway obstruction: for early-stage unilateral lesions, it can specifically eliminate tumors while preserving lung function; for advanced central airway obstruction, symptom relief rates as high as 80–90% can be achieved, paving the way for subsequent systemic therapy.7–9

Despite these significant advances, energy-based therapies continue to face multiple challenges. Intratumoral hypoxic regions, for example, drive robust radioresistance, and tumor cells can repair radiation-induced DNA damage by activating key signaling pathways such as ATM/ATR.10 Meanwhile, tumor heterogeneity results in uneven therapeutic effects across different cell subpopulations at the same dose.11 In NSCLC, adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma are frequently associated with extensive hypoxic areas and possess a strong DNA repair capacity, thus exhibiting radioresistance.12,13 SCLC, although highly sensitive to initial chemoradiotherapy, similarly develops cross-resistance upon relapse.14 Moreover, normal tissue tolerance strictly restricts dose escalation, directly compromising local control rates and overall efficacy. As a gas-exchange organ, the lung’s unique oxygenation physiology generates marked intratumoral hypoxic heterogeneity, which can directly influence the fixation of radiation-induced DNA damage.15 At the same time, entrapment by the pulmonary capillary network and the dense tumor stroma impede the penetration of systemically administered drugs into the tumor interstitium, making it difficult for both conventional chemotherapeutics and nanomedicines to reach effective intratumoral concentrations in the lung.16 Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel strategies that can simultaneously overcome radioresistance and drug delivery limitations.

Studies have shown that metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) offer a practical and feasible approach to overcoming the aforementioned limitations.17 Since Hainfeld et al first reported the radiosensitizing effect of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) on X-rays in 2004, investigations into the enhancement of radiotherapy, proton therapy, and even carbon ion therapy by MNPs have steadily increased, demonstrating substantial clinical translational potential.18–20 The sensitizing properties of MNPs arise from the superior energy absorption capacity of their high atomic number (Z) elements. Upon interacting with photons, both the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering cross-sections are significantly enhanced, generating localized microdosimetric reinforcement within the tumor target volume.21 When X-ray or γ-ray photons interact with high-Z atoms, in addition to the production of primary photoelectrons, atomic relaxation triggered by inner-shell ionization can also induce Auger cascades, whereby the vacancy left in the inner shell after the emission of a photoelectron or Compton electron is de-excited through a cascade emission of Auger electrons and fluorescent photons, releasing a large number of low-energy (eV–keV range) secondary electrons (SEs).22,23 These electrons possess extremely high linear energy transfer (LET) at the nanoscale (typically <100 nm), capable of inducing highly localized energy deposition and creating microdosimetric enhancement that cannot be captured by conventional macroscopic dosimetry. To quantify this effect, the local dose enhancement factor (DEF) has been introduced, defined as the ratio of locally deposited energy in the presence of nanoparticles to the background dose in their absence; Monte Carlo simulations, which precisely track particle transport on an event-by-event basis, are employed to predict DEF and play a central role in optimizing nanoparticle size, elemental composition, and energy combinations.24 Furthermore, the surface of MNPs can be functionalized with polyethylene glycol (PEG) or conjugated with targeting ligands to improve tumor selectivity and biosafety; certain MNPs, such as paramagnetic gadolinium-based and superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, also possess imaging capabilities, thereby laying the foundation for theranostic platforms.25,26 Notably, these nano-sensitization strategies have now moved beyond the proof-of-concept stage. A group of high-Z nano-radiosensitizers, exemplified by gadolinium-based AGuIX nanoparticles, has entered human clinical trials and has shown favorable safety profiles and unequivocal radiotherapy-enhancing effects in solid tumors, including brain metastases, cervical cancer, and lung cancer.27–29 CYT-6091, a PEGylated colloidal gold nanoparticle with recombinant human tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) covalently conjugated to its surface, has been evaluated in clinical trials enrolling patients with various advanced solid tumors, including lung cancer, and has demonstrated a favorable safety profile and preliminary antitumor activity.30 These advances attest to the practical value and timeliness of high-Z nanomedicine platforms and provide direct clinical precedents for their extension to lung cancer.

For lung cancer, high-Z nanoparticles can directly address the pathophysiological barriers of the lung. Inhalational delivery of nanoparticles can bypass the blood-gas barrier, enabling substantial deposition of the sensitizer on the bronchoalveolar epithelium and tumor surface. This approach achieves high local drug concentrations while markedly reducing systemic toxicity, thereby overcoming the limited pulmonary drug levels typically encountered with systemic administration. Simultaneously, the MNP-mediated local dose enhancement effect can selectively elevate the radiation dose within the tumor target volume without increasing the exposure of surrounding normal lung tissue, potentially breaking through the constraints imposed by dose-limiting toxicities such as radiation pneumonitis.31,32 Thus, integrating high-Z nano-sensitization with inhalational targeted delivery holds promise for providing novel combinatorial regimens for NSCLC and SCLC that transcend current standard-of-care paradigms.

In summary, this review systematically summarizes the advances in the application of metallic nanoparticles in energy-based therapies for lung cancer and offers an outlook on key future directions, including the construction of multifunctional theranostic nanoplatforms and the synergistic sensitization mechanisms for energy-based therapies.

Mechanisms of MNPs in Energy-Based Therapies

As sensitizers for energy-based therapies, MNPs can achieve tumor killing through synergistic effects across three dimensions: physical, chemical, and biological. To establish a rigorous quantitative evaluation framework, the field has introduced two core performance metrics: the DEF and the sensitization enhancement ratio (SER). The DEF is defined as the ratio of locally deposited energy in the presence of nanoparticles to the background dose in the absence of nanoparticles, reflecting the direct effect of physical dose enhancement.33 The SER is defined as the ratio of the radiation dose required to achieve the same biological endpoint without the sensitizer to that with the sensitizer, providing a comprehensive measure of the combined physical, chemical, and biological sensitization efficacy.34 Monte Carlo simulations, by precisely tracking every step of photon-matter interactions, including the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and subsequent Auger cascade relaxation can predict the spatial distribution of DEF at the nanodosimetric scale, thus offering a theoretical tool for quantitative comparison across different nanoparticle systems.35,36

Physical Sensitization Mechanisms

The physical sensitization mechanism represents the most direct and fundamental mode by which nanoparticles exert their effects. By virtue of the enhanced radiation capture capacity conferred by their high atomic number (Z), MNPs, upon enrichment in tumor tissue, can deposit incident energy in a highly localized manner within the cancerous region through radiation–matter interactions such as the Compton effect and the photoelectric effect.37 Compared with radiotherapy alone, this MNP-based radiosensitization strategy can more effectively elevate the radiation dose within the tumor target volume, thereby holding promise for improving tumor control while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue and reducing treatment complications.

High-Z Effect

A substantial body of research has firmly established that high-Z MNPs are a class of highly effective radiosensitizers. Their sensitizing capacity was originally discovered through a serendipitous clinical observation: during radiotherapy for mandibular and head and neck cancers, these materials exhibited greater tumor control effects than expected.38–40 Subsequently, a range of high-Z nanoparticles, such as gold, gadolinium, silver, bismuth, and several metal oxide nanostructures have been developed, and their sensitizing efficacy has been well validated.41,42 The core of their sensitization mechanism lies in the exponential amplification of radiation interaction cross-sections. The photoelectric effect cross-section is approximately proportional to Z4, whereas the Compton scattering cross-section scales linearly with electron density.43 The large number of electrons per atom in high-Z elements enables the nanoparticles to essentially act as “nano-concentrators” of radiation energy, generating localized energy deposition within the tumor region that far exceeds the background level.44 Accordingly, Monte Carlo simulations are employed to calculate the DEF by tracking the full transport of photoelectrons, Auger electrons, and scattered photons on a particle-by-particle basis, thereby enabling accurate assessment of the degree of local dose enhancement and its three-dimensional spatial distribution.45

Secondary Electron Effect

High-Z materials, such as gold and platinum, by virtue of their excellent X-ray absorption capability, can precisely deposit ionizing radiation dose within tumor tissue.18 The fundamental principle underlying this radiation enhancement effect lies in the highly efficient generation of abundant short-range electrons at the microscopic to nanoscopic scale. High-Z elements in MNPs markedly facilitate this process, increasing electron yield and enhancing lethal damage to malignant cells, thereby boosting the biological efficacy of radiotherapy.46 The primary products of these physical interactions are copious high-energy SEs, including photoelectrons, Auger electrons, and scattered electrons. Among these, Auger electrons, which possess relatively low energy and a range spanning the nanometer to micrometer scale, may undergo scattering within the metal and lose energy;47 their ionizing action can further generate low-energy electrons (LEEs) with energies below 20 eV.48 In addition, fluorescent photons absorbed within the nanoparticles can trigger the generation of other short-range electrons. However, some photons and particles released by the nanoparticles propagate beyond the micrometer scale, meaning that the absorbed X-ray energy is not completely confined within the cell; the energy deposition in critical targets such as nuclear DNA is thereby even more severely constrained.

It is precisely this limited range of short-range electrons that determines that the subcellular localization of MNPs can exert an order-of-magnitude impact on their radiation SER. When MNPs are localized in the cytoplasm, the Auger electrons generated must traverse a distance of several micrometers to damage intranuclear DNA; their energy attenuates sharply along the path, resulting in an exceedingly low dose actually reaching the DNA, and the improvement in SER remains very limited.49 In contrast, if MNPs are directly imported into the nucleus with the aid of nuclear localization signals, or accumulate in the vicinity of the nuclear membrane, the short-range, high-LET Auger electrons can act directly on chromatin DNA, causing complex and difficult-to-repair clustered damage; under these conditions, the SER can rise sharply to 2.0 or even higher.50 Thus, advancing the energy deposition of nanoparticles from the “cellular level” to the “intranuclear level” represents a key strategy for fully realizing their radiosensitization potential.

Direct Physical Damage

The high-density SEs generated within these “microscale dose hotspots” can directly act on DNA strands, causing chemical bond breakage and thereby inducing lethal DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs).51 DSBs are widely recognized as the most critical form of damage leading to radiation-induced cell death. Studies have demonstrated that AuNPs combined with X-rays can enhance cytotoxicity against tumor cells by markedly increasing the yield of DSBs.52 However, understanding the sensitization effect of MNPs solely in terms of DSB quantity is incomplete; more crucially, the damage they induce differs fundamentally from that caused by radiation alone in both spatial distribution and complexity.

In conventional radiotherapy, DSBs are distributed relatively uniformly and sparsely within the nucleus and consist predominantly of simple, easily repairable double-strand breaks. In contrast, the extremely high density of SEs generated within a few to tens of nanometers around MNPs can simultaneously induce multiple strand breaks in adjacent segments of the DNA double helix, leading to the formation of complex clustered damage.49,53,54 Such damage encompasses compound lesions consisting of double-strand breaks accompanied by single-strand breaks, base damage, and deoxyribose damage, forming “damage clusters” with exceptionally high local lesion density. Because repair pathways such as base excision repair and nucleotide excision repair exhibit competitive recognition constraints on the spatial positioning of substrates, the multiple lesions within a clustered damage region can mutually interfere with the accurate recognition and effective cleavage by repair enzymes, causing the difficulty of repair to increase exponentially. This shift in damage pattern is of decisive significance for the cell. Simple DSBs can be effectively repaired within hours via homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), whereas the repair half-life of complex clustered damage is markedly prolonged and repair fidelity is substantially reduced, readily giving rise to chromosomal aberrations, micronuclei, and reproductive cell death. Thus, compared with conventional radiotherapy, the highly concentrated and non-uniform distribution of radiation energy around nanoparticles enables a veritable “nanoscale precision strike” within the targeted region, not only increasing the absolute number of lethal DSBs but, more critically, transforming repairable simple damage into difficult-to-repair complex damage at the qualitative level, thereby greatly amplifying the lethal effect of radiation on tumor cells (Figure 1). This mechanism also provides an important theoretical basis for understanding the differential sensitization effects of MNPs in radiation fields of varying LET. Chemical Sensitization Mechanisms

The chemical sensitization mechanism primarily involves the chemical reactions induced by MNPs under radiation and their modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby indirectly enhancing therapeutic sensitivity. Solid tumors are universally characterized by hypoxia, which arises predominantly from the high oxygen consumption of tumor cells and is further exacerbated by abnormal stromal microvessels that restrict oxygen supply, which greatly enhances tumor radioresistance.55 To address this issue, one of the functions of MNPs is oxygen delivery and catalytic oxygen generation. Some nanoparticles can directly carry and release oxygen, or exploit their catalase-like activity to catalyze the decomposition of overexpressed hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) within tumors, generating oxygen in situ. As molecular oxygen is essential for the fixation of radiation-induced DNA damage, this strategy can effectively improve local oxygenation. Currently, numerous studies are actively combining such nanoparticles with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or PDT to alleviate tumor hypoxia.56–58 For instance, Chen et al developed protein-coated manganese dioxide nanoparticles that are dual-responsive to pH and H2O2; upon entering tumor tissue, they can react with H2O2 and protons to release oxygen in situ.59 Reversing radioresistance is another critical direction. Oxygen deficiency impedes the generation of oxygen free radicals, impairing the tumor-killing capacity of radiotherapy, while an excess of reducing species facilitates the repair of tumor cell DNA damage.60 Conversely, elevated oxygen levels can downregulate the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), thereby suppressing a cascade of HIF-1α-mediated pro-survival effects and ultimately reversing hypoxia-associated radioresistance.61–63 Furthermore, reactive oxygen species (ROS) play crucial roles in processes such as cell proliferation, cell cycle regulation, cell motility, and apoptosis and are also a major determinant of radiotherapeutic efficacy.64 In a radiation field, MNPs can serve as a highly efficient source of ROS, with the primary mechanism being the promotion of water radiolysis. Specifically, the high-energy SEs generated by the interaction of nanoparticles with radiation inject energy into surrounding water molecules, substantially accelerating water decomposition and producing large quantities of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Concurrently, the nanoparticle surface can act directly as a catalytic site, generating ROS species such as superoxide anions (O2−) and H2O2.65 These products exert toxic effects on both tumor cells and adjacent normal tissues. Radiation itself can stimulate mitochondria to produce endogenous ROS, leading to altered mitochondrial membrane permeability and further exacerbating ROS accumulation.66 This burst of ROS generation clearly surpasses the scavenging capacity of the tumor cell’s antioxidant system, disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, and triggers intense oxidative stress (Figure 2). Biological Sensitization Mechanisms

A growing body of evidence indicates that the radiosensitizing effects of MNPs primarily elicit a series of biological events at the cellular level, including the formation of ROS, oxidative stress, the induction of DNA damage, cell cycle alterations, and possibly effects mediated through the bystander effect.

ROS and the Induction of Oxidative Stress

High concentrations of ROS can disrupt the biomembrane structure of tumor cells and destabilize biological macromolecules, thereby inducing apoptosis or necrosis. This property renders ROS an important tool in cancer treatment. Currently, PDT, chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and radiodynamic therapy (RDT) are three standard cancer treatment modalities that are associated with ROS modulation.68

Platinum (Pt, Z=78) possesses several unique advantages in the field of radiosensitization. It not only efficiently absorbs X-ray photons, thereby enhancing the direct tumoricidal effect of radiotherapy through radiation-generated ·OH, but also transfers X-ray energy to photosensitizer molecules, activating the generation of singlet oxygen (1O2) and thus achieving a synergistic effect with PDT. Furthermore, platinum-based nanoparticles can exploit the weakly acidic TME and elevated glutathione (GSH) levels to inhibit oxidative phosphorylation in tumor cells, thereby producing an oxygen-sparing effect. This, in turn, amplifies the massive production of ROS and stabilizes radiotherapy-induced DNA damage, further improving radiosensitization efficacy.69

Hu et al demonstrated that their designed multifunctional nanoparticles, a Fe(III)-Pt(II) porphyrin framework loaded with long-chain amine (LND) (FPTM-LP), can precisely release and deliver zinc and manganese ions within the TME. Through triggering Fenton-like reactions to generate ROS, these nanoparticles efficiently kill tumor cells and markedly enhance their radiosensitivity. Simultaneously, the released chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin synergistically induces DNA damage and apoptosis, achieving multiple synergistic therapeutic effects.70 Moreover, this nanoplatform can trigger antitumor immune responses upon radiotherapy in vivo, significantly inhibiting primary tumor growth and distant metastasis, thus providing a safe and effective novel combined immuno-radiotherapy platform for advanced cancer. From the perspective of gene-regulating therapy, Chen et al developed an enhanced targeted delivery system based on Fe3O4 MNPs to deliver siBIRC5 and antisense oligodeoxynucleotides (AS-ODN), achieving blockade of specific genes and enhancing the radiosensitivity of lung adenocarcinoma cells.71 This innovative platform provides a new approach for improving radiosensitivity in lung adenocarcinoma and holds tremendous potential for clinical application.

Effects on the Cell Cycle

The radiosensitization effect and its associated biological consequences are closely linked to the cell cycle. MNPs can exert their radiosensitizing effects through two fundamental mechanisms: cell cycle regulation and apoptosis induction. Different phases of the cell cycle exhibit markedly distinct responses to radiation: cells in late S phase are relatively radioresistant due to their robust DNA repair capacity, whereas cells in late G2 and M phases are highly radiosensitive.72 Radiation-generated ·OH radicals can induce DNA double-strand damage, which in turn activates the ATM kinase pathway and thus triggers the phosphorylation of p53. Phosphorylated p53 upregulates p21 expression, leading to cell cycle arrest at the G1/S and G2/M checkpoints. However, the effects of MNPs on the cell cycle vary depending on their surface modifications. For instance, Cui et al found that AuNPs per se can inhibit DNA repair after radiation; however, they did not trigger cell cycle synchronization.78 In contrast, when AuNPs were capped with glucose to form Glu-GNPs, a shift in the G0/G1 phase and an enrichment of cells in the G2/M phase were observed within 24 h in prostate cancer cells, accompanied by activation of CDK kinases.79 Qian et al reported that bovine serum albumin-coated gold nanoparticles (BSA-GNPs), which are ultimately localized to lysosomes via endocytosis, can arrest G2/M progression through the binding activity of kinesin-5 to microtubules.80

Based on these studies, the mechanisms by which MNPs achieve radiosensitization through cell cycle modulation primarily involve two directions: increasing the proportion of cells in the sub-G1 phase, thereby producing a sustained cytotoxic effect; or inducing G2/M arrest, which retains tumor cells in a radiosensitive phase and thus theoretically enhances the tumor-killing capacity of radiotherapy.

DNA Damage Repair

Previous studies have demonstrated that nanoparticles can disrupt the molecular regulatory mechanisms of DNA repair in tumor cells, impairing their capacity to repair ionizing radiation-induced DNA damage, thereby generating cumulative damage effects and significantly enhancing radiotherapeutic efficacy. Jain et al were the first to demonstrate the radiosensitizing effect of AuNPs on MDA-MB-231 cells at megavoltage radiotherapy energies.77 Studies have revealed that AuNPs can disrupt the normal transcriptional cascade regulation of DNA damage repair pathways, downregulating the expression of key repair genes such as BRCA1, MSH3, and MRE11A, thereby inhibiting the ability of tumor cells to repair radiation-induced damage.81 Furthermore, DNA-targeted chemotherapeutic agents have also been tested as radiosensitizers.81 As a marker of DSBs, the formation of γ-H2AX foci has confirmed that AuNPs can effectively induce DNA breaks.82,83

To provide a reference for the design and material selection of MNPs for energy-based therapies in lung cancer, we briefly describe six representative types of MNPs in terms of their atomic number, primary sensitization mechanisms, imaging capability, and biosafety. Application of MNPs in Energy-Based Therapies for Lung Cancer

With the emergence of innovative therapeutic modalities such as immunotherapy, gene therapy, photothermal therapy (PTT), and PDT, the field of lung cancer treatment continues to advance. Concomitantly, the critical role of MNPs in improving lung cancer therapeutic outcomes, by virtue of their unique physicochemical properties, has attracted increasing attention. Studies have demonstrated that MNPs are versatile systems capable of enhancing the efficacy of energy-based therapies including radiotherapy and PDT. The conceptual scope of their application has greatly expanded from mere radiation sensitization to encompass functions such as active targeting, multimodal imaging, and synergistic therapy, thereby aligning closely with the goals of clinical precision medicine.95 More importantly, a number of nano-radiosensitizers, represented by NBTXR3 and AGuIX, have advanced to pivotal clinical trial stages, including cohort studies specifically designed for lung cancer indications.96,97

Radiosensitization in Radiotherapy

The intrinsic DNA damage repair capacity of tumor cells and radiotherapy-induced resistance mechanisms represent the core bottlenecks limiting radiotherapeutic efficacy, thus creating an urgent need for novel strategies to enhance radiotherapy outcomes or overcome resistance. Yao et al demonstrated that albumin-coated gold nanoparticles (Alb-GNPs), when combined with radiotherapy, can significantly enhance radiosensitization and antitumor activity, with safety evaluations revealing no overt toxicity or abnormal irritation.98 Through systemic administration, AuNPs can exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of tumor tissue to achieve intratumoral accumulation; the massively aggregated nanoparticles markedly induce tumor cell apoptosis, and when combined with radiotherapy, the cell-killing effect is synergistically enhanced, and tumor growth is significantly suppressed.99 Mechanistic studies have shown that, at clinically relevant radiation energies, A549 human NSCLC cells treated with AuNPs exhibit a marked reduction in clonogenic capacity, together with significantly upregulated expression of DSB markers as indicated by γ-H2AX nuclear foci staining, suggesting that AuNPs may enhance radiosensitivity by exacerbating radiation-induced DNA damage, a potential key molecular mechanism underlying their sensitizing effect.100 Furthermore, NBTXR3 is a non-biologically dependent, purely physical radio-enhancer composed of the high-electron-density material hafnium oxide (HfO2). It is fixed within the target lesion through a single intratumoral injection, enabling sustained local dose amplification.101,102 Its CE mark approval for soft tissue sarcoma has laid the foundation for its application in solid tumors.103

Sensitization Applications in PDT

PDT relies on oxygen interactions to generate therapeutic singlet oxygen; however, the TME is intrinsically hypoxic, and this contradiction constitutes the core bottleneck limiting PDT efficacy. MNPs provide an effective avenue for overcoming this hypoxia challenge while reducing treatment-associated side effects. Liu et al found that platinum nanoparticles with catalase-like activity can significantly enhance PDT efficacy by catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 within tumor cells into oxygen.104 Xia et al reported a facile and efficient method for preparing iodinated IR-780-loaded gold nanostars (GNS).105 This optimized multifunctional platform integrates dual imaging modalities, near-infrared fluorescence imaging and photoacoustic imaging, with a combined therapeutic modality of PTT and PDT. Through surface modification with an MMP2-responsive peptide coating, the constructed GNS@BSA/I-MMP2 nanoparticles can efficiently target tumor sites. Under laser irradiation, the synergistic effect between the GNS and IR-780 molecules significantly elevates local temperature, effectively inhibiting tumor growth. Experimental data demonstrated that, compared with the PBS control group receiving laser irradiation alone, this therapy achieved a tumor volume reduction of up to 93%.

Targeting Lung Cancer Stem Cells (CSCs)

Cancer stem cells, as key drivers of tumor recurrence and therapeutic resistance, have become an important research frontier.106 To overcome this bottleneck, a study innovatively constructed a multifunctional composite nanosystem: the photosensitizer aluminum phthalocyanine (AlPcS4Cl) was uniformly loaded onto the surface of AuNPs and conjugated with an anti-CD133 monoclonal antibody, thereby enabling active targeted delivery to lung CSCs. Experimental results confirmed that this system increased the internalization efficiency of the photosensitizer within tumor stem cells by 2.8-fold, consequently enhancing the PDT-induced ROS burst and mitochondria-dependent apoptosis. By precisely eliminating the tumor stem cell subpopulation, this approach offers a transformative strategy for preventing tumor recurrence.107 Kahr et al developed a photoactive nanocomposite system, namely curcumin-silver nanoparticle-polymer conjugates (Cum-PEG-BpAgNPs), which demonstrated superior efficacy in lung cancer PDT by markedly augmenting ROS generation.108 Cao et al successfully constructed a composite nanodelivery system based on manganese dioxide (MnO2) and the photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6).109 This system utilized physical adsorption to load Ce6 onto the surface of MnO2 nanospheres, enhancing the in vivo circulation stability of Ce6. Upon entering the TME, the MnO2@Ce6 platform, acting as a pH- and H2O2-dual-responsive system, effectively neutralizes excess H+ to alleviate tumor acidosis and catalyzes the decomposition of overexpressed H2O2 to generate O2 in situ. Under 633 nm laser excitation, this in situ oxygen supply strategy substantially improves the efficiency of Ce6 in generating cytotoxic singlet oxygen, thereby effectively overcoming the severe constraints imposed by tumor hypoxia on PDT.

Construction of Theranostic Nanoplatforms

With advances in nanoprobe fabrication, strategies that integrate diagnostic and therapeutic functions into a single entity are gaining increasing attention. For example, GNPs@PEG/Ce6-P nanoprobes, conjugated with a human programmed death-ligand peptide, can actively target tumor sites while simultaneously carrying the photosensitizer Ce6, endowing the probes with PDT functionality.110 Near-infrared fluorescence imaging and photoacoustic imaging have demonstrated that these probes efficiently accumulate at tumor sites, enabling precise tumor localization and visualization. Therapeutically, they effectively destroy tumor tissue through the synergistic action of PTT and PDT, fully showcasing the significant advantages of theranostic design.

Organelle-Targeted Delivery for Enhanced Efficacy

Li et al exploited the ultrasmall size and surface properties of gold nanoclusters (ACNPs), combined with their electron resonance relaxation effect and active targeting capability, to efficiently and specifically deliver the endogenous photosensitizer precursor 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) to the mitochondria of tumor cells.111 Upon light irradiation, the protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) generated within the mitochondria produces abundant ROS. Given that mitochondria serve as both the energy center of the cell and a key organelle in the regulation of apoptosis, this strategy can trigger apoptosis more effectively (Figure 4), demonstrating remarkable antitumor efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo experiments and thereby validating the significant strategic value of subcellular organelle-targeted delivery. Limitations of MNPs in Energy-Based Therapies and Potential Solutions

Although MNPs have demonstrated excellent diagnostic and therapeutic potential in tumor energy-based treatments, their translation from basic research to clinical application still faces considerable challenges. These challenges principally center on three aspects: the effectiveness of physical targeted delivery, the complex physicochemical properties and metabolic behavior of the materials, and the standardization of large-scale production. In response to these difficulties, current research strategies are evolving toward intelligent design, multifunctional integration, and programmable regulation.

Limitations and Core Challenges

Targeting Delivery Efficiency Bottleneck and Physical Delivery Optimization

Upon intravenous injection, nanoparticles first encounter the body’s innate clearance mechanisms. The mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) in organs such as the liver and spleen rapidly recognizes and clears these nanocarriers, resulting in an extremely short blood circulation half-life and severely compromising their enrichment and accumulation at the tumor site.112 Experimental evidence further reveals the severity of this delivery bottleneck: only approximately 0.001% of the injected dose reaches the tumor cell region, and the uptake ratio of nanoparticles by tumor cells is merely around 2%. Meanwhile, the extracellular matrix (ECM) within the TME can entrap up to 90% of the nanoparticles, with only a very small fraction dispersed among tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). These findings clearly indicate that achieving efficient nanoparticle accumulation in tumor tissue remains a pressing challenge.113,114 Currently, nanoparticle-targeted delivery relies primarily on the EPR effect of solid tumors. However, this effect exhibits considerable heterogeneity in humans, and factors such as elevated interstitial fluid pressure further impede nanoparticle penetration, often leading to insufficient intratumoral drug doses. This not only limits therapeutic efficacy but may also pose long-term radiation safety concerns due to nonspecific nanoparticle accumulation in normal tissues.115,116

Unknown Long-Term Biosafety and Metabolic Challenges

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), once regarded as biologically inert, can still disrupt iron and lipid metabolism after persisting in organs such as the liver and spleen for months or even years, driving macrophages toward pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype polarization, thereby inducing occult chronic inflammation and further activating hepatic stellate cells, which leads to progressive, irreversible fibrosis.117–119 The underlying mechanism lies in the dramatically increased specific surface area upon nanosizing, which renders surface atom coordination unsaturated and significantly accelerates ion leaching from the particles in biological fluids as mediated by oxygen, chlorine, thiols, and other species. Trace amounts of leached Au3+, Pt2+, Ag+, and other ions irreversibly inhibit the mitochondrial respiratory chain, competitively occupy the zinc finger domains of DNA repair proteins, and form hapten complexes with host proteins.

Furthermore, the immune recognition of nanoparticles further raises the ceiling of long-term risk. Their size, shape, and surface chemistry, by modulating complement recognition and protein corona composition, can either trigger complement activation-related pseudoallergic reactions or, upon repeated administration, induce the production of nanoparticle-specific antibodies, thereby drastically altering the pharmacokinetic behavior of subsequent doses. Consequently, for early-stage lung cancer, the gain in local control conferred by radiosensitization must be weighed against the risk of chronic sequelae spanning decades; material design should thus lean toward “rapid and safe clearance after the functional period”, for example, by adopting ultrasmall sizes (<5 nm) to enable the glomerular filtration pathway or by employing stimuli-responsive systems capable of complete degradation into nontoxic small molecules within the lysosomal environment.120 The gadolinium-based nanoparticle AGuIX exemplifies the response to such ionic toxicity: to address the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis induced by free Gd3+, the first strategy involves securely chelating Gd3+ within cyclic molecules and immobilizing it on the nanomatrix through chemical chelation and stabilization, thereby greatly minimizing the release of free ions; the second strategy exploits an ultrasmall size (<5 nm) to achieve rapid renal clearance and shorten the in vivo residence time.121,122

Standardization Challenges in Material Properties and Manufacturing Processes

The radiosensitization capacity of MNPs is intimately linked to their fundamental physicochemical properties, including size distribution uniformity, geometry, surface charge density, and aggregation state. Among these, size directly influences tumor penetration capability and cellular uptake efficiency, while shape exerts a significant impact on cellular interactions and clearance kinetics.18,123 However, current laboratory-scale synthesis methods generally suffer from inadequate reproducibility and pronounced batch-to-batch variability, and there is a lack of standardized Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant processes that can deliver the large-scale production with high batch-to-batch consistency required for clinical applications.124,125

Advanced Solutions and Future Directions

Precision Targeting and Smart Delivery Strategies

Surface functionalization primarily involves the covalent conjugation of PEG onto the surface of MNPs to achieve passive immune evasion, effectively reducing uptake by the MPS and significantly prolonging their blood circulation half-life. Building on this, the further conjugation of tumor-specific ligands can activate active targeting mechanisms, enhancing the specific binding and internalization efficiency of nanoparticles within tumor cells.126,127 At the drug delivery level, leveraging physical field guidance and stimuli-responsive release principles, external magnetic fields can drive nanoparticle enrichment at the tumor region, or ultrasound can facilitate their penetration across tumor barriers; simultaneously, responsive nanosystems can be constructed to achieve “on-demand” drug release at the target site, thereby improving the therapeutic index and reducing off-target side effects.128–131 In the realm of biomimetic nanotechnology, the strategy of coating nanoparticles with natural cell membranes has garnered widespread attention in recent years. Through such biomimetic modification, nanoparticles can effectively evade immune recognition and prolong in vivo circulation time. Researchers have employed membranes derived from both normal and tumor cells, with both sources demonstrating enhanced protein corona shielding capacity and attenuated phagocytic clearance.132,133 Notably, the first nanomaterial to achieve photothermal conversion functionality through an erythrocyte membrane coating has been reported, a development that has opened a new antitumor direction for near-infrared-responsive nanomaterials in PTT based on cell membrane drug delivery systems.134

Biosafety Optimization and Controlled Metabolic Design

In terms of material selection and structural design, priority should be given to metallic components that are inherently biocompatible or possess specific biological functions. For non-degradable MNPs, they can be fabricated as ultrasmall nanoclusters with a hydrodynamic diameter of less than 5 nm, which can achieve rapid systemic clearance via the glomerular filtration pathway, thereby circumventing the risk of long-term organ retention.135–137 For biodegradable MNPs, the research focus lies in constructing responsive degradation systems. For instance, porous silicon/gold composite nanorods or manganese-doped iron oxide nanoparticles can decompose in a predetermined and programmable manner within the acidic lysosomal microenvironment and, after fulfilling their energy-sensitization and theranostic imaging functions, can be converted into non-toxic small molecules or ions for safe elimination through biological excretory pathways.138

Standardization and Construction of Synergistic Therapeutic Platforms

Regarding the systematic manufacturing workflow and quality control system, a standardized production process should be established based on the Quality by Design (QbD) principle, using the Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP) as a blueprint. Through Design of Experiments (DoE), the quantitative relationships among critical material attributes (CMAs), critical process parameters (CPPs), and critical quality attributes (CQAs) can be defined, and automated control of the synthesis process can be implemented to precisely regulate key parameters such as the size distribution, crystal facet structure, and surface functionalization of MNPs, thereby ensuring high batch-to-batch consistency. On this basis, a combination of characterization techniques, including dynamic light scattering (DLS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), can be employed to conduct comprehensive quantitative assessments of the physicochemical properties of MNPs, providing robust support for quality control.139

In the development of multifunctional synergistic therapeutic platforms, MNPs should not serve merely as standalone radiosensitizers; rather, they should be integrated as the core component of a multifunctional theranostic system to achieve efficient synergy among multiple functions.140 For example, radiotherapy/PDT enhancement capabilities, targeted drug delivery, immunoadjuvant loading, and multimodal imaging (CT, magnetic resonance imaging, and photoacoustic imaging) can all be integrated into a single nanoplatform.141 Such platforms can sequentially trigger therapeutic cascades, induce systemic antitumor immune responses, and harness cross-modality synergistic effects to achieve therapeutic outcomes that surpass simple additive effects.

Conclusion and Outlook

As an emerging class of nano-radiosensitizers, MNPs have demonstrated increasingly significant potential in energy-based therapies for lung cancer. Their core value derives, first and foremost, from their unique physicochemical properties: the high atomic number (Z) endows MNPs with a robust capacity for radiation energy absorption, and by enhancing the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering, they generate abundant SEs and ROS locally within the tumor, forming “microdosimetric hotspots”. This effect can substantially augment the tumoricidal capacity of radiation while effectively sparing adjacent normal tissues. However, the functions of MNPs extend far beyond physical sensitization alone: surface modification enables active targeted delivery, and modulation of the TME can mediate CDT, thereby driving synergistic therapeutic effects at the physical, chemical, and biological levels. Although challenges remain in targeting efficiency, long-term biosafety, and large-scale standardized manufacturing, existing research has amply demonstrated that MNPs hold the potential to overcome the therapeutic bottlenecks of conventional radiotherapy and PDT, opening new prospects for the precision diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer (Figure 5). Precision Targeting and Intelligent Response

Overcoming the limitations of passive accumulation, multimodal responsive intelligent nanomedicines are being developed. By integrating multivalent targeting ligands, such as bispecific antibodies, with multimodal regulatory systems responsive to TME signals or external physical stimuli, a fully precision drug delivery process can be sequentially achieved, encompassing target recognition, tumor enrichment, deep penetration, and controlled release, thereby maximizing effective intratumoral concentration while minimizing off-target toxicity.

Synergistic Therapy and Immune Activation

A systematic synergistic therapeutic approach, rather than a single-modality therapy, is employed to develop an MNP-based theranostic platform. By integrating functions such as image-guided radiotherapy/PDT, controlled release of chemotherapeutic agents, and loading of immune adjuvants, tumor antigens are released in the context of local tumor cell killing, thereby stimulating systemic antitumor immune responses. The synergistic effects of cross-modality therapies overcome the challenges of tumor metastasis and recurrence while reducing the reliance on a single stimulus pathway.

Material Innovation and Deeper Mechanistic Insights

With a focus on biosafety, efforts are directed toward the development of novel MNPs that are biodegradable or capable of self-clearance. Concurrently, mechanistic investigations are being deepened, with emphasis on elucidating the interaction principles between MNPs and high-LET radiation (such as proton or carbon ion beams), for example, how the shell structural characteristics of dual MNPs influence their sensitization efficacy, in order to achieve breakthroughs in targeting precision at the subcellular level.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Clinical Translation

Whether the above-mentioned technological innovations can truly benefit lung cancer patients will depend on concurrent breakthroughs in regulatory science. Owing to their multicomponent and multifunctional nature, inorganic nanoparticles often fall into a “combination product” classification dilemma within the current regulatory frameworks of the FDA and NMPA, not fitting neatly into the category of conventional drugs, nor are they readily classifiable as routine medical devices. This classification ambiguity directly leads to extreme difficulty in defining chemistry, manufacturing, and control (CMC) standards. Regulatory agencies may require the establishment of a comprehensive quality control system encompassing dozens of parameters, including nanoparticle size distribution, crystalline form, surface ligand density, ion release kinetics, and batch-to-batch consistency, the complexity of which far exceeds that for small molecules or antibody drugs. At the clinical evaluation end, physical or biological surrogate endpoints such as the SER or DEF are not yet accepted as decisive evidence for regulatory approval; trials must be designed as randomized controlled studies with progression-free survival or overall survival as endpoints, and at the same time, patient populations most likely to benefit from a specific nanoplatform must be identified, substantially raising the cost and timeline of clinical development. Even more challenging is that, in light of long-term tissue accumulation and delayed immunotoxicity, regulatory agencies may mandate extended long-term follow-up monitoring spanning several years, which poses a formidable test to the sponsor’s financial sustainability and trial feasibility. Therefore, it is imperative to adopt a “safety-by-design” philosophy at the very initial stage of nanoplatform design, incorporate degradable or clearable elements, and reach consensus with the FDA or NMPA through early engagement in regulatory communication on product classification, CMC requirements, and clinical trial endpoints. Only in this way, by integrating the multidisciplinary strengths of materials science, clinical oncology, and regulatory science, can a novel paradigm of highly effective, low-toxicity precision radiosensitization therapy for lung cancer ultimately be established.

In summary, this review has systematically delineated the multidimensional mechanisms by which MNPs sensitize radiotherapy and PDT in lung cancer at the physical, chemical, and biological levels, along with the clinical translational progress of agents such as AGuIX and NBTXR3. In addition, it has proposed forward-looking strategies including intelligent design, synergistic immunotherapy, and regulatory science to address the bottlenecks in delivery, safety, and standardization.
