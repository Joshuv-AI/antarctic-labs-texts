Propionic Acidemia in a Neonate: The Clues to Early Identification
Authors: Mithun Krishna, Arushi Gahlot Saini, Jogender Kumar, Savita V Attri, Kagnur Ramya, Vikas Bhatia
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

An 8-day-old male neonate, second born to nonconsanguineous parents, presented with poor feeding and progressive lethargy from day 2 of life and generalized seizures from day 7 of life. The perinatal period was uneventful, and breast feeds were initiated. By day 3 of life, he developed neonatal jaundice (maximum serum bilirubin 16 mg/dL) and required double-surface phototherapy. The mother’s blood group was O (negative), and she did not receive Rh immune globulin previously. Encephalopathy progressed slowly despite correction of hyperbilirubinemia and treatment for presumed sepsis. Family history was not contributory.

On examination, he was lethargic with decreased responsiveness to painful stimuli, generalized hypotonia, weak cry, poor suck, and diminished deep tendon reflexes. His respiration was regular and vital parameters were maintained. There was no organomegaly, abnormal hair or skin pigmentation, abnormal body odor, cataract, or dysmorphism in the baby, or myotonia in parents. A clinical diagnosis of neonatal sepsis/meningitis, acute bilirubin encephalopathy, or an inborn error of small-molecule metabolism was considered.

Investigations showed normal electrolytes, serum lactate, blood sugar, C-reactive protein, total serum bilirubin, pH, and acid–base parameters. He had hyperammonemia (511 µmol/L) without ketosis. Blood culture was sterile. Total leukocyte count was 2.9 cells/109 litres (L), which dropped to 1.1 × 109 cells/L; platelet count dropped from 139 × 109 cells/L to 19 × 109 cells/L. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed elevated protein (449 and 266 mg/dL), and the culture was sterile. Polymerase chain reaction for herpes and enterovirus was negative in CSF. CSF glycine was 18.1 µmol/L; CSF: plasma glycine ratio was 0.05 (normal).

He was empirically initiated on intravenous meropenem (40 mg/kg/day) and acyclovir (30 mg/kg/day) and oral sodium benzoate, which reduced the ammonia levels in the next 48 h (487 and 380 µmol/L). Feeds were withheld due to progressive encephalopathy requiring mechanical ventilation from day 11 of life. He was initiated on 10% dextrose infusion, oral carnitine, and other cofactor supplements. Seizures were controlled with phenobarbitone and levetiracetam. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed diffusion restriction in deep gray nuclei, medulla, and selective tracts [Figure 1]. Mass spectrometry showed elevated propionyl carnitine (C3), C3/C2 and C3/C16 ratio, suggestive of methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) or propionic acidemia (PA). Gas chromatography and mass spectrometry of urine showed massive excretion of lactic acid, 3-hydroxypropionic acid, and 3-hydroxyvaleric acid with moderate excretion of 2-hydroxybutyric acid and methyl citric acid suggestive of PA. Peritoneal dialysis was planned; however, the child succumbed to cardiac arrest on day 13 of life. Whole exome sequencing showed compound heterozygous deletions in PCCA gene on chromosome 13 [c.284del (p.Asp95ValfsTer9) on exon 4 that caused a frameshift and premature truncation of the protein, nine amino acids downstream to codon 95, which was classified as pathogenic, and another c.(231 + 1_232-1)_(300 + 1_301-1) del located on exon 3–4, which resulted in a contiguous deletion and was classified as likely pathogenic]. A final diagnosis of neonatal-onset PA was considered, and parental testing was offered.

Axial diffusion-weighted images (a-c) show restriction in the cerebellum and middle cerebellar peduncles (black arrows), midbrain (black arrows), basal ganglia (star), and insular cortex. Axial susceptibility-weighted image (d) shows microbleeds in the cerebellar hemispheres

Our case highlights the acute encephalopathic neonatal presentation of a rare organic acidemia. The absence of typical biochemical abnormalities in PA, such as high-anion gap metabolic acidosis, elevated lactate, and ketosis, delayed the identification. Absence of metabolic acidosis at presentation in PA has been seen in 16% cases in retrospective cohorts.[1] Alternatively, since the neonate presented to us in the second week of life after being admitted outside, it is plausible that some of the metabolic abnormalities were corrected or modified due to omission of feeds and initiation of intravenous fluids.

Clinically significant isolated hyperammonemia with encephalopathy may be seen in transient hyperammonemia of the newborn, hepatic failure, urea cycle defects, and maple syrup urine disease and may require treatment. These possibilities were also considered in the index case as there was absence of acidosis typical of a metabolic crisis in organic acidemia. Hyperammonaemia in organic acidemia is multifactorial and results from the interaction of the substrates proximal to the metabolic block with other biochemical pathways such as urea cycle and tricarboxylic acid cycle.[2] PA and MMA often present with more severe hyperammonemia during metabolic crises in the newborn compared to older children where metabolic acidosis dominates.[3]

The presence of a symptom-free interval after a normal birth is an important clinical clue towards the small-molecule disorders that start building up after birth. The early nonspecific symptoms of refusal to feed, lethargy, and hypotonia progressed to seizures, coma, and death, despite supportive care. These features resemble neonatal sepsis and lead to delay in initiation of detoxication measures in a worsening neonate. The presence of persistent leukopenia and thrombocytopenia in the absence of sepsis is an important hematologic clue to organic acidemia.[4] The first affirmation often comes from mass spectrometry and is confirmed by genetic testing as in the index case. Neuroimaging findings commonly described include bilaterally symmetrical basal ganglia signal changes with diffusion restriction, patchy cortical and subcortical diffusion restriction, delayed myelination, and later in life, a varying degree of cerebral atrophy.[56] A rare case report which included an infant with PA showed diffuse subcortical diffusion restriction and vermian atrophy.[7] While in our index child, MRI of the brain showed more diffuse changes compared to classical descriptions, with bilaterally symmetrical diffusion restriction involving the basal ganglia, thalami, brainstem, and optic tracts [Figure 1], which raised the suspicion of maple syrup urine disease as well before the results of mass spectrometry became available.[8] Elevated levels of glycine and lysine may be seen in blood, urine, and CSF, but the CSF/plasma glycine ratio is normal.[910] Specific management for PA includes administration of intravenous l-carnitine (100–200 mg/kg/day) and gut decontamination with metronidazole.[9] Carnitine in PA helps to conjugate various organic acids to readily excreted metabolites like propionyl carnitine.
