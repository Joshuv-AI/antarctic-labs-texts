Hematin and CuII control generations of hydroperoxyl and superoxide radicals to activate galactose oxidase for 5-hydroxymethylfurfural conversion
Authors: Yao Chen, Jinxin Zou, Peijun Ji, Wei Feng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a significant finding that Cu(II) ions can activate hematin (Hem) to generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals from the decomposition of H2O2. Galactose oxidase (GO) and hematin have been simultaneously immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) ions (GO&Hem@Cu(II)). The radicals HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and dioxygen O2 can be in situ generated from the byproduct H2O2 by the Cu(II)-activated hematin. Ample experimental evidence supports the discovery that the immobilized GO is reactivated by the in situ generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅-. For the conversion of 100 mM 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) in water, GO&Hem@Cu(II) (0.8 mg/mL GO encapsulated) has achieved a 99.5% conversion within 180 min. In contrast, 0.8 mg/mL free GO M3-5 variant (ACS Catalysis 2018, 8, 4025) has achieved an HMF conversion of 17.3%. For the conversion of HMF (1,000 mM) by GO&Hem@Cu(II) (4 mg/mL GO encapsulated), the HMF conversion is 98.8% after 8 h reaction.

Introduction

Hydroperoxyl radical (HOO⋅) is generally characterized as an oxidant.1 Superoxide anion radical (O2⋅-) is the conjugate base of HOO⋅; it can be both an oxidant and reductant.2,3 HOO⋅ can react with some chemicals through H-atom-transfer processes.4 O2⋅- can undergo proton transfer reactions.5 HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are reactive with electron-rich aromatic compounds;3 they are not reactive with glucose.6 HOO⋅ can be generated by radiolytic and photolytic approaches.7 O2⋅- can be generated by electrochemical, photocatalytic, and photochemical processes.8,9

5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) is a biomass-based platform chemical. 2,5-Diformylfuran (DFF) can be used to synthesize pharmaceuticals and biopolymers.10 Enzymes, such as HMF oxidase,11,12 chloroperoxidase,13 aryl-alcohol oxidase,14 laccases,15 and galactose oxidase (GO),16 have been used to oxidize HMF to DFF. Single enzyme GO has shown a low conversion of HMF to DFF.17 Three enzymes GO, catalase (CAT), and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) were together used for the oxidation of HMF. After 96 h reaction, the DFF yield is 92%.16 The three enzymes GO, CAT, and Mn-superoxide dismutase (SOD) were co-immobilized.18 For the conversion of 10 mM HMF, the immobilized enzymes GO&CAT&SOD@CuII (with encapsulated GO 0.2 mg/mL, CAT 0.02 mg/mL, SOD 0.06 mg/mL) achieved 97% HMF conversion within 2 h reaction. In contrast, the free GO (0.2 mg/mL) achieved 25.3% HMF conversion.

GO catalyzes the oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes,19,20,21 with the concomitant reduction of O2 to H2O2. Being a copper enzyme, GO is widely used in bioanalytical applications,22 for oxidation of sugars,23 polysaccharides,24 and alcohols.25 The active site of GO contains a cysteinylated tyrosine radical (Cys-Tyr)⋅ and a Cu2+ metal center.19,26 During oxidation of the substrate, the complex ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ (active) is reduced to ECu1+/(Cys-Tyr) (reduced).19 Through the 2e−1 O2 reduction half-reduction, O2 is reduced to H2O2, and ECu1+/(Cys-Tyr) is oxidized to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅.19,26 However, the (Cys-Tyr)⋅ radical can readily react with various electron donors, causing the formation of the ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) complex (inactive).19 The interconversion between ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ and ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) may lead to that GO exists in an inactive form.27,28,29 The byproduct H2O2 can be converted to O2 and H2O by CAT.30,31 Generating O2 facilitates the O2 reduction half-reaction and the oxidation of ECu1+/(Cys-Tyr) (reduced) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ (active).18,19,31 However, dioxygen O2 is a two-electron oxidant, and it is not an effector for the oxidizing ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) into ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅.18,32 Ferricyanide was used as a single-electron oxidant for GO reactivation.32 Chloroiridate and manganic ions have been used to reactivate GO by reforming ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ upon decay.32 GO has been used together with HRP; HRP was used to reactivate GO.33,34 The application of HRP is limited because it is expensive. Electrochemical activation is an approach for enhancing the GO activity.35 GO was immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) and Zr(IV).36 By decomposition of the byproduct H2O2 under the synergistic catalysis of Cu(II) and Zr(IV), the immobilized GO is highly efficiently activated by in situ generation of hydroxyl radicals in concert with in situ generation of O2.36 The conjugate GO@Cu(II)&Zr(IV) exhibited a high catalytic efficiency for the conversion of HMF (100 mM) to DFF.

Hematin is the hydroxylated hemin, and it is a Fe+3 compound. In previous work, we used hematin to immobilize D-amino acid oxidase (DAAO).37 The byproduct H2O2 of DAAO was decomposed by hematin. The work by Kalyanararnan et al. showed that hydroperoxyl (HOO⋅) and superoxide anion(O2⋅-) radicals can be generated from the decomposition of H2O2 by hematin.38 However, our experimental results showed that abundance of O2 was evolved, and HOO⋅ was generated, but not as much as expected. The generated dioxygen O2 was utilized to oxidize the reduced factor flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) of DAAO, and the catalytic efficiency of the immobilized DAAO was significantly improved.37 Enzyme immobilization has been shown to improve enzymatic activity.39,40,41,42,43,44 In this work, GO and hematin (Hem) are simultaneously immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), forming GO&Hem@Cu(II). The decomposition of H2O2 by GO&Hem@Cu(II) was investigated. This investigation led to a significant finding that Cu(II) ions can activate hematin to generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals, and less O2 is evolved. For GO&Hem@Cu(II), it will be uncovered that the in situ generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- reactivate the immobilized GO, by oxidizing the active site ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) (inactive) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ (active). Generations of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- from the decomposition of H2O2 by hematin+Cu(II)

Cu(II) ions were added to the solutions of hematin (0.3 mM) dissolving in the NaOH solution, and then H2O2 was added. Quantitative measurements of the generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals are difficult because of their short lifetime.45 By spectroscopically monitoring the absorbances at 502 nm (HOO⋅) and 532 nm (O2⋅-), the relative amounts of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- were determined. Among the systems hematin+Cu(II), hematin+Cu(II)0.5 generated the lowest amount of O2 and the highest amounts of HOO⋅ and O2⋅-. Mechanism reactions for the generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 by hematin

Hematin catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 through the following reactions.38(Equation 1a)hematin(Fe+3) + H2O2 → hematin(Fe+2) + HOO⋅ + H+(Equation 1b)hematin(Fe+2) + H2O2 → hematin(Fe+3) + ⋅OH + OH−

And autoxidation of hematin(Fe+2) will form superoxide radical O2⋅-.(Equation 1c)hematin(Fe+2) + O2 → hematin(Fe+3) + O2⋅-

The self-reaction of HOO⋅ can generate O2.46,47,48(Equation 1d)HOO⋅ + HOO⋅ → H2O2 + O2

The proton of HOO⋅ is readily deprotonated to form the conjugate base O2⋅- in aqueous solutions; i.e., HOO⋅ is in equilibrium with O2⋅- (Equation 1e).46,47,48 Thus, O2⋅- is concomitantly evolved with the generation of HOO⋅.(Equation 1e)HOO⋅ ⇌ O2⋅- + H+

The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrum (Figure S1A) demonstrated that HOO⋅ was generated when hematin catalyzed the decomposition of H2O2. The black lines for hematin in Figures 1A and 1B illustrate the characteristic absorption peaks at 502 nm for HOO⋅ and 532 nm for O2⋅-. The black line in Figure 1C is for the generation of dioxygen O2 by hematin. All the experimental results support the aforementioned mechanism reactions.

Superoxide anion radical O2⋅- can be evolved from the deprotonation of HOO⋅ (reaction 1e). The autooxidation of hematin(Fe+2) can also generate O2⋅- through reaction 1c. Then one question is which reaction is the major reaction for generating O2⋅-. If reaction 1c is the major reaction, consuming O2⋅- would facilitate the regeneration of hematin(Fe+3), and more HOO⋅ would be generated through reaction 1a. If reaction 1e is the major reaction, consuming O2⋅- can promote the deprotonation of HOO⋅, and less HOO⋅ will be generated. To answer this question, we used hematin together with SOD for the decomposition of H2O2. SOD is the enzyme catalyzing the dismutation of O2⋅- into H2O2. Figure S1B shows that, in comparison to hematin, hematin+SOD generated less HOO⋅. It can be concluded that O2⋅- is dominantly evolved from the deprotonation of HOO⋅ through reaction 1e. In comparison to reaction 1c, reaction 1b is a major reaction for regeneration of hematin(Fe+3). H2O2 has a priority over O2 for the oxidation of hematin(Fe+2) to hematin(Fe+3), as H2O2 is a stronger oxidant than O2. Hence, for the decomposition of H2O2 by hematin, reaction 1a is mainly combined with reaction 1b to generate HOO⋅.

Discovery of the mechanism of the activation of hematin by Cu(II)

The systems Hematin+Cu(II) have been shown to generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- than hematin (Figures 1A and 1B). The mechanism of the activation of hematin by Cu(II) will be interesting. A hematin molecule has four pyrrole nitrogens, one hydroxyl group, and two carboxyl groups (Figure 2). Attributed to the coordination facility of the four pyrrole nitrogens,49 Cu(II) ions can have coordination interactions with the pyrrole nitrogens, as confirmed by the density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Figure 2). Cu(II) can also interact with the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. All these interactions contribute to the formation of the Cu(II)-hematin complex. In the systems hematin+Cu(II), some Cu(II) ions have coordination interactions with the pyrrole nitrogens of hematin; these Cu(II) ions are designated as Cu(II)h. Other Cu(II) ions that have no coordination interactions with the pyrrole nitrogens are designated as Cu(II)u. The Cu(II)u ions include the Cu(II) ions that interact with the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups and the free Cu(II) ions that have no interactions with hematin.Figure 2Coordination interactions of the pyrrole nitrogens of hematin with Cu(II)h

Coordination interactions of the pyrrole nitrogens of hematin with Cu(II)h

When hematin(Fe+3) catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2, hematin(Fe+3) is reduced to hematin(Fe+2) (reaction 1a). As Cu(II)h is proximal to the ferric ion of hematin(Fe+2), Cu(II)h can oxidize hematin(Fe+2) to hematin(Fe+3). It is similar to the process by which Cu+2 can oxidize Fe+2 to Fe+3 through the reaction Cu+2+ Fe+2 →Cu+1+ Fe+3.50,51 Thus, we can have the following reaction for the oxidation of hematin(Fe+2) by Cu(II)h.(Equation 1f)Cu(II)h + hematin(Fe+2) → hematin(Fe+3) + Cu(I)h

We performed a separate experiment to investigate whether reaction 1f can occur. Hematin(Fe+3)@Zn(II) was prepared by the coordination of hematin(Fe+3) (0.3 mM) with Zn(II) (40 mM) in PBS. Hematin(Fe+3)@Zn(II) in tube A exhibited a dark color (Figure 3A). The dark color is attributed to the fact that the coordinated hematin is in the state of hematin(Fe+3). Then H2O2 (220 mM) was added to tube A, in which the concentration of the encapsulated hematin was 0.24 mM. There were bubbles rising at 5 and 10 min as shown in tube A, attributed to the generated O2. After incubation for 20 min, the color of hematin@Zn(II) in tube A was changed from dark to light gray. In addition, the EPR spectrum of hematin@Zn(II) (Figure 3B) confirmed that HOO⋅ was generated from the decomposition of H2O2. The color change and the EPR spectrum indicate that the coordinated hematin(Fe+3) was reduced to hematin(Fe+2) through reaction 1a. Figure 3C shows that most Fe of hematin is in the state Fe+2, confirming reaction 1a. The supernatant of tube A was removed, and the precipitate hematin(Fe+2)@Zn(II) was washed with deionized water three times. Then the precipitate was collected and added to tube B containing deionized water (Figure 3D), followed by addition of the CuSO4 solution (80 mM). The color of the precipitate in tube B was changed from light gray to dark. It is indicated that the coordinated hematin(Fe+2) was oxidized back to hematin(Fe+3) by the Cu(II) ions. Figure 3E shows that most Fe of hematin is in the state Fe+3, confirming reaction 1f. The oxidation of hematin(Fe+2) to hematin(Fe+3) contributes to that the generation of HOO⋅ can be sustained through reaction 1a. Through both reactions 1b and 1f, hematin(Fe+2) can be oxidized to hematin(Fe+3). Then the questions arise: do reaction 1b occurs parallel to reaction 1f? Otherwise, which reaction is the major one? Reaction 1b suggests that hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) would be generated. The ⋅OH generation was monitored for hematin+Cu(II)0.5 by measuring EPR spectrum. The spin-trapping agent was 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO). DMPO itself remains EPR silent until adduct formation.52 There are no signals representing DMPO-⋅OH after 20 min reaction, indicating that ⋅OH could not be detectable (Figure S2A). In contrast, the characteristic peaks for DMPO-HOO⋅ appeared after 20 min reaction, indicating that HOO⋅ was generated (Figure S2B). On the basis of above experimental results, we can conclude that, in comparison to reaction 1b, reaction 1f is the major reaction. For accelerating the cycle of hematin(Fe+3) →hematin(Fe+2) →hematin(Fe+3), the combination of reactions 1a and 1f is advantageous over the combination of reactions 1a and 1b, as Cu(II) ion is a stronger oxidant than H2O2. Reaction 1a is promoted by reaction 1f to generate more HOO⋅. The activation mechanism can explain that hematin+Cu(II) can generate much more HOO⋅ than hematin (Figure 1A).

The activation of hematin by Cu(II) was further investigated through DFT calculations, as illustrated in Scheme 2. Figure S3 shows the corresponding structures. The calculations were started with initial structure 1 (IN1). After addition of Cu(II), the Cu(II) ion (Cu(II)h) interacts with the two pyrrole nitrogens and the ferric ion (initial structure 2 IN2). Upon receiving H2O2, it has a hydrogen bonding interaction with the hydroxyl group of hematin (reaction complex structure RC). The hydrogen atom of H2O2 moves toward the oxygen atom of the hydroxyl group (transition state structure TS1). Finally, the hydrogen atom is bonded to the oxygen atom of the hydroxyl group, and a water molecule is formed (intermediate structure IM1). In the meantime, an electron is transferred from the oxygen of HOO− to the ferric ion, HOO⋅ is formed, and the ferric ion Fe+3 is reduced to ferrous ion Fe+2 (intermediate structure IM1). Intermediate structure 2 (IM2) is the structure after releasing H2O and HOO⋅. Upon receiving a hydroxyl group from the solution, the hydroxyl group is bonded to the iron; in the meantime, Cu(II)h oxidizes the ferrous iron Fe+2 to ferric ion Fe+3 (structure IM3), and Cu(II)h is reduced to Cu(I)h. A dioxygen O2 can automatically bind to Cu(I)h (intermediate structure IM4) and oxidize Cu(I)h to Cu(II)h, in the meantime releasing a superoxide radical O2⋅-. The consumption of O2 has a contribution to the generation of O2⋅-. As O2⋅- is the conjugate base of HOO⋅, the consumption of O2 also has a contribution to the generation of HOO⋅. Mechanism of controlling the generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 by Cu(II)

Once HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are generated, a portion of the generated radicals can react with the coordinated Cu(I)h through reactions 1g and 1h.46,47,48(Equation 1g)Cu(I)h + HOO⋅ + H+ → H2O2 + Cu(II)h(Equation 1h)Cu(I)h + O2⋅- + 2H2O →Cu(II)h + H2O2 + 2OH−

These two reactions neither lead to the net decrease of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- nor lead to the net increase of HOO⋅ and O2⋅-, as the generated H2O2 can be used again as a source for generating HOO⋅ through reactions 1a and 1f. Thus, in Scheme 2 the steps of oxidation of Cu(I)h by HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are not included. Both reactions 1g and 1h consume HOO⋅. These two reactions are likely competitive with the self-reaction of HOO⋅ (reaction 1d), which consumes HOO⋅ to generate O2. When reacting with the radicals, the coordinated Cu(I)h can be assumed to possess the similar catalytic behavior as that of the free Cu+1 ion. The reaction rates46,47,48 for the reactions of Cu+1 with HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are utilized herein to represent the reaction rates for reactions 1g and 1h. They are >109 M−1S−1 for reaction 1g and ∼1010 M−1S−1 for reaction 1h. Dioxygen O2 can be generated from the self-reaction of HOO⋅ (Equation 1d) with a reaction rate 6.7x105 M−1S−1.46,47,48 Based on the representative reaction rates, it can be concluded that reactions 1g and 1h have priorities over reaction 1d. Through reactions 1g and 1h, HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are reduced to H2O2, which is used again as a source for generating HOO⋅ through reaction 1a. It is equivalent to that generating HOO⋅ has a priority over generating O2. This may contribute to that hematin+Cu(II) can generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and less O2 than hematin.

Among the systems hematin+Cu(II), hematin+Cu(II)0.5 has been shown to be able to generate the highest amounts of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and lowest amount of O2. When more Cu(II) ions are used, the amounts of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are decreased and the amount of O2 is increased. It is ascribed to that, when more Cu(II) ions are used in hematin(0.3 mM)+Cu(II), the amount of Cu(II)u is increased. Cu(II)h and Cu(II)u have opposite functions for the generation of HOO⋅. Cu(II)h activates hematin to generate more HOO⋅ (reactions 1a and 1f), while Cu(II)u consumes HOO⋅ and O2⋅- to generate O2 through reactions 1i and 1j.46,47,48 When more Cu(II)u are present, more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are consumed by Cu(II)u to generate more O2. This is the reason that the generation of O2 is opposite to the generations of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- for the systems hematin+Cu(II). Cu(I)u can be reoxidized to Cu(II)u through reactions 1k and 1l.46,47,48 Reactions 1i and 1j cause the decrease in the generations of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and the increase in the generation of O2, while reactions 1k and 1l do not lead the net decrease of HOO⋅ and O2⋅-, as the generated H2O2 can be used as a source for generating HOO⋅ through reactions 1a and 1f.(Equation 1i)Cu(II)u + O2⋅- → Cu(I)u + O2(Equation 1j)Cu(II)u + HOO⋅ → Cu(I)u + O2 + H+(Equation 1k)Cu(I)u + HOO⋅ + H+ → H2O2 + Cu(II)u(Equation 1l)Cu(I)u + O2⋅- + 2H2O →Cu(II)u + H2O2 + 2OH−

Encapsulation of GO and hematin by coordinating to Cu(II) in PBS forming GO&Hem@Cu(II)

Direct application of hematin is of a significant challenge because of its poor solubility in water.53 Cu(II) ions can form complexes with the hematin molecules, through the interactions with the pyrrole nitrogens and the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. Cu(II) ions can coordinate to PO4−3 forming copper phosphate.54 Enzymes can coordinate to Cu(II).36 By taking advantage of these coordination interactions, GO (0.05 mM) and hematin (0.3 mM) were simultaneously immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) in PBS. The initial concentrations of Cu(II) were 0.5, 1.5, 3, 6, and 8 mM, and the obtained hybrids are designated as GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II), GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, GO&Hem@Cu(II)3, GO&Hem@Cu(II)6, and GO&Hem@Cu(II)8, respectively. For preparing GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II), Cu(II) together with Zn(II) were used in order to get high encapsulation yields for GO and hematin. The hybrids exhibited nanoflower-like structures as shown in Figure 4. Figure S4 shows the mapping images and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) spectra. It is revealed that the elements Cu, Fe, and P are uniformly distributed in the hybrids. To observe the immobilized enzyme under microscopy, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FI) was used to label GO, and FIGO and hematin were simultaneously immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) in PBS, forming FIGO&Hem@Cu(II). The hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II) exhibit nanoflower structures (Figure 4). The nanoflower structures are formed due to the coordination of Cu(II) with PO43−, as confirmed by the scanning electron microscope (SEM) image for Cu3(PO4)2 (Figure S6). The hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II) were prepared by simultaneously encapsulating GO and hematin using Cu(II) in PBS. The Cu(II) ions coordinate to phosphate, hematin, and GO. It is speculated that when Cu(II) ions with a higher concentration are used for preparing the hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II), there will be more Cu(II)h, the Cu(II) ions that coordinate to the pyrrole nitrogens of hematin. There will be more Cu(II)u as well, the Cu(II) ions that are not coordinated to the pyrrole nitrogens of hematin, but they are coordinated to PO43− and GO. Some Cu(II)u may have been coordinately saturated by PO43− and GO, and they are not able to react with HOO⋅ and O2⋅- to generate O2 though reactions 1i and 1j. These are the reasons that the generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 have the sequences as demonstrated in Figure 5. Specifically, GO&Hem@Cu(II)8 generated more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and less O2 than GO&Hem@Cu(II)6.

The hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II) contain Cu(II) ions that are coordinated to GO and hematin. When catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2, potentially, hydroxyl radicals ⋅OH would be generated through the reaction of the coordinated Cu(II) with H2O2.36 The generations of ⋅OH and HOO⋅ were detected by measuring EPR spectra as shown in Figures 6A and 6B, respectively. It is indicated that ⋅OH could not be detectable (Figure 6A) and HOO⋅ was generated (Figure 6B). To investigate the reason of not-generating ⋅OH, we measured the capabilities of decomposing H2O2 by the coordinated hematin and the coordinated Cu(II). GO&Hem@Zn(II) was used as the coordinated hematin. The hybrids GO@Cu(II)1.5, GO@Cu(II)3, GO@Cu(II)6, and GO@Cu(II)8 represent the coordinated Cu(II). In Figure 7A, the HMF conversion by GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5 (control) was presented for comparison. GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5 was prepared by simultaneous immobilization of GO (0.05 mg/mL) and CAT (0.012 mg/mL) using Cu(II) ions (1.5 mM) in PBS. GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5 has shown a comparable capability of generating O2 with GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 (Figure S8). Dioxygen O2 is a co-substrate of the GO catalysis; it is reduced to H2O2 through the O2 reduction half-reaction.19 Obviously, generating O2 facilitates the reduction of O2 to H2O2 and hence can improve the catalytic efficiency.20 However, the control hybrid GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5 has shown a much lower catalytic efficiency than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 (Figures 7A and 7B). There must be some reasons behind the results. The significant difference between the two hybrids is that GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 can generate HOO⋅ and O2⋅- from decomposing H2O2, while GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5 cannot. Then one question naturally arises: whether the high catalytic efficiency by GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 is ascribed to the direct oxidation of HMF by the in situ generated radicals HOO⋅ and O2⋅-. To answer this question, the control hybrid DAAO&Hem@Cu(II)8 was prepared, by the simultaneous immobilization of DAAO (0.05 mg/mL) and hematin (0.3 mM) using Cu(II) ions (8 mM) in PBS. DAAO&Hem@Cu(II)8 has shown a comparable capability of generating HOO⋅ and O2⋅- with GO&Hem@Cu(II)8 as shown in Figure S9. It can generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5. DAAO&Hem@Cu(II)8 was used for the conversion of HMF (5 mM) in deionized water, with 8 mM H2O2 added. After incubation for 5 h at 37°C, the HMF concentration was monitored to be 4.94 mM. The control experiment demonstrated that HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are not reactive with HMF. HOO⋅ and O2⋅- are reactive with electron-rich aromatic compounds,3 and they are not reactive with glucose.6 The results by GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, GO&CAT@Cu(II)1.5, and DAAO&Hem@Cu(II)8 support that the in situ generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- from the decomposition of the byproduct H2O2 reactivate the encapsulated GO.

GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II) can generate more O2 than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, while GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II) has been shown with a lower catalytic efficiency than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5. It is ascribed to that GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II) exhibited lower capability of generating HOO⋅ and O2⋅- than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 (Figure 5). Based on the results of GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II) and GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, it seems that generating more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- is favorable for getting a higher catalytic efficiency. However, the three hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II)3, GO&Hem@Cu(II)6, and GO&Hem@Cu(II)8 have exhibited a different sequence of catalytic efficiency. The capability of generating HOO⋅ and O2⋅- is in the sequence GO&Hem@Cu(II)3< GO&Hem@Cu(II)6 < GO&Hem@Cu(II)8, while the catalytic efficiency has an opposite sequence, that is, GO&Hem@Cu(II)3 > GO&Hem@Cu(II)6 > GO&Hem@Cu(II)8 (Figures 7A and 7B). This sequence is the same as that of the capability of generating O2 (Figure 5C). In addition, the three hybrids can generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5. However, they have shown a lower catalytic efficiency than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5.

The catalytic results have demonstrated that the in situ generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- reactivate the encapsulated GO. However, just pursuing the generations of more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- cannot get a desired catalytic efficiency. Dioxygen O2 is the co-substrate of GO; generating O2 facilitates the reduction of O2 to H2O2. The in situ generating HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and the in situ generating O2 should be balanced. This can be achieved through controlling the generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 by the coordinated Cu(II) as shown in Figure 5. For example, GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 can generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and less O2 than GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II), and it can generate less HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and more dioxygen O2 than GO&Hem@Cu(II)8. Ascribing to the appropriate generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2, GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 has shown a larger catalytic efficiency than both GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II) and GO&Hem@Cu(II)8. The hybrid GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 has been used to oxidize HMF in a larger scale. Figure 7C shows the result for the conversion of HMF (100 mM) by GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 (with 0.8 mg/mL GO encapsulated). The gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum (Figure S10) show that DFF is the product. The two peaks in the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) spectra represent HMF and DFF, respectively (Figure S11). The HMF conversion is 99.5% after 180 min reaction. In contrast, free GO (0.8 mg/mL) exhibits an HMF conversion 17.3% after 180 min reaction (Figure 7C), and the HPLC spectra are shown in Figure S12. Figure 7C shows the result for the conversion of HMF (1,000 mM) by GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 (with encapsulated GO 4 mg/mL), and the HMF conversion is 98.8% after 8 h reaction.

Mechanism of the oxidation of ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ by HOO⋅

Quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) calculations were performed for investigating the mechanism of the HOO⋅ oxidation. The active site initial structure (IN) is for the inactive GO, and the optimized structure is shown in Figure S13A. When HOO⋅ enters into the active site, it binds to the copper automatically. Figure S14B shows the spin density distribution, indicating that the radical transfer does not occur with HOO⋅ binding to the copper. This means that HOO⋅ cannot immediately oxidize Tyr272 when binding to the copper. When HOO⋅ receives a proton from the environment, an electron is transferred from Tyr272 to HOO⋅, and H2O2 is formed (Figure S13C). The spin density distributions (Figures S14C and S14D) demonstrate that there is a radical on Tyr272 after HOO⋅ receives a proton. The mechanism of the O2⋅- oxidation of ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ was also investigated as shown in Scheme S1. The QM/MM calculations have demonstrated that both HOO⋅ and O2⋅- can oxidize ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅, supporting the experimental evidence of the reactivation by HOO⋅ and O2⋅-. Two extra protons from the solutions are needed for the O2⋅- oxidation in one overall cycle, while the HOO⋅ oxidation needs one extra proton, and HOO⋅ is a stronger oxidant than O2⋅-. Scheme 3B illustrates the reactions when GO&Hem@Cu(II) performs the catalysis. Through the non-enzymic reactions, HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 are generated from the decomposition of the byproduct H2O2. In the enzymic reactions, through the 1e− reductions of HOO⋅ and O2⋅- to H2O2, respectively, the active site ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) (inactive) is oxidized to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅ (active). The oxidation of substrate is coupled to the 2e− reduction of O2 to H2O2. The generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 can be sustained through the conversion cycle H2O2-to-(HOO⋅+O2⋅- + O2)-to-H2O2-to-(HOO⋅+O2⋅- + O2). Thus, the immobilized GO can be efficiently and persistently reactivated.

Discussion

Hematin can decompose H2O2, while the byproduct of GO is H2O2. The initial intention is that hematin is used together with GO to decompose the byproduct H2O2 and hence promote the catalysis of GO. Hematin and GO were co-immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) in PBS. During the experiments, we occasionally found that the Cu(II) ions can activate hematin to generate more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals from the decomposition of H2O2. The reactivation of GO by the HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals was discovered. The enzyme GO needs reactivation during its catalysis. Ferricyanide,32 chloroiridate,32 manganic ion,32 and HRP33,34 have been investigated for reactivating GO. In this work, we have shown a distinct way to reactivate GO. The byproduct H2O2 of GO is decomposed by hematin+Cu(II) to generate HOO⋅ and O2⋅-, and the radicals are utilized to reactivate GO. The generation of the HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals can be controlled by adjusting the ratio of hematin to Cu(II); thus, the reactivation of GO can be regulated and controlled. After the reactivation, the HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals are reduced to H2O2; thus, the immobilized GO can be persistently reactivated. The control experiments have shown that the high catalytic efficiency by GO&Hem@Cu(II) is not attributed to the potential direct oxidation of HMF by HOO⋅ and O2⋅-.

In our previous work,36 GO was immobilized by coordinating to Cu(II) and Zr(IV), forming GO@Cu(II)&Zr(IV). Under the synergistic catalysis of Cu(II) and Zr(IV), the in situ generation of hydroxyl radicals ⋅OH is in concert with the in situ generation of O2 from the byproduct H2O2. The generated ⋅OH oxidizes the active site ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅, and ⋅OH is reduced to H2O. The generated O2 is reduced to H2O2, which is recycled as a source to generate ⋅OH and O2. In this work, the radicals HOO⋅ and O2⋅- and dioxygen O2 are in situ generated from the byproduct H2O2 by the Cu(II)-activated hematin. HOO⋅ and O2⋅- oxidize ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr) to ECu2+/(Cys-Tyr)⋅, and they are reduced to H2O2; the H2O2 is recycled as one source to produce HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2. The H2O2 generated from the reduction of O2 is another source to produce HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2. For GO@Cu(II)&Zr(IV), the generations of ⋅OH and O2 are sustained through the conversion of O2-to-H2O2-to-(⋅OH + O2) by iterative use of O2. For GO&Hem@Cu(II), the generations of HOO⋅, O2⋅-, and O2 are sustained through the conversion cycle H2O2-to-(HOO⋅+O2⋅- + O2)-to-H2O2-to-(HOO⋅+O2⋅- + O2) by recycling H2O2.

To address the concern that the generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals would damage the encapsulated GO, a control experiment was performed. GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 was dispersed in water, and then free hematin (0.6 mM) and Cu(II) (1.0 mM) were added, followed by addition of H2O2 (10 mM). The amount of added free hematin 0.6 mM is much more than the encapsulated hematin (0.3 mM) in GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5. The concentration of added H2O2 (10 mM) is much higher than the instantaneous H2O2 concentration (0.54 mM min−1) during the conversion of HMF (100 mM). The control system (GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 + hematin0.6+Cu(II)1.0) can generate much more HOO⋅ and O2⋅- than GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5. After 10 min incubation, GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 was recovered through centrifugation, followed by washing with deionized water three times. The recovered GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 was used for the conversion of HMF (100 mM). There is little change of the HMF conversion after 180 min reaction, in comparison to the result in Figure 7. It is indicated that the generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- cannot damage the encapsulated GO.

Limitations of the study

Experimental evidence and QM/MM studies have proved that the in situ generated HOO⋅ and O2⋅- radicals reactivate the immobilized GO. It is speculated that HOO⋅ is advantageous over O2⋅- for the reactivation of GO. However, we cannot determine experimentally that HOO⋅ has played a dominant role in reactivating the enzyme, as O2⋅- can be concomitantly evolved with the generation of HOO⋅ (HOO⋅ ⇌ O2⋅- + H+). For the systems hematin+Cu(II), the Cu(II) concentrations are 0, 0.5, 1.5, 3, 6, and 8 mM. Considering that the hematin concentration is 0.3 mM, we did not investigate the effect of Cu(II) with concentrations between 0–0.5 mM.

Method details

Chemicals

Hematin, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF), 2′,7′-Dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), CuSO4, dimethyl sulfoxide, and Zinc nitrate were obtained from Sinopharm and Sigma-Aldrich.

Expression and purification of galactose oxidase

Galactose oxidase was prepared as described in our previous work.36 Briefly, the gene of galactose oxidase (M3-5 variant, ACS Catalysis 2018, 8, 4025) was amplified by PCR using Taq DNA polymerase. The PCR primers are 5`-CTCAAGCTTCTGAGTAACGCGAATCGTCG-3` (forward) and 5`-TATGGA TCCGCCTCAGCACCTATCGGA-3` (reverse). The DNA extraction kit (Omega Bio-tek) was used to recover the amplified fragment, it was digested with the BamH I and Hind III restriction endonucleases. Then the fragment was ligated into the vector pET28a using T4 ligase. The constructed plasmid pET28a-GO was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells, which are used for cloning and amplification. The competent cells Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) with pET28a-GO were cultured in LB medium (50 μg/mL kanamycin) at 37°C. When the OD600 reached around 0.5, isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (0.5 mM) was added. To express GO, the incubation was continued additional 20 h at 20°C under shaking (180 rpm). The cells were recovered through centrifugation at 5500 rpm at 4°C. The cells were disrupted using ultrasonic treatment in an ice-water mixture. Through the centrifugation at 6000 rpm at 4°C, the cell debris were removed. The supernatant was collected to a fresh tube. The expressed galactose oxidase was purified using Ni Sepharose beads (GE Life Sciences), the elution buffer (pH 8.0) contained 50 mM Tris-Cl, 300 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole. The imidazole was removed by dialysis into a buffer, containing 500 mM NaCl and 50 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5). Galactose oxidase was finally purified using AKTA Pure chromatography system (GE Healthcare) on a Superose 6 column. Other enzymes including catalase (CAT),12 superoxide dismutase (SOD),56 and D-amino acid oxidase37 were prepared as described in our previous work.

Preparation of GO&Hem@Cu(II)

120 μL of hematin solution (10 mM) in dimethyl sulfoxide, 80 μL of GO solution (2.5 mg/mL) in water, and 400 μL of CuSO4 solutions of various concentrations were added to 3.4 mL of PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4). The final concentrations of hematin were 0.3 mM, and the final concentrations of Cu(II) were 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 3, 6, and 8 mM. The incubations were carried out at 25°C for 24 h, forming the hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5Zn(II), GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, GO&Hem@Cu(II)3, GO&Hem@Cu(II)6, and GO&Hem@Cu(II)8. For the preparation of GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5Zn(II), in order to get high encapsulation yields for hematin and GO, the solution of Zinc nitrate was used, the final concentration of Zn(II) was 40 mM.

The precipitates were collected after centrifugation at 5000 rpm at 4°C for 12 min, and the supernatant was also collected. The precipitate was washed with distilled water and the washing was carried out in triplicate. The micro bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay was used to determine the enzyme concentration.57 The GO concentrations in the supernatants and washing solutions were determined, and then the amount of immobilized GO were finally determined. For GO&Hem@Cu(II)0.5&Zn(II), GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5, GO&Hem@Cu(II)3, GO&Hem@Cu(II)6, and GO&Hem@Cu(II)8, the enzyme immobilization yields are 96.3±3.5%, 96.7±3.1%, 97.0±2.6%, 97.8±2.0%, 98.2±1.5%, respectively. The immobilization yields were the average values based on triplicate measurements.

The amount of hematin was determined spectrophotometrically at a 402 nm wavelength. The measurements for standard hematin are shown in Figure SH1. The hematin concentrations in the supernatants and washing solutions were measured, and then the immobilization yields of hematin were determined, they are larger than 98.5%.

Spectrophotometrical determination of HOO⋅ generation

The DCFH solution was prepared as described in the article.58 Briefly, 4 mg of DCFH-DA was dissolved in 0.5 mL of methanol. Then the solution of DCFH-DA in methanol was mixed with 2.0 mL of NaOH (10 mM) and incubated at 25°C for 30 min. Then 10 mL of NaH2PO4 (25 mM, pH 7.4) was use to neutralize the mixture. The obtained solution was kept on ice in the dark ready for use.

HOO⋅ generation by hematin+Cu(II)

30 μL of hematin solution (10 mM) in NaOH (0.1 N) was added to 1 mL of Tris-Hcl (150 mM, pH 7.4). Then 100 μL of the CuSO4 solutions with various concentrations were added. The final concentrations of hematin in the solutions of hematin+Cu(II) were 0.3 mM, and final concentrations of Cu(II) were 0.5, 1.5, 3.0, 6.0, and 8.0 mM, respectively. These systems are designated as hematin+Cu(II)0.5, hematin+Cu(II)1.5, hematin+Cu(II)3, hematin+Cu(II)6, and hematin+Cu(II)8. H2O2 was added to the solutions of hematin+Cu(II), the final concentration of H2O2 was 40 μM. After 5 min reaction, 500 μL of the solution of Zinc nitrate (80 mM) was added to precipitate hematin. Through centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and then 180 μL of the DCFH solution was added to the supernatant. After shaking for 30 S, the solution was subjected to the detection of HOO⋅ by measuring the UV-vis absorption at 502 nm on a Shimadzu UV-2550 spectrophotometer.

HOO⋅ generation by GO&Hem@Cu(II)

The hybrids GO&Hem@Cu(II) (0.3 mM hematin) was added to 1 mL of Tris-Hcl (150 mM, pH 7.4). Then H2O2 was added with a final concentration 40 μM. After 5 min reaction, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Then 180 μL of the DCFH solution was added to the supernatant. After shaking for 30 S, the solution was subjected to the detection of HOO⋅ by measuring the UV-vis absorption at 502 nm.

Measurement of dioxygen (O2) concentration

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spin-trapping experiments

EPR for detection of ⋅OH radicals

GO&Hem@Cu(II) was added to 1 mL water (0.3 mM hematin), then H2O2 (2 mM) was added. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 30°C, then DMPO was added, the final concentration of DMPO was 0.85 mM. Biotransformations

GO&Hem@Cu(II)1.5 with encapsulated GO 4 mg/mL was used for the catalysis of the oxidation 1000 mM HMF at 37°C in 4 mL water. Preparation of FIGO&Hem@Cu(II) hybrids for confocal images

The collected FIGO&Hem@Cu(II)3 was washed with deionized water and subjected to imaging, using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus OLYMPUS FV1200).

GC-MS analysis

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations for the mechanism of the activation of hematin by Cu(II)

The calculation models comprise a hematin (HEM), a copper ion, and H2O2 or oxidant (O2). All calculations were performed in the Gaussian 09 program.59 The models were treated by density functional theory (DFT) with the B3LYP exchange–correlation functional method.60,61,62,63 Geometry optimizations were performed using a double-ζ Def2-SVP basis set (labeled B1).64 All minima were fully optimized without any symmetry restraints. The transition states (TSs) were determined by full TS optimizations. The energies were then corrected by single point calculations using a larger all-electron basis set, which is Def2-TZVP for all the atoms (labeled B2). Based on the ground-state electronic wave functions, the spin population analysis, spin density analysis, and charge analysis were performed using Multiwfn 3.6 program.65,66 Visualization was performed using PyMOL (PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Schrödinger, LLC).

Quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) calculations for the reactivation of GO by HOO⋅ and O2⋅-

System setup

The initial geometry of galactose oxidase was constructed from the X-ray crystal structure (PDB code: 2wq8). The protonation states of the titratable residues at pH 7.0 were determined using the protein pKa predictor PROPKA 3.0 online,67 by considering the neighboring hydrogen bond networks. All arginine (Arg) and lysine (Lys) residues existed in their protonated states, whereas all aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu) residues in their deprotonated states. All histidines (His) were in single protonated states and protonated at the Nδ1 position based on the local electrostatic environment, except for His40, which was in double protonated state. For the amino acid chain, the N-terminus was protonated, whereas the C-terminus was deprotonated. In the experiments, galactose oxidase (GO) and hematin were simultaneously encapsulated using Cu(II) in phosphate buffer saline. For the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, the initial system is composed of a GO, a Cu3(PO4)2 supercell, and 20 hematin. The crystallographic information file (cif) of the Cu3(PO4)2 crystal lattice structure was obtained from the crystallography open database (COD) (http://www.crystallography.net/cod/). The parameters for the Cu3(PO4)2 crystal lattice were a = 4.856Å, b = 5.291Å, c = 6.187Å, α= 72.363°, β = 86.997°, γ = 68.547°. The supercell model was generated by the Materials Studio software. The parameters for the Cu3(PO4)2 crystal model were 12.4 nm × 11.5 nm × 1.8 nm. The hematin structure was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/).

MD simulations

All classical molecular dynamics simulations were performed using AMBER99SB-ILDN force field68 with the GROMACS 2019.5 software package.69 The hematin was modeled by use of AMBER99SB-ILDN force field parameters derived from Becker et al.70 These parameters were supplemented to the existing parameters of AMBER99SB-ILDN release. The missing hydrogen atoms were added automatically. The TIP3P model was used for the solvent water molecules.71 The system was placed in a periodic truncated cubic dodecahedron boxer, and the closest distance between the surface of the boxer and the protein atom was set to 8 Å. The protein was hydrated using SPC216 water molecule, and then neutralized by adding ions (Na+ and Cl−) to generate 0.15 mol/L NaCl solution subsequently. The energy minimization was performed to relax the system using steepest descent and conjugate gradient algorithms. Then, the position restriction equilibrium simulations were conducted as following: 1) The minimized system was heated from 0 to 300 K at constant volume using the velocity-rescale thermostat in a NVT canonical ensemble for 100 ps. 2) The system was equilibrated in an NPT ensemble for 100 ps, employing the Berendsen barostat at the constant temperature of 300 K and pressure 1 bar. Isotropic pressure coupling was applied with a compressibility of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1. The protein and Cu1+ ion were restrained to the origin using harmonic potential of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 during the equilibrium simulation process. 3) A production MD run was performed for continuous 30 ns in an NPT ensemble with a target pressure of 1 bar and a pressure coupling constant of 2 ps. The Cu1+ ion and the residues coordinated to the Cu1+ ion were restrained using harmonic potential of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 during the production MD process. The periodic boundary conditions were employed in all the simulations, with time integration step size of 2.0 fs. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) summation algorithm72 with fast Fourier transform grid spacing of 1.6 Å and a cubic interpolation order of 4. Lennard-Jones and short-range Coulomb interaction cut off values were both 10 Å. During all simulations, the coordinates of the Cu3(PO4)2 crystal were fixed, while those of GO, hematin, and water were allowed to move freely. Frames were collected at an interval of 10 ps, and the production trajectories were analyzed using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD).73 Visualization was performed using PyMOL (PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Schrödinger, LLC).

QM/MM calculations

The initial structure of the QM/MM calculation was taken from the trajectory of the MD simulations. During the QM/MM calculations, the Cu3(PO4)2 supercell, hematin, and the solvent beyond 5 Å of the protein were not considered. All QM/MM calculations were performed using ONIOM74 implemented in the Gaussian 09 program.59 The QM region comprises a copper ion, the first coordination sphere of Cu (Tyr272, Tyr495, His496, His581, and hydroperoxyl radical HOO⋅ or superoxide radical O2⋅- ), Cys228, and Phe290. The remaining part of the system was regarded as the MM region. All amino acid residues and water molecules beyond 12 Å of the active site copper were frozen. An electronic embedding scheme75 was applied to include the polarizing effect of the enzyme environment on the QM region. Hydrogen link atoms76 with the charge shift model were employed to treat the QM/MM boundary. The AMBER force field was used for the MM region. The QM part was treated by density functional theory (DFT) with the B3LYP exchange–correlation functional method.59,60,61,62,63 The B3LYP functional, which is a density-functional theory (DFT) type of calculation based on hybrid functional, has been proved to provide a good balance between speed and accuracy for modeling the enzymatic reactions.77 Geometry optimizations were performed using a double-ζ Def2-SVP64 basis set (labeled B1). All minima were fully optimized without any symmetry restraints. The transition states (TSs) were determined by an initial potential energy surface (PES) scans followed by full TS optimizations. The energies were then corrected by single point calculations using a larger all-electron basis set, which is Def2-TZVP for all the atoms (labeled B2). Based on the ground-state electronic wave functions, the spin population analysis, spin density analysis, and charge analysis were performed using Multiwfn 3.6 program.65,66

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the results were the average of tri-measurements with standard error.
