Optimal Learning from Label Proportions with General Loss Functions
Lorne Applebaum, Travis Dick, Claudio Gentile, Haim Kaplan, Tomer Koren

Abstract
Motivated by problems in online advertising, we address the task of Learning from Label Proportions (LLP). In this partially-supervised setting, training data consists of groups of examples, termed bags , for which we only observe the average label value. The main goal, however, remains the design of a predictor for the labels of individual examples. We introduce a novel and versatile low-variance debiasing methodology to learn from aggregate label information, significantly advancing the state of the art in LLP. Our approach exhibits remarkable flexibility, seamlessly accommodating a broad spectrum of practically relevant loss functions across both binary and multi-class classification settings. By carefully combining our estimators with standard techniques, we substantially improve sample complexity guarantees for a large class of losses of practical relevance. We also empirically validate the efficacy of our proposed approach across a diverse array of benchmark datasets, demonstrating compelling empirical advantages over standard baselines.



1
Introduction

Learning from Label Proportions (LLP) is a type of weakly supervised problem where, instead of individual labels for each training instance, the algorithm has only access to the proportion of different labels within groups of instances, called
bags
. This may occur due to privacy/legal restrictions
[
36
,
45
]
, cost of supervision
[
7
]
, lack of resolution in labeling instruments
[
12
]
, etc.

While initial formulations of LLP date back to at least 15 to 20 years ago (e.g.,
[
7
,
10
,
31
,
35
,
36
]
), it is only with the recent changes in the Web advertising industry that we see a substantial resurgence of interest in this problem.
Web advertising, arguably one of the largest scale real-world applications of Machine Learning, is undergoing important changes in recent years.
Advertisers (or other entities working on their behalf, often called
AdTechs
) train models for predicting the chance that a user will
convert
(e.g., sign up for an account, buy a product, etc.) after they clicked advertisements posted on a publisher website. The output of such models is typically used as inputs when computing the bid price in online auctions that power automated bidding over the Web.
Now, since the ad click and conversion events happen on different websites (the auction and the click event is on a publisher website, the conversion event is on an advertiser website), collecting training labels amounts to linking the two events, thus tracking the user behavior across the two sites.

Historically, third-party cookies or link decoration has made this straightforward. Nonetheless, even acknowledging the critical role that online advertising is playing on the web (and the impossibility of perfect user tracking protection), growing privacy concerns have led major web browsers to introduce ad hoc APIs to measure ad performance while, at the same time, protecting user privacy. These include APIs from Apple’s Safari
[
44
]
, and Mozilla’s Firefox
[
8
]
which have both deprecated third-party cookies in past years, and Google’s Privacy Sandbox initiative
[
15
]
. These APIs allow AdTechs to collect cross-site data only
in aggregate
. For instance, from the publisher viewpoint, where the auction is run, it is of paramount importance to obtain effective
per-interaction
predictions. Yet, what the publisher observes is only a list of ad interactions (the click events) and an
unordered
list of conversion events (that is, their
number
) that originate from those interactions on the advertiser side.
We view LLP as a principled technique for making the best use of these APIs for training conversion models.

Given the above context, in this paper we unveil a highly versatile and effective debiasing methodology that extracts signals from aggregate label information. This novel low-variance approach represents a pivotal leap forward in LLP, as it allows us to effortlessly accommodate a panoply of practically relevant loss functions, in both binary and multi-class classification settings. Below we discuss our contribution in the context of the relevant LLP literature.

Related work.

The most relevant papers to our work are
[
41
,
23
,
6
]
. We defer to Appendix
A
for a more comprehensive survey of the LLP literature.
Like our work, the three aforementioned studies operate within a standard statistical learning framework with random, non-overlapping bags, but unlike ours, they are limited to
binary
classification.
[
41
]
proposes a general LLP reduction to standard learning, applicable to any bounded loss, which turns aggregate labels into unbiased individual estimates, albeit increasing variance. They show
k/\sqrt{n}
regret guarantees for methods like ERM and SGD, where
n
is the sample size and
k
is the bag size.
[
23
]
and
[
6
]
concentrate solely on square loss, and improve in various ways over
[
41
]
. Specifically,
[
23
]
achieve fast rates of the form
k^{3}/n
in (restricted) realizable settings, subsequently improved to the optimal rate
k/n
in
[
6
]
. The latter paper also contains non-realizable sample complexity bounds of the form
\sqrt{k/n}
, again improving over
[
41
]
when the loss is the square loss.

Contributions.

Inspired by
[
41
]
’s broad scope, our work refines and significantly extends prior LLP investigations. Notably, our analysis embraces a diverse spectrum of practical, even unbounded, loss functions—unlike the constraints of
[
41
,
23
,
6
]
. In pursuing this broader perspective, our work refines and significantly extends these prior investigations in multiple ways.
For instance, we show that the regret guarantees of
\sqrt{k/n}
for the non-realizable setting can also be achieved for arbitrary binary and multi-class classification scenarios with unbounded losses (like the log loss/cross entropy loss) under bounded moment conditions. We also extend
[
6
]
’s optimal
k/n
realizable regret far beyond square loss.
More importantly, we provide the first unified treatment of multi-class LLP. Within this framework, we introduce a fundamental distinction between
full histogram
and
total
multi-class scenarios, based on the granularity of class-wise aggregate label information. This practically motivated differentiation directly dictates regret guarantees: full histogram often yields
c
-independent bounds (
c
being the number of classes) whereas total multi-class regret scales as
c^{2}
.
Further, we report the results of a suite of experiments on a diverse set of real-world benchmarks, where we compare our debiasing technique to representative baselines available in the literature having the same breadth of applicability. We follow, whenever possible, prior experimental setups (like the one in
[
41
]
), but choose to report log loss test set results, as more in line with the afore-mentioned motivation about conversion prediction. Though somewhat preliminary in nature, these experiments reveal the effectiveness of our method, specifically when dealing with large bag sizes. This is indeed the scenario we are mostly concerned with for Web advertising, where the aggregate signal might even be at the level of ad campaigns (hence, very diverse and/or very large bags).

Preliminaries and Notation

We denote by
\mathcal{X}
the input space (sometimes called feature space or instance space), and by
\mathcal{Y}
the label space, which can be either binary,
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
, or multi-class,
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}
an unknown distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and denote the corresponding random variables by
x
and
y
.
We also denote by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}
the conditional distribution of random variable
y
given
x
.
For each class
r=0,\ldots,c-1
, we denote by
\eta_{r}(x)
the probability
\eta_{r}(x)=\PP_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}(y=r|x)={\E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}[\{y=r\}|x]
, where
\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function of the predicate at argument. We shall often gather such probabilities into a
c
-dimensional (column) vector
\eta(x)=[\eta_{0}(x),\ldots,\eta_{c-1}(x)]^{\top}
. Note that
\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\eta_{r}(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. In the binary case, where
y\in\{0,1\}
, there is no need for a vector notation, and we simply define
\eta(x)=\PP_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}(y=1|x)={\E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}[y|x]
to be the probability of drawing label 1 conditioned on feature vector
x
. When clear from the surrounding context, we shall often shorten or remove the subscripts from probabilities and expectations and write, e.g.,
{\E}_{(x,y)}[f(x,y)]
, instead of
{\E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(x,y)]
, and
{\E}[y|x]
instead of
{\E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}[y|x]
. No ambiguity will arise.

We take the standard viewpoint of statistical learning, and
operate within a given function class
\mathcal{H}
(sometimes called hypothesis space or hypothesis class), where each
h\in\mathcal{H}
is a deterministic mapping
h\,:\,\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is some
output
space. In the binary label case, we shall work with
\mathcal{Z}=[0,1]
, so that
h(x)
can be interpreted as the probability that
y=1
given
x
according to model
h
. In the multi-class case, the output space
\mathcal{Z}
will depend on the loss function (see below). For instance, we may have
\mathcal{Z}=\Delta_{c}
, the
c
-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta_{c}=\{(h_{0},\ldots,h_{c-1})\,:\,h_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}h_{r}=1\}
, thus
h_{r}(x)
can be interpreted as the probability that
y=r
given
x
according to model
h
.

A loss function
\ell~\colon\mathcal{Z}~\times~\mathcal{Y}~\to~\mathbb{R}^{+}
measures the discrepancy between
h(x)
and
y
. Notable examples of loss functions where
\mathcal{Z}=\Delta_{c}
include
the square loss
\ell(h(x),y)=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\bigl(\{y=r\}-h_{r}(x)\bigl)^{2}
(sometimes called
Brier
score), which reduces in the binary case to the more familiar expression
\ell(h(x),y)=(y-h(x))^{2}
, and the log loss
\ell(h(x),y)=-\log h_{y}(x)
, which in the binary case can be rewritten as
\ell(h(x),y)=-y\log h(x)-(1-y)\log(1-h(x))
.
Another loss function, often used to train and evaluate conversion prediction models in advertising domains, is the (capped) Poisson Log Loss, where
\mathcal{Z}=[0,c-1]
, and
\ell(h(x),y)=h(x)-y\log h(x)
. Disregarding the capping, this can be interpreted as the negative log likelihood of
y
generated by Poisson distribution with parameter
h(x)
. Hence minimizing this loss is equivalent to maximizing the log likelihood of the parameter
h(x)
given the observation
y
.

Following very recent works on the subject
[
6
]
, we decided to skip the heavy machinery of empirical process theory (e.g.,

[
2
,
3
,
43
,
27
]
) and restrict to the case where the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
is finite. The main ideas contained in this paper can be lifted to infinite hypothesis cases, at the cost of mathematical details which are, however, not specific to the LLP framework.
Given
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{H}
, and
\ell
, the
population loss
(or statistical
risk
) of
h\in\mathcal{H}
is

\mathcal{L}(h)=\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x),y)]~.

The
excess risk
, or (simple)
regret

{{Reg}}(h)
of
h
is defined as
{{Reg}}(h)=\mathcal{L}(h)-\mathcal{L}(\widehat{h}^{\star})
, where

\widehat{h}^{\star}=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}(h)
is the
best-in-class
hypothesis, that is, the hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
having the smallest population loss. The learning setting is
realizable
when the Bayes-optimal predictor
h^{\star}=\argmin_{h\,:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{L}(h)
, belongs to
\mathcal{H}
(here the minimum is taken over all possible (measurable) functions), and is otherwise
non-realizable
. Thus, in the realizable setting,
\widehat{h}^{\star}=h^{\star}
.

2.1
The Learning with Label Proportions problem

An
example
is a pair
(x,y)
drawn according to
\mathcal{D}
. A dataset
S
of size
n
is an i.i.d. sequence of examples
S=(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})
. In Learning with Label Proportions (LLP), the feature vectors
x_{i}
in
S
are available in the clear, while the labels are obfuscated by randomly grouping them into
m

bags
of a given
size
k
, with
n=m\times k
. That is, the dataset
S
is only available in the form

S=\underbrace{((x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,k}),\alpha_{1})}_{(\mathcal{B}_{1},\alpha_{1})},\ldots,\underbrace{((x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,k}),\alpha_{m})}_{(\mathcal{B}_{m},\alpha_{m})}~,

(1)

where
\alpha_{j}
is the aggregate label value associated with the
j
-th bag
\mathcal{B}_{j}=\{x_{j,i}\colon i\in[k]\}
. In the binary label case,
\alpha_{j}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j,i}
is simply the label proportion (fraction of positive labels) in the
j
-th bag. In the interpretation of click events and conversion events we alluded to in the introduction,
x_{j,1},\ldots,x_{j,k}
encode a sequence of (publisher side) click events and associated features occurring either within a given time frame or within a campaign (or aggregated is some other way), and
k\alpha_{j}
is the
number
of conversions associated with those clicks on the advertiser side. The multi-class case is a scenario where multiple conversions may be associated with a given click.
In this case, we need to separate two kinds of aggregate labels, corresponding to two different practical settings. We are in the
full histogram
multi-class case when the label proportion for the
j
-th bag is a
c
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=[\alpha_{j,0},\ldots,\alpha_{j,c-1}]^{\top}
where, for each class
r\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
,
\alpha_{j,r}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\{y_{j,i}=r\}
is the fraction of times label
r
occurs in the
j
-th bag (note that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
is a probability vector, in that
\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\alpha_{j,r}=1
).
We are in the
total
multi-class case
when the classes
0,1,\ldots,c-1
can themselves be interpreted as
counts
(e.g.,
y
is the number of purchases associated with a given user
x
within a given time frame, capped at
c-1
) and, for each bag
j
only the overall fractional count
\alpha_{j}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j,i}
within the bag is available. Note that in the binary case, the two notions collapse, and are in turn equivalent to the above-mentioned fraction of positive labels in a bag. The pair
(\mathcal{B}_{j},\alpha_{j})
will often be denoted by
z_{j}
.

Therefore, in LLP the learning algorithm receives information about the
n
labels
y_{j,i}
in
S
only in the aggregate form determined by the
m
signals
\alpha_{j}
. This signal is a (probability) vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
in the full histogram multi-class case, and a single number in the total multi-class case (as well as in the binary case).
Note that, for a given dataset size
n=mk
, the bigger
k
the smaller the overall amount of label information the algorithm receives. Moreover, we should expect the multi-class task to be somewhat harder than the binary task, with the total scenario being harder than the full histogram scenario.

Given a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, a hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
and a loss function
\ell
, our goal is to find
\widehat{h}\in\mathcal{H}
such that the population loss

\mathcal{L}(\widehat{h})

is as small as possible with high probability over the random draw of
S
. It is important to stress that in LLP the goal is that of minimizing the population loss at the
individual
label level
y
, still receiving information in the form of
aggregate
labels (
1
).
We aim to design LLP algorithms for
\widehat{h}
, and quantify
{{Reg}}(\widehat{h})
in both realizable and non-realizable settings, analyzing its dependence on bag size
k
(label obfuscation), total examples
n
, properties of the loss function
\ell
, and class count
c
.
Extending beyond recent square-loss investigations (
[
23
,
6
]
), we cover a wide range of practical, potentially unbounded, loss functions. Utilizing a Median-of-Means Tournament
[
27
]
, we derive sample complexity guarantees that significantly advance prior art in two key directions: general loss functions for binary classification, and multi-class classification with general losses (with either full histogram or total label information).

3
Bag-level Estimators for Arbitrary Loss Functions

This section contains our general treatment for the binary classification case. Due to space limitations, the multi-class setting is mainly contained in Appendix
C
.

Given a model
h\,:\,\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
for binary labels, consider a generic loss function
\ell(h(x),y)
. Note that, because labels
y
are binary in
\{0,1\}
, we can always rewrite
\ell(h(x),y)
as

\ell(h(x),y)=y\,\ell(h(x),1)+(1-y)\,\ell(h(x),0)=\underbrace{\ell(h(x),0)}_{f_{1}(h(x))}+y\underbrace{\Bigl(\ell(h(x),1)-\ell(h(x),0)\Bigl)}_{f_{2}(h(x))}~.

Hence, for suitable functions
f_{1},f_{2}\,:\,[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, any loss depending on binary labels is
linear
in the label
y
, and can be written as

\ell(h(x),y)=f_{1}(h(x))+yf_{2}(h(x)).

For instance, the log loss is obtained by setting
f_{1}(h)=\log\frac{1}{1-h}
,
f_{2}(h)=\log\frac{1-h}{h}
, and the square loss is obtained by
f_{1}(h)=h^{2}
, and
f_{2}(h)=1-2h
, where in both cases
h\in[0,1]
.

For bag
z=((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\alpha)
, consider now the
bag-
z
loss

\ell_{b}(h,z)={\E}_{x}[f_{1}(h(x))+p\,f_{2}(h(x))]+(\alpha-p)\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-k{\E}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))]\Bigl)~,

(2)

where
p=\E[y]
. We use here and throughout the notation
\ell_{b}
to emphasize the role of (
2
) as a bag-level loss, and to clearly differentiate it from the original loss
\ell
. Loss
\ell_{b}(h,z)
is a bag-level counterpart to
\ell(h(x),y)
, based on the centered variables
\alpha-p
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-k{\E}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))]
, where exact knowledge of
p
,
{\E}_{x}[f_{1}(h(x))]
, and
{\E}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))]
is assumed. This assumption will be lifted later. The following lemma shows that
\ell_{b}(h,z)
is an unbiased estimator of
\E_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]
. Moreover, the centering helps reduce the variance to a constant which depends on the loss
\ell
and the model
h
at hand but, crucially, is
independent
of the bag size
k
. All proofs are given in
Section

B.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
be drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
. Let
z=((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\alpha)
be the corresponding bag. For any function
h\in\mathcal{H}
, and any binary loss
\ell(h(x),y)=f_{1}(h(x))+yf_{2}(h(x))
, we have

{\E}_{z}[\ell_{b}(h,z)]={\E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]~,\qquad\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq\frac{5}{2}\,{\E}_{x}\bigl[\bigl(f_{2}(h(x))\bigl)^{2}\bigl]~.

A few comments are in order at this point.
First, we shall massage Lemma
3.1
to make it useful for our analysis (see Section
3.1
below). Yet, what this lemma is intuitively saying should be clear: any loss function for which the
n
examples
(x_{i},y_{i})
are available in the clear can be replaced in expectation by a loss function where only the
m
bags
z_{j}=((x_{j,1},\ldots,x_{j,k}),\alpha_{j})
are observable instead. Moreover, the fact that the variance of this bag-level loss is independent of
k
makes the price for this replacement quite crisp: Instead of dealing with
n
-many supervised signals
y_{i}
, we make do with only
m
-many aggregate signals
\alpha_{j}
. This is similar in spirit to the result contained in
[
5
]
(Proposition 4.2 therein), where an unbiased estimator for general losses is also provided. The important difference here is that whereas the variance of the estimator in
[
5
]
grows
linearly
with
k
(as shown in their Theorem 4.3), the variance of our estimator is
independent
of
k
.
In terms of regret, this directly translates into a bound of the form
1/\sqrt{m}
(or
1/m
in realizable scenarios) as opposed to

\sqrt{k/m}
,
as given in
[
5
]
. Note that when all
n
labels are available, the best possible bounds read
1/\sqrt{n}=1/\sqrt{mk}
or
1/n=1/(mk)
.
It is also worth observing that the very same arguments above can be adapted to the case where dataset
S
has bags of different sizes
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}
, with
\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{j}=n
, as we do still have
m
-many supervised signals,
k
being replaced now by
\smash{\widehat{k}}
, the
average
size of the bags in
S
.
Second, this is essentially the best one can hope for in terms of how regret depends on
n
, when the dataset is arranged into
m=n/k
non-overlapping bags of size
k
. As shown in Theorem 3.1 of
[
6
]
(see also Theorem 8 in
[
23
]
), there are simple realizable problems where the regret of any algorithm cannot be better than
O(k/n)=O(1/m)
.
Third, the centering of the variables in the estimator (
2
) is reminiscent of what the authors of
[
6
]
have very recently proposed for square loss, in order to reduce variance. Yet, our estimator (
2
) virtually applies to any loss, not just to square loss and, even for square loss, it does
not
reduce to the estimator in
[
6
]
(the right comparison is to what those authors call the non-clipped version of the loss at the bag level in Eq. (2) therein).

3.1
The Median of Means Tournament Algorithm

In order to achieve regret rates which are fast when realizable conditions allow them,
it is well-known (e.g.,
[
29
,
30
,
2
]
) that instead of estimating (population) losses we need to estimate loss
differences
, that is,
for each
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, we would like to actually use the estimator

\displaystyle\Delta\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)=\ell_{b}(h_{1},z)-\ell_{b}(h_{2},z)

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{1}(h_{1},h_{2},x)]+p\,{\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{2}(h_{1},h_{2};x)]

(3)

\displaystyle\quad+(\alpha-p)\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta f_{2}(h_{1},h_{2};x_{i})-k{\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{2}(h_{1},h_{2};x)]\Bigl)~,

where

\Delta f_{1}(h_{1},h_{2},x)=f_{1}(h_{1}(x))-f_{1}(h_{2}(x))~~

and

\Delta f_{2}(h_{1},h_{2};x)=f_{2}(h_{1}(x))-f_{2}(h_{2}(x))~.~~

In addition, since we want to cover losses of practical relevance, like the log loss, which are
unbounded
, we resort to a Median-of-Means (MoM) estimator.
1
1

1

Other estimators working with unbounded random variables could be used instead, see, e.g., the survey
[
28
]
.

This is in contrast to empirical mean estimators, which are the basis of the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) methods in
[
5
,
23
,
6
]
. (ERM is sensitive to the range of the loss values, even if mean and variance of the loss are bounded.)

Our algorithm is thus shaped as a MoM
tournament
. We first need to recall what a MoM estimator is. Given a set of i.i.d. random variables
S=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
with finite mean
\mu
and variance, consider the index set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and randomly partition it into
r
groups
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{r}
, each of size
|\mathcal{I}_{i}|\geq\lfloor\frac{n}{r}\rfloor
. Then set
\widehat{\E}(\mathcal{I}_{i})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{i}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}v_{j}
be the empirical mean for the
i
-th group. Then define the MoM estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{{\textrm{MoM}}}(S)
of
\mu
based on
S
as
2
2

2

If the median is not uniquely defined (which may happen when
r
is an even number) we take the average of the two middle points. So, for instance
{\textrm{median}}\{1,2,3\}=2
, but
{\textrm{median}}\{1,2,3,4\}=2.5
.
Note that this always implies

\widehat{\mu}_{{\textrm{MoM}}}(-S)=-\widehat{\mu}_{{\textrm{MoM}}}(S)
. E.g.,
{\textrm{median}}\{-1,-2,-3\}=-2
, and
{\textrm{median}}\{-1,-2,-3,-4\}=-2.5
.

\widehat{\mu}_{{\textrm{MoM}}}(S)={\textrm{median}}\Bigl\{\widehat{\E}(\mathcal{I}_{1}),\ldots,\widehat{\E}(\mathcal{I}_{r})\Bigl\}~.

We shall set throughout
r=\lceil 8\log(1/\delta)\rceil
(and assume in all our applications of MoM that
n\geq r
), where
\delta\in(0,1)
is the desired confidence level.

Now, in order to remove the knowledge of
p
,
{\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{1}(h_{1},h_{2},x)]
, and
{\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{2}(h_{1},h_{2};x)]
in (
3
), we replace the three expectations by empirical estimates on separate datasets. Specifically, we partition the set of bags
S
into three disjoint subsets,
S_{1},S_{2}
, and
S_{3}
of equal size:

S=\{\underbrace{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}}_{S_{1}},\underbrace{z_{m+1},\ldots,z_{2m}}_{S_{2}},\underbrace{z_{2m+1},\ldots z_{3m}}_{S_{3}}\}~.

(4)

Subset
S_{1}
will be used to estimate

{\E}_{x}[\Delta f_{1}(h_{1},h_{2},x)]
and

Example 3.3
.

We now apply Theorem
3.2
to standard losses, like square loss
and log loss, viewed here as canonical representatives of bounded (square) and unbounded (log) loss functions.
The square loss
\ell(h(x),y)=(h(x)-y)^{2}
is obtained by
f_{1}(h)=h^{2}
and
f_{2}(h)=1-2h
, with
h\in[0,1]
. Both
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
are bounded; it is immediate to see that
\sigma^{2}_{f_{1}}(\mathcal{H})\leq 1
,
\sigma^{2}_{f_{2}}(\mathcal{H})\leq 4
, and
{\E}^{2}[\mathcal{H}]\leq 4
.

The log loss
\ell(h(x),y)=-y\,\log h(x)-(1-y)\,\log\left(1-h(x)\right)
is obtained by
f_{1}(h)=\log\frac{1}{1-h}
, and
f_{2}(h)=\log\frac{1-h}{h}
, for
h\in[0,1]
. Neither
f_{1}(\cdot)
nor
f_{2}(\cdot)
is bounded. Yet, in order to guarantee the finiteness of the variance parameters in Theorem
3.2
, it suffices to assume the finiteness for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
of the second moments
{\E}_{x}\bigl[\log^{2}\frac{1}{h(x)}\bigl]
and
{\E}_{x}\bigl[\log^{2}\frac{1}{1-h(x)}\bigl]
. A paradigmatic example where this is the case is when the models
h(x)
are represented as sigmoidal functions
h(x)=\sigma(w_{h}(x))
, where
\sigma(a)=\frac{e^{a}}{1+e^{a}}
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and
w_{h}\,:\,\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the so-called logit of model
h
. With this representation, one can see that the above finiteness conditions is implied by the finiteness of
{\E}_{x}[(w_{h}(x))^{2}]
.
These are rather folklore facts. For completeness we give the details of these calculations in Appendix
B.3
.

The sample complexity from Theorem
3.2
is of the form
n=\widetilde{O}(k/\beta^{2})
.
The bound applies to the general non-realizable setting, and constitutes a so-called
slow
rate, as the dependence on the regret
\beta
is inverse
quadratic
. A sample complexity guarantee with a similar scope as ours is the one from
[
5
]
, whose bound is of the form
n=\widetilde{O}(k^{2}/\beta^{2})
, that is, a slow rate with, in addition, a quadratic dependence on
k
, instead of linear (the authors work under general, but bounded, loss functions).

The recent papers
[
23
,
6
]
do contain fast rates for realizable settings, but they only apply to the square loss case. In particular, the guarantee in
[
23
]
is of the form
n=\widetilde{O}(k^{3}/\beta)
, but it holds only in the more restricted realizability condition where
\mathcal{L}(h^{\star})=0
, and the models in
\mathcal{H}
have binary output, hence disallowing noise in the labels. The realizable guarantee in
[
6
]
, on the other hand, is a fast rate of the form
n=\widetilde{O}(k/\beta)
which, as we already recalled, is best possible (up to log factors).

We next show that a similar fast rate can be achieved by Algorithm
1
on a wider family of loss functions that are more relevant in practice than square loss.

Given loss function

\ell(a,y)=f_{1}(a)+yf_{2}(a),

with
a\in[0,1]
and
y\in\{0,1\}
, consider its extension
\bar{\ell}\,:\,[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined as

\bar{\ell}(a,b)=f_{1}(a)+bf_{2}(a),

where we have simply turned the range of the second argument from
\{0,1\}
to
[0,1]
. We say that
\ell
is quadratically sandwiched w.r.t.
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
if there exist constants
C_{1}>c_{1}>0
, and
C_{2}>c_{2}>0
such that, for all
a,b\in[0,1]
, and
i=1,2
,

\frac{C_{i}}{2}\Bigl(f_{i}(a)-f_{i}(b)\Bigl)^{2}\geq\bar{\ell}(a,b)-\bar{\ell}(b,b)\geq\frac{c_{i}}{2}\Bigl(f_{i}(a)-f_{i}(b)\Bigl)^{2}~.

(6)

As explained below, in order for fast rates in
realizable settings to hold, only the constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
that appear in the lower bound will matter.

Example 3.4
.

We illustrate (
6
) through some examples.
For square loss, recalling Example
3.3
, condition (
6
) is clearly verified for
f_{2}
, since
(f_{2}(a)-f_{2}(b))^{2}=4(a-b)^{2}=4(\bar{\ell}(a,b)-\bar{\ell}(b,b))
, resulting in
C_{2}=c_{2}=2
. As for
f_{1}
, we have
c_{1}=1/2
, but in order to get a finite

, we have to require that either
a
or
b
is bounded away from
0
. E.g., if
a+b\geq\epsilon>0
then
C_{1}=\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}}
. Yet, we emphasize that the constant
C_{1}
will be immaterial in realizable settings – see below.

For log loss, one can easily see that
\bar{\ell}(a,b)-\bar{\ell}(b,b)=b\log\frac{b}{a}+(1-b)\log\frac{1-b}{1-a}={\textrm{KL}}(b,a)
, the KL-divergence between two Bernoulli random variables with biases
b
and
a
.
As for condition (
6
), consider again the sigmoidal representation in Example
3.3
. This condition boils down to the strong convexity (
c_{2}
side) and strong smoothness (
C_{2}
side) of the function
w_{h}\rightarrow\log(1+e^{w_{h}})
. This function is known to be strongly smooth, but the strong convexity requires the logits
w_{h}
to be bounded. Boundedness is also needed in order to enforce (
6
) on
C_{1}
and
c_{1}
– see Appendix
B.4
for details.

The following theorem specifies the properties of

for quadratically sandwiched losses.
The proof is in

3.2
The multi-class case (sketch)

Any loss function
\ell(h(x),y)
for multi-class labels
y\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
can always be written as

\ell(h(x),y)=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\{y=r\}\,\ell(h(x),r)~.

The bag-level estimator for the
full histogram
multi-class scenario is a generalization of the one for binary labels, where the role of the two functions
f_{1}(h(x))
and
f_{2}(h(x))
is now played by the
c
loss components
\ell(h(x),r),r=0,\ldots,c-1
. The LLP algorithm is the very same as Algorithm
1
, but applied to the alluded bag-level estimator for full histogram multi-class— see Appendix
C
for details.

On the other hand, we are able to handle the
total
multi-class case only when
\ell
has the affine form
\ell(h(x),y)=f_{1}(h(x))+yf_{2}(h(x))
, where now,
y\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
. The loss admits this representation if it can be interpreted as the negative log-likelihood of a distribution in the (single-parameter canonical form) exponential family for which
y
is a sufficient statistic. Notable examples are the Poisson Log Loss
\ell(h(x),y)=h(x)-y\log h(x)
(negative log-likelihood of Poisson distribution) and the square loss
\ell(h(x),y)=(y-h(x))^{2}
(negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian distribution with unit variance; note that the
y^{2}
term can be disregarded here, as it is independent of
h(x)
).
The multi-class log loss/cross-entropy does not admit this representation.

From Appendix
C
, one can see that the regret guarantee we obtain for our algorithm in the full histogram case (Theorem
C.2
) is of the form

n=O\Bigl(\frac{k\,\Delta\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})\,\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{\beta^{2}}\Bigl)~,

where

\Delta\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\bigl[\max_{r}\bigl(\ell(h(x),r)\bigl)^{2}\bigl]

is the factor hiding the dependence on the number of classes
c
. This dependence can be removed completely if the loss is bounded, and turned into a mild
\log c
when the loss is unbounded but has light tails.
On the contrary, the bounds for the total multi-class case mimic rather closely those for binary labels (Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
) but with an extra
c^{2}
factor, due to the inflated variance terms. Details are again contained in Appendix
C
.

For practical reasons, in the experiments that follow we depart from the MoM tournament scheme, and rather test our bag-level estimators combined with SGD-like algorithms operating with log loss.

Experiments

We compare our general unbiased proportion matching loss against standard baselines, where the goal is to minimize the cross-entropy loss.
We evaluate against EasyLLP
[
5
]
and a standard proportion matching method (e.g.,
[
46
]
), which is shown to be a strong baseline in
[
5
]
.
Our dataset preparation and experimental setup is heavily inspired by
[
5
]
.
We conduct two kinds of experiments: (i) batch experiments, making many training passes over a dataset aimed at achieving low instance-level log loss on held-out test data, and (ii) an online experiment on the Criteo Display Advertising Challenge dataset
[
42
]
, where the goal is to make accurate predictions on a stream of data as it arrives.

Datasets and Models.

We conduct batch learning experiments on the MNIST
[
21
]
, CIFAR-10
[
20
]
, Higgs
[
1
]
, and Adult
[
18
]
datasets.
For MNIST the training labels are binarized based on whether each digit is even or odd, while for CIFAR-10 we consider two binarization strategies: Animal-vs-Machine and Cat-vs-Rest.
We train convolutional neural networks for MNIST and CIFAR-10 and fully-connected networks for Higgs and Adult.
Our online experiment is conducted on the Criteo Display Advertising Challenge
[
42
]
, which is a click prediction task where each training example contains information about an ad impression and the label corresponds to whether the ad was clicked or not.
For this dataset, we use a deep embedding network, where each feature value is associated with a learned embedding vector and the model’s prediction is the output of several fully-connected layers applied to the concatenated feature embeddings.
Further details are given in
Appendix

D
.

LLP Losses.

GeneralUPM
is our unbiased proportion matching loss,
EasyLLP
is the LLP loss of
[
5
]
, and
PM
is the standard proportion matching method.
In the batch setup, we instantiate each LLP loss with binary cross-entropy with smoothed labels; in the online setup no label smoothing is applied.
Note that label smoothing only changes the loss definition, the event-level labels are not modified.
Details of the losses and implementation of
GeneralUPM
and
EasyLLP
are in
Appendix

D
.

Batch Experimental Setup.

LLP training data is constructed per dataset by shuffling and grouping consecutive examples into non-overlapping bags of the desired size.
If the number of training examples is not divisible by the bag size
k
, the leftover examples are discarded.
We train models on each dataset using the Adam optimizer
[
17
]
to minimize each LLP loss.
We train for
E
epochs (passes through the training data) where on each epoch the order of the LLP bags is shuffled.
Note that we do
not
change the examples present in each bag per epoch, since that would gradually leak additional label information.
We use bag sizes
k=2^{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,11
and batches that contain
2^{12}=4096
training examples regardless of bag size.
Learning rates are tuned from
10^{-7}
to
10^{-1}
(16 log-spaced values); the label marginal
p
is estimated as the average label proportion over the entire training dataset. For each dataset, LLP loss, bag size, and learning rate, experiments are repeated 10 times with randomized data shuffling and model initialization. After each epoch, the average instance-level log loss is measured on test data. The lowest average test log loss achieved is then reported for each dataset, LLP loss, and bag size.

Online Experimental Setup.

The online experimental setup is similar to the batch setup with the following modifications.
We read the data in consecutive chunks of
2^{16}
examples.
On each chunk, the model is evaluated before the parameters are updated.
The model updates are performed similarly to the batch setting: the chunk examples are shuffled and partitioned into consecutive bags.
This simulates ad serving where predictions precede aggregate label updates of the model weights. The online experiment is repeated 5 times with different model initialization and data shuffling seeds.
For each bag size, we tune the learning rate over the same grid to optimize the average log loss over all chunks.
Finally, the label marginal
p
is estimated for each chunk as the average label proportion for bags in that chunk.
This is required to track the time-varying click rate in the Criteo dataset.

5
Conclusions and Limitations

Our LLP method applies to virtually any loss function. As in past investigations, we are centered around designing (almost) unbiased estimates of the instance level population loss through bag-level information. However, unlike past investigations, we have been able to reduce the variance of our estimators to a quantity that is
independent
of
k
. This directly translates to improved/optimal sample size results in both realizable and non-realizable settings, extending to a broader array of losses than recent square-loss studies.
Experiments on binary datasets demonstrate improved performance over baselines in large
k
regimes, a crucial aspect for modern applications of LLP to online advertising.
From a theoretical standpoint, the tightness of our multi-class bounds regarding
c
remains unknown. Additionally, we currently do not have a fast rate analysis for the full histogram multi-class case. Moreover, the necessity of the affine loss form we assumed for the total multi-class setting, which currently limits our technique’s applicability, is unclear.
Finally, the paper is limited in its experimental investigation, as it currently restricts to binary classification tasks.

Appendix A
Related Work

The problem of Learning with Label Proportions (LLP) has historically been motivated by real-world applications where labels are available not for each data point, but only in the form of aggregate labels for bags of data. This may occur due to privacy and legal restrictions (e.g.,
[
36
,
45
]
), cost of supervision (e.g.,
[
7
]
), lack of resolution in labeling instruments (e.g.,
[
12
]
), etc.

LLP garnered initial attention in the research community with early contributions from
[
10
,
31
,
35
,
36
,
41
,
46
,
33
]
.
In their work,
[
10
]
introduced a hierarchical model that generates labels according to the provided label proportions, and proposed an inference method based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo which, however, did not scale effectively to larger datasets.
[
31
]
emphasized the possibility of adapting conventional supervised learning techniques, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and
k
-Nearest Neighbors, to the LLP setting by reformulating their objective functions. However, their paper did not include any experimental results on classification problems.

[
35
,
33
]
developed algorithms for learning an exponential generative model. Nevertheless, their approaches relied on significant modeling assumptions, notably conditional exponential models, which are often inadequate for contemporary machine learning architectures, particularly Deep Neural Networks (DNNs).

[
36
]
and
[
46
]
both explored the adaptation of SVMs for LLP. Notably,
[
46
]
presented an SVM regression algorithm that optimizes the SVM loss by aligning with the given label proportions. This general strategy is referred to as “proportion matching" in
[
5
,
6
]
. However, their method was constrained to linear models within a specific feature space. Similar limitations were present in other SVM-based studies on LLP, such as
[
34
,
40
]
.

The natural appeal of the proportion matching concept led to its extension to other types of classifiers. For instance,
[
22
]
applied this idea to Convolutional Neural Networks using a generative model where the maximum likelihood estimator was computed via the Expectation Maximization algorithm. Regrettably, this approach proved to be computationally prohibitive even for moderately sized DNN architectures.
Empirical approaches based on DNNs for large-scale and multi-class data
[
19
,
24
,
14
,
32
]
have also been developed.

Delving into the more theoretically-grounded explorations of LLP, a notable body of work includes
[
37
,
38
,
4
,
26
,
25
,
39
,
47
]
, and the already cited
[
5
,
23
,
6
]
.

The theoretical framework presented by
[
37
,
38
,
4
]
is predominantly confined to linear-threshold functions, with some investigations further taking advantage of overlapping bags. In stark contrast, our approach aligns with
[
5
,
23
,
6
]
, where we operate within the more conventional setting of non-overlapping and i.i.d. bags, but cope with a broader and more versatile spectrum of model classes.

Adjacent to LLP is the problem of
learning from multiple unlabeled datasets

[
26
,
25
]
. While the authors put forth a debiasing method for the underlying loss function and demonstrate consistency (akin to our results and those in
[
5
,
23
,
6
]
), their method relies on restrictive assumptions, such as the separation of class priors over bags. This necessitates access to class-conditional distributions. In contrast, our setup involves bags drawn i.i.d. from the same prior, a scenario where the algorithms of
[

would be ineffective.

[

,
47
]
offer an alternative perspective on LLP, involving the reduction of the LLP problem to the problem of learning with label noise. In this framework, bags are paired, and each pair is analyzed as a noisy label scenario, with label proportions interpreted as label flipping probabilities. Nevertheless, this bag pairing, much like in
[
26
]
, fundamentally depends on the statistical variations between bags, a property that we deliberately exclude in our work. Additionally, the balanced risk they focus on as their population loss differs from the population losses we are concerned with in this study.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof.

Recall that
\eta(x)=\PP_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}(y=1|x)={\E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}[y|x]
.

We have

\displaystyle{\E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}\Bigl[{\E}_{y|x}[\ell(h(x),y)\,|\,x]\Bigl]

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}\Bigl[{\E}_{y|x}[f_{1}(h(x))+yf_{2}(h(x))\,|\,x]\Bigl]

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}\Bigl[f_{1}(h(x))+\eta(x)f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\E[\eta(x)f_{2}(h(x))]~.

(7)

Define the intermediate bag-level quantity

g(h,z)=\E\left[f_{1}(h(x))\right]+\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{y}_{i}\Bigl)\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)~,

with
\widetilde{y}_{i}=y_{i}-p
, and
\widetilde{f}_{2,i}=f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]
. The function
g(h,z)
is shaped so as to match (
7
) as much as possible.

Then

\displaystyle\E[g(h,z)]

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}{\E}_{z}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{y}_{i}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}+\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{y}_{i}\widetilde{f}_{2,j}\right]

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}{\E}_{z}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{y}_{i}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\right]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}{\E}_{z}\Bigl[\widetilde{y}_{i}\Bigl]{\E}_{z}\Bigl[\widetilde{f}_{2,j}\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}{\E}_{z}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{y}_{i}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\right]

(as
{\E}_{z}\bigl[\widetilde{y}_{i}\bigl]={\E}_{z}\bigl[\widetilde{f}_{2,j}\bigl]=0
)

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k}{\E}_{z}\left[\widetilde{y}_{1}\widetilde{f}_{2,1}\right]

\displaystyle=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k}{\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\bigl(y-p\bigl)\bigl(f_{2}(h(x))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]\bigl)\Bigl]~.

But

\displaystyle{\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\bigl(y-p\bigl)\bigl(f_{2}(h(x))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]\bigl)\Bigl]

\displaystyle={\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\bigl(y-p\bigl)f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]-{\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\bigl(y-p\bigl)\Bigl]\,\E[f_{2}(h(x))]

\displaystyle={\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\bigl(y-p\bigl)f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]

\displaystyle={\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[yf_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]-p{\E}_{(x,y)}\Bigl[f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}\Bigl[\eta(x)f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]-p\,{\E}_{x}\Bigl[f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]

where the last equality follows from an argument similar to the derivation in
Equation

7
.
Combining the last two derivations we get that

\E[g(h,z)]=\E[f_{1}(h(x))]+\frac{1}{k}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\eta(x)f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]-\frac{p}{k}\,{\E}_{x}\Bigl[f_{2}(h(x))\Bigl]~.

Thus, comparing (
7
) to the above expectation, we see that

{\E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]=k\,{\E}_{z}[g(h,z)]+p\,{\E}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))]-(k-1){\E}_{x}[f_{1}(h(x))]~,

making

\ell_{b}(h,z)=k\,g(h,z)+p\,{\E}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))]-(k-1){\E}_{x}[f_{1}(h(x))]

an unbiased estimator of
the sample-level loss

{\E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]
.

Substituting back the definition of the bag-level quantity
g(h,z)
we get that the estimator is

\ell_{b}(h,z)={\E}_{x}[f_{1}(h(x))+p\,f_{2}(h(x))]+(\alpha-p)\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-k\E[f_{2}(h(x))]\Bigl)~.

(8)

This proves the first part of the lemma.

As for the variance, we introduce the short-hand notation

\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha-p~,\qquad\widetilde{f}_{2}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]~,

and let

z=(\mathcal{B},\alpha)~,

with
\mathcal{B}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.
We can write using the total variance formula

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\ell_{b}(h,z))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{z}\Bigl(\widetilde{\alpha}\,\widetilde{f}_{2}\Bigl)

\displaystyle={\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\,\widetilde{f}_{2}\,|\,\mathcal{B})\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left({\E}_{\alpha}\left[\widetilde{\alpha}\,\widetilde{f}_{2}\,|\,\mathcal{B}\right]\right)

\displaystyle={\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[(\widetilde{f}_{2})^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\alpha}\,|\,\mathcal{B})\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,{\E}_{\alpha}\left[\widetilde{\alpha}\,|\,\mathcal{B}\right]\right)

\displaystyle={\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[(\widetilde{f}_{2})^{2}\,\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})(1-\eta(x_{i}))}{k^{2}}\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4k}{\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[(\widetilde{f}_{2})^{2}\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4k}\,\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{2}(h(x_{i}))\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4k^{2}}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)~.

(9)

The term

\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

can be treated as follows. Define

\widetilde{\eta}_{i}=\eta(x_{i})-p

and, as before,

\widetilde{f}_{2,i}=f_{2}(h(x_{i}))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]~.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{4}}\,\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k^{4}}\,{\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)^{2}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)^{2}\right]~.

Now, the square within the above expectation can also be rewritten as

\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)^{2}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{a=1}^{k}(\widetilde{f}_{2,a})^{2}+\sum_{a\neq b}\widetilde{f}_{2,a}\widetilde{f}_{2,b}\Bigl)\Bigl(\sum_{c=1}^{k}(\widetilde{\eta}_{c})^{2}+\sum_{c\neq d}\widetilde{\eta}_{c}\widetilde{\eta}_{d}\Bigl)~.

Since the variables
\widetilde{f}_{2,i}
and
\widetilde{\eta}_{j}
are zero mean, and independent of one another when
i\neq j
, the only terms that survive once we take the expectations are those of the form

(\widetilde{f}_{2,a})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{c})^{2}\qquad\forall a,c\in[k]

which are exactly
k^{2}
, and those of the forms

\widetilde{f}_{2,a}\,\widetilde{f}_{2,b}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{a}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{b}~,~~~~~~\widetilde{f}_{2,a}\,\widetilde{f}_{2,b}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{b}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{a}\qquad\forall a,b\in[k]~,a\neq b

which are exactly

.
Therefore

\displaystyle{\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)^{2}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\Biggl[\sum_{a,c}(\widetilde{f}_{2,a})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{c})^{2}+2\sum_{a\neq b}\widetilde{f}_{2,a}\,\widetilde{f}_{2,b}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{a}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{b}\Biggl]

\displaystyle=\E\Biggl[\sum_{a}(\widetilde{f}_{2,a})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{a})^{2}+\sum_{a\neq c}(\widetilde{f}_{2,a})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{c})^{2}+2\sum_{a\neq b}\Bigl(\widetilde{f}_{2,a}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{a}\Bigl)\,\Bigl(\widetilde{f}_{2,b}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{b}\Bigl)\Biggl]

\displaystyle=k\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]+k(k-1)\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}]\E[(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]+2k(k-1){\E}^{2}[\widetilde{f}_{2,1}\,\widetilde{\eta}_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq k\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]+k(k-1)\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}]\E[(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]+2k(k-1){\E}[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}\,(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=(2k^{2}-k)\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]+k(k-1)\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}]\E[(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]~.

But

\displaystyle{\E}[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}\Biggl[\Bigl(f_{2}(h(x))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]\Bigl)^{2}\,\Bigl(\eta(x)-p\Bigl)^{2}\Biggl]

\displaystyle\leq{\E}_{x}\Biggl[\Bigl(f_{2}(h(x))-\E[f_{2}(h(x))]\Bigl)^{2}\Biggl]=\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))~,

\displaystyle\E[(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde{f}_{2,1})=\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))~,

\displaystyle\E[(\widetilde{\eta}_{1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{x}(\eta(x))={\E}_{x}[\eta^{2}(x)]-{\E}_{x}^{2}[\eta(x)]\leq p(1-p)\leq\frac{1}{4}~.

Thus

\displaystyle{\E}_{\mathcal{B}}\left[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{f}_{2,i}\Bigl)^{2}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\eta}_{i}\Bigl)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2k^{2}-k)\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))+\frac{k(k-1)}{4}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{4}k^{2}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))~,

(10)

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\widetilde{f}_{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta(x_{i})-p\Bigl)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{4k^{2}}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))~.

Substituting back into
Equation

9

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq\frac{1}{4}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))+\frac{9}{4}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))=\frac{5}{2}\,\operatorname{Var}_{x}(f_{2}(h(x)))~,

as claimed.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,{{Reg}}(h)\geq\beta\}
, and assume
m
is such that, for
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
, we have

C_{\delta}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k)\leq\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})/4~,

(19)

for all

we have

C_{\delta}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k)\leq\frac{\beta}{4}~,

(20)

along with
m=\Omega(\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta))
.
Then with probability
\geq 1-\delta
over the generation of
S_{1},S_{2}
, and
S_{3}
the following holds:

1.

If
{{Reg}}(h)=\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})\geq\beta
then
h
is eliminated by
\widehat{h}^{\star}
;

2.

\widehat{h}^{\star}
is not eliminated. In particular, at the end of the tournament
{\mathcal{P}}
is not empty (as it contains at least
\widehat{h}^{\star}
).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
. We have, with probability
\geq 1-\delta

\displaystyle Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})-C_{\delta}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k)

\displaystyle{\mbox{(from (\ref{e:MoM_bound2})})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})

\displaystyle{\mbox{(from (\ref{e:condition_on_m_1})})}\,.

Now, if
\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})\geq\beta
(clearly this implies
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
) then
Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)\geq\frac{3}{4}\beta>\beta/2
, thus
h
is eliminated by
\widehat{h}^{\star}
. This proves the first item.

As for the second item, consider any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, and note that
\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})\geq 0
(since
\widehat{h}^{\star}
is the best in class) and that, by definition,
Q(\widehat{h}^{\star},h;S)=-Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)
.

We separate two cases:

•

\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})\geq\beta/4
(which is equivalent to
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
). In this case, from the previous chain of inequalities,
Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)\geq\frac{3}{16}\beta>0
, and
\widehat{h}^{\star}
is not eliminated as a second argument of
Q
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle Q(\widehat{h}^{\star},h;S)

\displaystyle=-Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)\leq-\frac{3}{16}\beta<\beta/2~.

Hence
\widehat{h}^{\star}
cannot be eliminated as first argument of
Q
either.

•

\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})<\beta/4
(which is equivalent to
h\notin\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
). In this case, we have, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
,

\displaystyle Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})-C_{\delta}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k)

\displaystyle{\mbox{(from (\ref{e:MoM_bound2})})}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})-\beta/4

\displaystyle{\mbox{(from (\ref{e:condition_on_m_2})})}\,.

The above expression larger than
-\beta/2
since
\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})\geq 0
. Hence,
\widehat{h}^{\star}
will not be eliminated as second argument of
Q
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle Q(\widehat{h}^{\star},h;S)

\displaystyle=-Q(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};S)\leq-\Delta\mathcal{L}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})+\beta/4\leq\beta/4<\beta/2~.

Hence
\widehat{h}^{\star}
will not be eliminated as first argument of
Q
either.

This concludes the proof.
∎

B.3
Finiteness of the second-moment parameters for log loss in Theorem
3.2

First, observe that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{f_{1}}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x))-f_{1}(\widehat{h}^{\star}(x)))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}2{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x)))^{2}]+2{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(\widehat{h}^{\star}(x)))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 4\,\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x)))^{2}]~,

and similarly

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{f_{2}}(\mathcal{H})\leq 4\,\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x)))^{2}]~.

Moreover,

\displaystyle{\E}^{2}[\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}^{2}_{x}[f_{2}(h(x))-f_{2}(\widehat{h}^{\star}(x))]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x))-f_{2}(\widehat{h}^{\star}(x)))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 4\,\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x)))^{2}]~.

Hence, we are reduced to bounding the two second moments
{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x)))^{2}]
and
{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x)))^{2}]
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Now, for log loss,

{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x)))^{2}]={\E}_{x}\left[\log^{2}\frac{1}{1-h(x)}\right]~,\qquad{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x)))^{2}]={\E}_{x}\left[\log^{2}\frac{1-h(x)}{h(x)}\right]~.

When
h(x)=\sigma(w_{h}(x))
, with
\sigma(a)=\frac{e^{a}}{1+e^{a}}
, we can also write

\displaystyle\log^{2}\frac{1}{1-h(x)}

\displaystyle=\log^{2}(1+e^{w_{h}(x)})\leq 1+(w_{h}(x))^{2}

using the fact that, for all
w\in\mathbb{R}
,

\log^{2}(1+e^{w})\leq 1+w^{2}

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\log^{2}\frac{1-h(x)}{h(x)}=(w_{h}(x))^{2}~.

Hence

{\E}_{x}[(f_{1}(h(x)))^{2}]\leq 1+{\E}_{x}[(w_{h}(x))^{2}]~,\qquad{\E}_{x}[(f_{2}(h(x)))^{2}]\leq{\E}_{x}[(w_{h}(x))^{2}]~,

and the finitess of
{\E}_{x}[(w_{h}(x))^{2}]
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
implies the finiteness of
\sigma^{2}_{f_{1}}(\mathcal{H})
,
\sigma^{2}_{f_{2}}(\mathcal{H})
, and
{\E}^{2}[\mathcal{H}]
.

B.4
On condition (
6
) for log loss

Recall that for log loss

f_{1}(a)=\log\frac{1}{1-a}
, and
f_{2}(a)=\log\frac{1-a}{a}
.
With the representation
a=\sigma(w_{a})=\frac{e^{w_{a}}}{1+e^{w_{a}}}
, and
b=\sigma(w_{b})=\frac{e^{w_{b}}}{1+e^{w_{b}}}
,
We have

(f_{1}(a)-f_{1}(b))^{2}=\left(\log\frac{1+e^{w_{a}}}{1+e^{w_{b}}}\right)^{2}

and

(f_{2}(a)-f_{2}(b))^{2}=(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}~.

We recall that

\bar{\ell}(a,b)-\bar{\ell}(b,b)=b\log\frac{b}{a}+(1-b)\log\frac{1-b}{1-a}={\textrm{KL}}(b,a)

Moreover, substituting the sigmoids for
a
and
b
we get

{\textrm{KL}}(b,a)=\log\frac{1+e^{w_{a}}}{1+e^{w_{b}}}-\sigma(w_{b})(w_{a}-w_{b})~,

which is the Bregman divergence

d_{\psi}(w_{a},w_{b})=\psi(w_{a})-\psi(w_{b})-\psi^{\prime}(w_{b})(w_{a}-w_{b})~,

with

\psi(w)=\log(1+e^{w})~.

Now, it is well known that
\psi
is strongly smooth, in that

d_{\psi}(w_{a},w_{b})\leq(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}=(f_{2}(a)-f_{2}(b))^{2}~,

implying condition (
6
) with
C_{2}=2
.
On the other hand, by
Taylor theorem

\psi(w_{a})=\psi(w_{b})+\psi^{\prime}(w_{b})(w_{a}-w_{b})+\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)}{2}(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}~,

for some
\xi
in the line connecting
w_{a}
to
w_{b}
.
Since
\psi^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)=\sigma^{\prime}(\cdot)
, it follows that

d_{\psi}(w_{a},w_{b})=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)}{2}(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}~.

If the logits
w_{a}
and
w_{b}
have bounded range, then so is
\xi
,
\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)
will be lower bounded by a positive constant, and
c_{2}
in (
6
) can be set to that constant.

As for
c_{1}
and
C_{1}
, it is known that

(f_{1}(a)-f_{1}(b))^{2}=\left(\log\frac{1+e^{w_{a}}}{1+e^{w_{b}}}\right)^{2}\leq(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}

for all
w_{a},w_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
. So in order to prove that

d_{\psi}(w_{a},w_{b})\geq\frac{c_{1}}{2}(f_{1}(a)-f_{1}(b))^{2}~

we need again to enforce a bound on the range of
w_{a}
and
w_{b}
.

Finally, in order to prove

d_{\psi}(w_{a},w_{b})\leq\frac{C_{1}}{2}(f_{1}(a)-f_{1}(b))^{2}~,

(23)

we observe that

f_{1}(a)-f_{1}(b)=\psi(w_{a})-\psi(w_{b})=\psi^{\prime}(\xi^{\prime})(w_{a}-w_{b})~,

for some
\xi^{\prime}
(not necessarily equal to
\xi
) in the line connecting
w_{a}
to
w_{b}
. Thus (
23
) becomes equivalent to

\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(\xi)}{2}(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{2}\,(\psi^{\prime}(\xi^{\prime}))^{2}(w_{a}-w_{b})^{2}~,

that is,

\frac{e^{\xi}}{(1+e^{\xi})^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{2}\,\frac{e^{2\xi^{\prime}}}{(1+e^{\xi^{\prime}})^{2}}~.

Since
\frac{e^{\xi}}{(1+e^{\xi})^{2}}\leq 1/4
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, a sufficient condition turns out to be

C_{1}=\max_{\xi^{\prime}\in\bigl(\min\{w_{a},w_{b}\},\max\{w_{a},w_{b}\}\bigl)}\left(\frac{1+e^{\xi^{\prime}}}{e^{\xi^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}~,

C.2
The total multi-class case

We now move on to sketch the
total
multi-class case. In this case the estimator (
25
) is not legit, as it assumes direct access to all multi-class aggregate value
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{c-1}
. It turns out the total multi-class case we can easily tackle only when the loss function has the affine form
\ell(h(x),y)=f_{1}(h(x))+yf_{2}(h(x))
, where now,
y\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
. Notable examples are the Poisson Log Loss
\ell(h(x),y)=h(x)-y\log h(x)
, as well as the square loss for regression
\ell(h(x),y)=(y-h(x))^{2}
, where the resulting
y^{2}
term can be disregarded, since it is independent of the model’s prediction
h(x)
. Note, however, that neither the multi-class log loss nor the multi-class square loss (aka Brier score) is an affine function of
y
.
The key observation here is that the above affine form of the loss
coincides
with the general form of losses for binary labels we analyzed in Section
3
, the only difference being that now the label
y
has a wider range than binary. In this sense, the binary label case presented in Section
3
is simply a special case of the total multi-class case, the bag-level estimator (
2
) immediately extending to this multi-class scenario.

From a theoretical standpoint, the corresponding version of Lemma
3.1
will feature an extra
c^{2}
factor in the variance bound, and so do as a consequence the variance/second moment terms occurring in Theorem
3.2
, and Theorem
3.5
. This is due to the need to replace
\eta(\cdot)
in the proof of Lemma
3.1
with
\E[y|\cdot]
, whose range of values is as wide as
c
instead of
1
. The easy details of this adaptation are omitted.

C.3
Proof of Lemma
C.1

Introduce the short-hand

\ell_{r}(x)=\ell(h(x),r)~,

and consider the intermediate bag-level quantity

g(h,z)=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(\alpha_{r}-p_{r})\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)~.

Recall that
\eta_{r}(x)=\PP_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}|x}}(y=r|x)

We want to relate the bag-level expectation of
g(h,z)
to the population loss at the individual label level. We have

{\E}_{(x,y)}[\ell(h(x),y)]=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}{\E}_{x}{\E}_{y|x}[\{y=r\}\,\ell_{r}(x)\,|\,x]=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}{\E}_{x}[\eta_{r}(x)\,\ell_{r}(x)]~,

and

\displaystyle{\E}_{z}[g(h,z)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}{\E}_{z}\Bigl[(\alpha_{r}-p_{r})\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}{\E}_{z}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\underbrace{\Bigl(\{y_{j}=r\}-p_{r}\Bigl)}_{\widetilde{y}_{j,r}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\underbrace{\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)}_{\widetilde{\ell}_{i,r}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}{\E}_{z}\left[\sum_{j=i}\widetilde{y}_{j,r}\widetilde{\ell}_{i,r}+\sum_{j\neq i}\widetilde{y}_{j,r}\widetilde{\ell}_{i,r}\right]~.

But when
j\neq i
we have
\E[\widetilde{y}_{j,r}\widetilde{\ell}_{i,r}]=\E[\widetilde{y}_{j,r}]\E[\widetilde{\ell}_{i,r}]=0
, so that the above simplifies as

{\E}_{z}[g(h,z)]=\frac{1}{k}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\E\left[\widetilde{y}_{1,r}\widetilde{\ell}_{1,r}\right]=\frac{1}{k}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\E\left[\Bigl(\{y=r\}-p_{r}\Bigl)\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x)-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)\right]~.

In turn

\displaystyle\E\left[\Bigl(\{y=r\}-p_{r}\Bigl)\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x)-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\Bigl(\{y=r\}-p_{r}\Bigl)\ell_{r}(x)\right]-\underbrace{\E\left[\Bigl(\{y=r\}-p_{r}\Bigl){\E}_{r}\right]}_{=0}

\displaystyle={\E}_{x}[\eta_{r}(x)\ell_{r}(x)]-p_{r}{\E}_{r}~.

Hence

{\E}_{z}[g(h,z)]=\frac{1}{k}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\Bigl(\E[\eta_{r}(x)\ell_{r}(x)]-p_{r}{\E}_{r}\Bigl)=\frac{1}{k}\E[\ell(h(x),y)]-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}{\E}_{r}~.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\ell_{b}(h,z)

\displaystyle=k\,g(h,z)+\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}{\E}_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(\alpha_{r}-p_{r})\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r}\Bigl)+\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}{\E}_{r}

(28)

is an unbiased estimator of
\E[\ell(h(x),y)]
. This proves the first part of the lemma.

Consider now the variance of
\ell_{b}(h,z)
. To facilitate the effort, introduce the short-hand notation

\widetilde{\alpha}_{r}=\alpha_{r}-p_{r}~,\qquad\widetilde{\ell}_{r,i}=\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r}~.

For bag
z
, set
\mathcal{B}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, and let us switch to a vector notation.

Define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=[\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{c-1}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}

\displaystyle=[p_{0},\ldots,p_{c-1}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\ell_{0}(x_{i}),\ldots,\ell_{c-1}(x_{i})]^{\top}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\E}

\displaystyle=[{\E}_{0},\ldots,{\E}_{c-1}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\eta_{0}(x_{i}),\ldots,\eta_{c-1}(x_{i})]~,

and centered versions thereof

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{p}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\E}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p}~.

Now,

\displaystyle\ell_{b}(h,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(\alpha_{r}-p_{r})(\ell_{r}(x_{i})-{\E}_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)~,

so that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))

\displaystyle=\E\Bigl[\Bigl(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]-{\E}^{2}\Bigl[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Bigl]

\displaystyle\leq\E\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\E\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Bigl]~.

Further,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}~,

and

\E[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}_{i}~.

Moreover, for
r\neq s
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}\alpha_{s}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\{y_{i}=r,y_{j}=s\}\Bigl)=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{i\neq j}^{k}\{y_{i}=r,y_{j}=s\}\Bigl)

so that

\E[\alpha_{r}\alpha_{s}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}^{k}\eta_{r}(x_{i})\eta_{s}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Biggl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{r}(x_{i})\Bigl)\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{s}(x_{j})\Bigl)-\sum_{i=1}\eta_{r}(x_{i})\eta_{s}(x_{i})\Biggl]

and

\displaystyle\E[\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})^{\top}-\Sigma^{*}\Bigl)

and

\displaystyle\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*})^{\top}-\Sigma^{*}\Bigl)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}~,\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{r}(x_{i})\eta_{s}(x_{i})\right]_{r,s=0}^{(c-1)\times(c-1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}}^{\top}

is positive semi-definite.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Biggl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*})^{\top}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Biggl]-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Biggl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\Sigma^{*}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Biggl]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Biggl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*})^{\top}\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)\Biggl]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Biggl[\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}\Biggl)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\Biggl)^{2}\Biggl]~.

But

\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}\Bigl)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}

and, because of the fact that
\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}]=\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}]=0
, and the variables
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}
are independent for
i\neq j
, we also have

\displaystyle\E\Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}\Biggl)^{2}\Biggl]

\displaystyle=k\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+k(k-1)\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+2k(k-1)\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)\,\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)\Bigl]~.

For similar reasons,

\E\Biggl[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\Biggl)^{2}\Biggl]=k\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+k(k-1)\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]~.

Hence we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}-\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{k-1}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}-\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2(k-1)}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)\,\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)\Bigl]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{k-1}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{2(k-1)}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)\,\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)\Bigl]~.

Also, note that

\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)\,\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)\Bigl]\leq\sqrt{\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]\,\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]}=\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]

since
\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]=\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{2}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]
. Plugging back
yields

\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq\frac{1}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{3(k-1)}{k}\E\Bigl[\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{2}\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]~.

(29)

Consider now the first expectation in (
29
). We have

\displaystyle\Bigl({\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{1}}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl((\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl((\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}-(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E})^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl((\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\Bigl)^{2}+2\Bigl((\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E})^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl(||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||_{\infty}||\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}||_{1}\Bigl)^{2}+2\Bigl(||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||_{\infty}||\boldsymbol{p}||_{1}\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle=4||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||^{2}_{\infty}

the last equality due to the fact that
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
are probability vectors. For the second expectation, the argument is exactly the same, just replace
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}
with
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}
.

Plugging back into (
29
) and overapproximating yields the handy upper bound

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq 16{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]~.

(30)

We can also eliminate the centering by observing the following:

\displaystyle{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]

\displaystyle\leq{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\bigl(||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||_{\infty}+||\boldsymbol{\E}||_{\infty}\bigl)^{2}\Bigl]

\displaystyle\leq 2{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]+2{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\E}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]

\displaystyle=2{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]+2||\boldsymbol{\E}||^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]+2{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]

(from the definition of vector
\E={\E}_{x}[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}]
, and the convexity of
||\cdot||^{2}_{\infty}
)

\displaystyle=4{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]~.

This results in

\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq 64{\E}_{x}\Bigl[||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}||^{2}_{\infty}\Bigl]=64\,{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\Bigl(\max_{r\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}}\ell(h(x),r)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]~,

as claimed.

C.4
Sub-Gaussian losses for the full histogram multi-class case

We can alternatively start from (
30
) and maintain centered variables.
Now, if the loss components
\ell(h(x),r)
are bounded by
L
for all
(x,y)
, and
r\in\{0,\ldots,c-1\}
, then clearly,

\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq 64L^{2}

independent of
c
. On the other hand, if the loss is not uniformly bounded, but is sub-Gaussian, then observe that
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}
is a zero-mean vector, and we can use standard inequalities for expectations of maxima of sub-Gaussian variables. For instance, the following claim comes in handy.

Proposition C.3
.

Let
X_{0},\ldots,X_{c-1}
be
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian random variable with zero mean (but not necessarily independent). Then, for all
t>0
,

\PP\Bigl(\Bigl(\max_{r=0,\ldots,c-1}X_{r}\Bigl)^{2}\geq 2\sigma^{2}(\log c+t)\Bigl)\leq 2e^{-t}~.

Proof.

Let
M=\max_{r=0,\ldots,c-1}X_{r}
, and
v=\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(\log c+t)}
. Then

\displaystyle\PP\Bigl(\Bigl(\max_{r=0,\ldots,c-1}X_{r}\Bigl)^{2}\geq 2\sigma^{2}(\log c+t)\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\PP(|M|\geq v)

\displaystyle\leq\PP(\exists r\,:\,|X_{r}|\geq v)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\PP(|X_{r}|\geq v)

\displaystyle\leq 2c\,e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}=2e^{-t}~,

thus concluding the proof.
∎

Now, when
M=||\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}||_{\infty}=\max_{r=0,\ldots,c-1}|\ell(h(x_{1}),r)-{\E}_{r}|
, with
{\E}_{r}={\E}_{x}[\ell(h(x),r)]
,
and
\epsilon=2\sigma^{2}\log c
, we have

\displaystyle\E[M^{2}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\PP(M^{2}>u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\PP(M^{2}>u)\,du+\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\PP(M^{2}>u)\,du

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+2\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\PP(M^{2}>\epsilon+2\sigma^{2}t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+2\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}2e^{-t}\,dt=\epsilon+4\sigma^{2}=2\sigma^{2}(\log c+2)~.

Thus if
\ell(h(x_{1}),r)-{\E}_{r}
are
\sigma^{2}
-subgaussian components of vector
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\E}
, then

\operatorname{Var}(\ell_{b}(h,z))\leq 12\Bigl(2\sigma^{2}(\log c+2)\Bigl)~,

hence, with a logarithmic dependence on
c
.

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{c}\,\max_{r}\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})~,

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}\,\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{r}\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\Biggl(\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}\Biggl)

\displaystyle=\max_{r}\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})~,

and similarly

\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}|\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{r}|\sqrt{p_{r}}\leq\sqrt{c}\,\max_{r}|\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{r}|~.

We have therefore obtained

\displaystyle\Bigl|{\E}_{z}[\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\,|\,S_{1},S_{2}]-{\E}_{z}[\Delta\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)]\Bigl|

\displaystyle=O\Biggl(\Biggl(\frac{\sqrt{c}\,\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{mk}}\Biggl)\,\,\sigma(h_{1},h_{2})\Biggl)+O\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{c\,\log(c/\delta)}{mk}}\,\max_{r}|\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{r}|\Biggl)~,

where
\sigma(h_{1},h_{2})=\max_{r}\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})
.
But clearly, from (
32
)

|\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{r}|\leq|\Delta{\E}_{r}|+A_{\delta,r}(h_{1},h_{2})=|\Delta{\E}_{r}|+O\left(\sigma_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\sqrt{\frac{\log(c\,|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{mk}}\right)~,

which we replace back into the previous display. This results in

\displaystyle\Bigl|{\E}_{z}[\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\,|\,S_{1},S_{2}]-{\E}_{z}[\Delta\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)]\Bigl|

\displaystyle=O\Biggl(\Biggl(\frac{\sqrt{c}\,\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{mk}}\Biggl)\,\,\sigma(h_{1},h_{2})\Biggl)+O\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{c\,\log(c/\delta)}{mk}}\,\max_{r}|\Delta{\E}_{r}|\Biggl)~.

(34)

As for
\operatorname{Var}_{z}(\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\,|\,S_{1},S_{2})
, we again observe that in the conditional space where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are given, the bound on this variance can be obtained by adapting the argument contained in Lemma
C.1
, as specified next.

Let

z=(\mathcal{B},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

with
\mathcal{B}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, and introduce the short-hand notation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\displaystyle=[\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{c-1}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=[\widehat{p}_{0},\ldots,\widehat{p}_{c-1}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\Delta\ell_{0}(h_{1},h_{2};x_{i}),\ldots,\Delta\ell_{c-1}(h_{1},h_{2};x_{i})]^{\top}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\E}

\displaystyle=[\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{0},\ldots,\widehat{\Delta}{\E}_{c-1}]^{\top}~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}

\displaystyle=[\eta_{0}(x_{i}),\ldots,\eta_{c-1}(x_{i})]^{\top}~,

and its (approximately) centered versions

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\widehat{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\E}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}~.

With this notation, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(\alpha_{r}-\widehat{p}_{S_{2},r})\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x_{i})-{\widehat{\E}}_{S_{1}}[\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})]\Bigl)+\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\widehat{p}_{S_{2},r}\,{\widehat{\E}}_{S_{1}}[\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)\,+\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\top}\widehat{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\E}~.

Thus, following the corresponding steps in the proof of Lemma
C.1
, in the conditional space where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are given (we again omit from the notation the conditioning on
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
for notational comfort), we can write

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{z}\Bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\Bigl)

\displaystyle\leq\E\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)\Bigl]~,

with

\displaystyle\E[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\,|\,\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Bigl(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*})^{\top}-\Sigma^{*}\Bigl)~,

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}~,\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{r}(x_{i})\eta_{s}(x_{i})\right]_{r,s=0}^{(c-1)\times(c-1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}}^{\top}~.

As a consequence, as in the proof of Lemma
C.1
,

\operatorname{Var}_{z}\Bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\Bigl)\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Biggl[\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}\Biggl)^{2}\Biggl]~.

But

\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}=\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E+(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E)~,\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p}+(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})~,

so that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)+k(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E)\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})+k(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)+k\,\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl)^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})

\displaystyle\qquad+k\,\Bigl(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)+k^{2}\,\Bigl(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl)^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})~,

and, using
(a+b+c+d)^{2}\leq 4a^{2}+4b^{2}+4c^{2}+4d^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{\Delta}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\Bigl)^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}\Biggl)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)\Biggl)^{2}+4k^{2}\,\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl)^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\Biggl)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+4k^{2}\,\Biggl(\Bigl(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)\Biggl)^{2}+4k^{4}\,\Biggl(\Bigl(\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl)^{\top}(\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\Biggl)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{4\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl)^{\top}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl)\Biggl)^{2}}_{(I)}+\underbrace{4k^{2}\,\Bigl|\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl|\Bigl|_{2}^{2}||\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}||_{2}^{2}}_{(II)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{4k^{2}\,\Bigl|\Bigl|\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl|\Bigl|^{2}_{2}\Bigl|\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl|\Bigl|_{2}^{2}}_{(III)}+\underbrace{4k^{4}\,\Bigl|\Bigl|\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl|\Bigl|^{2}_{2}\Bigl|\Bigl|\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}\Bigl|\Bigl|_{2}^{2}}_{(IV)}~.

Plugging back,

\operatorname{Var}_{z}\Bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\Bigl)\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(I)\Bigl]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(II)\Bigl]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(III)\Bigl]+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(IV)\Bigl]~.

We treat the four terms separately.
For the first term, we can immediately apply the variance bound in Lemma
C.1
, leading to

\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(I)\Bigl]\leq 64\,\E\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]~.

We know from (
33
) that

||\boldsymbol{p}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}||_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(B_{\delta,r}(h_{1},h_{2}))^{2}=O\left(\frac{\log(c/\delta)}{mk}\right)~,

and, from (
32
), that

\Bigl|\Bigl|\Delta\E-\widehat{\Delta}\E\Bigl|\Bigl|^{2}_{2}\leq\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}(A_{\delta,r}(h_{1},h_{2}))^{2}=O\left(\frac{\log(c\,|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{mk}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\right)~.

Moreover, since the vectors
\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E
and the vectors
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p}
are centered,

\E\Biggl[\Bigl|\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}-\Delta\E)\Bigl|\Bigl|_{2}^{2}\Biggl]=\E\Biggl[\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x_{i})-\Delta{\E}_{r})\Bigl)^{2}\Biggl]=k\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})

and

\displaystyle\E\Biggl[\Bigl|\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{p})\Bigl|\Bigl|_{2}^{2}\Biggl]

\displaystyle=\E\Biggl[\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\eta_{r}(x_{i})-p_{r})\Bigl)^{2}\Biggl]

\displaystyle=k\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{r}(x))

\displaystyle\leq k\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}(1-p_{r})

\displaystyle\leq k\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}p_{r}=k~.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(II)\Bigl]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\log(c/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(III)\Bigl]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\,\E\Bigl[(IV)\Bigl]

\displaystyle=O\left(\Biggl(\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\Biggl)^{2}\,\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\right)~.

Piecing together, and leveraging the condition
m=\Omega(\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta))
yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{z}\Bigl(\widetilde{\Delta}\ell_{b}(h_{1},h_{2};z)\Bigl)=O\left(\E\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})\right)~.

(35)

We are now ready to combine (
31
), (
34
), and (
35
). If we assume that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have been drawn in such a way that both (
31
) and (
34
) simultaneously hold for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\PP\Biggl(\,\forall\,h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\,\,\,\Bigl|Q(h_{1},h_{2};S)-\Delta\mathcal{L}(h_{1},h_{2})\Bigl|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq O\Biggl(\sqrt{\E\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})}\,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sqrt{\frac{c\,\log(c/\delta)}{mk}}\,\max_{r}|\Delta{\E}_{r}|\Biggl)\Biggl)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta~.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we define

\displaystyle C_{\delta}

\displaystyle(h_{1},h_{2},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k,c)

\displaystyle=O\Biggl(\sqrt{\E\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})}\,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sqrt{\frac{c\,\log(c/\delta)}{mk}}\,\max_{r}|\Delta{\E}_{r}|\Biggl)

and make further overapproximations to simplify the above expression. In particular, we set

\Delta\E=[{\E}_{0},\ldots,{\E}_{c-1}]^{\top}=[{\E}[\Delta\ell_{0}(h_{1},h_{2};x)],\ldots,{\E}[\Delta\ell_{c-1}(h_{1},h_{2};x)]]^{\top}=\E[\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}]~,

with

\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}=[\Delta\ell_{0}(h_{1},h_{2};x)],\ldots,\Delta\ell_{c-1}(h_{1},h_{2};x)]]^{\top}~.

Then note that, by the convexity of
||\cdot||_{\infty}
,

\max_{r}|\Delta{\E}_{r}|=||\Delta{\E}||_{\infty}~\leq\E[||\Delta\boldsymbol{\ell}||_{\infty}]={\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}|\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)|\Bigl]~.

Then

\displaystyle C_{\delta}(h_{1},h_{2},|\mathcal{H}|,m,k,c)

\displaystyle=O\Biggl(\sqrt{\E\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]+\frac{\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}\,\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h_{1},h_{2})}\,\,\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sqrt{\frac{c\log(c/\delta)}{mk}}\,{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}|\Delta\ell_{r}(h_{1},h_{2};x)|\Bigl]\Biggl)~.

We then apply Proposition
B.1
with
h_{1}=h\in\mathcal{H}_{\beta/4}
and
h_{2}=\widehat{h}^{\star}
, and following the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we conclude that the resulting sample complexity
is

\displaystyle m=O\Biggl(\frac{\Delta\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})\,\log(|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{\Sigma(\mathcal{H})\,\log(c|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{\beta}+\frac{c\,\Bigl(\Delta|\ell|(\mathcal{H})\Bigl)^{2}\,\log(c/\delta)}{\beta^{2}\,k}\Biggl)~,

where

\displaystyle\Delta\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]~,

\displaystyle\Delta|\ell|(\mathcal{H})=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl|\Delta\ell_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};x)\Bigl|\Bigl]~,

and

\displaystyle\Sigma(\mathcal{H})=\sqrt{\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{r=0}^{c-1}\sigma^{2}_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star})}~.

We further simplify the above expression by noting that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\Delta|\ell|(\mathcal{H})\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl|\Delta\ell_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};x)\Bigl|\Bigl]\Bigl)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\Bigl(\max_{r}\Bigl|\Delta\ell_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};x)\Bigl|\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\E}_{x}\Bigl[\max_{r}\Bigl(\Delta\ell_{r}(h,\widehat{h}^{\star};x)\Bigl)^{2}\Bigl]

\displaystyle=\Delta\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})~.

Plugging back and rearranging concludes the proof.

Appendix D
Experimental Setup for Section
4
and Further Experimental Results

This appendix contains further details on our experimental setup, as well as test set results based on the Area Under the Curve (AUC) metric.

D.1
Datasets and Models

MNIST:

MNIST
[
21
]
is a multi-class image prediction task where each example contains a
28\times 28
grayscale image of a hand-written digit and the label indicates which digit it is.
The data consists of 60,000 training images and 10,000 test images.
We consider a binarized version of MNIST where digits in
\{0,2,4,6,8\}
are positive examples and digits on
\{1,3,5,7,9\}
are negative, so that the task is to predict whether the digit is even or odd.
The model we use for MNIST is a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with the following layers: Convolution with 32 filters, max pooling with
2\times 2
window and stride, Convolution with 64 features, dropout layer with rate
0.5
, and finally a fully connected layer with a single output.
The two convolutional layers are followed by ReLU activations, while the final output has no activation so that it outputs a logit.

CIFAR-10:

CIFAR-10
[
20
]
is a multi-class image prediction task where each example contains a
32\times 32
color image which belongs to one of the following classes:
Airplane, Automobile, Bird, Cat, Deer, Dog, Frog, Horse, Ship,
or
Truck
.
The training data consists of 50,000 images and 10,000 test images.
We consider two binarized versions of CIFAR-10: Animal-vs-Machine, where the positive labels are
Bird, Cat, Deer, Dog, Frog,
and
Horse
, and Cat-vs-Rest, where
Cat
is the only positive label.
We use the same CNN architecture for CIFAR-10 as was used in MNIST.

Higgs:

The Higgs dataset
[
1
]
is a simulated dataset where the goal is to distinguish between processes that produce Higgs bosons and a background process that does not.
The dataset consists of 11,000,000 examples and we split them into training and testing by taking the first 10,000 examples as test examples with the remaining examples as being training examples.
Each example has 21 features which are a mix of directly measured properties of the system and hand-crafted high level features.
Our model class is a fully connected model with 4 hidden layers each having 300 neurons and ReLU activations followed by a fully connected layer with 1 output and no activation so that it outputs a logit.

Adult:

The Adult dataset
[
18
]
is a dataset derived from the 1994 US Census Database where the goal is to predict whether an individual’s income exceeds $50,000 per year or not.
The features include several categorical and numerical features describing the individual.
We pre-process the data by one-hot encoding all categorical features and rescaling each numerical feature so that the values fall within the interval
[0,1]
.
Our model class is a fully connected network with a single hidden layer with 32 neurons and ReLU activation, followed by the output layer with no activation.

Criteo:

The Criteo Display Advertising Challenge
[
42
]
is a competition where the goal is to predict whether a given ad impression will result in the user clicking on the ad.
Each example is described by a total of 39 features, 13 of which are integer “count” based features, and 26 of which are categorical features.
To preserve privacy, Criteo pre-processed the dataset by hashing each of the categorical features into 32 bit hashes, and they do not provide high-level descriptions of what the original feature values corresponded to.
We use only the training data which consits of 37M ad impressions together with whether they were clicked or not.
The examples are ordered by ad impression time and come from a span of 7 days.
An important characteristic of this dataset is that the click rate changes periodically.
The model class we use for the Criteo dataset is a Deep Embedding Model, which works by mapping each feature value to an embedding vector and then applying several fully connected layers to the concatenated embeddings.
To embed the integer-valued features, we first discretize each feature into buckets using logarithmically-spaced bucket boundaries with 40 boundaries.
Then, each bucket is associated with a learned embedding vector.
For each categorical feature, we map the hashed value to an integer bucket by mapping hashing again and taking the modulus with a per-feature number of buckets.
For all integer features, we use 40 buckets and an embedding dimension of 10.

The final output layer has no activation as it outputs a logit.

D.2
Instance-level Losses and Label Smoothing

For completeness, in this section we briefly define the instance-level losses that we use in our experiments.
These losses are transformed into bag-level losses via the recipes for each of
GeneralUPM, EasyLLP
, and
PM
.

In the online Criteo learning experiment, we instantiate each LLP loss for the binary cross-entropy instance-level loss.
That is, when the model predicts a logit
\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
for an example with label
y\in\{0,1\}
, the loss is given by

\ell_{\text{ce}}(\hat{y},y)=-y\log\bigl(\sigma(\hat{y})\bigr)-(1-y)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(\hat{y})\bigr),

where
\sigma
is the logistic function.
We use the otpax
[
11
]
implementation of the cross entropy loss which is implemented in log space to avoid numerical issues.

For the batch learning experiments, we modify the cross-entropy loss to use
label smoothing
, which is a common technique for preventing models from becoming overly confident over long training runs.
Concretely, this corresponds to defining a new loss

\ell_{\text{ces}}(\hat{y},y)=\ell_{\text{ce}}\bigl(\hat{y},(1-\epsilon)y+\epsilon/2\bigr),

where
\epsilon
is a parameter controlling the strength of the label smoothing.
In our batch experiments we simply choose
\epsilon=0.1
.
Note that we do not actually modify the labels of the training data.
We just apply
EasyLLP, GeneralUPM
and
PM
to the loss
\ell_{\text{ces}}
.

D.3
Bag-level Loss Implementations

In this section we review the definitions of
EasyLLP
and
PM
, together with details of how our implementations estimate the quantities
p
,
\E[f_{1}(x)]
and
\E[f_{2}(x)]
appearing in the definitions of
EasyLLP
and
GeneralUPM
.
Throughout this section, fix a bag
z=(\mathcal{B},\alpha)=((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\alpha)
and instance-level loss function
\ell
(like
\ell_{\text{ce}}
or
\ell_{\text{ces}}
from the previous section).

Proportion Matching (
PM
).

The bag-level log loss for
PM
is simply defined as

\ell_{\text{PM}}(h,z)=\ell(h(\mathcal{B}),\alpha)

where

h(\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{k}\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}h(x_{i})~.

EasyLLP

[
5
]
.

Starting from an instance-level loss
\ell
, the bag-level Easy LLP loss is defined as

\ell_{\textsc{EasyLLP}}(h,z)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(k(\alpha-p)+p\bigr)\cdot\ell(h(x_{i}),1)+\bigl(k(p-\alpha)+(1-p)\bigr)\cdot\ell(h(x_{i}),0).

Implementation of Parameter Estimation.

The definitions of both
EasyLLP
and
GeneralUPM
require estimates of the label marginal
p=\PP(y=1)
, which is the probability of observing a positive example from the underlying distribution.
Beyond this,
GeneralUPM
also requires estimates of
\E[f_{1}(x)]
and
\E[f_{2}(x)]
.
In this section we describe how those quantities are estimated in our experiments.

Label Marginal
p
.

In our batch learning experiments we estimate
p
to be the average label proportion among all the bags in the training data.
This calculation is done once before training begins and the same value of
p
is used in every evaluation of the LLP loss functions.
For the online Criteo experiment, as described in the main body, we process the data in chunks containing
2^{16}
examples.
After reading each chunk, we estimate
p
to be the average label proportion among all bags in the chunk, and that value is used in every evaluation of the LLP loss function for that chunk alone.
This is essential for achieving good performance on the Criteo dataset because the actual click rate (i.e. value of
p
) changes over time due to changes in the set of users interacting with ads at different times of the day, new ad campains launching, etc.

Model’s Average Loss.

Recall that
f_{1}(x)=\ell(h(x),0)
and
f_{2}(x)=\ell(h(x),1)-\ell(h(x),0)
correspond to the model’s loss on example
x
if the true label were
0
, and the difference in loss between label
1
and
0
, respectively.
Since these quantities depend on the model parameters, we cannot estimate them once at the beginning of training and treat them as a constant.
In particular, their values change during training, but also the gradient of these terms with respect to the model parameters is non-zero.
To estimate the expected value of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
on a random
x
drawn from the data distribution we employ the following scheme:
Each SGD training batch consists of at least
B\geq 2
bags.
When evaluating the
GeneralUPM
loss on one bag within a batch, we estimate the expected value of
f_{1}(x)
and
f_{2}(x)
by averaging their values over the feature vectors contained in the remaining
B-1
batches.
This results in unbiased estimates that are uncorrelated with the other components of the LLP loss for that bag.
The variance of the resulting estimate depends on the relative size of the bag and batch size, since when the bag size approaches the batch size, we have fewer examples for estimating the expectations.
