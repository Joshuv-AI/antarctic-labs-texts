BE Lyncis: An Extremely Eccentric Binary with the Nearest Known Black Hole
Jia-Shu Niu (牛家树), Ying Zhang (张颖), Hui-Fang Xue (薛会芳)

Abstract
We report the discovery of an exceptionally eccentric binary system, BE Lyncis (BE Lyn), which hosts the nearest known black hole (BH) to Earth. Through the analysis of TESS photometry combined with an extensive set of times of maximum light spanning 39 years, we identify BE Lyn as a high-amplitude δ \delta Scuti star in a binary with an orbital period of ≈ 15.9 \approx 15.9 years and an extraordinary orbital eccentricity of e = 0.9989 − 0.0021 + 0.0008 e=0.9989^{+0.0008}_{-0.0021} ( > 0.9968 >0.9968 at 95% confidence)—the highest reliably measured for any binary system. Dynamical constraints impose an upper limit on the orbital inclination of i ≲ 4.0 ∘ i\lesssim 4.0^{\circ} , corresponding to a companion mass of M 2 ≳ 17.5 ​ M ⊙ M_{2}\gtrsim 17.5~M_{\odot} , which unequivocally favors a black hole. This system provides a unique laboratory for studying asteroseismology in strong gravitational fields, the formation of black holes via asymmetric supernovae, and the evolution of extreme binary systems. Our work demonstrates, for the first time, the successful application of the light-travel time effect in a pulsating variable to unveil a dormant black hole, establishing a novel method for BH detection in non-interacting binaries.



I
Introduction

Black holes (BHs) of stellar origin represent one of the most enigmatic predictions of general relativity. While several dozen stellar-mass BHs have been identified in X-ray binaries through accretion signatures
(
R. W. Romani 1998
)
, the population of dormant (non-accreting) BHs in detached binaries remains largely unexplored. Detecting such systems is challenging, as they typically reveal themselves only through subtle gravitational interactions with luminous companions. Recent astrometric surveys, such as
Gaia
, have uncovered a handful of such quiescent BH binaries
(
K. El-Badry et al. 2023
;
A. Tanikawa et al. 2023
;
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2024
)
, yet they remain exceedingly rare. An independent, complementary detection method—leveraging the precise clocks provided by pulsating stars—offers a promising pathway to expand this census and probe different regions of parameter space.

Pulsating
\delta
Scuti stars are particularly well-suited for this purpose. These stars, with spectral types A–F, occupy the intersection of the main sequence and the classical Cepheid instability strip in the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. Their oscillations, driven by the
\kappa
mechanism operating in the partial ionization zone of helium, provide remarkably stable periodic signals with periods ranging from 15 minutes to 8 hours
(
T. Kallinger et al. 2008
;
G. Handler 2009
;
D. B. Guenther et al. 2009
;
K. Uytterhoeven et al. 2011
;
D. L. Holdsworth et al. 2014
;
T. Steindl et al. 2022
)
. The subclass of high-amplitude
\delta
Scuti stars (HADS) exhibits photometric variations exceeding 0.1 mag and typically rotates slowly (
v\sin i\leq 30
km s
-1
), making them ideal precision tools for detecting the minite period modulations induced by orbital motion in binary systems—the light-travel time effect (LTTE).

BE Lyncis (BE Lyn; HD 79889) is a well-studied HADS with a mean magnitude of
\langle V\rangle=8.8
mag, spectral type A3, and sub-solar metallicity ([Fe/H] =
-
0.598)
(
C. Kim et al. 2012
;
Abdurro’uf et al. 2022
)
. It was first identified as a variable star by
T. Oja 1986
and later confirmed as a HADS with a pulsation period of
\sim
0.09587 days
(
T. Oja 1987
)
. Subsequent studies of its period variations revealed anomalies in
O-C
diagrams, occasionally suggestive of a parabolic trend indicative of a binary companion
(
E. Rodriguez et al. 1990
;
Y. Liu et al. 1991
;
E. Wunder et al. 1992
;
C. Boonyarak et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
. Several investigations indeed proposed the existence of a low-mass companion
(
L. L. Kiss & K. Szatmary 1995
;
A. Derekas et al. 2009
;
C. Boonyarak et al. 2011
;
J. H. Peña et al. 2015
;
A. Liakos & P. Niarchos 2017
)
, though inferred orbital parameters varied significantly due to limitations in the temporal coverage and precision of the data.

Two key developments now enable a definitive reassessment of this system. First, high-precision, continuous photometry from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (
TESS
) allows for an exquisite characterization of the primary star’s pulsation modes and yields significantly more accurate times of maximum light. Second, modern computational techniques, such as robust multi-parameter global fitting via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, permit a physically consistent and precise determination of binary orbital parameters.

Here, we present a comprehensive analysis of BE Lyn, combining
TESS
photometry with a newly compiled, 39-year baseline of times of maximum light. Our work not only confirms its binary nature but reveals an astrophysical gem: an ultra-wide, extremely eccentric orbit harboring a massive black hole companion. This system holds multiple distinctions: (i) it possesses the highest orbital eccentricity ever reliably measured in a binary; (ii) it hosts the closest known black hole to Earth, at a distance of approximately 250 pc
(
C. A. L. Bailer-Jones et al. 2021
)
; (iii) it is the only known binary comprising a pulsating star and a quiescent black hole, opening a new window for asteroseismology in strong gravity; and (iv) its extreme orbital configuration places stringent constraints on the natal kick imparted during the black hole’s formation, challenging models of asymmetric supernova explosions. The detection methodology itself—using pulsation timing in a
\delta
Scuti star—pioneers a new, independent channel for discovering dormant black holes.

II
Results and Discussion

II.1
Pulsation Properties and the Stellar Clock

Analysis of the
TESS
photometry (Figure
A1
) reveals BE Lyn as a multi-periodic pulsator. We identify 37 significant frequencies, comprising 7 independent modes and 30 harmonics or combination frequencies (Figure
A2
, Table
A1
). The two dominant frequencies,
f_{0}=10.4309\pm 0.0005
c/d and
f_{1}=13.4226\pm 0.0005
c/d, exhibit a period ratio of
P_{1}/P_{0}=0.777
, unambiguously identifying them as the fundamental and first-overtone radial modes, respectively. The fundamental mode amplitude dominates the light curve, exceeding that of the first overtone by a factor of
\sim 45
and the remaining independent modes by factors
>700
(Figure
A2
). This clear amplitude hierarchy renders the
f_{0}
pulsation a remarkably clean and stable clock, enabling the application of the classical
O-C
method to detect minute orbital perturbations with negligible contamination from multi-periodic effects. The five additional independent frequencies (
f_{2}
–
f_{6}
), residing in the
9-17
c/d range with low amplitudes, are likely non-radial p-modes. This rich pulsation spectrum establishes BE Lyn as a promising target for future detailed asteroseismic modeling.

II.3
Characterization of the Pulsating Primary

To place dynamical constraints in context, we determined the properties of the primary star through asteroseismic modeling. We computed a grid of stellar evolutionary tracks using MESA
(
B. Paxton et al. 2011
;
B. Paxton et al. 2013
;
B. Paxton et al. 2015
;
B. Paxton et al. 2018
;
B. Paxton et al. 2019
;
A. S. Jermyn et al. 2023
, Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics;)
and corresponding adiabatic pulsation frequencies using GYRE
(
R. H. D. Townsend & S. A. Teitler 2013
;
R. H. D. Townsend et al. 2018
;
J. Goldstein & R. H. D. Townsend 2020
;
M. Sun et al. 2023
)
. The optimal model, selected by matching the observed
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
, and the intrinsic period variation rate
(1/P_{0})(dP_{0}/dt)
, is summarized in Table
A2
and located on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram in Figure
2
.

The best-fit model places the primary on the post-main sequence with a mass of
M_{1}=1.55M_{\odot}
, effective temperature
\log T_{\mathrm{eff}}=3.902
, and luminosity
\log(L/L_{\odot})=1.326
. The agreement between the observed
(1.02\pm 0.08)\times 10^{-8}
yr
-1
and modeled (
1.34\times 10^{-8}
yr
-1
) period variation rates is noteworthy. Unlike some HADS in binaries that exhibit anomalously large period variations due to mass transfer
(
H.-F. Xue & J.-S. Niu 2020
;
M. Lian et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, BE Lyn’s primary appears consistent with single-star evolution. This suggests the system is currently detached and has not undergone significant mass exchange.

II.4
A Nearby Black Hole in an Extreme Orbit

Combining the orbital parameters with the primary mass (
M_{1}=1.55M_{\odot}
) introduces critical constraints from orbital stability: (i) the periastron separation must exceed the primary’s radius to avoid catastrophic collision; (ii) the primary’s Roche lobe radius must exceed the primary’s radius to avoid Roche-lobe overflow near the periastron. Applying these conditions, along with the mass function (Eq.
A3
), tightly constrains the system geometry (Figure
3
). We derive an upper limit on the orbital inclination of
i\lesssim 4.0^{\circ}
, implying a lower limit on the companion mass of
M_{2}\gtrsim 17.5M_{\odot}
.

A compact object of this mass cannot be supported by electron or neutron degeneracy pressure, ruling out a white dwarf or neutron star. The companion must therefore be a black hole. With a
Gaia
DR3 distance of approximately 250 pc
(
C. A. L. Bailer-Jones et al. 2021
)
, this black hole becomes the nearest known to Earth, residing about half the distance of the previous record-holders Gaia BH1 (480 pc
(
K. El-Badry et al. 2023
)
) and Gaia BH3 (590 pc
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2024
)
). Its proximity offers unparalleled opportunities for detailed follow-up study.

The system’s most enigmatic feature—its extreme eccentricity—holds the key to its formation history. The absence of evidence for a dense stellar environment makes a dynamical scattering origin unlikely. Instead, the
e\approx 0.9989
orbit is a natural signature of a strong natal kick imparted to the black hole during an asymmetric supernova explosion
(
H.-T. Janka 2013
;
S. Popov et al. 2025
)
. BE Lyn thus provides a pristine laboratory to constrain kick velocities and the physics of mass ejection in fallback supernovae, challenging models to account for the most eccentric black-hole binary known.

II.5
Broader Implications and Future Prospects

BE Lyn represents a system of singular importance across multiple astrophysical domains. First, it is the only known binary pairing a pulsating star with a quiescent black hole. This unique configuration opens the novel field of
strong-gravity asteroseismology
, where the pulsations of the primary could, in principle, probe the local spacetime metric perturbed by the black hole companion, especially during the brief, close periastron passages.

Second, the discovery validates the pulsation-timing method as a powerful, independent technique for detecting dormant stellar-mass black holes in wide binaries. This approach is complementary to radial-velocity and astrometric surveys, being particularly sensitive to long-period systems where other methods require extended baselines.

Key questions remain. Precise dynamical mass measurements via future astrometric missions (e.g.,
Gaia
,
Theia
) or very-long-baseline interferometry could pin down
M_{2}
and
i
. Multi-wavelength searches for accretion signatures or bow-shock emission are warranted, despite the wide separation. Theoretical work is urgently needed to simulate binary evolution pathways that yield such extreme eccentricities post-supernova.

In conclusion, BE Lyn is not merely another binary system; it is a benchmark object. It challenges our understanding of black hole formation kicks, offers a new tool for black hole discovery, and provides a unique cosmic laboratory at our galactic doorstep.

III
Conclusion

We have established BE Lyncis as an extraordinary binary system. The primary is a post-main sequence high-amplitude
\delta
Scuti star with a mass of
1.55\,M_{\odot}
. Its unseen companion orbits with an unprecedented eccentricity of
e=0.9989^{+0.0008}_{-0.0021}
(95% confidence
>
0.9968), making this the most eccentric binary known to date. This extreme orbit, coupled with dynamical constraints that demand a companion mass
M_{2}\gtrsim 17.5\,M_{\odot}
at an orbital inclination
i\lesssim 4.0^{\circ}
, unequivocally identifies the companion as a stellar-mass black hole. With a distance of approximately 250 pc, this black hole is the closest known to Earth.

The discovery of BE Lyn carries profound implications across several astrophysical frontiers. First, its record-breaking eccentricity serves as a direct observational constraint on the strength of natal kicks imparted during asymmetric supernova explosions, challenging models of black hole formation. Second, it is the first known binary comprising a pulsating star and a quiescent black hole, creating a unique laboratory for strong-gravity asteroseismology. Third, this work validates the pulsation-timing method—using the precise clocks of
\delta
Scuti stars—as a novel, independent pathway to discover dormant black holes in wide binaries, thereby opening a new observational channel in the quest to unveil the hidden compact-object population.

Key questions regarding the exact black hole mass, the detailed evolutionary pathway leading to such an extreme configuration, and the system’s ultimate fate remain. Future multi-wavelength observations, astrometric monitoring, and theoretical modeling of supernova kicks in binary systems will be essential to fully understand the origins and implications of this remarkable cosmic benchmark.

Appendix A
Methods

A.1
Photometric Data and Frequency Analysis

BE Lyn was observed by the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (
TESS
) in Sector 21 (January 2020) with a 120-second cadence. We retrieved the Presearch Data Conditioning Simple Aperture Photometry (PDCSAP) flux from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) and converted it to magnitudes using the
TESS
magnitude system
(
J. C. Smith et al. 2012
;
M. C. Stumpe et al. 2012
;
M. C. Stumpe et al. 2014
;
K. G. Stassun et al. 2019
)
.

We extracted pulsation frequencies using an iterative prewhitening procedure
(
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2022
;
J.-S. Niu et al. 2023
;
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2024
)
. The light curve was modeled as a superposition of sinusoids:

m(t)=m_{0}+\sum_{i}a_{i}\sin[2\pi(f_{i}t+\phi_{i})],

(A1)

where
m_{0}
is the mean magnitude, and
a_{i}
,
f_{i}
, and
\phi_{i}
are the amplitude, frequency, and phase of each component. Frequencies were accepted as significant if their signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) exceeded 5.6
(
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2022
;
J.-S. Niu et al. 2023
;
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2024
)
. After removing aliases, we identified 37 significant peaks, consisting of 7 independent modes and 30 harmonics or linear combinations (Table
A1
).

A.2
Compilation and Processing of Times of Maximum Light

The light curve is dominated by the fundamental mode (
f_{0}
), with the first overtone (
f_{1}
) contributing less than 3% of its amplitude
(
W. Xue et al. 2023
)
. This allows the classical
O-C
method
(
C. Sterken 2005
)
to be applied without significant contamination from multi-periodic effects.

We compiled times of maximum light (TML) from three sources: (1) historical literature, (2) the American Association of Variable Star Observers (AAVSO) database, and (3)
TESS
Sector 21 photometry. For AAVSO and
TESS
data, we determined TML by fitting a cubic polynomial to each light-curve maximum, with uncertainties estimated via Monte Carlo simulations.

To ensure temporal consistency, all times were converted to Barycentric Julian Date in the TDB system (BJD
TDB
). Heliocentric Julian Dates (HJD) were transformed using the tool of
J. Eastman et al. 2010
, while Julian Dates (JD) were converted using the
Astropy
time package.

Our final dataset includes 445 TML spanning 39 years (1986–2025), after excluding 13 outliers identified in a preliminary
O-C
analysis. For TML from
C. Boonyarak et al. 2011
published without uncertainties, we adopted a conservative error of 0.0001 d, consistent with their reported precision and twice the mean uncertainty of our
TESS
-based measurements.

A.3
Orbital Modeling via MCMC

The
O-C
residuals were modeled with a function combining a quadratic term (intrinsic period variation) and a light-travel time effect (LTTE) term for orbital motion
(
H.-F. Xue & J.-S. Niu 2020
)
:

C=\mathrm{BJD}_{0}+P_{0}E+\frac{1}{2}\beta E^{2}+A\left[\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin{\phi}\cos{\omega}+\cos{\phi}\sin{\omega}\right],

(A2)

where
\phi
is the eccentric anomaly from Kepler’s equation,
\mathrm{BJD}_{0}
is the reference epoch,
P_{0}
the pulsation period,
\beta
the linear period variation rate,
A\equiv a\sin i/c
the projected semi-major axis,
e
the orbital eccentricity,
\omega
the argument of periastron,
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
the orbital period, and
T_{0}
the time of periastron passage.

We employed a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach using the
emcee
package
(
D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
to sample the posterior distributions of all parameters. After chain convergence, we adopted the median of each posterior as the best-fit value, with uncertainties derived from the 5th and 95th percentiles. The best-fit model (
\chi^{2}/\mathrm{d.o.f.}=35.23
) is shown in Figure
1
, with parameters listed in Table
1
.

A.4
Asteroseismic Modeling of the Primary Star

We estimated the primary star’s properties by matching the observed pulsation characteristics to stellar models. Using MESA (Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics)
(
B. Paxton et al. 2011
;
B. Paxton et al. 2013
;
B. Paxton et al. 2015
;
B. Paxton et al. 2018
;
B. Paxton et al. 2019
;
A. S. Jermyn et al. 2023
)
, we computed a grid of evolutionary tracks from the pre-main sequence to the red giant branch, with masses from 1.3 to 2.3
M_{\odot}
in steps of 0.01
M_{\odot}
and metallicity
Z=0.0034
(from APOGEE-2
(
Abdurro’uf et al. 2022
)
, corresponding to [Fe/H] =
-
0.598). At each time step, we calculated adiabatic oscillation frequencies using GYRE
(
R. H. D. Townsend & S. A. Teitler 2013
;
R. H. D. Townsend et al. 2018
;
J. Goldstein & R. H. D. Townsend 2020
;
M. Sun et al. 2023
)
.
More details about the theoretical calculation can be referred to in
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2022
and
J.-S. Niu & H.-F. Xue 2025
.

The optimal model was selected by minimizing
\chi^{2}
across three observables: the fundamental frequency (
f_{0}
), first-overtone frequency (
f_{1}
), and the intrinsic period variation rate
(1/P_{0})(dP_{0}/dt)
. This methodology follows established asteroseismic analyses of HADS
(
J.-S. Niu et al. 2017
;
H.-F. Xue et al. 2018
;
H.-F. Xue et al. 2022
)
. The best-fit model parameters are given in Table
A2
.

A.5
Dynamical Constraints on the Companion

The companion’s nature was inferred from the mass function,

f(M_{2})=\frac{(M_{2}\sin i)^{3}}{(M_{1}+M_{2})^{2}}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{GP_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}}(a\sin i)^{3},

(A3)

combined with the constraints that the periastron separation
d_{\mathrm{peri}}=a(1-e)(M_{1}+M_{2})/M_{2}
must exceed the primary’s radius
R_{1}
, and the Roche lobe radius
R_{1,\mathrm{R}}
must exceed the primary’s radius
R_{1}
. The Roche lobe radius was calculated using the approximation of
P. P. Eggleton 1983
. This yields an upper limit on the inclination (
i\lesssim 4.0^{\circ}
) and a corresponding lower limit on the companion mass (
M_{2}\gtrsim 17.5M_{\odot}
), as shown in Figure
3
.
