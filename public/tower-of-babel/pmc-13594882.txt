Comparative analysis of meat quality, nutritional composition and gut microbiota in different breeds of meat-type quails
Authors: Xiaolong He, Ji Jiang, Yan Wu, Jinsong Pi, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuail meat is favored by consumers because of its low fat and cholesterol contents. In this study, the differences in carcass traits, meat quality, serum biochemical profiles, amino acid and fatty acid profiles, and cecal microbiota among Chinese white feather quail (CWQ), Savimalt meat quail (SMQ), and French giant quail (FGQ) were systematically evaluated. The results revealed that the FGQ and SMQ exhibited significantly greater body weight, carcass weight, and breast muscle weight than the CWQ did (P < 0.05). In addition, the CWQ showed greater lightness (L*) and shear force, whereas the FGQ exhibited greater redness (a*) (P < 0.05). With respect to serum biochemical indices, only the serum albumin and creatinine levels differed among the breeds (P < 0.05). In terms of nutritional composition, the protein and multiple amino acid contents in the breast muscle of the FGQ and SMQ were greater, whereas the C20:4n6 and C22:6n3 contents were greater in the CWQ breast muscle (P < 0.05). Moreover, the crude fat content in the CWQ and FGQ was significantly greater than that in the SMQ (P < 0.05). 16S rRNA sequencing revealed significant differences in cecal microbial community structure among the breeds. Several bacterial genera, including Bifidobacterium and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group, were significantly associated with amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, and meat quality traits. In conclusion, significant differences in meat quality, nutritional components, and intestinal microbial composition were detected among different breeds of meat-type quails. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis for the breeding and quality regulation of meat quails.Keywords: Quail, Meat quality, Amino acid, Fatty acid, Gut microbiota

Introduction

The rapid development of the global livestock industry has effectively met the increasing demand for high-quality animal protein. With the growing awareness of healthy diets, consumers are paying more attention to meat products that are high in protein and low in fat and cholesterol, which has further promoted the development of the quail industry (Mnisi et al., 2021; El Sabry et al., 2022). Compared with red meats such as pork, beef, and mutton, as well as common poultry meats such as chicken and duck, quail meat occupies a unique position in the consumer market because of its lower fat and cholesterol content, as well as its rich bioactive components such as lecithin and cephalin (Ikhlas et al., 2010; Ionita et al., 2011; Santhi and Kalaikannan, 2017).

Meat quality is an important indicator for evaluating the economic value of meat-type poultry. Previous studies have demonstrated that meat quality is significantly influenced by genetic background, with distinct breeds exhibiting marked variations in meat color, tenderness, and nutritional composition (Xiao et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). A comparative study by Wegner et al. (2024) revealed that compared with Japanese quails, Pharaoh quails have significantly higher intramuscular fat (IMF) and water contents in their breast muscle but also display distinct breed-specific characteristics in terms of protein content and myofiber microstructure. Furthermore, within the same species, Japanese quails with different genetic backgrounds present significant differences in growth performance, meat quality, and amino acid profiles (Nasr et al., 2017). Further studies have indicated that different production purposes (meat type vs. egg type) within the same breed also affect the nutritional composition of the muscle (Lukanov et al., 2023). However, current research on quail meat quality remains largely focused on Japanese quails and dual-purpose breeds, while systematic investigations into the differences in meat quality among specialized meat-type lines remain relatively limited.

In addition to host genetic factors, the gut microbiota, as a key regulator of host metabolism, has attracted increasing attention because of its role in meat quality. The gut microbiota participates in nutrient digestion and absorption, immune regulation, and metabolite production, thereby profoundly influencing muscle traits in animals (Chen et al., 2022). In broiler research, an enterotype characterized by the Clostridia_vadinBB60_group was found to significantly increase muscle fat deposition (Wen et al., 2023). The enrichment of specific genera, such as Alistipes, Synergistes, and Subdoligranulum, may promote IMF accumulation by mediating bile acid metabolic pathways (Long et al., 2024). Moreover, the regulation of meat quality by the gut microbiota is characterized by cross-breed plasticity; remodeling the intestinal microecology via FMT can significantly alter myofiber diameter and reduce drip loss in broilers (Lei et al., 2022). Nevertheless, systematic characterization of the gut microbial features of different meat-type quail breeds and their relationships with meat quality traits remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to systematically compare the differences in meat quality components among various meat quail breeds and, by integrating gut microbiomics analysis, to investigate the characteristics of the breed-specific gut microbiota and their correlations with meat quality traits. This study provides a theoretical basis for the selection of high-quality meat-type quail breeds, meat quality evaluation, and subsequent nutritional regulation strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and feeding management

A total of 300 one-day-old male quails were used in this study. The cohort included Chinese white feather meat quail (CWQ), Savimalt meat quail (SMQ), and French giant quail (FGQ), with 100 birds per breed. The birds hatched in the same batch from eggs supplied by Hubei Shendan Co., Ltd. From 1 to 2 weeks of age, the birds were housed in a disinfected brooding facility. Three-tier stepped cages (40 cm L × 40 cm W) were used, with 10 birds per cage. At 3 weeks of age, the birds were transferred to individual cages and reared until 7 weeks of age. The individual cages were arranged in a five-tier stepped system (29 cm L × 22 cm W × 24 cm H). Each cage was equipped with an automatic feeder and nipple drinker, and feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experiment. All birds were vaccinated according to a standard commercial vaccination program for meat-type quails. All three breeds received the same corn–soybean meal-based diet. From days 1 to 21, the diet contained 24% crude protein and 3,100 kcal/kg metabolizable energy. Thereafter, it contained 20% crude protein and 2,900 kcal/kg metabolizable energy. The brooding temperature was maintained at 38–40°C for the first 3 days. It was gradually decreased to 35–37°C by the end of the first week and maintained at 20–24°C thereafter. Relative humidity was maintained at 55–65%. Continuous lighting (24 h/day) was provided from days 1 to 5, after which the photoperiod was gradually reduced to 14 h/day.

Body measurements

At 49 days of age, six quails from each breed were randomly selected for measurement of body weight and body dimensions. Body measurements were obtained in accordance with Nomenclature and Metric Calculation Methods for Poultry Production Performance (NY/T 823-2020). Body oblique length was measured from the shoulder joint to the ipsilateral sciatic tuberosity using a measuring tape. Keel length was measured from the anterior keel eminence to the end of the keel. Chest width was measured as the distance between the shoulder joints using a digital vernier caliper. Tibial length was measured from the supratarsal joint to the junction of the third and fourth toes using a vernier caliper. Tibial girth was measured at the midpoint of the metatarsus using a measuring tape.

Slaughter and serum biochemical determination

At 49 days of age, six quails per breed were randomly selected for blood sampling via the jugular vein. The blood samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 hour and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min (Eppendorf 5810 R, Germany). The serum was transferred into 2 mL enzyme-free tubes and stored at -80°C for subsequent analysis. Serum parameters including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), creatinine (CREA), immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, IgM), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glucose (GLU), and uric acid (UA) were determined using an automatic biochemical analyzer (PUZS-300X, Beijing Plan New Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). All kits were provided from Wuhan Shengzhiyuan Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Following blood collection, the quails were humanely euthanized and slaughtered to record live body weight, eviscerated weight, and the weights of breast and leg muscles. Simultaneously, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested and weighed. Organ indices were calculated as the ratio of organ weight to pre-slaughter live body weight.

Meat quality

The pH of the left breast muscle was measured at 24 h postmortem using a portable pH meter (PH-STAR, Matthäus, Germany). Each sample was measured at three locations, and the mean value was recorded. Breast muscle color was measured using a portable colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) and expressed as L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness). Three measurements were obtained per sample. Drip loss was determined as described by Xiong et al. (2022). Briefly, each meat sample was weighed (W1), suspended by a nylon string in a sealed polyethylene container without contacting the container walls, and stored at 4°C for 24 h. Surface moisture was removed, and the sample was reweighed (W2). Drip loss (%) was calculated as (W1 − W2)/W1 × 100. For cooking loss, each sample was weighed (M0) and placed in a sealed plastic bag. The sample was heated in an 80°C water bath until its core temperature reached 70°C (Xiao et al., 2024). The core temperature was monitored using a thermometer. After the sample had cooled to room temperature, surface moisture was removed, and the sample was reweighed (M1). Cooking loss (%) was calculated as (M0 − M1)/M0 × 100. Shear force was measured in the left breast muscle using a digital tenderness meter (C-LM3B, Beijing, China) and expressed in newtons (N). Each sample was measured three times.

Chemical composition analysis

The left breast muscle was trimmed of tendons, fascia, and external fat and used for chemical composition analysis. Moisture content was determined using the direct-drying method according to GB 5009.3-2016. Crude protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method according to GB 5009.5-2016. Briefly, a 1-g sample was digested with copper sulfate, potassium sulfate, and sulfuric acid until the solution became clear and green. After cooling, the digest was analyzed using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer and titrated with a standard HCl solution. Crude protein content (g/100 g) was calculated as [(V1 − V0) × c(HCl) × 0.014 × 6.25]/m × 100. In this equation, V1 is the volume of titrant consumed by the sample (mL), V0 is the volume consumed by the blank (mL), and m is the sample mass (g). Crude fat content was determined using Soxhlet extraction according to GB 5009.6-2016. A 2-g sample was wrapped in filter paper and extracted with anhydrous ether in a Soxhlet apparatus for 8 h. After solvent recovery, the extraction flask was dried to a constant weight. Fat content (g/100 g) was calculated as (M1 − M0)/M2 × 100. In this equation, M2 is the sample mass, M0 is the mass of the empty flask, and M1 is the mass of the flask containing the extracted fat.

Determination of muscle amino acid and fatty acid profiles

The amino acid profile was determined according to Chinese National Standard GB 5009.124-2016. Briefly, a 0.5-g muscle sample was hydrolyzed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L HCl in a sealed tube at 110°C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered, and 0.5 mL of the filtrate was evaporated to dryness. The residue was redissolved in sodium citrate buffer and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. The filtrate was collected for analysis. Amino acid contents were determined using an automatic amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with a sulfonic acid-type cation-exchange resin column. The injection volume was 20 μL. The postcolumn ninhydrin reaction was conducted at 135°C, and absorbance was measured at 570 and 440 nm.

Fatty acid composition was analyzed according to GB 5009.168-2016. A 1-g homogenized sample was subjected to Soxhlet fat extraction. The extracted fat was saponified with 2% sodium hydroxide in methanol in an 80°C water bath. Fatty acids were subsequently methylated by adding 15% boron trifluoride in methanol under the same heating conditions. After cooling to room temperature, 10 mL of n-heptane was added, and the mixture was shaken vigorously for 2 min. Saturated sodium chloride solution was then added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for phase separation. The upper n-heptane layer containing fatty acid methyl esters was collected, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and subjected to gas chromatography. Separation was performed using a capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm). The oven was initially maintained at 100°C for 13 min. The temperature was then increased to 180°C at 10°C/min and held for 6 min. It was subsequently increased to 200°C at 1°C/min and held for 20 min. Finally, it was increased to 230°C at 4°C/min and held for 10 min. The injector and detector temperatures were 270 and 280°C, respectively. Fatty acids were identified by comparison with known standards and quantified by peak-area integration.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Cecal contents were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quality and integrity were assessed by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were purified using an Omega DNA purification kit (Omega Inc., Norcross, GA, USA) and quantified using a Qsep-400. Sequencing libraries were constructed and subjected to 2 × 250-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Raw sequences were filtered and denoised to obtain high-quality sequences. Taxonomic annotation was performed against the SILVA database. Alpha- and beta-diversity indices were calculated and visualized using QIIME 2 and R. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify differentially abundant taxa among breeds.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v27.0. Data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Differences among breeds were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Data were presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Muscle amino acid profiles

The amino acid composition of quail breast muscle is presented in Table 5. Significant differences in amino acid contents were observed among the three breeds. The concentrations of leucine, lysine, and essential amino acids (EAA) were significantly greater in the FGQ and SMQ than in the CWQ (P < 0.05). Additionally, the threonine content in the FGQ was significantly greater than that in the CWQ (P < 0.05). Conversely, the phenylalanine content in the FGQ was significantly lower than that in both the CWQ and the SMQ (P < 0.05), while the levels of valine, methionine, and isoleucine did not significantly differ among the three breeds (P > 0.05). With respect to nonessential amino acids, the FGQ and SMQ presented significantly higher levels of aspartic acid, alanine, nonessential amino acids (NEAA), and total amino acids (TAA) than did the CWQ (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the concentrations of glutamic acid, glycine, histidine, and arginine in the FGQ were markedly greater than those in the CWQ (P < 0.05). Notably, the levels of umami amino acids (UAA) and sweet amino acids (SAA) in the FGQ were significantly greater than those in the CWQ (P < 0.05). Interestingly, despite having the highest total amino acid content, the FGQ had significantly lower ratios of EAA/TAA and EAA/NEAA compared with the CWQ and SMQ (P < 0.05).

Muscle fatty acid profiles

The fatty acid composition of the breast muscle of the different meat-type quail breeds is presented in Table 6. Significant differences in several fatty acids were observed among the three breeds. The content of saturated fatty acids (SFA) in the CWQ was significantly greater than that in the SMQ (P < 0.05). Specifically, the level of C18:0 in the CWQ was significantly greater than that in the SMQ and FGQ (P < 0.05), whereas the level of C14:0 was significantly greater in the FGQ than in the CWQ and SMQ (P < 0.05). In addition, the content of C18:1n9c in the SMQ and FGQ was significantly greater than that in the CWQ (P < 0.05). Notably, the CWQ exhibited significantly higher levels of arachidonic acid (C20:4n6) and docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6n3, DHA) than did the SMQ and FGQ (P < 0.05).

Cecal microbial diversity

To further evaluate the variations in the gut microbiota among the different quail breeds, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to analyze changes in the cecal microflora. A total of 1,367,182 raw reads were obtained from 18 cecal samples. After quality control and the merging of paired-end reads, 1,202,581 clean reads were generated, with an average of 66,810 clean reads per sample. The results of rank abundance curve analysis indicated that the sequencing depth was sufficient to meet the requirements for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1A). Sequence clustering revealed the total number of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) for each group; 247 ASVs were shared among the three groups, while 1,780, 2,108, and 2,593 unique ASVs were identified in the CWQ, SMQ, and FGQ groups, respectively (Fig. 1B). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) demonstrated that the cecal bacterial communities of the CWQ and FGQ groups clustered independently, whereas those of the SMQ group clustered together, indicating significant differences in the overall structure of the cecal microbiota among the breeds (R2 = 0.201; P = 0.001; Fig. 1C). Further analysis of microbial richness and diversity using the ACE, Chao1, Simpson, and Shannon indices revealed that the ACE, Chao1, and Shannon indices of the FGQ group were significantly greater than those of the CWQ group (P < 0.05), highlighting the distinct differences in microbial species richness and diversity (Fig. 1D).

Cecal microbial composition

To understand the compositional changes in the cecal microbiota across breeds, we analyzed the top 10 most abundant taxa. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were dominant in all three groups, accounting for more than 80% of the total abundance (Fig. 2A). At the genus level, Bacteroides, Phascolarctobacterium, unclassified_Muribaculaceae, Alistipes, and Megamonas were the top five genera (relative abundance >3%) (Fig. 2B). Compared with that in the CWQ group, the relative abundance of Actinobacteria in the FGQ group tended to be lower (P = 0.069) (Fig. 2C). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Alistipes in the FGQ group was significantly lower than that in the SMQ group (P < 0.05), and the abundance of Bifidobacterium was significantly lower than that in the CWQ group (P < 0.05). Conversely, the abundances of Colidextribacter and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group were significantly greater in the FGQ group than in the CWQ group (P < 0.05), whereas the abundance of Butyricimonas also tended to increase (P = 0.083). Additionally, the relative abundance of Collinsella in the SMQ group was lower than that in the CWQ group (P = 0.060) (Fig. 2D). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) further revealed key discriminative taxa: 10, 7, and 10 taxa were significantly enriched in the CWQ, SMQ, and FGQ groups, respectively. Bifidobacterium and Faecalicoccus were enriched in the CWQ group; Alistipes and Coprobacter were characteristic of the SMQ group; and unclassified_F082, Erysipelatoclostridium, and unclassified_Tannerellaceae were enriched in the FGQ group (Fig. 2E).

Correlation analysis

To elucidate the relationships between microbial variation and muscle physicochemical properties, fatty acid profiles, and amino acid compositions, correlation analyses were performed. Mantel test results indicated significant correlations between muscle physicochemical traits and specific amino acid and fatty acid profiles. Lightness (L∗24h) was significantly positively correlated with various amino acids, including leucine, lysine, arginine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid (P < 0.05). Concurrently, L∗24hwas significantly positively correlated with several fatty acids, such as C18:0, C18:1n9c, C20:4n6, and C22:6n3 (P < 0.05). Similarly, shear force was positively correlated with amino acid indicators such as alanine and leucine (P < 0.05) and positively correlated with C18:0 (P = 0.029). Additionally, breast muscle weight was positively correlated with alanine, leucine, and specific fatty acids such as C18:0 and C22:6n3 (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the crude protein content was significantly positively correlated with alanine (P = 0.026), C18:0 (P = 0.046), and C18:1n9c (P = 0.010) levels (Fig. 3A and B).

Spearman correlation analysis further revealed complex associations between differential and dominant bacterial genera and meat quality traits. Significant correlations were observed among the gut microbiota, meat quality parameters, amino acid composition, and fatty acid profiles (Fig. 3C). Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was highly significantly positively correlated with redness (a∗24h) (P < 0.001). Notably, Butyricimonas (P = 0.005) and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group (P = 0.038) were negatively correlated with shear force. Moreover, the abundances of Colidextribacter and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group were significantly positively correlated with breast muscle weight (P < 0.05). Megasphaera was significantly positively correlated with multiple amino acids, such as glycine, aspartic acid, threonine, and arginine (P < 0.05). Christensenellaceae_R_7_group and Colidextribacter were significantly positively correlated with histidine (P < 0.05). In contrast, the abundance of Bifidobacterium was significantly negatively correlated with threonine, alanine, and lysine levels (P < 0.05). Moreover, the abundance of Bacteroides was significantly negatively correlated with the levels of leucine, alanine, arginine, and glycine (P < 0.05). In terms of fatty acids, several genera were associated with fatty acid-related traits. Collinsella and Bifidobacterium were significantly positively correlated with C18:0 (P < 0.05), Megamonas and Megasphaera were significantly positively correlated with C14:0 (P < 0.05), and Coprobacter and Alistipes were negatively correlated with C14:0 (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Quail meat is rich in various nutrients and has particularly low fat and cholesterol contents, which are beneficial to human health. In this study, the differences in slaughter performance and meat quality among different meat-type quail breeds at 49 days of age were investigated. Significant differences in carcass traits, meat quality characteristics, amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, and cecal microbiota were detected among the breeds. Slaughter performance is the most direct indicator for evaluating the meat production value of poultry and reflects the direction of genetic selection among breeds. In the present study, the FGQ and SMQ exhibited significantly greater body weight, carcass weight, breast muscle weight, and leg muscle weight than the CWQ did. Numerous studies have indicated that the body weight, carcass quality, and yield of quails are influenced by factors such as genotype, sex, and nutrition (Abou-Kassem et al., 2019; Cullere et al., 2023). Previous research has shown differences in carcass quality among Japanese quails with different feather colors, and the body weight and carcass yield of white-feathered quails at 6 weeks of age were significantly greater than those of golden, gray, and brown quails (Nasr et al., 2017). Similarly, Sabow (2019) reported significant differences in the carcass weight and carcass yield among groups of three different quail strains. Consistent with these findings, the results of the present study confirm that carcass traits in meat-type quails are significantly affected by breed. Previous studies have suggested that differences in quail body weight are related to intrinsic factors such as genotype (Faruque et al., 2020). Therefore, the observed differences in body weight and carcass characteristics among the different meat quails in this study may be attributable to the regulatory role of genotype. Notably, the increase in body weight was not accompanied by significant changes in organ indices. In this study, no significant differences in the relative weights of major organs, including the liver, heart, spleen, and kidney, were detected among the breeds. Previous studies have reported that visceral organ weights in quails (such as the heart, liver, proventriculus, and gizzard) are influenced by nutritional level, feed intake, sex, and age (Abou-Kassem et al., 2019; Mahmoudi Zarandi et al., 2023; Kirrella et al., 2023). Kirrella et al. (2023) reported that the weights of organs such as the heart, liver, and spleen in 6-week-old quails were not affected by the breed, although sex did influence liver weight. The proportions of the heart, liver, and visceral organs in females are significantly greater than those in males (Bonos et al., 2010). However, Wegner et al. (2024) demonstrated that the proportions of the heart, liver, and proventriculus in 6-week-old quails were regulated by genotype and did not differ between sexes. In the present study, genotype influenced the relative weight of the gizzard, whereas other organ indices remained unchanged, which is generally consistent with most previous findings.

The physicochemical properties of meat are critical determinants of consumer purchasing decisions, among which meat color is among the most intuitive indicators of quality. Genotype is considered one of the key factors influencing meat quality (Baéza et al., 2022). In the present study, significant differences in muscle L*, a*, and shear force were observed among the breeds. Specifically, the CWQ resulted in higher L* values and shear forces, whereas the FGQ resulted in higher a* values. Previous studies have also demonstrated breed-dependent variation in meat color. Ribarski and Genchev (2013) reported differences in a* and b* values between Pharaoh and Manchurian golden quails and reported that chroma (C*) is influenced by genetic background. Similarly, Wegner et al. (2024) reported that compared with Pharaoh quails, Japanese quails presented higher L* and b* values and greater shear forces, which is consistent with our findings that meat quality differs across breeds. Notably, other research suggests that age and sex also affect meat color changes in quails, with L* and b* values in Japanese quails decreasing with age but a* increasing (Abou-Kassem et al., 2019). In addition to meat color, crude protein and fat contents in muscle were affected by genotype in the present study. The SMQ presented higher protein and lower fat contents, whereas compared with the other breeds, the FGQ presented relatively higher levels of both protein and fat. Wegner et al. (2024) reported that Japanese quails had higher IMF content than Pharaoh quails did, while Pharaoh quails were characterized by higher moisture and lower fat levels. The results of the present study further indicate that different breeds exhibit distinct patterns of protein deposition and lipid accumulation.

The amino acid composition of muscle is closely associated with both the flavor and nutritional quality of the meat. In meat evaluation, protein quality is largely determined by amino acid concentrations and the balance between EAA and NEAA, which directly influence the nutritional value of meat proteins (Macelline et al., 2021). In the present study, significant differences in amino acid composition were observed among quail breeds, particularly in EAA, TAA, and flavor-related amino acids. Compared with the CWQ, the FGQ and SMQ had higher levels of leucine, lysine, aspartic acid, alanine, histidine, and TAA, indicating that the nutritional quality of these proteins was superior in these two breeds. Previous studies have confirmed that quail meat is rich in essential amino acids and has a well-balanced amino acid profile, conferring high biological value (Sabow, 2019). According to FAO/WHO standards, high-quality protein is characterized by an EAA/TAA ratio of approximately 40% and an EAA/NEAA ratio above 60% (Tessari, 2019). In the present study, the EAA/TAA ratio ranged from 39.88% to 40.71%, and the EAA/NEAA ratio ranged from 66.34% to 68.66%, meeting the criteria for high-quality protein. These results suggest that quail meat serves as a dietary protein source with extremely high biological significance for humans. Notably, differences in amino acid proportions were observed among the breeds, indicating that not only the total amino acid content but also the balance between EAA and NEAA plays a critical role in determining protein quality. In addition to nutritional value, meat flavor is an important factor influencing consumer preference. Flavor-related amino acids contribute significantly to meat palatability, and their increased levels are associated with enhanced taste characteristics (Yang et al., 2025). In this study, the FGQ presented higher levels of UAA and SAA, including glutamic acid, alanine, and glycine. Previous studies have confirmed that these amino acids impart umami and sweetness to meat, enhancing the flavor profile and improving sensory quality (Lorenzo and Franco, 2012). Therefore, the higher content of these flavor-related amino acids in the FGQ suggests that compared with other quail breeds, this breed may possess superior flavor characteristics.

Notably, although the muscle crude protein content measured in the present study was consistent with that in previous reports, the levels of individual amino acids were generally higher than those reported for different strains of Japanese quail (Nasr et al., 2017; Sabow, 2019). This discrepancy may be attributable primarily to differences in genetic background among the breeds. Meat-type quails have been selectively bred for rapid growth and enhanced muscle development, traits that are closely associated with improved protein deposition capacity. Muscle growth is governed by the dynamic balance between protein synthesis and degradation, and fast-growing poultry lines typically exhibit higher rates of protein deposition and turnover efficiency (Tesseraud et al., 2000). As amino acids are the fundamental substrates for protein synthesis, the elevated amino acid levels observed in meat-type quails may reflect an increased demand for nitrogenous precursors during rapid growth. Therefore, genetic background may play a key role in shaping amino acid composition, which could explain the inconsistencies observed among different studies. Similar breed-dependent variations in muscle amino acid composition have also been reported in other poultry species, such as chickens and ducks, indicating that genetic factors significantly influence amino acid profiles (Jo et al., 2021; Ungkusonmongkol and Wattanachant, 2024). However, owing to the limited availability of studies focusing on different meat-type quail breeds, further comparative research is needed to validate these findings. Despite these differences, the overall pattern observed in this study—namely, lysine being the most abundant essential amino acid and glutamic acid being the most abundant nonessential amino acid—is consistent with previous reports (Nasr et al., 2017).

Fatty acid composition is a key determinant of both the nutritional value and the sensory quality of meat. In recent years, increasing attention has been given to intramuscular fatty acid profiles, particularly the distribution of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) (Wood et al., 2008). In the present study, significant differences in fatty acid composition were observed among quail breeds. The CWQ exhibited higher levels of saturated fatty acids, particularly C18:0, whereas the SMQ and FGQ exhibited higher levels of C18:1n9c. Although SFAs are generally considered less favorable for health, some long-chain fatty acids, such as C14:0 and C18:0, have been reported to be negatively associated with blood pressure and to possess antimicrobial properties (Nakamura et al., 2019; Arellano et al., 2023). Oleic acid (C18:1n9c), a major monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) in muscle lipids, is positively associated with meat palatability and contributes to improved tenderness, juiciness, and overall flavor quality (Smith, 2016). Moreover, oleic acid has been linked to improved lipid profiles and is considered beneficial for cardiovascular health (Prates, 2025). Polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6n3) and arachidonic acid (C20:4n6), play important roles in human health because of their anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects (Albracht-Schulte et al., 2018). In addition, PUFAs can increase meat freshness and flavor intensity, thereby improving sensory quality (Takahashi, 2018). In the present study, the CWQ exhibited significantly higher levels of C20:4n6 and C22:6n3 than did the SMQ and FGQ, suggesting distinct genotype-dependent differences in lipid deposition patterns among the breeds. However, previous studies have reported inconsistent results. For instance, Sabow (2019) reported no significant differences in fatty acid composition among Japanese quail lines with different feather colors. Fatty acid composition is regulated by multiple factors, including dietary lipid intake and genetic background (Smet et al., 2004). Therefore, the discrepancies among studies may be attributable to differences in genotype, diet, or their interactions. Similar breed-dependent variations in fatty acid composition have also been reported in other poultry species (Cömert et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2025). In addition, although some fatty acid levels, as well as SFA and n-6/n-3 ratios, were comparable to those in previous reports, higher concentrations of MUFAs and PUFAs were observed in the present study. These differences may be related to variations in fatty acid composition and metabolic characteristics among breeds.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in host nutrient metabolism, immune regulation, and growth performance. In the present study, significant differences in the cecal microbial community structure were observed among quail breeds. The ACE, Chao1, and Shannon indices were significantly greater in the FGQ group than in the CWQ group, indicating greater microbial richness and diversity. High gut microbial diversity is generally associated with a relatively stable ecosystem and enhanced metabolic capacity, which may improve nutrient utilization efficiency and host health (Lozupone et al., 2012; Heiman and Greenway, 2016). This may partially explain the phenotypic advantages observed in the FGQ. Previous studies have suggested that the composition of the gut microbiota is influenced by host genetic background. Even under controlled environmental and dietary conditions, different breeds of animals still exhibit specific gut microbial structures (Bergamaschi et al., 2020; Paul et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025). Consistent with these findings, the results of the present study revealed distinct microbial community differences among breeds under identical rearing conditions, highlighting the important role of host genetics in shaping the composition of the gut microbiota. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were dominant across all groups, which is consistent with the gut microbial profile of Japanese quail (Ma et al., 2021). However, at the genus level, several taxa differed among the breeds and were closely associated with host metabolism and meat quality traits. Among these genera, Bifidobacterium, a well-known probiotic genus, was significantly enriched in the CWQ group. Bifidobacterium can ferment carbohydrates to produce short-chain fatty acids, thereby regulating host energy and lipid metabolism (O’Callaghan and van Sinderen, 2016). In the present study, Bifidobacterium was positively correlated with C18:0 and total SFAs but negatively correlated with threonine, alanine, and lysine, suggesting that it may be involved not only in lipid metabolism but also in amino acid utilization or turnover, thereby influencing amino acid accumulation in muscle. The Christensenellaceae_R_7_group also differed significantly among the breeds and was negatively correlated with shear force, positively correlated with histidine, and negatively correlated with phenylalanine. Previous studies have linked this taxon to host health, metabolic efficiency, and lean phenotypes, suggesting that it may influence muscle structure and meat quality through the modulation of energy utilization and protein metabolism (Waters and Ley, 2019; Ang et al., 2023). In addition, Alistipes was enriched in the SMQ group and is associated with lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation, often reflecting changes in host metabolic status (Yang et al., 2024). In the present study, Alistipes was negatively correlated with C14:0, indicating a potential role in fatty acid transformation or degradation, which may affect lipid deposition patterns. Notably, although Megasphaera did not significantly differ among the groups, it was positively correlated with multiple amino acids, including glycine, aspartic acid, threonine, and arginine. Previous studies have demonstrated that Megasphaera possesses diverse metabolic capabilities, participating in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism and producing short-chain fatty acids through lactate utilization, thereby influencing host energy metabolism and nutrient utilization (Shetty et al., 2013; Dai et al., 2015). This suggests that it may act as a key functional taxon involved in amino acid accumulation. Furthermore, the abundance of Colidextribacter was positively correlated with histidine levels and breast muscle weight, suggesting a potential role in amino acid metabolism and muscle growth. However, owing to the limited functional characterization of this genus, its specific mechanisms require further investigation.

Overall, the results of the present study clearly revealed breed-specific differences in the gut microbiota composition, amino acid and fatty acid metabolism, and meat quality traits. The gut microbiota not only differed structurally among the breeds but also showed significant associations with amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, and meat quality parameters, suggesting its potential involvement in host metabolic regulation and indirect effects on meat quality. These findings indicate that the gut microbiota and metabolic traits may interact synergistically across different breeds to influence meat quality. These results provide a theoretical basis for future regulation of the gut microecosystem to optimize the breeding of meat-type quails and improve meat quality.

Conclusion

This study systematically compared the meat quality traits, nutritional composition, and cecal microbiota among different breeds of meat-type quails. The results revealed significant differences in physicochemical properties, amino acid profiles, and fatty acid composition among the different breeds. Compared with the CWQ, the FGQ and SMQ presented better carcass traits and higher protein and amino acid contents, whereas the CWQ presented higher levels of functional fatty acids, including C22:6n3 and C20:4n6, as well as higher saturated fatty acid contents. In addition, distinct differences in the cecal microbial community structure were observed among the breeds. Several bacterial genera, such as Bifidobacterium and Christensenellaceae_R_7_group, were significantly associated with amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, and meat quality traits. This study revealed the differences in physiological metabolism among different breeds of meat-type quails from multiple dimensions, providing a theoretical basis for improving quail meat quality through genetic selection and gut microbiota modulation.
