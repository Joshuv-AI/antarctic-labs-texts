A Micro-costing Study of Community-Based Primary HPV Self-Sampling and Triage Test for Cervical Cancer Screening in Rural Cambodia
Authors: Ziyue Zhang, Hermann Bussmann, Sathiarany Vong, Hero Kol, Magnus von Knebel Doeberitz, Manuela De Allegri, Kavita Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-based human papillomavirus (HPV) self-sampling can expand cervical cancer screening coverage in rural settings, but local programmatic cost evidence is limited.MethodsWe conducted an activity-based micro-costing study from the healthcare system perspective to estimate start-up (January 2022–February 2023) and implementation (March–December 2023) costs of a community-based HPV self-sampling screen–triage approach in Siem Reap Province, Cambodia. Resource use and unit costs were measured using direct observation, semi-structured interviews with program staff, and review of budgets and financial reports. All costs were converted to 2024 US dollars.ResultsBetween January 2022 and December 2023, 7524 women were screened; 359 (4.77%) tested HPV-positive and 312 of 359 (86.91%) completed triage during the study period. Total programmatic cost was US$204,507.14, yielding an average cost of US$27.18 per woman screened and US$569.66 per HPV-positive case identified. Laboratory testing was the largest cost component (55.01% of total programmatic cost), followed by the community-based self-sampling activity (31.12%). Estimated incremental unit costs for triage options were US$3.69 for visual inspection of cervix with acetic acid (VIA), US$16.42 for partial HPV genotyping (types 16 and 18), and US$32.92 for colposcopy per woman receiving the respective triage test.ConclusionsCommunity-based HPV self-sampling can be delivered at moderate programmatic cost in rural Cambodia, with overall costs driven primarily by laboratory testing and community-based screening delivery. These findings provide inputs for budgeting and planning scale-up of HPV screening from a healthcare system perspective in Cambodia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00659-6.

Key Points for Decision Makers

This is the first study to comprehensively estimate the program costs of a community-based HPV self-sampling and triage program in rural Cambodia.In total, 7524 women were screened at a cost of US$27.18 per woman screened and US$569.66 per HPV-positive case identified (2024 USD), and costs were driven primarily by laboratory testing and, secondarily, by community-based screening activities.These findings provide practical cost data to inform national policy on scaling up cervical cancer screening in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality among the female population globally, with over 90% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) aims to eliminate cervical cancer through a comprehensive strategy built on three pillars: human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination to prevent infections, screening for early detection of precancerous lesions, and treatment of both precancerous and invasive disease [2, 3]. While screening alone does not prevent cervical cancer, it enables early identification of abnormalities, allowing for timely treatment of precancerous lesions and prevention of disease progression [2]. Three commonly used screening methods include cytology, visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) [4, 5], and HPV DNA testing. Although cytology is highly effective, it is difficult to implement in LMICs due to high laboratory infrastructure requirements and the need for trained personnel [5, 6]. VIA involves direct visual assessment of the cervix using a 3–5% acetic acid solution, but suffers from limited accuracy, variable quality, and potential for overdiagnosis and overtreatment [7, 8]. Molecular HPV testing detects DNA from high-risk HPV types in vaginal or cervical samples and offers higher sensitivity [9]. In 2021, WHO recommended a ‘screen-triage-treat’ approach using primary HPV DNA test followed by a triage method for confirmatory diagnosis and treatment when indicated. Triage options include cytology, VIA, colposcopy, and HPV genotyping for high-risk strains, such as strains 16 and 18 (HPV 16/18 genotyping), which cause more than 70% of cervical cancers, with or without biopsy for histological confirmation of cervical cancer [5, 10].

Despite being the second-most common cancer among females in Cambodia—accounting for an estimated 1500 new cases and 800 deaths annually—screening coverage remains low, with only 17% of females aged 30–49 years having ever been screened [11–13]. The WHO target is that 70% of women are screened using a high-performance test (a test which would have performance characteristics similar to or better than an HPV DNA test) by 35 years and again by 45 years of age [12, 14]. Cambodia’s national guidelines recommend screening every 2 years for sexually active females aged 25–65 years, but in practice, clinic-based VIA is primarily offered to females aged 30–49 years, due to resource limitations, and only if requested or initiated by healthcare providers [11, 15, 16]. To improve coverage, the Cambodian government is considering the introduction of primary HPV DNA testing as a replacement for clinic-based VIA screening [15]. This method has several advantages, including high sensitivity (94%) and specificity (88%) [17]. Moreover, HPV self-sampling enables females to collect samples themselves at home or in community settings, potentially increasing screening uptake among underserved and hard-to-reach populations in rural areas [18, 19]. Compared with the clinic-based and provider-initiated screening, community HPV self-sampling-based screening strategies address barriers such as limited healthcare access, low health literacy, and transportation challenges, while also enhancing outreach and awareness efforts by utilizing community health workers [20–22].

Although prior studies have reported the costs of community-based HPV self-sampling in other LMICs such as Peru, Nicaragua, India, and Kenya, ranging from US$8.50 to US$45.40 per female screened—evidence on the programmatic costs of implementing a screen-triage-treat approach is limited [23–29]. Programmatic costs are a critical component influencing cervical cancer screening program feasibility, adoption, and sustainability, yet costs data in the local context of Cambodia are limited. To address the existing gap in evidence for Cambodia, we conducted a micro-costing study to estimate the programmatic costs associated with community-based HPV self-sampling in rural areas. Specifically, this study aimed to determine (i) the average cost per female screened using primary HPV self-sampling, (ii) the average cost per HPV-positive case identified, and (iii) the average cost per female undergoing each triage testing option—VIA, colposcopy, or HPV16/18 genotyping—following a positive HPV primary test. This study provides Cambodia-specific costs data to inform national policy and support the scale-up of the cervical cancer screening program.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

In a resource limited setting of Cambodia, community-based HPV self-sampling offers a vital opportunity to expand reach, empower women, and improve early detection of (pre-)cancer. This costing study was embedded within and conducted alongside an implementation trial comparing the effectiveness of a community-based primary HPV self-sampling test—followed by a triage-and-treat strategy—with a hospital-based VIA screen-and-treat approach in Cambodia. The study was implemented in Siem Reap Province from January 2022 to December 2023, and encompassed both the start-up and implementation phases.

The start-up phase, conducted from January 2022 to February 2023, focused on program planning, training, general coordination, and management. This was followed by the implementation phase, which took place from March 2023 to December 2023. During this phase, community-based HPV self-sampling was offered to eligible females aged 25–65 years in 60 villages across eight communes in Siem Reap Province—four communes from Sot Nikum Operational District (OD) and four from Siem Reap OD (Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary material [ESM]). The selected areas covered a total catchment of 277.51 km2, with an estimated female population of 55,152 (Table S1 in the ESM) [30]. Female participants who had a cervix were recruited in catchment areas.

Description of the Intervention: Community-Based Cervical Cancer Screening

Cervical cancer screening through community-based HPV self-sampling was facilitated by Village Health Support Group (VHSG) members, who played a crucial role in community outreach. Health center midwives served as the primary point of care at the commune level and were responsible for overseeing the screening process. They conducted outreach activities and coordinated sample collection within their assigned areas. In designated villages with access to restrooms, midwives organized educational seminars on cervical cancer and HPV self-sampling. After the seminar, they distributed sample collection kits and instructed females to collect their own samples privately. The collected samples were then transported to Institut Pasteur du Cambodge in Phnom Penh, located approximately 320 km from Siem Reap Province, for HPV DNA testing using the Roche cobas 4800 platform. Females who tested positive for HPV were informed by the midwives and referred for triage testing at one of the two nearby facilities, Siem Reap Provincial Hospital (in Siem Reap OD) and Sot Nikum Referral Hospital (in Sot Nikum OD). Triage testing options included VIA, colposcopy, or HPV16/18 genotyping (Figs S2–S3 in the ESM). Seamless dataflow between all screening actors was assured by the project’s cloud-based data management system using mobile app-based and web-based devices.

Data Collection and Measurements

We conducted a micro-costing study from the healthcare system perspective using an activity-based costing approach [31], following four key steps: (i) Identifying and mapping all activities related to the start-up and implementation phases of the community-based cervical cancer screening in Siem Reap Province, Cambodia; (ii) Developing and pilot testing a customized cost data collection tool based on the WHO Cervical Cancer Prevention and Control Costing (C4P) tool [32]; (iii) Measuring resource consumption associated with program set-up and implementation; and (iv) Assigning prices and values to resources used in the community-based screen, triage and treat approach.

Two major activities were identified during the start-up phase (January 2022–February 2023): general management and training. Five activities were identified during the implementation phase (March 2023–December 2023): general management and coordination, medical supplies and sample transportation, community-based HPV self-sampling, laboratory testing, and monitoring and evaluation (Fig. 1). Costing tools developed included a semi-structured interview guide and a customized Excel-based costing spreadsheet. We determined unit costs and resource consumption through direct observation, semi-structured interviews with 20 program staff (Fig. S4 in the ESM), and a review of program budgets and financial reports. We used purposive sampling to interview staff across implementation levels and roles (VHSG, health-center midwives, OD and hospital staff), prioritizing those with direct responsibility for key activities and availability during fieldwork. Informants who were able to communicate in English were preferred for interviews; when this was not possible, a trained interpreter was present. We calculated the total costs by multiplying quantities of resources used by their respective unit prices. To enhance accuracy, we triangulated data from multiple sources, including interview responses, project documentation, and online price data. Clinical data on the number of females screened through the community-based program were obtained from the Emerging Technologies in Cervical Cancer Screening (ETiCCS) digital study database [33].

Schematic flow diagram of activity-based micro-costing study design and major cost components/categories. Figure 1 presents the schematic flow diagram of the costing study. We divided the study into two phases: a start-up phase (January 2022–February 2023) and an implementation phase (March 2023–December 2023). The costs of seven activities were accounted for in the community-based primary HPV self-sampling method: general management and training in the start-up phase; general management, samples and supplies transportation, monitoring and evaluation, community HPV self-sampling screening, and laboratory testing in the implementation phase

Our total programmatic cost estimation included both direct medical costs (e.g., HPV test kits, laboratory testing, personnel) and direct non-medical costs (e.g., training, transportation of samples and supplies, community outreach, and monitoring and evaluation) incurred during both the start-up phase and the implementation phase of the program (Tables S3–S4 in the ESM). Laboratory testing costs were valued using the program’s fee-for-service payment to the reference laboratory for primary HPV screening only and excluded laboratory or diagnostic costs related to triage procedures. Laboratory cost for triage HPV 16/18 genotyping test was estimated separately and included within the triage cost component. Triage costs were estimated by modality for each triage option, and each included allocated overheads for personnel time, equipment, and supplies. The average cost per triage option was calculated by dividing the total costs associated with each triage method (including VIA, HPV16/18 genotyping, and colposcopy) by the number of females who received that triage test.

Research-related costs were excluded from both total and average cost calculations (e.g., trial-specific research staff time not required for routine service delivery, research data collection beyond routine program records, ethics submission activities, and dissemination activities not required for service delivery).

Data Analyses

We calculated the average cost per female screened by dividing the total programmatic cost by the total number of females screened with the HPV DNA test. The cost per HPV-positive case was determined by dividing the total cost by the number of females who tested positive on the primary HPV self-sampling test.

In addition, we also reported the cost per woman completing triage as the total programmatic cost divided by the number of HPV-positive women who completed triage during the study period. Average costs for each triage option (VIA, partial HPV genotyping for HPV types 16 and 18, and colposcopy) were calculated as the total economic cost of delivering that triage service divided by the number of women who received the specific triage option.

To account for capital equipment depreciation, we applied the inflation-adjusted present value of the annuity formula as described by Drummond [34]. This method annualizes equipment costs over their useful life while adjusting for inflation. Specifically, the annuity factor was calculated as 7.22, based on a 10-year useful life and a 5.30% inflation rate in Cambodia (Equation S1 and Tables S2–S4 in the ESM) [34, 35]. Using this factor, we annualized costs for one obstetric/gynecological bed, two laptops, and one barcode scanner, incorporating these into the total cost estimates. Costs were initially recorded in Euros (EUR), United States Dollars (USD), and Khmer Riels (KHR). We converted EUR and KHR to USD using the 2024 exchange rates: 1 USD = 0.94 EUR, and 1 USD = 4100.00 KHR [36, 37]. All costs are presented in 2024 USD from the healthcare system perspective and cover the period from January 2022 to December 2023.

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted one-way sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our cost estimates. Six key input variables were selected based on the literature and identified cost drivers: (1) screen-positive rate, (2) training cost, (3) laboratory testing, (4) monitoring and evaluation, (5) supplies and transportation, and (6) personnel costs.

For cost and programmatic parameters (training, laboratory testing, monitoring and evaluation, supplies and transportation, and personnel costs), each variable was increased and decreased by 50% to examine the resulting changes in average cost of community-based cervical cancer screening. The univariate sensitivity analysis allowed us to determine the impact of each factor on cost variability.

For the screen-positive rate, which represents an epidemiological parameter, sensitivity bounds were defined using the 95% confidence interval (95% CI Calculation in ESM). We used these bounds to examine how plausible epidemiological variation translates into variability in cost outcomes, particularly the average cost per HPV-positive case.

Results

Cost Results

Between January 2022 and December 2023, a total of 7524 eligible females were screened, and vaginal swab samples were collected for primary HPV testing. Of these, 359 females tested HPV positive, resulting in a screen positive rate of 4.77%, and of these, 312 females completed the triage examination during the study period.

The total cost of the community-based HPV self-sampling program was US$204,507.14, including US$17,545.31 for the start-up phase (January 2022–February 2023) and US$186,961.83 for the implementation phase (March 2023–December 2023).

During the start-up phase, general management accounted for US$4800.00 (2.35% of total programmatic costs), while training expenses totaled US$12,745.31 (6.23% of total programmatic costs). In the implementation phase, general management costs were US$8706.80 (4.26% of total programmatic costs). Transportation of supplies and samples accounted for US$740.10 (0.36% of total programmatic costs). The community-based primary HPV self-sampling process cost US$63,640.93 (31.12% of total programmatic costs), while laboratory testing represented the largest expenditure at US$112,500.00 (55.01% of total programmatic costs). Monitoring and evaluation activities totaled US$1374.00 (0.67% of total programmatic costs) (Table 1 and Fig. S5 in the ESM).

Sensitivity Analysis Results

Sensitivity analyses were conducted on average cost per female screened, except for screen-positive rate analyses, which assessed average cost per HPV-positive case. Our one-way sensitivity analyses indicated laboratory costs were the most noteworthy cost component. When laboratory costs varied by ± 50%, the average cost per female screened changed from the base case of US$27.18 to US$19.77 (− 50%) and US$34.77 (+ 50%). A ± 50% change in personnel costs resulted in a range of US$23.90 (− 50%) to US$30.64 (+ 50%). Training cost variations shifted the average cost to US$26.42 (− 50%) and US$28.12 (+ 50%). Monitoring and evaluation cost variation had a minimal effect, with values ranging from US$27.18 to US$27.36. Transportation costs also showed only marginal variability (Table 2).

Discussions

This study makes an important contribution to the existing literature on the programmatic costs associated with designing and implementing community-based HPV self-testing for cervical cancer screening in rural Cambodia. Through a comprehensive, activity-based micro-costing approach, the study findings provide important evidence to support policymakers in making informed resource allocation decisions and developing evidence-based policies for cervical cancer prevention in low-resource settings. The study found that the total cost of implementing community-based HPV self-sampling was US$204,507.14 over 2 years, reaching 7524 females across 60 communities in Siem Reap Province.

Training was the primary start-up cost driver (72.64% of start-up costs), consistent with previous studies showing that preparatory and capacity-building activities represent substantial early expenditures in LMIC screening programs [27, 38]. During the implementation phase, laboratory testing was the largest cost component, comprising 60.17% of implementation costs and 55.01% of total programmatic costs, aligning with earlier research identifying laboratory services as major cost drivers in HPV screening programs [23, 27, 38, 39]. The cost attributed to community-based HPV self-sampling (31.12% of total programmatic costs) also reflects trends observed in previous evaluations, emphasizing the resource-intensive nature of outreach and screening interventions [28, 29, 38, 41]. Minor cost components, such as transportation (0.36% of total programmatic costs) and monitoring and evaluation (0.67% of total programmatic costs) also mirror patterns reported in earlier cost analyses, where these activities comprised a small proportion of total implementation expenses [27, 38].

Differences in healthcare system contexts, national cervical cancer screening policies, baseline HPV prevalence, and implementation strategies are all known to influence the delivery and cost of primary HPV self-sampling programs [27–29]. Our study found that the cost per female screened was broadly consistent with findings from other LMICs, though some differences emerged due to the inclusion of opportunity costs in the total programmatic cost estimates. For example, a study in Peru reported a cost of $45.40 (2018 USD ≈ $56.70 in 2024 USD) per female screened, largely driven by opportunity costs associated with community health volunteers’ time and travel, which accounted for 36.90% of the total cost [27–29, 39]. A study in Kenya reported a higher cost of $37.70 (2018 USD ≈ $47.10 in 2024 USD), largely due to a lower number of females screened per campaign, which reduced cost efficiency by increasing the per-person share of fixed overheads [29]. These differences highlight the importance of scale in community-based screening efforts and the impact of contextual factors on programmatic costs.

Our programmatic cost estimates fall within the range reported across LMIC implementation studies; however, differences in costing perspective, included inputs (e.g., opportunity costs), scale, and delivery model limit direct comparability of absolute USD values across settings. The average cost per HPV-positive case detected in our study ($569.66 in 2024 USD) was substantially higher than the Peruvian study ($378.14 in 2018 USD ≈ $472.30 in 2024 USD), a disparity primarily driven by Cambodia’s lower screen-positive rate (4.77% compared with 12.00% in Peru) [27]. Further, our sensitivity analyses confirmed that the screen-positive rate is a major driver of cost efficiency. When varied within its 95% CI, lower screen-positive rates were associated with higher average costs per HPV-positive case ($632.11 in 2024 USD), while higher screen-positive rates were associated with lower average cost ($512.84 in 2024 USD). This underscores the importance of targeted screening strategies, particularly for high-risk sub-populations such as females aged 35–49 years, those aged 25–49 years living with HIV, and immunocompromised individuals. Prioritizing these groups could optimize resource allocation and improve cost effectiveness in similar low-prevalence settings [5, 42].

In terms of triage testing following a positive primary HPV screen test, our study found comparable costs to those reported in the literature, suggesting the feasibility of various triage options in LMIC contexts. The additional cost per female for VIA triage was US$3.69—consistent with evidence supporting VIA as the most cost-effective triage method in resource-constrained settings. HPV16/18 genotyping cost US$16.42 per female and falls within the reported range for molecular triage strategies, which, while more expensive, may offer improved specificity. Colposcopy remained the most expensive triage option at US$32.92 per female, reflecting the need for specialized personnel and infrastructure, which increases costs significantly, as shown in prior studies [40, 43].

Laboratory testing costs emerged as the most significant cost driver during implementation, and our sensitivity analysis confirmed its strong influence on average cost per female screened. Laboratory testing costs were primarily driven by several components, including capital investments in laboratory equipment and infrastructure, fixed overhead costs associated with laboratory set-up and quality assurance, recurring expenditures for assay reagents and consumables, and personnel costs required for sample processing and analysis. Potential opportunities to reduce laboratory costs include negotiating lower assay prices through pooled procurement or competitive tendering, fostering supplier competition, and committing to predictable testing volumes to achieve economies of scale [44]. In contrast, reducing costs for training, monitoring and evaluation, or transportation, had only marginal effects on average screening costs. Yet, adequate training, supervision, and monitoring are essential to maintain correct sample collection, testing procedures, and follow-up, while reliable transportation supports timely and appropriate handling of samples and referrals. Cutting funding on training, monitoring and evaluation, or transportation leads to the risks of undermined program quality and screening accuracy, thereby limiting the feasibility of scaling and sustaining national cervical cancer screening programs [5]. Therefore, cost-containment strategies should prioritize efficiency gains in high-impact cost drivers rather than reductions in training, monitoring and evaluation, or transportation functions.

Our results offer robust, real-world cost estimates and identify key cost drivers for community-based HPV self-sampling programs. Importantly, this study also captures the downstream costs of triage testing, including VIA, colposcopy, and HPV16/18 genotyping—integral elements of the WHO-recommended ‘screen-triage-treat’ model. While our findings provide essential inputs for policymakers, sustainable implementation will also require attention to financing mechanisms, pricing policies, reimbursement, and co-payment structures to ensure cervical cancer screening program accessibility and scalability [45, 46].

Our study has several limitations. While it aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of resource use and cost data associated with the design and implementation of community-based primary HPV cervical cancer screening in rural Cambodia, the results of this study should be interpreted with some caution. First, despite our efforts to gather data from multiple sources, there is a potential inherent risk of incomplete data due to lack of detailed breakdown of cost data across categories, which may affect the comprehensiveness of our economic data. To mitigate this, we synthesized data from multiple data sources (quantitative costing tools designed for this micro-costing study and qualitative interviews with program implementers and other relevant stakeholders), ensuring a broader coverage and validation of the information collected. The pragmatic approach we adopted, combining program-level costs with selective micro-costing, introduced a trade-off between precision and feasibility. However, this hybrid approach allowed us to balance the need for detailed cost data against the constraints of time and resources, ultimately providing a practical and sufficiently accurate estimation of costs. Additionally, potential biases such as reporting, measurement, and recall biases were minimized through standardized data collection tools and prospective collection of clinical study data (females screened and referred for triage testing). Although we triangulated cost data using multiple sources, including program documents, direct observation, and semi-structured interviews, the number of health workers interviewed and observed was relatively small. Despite the limited number of key informants interviewed, we did not identify substantial variation in resource use reported by those informants, suggesting that within-site heterogeneity in resource use was limited. We sought to consult all relevant key personnel who had knowledge about resource use and costs for the program, to strengthen completeness of data inputs. Finally, the cost data were directly collected from females only in Siem Reap province in Cambodia. The sample population included females from villages in both remotely rural areas and suburban areas, providing a diverse sample to better represent the target population. Yet, when generalizing our results to other regions or countries with varying HPV prevalence, socio-economic status, religious beliefs, or access to healthcare, it is essential to consider the specific local context. These factors are known to significantly influence the applicability and relevance of micro-costing study findings, necessitating careful adaptation of our results to different healthcare settings.

Conclusions

This study provides the first detailed costing of a community-based, HPV self-sampling using a screen-triage-treat approach in rural Cambodia. Our findings offer essential economic costing evidence for policymakers seeking to implement equitable, cost-effective, cervical cancer screening in low-resource settings. Community-based cervical cancer screening programs with active outreach can increase access among underserved rural female populations while offering financial risk protection. The study results underscore the importance of addressing cost drivers like laboratory testing to enhance program efficiency. Further research could explore targeted cost-reduction strategies that preserve screening accuracy, service efficiency, and patient-centered care while improving program sustainability. Cost effectiveness and budget impact analyses comparing community-based versus facility-based approaches will be critical to inform scale-up strategies in Cambodia and other similar LMIC contexts. Sustained community engagement and strong policy support remain essential to achieve the global goal of eliminating cervical cancer, ensuring that no female is left behind.
