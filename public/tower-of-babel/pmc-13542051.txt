From Residency to Retirement: An Overview of Personal Finance for Plastic Surgery Trainees
Authors: Ferris Zeitouni, Myiah P. Quach, Madeline Tierney, Jasmine N. Craig, Pradeep K. Attaluri, Venkat K. Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Plastic surgeons, particularly those in residency and early career, face unique situations that require financial literacy to navigate. Yet, most residency programs lack personal finance education, leaving trainees inadequately prepared to manage student loans, negotiate employment contracts, or participate in long-term financial planning. This article aims to bridge this educational gap by providing a concise overview of essential financial principles tailored to plastic surgery trainees and early practice surgeons. Key topics addressed include student loan and debt management, early saving and investment strategies, insurance and risk protection, and retirement planning. Practical considerations for career transitions include implications of different employment models, insurance considerations, and contract negotiation. Additional focus is placed on career-stage considerations, such as financial advisor selection. Improving financial literacy at the trainee level is critical for physician well-being and professional development. By offering actionable recommendations, trainees and early-career surgeons will be better equipped with the skills necessary to build a financially secure and successful future.

Takeaways

Question: What are the essential financial concepts plastic surgery trainees should understand to successfully navigate financial decisions throughout their training and early career?

Findings: This article provides a concise and comprehensive overview of key financial considerations and recommendations for decisions faced specific to career stage and training level. Recommendations for student loan management, investment strategies, retirement planning, and asset protection are highlighted.

Meaning: Early financial literacy in plastic surgery is essential to building a financially secure and successful future.

INTRODUCTION

Basic financial literacy is essential to long-term career satisfaction and wellness. Medical training imposes a significant financial burden on medical students, trainees, and early-career surgeons, often resulting in substantial debt by the time they enter independent practice.1,2 Despite this reality, discussions regarding personal finance remain largely neglected across medical specialties, leaving trainees inadequately prepared to make financial decisions in their early careers. Recent data indicate that most plastic surgery trainees have at least 1 student loan, with 1 in 5 trainees possessing a loan exceeding $300,000.1 Shouldering large amounts of debt during residency contributes to higher levels of anxiety, stress, and resident burnout.3,4

The importance of financial education during surgical training is increasingly recognized. This attitude is widely shared within plastic surgery, with most plastic surgeons agreeing that formalized business education is a necessary component of resident education.5,6 However, there remains an educational gap in residency curricula across specialties, with less than half of plastic surgery residents receiving formal personal finance education as part of their training.1,7,8 The lack of resident-focused financial education has highlighted the need for standardized, integrated education and resources in finance, business management, and marketing principles to ensure that residents are well equipped for professional success.9 In addition to formalizing financial education, self-education is essential for trainees entering the surgical field. Financial literacy extends far beyond a mere business tool, as the psychological well-being of trainees has been correlated with their financial situation.10

The purpose of this article is to provide a practical overview of financial literacy tailored to plastic surgery trainees. We aim to bridge the educational gap by providing a framework to address common financial stressors encountered during training and early practice, including student loans, budgeting, insurance, and introductory wealth-building strategies. The topics included were selected based on their relevance to financial decisions commonly faced during plastic surgery training and early practice.

MEDICAL SCHOOL AND TRANSITION TO RESIDENCY

Debt Management

The average medical student graduates with more than $200,000 in educational debt.3,11 This is especially pertinent to students interested in competitive specialties, such as plastic surgery, who are likely to accumulate additional expenses associated with away rotations, in-person interviewing, and research-related expenses.12,13 It is critical to make sound financial decisions during medical school tenure to prevent significant debt accrual when possible. This can be accomplished by choosing loans that do not accrue interest until after graduation, such as subsidized federal loans, before using unsubsidized loans or private loans. With the new legislation resulting in caps on federal student loans, students may need to take out additional private loans for medical education. Thus, cost of living and tuition may become important considerations when deciding where to attend medical school.14 Students should also consider applying to tuition-free medical schools when possible.

For preexisting undergraduate debt, prioritize paying off high-interest consumer debt (ie, credit card debt).15 The average annual credit card interest rate has nearly doubled from 12.9% in 2013 to 22.8% in 2023.16 These high interest rates are further exacerbated by continuous compounding interest and have the potential to quickly become overwhelming. Federal student loans allow a deferment period during medical school and a 6-month grace period following graduation before monthly payments begin. In 2025, the average interest rate for a federal student loan was 7.94% for graduate students.17 Depending on the lender, certain qualifying loans may be consolidated for a lower interest rate.18 However, discretion is advised, as consolidation may end the grace period early.18

RESIDENCY TRAINING

Budgeting and Saving

Significant earning transitions require disciplined budgeting to prevent poor spending habits and potential economic issues in early careers. Maintenance of an existing budget or creation of a new one will help to avoid the incurrence of additional debt. One approach is the 50-20-30 rule, where 50% of income is used for essential expenses, 20% towards savings, and 30% for lifestyle and recreational purchases.19,20 Multiple free online budgeting tools can assist in tracking expenses and help manage balances for banking or credit card accounts. Intentionally putting aside money for savings in the event of an emergency is also highly important to prevent unnecessary borrowing. Having an available emergency fund has been shown to influence financial well-being.21 We recommend keeping about 3–6 months of rent in an emergency fund.22 These funds can be housed in high-yield savings accounts from which cash can be easily withdrawn.

Debt Management

It is critical to avoid defaulting on loans to avoid future financial ramifications. For certain qualifying individuals (eg, those experiencing financial hardship, school attendance, etc.), deferment may be granted, allowing a temporary pause in required payments with continued interest accrual. For borrowers who do not qualify for deferment, forbearance may be requested to temporarily reduce or pause payments with similar continued interest accrual.18

We recommend that all borrowers with qualifying loans enroll in an income-driven repayment (IDR) plan, which caps payments at a percentage of discretionary income, keeping payments manageable during residency.23 This allows for eligibility for the Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) program for employees of qualifying public service employers. The PSLF Program forgives public and nondefaulted loans after 120 qualifying monthly payments.23,24 For those who are not employed by PSLF program–qualifying employers, enrollment in an IDR plan still provides substantial benefits, such as reducing accrued interest, lowering monthly payments, and forgiving any remaining balance after 20–25 years.25,26 Recent legislation has been passed that will significantly impact traditional IDR plans after July 1, 2028.14,27 A comparison of currently available IDR plans and future eligibility is included in Table 1. Refinancing government loans with private lenders is another option for loan repayment. Private lenders may offer lower interest refinance options with reduced payment terms for physicians during training (∼$100/mo) that then enter full repayment upon entering practice. However, refinancing should be approached with caution, as extending or resetting the loan term may increase the total interest paid over the life of the loan. In addition, refinancing makes borrowers ineligible for PSLF; therefore, in most cases, residents should only refinance their federal loans upon completion of training if job selection warrants them ineligible for PSLF.28 Physicians with a combination of loans can refinance their private loans while repaying their federal loans within a PSLF-qualifying plan.23 Because federal repayment and forgiveness programs are subject to evolving statutes and regulations, readers should verify current eligibility requirements and repayment options through Federal Student Aid and applicable federal regulations.

Insurance Considerations

An often-overlooked aspect of financial planning during surgical training is insurance coverage. In the setting of disability preventing a plastic surgeon from practicing, disability insurance provides 60%–80% of the respective surgeon’s average income.29 However, disability insurance can only insure current income and not future earning potential. The decision to purchase disability insurance during residency should therefore be individualized based on financial circumstances, health status, anticipated future needs, and risk tolerance. For some trainees, obtaining coverage earlier may offer advantages, such as lower premiums and more favorable underwriting. In practice, however, privately purchased insurance amounts should be coordinated with employer-provided disability insurance.

Life insurance is also worth consideration, especially if there are dependent children. Term life insurance provides coverage for death, making it affordable to obtain substantial coverage when the insured is young and healthy. Whole-life insurance, in contrast, has a savings component and guarantees a payout and functions more like a savings/investment account. Because whole-life insurance is generally more expensive and carries higher fees than term life insurance, it is often not recommended for trainees. It is also important to note that whole-life policies may be marketed by financial advisors who are not acting in a fiduciary capacity and who may receive commission-based incentives.30

Investing Early and Simply

The complexities of investment can seem overwhelming. However, plastic surgery trainees can benefit greatly from establishing a simple, disciplined investment strategy. Even with outstanding student debt, trainees should allocate some available funds for investment.31 Investing early provides a larger window for compounding interest to accelerate your account’s growth and, ultimately, a greater return on investment.

Although the temptation may exist to pursue higher-risk opportunities in seeking more rapid returns, studies have shown that even professional investors fail to beat the market over time.32,33 Although several investment options are available, passively managed funds, such as index funds, target date funds, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs), offer diversification, long-term growth potential, and an easy-to-maintain investment framework. Target date funds use a target date (typically the date of expected retirement) and invest in a mix of stocks, bonds, and money market funds. These funds are simple to invest in and automatically make the needed asset allocation adjustments over time. Most large investment firms, such as Fidelity, Vanguard, and Charles Schwab, have these funds readily available. These investments typically have lower fees compared with managed funds while offering stability against volatile markets.

Another strategy is a 3-fund portfolio.35 This option is low-cost, easy to manage, and allows for diversification of investments. The 3-fund portfolio comprises 3 index funds: a total US stock market fund, a total international stock market fund, and a total US bond market fund.35,36 This structure offers broad diversification, low fees, and long-term growth, providing an easy-to-maintain investment framework well suited for physicians with limited time and low early income. Actively managed funds have an account manager who hand-picks investments in an attempt to beat the market. However, studies have shown that passive index funds consistently outperform managed funds.37,38 Furthermore, investors in actively managed funds incur higher fees compared with passive index funds and ETFs. These fees are directly deducted from the returns.39

For surgical trainees, a Roth IRA is particularly advantageous, as investing in this early allows for maximum income growth potential.41 Contributions are taxed at the time of contribution and can be withdrawn at the age of 60 years without subsequent withdrawal taxes.42 This provides an opportunity to maximize contributions during training to “lock in” a low tax rate at the time of contribution. We recommend that trainees use employer retirement plans (403b, 401k, 457 plans) contributing the minimum amount for contribution matching, if applicable. If additional funds remain, taxable brokerage accounts are a worthwhile option for consideration. These accounts, however, should only be used for immediate-term goals, such as down payments or practice buy-in costs.

TRANSITION TO EARLY-CAREER SURGEON

Employment Options

Independent practitioners have historically dominated plastic surgery, but insurer consolidation, declining reimbursement, and hospital system expansion have driven more surgeons into employed roles.43,44 Current popular practice models include independent practice, group practice, and hospital-based employment. Independent practice offers maximum autonomy but carries significant overhead costs (office space, equipment, staff), the hassle of payer negotiations, and the risk of fluctuating patient volumes.45 Recruitment agreements can assist with startup expenses and are usually offered as repayable loans; however, interest rates and terms should be negotiated carefully.44 Other funding options include bank or small business loans, business lines of credit, equipment financing, and venture capital loans.

Group practice provides shared overhead costs and administrative work, built-in referrals, and superior reimbursement rates. However, this model may limit individual decision-making. Prospective members should clarify expectations regarding capital contribution or buy-in required, exit terms, and noncompete clauses.45 Hospital/health system employment offers a stable initial income stream, minimal overhead costs, and reduced administrative responsibilities. Although attractive to newly graduated residents paying off student debt, hospital employment generally yields lower long-term salaries compared with independent or group practice.46

Contract Negotiation

A surgeon’s first employment contract sets the tone for their overall financial and professional trajectory. Unfavorable employment terms can lead to administrative burden; unclear roles and responsibilities; and, ultimately, physician burnout.47 Begin by negotiating a competitive base salary and consider a sign-on bonus or loan-repayment assistance to help manage lingering student debt. Clarify malpractice, disability, and life insurance policies, including responsibility for deductibles or tail coverage. Understanding the contract term length (fixed-length versus rolling), termination provisions, and any noncompete clauses is essential to prevent future restrictions. If noncompete clauses are unavoidable, negotiate a buy-out option.48 Define call schedules, postcall recovery time, and compensation for call coverage. For academic roles, ensure clarity on protected research or teaching time, promotion criteria, and bonus structures (relative value unit versus collections-based). Additional considerations with group practice negotiations include pathways to ownership, which may be based on investment capital; sweat equity (where ownership is granted over time); or a combination of both. Obtaining equity or shared ownership allows for greater voice in hiring decisions and expansion, autonomy, and profit sharing. Finally, before signing any contract, seek guidance from mentors or colleagues with experience and have a healthcare attorney review the contract. For additional information regarding contract negotiation, see Fine et al48 and Johnson et al.44

Malpractice Insurance Considerations

When transitioning to practice from residency, plastic surgeons should pay careful attention to their malpractice insurance coverage. Most young physicians entering a new practice are offered claims-made malpractice insurance as an employment benefit.49 Claims-made malpractice insurance protects the provider from claims only during the policy or employment period. In addition, tail insurance is required to protect against claims filed after the initial policy ends. In contrast, occurrence-made coverage protects the provider regardless of when the malpractice occurrence happened.49 The cost of tail insurance can be as much as 1.5–2 times a typical annual malpractice premium.50

Employer Disability Insurance

Employer-offered or organization-offered disability insurance typically awards an average of 40%–60% of average income and has longer elimination periods, meaning a prolonged time between disability and receiving benefits.29 As such, we recommend purchasing an additional personal policy. For plastic surgeons, the “own occupation” policy allows a newly disabled physician who takes a different job with a lower salary to still have coverage, with the new wage subtracted from the disability benefits.29

Financial Advisors

Another consideration for the early-career surgeon is whether to hire a financial advisor. Choosing the right financial advisor can be challenging but begins with understanding types of advisors, how fees are assessed, and whether financial goals are aligned. When seeking an advisor, ensure they are governed by the fiduciary standard, which legally requires the advisor to act in the client’s best interest.19,51 Surgeons should verify fiduciary governance by obtaining a signed fiduciary oath52 or verification through tools, such as https://brokercheck.finra.org/.53 Further, surgeons should mitigate potential conflicts of interest by seeking fee-only rather than fee-based advisors, as fee-only advisors do not receive commission or compensation from the products they sell to clients and instead receive a flat rate, hourly rate, or percentage of assets they manage for the client.54 Fees based on a percentage of assets under management typically range from 50 to 150 basis points (0.5%–1.5% of assets under management) and increase as the portfolio grows over time.54 Another option to consider is roboadvisors, which are automated services that manage investments based on an individual’s preferred risk tolerance and personal goals.55 These platforms have grown in popularity because they are customizable, accessible, require low starting deposits, and are typically less expensive than traditional financial advisors.55

BUILDING LONG-TERM WEALTH AND RETIREMENT PLANNING

When investing for the future, physicians may wonder, “how much is enough?” Retirement planning commonly follows the 4% rule, which suggests that withdrawing 4% of a retirement portfolio annually will allow retirement savings to last throughout retirement.56 To calculate the required savings, the desired annual retirement income is divided by 4%. For example, an annual retirement income goal of $200,000 would require approximately 5 million in savings using the 4% rule.15 The 4% rule assumes a 30-year retirement and no additional income.56 Because most plastic surgeons work into their late 60s, and typically have additional income postretirement, they should consider using a 5% value.57,58

Other philosophies include the financial independence/retire-early movement, which promotes heavy investing and saving during your prime earning years, coupled with frugal living, to achieve retirement in your 30s or early 40s.59 In contrast, the “die with zero” philosophy advocates for spending money intentionally throughout life to maximize experiences and create lasting memories instead of accumulating a large sum of money that one might not live to enjoy.60 Regardless of which method or philosophy is chosen, the answer to “how much is enough” is tailored to the individual goals of the surgeon. Although this article provides an overview of financial recommendations, financial models and comparative projections should be interpreted with caution. These approaches rely on assumptions and observational data, and their real-world applicability may vary based on individual circumstances, market conditions, policy changes, and behavioral factors.34 Accordingly, the recommendations presented here are intended to guide decision-making rather than predict outcomes, and observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causal or reproducible effects in practice.

CONCLUSIONS

Understanding the importance of financial literacy for trainee and early-career plastic surgeons can help ensure that plastic surgeons feel secure in their financial future. Identifying and establishing good spending habits early in training, using federal resources to manage debt, and enacting relevant safeguards are important steps in preventing future issues. Early saving and investing while understanding how to make money work effectively can provide dividends in the future. These small and consistent steps can position trainees for future financial success.
