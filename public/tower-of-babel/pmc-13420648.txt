New-Onset Atrial Flutter Revealing a Large Right Coronary Artery to Superior Vena Cava Fistula
Authors: Sankalp Acharya, Vyoma Patel, Sai Shahane, Prateek Jain, Hans Del Mundo, Hardikkumar Bhanderi, Yasmin Shamshuddin Hamirani, Pratik Panchal, Ajay Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery fistulas are rare anomalous communications between a coronary artery and a cardiac chamber or great vessel. Large fistulas can cause chamber dilation, myocardial ischemia, heart failure, and arrhythmias.Case SummaryA 63-year-old man presented with palpitations, hypotension, and atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response requiring urgent cardioversion. Multimodality imaging with coronary computed tomography angiography and cardiac magnetic resonance revealed a large right coronary artery–to–superior vena cava fistula with right-sided chamber dilation. Cardiac catheterization confirmed the diagnosis, and the patient underwent successful transcatheter coil embolization.DiscussionCoronary artery fistulas may present with atrial arrhythmias due to chronic left-to-right shunting and atrial remodeling. This case highlights atrial flutter as an uncommon presentation and underscores the importance of multimodality imaging for diagnosis and procedural planning.Take-Home MessagesCoronary artery fistulas should be considered in patients with unexplained atrial arrhythmias and cardiac chamber enlargement. Early recognition enables definitive treatment with transcatheter closure.

History of Presentation

A 63-year-old man with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with a chief complaint of palpitations for 2 days. He reported a sudden onset of a rapid heartbeat, lightheadedness, and flushing while working out. Initially, the symptoms resolved with rest, but symptoms recurred the next day, prompting an emergency department visit. On arrival, his vital signs were notable for hypotension (blood pressure 80/60 mm Hg) and tachycardia (heart rate 120 beats/min). Physical examination revealed normal heart sounds. Electrocardiogram (Figure 1) revealed atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response. Given his hemodynamic instability, urgent cardioversion was performed. Laboratory evaluation showed troponin 1.8 ng/mL, white blood cell count 7.5 × 109/L, hemoglobin 16.2 g/dL, potassium 3.9 mmol/L, sodium 147 mmol/L, magnesium 1.92 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 24 mg/dL, creatinine 1.26 mg/dL, thyroid-stimulating hormone 2.49 μIU/mL, T4 6.9 μg/dL, and free T3 2.8 pg/mL. The patient underwent successful direct current cardioversion with a single 250-J shock, restoring normal sinus rhythm. Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical history.

Differential Diagnoses

The differential diagnosis for new-onset atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response includes primary atrial arrhythmias related to structural heart disease, ischemic heart disease, pulmonary embolism, hyperthyroidism, electrolyte abnormalities, and alcohol or stimulant use.

Investigation

Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a low-normal left ventricular ejection fraction with borderline global hypokinesis. The left atrium was normal in size, and no thrombus or mass was visualized in the left atrial appendage. The interatrial septum was intact without evidence of atrial septal defect, and contrast injection confirmed the absence of an interatrial shunt. Valvular assessment showed trace to mild mitral regurgitation and mild tricuspid regurgitation. Mild atherosclerotic plaques were noted in the descending aorta. The patient remained asymptomatic and was discharged on apixaban 5 mg twice daily for 30 days with outpatient cardiology follow-up.

During follow-up in the cardiology office, a computed tomography angiogram of the heart and coronary arteries with contrast was ordered to rule out underlying coronary artery disease. The coronary calcium score was zero (Coronary Artery Disease–Reporting and Data System 0), with widely patent coronary arteries and no plaque or stenosis. Notably, imaging revealed a large coronary artery fistula originating from the proximal right coronary artery (RCA), coursing posterosuperiorly behind the ascending aorta, and draining into the superior vena cava (SVC), as shown in Figures 3A to 3C. There was associated severe dilation of the right atrium and right ventricle. The ascending aorta measured 44 mm at the level of the right pulmonary artery, with no atheromatous calcification in the aortic root, arch, or descending thoracic aorta. Further evaluation with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 59%) and moderate dilation of the right atrium and right ventricle with preserved right ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 52%). The calculated ratio between pulmonary (Qp) and systemic flow (Qs) (Qp/Qs) was 1.18. Subsequent cardiac catheterization revealed a large arteriovenous malformation originating from the proximal RCA and draining into the SVC, as shown in Figure 3E.

Management

Specific indications for closure include symptomatic presentation, arrhythmias related to coronary artery fistulas (CAFs), and cardiac chamber enlargement. Although the patient had no significant symptoms aside from newly developed arrhythmias, imaging evidence of right-sided chamber dilation on both MRI and computed tomography (CT) prompted the decision to proceed with coil embolization of the fistula. The patient underwent successful coil embolization. The post-coil embolization catheterization image is shown in Figure 3F.

Outcomes and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged with close outpatient follow-up with cardiology. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, the patient appeared to be symptom-free.

Discussion

This case illustrates a significant and potentially underrecognized association between CAFs and atrial arrhythmias. The patient's presentation with new-onset atrial flutter, hemodynamic instability, and subsequent discovery of a large RCA-to-SVC fistula with right-sided chamber enlargement strongly suggests a causal relationship (Table 1).

The mechanism by which CAF leads to atrial arrhythmias is multifactorial. Chronic left-to-right shunting results in progressive atrial dilation and remodeling.1,2 In this case, moderate right atrial and right ventricular dilation on cardiac MRI provided the substrate for atrial flutter. The fistula created a continuous high-flow shunt from the systemic arterial circulation to the venous system, bypassing the myocardial capillary bed and imposing chronic volume stress on the right heart chambers.3 The Qp/Qs ratio of 1.18 indicates an 18% increase in pulmonary blood flow compared with systemic flow, representing a small but hemodynamically significant shunt.4 Although this ratio is below the traditional threshold of 1.5, often cited for intervention in isolated shunt lesions, the presence of symptoms (arrhythmia with hemodynamic instability) and progressive chamber dilation justify closure.4,5 Although thrombosis within a fistula has been reported to trigger arrhythmias, no thrombus was identified in this case.2,6 Alternative mechanisms that may contribute include right atrial ischemia, as the right atrium is primarily supplied by the RCA; a steal phenomenon may result in relative ischemia, particularly with a small shunt. Involvement of the sinus node artery, which typically arises from the proximal RCA, may also affect the sinoatrial region even in the absence of overt dysfunction.7 In addition, aortic root dilation may increase interatrial septal mobility through mechanical effects, potentially creating an arrhythmogenic substrate.5

This case demonstrates the complementary roles of different imaging modalities in evaluating CAF. CT angiography provided excellent anatomic detail of the fistula origin, course, and drainage site, which is essential for procedural planning.5,8 Cardiac MRI offered superior quantification of ventricular volumes, function, and shunt magnitude through Qp/Qs calculation.8 The discrepancy in left ventricular ejection fraction between echocardiography, CT, and MRI underscores the superior accuracy of MRI for volumetric assessment, particularly in volume overload.8 Cardiac catheterization provided definitive anatomic confirmation and enabled therapeutic intervention.5

This patient meets multiple criteria for fistula closure according to current guidelines. Specific indications for closure include symptomatic presentation, arrhythmia related to the CAF, and cardiac chamber enlargement, all of which are present in this case.9 The fistula in our case would be classified as large based on the chamber dilation and shunt physiology.5 Large fistula carries risks for coronary steal, myocardial ischemia, progressive chamber dilation, heart failure, and recurrent arrhythmias if left untreated.9,10 The borderline global hypokinesis noted on initial echocardiography likely reflected volume overload rather than primary coronary disease, given the zero calcium score, absence of coronary stenosis, and normalization of ejection fraction on MRI after cardioversion.

The decision to proceed with coil embolization was appropriate given the symptomatic presentation and imaging evidence of chamber dilation. Transcatheter closure is the preferred first-line approach for most CAFs, with coil embolization being the most commonly employed technique.5 For proximal RCA-to-SVC fistulas, the transarterial approach is typically used, with controlled deployment of coils within the fistula tract to achieve complete occlusion while preserving normal coronary flow.5 Procedural success rates for transcatheter closure are generally high (approximately 90%), although complications can occur, including myocardial ischemia, device embolization, residual shunting, and coronary thrombosis.5,6 The absence of marked proximal coronary dilation in this patient is favorable, as closure of fistulas with severely dilated proximal coronary arteries carries an 11% to 15% risk of postoperative myocardial infarction due to thrombosis or low flow.4 Alternative devices, including vascular plugs and covered stents, may be considered when coils are insufficient or anatomically unsuitable.5 The choice of device depends on fistula size, location, tortuosity, and operator experience.5

Following successful coil embolization, patients require careful monitoring for complications and assessment of procedural success. Immediate complications include coronary ischemia from inadvertent occlusion of normal coronary branches, device embolization, and hemopericardium.5 Antiplatelet therapy is typically continued for 3 to 6 months postprocedure to reduce the risk of device thrombosis.5 The need for continued anticoagulation in this patient depends on arrhythmia recurrence and CHA2DS2-VASc score assessment.

Untreated large CAFs can lead to progressive complications, including worsening heart failure, recurrent arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia from coronary steal, endarteritis, and, rarely, fistula rupture.2,10 A small fistula may close spontaneously, but large fistulas seldom do and typically enlarge over time.4 The frequency of the symptoms and complications increases with age, making early intervention in symptomatic patients advisable.3

It is important to acknowledge that this case lacks long-term follow-up to confirm whether fistula closure led to sustained improvement in right atrial and right ventricular size or arrhythmia. Conclusions

This case illustrates that large CAFs may manifest as new-onset atrial flutter with hemodynamic instability secondary to chronic volume overload and atrial enlargement. CAFs should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained atrial arrhythmias, particularly when cardiac chamber dilation is present. Multimodality imaging, including CT angiography, cardiac MRI, and cardiac catheterization, is essential for accurate diagnosis, shunt assessment, and procedural planning. Overall, this case underscores the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular evaluation in patients with new-onset arrhythmias and reinforces the notion that, although rare, structural cardiac anomalies can be treatable causes of atrial flutter.
