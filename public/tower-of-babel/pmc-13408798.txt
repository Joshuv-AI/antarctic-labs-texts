Synthesis and in vitro/in silico evaluation studies of arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl amine and amino acid derivatives as preliminary acetylcholinesterase inhibitors
Authors: Olla Sharhan, Wafa M. Al-Madhagi, Mohammed M. Al-Ghubari, Yousef A. Al-Gailani, Abdulkarim K. Y. Alzomor, Somaia A. Al-Madhagy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives were synthesised via a modified two-step procedure involving oxalyl activation followed by sulfonation and were fully characterized using IR, UV–Vis, 1H NMR, and MS analyses, confirming their structures and high purity. The biological profiles of the compounds were evaluated using combined in vitro and in silico approaches. Both derivatives exhibited measurable acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory activity, with compound 2 showing greater potency (IC₅₀ = 6.51 ± 0.33 µM) than compound 1 (IC₅₀ = 24.05 ± 1.29 µM), although both less active than the reference inhibitor donepezil. Cytotoxicity evaluation revealed weak activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells and moderate activity against HepG2 liver cancer cells, with compound 2 again demonstrating superior activity (IC₅₀ = 811.5 ± 32.2 µM). Molecular docking studies supported favourable binding of both compounds within the active gorge of human AChE (PDB: 4EY7), while interactions with BACE1 were weak and non-specific. In silico ADME/T analysis suggested acceptable drug-likeness with no violations of Lipinski’s rule, although the high polarity of compound 1 indicated low oral absorption. PASS predictions did not indicate Alzheimer-specific activity but suggested general potential protease- and kinase-related inhibition. Overall, these compounds represent preliminary scaffolds with modest AChE inhibition and limited cytotoxicity, requiring structural optimisation for further therapeutic development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52865-1.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders, particularly Alzheimer’s disease (AD) remain a major global health burden, and are characterised by progressive cognitive decline, memory impairment, and cholinergic dysfunction1,2. Despite extensive research efforts, current therapeutic strategies are largely limited to symptomatic relief for many patients, highlighting the urgent need for novel chemotypes of modulating multiple pathological pathways associated with AD3–5. Among the currently approved treatments, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors, such as donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine, constitute one of the primary therapeutic strategies used to improve cholinergic transmission6,7. However, these agents exhibit limited long-term efficacy and are often associated with adverse effects, reinforcing the demand for alternative scaffolds with improved pharmacological profiles8–11.

In recent years, hybrid molecules incorporating sulfonylhydrazide motifs have attracted significant attention in medicinal chemistry owing to their structural flexibility, tunable polarity, and capacity to engage in diverse biological interactions12–14. Compounds bearing sulfonylhydrazide and oxalyl functionalities have been reported to exhibit a broad range of biological activities, including antimicrobial, anticancer, and enzyme-inhibitory effects10,11,15–17. Recent reports further demonstrate that structurally diverse derivatives, including sulfonyl- and hydrazide-based compounds, show promising biological activities across multiple targets, supporting their relevance in modern drug discovery18. These features render such scaffolds attractive candidates for further exploration in drug discovery, particularly within multi-target therapeutic strategies9,15,16,19. The design of the present compounds was based on combining sulfonylhydrazide functionality, known for its enzyme-interacting capability, with oxamoyl-linked polar (glutamic acid) and lipophilic (n-butyl) fragments. This strategy was intended to explore how polarity and side-chain variation influence acetylcholinesterase binding and biological response at a preliminary level, rather than to generate optimized inhibitors. The synthesized compounds were based on the strategic inclusion of pharmacologically significant structural characteristics known to improve enzyme binding and biological activity.

In this context, the present study describes the synthesis of two arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives incorporating either an L‑glutamic acid residue or an n‑butyl substituent. Their structural were fully characterised, and their biological activities were evaluated through in vitro AChE inhibitory assays, cytotoxicity evaluation against cancer cell lines, and in silico molecular docking and ADME/T predictions. Furthermore, recent advances in computational methodologies, including molecular docking and structure-based drug design, have significantly enhanced the identification and optimization of bioactive compounds20. Accordingly, this study seeks to address the following research questions: (i) what is the binding affinity of the designed ligands toward thymidylate synthase (PDB ID: 1TIS), human acetylcholinesterase complexed with fasciculin-ii, glycosylated protein (PDB ID; 1B41) and Recombinant Human Acetylcholinesterase PDB ID: 4EYZ using molecular docking approaches? (ii) how do the docked ligands interact with key active-site residues based on 2D and 3D interaction analyses? and (iii) can the docking protocol be validated through redocking of a reference ligand (e.g., donepezil) using RMSD comparison with its crystallographic pose? This evaluation is intended to elucidate their potential molecular interactions and to support their preliminary assessment as multi-target therapeutic candidates relevant to both anticancer and anti-Alzheimer disease pathways.

Materials

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Benzenesulphonyl chloride, diethyl oxalate, L‑glutamic acid, and n‑butylamine were obtained commercially. Reaction progress was tentatively monitored using thin‑layer chromatography (TLC), and purified products were characterised by IR, UV–Vis, 1H NMR, and mass spectrometry data. UV–Vis analysis qualitatively supports the presence of conjugated aromatic and carbonyl chromophores.

General synthetic procedure

Chemical synthesis of the starting substance

Synthesis involved two main steps: preparation of the hydrazidooxalyl intermediates, followed by coupling with either L‑glutamic acid or n‑butylamine21–23. Sulfonylation using benzenesulphonyl chloride afforded the final derivatives. Purity was verified by MS.

Synthesis of benzensulphohydrazidooxalylglutamic acid (Compound 1)

A solution of sodium methoxide (prepared from sodium metal, 0.0067 g, in 5 mL methanol) was added to hydrazidooxalylglutamic acid (0.003 mol). Triethylamine (0.304 g, 0.003 mol) and benzenesulphonyl chloride (0.529 g, 0.003 mol) were added dropwise with stirring at ambient temperature. The reaction mixture was left overnight, after which the precipitate was filtered, dissolved in water, and acidified with HCl to pH 3–4. The product was collected by filtration, washed with cold water, and dried to yield compound 1 as a crystalline solid (72% yield, m.p. 225–230 °C).

Determination of biological activity

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory assay

The anti-Alzheimer potential of the synthesised compounds was evaluated by measuring their inhibitory activity against AChE using the standard colorimetric method based on Ellman’s reaction22,24,25. A reconstituted AChE solution was diluted 25-fold in assay buffer, and 10 µL of the enzyme solution was added to each designated well of a 96-well plate, including enzyme control (EC), inhibitor control (IC), solvent control (SC), and sample (S). The final reaction volume was adjusted to 160 µL with assay buffer. After incubation at room temperature (10–15 min, protected from light), absorbance was measured kinetically at 412 nm for 40 min. Two time points (t₁ and t₂) were selected within the linear range, and corresponding absorbance values (OD₁ and OD₂) were recorded. Donepezil served as the reference inhibitor. Cytotoxicity (MTT) assay

The anticancer activity was assessed using the MTT assay against MCF‑7 (ATCC) and HepG2(ATCC) cell lines following the standard protocol24. All cells were American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ to form a monolayer. After washing with maintenance medium, serial two-fold dilutions of the test compounds were added and incubated for a further 24 h. Following treatment, 20 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS; Bio Basic Canada Inc.) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h to allow the formation of formazan crystals. The supernatant was removed, and the crystals were dissolved in 200 µL DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm with a reference wavelength of 620 nm. The percentage of cell viability was calculated, and IC₅₀ values (concentration causing 50% inhibition) were derived from the dose–response curves. Doxorubicin was used as the positive control26.

Computational studies

ADME/T prediction

ADME/T properties were predicted using SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch) and pkCSM, assessing molecular weight, TPSA, LogP, CYP450 inhibition6,7,27, oral absorption, and toxicity risks. Lipinski’s and Veber’s rules were applied to assess drug-likeness and oral bioavailability28. Techniques used in the computational investigation are shown in a flowchart (Scheme 1). The study followed a stepwise workflow starting with compound synthesis, followed by molecular docking studies, reverse docking analysis to identify potential targets, and finally lead compound selection based on binding affinity and pharmacological properties. Physicochemical properties including Log P, pKa, TPSA, and GI absorption were predicted using the ADME/T software. PASS prediction of biological activity

PASS Online was used to predict potential biological activities based on Pa/Pi probabilities; http://www.way2drug.com/PASSOnline), which calculates probabilities of activity (Pa) and inactivity (Pi) for various pharmacological classes. Compounds with Pa > 0.7 were considered likely to exhibit the predicted activity experimentally, while Pa < 0.5 suggested low likelihood29.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.2 to evaluate the binding affinity towards two target proteins: thymidylate synthase from T4 phage (PDB ID: 1TYS), human acetylcholinesterase complexed with fasciculin-ii, glycosylated protein (PDB ID; 1B41) and Recombinant Human Acetylcholinesterase PDB ID: 4EYZ. Blind docking was conducted using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm with a population size of 100, 1 × 10⁶ energy evaluations, and 100 search runs30–32. Nine conformations were generated for each ligand and clustered at an RMSD threshold ≤ 1.5 Å. The best conformation was selected based on the lowest binding energy and key active-site interactions, as identified through the scPDB database. Binding energies (kcal/mol) were recorded, and interaction analyses (2D and 3D) were visualised using Discovery Studio Visualiser 4.533. To verify the docking parameters, redocking of the standard drug donepezil and native co-crystallized ligand that was extracted from the protein–ligand complex and re-docked into the same binding site using the same docking parameters applied to the test compounds. The reliability of the protocol was assessed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the redocked pose and the original crystallographic (native) ligand pose. was performed using the crystal structure with PDB ID: 4EY7. Redocking of donepezil into 4EY7 was carried out using AutoDock with the parameters described above.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Normal mode analysis (NMA) was performed using the iMODS to evaluate the intrinsic flexibility, stability, and collective motions of the protein structure. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of protein–ligand complexes were carried out by and the iMOD server (iMODS) (http://imods.chaco nlab.org). The input model consisted of best docked posed of compound 1 and 2 docked into Recombinant Human Acetylcholinesterase PDB ID: 4EYZ protein, with all hydrogen atoms, heteroatoms, and water molecules removed prior to analysis. iMODS was utilized for the analysis of the structural dynamics of the docking complexes, along with the determination of the molecular motion. The stability of the protein–compound 1 and compound 2 complexes was depicted with reference to its deformability, B-factor, eigenvalues, variance, covariance map and elastic network. The input files were docked PDB files, which were uploaded to the iMODS server, with all parameters set to default.

Statistical and data analysis

Data from in silico models were analysed using the built-in algorithms of SwissADME, pkCSM, and PASS software. All results were validated through comparison with known standards and previously reported sulphonylhydrazide derivatives.

Results and discussion

Chemical synthesis and characterisation

The arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives (compounds 1 and 2) were successfully synthesised using a modified two-step procedure21,23,34 (Scheme 2). First, the hydrazidooxalyl intermediates were prepared via nucleophilic substitution of diethyl oxalate (z)35, followed by coupling with either L-glutamic acid or n-butylamine under basic methanolic conditions. Subsequent sulfonylation with benzenesulphonyl chloride afforded the desired derivatives in good yields 72% (compound 1) and 62% (compound 2), respectively, (Fig. 1)36.Scheme 2Synthetic route for the preparation of arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives.Fig. 1Synthesis of benzenesulphohydrazidooxalic acid.

Synthetic route for the preparation of arensulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives.

Synthesis of benzenesulphohydrazidooxalic acid.

The successful formation of the target hybrid compounds was confirmed through combined spectroscopic analyses (IR, 1H NMR, UV–Vis, and MS). In the IR spectra, the appearance of strong absorption bands in the range of 1680–1720 cm⁻1 is characteristic of carbonyl stretching vibrations, consistent with the formation of amide and oxamoyl functionalities. The presence of additional bands around 3200–3400 cm⁻1 further supports the existence of NH groups involved in hydrogen bonding.

The 1H NMR spectra provide further evidence of successful scaffold construction. The disappearance of signals corresponding to reactive precursor functionalities, alongside the emergence of new resonances attributable to amide NH protons and aliphatic side-chain protons, confirms the formation of the hybrid framework. Aromatic proton signals appeared in the expected downfield region (δ 6.8–7.9 ppm), indicating preservation of the aromatic moiety and its electronic environment within the final structure.

UV–Vis spectra exhibited absorption bands associated with π → π* and n → π* electronic transitions, arising from the conjugated aromatic system and carbonyl groups. The observed bathochromic shift compared to the starting materials suggests enhanced conjugation following hybridisation, further supporting successful molecular assembly37. UV–Vis analysis qualitatively supports the presence of conjugated aromatic and carbonyl chromophores.

Collectively, these spectroscopic features, together with mass spectrometric data confirming the expected molecular weights, validate the successful synthesis of the proposed hybrid compounds.

Docking analysis of the two tested compounds against recombinant human acetylcholinesterase (PDB ID: 4EYZ) revealed distinct binding interactions compared to the reference inhibitor donepezil. Compound 1 exhibited conventional hydrogen bonding with Arg525, Arg522, Gln527, and Ala528, alongside hydrophobic contacts involving Val429, Val408, Arg525, and Tyr382 (Fig. 6). These interactions suggest that compound 1 engages residues located deeper within the catalytic gorge, potentially stabilizing its binding through a combination of polar and nonpolar contacts. In contrast, donepezil displayed a conventional hydrogen bond with Gly345 and hydrophobic interactions with Pro344 and Trp286, consistent with its known binding at the peripheral anionic site (Fig. 6). The differences in binding profiles highlight that compound 1 may exploit alternative binding pockets within the enzyme, which could contribute to variations in inhibitory potency or selectivity compared to donepezil. Structure–activity relationship and pharmacophore analysis

The SAR evaluation of the synthesized arenesulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives underscores the importance of three structural features: the sulfonylhydrazide core, the oxamoyl linker, and the nature of the terminal substituents. The sulfonylhydrazide moiety functions as a central pharmacophoric element, enabling hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with catalytic residues of acetylcholinesterase (AChE). This scaffold has previously been reported to enhance enzyme inhibition and cytotoxicity in NSAID-hydrazidoxamoyl conjugates, supporting its relevance in multifunctional drug design51.

The oxamoyl linker contributes conformational flexibility, allowing the ligands to adapt within the enzyme’s active gorge and stabilize binding through a combination of polar and hydrophobic contacts. Substituent variation further modulates activity: the polar L-glutamic acid residue in compound 1 facilitated hydrogen bonding with hydrophilic residues, whereas the n-butyl substituent in compound 2 favored hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals stabilization. These differences are consistent with the observed biological profiles, where compound 2 exhibited moderately higher AChE inhibition and cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells.

Molecular docking analyses corroborated these findings, revealing hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, and π–π stacking interactions with key active-site residues. The distinct binding orientations of the two derivatives highlight the balance between polarity and lipophilicity in determining inhibitory potency.

Taken together, the integration of the sulfonylhydrazide scaffold, flexible oxamoyl linker, and substituent-specific interactions defines a preliminary pharmacophoric framework for AChE inhibition. These insights parallel observations in NSAID-hydrazidoxamoyl amino acid conjugates, where amino acid incorporation enhanced both enzyme inhibition and anticancer activity. Thus, the present study reinforces the broader SAR rationale that combining hydrazidoxamoyl motifs with judiciously selected substituents can yield multifunctional scaffolds with potential for optimization in neurodegenerative and oncological contexts.

Conclusion

In this study, two arenesulfonylhydrazinoxamoyl derivatives were successfully synthesised and structurally characterised using IR, UV–Vis, MS, and 1H NMR spectroscopy. Their physicochemical properties and preliminary biological profiles were evaluated through combined in vitro and in silico approaches. Biological testing revealed weak-to-moderate cytotoxic activity against HepG2 liver cancer cells and limited activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells, with both compounds showing lower potency than the reference drug doxorubicin. In addition, modest acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory activity was observed, particularly for compound 2, though this activity remained significantly weaker than that of donepezil.

Importantly, the anticancer assays were exploratory and independent of the AChE inhibition studies. While some literature suggests that AChE may influence tumor biology, our data do not establish a mechanistic link between AChE inhibition and cytotoxicity. The anticancer findings should therefore be considered preliminary, providing only a broader view of the compounds’ biological potential.

Molecular docking studies supported plausible binding interactions within the AChE active site, consistent with the experimental inhibition observed, although the predicted affinities were not strong enough to indicate high potency. PASS predictions did not highlight Alzheimer-specific activity, but such computational tools may fail to capture weak or indirect enzyme interactions. In silico ADME/T analysis indicated acceptable drug-like properties with no violations of Lipinski’s rule, suggesting reasonable physicochemical characteristics for early-stage scaffolds.

Overall, the synthesised derivatives represent preliminary chemical frameworks with modest AChE inhibition and weak-to-moderate cytotoxicity. While these results provide a foundation for further structural optimisation, additional chemical modification and mechanistic studies are required to clarify therapeutic relevance and to determine whether cholinergic modulation contributes to anticancer effects.
