The Geometry of Qubit Decoherence: Linear vs. Nonlinear Dynamics in the Bloch Ball
Alan C. Maioli, Evaldo M. F. Curado, Jean-Pierre Gazeau, Tomoi Koide

Abstract
We present two complementary approaches to the GKSL equation for an open qubit. The first, based on linearity, yields solutions illustrated by mixed states trajectories in the Bloch ball, including non-random asymptotic fixed points, and exceptional points. The second, exploiting the SU ( 2 ) (2) symmetry, leads to a nonlinear dynamical system that separates angular dynamics from radial dissipation. This symmetry-based perspective offers a promising route toward generalisation to open qudits.

1
Introduction

Studies in quantum physics have traditionally focused on systems isolated from their environment.
However, with the recent developments in quantum computing
[
1
]
and quantum thermodynamics
[
2
]
, the dynamics of open quantum systems has attracted growing interest.
This line of research is also deeply connected to the measurement problem
[
3
]
, an unresolved issue since the dawn of quantum mechanics, making it critically important from both fundamental and applied perspectives
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

The dynamics of such open quantum systems should, in principle, be derived
from the microscopic unitary evolution of the total system (system plus environment),
but this is generally difficult to perform. An axiomatic approach,
therefore, becomes effective. Specifically, we assume that the dynamics of the system of interest are linear and Markovian.
Under these assumptions, if we impose the requirement that the time evolution must preserve physical states,
that is, a complete positivity and trace-preservation (CPTP) map,
it is known that the quantum master equation is
the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) equation
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
This equation unifies the treatment of the unitary evolution governed by the system’s Hamiltonian and the non-unitary dissipative processes described by Lindblad’s jump operators, which represent the interaction with the environment.
In particular, when applied to a single qubit (a two-level system), the fundamental unit of quantum information, its time evolution can be intuitively understood through a geometric picture using the Bloch vector
[
10
]
.
In the representation based on Cartesian coordinates, this GKSL equation reduces to a tractable system of linear equations, and its solutions and properties have already been extensively studied. Furthermore, we analyze specific anisotropic cases to reveal how the interplay between Hamiltonian evolution and environmental dissipation leads to phenomena such as damped oscillations and the emergence of asymptotically stable fixed points, which are not random, in the mixed state space. This investigation highlights a feature in designing robust qubits, wherein engineering the qubit-environment interaction can protect quantum states.

In this paper, we begin by revisiting
this approach, discussing new aspects
like a theoretical proposal to stabilize
the qubit and a discussion about the
existence of exceptional points. Then,
we propose a new descriptive method for the same physical system using spherical coordinates.
This coordinate transformation renders the equations of motion nonlinear, but it offers advantages that more than compensate for this complexity.
First, it reveals a clear variable separation structure. Specifically, the radial variable, which is related to the change in the system’s purity (and thus the informational entropy due to interaction with the environment), is naturally decoupled from the angular variables, which describe the orientation of the quantum state. This structure of variable separation is expected to hold universally, not only for two-level systems but also for general N-level systems.
This provides an insightful theoretical framework for uniformly treating the dynamics of more complex quantum systems.
Hence, we believe that our approach offers a new perspective on the geometric interpretation of the quantum information space.

The organisation of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the GKSL equation in the qubit case and proceed with its standard linear analysis in Cartesian coordinates. We then study the isotropic case and particular anisotropic situations. The examples are illustrated by figures showing trajectories in the Bloch ball. In Section
3
we proceed with a SU
(2)
approach to the GLKS system which leads to the nonlinear dynamical system in spherical coordinates whose the radial part shows the dissipative regime in a straightforward way. In Section
4
, we carry out a detailed study of the existence of fixed points and explore how they can be exploited to control the evolution of a qubit. In Section
5
we give insights about possible generalizations. Some details of our computations are made explicit in Appendices
A
and
B
.

2
GKSL

In this section, we review the standard formulation of the GKSL equation for a single qubit. We employ the Bloch vector representation, which maps the density matrix to a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and derive the linear system of differential equations that governs its dynamics. The starting point is the GKSL equation in the Schrödinger picture for a density matrix (i.e., quantum state)
\rho
, with the standard sign convention:

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-\mathsf{i}\,[H,\rho]+\sum_{k}h_{k}\left[L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}\rho\right)\right]\\
&\equiv\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{un}}(\rho)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diss}}(\rho)\,,\end{split}

(1)

where the
L_{k}
’s together with the identity form an arbitrary basis of matrix operators and the coefficients
h_{k}
are non-negative constants, and we set
\hbar=1
. We also distinguish between the unitary Hamiltonian regime (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{un}}
) and the dissipative regime (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diss}}
) in the action of the Lindblad operator
\mathcal{L}
. In the present two-dimensional case, a natural basis would be the set compounded of the identity
\mathbbm{1}_{2}
, the three Hermitian Pauli matrices
\sigma_{i}
, and the three antihermitian matrices
\mathsf{i}\sigma_{i}
. Actually one shows that we just need the subset
\{\mathbbm{1}_{2},\sigma_{i}\,,\,i=1,2,3\}
.

Hence, writing the density (i.e. (nonnegative hermitian unit trace) operator as

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{2}+a_{1}\sigma_{1}+a_{2}\sigma_{2}+a_{3}\sigma_{3}\right)\,,

(2)

with the restriction
\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}}\leq 1
, the
L_{k}
as the Pauli matrices, and the Hamiltonian written in Pauli’s matrices basis,

\displaystyle H=e_{0}\mathbbm{1}_{2}+e_{1}\sigma_{1}+e_{2}\sigma_{2}+e_{3}\sigma_{3}\,,

(3)

the GKSL equation result in the set of 3 equations (see appendix),

\begin{split}\dot{a}_{1}&=2(e_{2}a_{3}-e_{3}a_{2})-2(h_{2}+h_{3})a_{1}\\
\dot{a}_{2}&=2(e_{3}a_{1}-e_{1}a_{3})-2(h_{1}+h_{3})a_{2}\\
\dot{a}_{3}&=2(e_{1}a_{2}-e_{2}a_{1})-2(h_{1}+h_{2})a_{3}\,.\end{split}

(4)

Thus, it can be rewritten in vector form characterizing a dynamical system

\vec{\dot{a}}=2\vec{e}\times\vec{a}-2D_{r}\cdot\vec{a}\,,

(5)

where
\vec{a}
is the usual Bloch vector represented by a column matrix
\vec{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})^{T}
,
\vec{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})^{T}
, while
\times
depicts the cross product. The matrix

D_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{2}+h_{3}&0&0\\
0&h_{1}+h_{3}&0\\
0&0&h_{1}+h_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(6)

contains the decay rates
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
, and
h_{3}
. Additionally, we write the cross product
\vec{e}\times\vec{a}
as the product between the skew-symmetric matrix

e_{\times}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e_{3}&e_{2}\\
e_{3}&0&-e_{1}\\
-e_{2}&e_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(7)

and vector
\vec{a}
.
We observe that the time derivative of the Bloch vector is equivalent to the matrix multiplication

\vec{\dot{a}}=T_{d}\cdot\vec{a}\,,

(8)

where

T_{d}=-2\begin{pmatrix}(h_{2}+h_{3})&e_{3}&-e_{2}\\
-e_{3}&(h_{1}+h_{3})&e_{1}\\
e_{2}&-e_{1}&(h_{1}+h_{2})\end{pmatrix}\,.

(9)

2.1
Solutions to the system of ode: isotropic case

In the case of time independent dissipative factors
h_{k}
, Equation (
8
) can be solved by the well-known ansatz
\vec{a}(t)=\vec{v}e^{\lambda t}
, where
\vec{v}
is a constant vector. Then, it becomes

\lambda\vec{v}e^{\lambda t}=T_{d}\cdot\vec{v}\,e^{\lambda t}\,,

(10)

and the exponentials cancel out, leaving us with an eigenvalue problem. To simplify the problem, we will consider the isotropic case
h_{1}=h_{2}=h_{3}=h
, which gives the following results for the eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{1}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})^{T}=\vec{e}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{1}=-4h\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{2}=\left(e_{1}e_{2}+\mathsf{i}e_{3}\|\vec{e}\|\,,\ -(e_{1}^{2}+e_{3}^{2}),\ e_{2}e_{3}-\mathsf{i}e_{1}\|\vec{e}\|\right)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{2}=-4h-2\mathsf{i}\|\vec{e}\|\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{3}=\left(-e_{1}e_{2}+\mathsf{i}e_{3}\|\vec{e}\|\,,\ (e_{1}^{2}+e_{3}^{2}),\ -e_{2}e_{3}-\mathsf{i}e_{1}\|\vec{e}\|\right)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{3}=-4h+2\mathsf{i}\|\vec{e}\|\,.

(13)

Then we find the solution:

\vec{a}(t)=C_{1}\vec{e}\ e^{-4ht}+C_{2}\vec{v}_{2}e^{-4ht-2\mathsf{i}\|\vec{e}\|t}+C_{3}\vec{v}_{3}e^{-4ht+2\mathsf{i}\|\vec{e}\|t}\,,

(14)

where the coefficients
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
are to be determined by an initial condition
\vec{a}(0)
.

2.3
Anisotropic case

Defining
h_{1}=h
, and
h_{2}=h_{3}=0
, and the Hamiltonian
H=\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}
, gives

T_{d}=-2\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega_{0}&0\\
-\omega_{0}&h&0\\
0&0&h\end{pmatrix}\,.

(17)

It is convenient to introduce the parameter
\beta=h/2\omega_{0}
and its pseudo-Lorentz companion
\widetilde{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}-1}}
.
The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
T_{d}
are then written as

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{4}

\displaystyle=(0,0,1)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{4}=-2h\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{5}

\displaystyle=(\beta-1/\widetilde{\gamma},\ 1,\ 0)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{5}=2\omega_{0}(-\beta-1/\widetilde{\gamma})\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{6}

\displaystyle=(\beta+1/\widetilde{\gamma},\ 1,\ 0)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{6}=2\omega_{0}(-\beta+1/\widetilde{\gamma})\,.

(20)

One obtains

\vec{a}(t)=C_{4}\vec{v}_{4}e^{-2ht}+C_{5}\vec{v}_{5}e^{-ht-2\frac{\omega_{0}}{\widetilde{\gamma}}t}+C_{6}\vec{v}_{6}e^{-ht+2\frac{\omega_{0}}{\widetilde{\gamma}}t}\,.

(21)

Supposing an initial condition
\vec{a}(0)=(1,\ 0,\ 0)^{T}
, we get the solution

\vec{a}(t)=\left(e^{-ht}\left[\cosh\left(2\frac{\omega_{0}}{\widetilde{\gamma}}t\right)+\widetilde{\gamma}\beta\sinh\left(2\frac{\omega_{0}}{\widetilde{\gamma}}t\right)\right],\ \widetilde{\gamma}e^{-ht}\sinh\left(2\frac{\omega_{0}}{\widetilde{\gamma}}t\right),\ 0\right)^{T}\,.

(22)

Assuming different values for
\beta
, the system enters different dissipative regimes, such as oscillatory
\beta<1
, damped
\beta>1
, and critical
\beta=1
. These differences can be visualized in Figure

.

\beta<1
, where the qubit behaves like an oscillatory system with damping. By introducing the Lorentzian companion

2.4
One direction

Here we explore the behavior of the system when both the Hamiltonian and decay rate are related to
\sigma_{z}
. So,
H=\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}
,
h_{1}=h_{2}=0
, while
h_{3}=h
, and

T_{d}=-2\begin{pmatrix}h&\omega_{0}&0\\
-\omega_{0}&h&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(26)

Therefore, the action of the matrix
T_{d}
over the Bloch vector
\vec{a}
only affects the
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
directions, leaving the
\sigma_{z}
unchanged. Along these lines, a state that is initially
\vec{a}(0)=(0,0,1)^{T}
will not change over time, which can be represented as a fixed point of the GKSL equation.

On the other hand, a state that has
\sigma_{x}
or
\sigma_{y}
components will rotate and decay, for example the state
\vec{a}(0)=(1,0,1)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
will evolve according to

\vec{a}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{-2ht}\cos 2\omega_{0}t\ ,\ e^{-2ht}\sin 2\omega_{0}t\ ,\ 1\right)^{T}\,,

(27)

and it can be visualized in Fig.
6
, while the
\sigma_{z}
component remains constant.

Figure 6:
Trajectory of the state given by Eq. (
27
) where
\omega_{0}=5
and
h=1
. The initial condition is
\vec{a}(0)=(1/\sqrt{2})(1,0,1)^{T}
. In this example, the
\sigma_{z}
direction does not change over time.

This example shows that a state with components only along the
\sigma_{z}
direction is a fixed point of the evolution, and it can be seen in the solution before setting the initial condition

\vec{a}(t)=\left(-\mathsf{i}c_{2}e^{-2t(h+i\omega_{0})}+\mathsf{i}c_{3}e^{-2t(h-i\omega_{0})}\ ,\ c_{2}e^{-2t(h+i\omega_{0})}+c_{3}e^{-2t(h-i\omega_{0})}\ ,\ c_{1}\right)^{T}\,,

(28)

and setting
c_{2}=c_{3}=0.

More generally, each point in the z-axis acts as an attractor for the dynamics in xy-plane. In other words, any initial state will decohere in the xy-plane while preserving its z-component, eventually converging to a final state on the z-axis. This line of fixed points represents the set of stable equilibrium states for the qubit under this specific Hamiltonian and dissipative channel.
This phenomenon illustrates a strategy for enhancing qubit stability: by engineering the dominant environmental interaction to create a (almost) decoherence-free subspace, the computational basis states (i.e,
|\uparrow\rangle
and
|\downarrow\rangle
as eigenstates of
\sigma_{z}
) become robust states, that act like a pointer state.

2.5
Exceptional points: discussion

In a broader context, the exceptional points (EPs)
[
11
,
12
]
are non-Hermitian degeneracies at which both eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors coalesce, leading to a breakdown of the conventional Hermitian spectral theory. In quantum systems, EPs emerge when dissipation or gain introduces non-conservativity, rendering the effective Hamiltonian non-Hermitian. This results in a characteristic spectral topology, where eigenvalues exhibit square-root branch point behavior. For a single qubit subject to engineered loss, such as in superconducting circuits or optical platforms, the Hamiltonian can be tailored to realize an EP by balancing coherent coupling and decay rates. In this regime, the qubit exhibits a non-trivial evolution marked by spontaneous symmetry breaking, mode non-orthogonality, and enhanced sensitivity to perturbations. Importantly, recent experimental realizations have demonstrated quantum state tomography across EPs
[
13
]
, revealing distinctive features such as the coalescence of decay rates at the EP, the transition between underdamped and overdamped dynamics, and signatures of PT-symmetry breaking in the relaxation pathways of the qubit.

In the anisotropic single-qubit model presented in this section, the effective Bloch evolution is generated by the non-Hermitian matrix
T_{d}
, Eq. (
17
). The eigenvalues of
T_{d}
determine three dynamical regimes (sub-critical, critical, and super-critical), analogous to a damped harmonic oscillator. At the critical point (
\beta=1
), two eigenvalues and eigenvectors coalesce, indicating a Liouvillian exceptional point. In another words, a non-Hermitian degeneracy occurs in the Liouvillian superoperator of the open quantum system. The dynamics near this point are qualitatively distinct, with the fastest decay consistent with known non-Hermitian degeneracies.

3
SU
(2)
symmetry content of GLKS dynamics

In this section we adopt an approach to the GLKS system based on the underlying SU
(2)
symmetry and related tools. Although we (artificially) leave linearity of the system, we unveil a useful separation between the angular regime, which involves the whole Lindblad operator
\mathcal{L}
, and the radial regime rule, which is explicitly ruled by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diss}}
only. More precisely, the SU
(2)
symmetry approach, actually the left coset
\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, decouples the dynamics of the system. It provides a link between the change in the state’s purity, the decay rates, and the evolution of its orientation and on the Bloch sphere as a nonlinear dynamical system.

3.1
SU
(2)
content of density matrix

An arbitrary density operator in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
has the following spectral decomposition

\rho=p_{1}\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{u}_{1}}+p_{2}\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{u}_{2}}\,,\quad p_{1}+p_{2}=1\,,

(29)

where the
p_{i}\in[0,1]
have a classical probability meaning and the
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}=|\mathbf{u}_{i}\rangle\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}|
are orthogonal projector (pure states) on eigendirections
\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
We suppose that there is no degeneracy and
p_{1}>p_{2}>0
(generic situation). By introducing the element of
U(\theta,\phi)\in\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)
which diagonalizes
\rho
, the later can be written as

\rho\equiv\rho_{r,\theta,\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{2}+rU(\theta,\phi)\sigma_{3}U^{{\dagger}}(\theta,\phi)\right)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{2}+r\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)\,,

(30)

with

U(\theta,\phi):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\theta\in[0,\pi]\,,\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi)\,,

(31)

and
r=2p_{1}-1\in[0,1]
is the normalized excess of
p_{1}
above the uniform baseline.
The matrix
\sigma_{\theta,\phi}
in (
30
) is a traceless involution (involution means its square is the identity) in SU
(2)
:

\sigma_{\theta,\phi}=U(\theta,\phi)\sigma_{3}U^{{\dagger}}(\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\,e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\sin\theta\,e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\sigma_{\theta,\phi}=\sigma^{{\dagger}}_{\theta,\phi}\,,\quad\sigma^{2}_{\theta,\phi}=\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,.

(32)

We check that
(\theta,\phi)\mapsto U(\theta,\phi)
is a representation of the half-circle,

U(\theta,\phi)U(\theta^{\prime},\phi)=U(\theta+\theta^{\prime},\phi)\,,

(33)

This property is consistent with the fact that
U(\theta,\phi)
is an element of the left coset
\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)
, since any right U
(1)
factor
\begin{pmatrix}e^{\mathsf{i}\omega}&0\\
0&e^{-\mathsf{i}\omega}\end{pmatrix}
of
U(\theta,\phi)
in (
30
) is transparent due to the fact it commutes with
\sigma_{3}
.
Its column vectors

\mathbf{u}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbf{u}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(34)

which form an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, are precisely the normalised eigenvectors of
\rho
, consistently with the notation in (
29
). Moreover, they can be viewed as spin one-half coherent states
|\theta,\phi\rangle
, defined in terms of spherical coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
as the quantum counterpart of the classical state
\hat{\mathbf{n}}(\theta,\phi)
in the (Bloch) sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by

\begin{split}&\mathbf{u}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}\end{pmatrix}\equiv|\theta,\phi\rangle=\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|\uparrow\,\rangle+e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|\downarrow\,\rangle\right)\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=U(\theta,\phi)|\uparrow\,\rangle\equiv D^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\xi^{-1}_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right)|\uparrow\,\rangle\,.\end{split}

(35)

Here,
\xi_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
corresponds, through the homomorphism SO
(3)

\mapsto
SU
(2)
, to the specific rotation
\mathcal{R}_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
, in the Bloch sphere, which maps the unit vector pointing to the north pole,
\hat{\mbox{{{k}}}}=(0,0,1)
, to
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
. The operator
D^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\xi^{-1}_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right)
represents the element
\xi^{-1}_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
of SU
(2)
in its complex two-dimensional unitary irreducible representation. Thus, the spectral decomposition (
29
)of
\rho_{r,\theta,\phi}
is expressed in terms of the two orthogonal spin CS:

\rho_{r,\theta,\phi}=\frac{1+r}{2}|\theta,\phi\rangle\langle\theta,\phi|+\frac{1-r}{2}|\theta+\pi,\phi\rangle\langle\theta+\pi,\phi|\,.

(36)

We remind here some fundamental properties of these CS:

\langle\theta,\phi|\theta,\phi\rangle=1\,,\quad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{4\pi}\,|\theta,\phi\rangle\langle\theta,\phi\rangle=\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}=\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi\,.

(37)

Note the extreme cases,
r=1
(pure state
\equiv
spin coherent state), and
r=0
(totally random state):

\rho_{1,\theta,\phi}=\left|\theta,\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\theta,\phi\right|\,,\quad\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,.

(38)

3.2
GLKS system in terms of parameters
(r,\theta,\phi)

3.2.1
Radial dissipation

We now formulate the GLKS system (
1
) in functions of the Bloch ball parameters
(r,\theta,\phi)
.
First, by using the expression (
30
), we have for the time derivative of
\rho_{r,\theta,\phi}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{r,\theta,\phi}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\dot{r}}{2}\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}+\frac{r}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,.

(39)

Now, from (
32
) and the identities
UU^{{\dagger}}=\mathbbm{1}_{2}=U^{{\dagger}}U\Rightarrow\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}}=-U\dot{U}^{{\dagger}}
, we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\dot{U}\sigma_{3}U^{{\dagger}}+U\sigma_{3}\dot{U}^{{\dagger}}=\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]\,.

(40)

Thus the Lindblad equation takes the form

\frac{\dot{r}}{2}\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}+\frac{r}{2}\,\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]=-\mathsf{i}\frac{r}{2}\,\left[H,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]+\frac{r}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}\left(\sigma_{k}\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\,\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)\,.

(41)

By using
\sigma^{2}_{\theta,\phi}=\mathbbm{1}_{2}
,

\frac{\dot{r}}{r}\mathbbm{1}_{2}=\left[\sigma_{\theta,\phi},\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}}+\mathsf{i}H\right]\sigma_{\theta,\phi}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}\left[\left(\sigma_{k}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)^{2}-\mathbbm{1}_{2}\right]\,.

(42)

Taking the trace of this equation and using
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left[\sigma_{\theta,\phi},A\right]\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma_{\theta,\phi}A\sigma_{\theta,\phi}-A\right)=0
for all
A\in\mathrm{M}(2,\mathbb{R})
, we get the equation:

\begin{split}\frac{\dot{r}}{r}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma_{k}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)^{2}-2\right)\right)\\
&=-2\left[h_{1}(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\phi)+h_{2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\phi)+h_{3}\sin^{2}\theta\right]\,.\end{split}

(43)

We note that there is no explicit dependence of the Hamiltonian. The action of the latter is implicit only through the time dependence of the angles
\theta
and
\phi
.
Since the decay rates
h_{k}
are non-negative, the entire expression on the right-hand side is always less than or equal to zero.
Henceforth, Eq. (
43
) is a fundamental result that directly connects the change in the state’s purity to the decay rates. The parameter
r
is a measure of the state’s purity, where
r=1
is a pure state and
r=0
is a maximally mixed state. This proves that
\dot{r}\leq 0
, meaning the purity of the state can only decrease or remain constant over time. The fact that
\dot{r}\leq 0
confirms the principle of dissipation, as the system loses coherence to its environment.
This interpretation is confirmed with the increase of the von Neumann entropy:

S_{\rho}:=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\,\ln\rho)=-\frac{1+r}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{2}-\frac{1-r}{2}\ln\frac{1-r}{2}\,,\quad\dot{S_{\rho}}=-\frac{\dot{r}}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}\geq 0\,.

(44)

3.2.2
Angular dynamical system

Let us now decouple the angular variables motions from the radial one, starting from (
42
) and using (
43
). By introducing the two traceless matrix expressions:

\displaystyle M(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}h_{k}\left[\left(\sigma_{k}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sigma_{k}\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right)^{2}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{2}\right]\,,\quad\mathrm{Tr}\,M(\theta,\phi)=0\,,

(45)

\displaystyle N(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{i}\left[H,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}+M(\theta,\phi)\,,

(46)

we then obtain the matrix dynamical system for angular variables
(\theta,\phi)
:

\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]\sigma_{\theta,\phi}=N(\theta,\phi)\,.

(47)

We now make explicit
\dot{U}
and
\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}}\equiv\dot{X}
in terms of
\dot{\phi}
and
\dot{\theta}
from the matrix (
31
). Here
X
is the antihermitian “logarithm” of
U
:

U=e^{X}\,,\quad X\in\mathfrak{su}(2)\,,\quad\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}}=\dot{X}\,.

(48)

\dot{U}=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{\dot{\theta}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&-e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\
e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,\mathrm{n}(\phi)+\frac{\dot{\theta}}{2}\,U(\theta+\pi,\phi)\,,

(49)

where we have introduced the Hermitian involution

\mathrm{n}(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathrm{n}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)=\mathrm{n}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)\,,\quad\mathrm{n}^{2}(\phi)=\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,.

(50)

Hence we get for (
49
):

\begin{split}\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}}&=\dot{X}=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{\dot{\theta}}{2}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&0\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)+\frac{\dot{\theta}}{2}\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)\,,\end{split}

(51)

where we have introduced the traceless involution matrix

\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathrm{F}^{2}(\theta,\phi)=\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,,\quad\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)=-1\,,

(52)

and the traceless anti-involution Hermitian matrix

\mathrm{s}(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathrm{s}^{2}=-\mathbbm{1}_{2}\,,\quad\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)=1\,.

(53)

Note the relations preserving the traceless property:

\mathrm{s}(\phi)\,\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}-\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{-\mathsf{i}\phi}\\
-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathsf{i}\phi}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathrm{F}(\theta+\pi,\phi)=-\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)\,.

(54)

We also note that the traceless features on both sides of (
51
) is respected.

We now calculate the commutator
\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]
on the l.h.s. of (
49
). From the relations

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}

\displaystyle=U(\pi-\theta,\phi)\,,\quad\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\,\mathrm{F}(\theta,\phi)=U^{{\dagger}}(\pi-\theta,\phi)\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s}(\phi)\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}=-\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)\Leftrightarrow\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\,\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}=-\mathrm{s}(\phi)=-\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}\,\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\,.

(56)

we obtain

\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\theta\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)+\dot{\theta}\,\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}\,.

(57)

and

\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]\sigma_{\theta,\phi}=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\theta\,\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}+\dot{\theta}\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)\,.

(58)

The above set of properties allow to obtain the separate equations for the angular variables.
Starting from (
47
) we write

\left[\dot{U}U^{{\dagger}},\sigma_{\theta,\phi}\right]\sigma_{\theta,\phi}=\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\theta\,\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}+\dot{\theta}\,\mathrm{s}(\phi)=N(\theta,\phi)\,,

then multiplying each member of this equation by
\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}
and tracing the result yield

\begin{split}&2\mathsf{i}\dot{\phi}\,\sin\theta=\mathrm{Tr}\left[N(\theta,\phi)\,\sigma_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2},\phi}\right]\\
&=2\mathsf{i}\left[\sin\theta\,(h_{00}-h_{11})-2\cos\theta\,(\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}\,\cos\phi-\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}\,\sin\phi)+(h_{2}-h_{1})\sin\theta\,\sin 2\phi\right]\,,\end{split}

(59)

where the
h_{ij}
are the matrix elements of
H
,
H=\begin{pmatrix}h_{00}&h_{01}\\
\overline{h_{01}}&h_{11}\end{pmatrix}
.
After simplification, for
\theta\neq 0,\pi
,

\begin{split}\dot{\phi}=h_{00}-h_{11}-2\cot\theta\,(\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}\,\cos\phi-\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}\,\sin\phi)+(h_{2}-h_{1})\,\sin 2\phi\,.\end{split}

(60)

Similarly, multiplying (
51
) by
\mathrm{s}(\phi)
, tracing the result and dividing by 2 yield:

\dot{\theta}=-2(\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}\,\sin\phi+\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}\,\cos\phi)+\sin 2\theta(h_{1}\cos^{2}\phi+h_{2}\sin^{2}\phi-h_{3})\,.

(61)

These Ricatti-like equations can be interpreted as the equations of motion for the orientation of the Bloch vector on the sphere. They demonstrate how both the Hamiltonian and the decay rates contribute to the rotation and evolution of the quantum state’s direction. The Hamiltonian terms
h_{ij}
drive the coherent precession, while the decay rates
h_{k}
induce a drift, or move towards a new non-rotational final position. The final state of the system is a balance between these two competing effects.

3.2.3
The full dynamical system

Collecting the evolution equations for angular and radial variables we finally obtain the system:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=h_{00}-h_{11}-2\cot\theta\,(\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}\,\cos\phi-\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}\,\sin\phi)+(h_{2}-h_{1})\,\sin 2\phi\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}

\displaystyle=-2(\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}\,\sin\phi+\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}\,\cos\phi)+\sin 2\theta(h_{1}\cos^{2}\phi+h_{2}\sin^{2}\phi-h_{3})\,,

(63)

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{r}}{r}

\displaystyle=-2\left[h_{1}(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\phi)+h_{2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\phi)+h_{3}\sin^{2}\theta\right]\,.

(64)

As a simple example, for the particular cases where
H
is diagonal and time-independent, and time independent
h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}
with

, we obtain the solution

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}+\left(h_{00}-h_{11}\right)(t-t_{0})\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\tan\theta(t)

\displaystyle=\tan\theta_{0}\,e^{-2(h_{3}-h_{1})t}\,,

(66)

\displaystyle r(t)

\displaystyle=r_{0}\,e^{-4h_{1}t}\,\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\tan^{2}\!\theta_{0}\,e^{-4(h_{3}-h_{1})t}}{1+\tan^{2}\!\theta_{0}}}\,.

(67)

Note the invariant

The solution (

,

,

,

4
Fixed points: Discussion

The asymptotic behavior of an open quantum system is characterized by its set of stationary or fixed points. As already presented in Subsec.
2.4
, the z-axis of the Bloch ball represent a set of fixed points. This corresponds to
h_{1}=h_{2}=0
,
\theta_{0}=0
, and
e_{1}=e_{2}=0
( in Eq. (
3
) ), or
\mathrm{Re}\,h_{01}=\mathrm{Im}\,h_{01}=0
( in Eq. (
63
) ). The other two axes (x and y) of the Bloch ball also are a set of fixed points, and this situation corresponds to the values of parameters
e_{2}=e_{3}=h_{2}=h_{3}=0
for the x-axis (
\phi_{0}=0
,
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
) and
e_{1}=e_{3}=h_{1}=h_{3}=0
for the y-axis (
\phi_{0}=\pi/2
,
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
). This can be verified by substituting those values in the equations (
64
), (
63
), and (
62
).

The fixed points of the system, denoted by
\vec{a}_{*}
, are identified by solving the steady-state condition
\vec{\dot{a}}=0
for the governing dynamics in Eq. (
5
). Once the fixed points are determined, their stability is ascertained through linear stability analysis. This procedure involves analyzing the evolution of an infinitesimal perturbation,
\vec{\delta}
, from a fixed point, such that
\vec{a}(t)=\vec{a}_{*}+\vec{\delta}(t)
. Substituting this into the equations for the system and retaining only first-order terms in
\vec{\delta}
yield a set of linear differential equations describing the dynamics of the perturbation. The stability of the fixed point is then determined by the eigenvalues of the corresponding Jacobian matrix.

To illustrate this methodology, we apply it to the system presented in Subsec.
2.4
, whose dynamics are given by Eq. (
4
). The linearization around a fixed point
\vec{a}_{*}
results in a system for the perturbation
\vec{\delta}
. By the definition of a fixed point, the static terms vanish, yielding the following linear system:

\dot{\vec{\delta}}=-2\begin{pmatrix}h&\omega_{0}&0\\
-\omega_{0}&h&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{\delta}\ .

(68)

The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix are readily found to be
\lambda_{1}=-2h-2i\omega_{0}
,
\lambda_{2}=-2h+2i\omega_{0}
, and
\lambda_{3}=0
.

Because
h>0
, the real parts of the complex conjugate eigenvalues
\lambda_{1,2}
are negative. This implies that the dynamics are asymptotically stable in the
a_{1}a_{2}
-plane. Any perturbation in this plane will decay, returning the system to the fixed point. Conversely, the null eigenvalue,
\lambda_{3}=0
, indicates neutral stability along the
a_{3}
direction. This signifies the existence of a continuum of equilibrium points. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the system possesses a line of fixed points, where each point on this line is an attractor for the dynamics within its respective
a_{1}a_{2}
-plane (i.e., for a constant
a_{3}
). Since the system under consideration is linear, this stability analysis is exact and provides a complete characterization of the equilibrium structure.

It is known
[
14
]
that the existence of multiple steady states is fundamentally linked to the presence of symmetries or a decomposable structure in the dynamics of the system. A system is considered decomposable if its Hilbert space can be broken down into two or more orthogonal subspaces that are invariant under the evolution. If such subspaces exist, the system cannot have a single globally attractive fixed point.

The study of fixed points and steady states is related to reservoir engineering
[
15
]
. By carefully designing a qubit’s Hamiltonian and its dissipative interactions with an environment, one can control the final steady state the system evolves towards. This is a powerful tool for both state preparation, where a qubit is forced into a desired pure or mixed state, and for stabilization. In the latter case, the dissipative process actively protects a specific quantum state from noise by providing a form of continuous error correction, always pulling the state back towards the stable fixed point. Then, if the dynamics allow for multiple stable fixed points, this collection of states can be used as a robust memory. Each distinct fixed point can encode a piece of information, and the stability of these points makes the memory fault-tolerant or self-correcting, as the system’s natural evolution will resist perturbations that try to alter the stored information.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have explored two complementary approaches to the GKSL equation for an open qubit. The first approach exploits the linearity of the equation, which allows one to construct standard solutions straightforwardly; we illustrated this method with examples of trajectories inside the Bloch ball. The second approach relies on the underlying SU
(2)
symmetry of the Bloch ball and leads to a nonlinear dynamical system. Although more intricate, this formulation highlights in a natural way the distinction between the angular dynamics of the system and the radial component associated with dissipation. The main appeal of this second perspective lies in its potential for generalisation to the GKSL equation for open qudits, i.e., higher-dimensional generalisations of qubits.

The linear analysis, in particular, provided clear physical intuition, as illustrated by the case where dissipation affects only the xy-plane components, revealing a line of stable fixed points along the z-axis that acts as an attractor for the quantum state. This lines (the three axis) of stable fixed points can be employed to protect quantum information by engineering the right qubit-environment interactions.
