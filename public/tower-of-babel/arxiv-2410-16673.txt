Efficient Antibody Structure Refinement Using Energy-Guided SE(3) Flow Matching Thanks: This work was accepted as a regular paper by BIBM 2024.
Jiying Zhang, Zijing Liu *, Shengyuan Bai, He Cao, Yu Li *, Lei Zhang

Abstract
Antibodies are proteins produced by the immune system that recognize and bind to specific antigens, and their 3D structures are crucial for understanding their binding mechanism and designing therapeutic interventions. The specificity of antibody-antigen binding predominantly depends on the complementarity-determining regions (CDR) within antibodies. Despite recent advancements in antibody structure prediction, the quality of predicted CDRs remains suboptimal. In this paper, we develop a novel antibody structure refinement method termed FlowAB based on energy-guided flow matching. FlowAB adopts the powerful deep generative method SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE(3)} flow matching and simultaneously incorporates important physical prior knowledge into the flow model to guide the generation process. The extensive experiments demonstrate that FlowAB can significantly improve the antibody CDR structures. It achieves new state-of-the-art performance on the antibody structure prediction task when used in conjunction with an appropriate prior model while incurring only marginal computational overhead. This advantage makes FlowAB a practical tool in antibody engineering.

Despite their effectiveness in refining specific protein structures with the help of comprehensive psychical information, these methods require significant computational resources and extensive conformation sampling.
Recently, deep learning has been applied to protein structure refinement with notable efficiency

Additionally, these methods cannot incorporate essential physical prior information, such as the inter-atomic potentials. This limits their ability to generate reasonable antibody structures that adhere to the physical principles. Given these challenges, a natural question arises:
How can we design a deep learning method that incorporates physical prior information to efficiently optimize existing antibody structures

Flow model is a popular generative model known for its impressive sampling efficiency
[
16
,
17
]
and has been successfully applied in various domains
[
18
,
19
]
.
It assumes a deterministic continuous-time generative process and fits an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that transforms the source density into the target density. It is a promising method to refine the antibody structure by transforming the initial antibody structure to the optimized one.
In this paper, we design a new energy-guided flow model

The main contributions of our work are summarized as follows:

•

Energy guidance is integrated in

•

The extensive experiments on antibody structure prediction and refinement demonstrate the superior performance of our model. With an appropriate prior and minimal additional computation, our method achieves SOTA performance on the SAbDab dataset.

Recently, a new paradigm of generative models called flow matching has been introduced

Recent works

Meanwhile, Riemannian flow matching is proposed to handle the data on manifolds

,
4

[
27
]
.
The unique ability of flow matching to transform between two distributions presents a significant opportunity for refining existing protein structures. This is particularly relevant for antibodies, which feature highly diverse CDRs. Given this potential, there is a compelling case for developing a flow-matching-based model specifically tailored for optimizing antibody structures.

II-B

Antibody structure prediction with deep learning

Deep learning has revolutionized protein structure prediction, including the specialized area of antibody structures. For instance,
DeepAb
[
28
]
utilizes interresidue geometric constraints to generate a comprehensive FV (variable fragment) structure, which is subsequently refined using Rosetta. ABlooper
[
9
]
employs an end-to-end approach to predict CDR loop structures, supplemented by post-prediction refinement. DiffAB
[
29

utilizes the
\mathrm{SE(3)}

IgFold
[
8
]
directly predicts the coordinates of the backbone atom by an invariant point attention network and a pre-trained language model trained on antibody sequences.
Despite these advancements, existing methods primarily focus on
de

novo
structure prediction rather than refining suboptimal structures.
Further, compared with the diffusion-based model that requires extensive computational resources and demands thousands of inference steps, our
FlowAB
only needs very few sampling iterations, thereby largely increasing the efficiency of antibody structure prediction.

III-C

Flow matching on Riemannian manifolds

Flow matching (FM) is a cutting-edge simulation-free approach for training continuous normalizing flows (CNFs), a powerful class of deep generative models that integrate an ordinary differential equation (ODE) over a learned vector field.
Recent advances have extended FM to handle Riemannian manifolds
[
25
]
.
In particular, on a manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
, the CNF
\phi_{t}(\cdot):{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
is defined as an ODE associated with a time-dependent vector field
v(z,t):{\mathcal{M}}\times[0,1]\to{\mathcal{T}}_{z}{\mathcal{M}}
, where
{\mathcal{T}}_{z}{\mathcal{M}}
is the tangent space of the manifold at
z\in{\mathcal{M}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}(z_{0})=v_{t}(\phi_{t}(z_{0}),t);\quad\phi_{0}(z_{0})=z_{0}.

(1)

When
z_{0}
is sampled from a simple prior distribution
p_{0}
,
Equation 1
can induce a probability path
p_{t}
through the push-forward equation:
p_{t}=[p_{t}]*p_{0}
. The goal is to get the vector field
v
that can generate the target data distribution
p_{1}
. However, a closed-form solution for
v
is generally not available. Thus,
[
17
]
proposes to learn
v
by regressing the conditional vector field
u(\phi_{t}(z_{0}|z_{1})|z_{0},z_{1})=\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}(z_{0}|z_{1})
, where
\phi_{t}(z_{0}|z_{1}):=z_{t}
interpolates between
z_{0}\sim p_{0}
and
z_{1}\sim p_{1}
. On a simple manifold,
z_{t}
can be calculated through the geodesic path
z_{t}=\exp_{z_{0}}(t\log_{z_{0}}(z_{1}))
. Here,
\exp_{z_{0}}
and
\log_{z_{0}}
are the exponential and logarithmic maps at the point
z_{0}

[
25
]
. The induced vector filed is:
u_{t}(z_{t}|z_{1})=\log_{z_{t}}(z_{1})/(1-t)
. Assume that
t\sim{\mathcal{U}}([0,1])
and
|\cdot|_{g}
represent the norm induced by the Riemannian metric
g:{\mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. With the fact that learning the unconditional vector field
v
is equivalent to learning the conditional vector field
u
, one can use a neural network
{v}_{\theta}
to regress
u
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}={\mathbb{E}}_{p_{1}(z_{1}),p_{t}(z_{t}|z_{1})}\|{v}_{\theta}(z_{t},t)-u_{t}(z_{t}|z_{1})\|_{g}^{2}.

(2)

Once the vector field
v_{\theta}
is learned, samples can be generated by solving the ODE in
Equation 1
with
{v}_{\theta}
substituted for
v
.

FlowAB: An Energy-Guided Flow Matching for Antibody Refinement

IV-A

SE(3) flow matching for backbone generation

The
\mathrm{SE(3)}
flow matching, a Riemannian flow matching, is developed in
[
25
]
and
[
18
]
. Here, we use the
\mathrm{SE(3)}
flow matching framework for generating the backbone of the antibody. The
\mathrm{SE(3)}
can be decomposed into
\mathrm{SO(3)}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and then the vector field becomes
v_{\mathrm{SE(3)}}=(v_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}},v_{\mathrm{SO(3)}})
. These components are utilized for modeling the
\text{C}_{\alpha}
positions and amino acid (residue) orientations as follows.
This allows us to accurately capture both the spatial positions and the rotational alignments of the residues, crucial for correctly predicting the three-dimensional structure of the antibody.

Flow matching for
\text{C}_{\alpha}
position.

Given the initial atomic coordinates
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
from the antibody structure prior distribution
p_{0}
and the ground truth coordinates
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\sim p_{1}
, the probability path defined by interpolating linearly between
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
is
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\mathbf{x}}_{0}+t{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
. Then
the probability path is associated with the vector field:

\displaystyle u_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{x}}_{1})=\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{1-t}={\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{0}.

(3)

Thus, the training objective is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbb{R}}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}),p_{t}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{1})}\|v_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},t,{\mathcal{C}})-u_{t}({\mathbf{x}}|{\mathbf{x}}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}.

(4)

\mathrm{SO(3)}
flow matching for residue orientations.

Given orientation
{\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\in\mathrm{SO(3)}
from a given initial sub-optimal antibody structure and native antibody structure, the probability path is defined as the geodesic interpolant form
{\mathbf{r}}_{t}=\exp_{{\mathbf{r}}_{0}}(t\log_{{\mathbf{r}}_{0}}({\mathbf{r}}_{1}))

[
25
]

and the corresponding vector field is:

\displaystyle u_{t}({\mathbf{r}}|{\mathbf{r}}_{1})=\frac{\log_{{\mathbf{r}}_{t}}{{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}}{1-t}.

(5)

Thus, the training objective is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}({\mathbf{r}}_{1}),p_{t}({\mathbf{r}}_{t}|{\mathbf{r}}_{1})}\|v_{\theta}({\mathbf{r}}_{t},t,{\mathcal{C}})-u_{t}({\mathbf{r}}|{\mathbf{r}}_{1})\|_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}^{2}.

(6)

In this work, we do not employ the optimal transport plan
\pi
calculated from the Monge and Kantorivich problem
\text{OT}(p_{0},p_{1})
as the joint distribution to sample
p_{0}({\mathbf{r}}_{0}),p_{1}({\mathbf{r}}_{1})
for training
[
18
]
, because we assume that the residue
i
in initial antibody structure given by a SOTA method has a good alignment with the residue
i
in the target antibody structure.
The rationale for this decision is based on the assumption that the residue
i
in the initial antibody structure, provided by a SOTA method, already exhibits a good alignment with the corresponding residue in the target antibody structure. This allows us to bypass computationally intensive optimal transport calculations, while maintaining alignment accuracy between the corresponding residues in the initial and target structures.

IV-B

Energy-guided flow matching

For antibody structure refinement, initial structures produced by SOTA CDR predictors present a challenge due to the minor differences between them and native structures. This subtlety complicates the task of learning an effective vector field for structural adjustments.
Existing flow models, however, do not consider utilizing crucial physical prior information, such as the interatomic potential, to guide their sampling process. This omission limits their ability to robustly optimize the antibody structure when the vector field alone provides insufficient guidance.

To address these challenges, we introduce a novel energy-guided flow model designed to generate high-fidelity antibody conformations that can better match the molecular physical property. We propose incorporating human preferences to modulate the desired distribution and generate samples through ODE sampling with additional guidance. Inspired by the energy-guided technique in diffusion models
[
31
,
32
]
, we make the intermediate state
\mathcal{R}_{t}
sample from the desired distribution:

\displaystyle q_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})=p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\frac{e^{-\beta{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}}{Z},

(7)

where
Z:=\int p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\exp(-\beta{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R}_{t}))d\mathcal{R}_{t}
is the intractable normalizing constant,
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
is the intermediate energy function from
\mathrm{SE(3)}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
\beta\geq 0
represents the inverse temperature, which controls the energy strength. The high-density region of the probability distribution
q_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
is approximately the intersection of the high-density regions of
p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
and
e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}
.

Next, we need to design a new flow to get the probability path that complies with the
Equation 7
.
Inspired by
[
33
]
, we connect flow matching to diffusion models and adopt the probability flow ODE
[
22
]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_{t}=\tilde{v}(\mathcal{R}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t=\left[f(\mathcal{R}_{t},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(8)

where
f
and
g
are the drift and diffusion coefficients, respectively. By sampling from the prior distribution
p_{0}
and subsequently integrating the learned vector field
\tilde{v}
that is trained through minimizing the objective in
Equation 2
, the ODE in
Equation 8
can get samples from
p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. This is the probability flow ODE that shares the same marginal probability as the stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the diffusion
model
[
22
]
. This vector filed contains score
\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, to which the energy guidance can
be applied.
In particular, modifying
Equation 8
to involve the energy function of
Equation 7
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[f(\mathcal{R}_{t},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla\log(p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})e^{-\beta{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})})\right]\mathrm{d}t

(9)

\displaystyle=\left[f(\mathcal{R}_{t},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\left(\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})-\nabla\beta{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\underbrace{\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{SE(3)}}(\mathcal{R}_{t},t)}_{\text{unconditional vector field}}+\tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\underbrace{~\nabla\beta{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}_{\text{guidance term}}\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t,

(10)

where
g(t)
can be used as a scheduler that controls the
magnitude of the guidance.
We can interpret
Equation 10
as doing unconditional generation by following
\hat{{v}}_{\mathrm{SE(3)}}(\mathcal{R},t)
while
\nabla\beta{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
guides the residues by giving the force to the velocity (cf. Appendix
A-B
).
Following
\mathrm{SE(3)}
flow matching,
Equation 10
becomes the following:

\displaystyle\text{C}_{\alpha}~\text{position: }\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\tilde{v}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)+\tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t,

(11)

\displaystyle\text{Orientation: }\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}({\mathbf{r}}_{t},t)+\tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\mathbf{r}}_{t}}{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t.

(12)

In practice, we still utilize the training objectives presented in
Equation 4
and
Equation 6
to learn the unconditional vector fields
\tilde{v}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)
and
\tilde{v}_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}({\mathbf{r}}_{t},t)
. Once the vector field is learned, there is no need for further model retraining. Instead, we can directly incorporate the energy-guided term during the sampling process. This approach becomes particularly valuable when an unconditional generative flow model is readily available, and further training is prohibitively expensive. In practice, the
\mathrm{d}t
will be changed into
-\mathrm{d}t
due to the time
t
from
0
to
1
in sampling. Further explanations can be found in Appendix

Interatomic potential as energy

Illustration of inter-atomic potentials:

Fig. 5:

The model architecture of

. The model takes in the residue embedding

, pairwise embedding

\mathbb{R}^{3}

From the antibody refinement perspective, the
\mathrm{SE(3)}
flow on each residue depends only on its vector field, with
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
flow refining the global position and
\mathrm{SO(3)}
flow adjusting the local frame. However, these flows focus on individual residues and do not account for interactions with adjacent residues, potentially compromising the protein’s physical properties in 3D space. Therefore, an energy-guided term is crucial for constraining the flow to produce physically plausible models. Motivated by molecular dynamics (MD), we consider bond, angle, and torsion potentials as physics-based preference functions.

Bond potential

The bond potential (
Figure 4

b
) describes an oscillation about an equilibrium bond length
d_{0}
with bond constant
k_{b}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(d)=k_{b}(d_{ij}-d_{0})^{2},

(13)

where
d
is the distance between the atoms,

d_{0}
is the equilibrium bond distance.

Angle potential

This potential (
Figure 4

c
) describes oscillation about an equilibrium angle
\vartheta_{0}
with force constant
k_{\vartheta}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\vartheta)=k_{\vartheta}(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0})^{2}.

(14)

When the lengths of the two bonds that form the angle are known, the angle potential can be implemented by a three-bond potential among the three atom pairs. For example, the angle potential on
\vartheta
can be equivalent to bond potential in
\text{C}-\text{C}_{\alpha}
,
\text{C}-\text{N}
and virtual bond
\text{C}_{\alpha}-\text{N}
.

Improper torsion potential

This potential is usually used to enforce planarity. It can be defined as a harmonic function:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\varphi)=k_{\varphi}(\varphi-\varphi_{0})^{2},

(15)

where
\varphi
is the dihedral angle between two planes. As shown in
a
) of
Figure 4
, the dihedral angle
\varphi
is the angle between planes
[\text{C}_{\alpha}^{i},\text{C}^{i},\text{N}^{i+1}]
and
[\text{C}^{i},\text{N}^{i+1},\text{C}_{\alpha}^{i+1}]
.

A bond potential on the peptide bond

] and [

and

]

•

An improper torsion on the four atoms

with

to make them to be located on the amide plane
[
34
]
.

In practice, since the length of bond
\text{C}^{i}_{\alpha}-\text{C}^{i}
and bond
\text{N}^{i+1}-\text{C}_{\alpha}^{i+1}
is known, these above potentials are equivalent to six bond potentials between those four atoms, as shown in

The energy terms introduced will provide the necessary dependency between the joint residues, ensuring more reasonable positioning in the refined structure. This will also enhance the robustness of

IV-D

Model architecture

Our main model architecture integrates the Invariant Point Attention (IPA)
[
30
]
for geometric feature extraction and Multiple Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) for feature embedding and vector field prediction
[
29
]
. MLPs generate embeddings for both individual amino acids and pairs of amino acids.
The MLP of a single amino acid creates an embedding vector
\mathbf{h}_{0}
for each amino acid, capturing critical data that include amino acid types, torsional angles, and 3D coordinates of heavy atoms. For pairwise embeddings, another MLP processes the Euclidean distances and dihedral angles between amino acids
i
and
j
, producing vectors
\mathbf{z}^{ij}_{0}
. These embeddings are transformed to hidden representations
\mathbf{h}_{l}
via the IPA modules
[

V-C

Structure prediction time analysis

We compare the structure prediction time with the baseline to demonstrate the impressive efficiency of our method. For our benchmark, all deep learning methods were executed on identical hardware (a 32-core CPU with a single RTX 3090 GPU), ensuring a direct comparison of their runtimes. In our experiment, we find that

VI
Conclusion and limitations

In this paper, we have proposed
FlowAB
, a novel flow-based generative model for the refinement of the antibody structure. By incorporating the flow matching training objective and integrating the energy function into the flow model,
FlowAB
facilitates the generation of antibody structures in a simple and efficient manner. However, one limitation of this method is that the energy terms used in
FlowAB
employ a relatively simple potential function. The design of a more comprehensive energy function could further enhance the quality of the generated antibody structures.
In terms of future work,
FlowAB
can be extended to support
de novo
antibody design by incorporating a discrete flow component for the generation of amino acid types. This would allow the model to not only refine existing antibody structures but also generate novel antibody sequences and structures from scratch.

C-E

Vector field parameterization

In FlowAB, we use the IPA from AF2 as the core module for feature extraction
[
42
]
. Since the prior structures usually have a high similarity with the target structures, we consider using the antibody complex feature to predict the relative transformation from the priors (i.e.
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{0}
as the standing point) to the target (i.e. ground truth
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
):

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{r}}_{0}\operatorname{MLP}_{1}(\mathbf{h}_{i},t)+{\mathbf{x}}_{0}

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}_{1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{r}}_{0}\operatorname{MLP}_{2}(\mathbf{h}_{i},t)

(68)

One can also use the
{\mathbf{r}}_{t}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
to be the standing point if the prior is significant distinct with the target, such as Gaussian prior or Harmonic prior.
Then, we parameterize the vector field by directly regressing the target
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
. Thus, the predicted vector fields
\hat{v}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
and
\hat{v}_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}
for sampling are:

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathbf{x}},t)

\displaystyle=f_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}(\mathbf{h}_{l},t)=\frac{\hat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{1-t}

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}({\mathbf{r}},t)

\displaystyle=f_{\mathrm{SO(3)}}(\mathbf{h}_{l},t)=\frac{\log_{{\mathbf{r}}_{t}}\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}_{1}}{1-t}

(70)

The losses for training is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbf{r}}}

\displaystyle=ConsineEmbeddingLoss({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1})

(71)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}

(72)

where
ConsineEmbeddingLoss
is the Consine Embedding Loss that works better than the L-2 loss. This loss can adopt the function
F.Consine_embedding_loss
implemented in PyTorch. We find it can reduce variance and numerical instability in our experiments. Additionally,
we weight the rotation loss with a coefficient
0.5

as compared to the translation loss that has a weight
1.0
.

\displaystyle={\sqrt{{\frac{1}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}((v_{ix}-w_{ix})^{2}+(v_{iy}-w_{iy})^{2}+(v_{iz}-w_{iz})^{2}}})
