Harnessing a single molecule for dual bioorthogonal regulation of RNA function and m6A methylation
Authors: Wei Xiong, Enyi Zhou, Qianqian Qi, Xingyu Liu, Kaisong Zhang, Siqi Huang, Ming Li, Chao Wang, Xiang Zhou, Tian Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a multifunctional RNA regulation strategy that enables RNA molecules to undergo both bioorthogonal ligation and cleavage reactions within the same system. Using guide RNA (gRNA) as an example, we demonstrate on-demand inactivation via click chemistry and subsequent reactivation by light exposure. Applied to CRISPR-mediated, site-specific RNA methylation, this technology enables continuous, multistep programmable control, overcoming the one-way limitation of traditional methods. Results show that gRNA can stably tolerate both azide and photodegradable groups, facilitating efficient targeting of M3M14–dCas9 and dCas13b–M3M14 methylation systems to introduce m6A at specific RNA sites. Small molecules can turn off methylation through in situ click chemistry, while 365-nm light exposure rapidly restores gRNA function, allowing precise control over RNA methylation. This strategy highlights the efficiency and flexibility of progressive bioorthogonal RNA modulation and paves the way for multisite, dynamic regulation in complex biological systems.

Introduction

RNA molecules perform a variety of essential functions within cells, and their chemical modifications play a crucial role in regulating these functions [1–3]. As biology and chemistry converge, scientists have come to recognize that precise chemical modifications can dynamically control RNA functions, offering deeper insights into their roles in gene expression, cellular signaling, and disease development [4–7]. However, current RNA regulation technologies are mostly limited to one-way modulation, failing to meet the demand for flexible and stepwise control of RNA functions [8–10]. This limitation significantly restricts the depth and breadth of RNA function studies, hindering comprehensive exploration of the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of RNA in complex biological systems. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a multifunctional RNA regulation strategy that can perform both ligation and cleavage reactions within the same system [11, 12].

N 6-Methyladenosine (m6A) is the most common and reversible internal RNA modification in messenger RNA (mRNA) across eukaryotes, highly conserved in mammals, and dynamically regulated in specific cells. m6A modifications are added by “writer” complexes (e.g. METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP) and removed by “eraser” demethylases (e.g. FTO and ALKBH5), which control methylation [13–16]. However, these writers and erasers target multiple m6A sites, and their overexpression or knockout can affect global m6A levels. Even minor perturbations to a single m6A site can significantly alter cell fate and biological processes [17–19]. Real-time control of m6A methylation is crucial for understanding its specific functions, yet current methods face challenges in precise regulation due to their one-way nature, limiting the study of m6A dynamics. Therefore, a programmable and switchable tool for the precise spatiotemporal control of m6A is critically needed in RNA biology.

This study introduces a multifunctional RNA regulation strategy that enables RNA molecules to undergo both bioorthogonal ligation and cleavage reactions within the same system. Using guide RNA (gRNA) as a model, we demonstrate on-demand inactivation via click chemistry and subsequent reactivation via light exposure. By applying this technology to clickable and photoactivable gRNAs for m6A editing (CliPEdit), it enables continuous, multistep programmable regulation, overcoming the limitations of traditional unidirectional methods. By modifying the RNA 2′-OH group with BANEIC reagents (Supplementary Fig. S1), azide and light-cleavable groups are introduced, allowing gRNA to guide M3M14–dCas9 and dCas13b–M3M14 methylation systems to specific RNA target sites for m6A addition without affecting its function. RNA methylation can be completely turned off by adding small molecules for in situ click chemistry, and gRNA function can be rapidly restored by exposure to 365-nm light (Fig. 1), enabling precise control of RNA methylation. This strategy not only demonstrates the efficiency of CliPEdit in RNA regulation but also provides a foundation for future multisite, dynamic regulation in complex biological systems.

CliPEdit strategy for multistep control of site-specific RNA methylation using bioorthogonal modulation.

BANEIC reagents acetylate the RNA 2′-OH group, imparting clickable and light-activatable properties without altering gRNA function. Guided by sgRNA, M3M14–dCas9 methylates the target site. The click reaction with t-DBCO introduces steric hindrance and potential crosslinking, deactivating the sgRNA. Exposure to 365-nm light reverses this, enabling ON-OFF-ON control of site-specific RNA m6A methylation.

Materials and methods

Chemical synthesis

Experimental details for synthesis of BANEIC and t-DBCO are demonstrated in Supplementary Data.

Mammalian cell

HEK293T cells and HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modification of Eagle’s medium media (DMEM; L-glutamine, high glucose, sodium pyruvate, phenol red, Cytiva) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (CellMax) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cytiva). All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 incubator and passaged with 0.25% trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid containing phenol red.

Preparation of BANEIC modified RNAs

For this assay, RNA (20 µM) was incubated in a 10 μl reaction mixture containing 1 × Shape buffer (100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5 at 25°C), 6 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl), 60% DMSO, 100 mM BANEIC, and 100 mM DMAP. After shaking (200 rpm) at 37°C for the indicated time, the RNA was precipitated by adding 2 μl NaOAc (3 M), 75 μl ethanol, and 1 μl glycogen (5 mg/ml) and storing at −80°C for 3 h. Subsequently, the resulting suspension was centrifuged at 4°C, 12 000 × g for 1 h. The supernatant was removed, and the RNA solids were rinsed twice with 75% ice-ethanol, then dried and dissolved with RNase-free H2O.

Intracellular confocal imaging

HEK293T cells (6 × 105) were seeded in 20 mm glass-bottom dishes and cultured overnight. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, 1 μg unmodified and BANEIC-modified Fam-R-21 nt were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After incubating the cells for 6 h, 8 μM DBCO-Cy5 was added to the wells, allowing the reaction to proceed in a 37°C incubator, and 2 h later the cells were irradiated for 8 min using 365-nm light exposure. The cells were washed 3 times with warm PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde fixative solution (Beyotime) for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were then washed again with warm PBS 3 times for 5 min each, stained with Hoechst 33 342 (1 μg/ml, Sigma–Aldrich) for 5 min, and then washed again with warm PBS 3 times for 5 min each. Images of cells were acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 DIVE microscope equipped with an HC PL APO CS2 63×/1.40 OIL objective, HyD, and HyD-RLD detectors. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Hoechst 33 342 was imaged using 405 nm laser excitation; FAM fluorophore was imaged using 488 nm laser excitation; and Cy5 fluorophore was imaged using 552 nm laser excitation.

SELECT for detection of m6A

Following the well-established protocol reported by Xiao et al. [20], a 17-μl reaction mixture was initially prepared, containing 2 μg of total RNA, 40 nM each of the Up and Down Primers, and 5 μM dTTP in 1× CutSmart buffer (50 mM KAc, 20 mM Tris–HAc, 10 mM MgAc2, 100 μg/ml BSA, pH 7.9). The mixture was annealed following a series of steps: 90°C for 1 min, followed by 80°C, 70°C, 60°C, 50°C each for 1 min, and 40°C for 6 min. Subsequently, 3 μl of an enzyme mixture containing 1× CutSmart buffer, 0.01 U Bst 2.0 DNA polymerase, 0.5 U SplintR ligase, and 10 nmol ATP was introduced to the annealed products. The final 20 μl mixture was then incubated at 40°C for 20 min, denatured at 80°C for 20 min, and cooled to 4°C. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system to evaluate the SELECT assay outcomes, which included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 45 s, with results normalized relative to the RNA levels of the transcripts at the targeted sites. Primers used in the SELECT assay are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

m6A immunoprecipitation and RT-qPCR

HeLa or HEK293T cells were first seeded on 10-cm dish for overnight culture. At ~75% confluency after plating, cells were transfected with 6 μg of M3M14–dCas9 or dCas13b–M3M14 using Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime Biotechnology), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After a 6 h incubation, the cells were washed with warm DMEM and underwent a second transfection with 4.0 μg of gRNAs using Lipofectamine™ 3000. Six hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM, with or without indicated concentrations of t-DBCO. Following an additional hour of incubation, the cells were then irradiated with 365-nm light exposure for 8 min or kept untreated and further incubated for 36 h before collection.

Subsequently, cells were rinsed with PBS, harvested, and total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples were fragmented by incubation at 94°C for 1 min in freshly prepared RNA fragmentation buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) and 10 mM ZnCl2, followed by the addition of 3 M NaOAc (pH 5.0) and 100% ethanol to precipitate RNA. Subsequent to at least 3 h of storage at −80°C, samples were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C and washed with 75% ethanol, followed by washing with 75% ethanol, and drying in a vacuum centrifuge (Labconco) for 5–10 min. The fragmented RNA pellet was then resuspended in RNase-free water and its concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). A portion of fragmented RNA was reserved as an input control for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis.

Following the well-established protocol reported by Dominissini et al. [21], m6A immunoprecipitation was initiated at the preparation of protein A/G magnetic beads (20 μl, Thermo Fisher Scientific), which were washed twice in 1 × m6A IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40, RNase-free H2O) and subsequently suspended in 300 μl of the same buffer. Three micrograms of m6A antibody (New England Biolabs) was then introduced to this mixture, which was incubated under gentle rotation at 4°C overnight. After two buffer washes, the antibody-bound beads were further incubated under gentle rotation at 4°C for 6 h with a 300 μl solution containing 1 × m6A IP buffer, 90 μg fragmented total RNA, 4 μl RNase inhibitor (ABclonal), and 2 mM RVC (Ribonucleoside vanadyl complexes). The beads were then subjected to a series of washes: once with 1 × m6A IP buffer, twice with low-salt IP buffer (50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40, RNase-free H2O), and twice with high-salt IP buffer (500 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.1% NP-40, RNase-free H2O). Ultimately, the m6A-enriched RNA fragments were eluted with RLT buffer (Qiagen), purified with RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kits (Zymo), and reserved as IP samples for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis.

For the subsequent reverse transcription, the input and IP samples were used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Hifair® V one-step RT-gDNA digestion SuperMix for qPCR Kit. These cDNAs were mixed with the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix for qPCR analysis in 96-well plates, with technical duplicates for each reaction performed on a C1000™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Gapdh was selected as the internal reference gene for normalizing Actb and Foxm1 gene expression analysis, whereas Actin was used to validate the expression of Gapdh. The quantification of target RNA levels was normalized to the geometric mean of input control samples, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was applied for the calculation.

mRNA stability assay

HeLa cells were transfected with M3M14–dCas9 or dCas13a–M3M14 systems, paired with the corresponding gRNAs, followed by t-DBCO and light exposure treatment. After incubation of 48 h, cells were exposed to the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D and collected at intervals of 0, 2, 4, and 6 h. Subsequently, cells were rinsed with PBS, harvested, and total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and quality of the total RNAs were assessed with NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher). cDNA was synthesized utilizing the Hifair® V one-step RT-gDNA digestion SuperMix for qPCR Kit, followed by qPCR analysis on a C1000™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad), with each reaction performed in duplicate. Gapdh was selected as the internal reference gene for normalization of Actb gene expression analysis.

Western blot

HeLa or 293T cells, seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates, were cultured overnight, followed by transfection with 1.5 μg of either M3M14–dCas9 or dCas13b–M3M14 plasmids using Lipofectamine 8000, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After a 6-h incubation, the cells were washed with warm DMEM and further transfected with 1.0 μg of gRNAs using Lipofectamine™ 3000. Six hours after the secondary transfection, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM, with or without the specified concentrations of t-DBCO, followed by exposing to 365-nm light exposure for 8 min or left untreated after an hour, and then incubated for an additional 36 h before collection.

Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS, lysed with 100 μl of RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and kept on ice for 10 min. The lysates were scraped and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes. Supernatants were separated by centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C and then mixed with 5× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer. Denaturation of samples was performed at 95°C for 5 min prior to 12% SDS–PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis), with a 10 μl sample volume applied to each well. Electrophoresis was initiated at 80 V and subsequently increased to 120 V upon clear separation of the marker bands.

For protein transfer, a pre-activated PVDF membrane was used at a constant 200 mA, with the duration adjusted to suit protein size. After transfer, membranes were rinsed with TBST, blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at 37°C, and washed extensively with TBST before overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4°C. Following this, membranes were washed five times with TBST, incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) for 1 h at 37°C, and then washed five times with TBST before visualization with the ChemiDocMP Imaging System.

Results

Design of the CliPEdit strategy based on progressive bioorthogonal modulation

CRISPR technology has revolutionized genome editing with its precision, significantly impacting biomedical research [22–24]. In this study, we explored dynamically controlling site-specific m6A writing by fusing catalytically inactive dCas9 with m6A “writers” (e.g. METTL3–METTL14) and modulating gRNA structure and function. While conditional control of CRISPR-mediated genome editing via gRNA manipulation is feasible, current strategies are limited to one-way control, allowing only binary regulation of CRISPR system activity-ON or OFF [25–30]. To address this, we designed a new strategy where small molecule modifications to RNA enable bioorthogonal ligation and allow for removal of these modifications via light exposure. This approach provides a multifunctional strategy for multistep, on-demand regulation of RNA structure and function.

To achieve this, we designed and synthesized BANEIC, which contains two azide groups for efficient click chemistry and a nitrobenzene group for light-activated removal. We also synthesized t-DBCO, a derivative with three DBCO monomers. t-DBCO can react with an additional azido group to form intra- or interstrand crosslinks, resulting in conjugated products that crosslink RNA and introduce steric constraints [31]. These crosslinked products exhibit stronger inhibitory potency compared to monomer DBCO (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1). The process begins with BANEIC modification of RNA, ensuring no disruption to its structure or function. Next, the strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) reaction with t-DBCO introduces steric hindrance and crosslinking, deactivating RNA. Finally, exposure to 365-nm light removes the modifications, restoring RNA activity. The CliPEdit strategy, based on progressive bioorthogonal modulation, enables programmable control of RNA m6A methylation through multistep regulation of RNA structure and function.

Validation of progressive bioorthogonal regulation strategy for RNA modulation

To validate the progressive bioorthogonal modulation strategy for manipulating RNA function and structure in vitro, we introduced BANEIC into sgRNA targeting the A690 site of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) gene and analyzed it by denaturing PAGE. As modification time increased, the BANEIC-modified sg-Gapdh band slowed and broadened (Supplementary Fig. S2B). After t-DBCO treatment, only modified sg-Gapdh showed crosslinking, stalling below the gel wells (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Exposure to 365-nm light for 8 min restored the band mobility to levels similar to unmodified sg-Gapdh (Supplementary Fig. S2D). These results provide preliminary evidence of the strategy’s effectiveness in manipulating RNA structure in vitro.

We selected a 20-nt segment complementary to the target-Gapdh site in sg-Gapdh to investigate whether progressive bioorthogonal modulation could enable programmable regulation of its double-stranded structure. We expected that BANEIC modification would not affect the sg-Gapdh’s ability to form this structure. After t-DBCO treatment, crosslinking was expected to disrupt the structure, preventing complementary pairing. Following 365-nm light exposure, the BANEIC and click reaction products were removed, restoring the sg-Gapdh’s ability to form double-stranded structures (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

We used UV melting assays to test the stability of the double-stranded structure. The results indicated that sg-Gapdh showed tolerance to BANEIC modification, and only a moderate level of BANEIC introduction did not significantly impair its ability to perform complementary pairing (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Compared to unmodified sg-Gapdh, the melting temperature decreased by only 8.15°C after 6 h of modification (Supplementary Fig. S3C and F). However, the reaction with t-DBCO greatly disrupted its structure, causing a 16.23°C decrease in melting temperature at 16 μM t-DBCO, which was concentration-dependent. After further exposure to 365-nm light for 8 min, the sg-Gapdh was released, restoring its ability to form double strands (Supplementary Fig. S3G–K). Correspondingly, these structural alterations were further validated by RNA-RNA hybridization assay (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The successful in vitro control of RNA structure and activity encouraged us to further explore the progressive bioorthogonal modulation strategy for precise regulation of RNA structure and function in human cells. We first investigated the regulation of RNA imaging strategies in live cells, which allow direct visualization of the efficiency of RNA structural modulation. We chose FAM-labeled R-21 nt as a probe and introduced appropriate levels of BANEIC modification before transfecting it into HEK293T cells. These cells were then treated with DBCO-Cy5 and exposed to 365-nm light for 8 min, followed by analysis using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Fig. 2A).

Modulation strategy validation for mediating in situ bioorthogonal reactions on RNA. (A) Fluorescent visualization of the efficacy of progressive bioorthogonal modulation on R-21 nt. (B) CLSM images of HEK293T cells transfected with either unmodified FAM-R-21 nt or BANEIC-modified FAM-R-21 nt, followed by DBCO-Cy5 treatment and 365-nm light exposure. The cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst (blue), FAM-R-21 nt was visualized in the green channel, and DBCO-Cy5 was detected in the red channel. Scale bar = 50 μm.

We observed that both unmodified and BANEIC-modified FAM-R-21 nt displayed bright fluorescence spots in the FAM channel, indicating that the introduction of BANEIC at appropriate levels (100 mM) did not affect the cellular behavior of the RNA. After DBCO-Cy5 treatment, only BANEIC-modified FAM-R-21 nt showed Cy5 fluorescence, which significantly weakened following further 365-nm light exposure for 8 min. In contrast, the unmodified FAM-R-21 nt showed no change in fluorescence properties after DBCO-Cy5 treatment or 365-nm light exposure (Fig. 2B). These results demonstrate that the progressive bioorthogonal modulation strategy, based on BANEIC, can precisely control RNA function and activity through a series of intracellular bioorthogonal reactions.

Progressive bioorthogonal modulation-based CliPEdit strategy for regulating dCas9-mediated RNA m6A methylation in human cells

To test whether the progressive bioorthogonal modulation-based CliPEdit strategy enables multistep control of dCas9-mediated site-specific RNA methylation in human cells, we targeted the β-actin (Actb) A1216 site, which shows moderate methylation in HeLa cells. sg-Actb was modified with BANEIC for varying durations to introduce different modification levels [32]. The M3M14–dCas9 fusion protein plasmid was transfected into HeLa cells along with both unmodified and BANEIC-modified sg-Actb. After switching to the medium with or without t-DBCO, cells were exposed to 365-nm light and incubated for 36 h before RNA isolation (Fig. 3A).

The SELECT method, which inhibits DNA polymerase’s single-base extension activity and ligase’s gap-filling efficiency, can detect transcripts with low m6A levels [20] (Fig. 3B). We used it to assess the CliPEdit strategy, optimizing modification times and t-DBCO concentrations. Under the guidance of sg-Actb, a significant increase in m6A levels was observed at the Actb A1216 site. Notably, these levels were barely affected following BANEIC modification, indicating the high tolerance of chemical modification in sgRNAs. After 5 h of BANEIC modification, a modest decrease in m6A levels at the target site was observed, whereas subsequent treatment with 8 µM t-DBCO led to a complete inhibition of methylation in a concentration-dependent manner. Furthermore, when the duration of BANEIC modification was extended to 9 h, m⁶A levels at the target site converged to a similarly low baseline, irrespective of t-DBCO treatment. This convergence likely arises from excessive BANEIC incorporation into the sgRNA, which introduces substantial steric hindrance and thereby abolishes its ability to guide M3M14–dCas9 to the target locus (Fig. 3C). Following 365-nm light exposure, sg-Actb regained its ability to guide M3M14–dCas9-mediated methylation at the target site (Fig. 3D).

Based on the SELECT results, we selected a 5-h modification time as the optimal modification duration, and 8 µM as the optimal t-DBCO treatment concentration. The extracted total RNA was then fragmented and immunoprecipitated with an anti-m6A antibody, followed by methylated RNA immunoprecipitation qPCR (MeRIP-qPCR) to assess m6A enrichment levels (Fig. 3E). As expected, when guided by unmodified sg-Actb, the m6A levels at the target transcript were significantly increased, while the BANEIC-modified sg-Actb retained its ability to introduce site-specific m6A. After t-DBCO treatment, sg-Actb activity was nearly completely lost due to potential crosslinking, but after further exposure to 365-nm light for 8 min, sg-Actb was released and restored its m6A-writing activity, achieving continuous multistep control of site-specific RNA methylation (Fig. 3F).

To test the CliPEdit strategy for continuous multistep control of m6A levels in other RNA transcripts, we used sgRNAs targeting Gapdh A690, forkhead box protein M1 (Foxm1) A3488, and A3504, all of which have low endogenous m6A levels and are prone to methylation. MeRIP-qPCR analysis showed that BANEIC modification preserved sgRNA activity, guiding M3M14–dCas9 to introduce m6A, with a 7.7- and 8.1-fold increase in m6A levels for Gapdh and Foxm1, respectively. t-DBCO treatment nearly abolished sgRNA activity, reducing methylation to endogenous levels. After exposure to 365-nm light for 8 min, sgRNA function was restored, and m6A levels increased by 10.8-fold for Gapdh and 10-fold for Foxm1 (Fig. 3G and H). Furthermore, t-DBCO and light exposure had minimal effect on unmodified sgRNAs. These results demonstrate the CliPEdit strategy’s ability to enable multistep control of dCas9-mediated, site-specific RNA methylation in human cells.

CliPEdit strategy for the selective control of multiple RNAs

Selective control of specific biomolecules is crucial for developing a universal strategy for regulating site-specific RNA methylation. To assess the applicability of this strategy for editing m6A in specific RNA transcripts, we selected sgRNAs targeting Gapdh A690 and Foxm1 A3488. We first transfected a plasmid expressing the M3M14–dCas9 fusion protein into cells, followed by co-transfection with either native sgRNA or BANEIC-modified sgRNA. The cells were then treated with t-DBCO or 365-nm light exposure. Samples were processed and analyzed using the aforementioned SELECT method to quantify the m6A levels at each site under different gRNA targeting conditions. We anticipated that, under the guidance of specific gRNAs, the m6A level at the target RNA site would change, while m6A levels at other tested sites would remain unaffected (Fig. 4A).

As expected, co-transfection of modified sg-Gapdh with native sg-Foxm1 led to a significant decrease in m6A levels at the Gapdh A690 site after t-DBCO treatment. This reduction was markedly reversed upon exposure to 365-nm light, whereas no corresponding change in m6A modification was observed at the Foxm1 A3488 site (Fig. 4B). A similar selective effect was observed when modified sg-Foxm1 was co-transfected with native sg-Gapdh. When modified sg-Gapdh and sg-Foxm1 were co-transfected simultaneously, the m6A levels at Gapdh A690 and Foxm1 A3488 sites underwent corresponding OFF-ON switching in response to t-DBCO and 365-nm light treatment. These results indicate that by selecting appropriate gRNAs, CliPEdit can be used for m6A editing of different RNAs, allowing selective control of multiple RNAs for RNA site-specific methylation manipulation. Furthermore, the high precision of CliPEdit in targeting site-specific RNA methylation, without disrupting the methylation patterns of other endogenous RNAs, underscores its potential for bioorthogonal modulation of RNA biology.

CliPEdit regulates the biological effects of site-specific m6A levels

Encouraged by the above results, we next tested the CliPEdit strategy for its unique biological effects in controlling site-specific RNA m6A methylation through multiple steps, such as regulating the impact of site-specific methylation levels on downstream mRNA stability and protein expression (Fig. 4C). It has been shown that a single m6A modification at the Actb A1216 site is sufficient to affect mRNA stability [23]. As described earlier, we transfected plasmids expressing M3M14–dCas9, along with unmodified or BANEIC-modified sg-Actb, into Hela cells, followed by t-DBCO and 365-nm light exposure treatments. After 48 h of incubation, cells were treated with actinomycin D, a transcriptional inhibitor that suppresses new RNA synthesis [33]. At different time points after actinomycin D treatment, total RNA was extracted from the cells and subjected to RT-qPCR to quantify Actb mRNA abundance.

We observed that transfection with native sg-Actb led to a significant decrease in Actb mRNA stability, while BANEIC-modified sg-Actb had a moderate effect, still maintaining the ability to decrease mRNA stability (Fig. 4D). In contrast, t-DBCO treatment severely disrupted the structure and activity of sg-Actb, preventing it from promoting Actb mRNA degradation, which resulted in a phenotype similar to that of the nontargeting control treatment. After 365-nm light exposure, sg-Actb was released and its activity was restored to levels comparable to that of native sg-Actb (Fig. 4F). Additionally, t-DBCO treatment and 365-nm light exposure had minimal effects on the activity of native sg-Actb (Fig. 4E).

In addition to transcriptional stability, we also sought to investigate whether the CliPEdit strategy could be used to regulate the impact of site-specific RNA m6A levels on translation. Understanding the multiple roles of FOXM1 in regulating cell cycle progression, proliferation, and self-renewal is crucial for uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying cancer progression [34–36]. Specifically, the expression of Foxm1 mRNA is correlated with its m6A levels [37]. We observed that targeting and methylating the A3488 and A3504 sites of Foxm1 led to a significant decrease in Foxm1 mRNA levels, and this could be reversibly regulated through the CliPEdit strategy (Supplementary Fig. S5C and D).

Western blot analysis revealed that transfection of native sg-Foxm1 to methylate the target sites significantly reduced Foxm1 protein expression, while BANEIC-modified sg-Foxm1 maintained similar functionality for 6 h. After t-DBCO treatment, cross-linking of BANEIC-modified sg-Foxm1 disrupted its structure and function, preserving endogenous FOXM1 protein levels. Exposure to 365-nm light restored sg-Foxm1 activity, enabling M3M14–dCas9-mediated methylation. t-DBCO and 365-nm light had no effect on native sg-Foxm1 (Fig. 4G). These results show that the CliPEdit strategy enables programmable, multistep control of mRNA stability and protein expression, offering an effective tool for studying individual m6A modifications in real-time.

CliPEdit regulates dCas13b-mediated RNA methylation in human cells

Compared to the dCas9 system, which requires a Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM) sequence for binding, the dCas13b system can directly recognize target RNA guided by shorter CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs), offering a broader range of applications [38–40]. However, dCas13–M3M14 exhibits non-negligible off-target effects [24]. Building on the effective control of site-specific RNA methylation at target sites in the M3M14–dCas9 system using the CliPEdit strategy, we sought to explore whether this approach could also be successfully applied to the dCas13b–M3M14 system. We hypothesized that, similar to the dCas9 system, introducing an appropriate level of BANEIC into crRNA would maintain its original activity and allow m6A writing. The addition of t-DBCO would crosslink BANEIC-modified crRNAs, leading to a complete loss of crRNA function, while exposure to 365-nm light would release the crRNA and restore its m6A-writing activity (Fig. 5A).

To test stepwise control of dCas13b-mediated RNA methylation with CliPEdit, we targeted Actb A1216, Gapdh A690, and Foxm1 A3488/A3504 sites. The SELECT method was employed to optimize the modification time and t-DBCO concentration for each site (Supplementary Fig. S6). MeRIP-qPCR showed that native crRNA increased methylation by 9-fold for Actb, 14.6-fold for Gapdh, and 16.9-fold for Foxm1. BANEIC-modified crRNA also guided dCas13b–M3M14 methylation, but t-DBCO treatment nearly abolished this, returning methylation to endogenous levels. Exposure to 365-nm light restored methylation by 7.9-fold for Actb, 11.2-fold for Gapdh, and 14-fold for Foxm1 (Fig. 5B–D). SELECT confirmed selective control of RNA methylation at specific sites. The dCas13b–M3M14 system was toggled ON and OFF by t-DBCO and light (Fig. 5E). In contrast to simple ON/OFF systems, the CliPEdit strategy enables stepwise control over mRNA abundance dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S7). These results demonstrate the feasibility and broad applicability of CliPEdit for programmable control of dCas13b-mediated RNA methylation.

After achieving site-specific m6A modification at the Actb A1216 site using crRNA-guided dCas13b–M3M14, we assessed the stability of Actb mRNA in HeLa cells. Native cr-Actb reduced Actb mRNA stability, and similar effects were observed with BANEIC-modified cr-Actb after 7 h (Supplementary Fig. S8A). t-DBCO treatment disrupted m6A writing activity by forming crosslinked products, with no impact on Actb mRNA stability. However, exposure to 365-nm light removed the click products, restoring cr-Actb’s m6A activity and mRNA stability to levels similar to native cr-Actb (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Notably, 8 µM t-DBCO and 8 min light exposure had no effect on cr-Actb (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

We applied the CliPEdit strategy to regulate FOXM1 protein expression using the dCas13b–M3M14 system. Western blot analysis showed that cr-Foxm1 significantly reduced FOXM1 protein expression, and BANEIC-modified cr-Foxm1 had a similar effect. After t-DBCO treatment, the click reaction disrupted cr-Foxm1, restoring Foxm1 expression to endogenous levels. Exposure to 365-nm light released cr-Foxm1, restored its m6A activity, and reduced Foxm1 expression (Fig. 5F). These results demonstrate that the CliPEdit strategy can regulate biological functions via site-specific m6A levels mediated by dCas13b, enabling multistep control in any CRISPR-based system with appropriate BANEIC modification.

Discussion

Several light-induced platforms fusing split proteins with dCas proteins and m6A “writers” or “erasers” have been developed for controlling RNA methylation [41, 42]. While light offers high spatial and temporal resolution, noninvasiveness, and ease of use, these methods require two independent modules, making them complex and time-consuming compared to direct RNA regulation. Additionally, they only provide “ON-OFF” control and do not allow stepwise studies of m6A site functions. In this study, we introduce a multifunctional RNA regulation strategy (CliPEdit) that enables both bioorthogonal ligation and cleavage within the same system, enhancing RNA regulation flexibility and precision. Notably, CliPEdit allows stepwise control of site-specific RNA methylation in both M3M14–dCas9 and dCas13b–M3M14 systems. It also enables methylation regulation at individual sites in complex systems using multiple gRNAs. Overall, this method, applied in CRISPR-mediated site-specific RNA methylation, enables continuous programmable control, overcoming the unidirectional limitations of traditional strategies.

Given these advantages, we anticipate that the CliPEdit strategy developed in this study will contribute to advancing the detailed study of m6A biology. While much work remains, such as addressing the harmful effects of 365-nm light exposure on cells, future structural modifications could enable a redshift of the required wavelength for light activation, reducing cellular light damage. Furthermore, by modifying the effectors fused with dCas proteins, the CliPEdit strategy could also be applied to control other types of RNA modifications, such as m1A and ψ, further expanding its range of applications. In summary, our approach provides a convenient and efficient toolkit for precise and programmable control of site-specific m6A writing in human cells, with broad potential applications and biomedical value.

Conclusion

This study introduces a multifunctional RNA regulation strategy that enables bioorthogonal ligation and cleavage within the same system, providing a novel approach for precise, programmable CRISPR-mediated RNA methylation regulation with enhanced flexibility and precision.
