Examining the link between nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction with nursing services in clinical units: a cross-sectional study in southwest Iran
Authors: Ahmad Tahmasebi-Ghorrabi, Samaneh Moradi, Effat Jahanbani, Shiva Khaleghparast, Mohammad Veysi Sheikhrobat, Mohammad Hosein HaghighiZadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWorking conditions and occupational stress among nurses affect the quality of care and, consequently, patient satisfaction. Moreover, spiritual health among nurses is an essential component of healthcare services in achieving patient satisfaction. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the relationship between nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction with nursing services (PSNS) provided in southwest Iran.MethodsThis descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional study was conducted among 80 nurses and their corresponding 80 patients in the clinical wards of Imam Khomeini and Razi Hospitals in Ahvaz in 2022. Data were collected using standardized questionnaires assessing spiritual health and PSNS, employing a convenience sampling method. Data analysis was performed using descriptive statistics, Spearman’s correlation test, and multiple linear regression in SPSS version 24. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered.ResultsThe mean score of nurses’ spiritual health was 90.27 ± 15.32, and the mean score of PSNS was 97.23 ± 14.13, both at a moderate level. No significant association was found between spiritual health scores and demographic variables (p > 0.05). A significant association was observed between patient satisfaction scores and the frequency of hospital visits (p = 0.029). No statistically significant relationship was found between nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction (p > 0.05). The coefficient of determination (R² = 0.175) indicated that the type of insurance, frequency of hospital visits, and marital status of patients together predicted 17.5% of the variance in patient satisfaction.ConclusionSpiritual health among nurses had no effect on patients’ satisfaction with nursing care, and no statistically significant relationship was found between these two variables. PSNS was influenced by their demographic characteristics and environmental factors such as frequency of visits, educational level, insurance coverage, and marital status, rather than by the nurses’ spiritual health. By improving service delivery processes, enhancing patients’ awareness of the treatment process, providing financial support for patients, and identifying other contributing factors, patients’ satisfaction can be improved.Keywords: Spiritual health, Patient satisfaction, Nursing care, Nurse–patient relationship, Healthcare services

Introduction

Spirituality is fundamental to holistic, patient-centered care, as it provides individuals with meaning, direction, and a sense of connection to what is considered sacred [1]. Within the multidimensional framework of health, spiritual health represents a vital dimension of overall human well-being, promoting inner peace, harmony, and alignment of personal values and beliefs with moral behaviors [2–4]. Beyond its influence on coping and recovery, spiritual health contributes to health promotion and disease prevention by fostering stability in life and a sense of connection with God, society, and the environment [5–7]. This integration of spirituality not only enhances care but also intersects with key outcomes such as patient satisfaction [8]. Given this central role, nurses, as the largest group of healthcare providers, are uniquely positioned to integrate spirituality into patient care. By acknowledging patients’ spiritual beliefs and cultural values, demonstrating empathy, facilitating participation in spiritual practices, and maintaining a therapeutic presence, nurses can address both the physical and spiritual needs of patients [9, 10].

Incorporating spiritual and religious beliefs into routine care strengthens nurse–patient relationships and promotes comprehensive well-being. Patient satisfaction is widely recognized as a key indicator of healthcare quality [11, 12].It reflects patients’ evaluation of care received and directly influences the credibility and performance of healthcare systems. Various factors, including nursing practices, prior experiences, communication, and trust, affect satisfaction levels [13–15]. Since nurses play a pivotal role in patient experiences, their professional competence, interpersonal skills, and capacity to integrate spiritual care may significantly shape satisfaction outcomes [16].

The literature presents mixed evidence regarding the relationship between nurses’ spiritual health and patient satisfaction. While some studies have reported positive associations [4, 17, 18], others found no significant link [19, 20]. These inconsistencies may be partly explained by cultural differences: in Western healthcare contexts, spirituality is often considered private or marginal to professional practice [4, 21], whereas in Iran, cultural and religious norms strongly influence both nurses’ and patients’ perceptions of spirituality [3, 22]. This cultural contrast highlights the need to explore how spirituality functions within different healthcare systems. These cultural variations underscore the importance of context-specific research, limiting direct generalizability to non-Islamic setting.

Given that few studies in Iran have simultaneously examined nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction with the quality of nursing care provided [9, 20, 23], and considering the importance of this issue for nurses, particularly in religious communities, the present study investigated the relationship between these two variables in clinical settings in Iran. Based on previous evidence and theoretical assumptions, we hypothesized that higher levels of nurses’ spiritual health would be associated with greater patient satisfaction.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive–correlational, cross-sectional design because the primary aim was to assess nurses’ spiritual well-being and patients’ satisfaction at a single time point and to examine their association. This design allows efficient estimation of prevalence and detection of relationships and is appropriate when causal inference is not intended.

Study settings

This study was conducted in 2022 in the clinical units of Imam Khomeini and Razi Hospitals, Ahvaz, Iran. These hospitals were selected because they are major teaching hospitals in Ahvaz that adopt a case-method nursing model and provide a range of clinical wards (ICU, CCU, internal medicine, women’s ward, etc.). Selection of these two centers allowed sampling across diverse unit types while remaining feasible under local logistical and ethical approvals.

Sampling and sample size

Nurses were recruited through convenience sampling from the clinical wards; in other words, eligible nurses who were on duty during the data collection period and voluntarily agreed to participate were included. Because both hospitals used the case method, in which each nurse is responsible for providing comprehensive care to specific patients during a shift, the selection of patients followed the same structure. For each participating nurse, one patient was chosen from among those assigned to that nurse. Patient selection was performed using a simple random sampling procedure. Consecutive numbers were assigned to all eligible patients, and the final patient was selected by applying a random numbers table.

Sample size was estimated using a bivariate correlation model (Fisher z / G*Power approach). Based on an expected correlation coefficient of r = 0.38 (a moderate effect size informed by previous studies on nurses’ spirituality and care outcomes), α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power = 0.90, the required sample size was approximately 69 nurse–patient pairs. To allow for potential non-response/attrition, we distributed 80 questionnaires to each group; the final analysis included 80 nurses and their corresponding 80 patients [24]. All questionnaires were fully completed and included in the final analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for nurses were: (1) a minimum of a bachelor’s degree in nursing; (2) at least six months of work experience in a clinical ward; and (3) performance of at least one complete work shift in the ward under study. Inclusion criteria for patients were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) sufficient literacy to independently complete the questionnaire; (3) no history of psychiatric disorders; and (4) willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria for both groups included incomplete questionnaires and withdrawal from participation at any stage of the study.

Data collection instruments

Questionnaires were administered between May and September 2022. Data collection tools comprised three components: (1) a demographic and background information form; (2) the Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS); and (3) the Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Questionnaire.

The demographic form collected information on age, sex, marital status, educational level, ward type, frequency of hospital visits, place of admission, and type of health insurance.

Spiritual well-being scale (SWBS) – Developed by Paloutzian and Ellison (1982) [25], this standardized instrument contains 20 items rated on a six-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree (6 points) to strongly disagree (1 point). It comprises two subscales—Religious Well-Being (RWB) and Existential Well-Being (EWB)—each with 10 items (odd-numbered items for RWB, even-numbered items for EWB. Total scores range from 20 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater spiritual well-being. Scores of 20–40, 41–99, and 100–120 represent low, moderate, and high levels, respectively. Reverse scoring is applied to negatively worded items. The validity of the SWBS has been confirmed in multiple studies [26, 27], and Karimnejad et al. (2014) reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82 (5). In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91.

Patient satisfaction with nursing care questionnaire – Originally developed by Wolf and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Persian by Hajinezhad [28], this instrument consists of 25 items across three subscales: Technical–Professional care (7 items), trust (11 items), and patient education (7 items). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point). Thirteen items are positively worded, while 12 are negatively worded and reverse scored. For the total score, < 78 indicates dissatisfaction, 78–104 indicates moderate satisfaction, and > 104 indicates complete satisfaction. For subscales:

Technical–Professional care: <21 = dissatisfied; 21–28 = moderate satisfaction; >28 = complete satisfaction.

Technical–Professional care: <21 = dissatisfied; 21–28 = moderate satisfaction; >28 = complete satisfaction.

Trust: <39 = dissatisfied; 39–52 = moderate satisfaction; >52 = complete satisfaction.

Trust: <39 = dissatisfied; 39–52 = moderate satisfaction; >52 = complete satisfaction.

Patient education: <18 = dissatisfied; 18–24 = moderate satisfaction; >24 = complete satisfaction.

Patient education: <18 = dissatisfied; 18–24 = moderate satisfaction; >24 = complete satisfaction.

The Persian version’s validity was confirmed by an expert panel, and Joolaee et al. [29] reported a test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.92. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79.

To reduce social desirability and gratitude biases, questionnaires were completed anonymously and self-administered. Trained research assistants, who were not involved in participants’ clinical care, distributed and collected the questionnaires and informed participants that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their care. Furthermore, the instruments used are validated and include reverse-scored items. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that self-report bias may persist and have included this point in the Limitations.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequencies) were used to summarize participants’ demographic and study variables. To examine bivariate relationships, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was applied given the ordinal nature of some variables. Variables that had p < 0.25 in bivariate analyses were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model using the ENTER method, following recommended practices for variable screening in modest-sized samples to minimize the exclusion of potentially relevant predictors [30, 31]. To fit the regression model, the assumptions of data normality, independence of residuals, normality of residuals, and absence of multicollinearity (Variance Inflation Factor, VIF) were assessed. VIF values less than 5 indicated the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Results of regression analyses are reported with unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), standardized coefficients (β), t-values, p-values, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), as well as model R and R² statistics. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

The mean age of patients was (M = 41.7, SD = 12.5 years); 54.0% were female, and 62.0% were married. Regarding education, 38.0% of patients had a high school diploma, 34.0% had a university degree, and 28.0% had primary education or less. Correlation analysis

The examination of the relationship between demographic variables and spiritual health in nurses with patients’ satisfaction, using Spearman’s correlation test, showed a significant and inverse correlation between overall patient satisfaction and the frequency of hospital visits (r = − 0.244, P = 0.029). In other words, as the number of visits increased, patients’ satisfaction with nursing care decreased. Among the dimensions of patients’ satisfaction with nursing care, the trust dimension showed a significant and inverse correlation with the number of hospital visits (r = − 0.241, P = 0.031), and a significant and direct correlation with patients’ level of education (r = 0.261, P = 0.019) and marital status (r = 0.232, P = 0.039). Thus, as the frequency of hospital visits increased, patients’ satisfaction with the trust dimension decreased, while patients with higher educational attainment and those who were married reported higher levels of satisfaction. Additionally, there was a significant relationship between the type of health insurance (medical services insurance) and the trust dimension (P = 0.016) (Table 4).

Regression analysis

The multiple linear regression analysis using the ENTER method indicated that the type of health insurance (P = 0.008), frequency of hospital visits (P = 0.026), and marital status (P = 0.044) were significant predictors of patients’ satisfaction with nursing care. Having medical services insurance (B= -1.44, CI (-2.48, -0.4)), fewer hospital visits (B= -2.21, CI (-4.1, -0.27)), and being married (B = 1.91, CI (0.06, 3.77)) were associated with higher patient satisfaction. Collectively, these variables explained 17.5% of the variance in patient satisfaction (R² = 0.175), suggesting that factors other than nurses’ spiritual health play a role in determining patients’ satisfaction (Table 6).

Discussion

Nurses, as the largest group of healthcare providers, play a pivotal role in delivering continuous patient care, and their spiritual and religious well-being can directly influence the quality of services they provide. In the present study, approximately 60% of nurses demonstrated a moderate level of spiritual health, while about 40% exhibited a high level. Overall, the mean spiritual health score and its subdimensions were within the moderate range. Notably, religious well-being scores exceeded those for EWB, suggesting that nurses may derive greater strength and guidance from religious beliefs and practices than from broader existential perspectives.

These findings align with previous research, Atashzadeh-Shoorideh et al. [32], reported moderate spiritual health among nurses, with religious health surpassing existential health. Moeini et al. [19] similarly observed moderate scores across both dimensions, and other studies [33, 34] have confirmed this pattern. The generally higher level of religious health in both the present and prior studies may reflect the Islamic governance context and prevailing religious orientations, which encourage individuals to rely on spiritual principles and turn to religion as a coping mechanism in times of crisis.

PSNS in this study was high in the technical and educational dimensions but moderate in the trust dimension, resulting in an overall moderate rating for quality of care. Comparable results were reported by Peyrovi et al. [12], who also found high satisfaction in technical and educational areas, although their trust dimension scores were higher than in our study. High satisfaction in the technical-professional and educational dimensions may be explained by managerial supervision, the importance nurses place on these aspects of care, and their skills in providing these services. Lower trust scores could be attributed to limited time for direct patient interaction, reduced engagement due to absent companions, and high workloads [12, 35].

Regarding demographic variables, no significant associations were observed with nurses’ spiritual health or its dimensions. Moeini et al. [19] also reported no significant demographic associations except for age, and Shahbazpour et al. [23] found no links with age or gender, although marital status and education level were significant in their study. Zare et al. [9] observed an association between spiritual health and age, contrasting with our results. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study populations.

Analysis of patient-related factors revealed a significant inverse relationship between the number of hospital visits and both overall satisfaction and trust. Educational level and marital status were positively associated with trust, and patients covered by medical service insurance reported higher trust scores. Farajzadeh et al. [36] found that ward type influenced satisfaction, while age and gender did not, and marital status and insurance type were non-significant, partially differing from our results. Other studies [3, 37] similarly reported no association between age or gender and satisfaction but observed links with education level. Variations in patient characteristics, ward types, and satisfaction with other services likely explain these differences. For instance, Moeini et al. [19] noted that satisfaction with nurses was higher in therapeutic wards compared to welfare services.

No significant relationship was found between nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction. Fatemi et al. [17] in Iran, however, reported a positive association between EWB and overall spiritual health with patient satisfaction. Differences may be due to study design, patient conditions, timing, and ward types. In the present study, a case-method approach allowed patients to evaluate satisfaction with their own assigned nurse, whereas Fatemi et al. used random sampling. While nurses’ spiritual health remains above average in both studies, evolving societal and institutional priorities have emphasized service delivery and treatment outcomes over nurses’ spiritual health as determinants of patient satisfaction.

Although no direct association with patient satisfaction was observed, Hosseini et al. [38] demonstrated that spiritual-oriented psychological counseling reduced death anxiety among patients with end-stage renal disease. This suggests that educating nurses on applying spirituality in care could enhance patient satisfaction and improve outcomes, though structural or institutional factors may limit the translation of personal spiritual health into observable caregiving behaviors.

Overall, these findings indicate that environmental factors, service delivery processes, and patient conditions are key drivers of satisfaction with nursing care. Consequently, while nurses in Iran demonstrate moderate-to-high spiritual health, this alone does not necessarily lead to measurable improvements in patient satisfaction.

Limitations and strengths

This study faced certain limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and drawn from only two hospitals in southwest Iran, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other clinical contexts. Second, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias. Third, both nurses and patients completed self-report questionnaires, raising the possibility of social desirability and response bias. Fourth, because of the cross-sectional design, the study cannot establish causal relationships between nurses’ spiritual health and patient satisfaction.

Despite these limitations, the study possessed notable strengths. It was conducted across multiple care wards, thereby capturing a broader range of clinical contexts. Furthermore, the use of standardized and psychometrically validated questionnaires enhanced the reliability and validity of the measurements.

Conclusion

In this study, nurses’ spiritual health was at a moderate level. No significant relationship was found between nurses’ spiritual health and patients’ satisfaction with nursing care. The prevailing perspective in Iran’s healthcare system regarding the existence of spiritual health among nurses and its influence on patients’ satisfaction with nursing care may stem from underlying religious beliefs. Nevertheless, factors such as frequency of visits, educational level, insurance coverage, and marital status of patients were found to influence their satisfaction with nursing care. These findings suggest that the impact of nurses’ spirituality on patient outcomes is not universally generalizable and underscore the importance of examining environmental and policy influences when investigating spiritual Health in clinical settings.

By improving service delivery processes, increasing patients’ awareness of the treatment process, providing financial support to patients, and identifying other influencing factors, patient satisfaction can be enhanced. Future research should include interventional and longitudinal study designs to identify determinants of patient satisfaction with nursing care, such as individual, environmental, organizational, and political factors, and to examine how these factors influence satisfaction outcomes.
