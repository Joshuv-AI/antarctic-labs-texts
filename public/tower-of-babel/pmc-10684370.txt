Incentive pay sensitivity to firm performance prior to anticipated CEO turnover
Authors: Dmitriy V Chulkov, John M Barron
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores whether the sensitivity of CEO compensation to changes in firm performance depends on the CEO's likelihood of leaving the position. In a sample of 3180 US publicly-traded firms, we find that the sensitivity of bonus pay to firm performance is enhanced for a CEO who has reached retirement age. Further, we find that the sensitivity of bonus pay to firm performance is greater when there is evidence of a planned CEO turnover within a two-year window. We show that these findings are consistent with a principal-agent model in which firms who anticipate a greater likelihood of CEO departure find it advantageous to enhance the link between bonus pay and concurrent performance measures to compensate for the lowering of incentives that rely on the manager being employed long-term. The results provide a rationale for including bonus pay in the overall executive compensation package.Keywords: CEO turnover, Executive compensation, Planned succession, Incentive pay, Financial economics

Introduction

There are few topics in finance and economics research that receive as much popular interest as the compensation of top executives. Academic research identifies the determinants of executive compensation and vigorous public debates explore the potential for its reform [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Top executive pay is a complex topic that is unlikely to be summarized in a single approach [6,7]. In general, the shareholder value perspective on top executive compensation considers how pay provides incentives designed to maximize firm value within the framework of a principal-agent model. In contrast, the rent extraction perspective on top executive pay sees the executives maximizing their personal rents through their influence on compensation [5,6].

The shareholder value perspective suggests that compensation contracts may help address agency issues by aligning the incentives of CEOs with the interests of shareholders [8,9]. Such contracts use equity and incentive bonus instruments that create links between top executives' pay and shareholder value. For a contract to serve as an effective incentive, there must be a connection between the executives' pay and performance [5,10]. Extant research identifies a positive relationship between the level of executive compensation and the firms' stock market performance [11,12] as well as between executives’ wealth and firm performance [13]. This connection between wealth and performance became more pronounced as the prevalence of stock options grew in the 1990s and 2000s [6,14,15].

The incentivizing role of compensation may be realized through different forms of compensation including equity – stocks and options – as well as incentive bonuses. Executive compensation literature primarily explored the role of stocks and options as incentives that link executive compensation to the firm's market value [6]. However, a large share of US top managers also take part in cash bonus plans that link incentive compensation to reaching specific targets [1,4,16]. While equity compensation is directly linked to the firm's market performance, a number of studies indicate that cash bonus compensation emphasizes accounting earnings instead of market value [[16], [17], [18]]. Cash bonuses provide a clear and direct link between annual CEO pay and firm performance [4]. Studies also show that cash bonus compensation is focused on short-term incentives and plays a different role compared with deferred, longer-term incentives provided by equity-based pay [19,20]. Cash bonuses affect CEOs' investment and reporting decisions [[21], [22], [23]]. They also help to provide collective incentives for the top management team [16] and to communicate the firm's strategic objectives [18].

Bonus pay makes up a significant and stable part of U.S. CEO compensation packages. Table 1 shows that in the U.S. between 1993 and 2019, the proportion of bonuses as well as other non-salary and non-equity pay consistently comes to around 29% of total compensation. In the same time, the proportion of salary was down by almost half and the proportion of equity pay doubled. Bonus pay is an important part of compensation not only for U.S., but also for other major economies. Between 2002 and 2009, the share of bonus pay in CEO compensation was up to 40% for Germany, 18% for France, 19% for Holland, and 20% for Belgium [6]. Non-equity incentive compensation increased from 12.6% of total pay in 2000 to 20.6% in 2006 in major British firms [24].

Bushman (2021) points out that while executive compensation literature views cash and bonus compensation as a second order effect compared to the magnitude of the demonstrated incentive effects of equity, bonus pay is widely used, and compensation boards clearly see distinct value in bonus schemes. According to Bushman (2021), “researchers have struggled to understand precisely what value-adding roles these [bonus] plans play, given that stock prices impound all available information and incentives from executives’ firm-specific equity portfolios appear to be orders of magnitude larger than bonus plan incentives.” [25] We strive to add to this literature by examining pay-performance sensitivity using an extensive dataset of US publicly-traded firms. Our results identify significant differences between bonuses and other compensation components in the pay-performance relationship when the anticipated likelihood of a CEO departure from the firm is increased.

Our focus on CEOs' incentives close to their anticipated departure from the firm follows from the literature on career concerns. The idea that incentive pay has a role in addressing the horizon problem for CEOs with reduced career concerns was proposed by Gibbons and Murphy (1992) [26]. The empirical evidence is mixed as researchers explored different types of compensation mechanisms. Dechow and Sloan (1991) find that CEOs reduce discretionary R&D spending when they are close to leaving the firm to improve short-term performance, but this is mitigated through CEO stock ownership [27]. Cheng (2004) reports that option grants are effective in addressing myopic reduction in R&D close to CEO retirement [28]. Baber, Kang and Kumar (1998) identify an enhanced link between accounting earnings and cash compensation close to CEO retirement [20]. In contrast, Guay, Kepler and Tsui (2019) find that bonus pay increases with executive tenure, but do not observe increased sensitivity of bonus payouts to firms' income close to CEO retirement [16]. We aim to complement this literature by exploring the impact of different variables that identify increased likelihood of a CEO's turnover on three major components of CEO compensation including bonus, as well as salary and equity pay.

Our analysis adopts a fixed-effects approach across firm-CEO pairs. This fixed-effects specification is important as it controls for compensation differences across firm-CEO pairs that are due to such factors as industry type, company life cycle stage, and business strategy which may affect the use of bonus compensation and are otherwise difficult to control. We also control for changes over time. In this setting, we find that firm size and CEO tenure are positively related to CEO compensation, while the firm's leverage – debt-to-asset ratio – is negatively related. All three types of CEO compensation decrease for CEOs who reach retirement age. Controlling for all of these characteristics, we explore the impact of firm performance measured by the return on assets (ROA) on annual CEO compensation. We find a positive and significant relationship between annual compensation and concurrent firm performance only for the bonus pay component of compensation.

A key empirical contribution of this paper is the finding that two measures that indicate a higher likelihood of anticipated CEO departure are associated with greater sensitivity of bonus pay to changes in firm performance. Specifically, we find that the pay-performance link is enhanced for CEOs reaching retirement age. Further, the sensitivity of bonus pay to firm performance is significantly greater when there is planned CEO turnover within a two-year window. Changes in leadership are important events for a firm, and we consider different CEO turnover scenarios by distinguishing planned and unplanned turnover [29]. Some turnover is anticipated by the firm, such as in cases of retirement and planned succession, while other turnover cases are not anticipated by the firm such as ones caused by illness or resignation [30]. When turnover is anticipated, there are implications for CEO compensation incentives. Suppose that current CEO effort affects firm performance both concurrently and in the future. When the likelihood of turnover increases, incentives for current effort based on anticipated future pay are diminished if the likelihood of continued employment is reduced. Firms may compensate for such muting of long-term incentives by increasing short-term incentive bonus compensation.

Our empirical results demonstrate that this is the case. There are significant differences in the sensitivity of the three annual compensation components to firm performance when anticipated CEO turnover becomes more likely. Specifically, we find that the link between bonus compensation and the rate of return on assets is enhanced for CEOs who reach retirement age, suggesting that the decrease in total compensation for retirement-age CEOs is accompanied by greater sensitivity of bonus pay to concurrent changes in performance. The link between bonus compensation and the rate of return on assets is also enhanced for CEOs whose departure is imminent – happens with two years – when the departure is due to a planned succession.

These results shed new light on the role of bonus as a part of the total executive compensation package. Incentives in equity-based compensation receive much attention, however there is limited empirical evidence on what makes bonus pay different from equity-based incentives. While our results do not put in question the significant role of equity compensation in long-term managerial incentives, we find significant variation in the short-term incentive effects between salary, equity, and bonus compensation. Firms in our sample enhance the link between bonus pay and firm performance when the likelihood of CEO departure is increased. This clarifies the conditions in which bonus pay has a role in complementing equity compensation. Furthermore, the results underscore the differences between planned and unplanned CEO turnover. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 frames the research hypotheses. Section 3 presents the data and methods. Section 4 outlines the results. Finally, Section 5 provides a discussion of the contributions and a conclusion.

Theory and hypotheses

The shareholder value view of executive compensation focuses on aligning the interests of managers with those of shareholders to relieve agency problems [8,26]. To make compensation contracts effective as incentives, there must be a link between executive compensation and firm performance. Multiple studies examined this issue. Among others, Murphy (1985) as well as Coughlan and Schmidt (1985) report a positive relationship between executive compensation and stock returns [11,12]. Jensen and Murphy (1990), Hall and Liebman (1998), Chen, Jeter and Yang (2015) as well as Edmans, Gabaix and Jenter (2017) report evidence of a positive relationship between executives’ income and firm performance [6,[13], [14], [15]]. We intend to explore how this relationship changes when a CEO departure from the firm is anticipated.

CEO effort can improve firm performance both concurrently and in future periods, so it has lasting effects [[31], [32], [33]]. The connection between a CEO's compensation and firm performance is particularly important during the CEO transition period. When the CEO is employed at the firm, the future pay that depends on the firm's relative performance can motivate the CEO to exert more effort in the present. However, when CEO turnover is expected, the firm can enhance the incentives for the CEO's effort based on the firm's current performance to offset the decline in the incentives for the present effort that depend on the CEO's employment in later periods. Chulkov and Barron (2022) present a principal-agent model that identifies conditions under which a decrease in the expected probability of future employment increases the optimal sensitivity of bonus pay to performance when the CEO's effort has persistent effects on firm performance [34]. In this model, an increase in the probability of CEO turnover anticipated by the firm reduces the effectiveness of future incentives in encouraging concurrent effort of the CEO. This creates a benefit for the firm from providing stronger incentives based on the CEO's current performance. The outcome is a higher optimal bonus rate in response to an increase in the likelihood that the CEO leaves the firm.

Several studies suggest that compensation incentives are enhanced over the CEO's tenure. In a theoretical study, Edmans et al. (2012) show that as CEO tenure increases, there is less time left for the CEO to enjoy lifetime utility so the increase in compensation must be greater to incentivize effort by the CEO [10]. Bloomfield et al. (2021) report that firms use cost shielding to limit the sensitivity of bonus pay especially for outsider CEOs, and that this practice reduces with the tenure of CEOs leading to higher sensitivity of bonus pay later in the tenure [18]. Mangen (2016) demonstrates that the sensitivity of CEO pay to performance becomes greater with time after economic shocks to the firm [35].

Studies on CEO's career concerns suggest that incentive pay helps address the horizon problem for retiring CEOs [26]. Dechow and Sloan (1991) demonstrate that CEOs reduce discretionary spending on research and development (R&D) when they are close to leaving the firm to improve short-term earnings performance and that CEO stock ownership attenuates this link [27]. Cheng (2004) finds that option grants are also effective in addressing such myopic reduction in R&D spending close to CEO retirement [28]. Focusing on cash compensation, Baber, Kang and Kumar (1998) identify an enhanced link between accounting earnings and cash compensation including bonus pay close to CEO retirement [20]. In contrast, Guay, Kepler and Tsui (2019) do not observe increased sensitivity of individual bonus payouts to firms' income close to CEO retirement, but report that bonuses provide effective team incentives [16].

The idea that CEO incentives need to strengthen when the board of directors can anticipate CEO departure as suggested by the career concerns literature helps identify several specific predictions for empirical testing. One hypothesis concerns the age of the CEO. It is safe to assume that when a CEO is of retirement age, the probability of an impending CEO departure is increased. This higher probability of departure should result in a compensatory increase in the incentives for performance. Literature on executive compensation identifies different roles for cash bonuses focused on short-term incentives and the deferred, longer-term equity-based pay [19,20]. While equity compensation is directly linked to the firm's performance in the market, a number of studies indicate that cash bonus compensation emphasizes accounting earnings instead of market value [[16], [17], [18]]. As our focus is on the incentives in the short-term period before anticipated CEO departure, the relevant incentive mechanism should be cash bonus compensation. This suggests the following Hypothesis 1.

CEOs who reach retirement age experience an increase in the sensitivity of cash bonus compensation to changes in firm performance.

Another hypothesis follows from classifying CEO turnover into types based on the degree to which the turnover is anticipated by the firm. Murphy and Zimmerman (1993) as well as Parrino (1997) report significant differences between the two broad categories of planned and unplanned CEO successions [36,37]. The first group includes normal retirements and changes in the duties of the CEO. These departures represent planned successions that are expected by the firm [38]. The second grouping includes CEO turnover cases are less likely to be anticipated by the firm. These departures include firings, early retirements, resignations, as well as turnover due to illness. The two categories are distinguished by the anticipated likelihood of CEO departure which provides Hypothesis 2.

CEOs close to a planned succession departure are associated with an increase in the sensitivity of cash bonus compensation to changes in firm performance.

Materials and methods

We begin creating our empirical dataset with the S&P ExecuComp data on executive pay for the period between 1992 and 2020. We merge this dataset with the CompuStat financial data for every firm. The combined dataset comprises 3879 U S. publicly-traded firms and 56,255 observations. Our method for building the dataset is consistent with studies that utilize ExecuComp and CompuStat data [[39], [40], [41]]. Our analysis concentrates on CEOs, so we first identify the CEO for each firm in the dataset. We then eliminate firms for which there is no clear definition of the CEO at the beginning of the fiscal year. These eliminations involve co-CEOs and cases when the same CEO was listed at several firms. We also eliminate firm-year observations with missing financial data and ones that include firm restructuring such as merger or bankruptcy. To ensure adequate spell length for each firm in the dataset, we restrict our analysis to firms that appear in the sample for at least six years.

We identify cases of CEO turnover in our sample by tracking the changes in the executive listed as CEO at a firm each year. We then determine the departure year of this CEO. We also determine the year each manager became CEO and the length of their tenure. We exclude turnover cases of interim CEOs, usually such managers serve as CEO for less than two years. We further eliminate cases related to the last year the firm is included in the sample since we cannot determine whether the CEO departs at that point. We also exclude a small number of observations in which a negative compensation component has been recorded. We then eliminate observations that include a manager's first year as CEO at the firm because first-year compensation can include hiring bonuses. Finally, we eliminate cases that include the departure year of the CEO because they may reflect partial employment for the year as well as severance pay. Adding firm-level lagged variables in the analysis further eliminates the first year a firm is in the sample and reduces our sample size to 3180 firms and 35,647 observations over the period from 1993 to 2019. CEO turnover cases were classified into planned and unplanned successions following the process used by Chulkov and Barron (2021) as well as Parrino (1997) [37,38]. In order to identify the reasons for turnover, we start with the data from the ExecuComp database that provides basic turnover reasons including cases when CEOs retired, resigned, and were deceased. For a large number of observations, however, such information is missing. Consequently, we examined the news stories related to CEO departures identified in the dataset to verify the type of each case in our sample. We classified CEO departures into two broad categories of planned and unplanned turnover. Two researchers independently reviewed the cases and agreed on their classification. The planned succession group includes CEOs who retired after reaching the retirement age of 65 as well as CEOs who left the post to move to another senior position in a change of duties. These changes of duties are typically moves to the board of directors, often as the chair. The unplanned succession group incorporates CEOs who resigned, were fired, took early retirement, or left because of illness. In the process of verifying turnover types, we also confirmed the departure dates for executives obtained during dataset construction.

Next, we calculate measures of annual CEO compensation components and of firm performance to determine pay-performance sensitivity. We estimate all empirical models for three individual components of CEO compensation including salary, equity, as well as non-salary and non-equity compensation that primarily represents bonus pay. Table 1 presents the relative magnitude of these compensation components over time. For our firm performance measure, we calculate the annual rate of return on assets (ROA) as the net income (before extraordinary items and discontinued operations) divided by the firm's total assets. We include control variables that are likely to affect CEO compensation including firm size measured as the natural logarithm of the firm's assets, the market to book value of assets ratio, and leverage defined as the ratio of the firm's total debt to assets. We include controls for the length of CEO tenure at the firm. A description of the variables and data sources appears in Appendix Table A1.

Firms’ size and market-to-book value ratio are positively related to CEO compensation, while leverage is negatively related. Longer CEO tenure positively impacts all forms of compensation. This is consistent with the principal-agent optimal contracting view that would create a stronger pay-performance link for CEOs with higher likelihood of anticipated departure from the firm [34]. Higher probability of anticipated CEO turnover reduces the effectiveness of future incentive contracts in encouraging current effort by the CEO, resulting in a benefit to the firm from providing stronger bonus incentives based on current performance.

Discussion

This study uses an extensive dataset of compensation for US-based CEOs at 3180 firms to explore the changes in the annual compensation of CEOs when the firm anticipates their likely departure from the position. The empirical analysis presents several findings. We observe that over the sample period the share of annual equity-based compensation has grown at the expense of salary, while annual bonus compensation has remained steady at approximately 29% of total compensation. Our results confirm that firm size and CEO tenure are positively related to CEO compensation, while firm's leverage is negatively related. All three types of CEO compensation decrease for CEOs who reach retirement age. Controlling for these firm and manager characteristics, we find a positive and significant relationship between annual compensation and firm performance measured by ROA for the bonus component of CEO compensation.

One important contribution of this study is the observed increase in the link between bonus pay and performance when the anticipated likelihood of a CEO departure from the firm rises. First, we find that the link between bonus pay and firm performance measured by concurrent ROA is significantly enhanced for CEOs who reach retirement age, suggesting that the decrease in total compensation for retirement-age CEOs is accompanied by an increase in the sensitivity of bonus pay to contemporaneous changes in firm performance. Second, we observe that the link between the ROA and bonus pay is significantly enhanced for CEOs whose departure is imminent when the departure can be classified as planned. Third, we find no significant differences in such pay-performance sensitivity for equity pay, highlighting different roles between equity-based and non-equity-based annual incentive compensation.

These results clarify how the nature of compensation for a CEO is influenced by the anticipated likelihood of turnover. CEO effort choices have lasting effects and impact the firm's performance not only concurrently, but also in the future [[31], [32], [33]]. However, the firm has limits in enforcing incentive compensation contracts after a CEO departs. CEOs vary in their likelihood of separating from the firm. Sometimes their separation timing can be accurately anticipated in advance – these are the cases of normal retirements and other planned turnover. Other cases may be more of a surprise for the firm and its board of directors. The degree to which a CEO's separation is anticipated has implications for how tightly the board of directors may wish to tie the CEO's pay to current firm performance. A board that believes the CEO is soon to separate would be inclined to tie the compensation more closely to firm performance so as to preserve incentives. That is, among CEOs who are soon to depart, the sensitivity of the CEO's current pay to firm performance is expected to be greater when the board anticipates the CEO's departures than when the board does not. When impending turnover is anticipated, such as when the CEO reaches retirement age or in the years prior to a planned succession, the incentive pay contract should exhibit a higher degree of pay-performance sensitivity.

While both equity and bonus pay may be used to provide incentives to managers, our empirical findings identify a significant difference in the way firms employ equity and bonus compensation close to anticipated CEO departures. This helps explain the relevance of bonus incentives in comparison to equity. Much of the executive compensation literature focuses on sensitivity of CEO wealth to performance [6,13] and suggests that over a CEO's entire career path, equity dwarfs all the other types of compensation [14,42]. Our study does not question the incentivizing role of stock incentives. However, non-equity bonus compensation has been a large and stable part of executive compensation over our sample period leading Bushman (2021) to call for research to identify the potential value-adding role played by bonus pay [25]. We find that bonus compensation provides a highly significant and direct link between concurrent pay and firm performance, and this link is enhanced prior to anticipated CEO turnover.

Our empirical results complement studies on the link between earnings and cash compensation [17,20] by providing evidence on how anticipated CEO turnover affects bonus pay sensitivity to performance. Our results differ from the findings of Guay, Kepler and Tsui (2019) who did not find a significant link between CEO career concerns and bonus pay sensitivity for individual CEO contracts [16]. However, that study used a different sample of firms sourced from the Incentive Lab database that covered a smaller number of relatively larger firms over the period of 2006–2014. The definition of career concerns used by Guay, Kepler and Tsui (2019) focused on CEOs over the age of 60. In contrast, we used two measures of increased likelihood of CEO departure from the firm including reaching the retirement age of 65 and departing in a planned succession based on public reports. Our empirical strategy of more precise variables indicating reduced career concerns, a larger dataset, and analysis with firm-CEO fixed effects helped us identify scenarios that significantly affect bonus pay sensitivity close to CEO departure from the firm.

Several studies discuss additional theoretical bases for increased sensitivity of CEO compensation to firm performance over time. Gibbons and Murphy (1992) identify weakening career concerns close to CEO retirement [26]. As a result, financial incentives must be enhanced to keep CEO motivation. Edmans et al. (2012) explain that as a CEO comes closer to retirement, there is less time left to get utility from earnings and so the rewards must be higher to induce the same level of effort [10]. Bloomfield et al. (2021) document that due to cost shielding for incoming CEO bonus contracts the pay-performance link for bonus pay increases later in a CEO's tenure [18]. Our empirical design differs from these studies. Gibbons and Murphy considered the time period from 1971 to 1989 and reported the results for total cash compensation that included the sum of salary and bonus [26], while we separate these compensation components and find significant differences between them. Edmans et al. (2012) provide no empirical test for their theoretical model and Bloomfield et al. (2021) focus exclusively on bonus plans to explore the cost-shielding feature [10,18]. We link greater sensitivity of bonus pay to performance not only to CEOs reaching retirement age, but also to CEOs leaving the firm in planned successions. Our results highlight the pay sensitivity differences in turnover cases anticipated by the firm. These results help inform additional theoretical development as well as decisions on optimal executive compensation policies over time.

Limitations of this study include the nature of our compensation dataset. While the breadth and scope of the data is extensive, as it covers 3180 publicly-traded US firms over more than a quarter of a century, the level of detail on each compensation contract is limited. The granularity of our data does not allow us to consider the specific conditions of bonus compensation contracts that may be available in smaller-scale datasets. This also limits the examination of the institutional context of compensation.

Developments following the financial crisis of 2007–2008 have led to institutional changes due to concerns that short-term performance pay fostered incentives for the managers to engage in fraudulent financial reporting. One institutional change has been the rollout of clawback provisions in executive pay that allow for recovery of previously awarded incentive compensation if a financial restatement occurs at the firm [43]. The Dodd-Frank act mandated such provisions and clawback provisions become increasingly common after 2010. Chen, Greene and Owers (2015) found increased sensitivity of total incentive pay including both equity and bonus for firms that had clawback provisions for a sample that focused on the period of the financial crisis [44]. We explored whether the greater adoption of clawback provisions following the Dodd-Frank Act impacts our empirical findings. We included interaction variables that identify observations for the time period starting in 2010 but did not find any significant differences in our results for these observations that would indicate a change in our results for the period associated with greater adoption of clawback provisions. Exploring the pay-performance link close to CEO departure for a sample of firms that have been specifically identified as using clawback provisions remains a direction for future research.
