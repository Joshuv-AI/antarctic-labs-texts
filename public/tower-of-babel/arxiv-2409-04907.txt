A Toric Analogue for Greene’s Rational Function of a Poset
Elise Catania

Abstract
. Given a finite poset, Greene introduced a rational function obtained by summing certain rational functions over the linear extensions of the poset. This function has interesting interpretations, and for certain families of posets, it simplifies surprisingly. In particular, Greene evaluated this rational function for strongly planar posets in his work on the Murnaghan–Nakayama formula. In 2012, Develin, Macauley, and Reiner introduced toric posets, which combinatorially are equivalence classes of posets (or rather acyclic quivers) under the operation of flipping maximum elements into minimum elements and vice versa. In this work, we introduce a toric analogue of Greene’s rational function for toric posets, and study its properties. In addition, we use toric posets to show that the Kleiss–Kuijf relations, which appear in scattering amplitudes, are equivalent to a specific instance of Greene’s evaluation of his rational function for strongly planar posets. Also in this work, we give an algorithm for finding the set of toric total extensions of a toric poset.

associated to
G
is

\mathcal{A}_{\rm{tor}}(G)=\pi(\mathcal{A}(G)).

A connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}-\mathcal{A}_{\rm{tor}}(G)
is a
toric chamber
. A
toric poset
is a set that arises as a toric chamber in a toric graphic hyperplane arrangement for at least one graph
G
.

Naturally, given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we know that these points lie in the same equivalence class in
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
exactly when for each coordinate
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
x_{i}\mod 1=y_{i}\mod 1
. Therefore, we can still recover an acyclic quiver with underlying graph
G
for each point
[\mathbf{x}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by orienting
\{i,j\}\in G
as
i\to j
if
x_{i}\mod 1<x_{j}\mod 1
and orienting
\{i,j\}
as
j\rightarrow i
otherwise.

Key point:
By this construction, two points in the same toric chamber do not necessarily map to the same acyclic quiver. To account for this, the following
flip operation
is defined.

Definition 3.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

Consider acyclic quivers
Q_{1},Q_{2}
that differ by converting one source vertex (all edges directed outward) to one sink vertex (all edges directed inward). Then, we say that
Q_{1},Q_{2}
differ by a
flip
. This flip operation induces an equivalence relation on the set of acyclic quivers with the same underlying graph
G
, and we denote this equivalence relation as
\equiv
.

Remark 3.2
.

This flip operation was studied by Mosesian and Pretzel in
[

and
[
26
]
, respectively. Moreover, this flip operation has appeared in other works including Chen
[

]
, Defant and Kravitz
[

[

]
, Macauley and Mortveit
[

]
, Speyer
[

[

. This flip operation also appears in the context of reflection functors in quiver representations
[

.

These flips are an instance of quiver mutation at a sink or source vertex
[
11
]
. Caldero and Keller show that for two mutation-equivalent acyclic quivers
Q_{1},Q_{2}
, there exists a source-sink flip sequence one can apply to
Q_{1}
that yields a quiver isomorphic after relabeling vertices to
Q_{2}
, but not necessarily equal to
Q_{2}

[

[

With ordinary posets, we saw that there is a bijection between chambers of
\mathcal{A}(G)
and the set of acyclic quivers with underlying graph

. For toric posets, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(

There is a bijection between the chambers of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{tor}}(G)
and the set of acyclic quivers with underlying graph

A

3.1.
Properties of Toric Posets

Throughout this paper, all of our quivers will be acyclic (no directed cycles). We will still specify the acyclic assumption for clarity throughout. Also, all of our quivers will be simple. By simple we mean that there are no parallel directed arcs; self-loops and anti-parallel directed arcs are already prevented due to the acyclic assumption.

For ordinary posets, we saw that the definition of the Hasse diagram and transitive closure depended on chains in the poset. A similar story will be true for toric posets, except using
toric chains
. We first define a
toric directed path
.

Definition 3.6

(
[
9
]
)
.

Elements
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k}\in V
form a
toric directed path
if
Q
contains all of the following arcs:
x_{1}
x_{2}
x_{3}
\vdots
x_{k-1}
x_{k}

Let
C
be the set of vertices in a toric directed path.
The
length
of the toric directed path is
|C|-1
. We note that an arc is a toric directed path of length
1
and a vertex is a toric directed path of length
0
.

Definition 3.7

(
[
9
]
)
.

A
toric chain
is a subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
that is totally ordered for every poset induced by an acyclic quiver in
[Q]
.

Just as chains are closed under subsets in a poset, toric chains are closed under subsets in a toric poset.
In
[
9
, Prop 6.3]
, Develin, Macauley, and Reiner show that a subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
is a toric chain if and only if the elements of
V^{\prime}
lie along a toric directed path. Moreover, for
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
, every toric directed path in
Q^{\prime}
is contained in at least one maximal toric directed path, and every toric chain of
[Q]
is contained in at least one maximal toric chain. By maximal, we mean with respect to containment.

Definition 3.8
.

Two elements
a,b
of a toric poset
[Q]
are
torically comparable
if there exists a toric chain in
[Q]
that
a,b
lie on together. If there is no toric chain that
a,b
lie on together, we say that
a,b
are
torically incomparable
.

Similarly to the ordinary poset setting, a toric poset may arise as a chamber in the complement of the corresponding toric graphic hyperplane arrangement for several graphs. Thus, there is also ambiguity for toric posets when identifying
[Q]
with an acyclic quiver. However, once again we have two natural choices which we will define below:

(1)

\overline{[Q]}
, the
toric transitive closure
of
[Q]

(2)

[Q]_{\rm{Hasse}}
, the
toric Hasse diagram
corresponding to
[Q]

(

We saw that Greene’s rational function

A

corresponds to a chamber in the complement of the toric complete graphic arrangement

\displaystyle[w]:=[(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})]=\left\{\right.

\displaystyle(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1},w_{n}),

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle\left.(w_{n},w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1})\right\}

Definition 3.10

(

Let

.

Let

.

and

correspond to the same toric chamber

as

, arising in different toric graphic hyperplane arrangements.
∎

In Remark
3.2
, we saw that sink-source flips are an instance of quiver mutation at a sink or source vertex. Thus, we
carry over the notation for mutation and let
\mu_{k}(Q)
denote the resulting quiver after flipping source (or sink)
k
in
Q
and we also let
[Q]_{v}
denote the set of quivers in
[Q]
where
v
is a source.

Proposition 3.12
.

For any vertex
v
in any acyclic quiver
Q
, there exist an acyclic quiver
Q^{\prime}
in
[Q]
having
v
as a source. That is,
[Q]_{v}
is never empty.

Proof.

Let
Q_{1}\in[Q]
and let
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{L}(Q_{1})
where
v=a_{i}
for some
a_{i}
. If
i=1
, vertex
v
is necessarily a source. Otherwise, we may flip
a_{n}
, since
a_{n}
is a sink in
Q_{1}
. In the resulting quiver, vertex
a_{n}
is a source and vertex
a_{n-1}
is a sink. We can keep flipping in this way until we reach a quiver where
a_{i}
is a source.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

For a toric poset
[Q]
, the set of toric total extensions can be written in terms of ordinary linear extensions in the following ways:

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])=\{[w]:w\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})\text{ for some }Q^{\prime}\in[Q]\}

(2)

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{Q^{\prime}\in[Q]_{1}}\left\{[1\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}-\{1\})\right\}

where
\bigsqcup
denotes disjoint union.

Proof.

Assertion (i)
. We start by showing

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])\subseteq\{[w]:w\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})\text{ for some }Q^{\prime}\in[Q]\}.

Let
[\alpha]\in\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
. Then by Definition
3.10
, we have
c_{[\alpha]}\subseteq c_{[Q]}
. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be a point in
c_{[\alpha]}
and let
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}-\mathcal{A}(G)
such that
\pi(\mathbf{\tilde{x}})=\mathbf{x}
. By
[
9
, Thm. 1.4]
,
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\in c_{w^{\prime}}
for some
w^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
. Since
c_{[\alpha]}\subseteq c_{[Q]}
, we similarly have
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\in c_{Q^{\prime}}
for some
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
. Thus, we have that
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\in c_{Q^{\prime}}\cap c_{w^{\prime}}
. Using Equation (
3
), it must be the case that
c_{w^{\prime}}\subseteq c_{Q^{\prime}}
, implying
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
.

Now, we show

\{[w]:w\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})\text{ for some }Q^{\prime}\in[Q]\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q]).

Consider
[\alpha]
such that
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
for some
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
. If
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
, then
a_{n}
is a sink in
Q^{\prime}
, so we may flip at
a_{n}
. We note that
(a_{n},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})\in\mathcal{L}(\mu_{a_{n}}(Q^{\prime}))
. We can keep flipping in this way until we have visited every element of
[\alpha]
. Then, by Equation (
3
), we have that for all
w^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
,
c_{w^{\prime}}\subseteq c_{Q^{\prime\prime}}
for some
Q^{\prime\prime}\in[Q]
. From Theorem
[
9
, Thm. 1.4]
, we have
\pi(c_{w^{\prime}})\subseteq c_{[\alpha]}
for all
w^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
and
\pi(c_{Q^{\prime\prime}})\subseteq c_{[Q]}
for all
Q^{\prime\prime}\in[Q]
. Since every point in
c_{[\alpha]}
is the projection of a point in
c_{w^{\prime}}
for some
w^{\prime}\in[\alpha]
, we have that
c_{[\alpha]}\subseteq c_{[Q]}
, and
[\alpha]\in\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
.

Assertion (ii)
We now show assertion (ii) is equivalent to assertion (i). If
[w]\in\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
, then for all
w^{\prime}\in[w]
, we have that
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
for some
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
. Moreover, there exists a unique
w^{\prime}\in[w]
such that
w^{\prime}_{1}=1
. Thus, (i) is equivalent to

\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in[Q]}\{[w]:w\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}),w_{1}=1\},

which we can rewrite as

\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in[Q]}\{[1\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}-\{1\})\}.

We show that this union is in fact disjoint. In other words, we show that if

\{[1\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}-\{1\})\}~\bigcap~\{[1\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime\prime}-\{1\})\}\neq\emptyset

for
Q^{\prime},Q^{\prime\prime}\in[Q]
, then
Q^{\prime}=Q^{\prime\prime}
. Consider
[1\hat{w}]
such that
\hat{w}=(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}-\{1\})\bigcap\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime\prime}-\{1\})
. For all
\{i,j\}\in G-\{1\}
, one has

i\rightarrow j\text{ in }Q^{\prime}-\{1\}\iff\hat{w}^{-1}(i)<\hat{w}^{-1}(j)\iff i\rightarrow j\text{ in }Q^{\prime\prime}-\{1\}.

We also note that since quivers
Q^{\prime},Q^{\prime\prime}
have
1
as a source, any edges incident to
1
will be directed away from
1
. Thus,
Q^{\prime}=Q^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore, we have shown that

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{Q^{\prime}\in[Q]_{1}}\left\{[1\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime}-\{1\})\right\}.\qed

Remark 3.14
.

Let
[Q]
be a toric poset where
1
is an isolated vertex in the underlying graph of
[Q]
. Then,
1
is both a source and a sink, so when finding the set of toric total extensions of
[Q]
, we can still use part (ii) of Proposition
3.13
.

Before stating the next result, we recall that a
bounded
poset
P
is one that has a unique minimal element
\hat{0}
and a unique maximal element
\hat{1}
.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
P
be a bounded poset, and let
Q
be the quiver resulting from adding the directed edge
\hat{0}\rightarrow\hat{1}
to the Hasse diagram
H(P)
. Then
one has a bijection

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\theta:\mathcal{L}(P)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])\\
(\hat{0},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1},\hat{1})&\longmapsto&[(\hat{0},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1},\hat{1})].\end{array}

Proof.

We first show that this map is injective. Let
\mathbf{a}=(\hat{0},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1},\hat{1})
and
\mathbf{b}=(\hat{0},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},\hat{1})
be two linear extensions of
P
, and suppose
\theta(\mathbf{a})=\theta(\mathbf{b})
. Then,
[(\hat{0},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1},\hat{1})]=[(\hat{0},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1},\hat{1})]
. Since these two cyclic equivalence classes are equal, the representatives where
\hat{0}
comes first are also equal.

We now show that
\theta
is surjective. First, we consider
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
. Using Proposition
3.13
part (ii), we consider all quivers in
[Q]
where
\hat{0}
is a source and let
Q^{\prime}
be such a quiver. Since
Q^{\prime}
contains the directed edge
\hat{0}\rightarrow\hat{1}
, we cannot flip
\hat{1}
or else
\hat{0}
would no longer be a source. We note that besides
\hat{0}
and
\hat{1}
there are no other sinks nor sources in
Q^{\prime}
to consider flipping. Thus,
Q^{\prime}
is the only quiver in
[Q]
with
\hat{0}
a source, so an arbitrary toric total extension of
[Q]
is of the form
[(\hat{0},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1},\hat{1})]
. It is clear that
(\hat{0},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1},\hat{1})
is a linear extension of
P
and thus we can conclude that
\Phi
is surjective.
∎

Theorem 3.16
.

Counting the toric total extensions for a toric poset
[Q]
is
\#P
-complete.

Proof.

This result follows from the main result of Brightwell and Winkler
[

]
, since Proposition
3.15
shows that counting the elements of

has counting ordinary linear extensions

and then Proposition

that has exactly that many toric total extensions.
∎

Although part (ii) of Proposition

Definition 5.1

(

A

such that for all distinct

(1)

(Cyclicity)
(a,b,c)\in C\implies(c,a,b)\in C

(2)

(a,b,c)\in C\implies(c,b,a)\notin C

(3)

(Transitivity)
(a,b,c)\in C

(a,c,d)\in C\implies(a,b,d)\in C

A partial cyclic order

is a
total cyclic order
if for all

(a,b,c)\in C
or
(a,c,b)\in C

Definition 5.2

A total cyclic order

Source-Sink Equivalence with a Fixed Source

In order to find the set of toric total extensions

We provide a recurrence for finding

Theorem
1.9

Let
v
be any vertex in an acyclic quiver
Q
, and let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be any two acyclic quivers in the subset
[Q]_{v}
of the source-sink flip-equivalence class
[Q]
, so

is a source in both
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.

Then there exists a source-sink flip sequence from
Q_{1}
to
Q_{2}
such that every intermediate quiver in the sequence also has
v
as a source. In other words, the flip sequence does not flip at
v
, nor at neighbors of
v
.

To prove Theorem
1.9
, we first show in Lemma
6.2
that we can freeze a vertex in the sense that we can find a flip sequence between any two representatives of a toric poset which never flips at this vertex. We highlight that the sequence described in Lemma

may flip at neighbors of

, just not at

itself. We then relate this to quivers in
[Q]_{v}
by building a quiver which reduces all vertices torically comparable with

, including

itself, to one vertex and then in Lemma

, showing that the flip equivalence class of this quiver is in bijection with
[Q]_{v}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
, and take an ordinary linear extension
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
of
Q^{\prime}
. Performing a sequence of flips at the elements
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
in that order is well-defined as a sequence of flips, and results in the original quiver
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We note that the process of flipping at vertices in this way is well-defined. Since
a_{1}
is a minimal element of the linear extension,
a_{1}
is a source in
Q^{\prime}
, so we can flip at
a_{1}
. The resulting quiver will have
(a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},a_{1})
as a linear extension and
a_{2}
as a source, so we can flip at
a_{2}
. Proceeding in this way, after flipping at the first
i-1
vertices, we reach a quiver where we can flip at
a_{i}
. Flipping at all vertices once causes the orientation of each edge to change twice, resulting in the same quiver we started with,

.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

Consider a flip sequence between

(also seen in

]
). Let
Q
be a quiver, and let
G
be the underlying graph of
Q
. A
walk

W
in
G
is a sequence of vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
v_{i}
is adjacent to
v_{i-1}
. The
inverse walk

-W
is obtained by reversing the sequence
W
. If
v_{1}=v_{n}
, then the walk is called a
circuit
. A circuit
C
is
trivial
if it is equal to traversing the walk
W
and then the reverse walk
-W
. For a walk
W=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})
in a quiver
Q
, an edge
\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}
is a
forward edge
if it is directed towards
v_{i}
in
Q
and it is a
backward edge
otherwise. Let
|C_{Q}^{+}|
and
|C_{Q}^{-}|
denote the number of forward edges and backward edges, respectively, of circuit
C
in quiver
Q
. The flow-difference of
C
in
Q
is defined as

d_{Q}(C):=|C_{Q}^{+}|-|C_{Q}^{-}|
. Two quivers
Q,Q^{\prime}
with the same underlying graph
G
are said to have the same flow-difference if
d_{Q}(C)=d_{Q^{\prime}}(C)
for each circuit
C
of
G
.

Remark 6.8
.

The flow-difference of a circuit appears under other names in the literature. For instance, in
[
28
]
, Propp refers to the flow-difference of a circuit as the circulation of the circuit and in
[
9
]
, the authors use Coleman’s
\nu
-function on a circuit
[
7
]
.

Mosesian introduced the idea of pushing down maximal vertices in
[
22
]
. The operation of pushing down maximal vertices is the same as flipping sinks to sources. In 1984, Pretzel showed the following.

Theorem 6.9
.

(
[
26
, Thm. 1’]
)
Let
G
be a finite simple graph. Two acyclic orientations of
G
can be obtained from each other by pushing down if and only if they have the same flow-difference.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
[Q]
be a toric poset, and let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in[Q]_{v}
. Then,
\Phi(Q_{1})
is flip equivalent to
\Phi(Q_{2})
.

Proof.

Using Theorem
6.9
, it suffices to show that
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})
have the same flow-difference. Since the map
\Phi
never creates any cycles, any circuits present in
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})
must come from circuits in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
respectively. There are three types of circuits in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
to consider:

(1)

Circuits that do not contain elements that are torically comparable to
v
.

(2)

Circuits that only contain elements that are torically comparable to
v
.

(3)

Circuits where a proper subset of the elements are torically comparable to
v
.

In case (i), cycles that do not contain
v
remain the same under
\Phi
. Since the flow-differences of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are equal in any such circuit, we immediately know that the flow-differences of
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})
at such circuits are equal.

Circuits from case (ii) are deleted under
\Phi
, so they will not affect the flow-differences of
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})
.

We now consider case (iii), the most interesting case. Without loss of generality, let
C
be a circuit in the underlying graph of
Q_{1}
such that a nonempty proper subset of the vertices on
C
are torically comparable to
v
in
Q_{1}
. Such a circuit can be divided into walks
S_{1},T_{1},S_{2},T_{2},\ldots,S_{m},T_{m}
which appear consecutively where all vertices along
S_{i}
are torically incomparable to
v
and all vertices along
T_{i}
are torically comparable to
v
. Under the map
\Phi
, all vertices on each
T_{i}
are merged into
v^{*}
, edges that are completely contained in each
T_{i}
are deleted, and each
S_{i}
becomes a circuit that contains
v^{*}
.

We claim that in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, the number of forward edges along each
S_{i}
is fixed, which implies the number of backward edges is also fixed. Consider
S_{1}
and let the first and last vertices be
w
and
u
. By construction,
w
is incident to
x
, a vertex torically comparable to
v
, and similarly
u
is incident to
y
, a vertex that is torically comparable to
v
. Since
v
is torically comparable to both
x
and
y
, there exists a walk
W_{1}
from
v
to
x
and a walk
W_{2}
from
v
to
y
. We note a circuit that is formed by these two walks and
S_{1}
:
W_{1},S_{1},W_{2}
. By Theorem
6.9
, this circuit has the same flow-difference in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. However, since
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are in
[Q]_{v}
, the edges along the walks

and

must have all arrows directed away from

. Therefore, to preserve the flow-difference, the number of forward edges in

must be the same in

and

, implying that the flow-differences of the created circuit in
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})
are equal.

We have now addressed all possible circuits in
\Phi(Q_{1})
and
\Phi(Q_{2})

\Phi(Q_{1})

Lemma 6.12

Let

be a toric poset, and let

We first show

(1)

and

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

7.
An Algorithm for Finding Toric Total Extensions

We recall from Lemma
1.8
the well-known recursive description of the set of linear extensions
\mathcal{L}(P)
of an ordinary poset
P
.

Lemma
1.8

Let
P
be a poset, and let
a,b
be two incomparable elements of
P
. Then,

\mathcal{L}(P)=\mathcal{L}(P_{a\rightarrow b})\sqcup\mathcal{L}(P_{b\rightarrow a})

where
P_{a\rightarrow b}
is obtained from
P
by adding the relation
a<b
and
P_{b\rightarrow a}
is defined similarly.

We use this fact as motivation to provide a recursive algorithm for finding the set of toric total extensions
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
of a toric poset
[Q]
.

Let
Q
be a quiver with vertices
a
and
b
such that neither arc
a\rightarrow b
nor
b\rightarrow a
is in
Q
.
We define
Q_{a\rightarrow b}
to be the quiver
Q
with an added arc
a\rightarrow b
, and
Q_{b\rightarrow a}
is defined similarly. Note that an equivalent definition of
a,b
being torically incomparable in
[Q]
is that there exists a quiver
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
such that
a,b
are ordinary incomparable in
Q^{\prime}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
a,b
be two torically incomparable elements in the toric poset
[Q]
.

(i)

If
a,b
are in different connected components of the graph of
[Q]
, then
[Q_{a\rightarrow b}]=[Q_{b\rightarrow a}]
and

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])=\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])=\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}]).

(ii)

If
a,b
are in the same connected component and
Q^{\prime}\in[Q]
is a representative where
a,b
are ordinary incomparable, then the sets
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{a\rightarrow b}])
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{b\rightarrow a}])
are disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
, but the inclusion of the disjoint union

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{a\rightarrow b}])\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{b\rightarrow a}])\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])

may be proper.

(iii)

On the other hand, assume that
a,b
are distance two in the graph of the toric transitive closure
\overline{[Q]}
,
say both adjacent to the vertex
v
. Then if one chooses
Q^{\prime}\in\overline{[Q]}_{v}
, that is,
Q^{\prime}
is a representative of
\overline{[Q]}
with
v
a source (as in Proposition
3.12
), the inclusion in (ii) becomes an equality:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])=\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{a\rightarrow b}])\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q^{\prime}_{b\rightarrow a}]).

Proof.

Assertion (i)
We note that adding either the directed edge
a\rightarrow b
to
Q
or the directed edge
b\rightarrow a
to
Q
does not create any new cycles and therefore does not change the flow-difference of
Q
. By Theorem
6.9
, the toric poset
[Q_{a\rightarrow b}]
is equal to
[Q_{b\rightarrow a}]
, so we have that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])=\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}])
.

Assertion (ii)
Suppose
a,b
are in the same connected component of
G
, the underlying graph of
[Q]
. We first show that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}])
are disjoint. The quivers
Q_{a\rightarrow b}
and
Q_{b\rightarrow a}
will have different flow-differences. By Theorem
6.9
, the toric posets
[Q_{a\rightarrow b}]
and
[Q_{b\rightarrow a}]
are not equal, so they correspond to disjoint toric chambers of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm{tor}}(G)
. Therefore, by Definition
3.10
,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}])
are disjoint.

Let
c_{[Q]}
be the toric chamber in the associated toric graphic hyperplance arrangement that corresponds to the toric poset
[Q]
and let
c_{[Q_{a\rightarrow b}]}
be defined similarly. By
[
9
, Prop. 3.2]
, we have that
c_{[Q_{a\rightarrow b}]}\subseteq c_{[Q]}
and
c_{[Q_{b\rightarrow a}]}\subseteq c_{[Q]}
. It follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}])\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
. Therefore, we have shown that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{a\rightarrow b}])\bigsqcup\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q_{b\rightarrow a}])\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q]).
In Example
7.2
, we demonstrate an instance where this inclusion is in fact proper.

Assertion (iii)

Let
a,b
be a torically incomparable pair such that both vertices are adjacent to a common vertex

in the graph of
\overline{[Q]}
. We first show that in any representative

where
v
is a source, elements
a,b
are ordinary incomparable in
Q^{\prime}
. Consider for the sake of contradiction that for such a representative

and
b
are ordinary comparable, i.e. up to relabeling, there is a directed path from
a
to
b
.

By assumption, vertices
a,b
are connected to
v
in the graph of
\overline{[Q]}
, so
a
is torically comparable to
v
and
b
is torically comparable to
v
. Therefore,
a
and
v
both lie on a toric directed path and
b
and
v
live on a different toric directed path. Note that these toric directed paths cannot be the same, since by assumption
a
and
b
are torically incomparable. The most general case for these toric directed paths is shown here

v
x_{1}
x_{2}
\ddots
\iddots
\ddots
a
x_{k}
y_{1}
y_{2}
z_{1}
\cdots
z_{j}
\iddots

where the dashed edges from

to

and from

to

are two of the edges that appear in

, but are possibly not in

We see that the elements

form a toric chain, contradicting the fact that

and

are torically incomparable elements.

Using Proposition
3.13

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}(\overline{[Q]})=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\overline{Q^{\prime}}\in\overline{[Q]}_{v}\end{subarray}}\left\{[v\hat{w}]:\hat{w}\in\mathcal{L}(\overline{Q^{\prime}}-\{v\})\right\}.

Corollary 7.3
.

For a toric poset
[Q]

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{tor}}([Q])
