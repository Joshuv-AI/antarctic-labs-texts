Diabetes-induced right ventricular remodeling precedes left ventricular remodeling and occurs via a distinct fatty acid uptake pathway
Authors: Sushumna B. Satyanarayana, Jaden S. Campbell, Marjie P. Schmitt, Jose E. Campos, Rammohan Shukla, Donald F. Smith, Sreejayan Nair, Danielle R. Bruns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes is a major public health concern, with both type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) resulting in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). The hallmark of DCM is metabolic inflexibility, characterized by excess uptake of fatty acids (FA). Elevated FA uptake results in an imbalance between uptake and utilization of FA, leading to lipotoxicity, lipid accumulation associated with DCM. Although DCM is defined by left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, recent work has demonstrated that the right ventricle (RV) also remodels in diabetes but through poorly understood mechanisms. The temporal development of LV versus RV dysfunction is unclear, alongside the metabolic mechanisms that underlie ventricular-specific remodeling. Therefore, we set out to quantify the temporal dynamics of RV and LV remodeling in DCM as well as to identify the mechanisms by which the RV uptakes FA. We utilized several mouse models of T2D as well as a large-animal pig model of T1D to gain mechanistic insight into RV remodeling. In two models of T2D, we show that RV remodeling begins early in disease pathogenesis, before changes in LV structure or function. We quantified FA uptake across models of T1D and T2D, performed lipidomics and found that the LV and RV accumulate different lipid species and that the mechanism of uptake of FA is distinct between the ventricles. Unlike the diabetic LV in which the primary FA transporter CD36 is robustly upregulated, CD36 does not appear to be involved in diabetic RV myopathy. Together, we show that the RV remodels before and independently of the LV via distinct FA metabolic mechanisms. Background

Diabetes mellitus is a major global health concern. Recent estimates suggest there are currently 451 million individuals with diabetes worldwide, and this figure is projected to increase to 693 million by 2045 [1]. The most deleterious consequence of developing either type 1 (T1D) or type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a substantially elevated risk of cardiovascular disease [2]. Diabetes induced pathological changes that occur in the absence of coronary artery disease, hypertension, and valvular disease are called diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). DCM is a significant contributor to mortality in individuals with diabetes [2], emphasizing the importance of delineating its pathophysiological mechanisms.

The diabetic heart is well-characterized by elevated left ventricular (LV) size and mass, thicker LV walls [3] and diastolic dysfunction [4] that over time progresses to impaired systolic function [5]. Although DCM is therefore by definition the presence of LV dysfunction, the right ventricle (RV) also remodels with diabetes [5]. The first evidence of diabetic RV myopathy was reported in human myocardial biopsies that demonstrated larger RV myocytes in patients with diabetes compared to controls [6]. Clinical findings in patients with T2D suggest functional remodeling with evidence of diastolic dysfunction [7, 8]. A recent report using a genetic discovery-based approach to identify risk factors for RV dysfunction found that the most statistically significant causative risk factor for RV load stress was diabetes [9]. Although pre-clinical reports are limited, RV dysfunction has been reported in both pig and rodent models of T1D and T2D [10, 11]. In response to diabetes, the RV underwent hypertrophy and fibrosis and had impaired systolic function. However, despite these findings, the mechanisms of RV remodeling remain poorly described. Further, the temporal nature of RV versus LV remodeling in DCM has not yet been reported. Because of ventricular interdependence, chronic LV dysfunction also impairs the function of the RV [12]. However, it is also possible that RV remodeling may precede LV remodeling. Indeed, in male subjects with T1D, alterations in RV function preceded any functional changes in the LV as measured by echocardiography [13]. In type I diabetic children, subclinical RV systolic and diastolic dysfunction also developed before changes in LV function [14]. To assess the timeline of development of LV and RV dysfunction, we performed temporal analysis of function in two classic mouse models of T2D- a genetic (the db/db mouse) and diet/environmental (dual high fat diet and low dose streptozotocin; HFD+STZ). We performed temporal analysis at early stages of diabetes pathogenesis, where these models are established to have normal LV function [5, 15, 16], providing a unique opportunity to understand RV DCM independent of changes in the LV.

DCM is multifactorial but occurs in part due to a metabolic switch. As a consequence of the inability to use glucose as a fuel, the heart utilizes excess fatty acids (FA). To permit reliance on FA as a fuel, the primary FA transporter CD36 translocates from the cytosol to the cell membrane [17]. Higher membrane localization and expression of CD36 facilitate excess FA uptake and consequent lipotoxicity. Indeed, the causative role of CD36 has been well studied in the LV in T2D [18, 19]. Loss of CD36 in diabetes is therapeutic, where contractile dysfunction caused by lipotoxicity was attenuated in CD36 knockout mice [20]. However, FA uptake mechanisms have not yet been identified in the diabetic RV. Therefore, we quantified CD36 and FA uptake as a mechanism of RV dysfunction. To rigorously determine whether CD36 expression is involved in RV diabetic myopathy as in the LV, we quantified CD36 expression in several pre-clinical models of T1D and T2D. Across several mouse and large-animal models, we consistently found that CD36 is either unchanged or downregulated in the RV in T1D and T2D. Given that RV dysfunction is the strongest predictor of mortality in many cardiovascular contexts [21], there remains a need to identify mechanisms of RV-specific remodeling in the diabetic heart.

Methods

Mouse models of T2D

All protocols were approved by the University of Wyoming IACUC. Male and female 6–8 week old leptin receptor mutant (db/db) that are homozygous for a spontaneous mutation in the leptin receptor [22] and heterozygote lean littermates (wild type, WT) were ordered from Jackson Laboratories (#00697). Mice were housed in groups of two to five animals per cage in a temperature-controlled room (21°C) on a standard 12:12 light/dark cycle. Food (#5001) and water were provided ad libitum.

2-month-old male and female C57BL6 mice were assigned to control (Con) or high-fat diet + streptozotocin (HFD + STZ) and housed with littermates in a temperature-controlled vivarium (21°C) with a 12:12-hour light/dark cycle. The combination of HFD+ low-dose STZ is well-reported to mimic the pathology of hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, and glucose intolerance with concomitant cardiac dysfunction [23, 24]. The HFD consisted of 45% fat, 35% carbohydrate, 25% protein (Research Diets D12451), compared to the Con diet of 10% fat, 68% carbohydrate, 22% protein (Research Diets D12450Ji). Following eight weeks of HFD, mice were administered STZ at 30 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection once a day for five consecutive days following a four-hour fast [25]. STZ was dissolved in sodium citrate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.5) [26]. Control animals received the citrate vehicle. Mice then continued the HFD or Con diet for another 4–8 weeks.

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory due to their predisposition to diet-induced obesity. Upon arrival, four-week-old mice were acclimated for two weeks to mitigate the weight loss associated with shipping. Mice were housed four per cage under a 12:12 light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. Following acclimation, mice were assigned to either a standard diet of 10% kcal from fat (Research Diets D12450Ji) or a HFD of 60% kcal from fat (Research Diets D12492). Body weight and food intake were recorded weekly. At the end of the 12-week treatment period, mice were euthanized for tissue collection.

For all models, food was pulled the night prior to morning sacrifice. Animals were euthanized by pentobarbital (FatalPlus) overdose, consistent with AVMA guidelines. Heart, LV, and RV weights were collected along with left tibia length for normalization. RV were frozen for biochemical analyses or embedded in optimized cutting temperature compound (OCT) and frozen for histology. Tibia length (TL) was measured with a caliper to normalize cardiac mass to animal body size.

Pig model of T1D

Female domestic 8-week-old pigs were anesthetized with intramuscular injections of ketamine (10 mg/kg), xylazine (5 mg/kg), and acepromazine (0.2 mg/kg) followed by general anesthesia via isoflurane (1–2%). Under anesthesia, 150 mg/kg STZ was administered intravenously. 200 mL of 5% glucose was given for 1 hour after STZ injection to avoid hypoglycemia [27]. Pigs were fed ad libitum with Agland Hog Grower Pellet (High Plains Cattle Supply & Armory, Brush, CO), formulated to contain not less than 16% crude protein and 2.5% crude fat. Animals were subject to blood glucose tests over 4 weeks to monitor the development of the diabetic phenotype. At the end of 10 weeks, animals were euthanized by pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, intravenous) [27]. Biopsies of the LV and RV were harvested for biochemical analyses and flash frozen.

Details of the mouse and pig diet compositions are provided in Supplemental Table S3.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was assessed via transthoracic echocardiography (Visual Sonics Vevo 2100; FujiFilm). Mice were induced with 2.5% isoflurane in compressed air and maintained between 1% and 3% to permit a heart rate (HR) >400 bpm. Respiratory rate and HR were monitored with 4 lead limb ECG (FujiFilm). Parasternal short axis views of the RV were captured in B- Mode and M- Mode. Parasternal long axis views of the RV and pulmonary artery (PA) were captured in B- Mode. Pulse wave Doppler views of the PA were captured for Pulmonary Acceleration times (PAT). RV M-mode tracings were used to assess RV wall thickness during systole and diastole (RVFW;s and RVFW;d). Short axis B-mode tracings were used to assess fractional area change (FAC). Pulse wave Doppler views were traced for PAT, a surrogate for mean PA pressure [28]. RV stroke volume (SV) and cardiac output (CO) were calculated by Visual Sonics software.

Immunoblotting

Membrane proteins were isolated using Mem-PER Plus Membrane Extraction Kit, as per manufacturer instructions (Thermo Fisher # 89,842). For total proteins, tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCL, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 01.% SDS, 1% Nonidet P-40, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, pH 7.4) containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using bicinchoninic acid assay and prepared in Laemmli sample buffer (BioRad). Equal quantity of protein was separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin or 5% non-fat dry milk before overnight incubation in primary antibody. Membranes were washed and incubated with the secondary antibody following the overnight incubation. Protein bands were visualized with chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher #34578) and autoradiography and quantified in ImageJ. Even loading of proteins was verified by appropriate loading control and Ponceau S staining. The following primary antibodies were used: CD36 (Abcam 124,515, RRID- AB_2924667), FATP6 (NBP2-81909, RRID- AB_3418864), Caveolin 1 (Cell Signaling- D46G3, RRID- AB_2275453), Na+K+ATPase (Cell Signaling 3010, RRID- AB_2060983), α-actinin (Abcam 68,167, RRID- AB_11157538). Secondary antibodies included Sigma A21140 and Invitrogen A11008.

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from the RV using standard Trizol protocols and reverse transcribed using High- Capacity RNA- to- cDNA™ Kit (ThermoFisher #4387406). Real-time RT- PCR was performed using iQ SYBR Green Supermix (ThermoFisher #A46109) and normalized to the housekeeping gene ribosomal protein l 26 (rpl26). Expression was calculated by ΔΔCt relative to Con and expressed as fold change. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Histology

LV and RV were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen in Optimal Cutting Temperature buffer. 4 μm sections were cut using a pre-cooled cryostat −20 °C (Leica Biosystems). Only sections with intact morphology were included for histological and histochemical analyses. All sections were obtained from the mid-ventricular free wall.

For myocyte cell area, sections were fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde, then permeabilized with 1% Triton X- 100. Slides were washed three times with deionized water and stained with wheat germ agglutinin- 488 (ThermoFisher W11261) in the dark. After incubation, samples were washed with PBS and mounted for imaging by fluorescent microscopy (Zeiss LSM 980). Cardiomyocyte area was calculated using ImageJ in a blinded manner by tracing all clear and complete cells per picture. To quantify fibrosis, LV and RV were stained following PicroSirius Red (Abcam 246,832) protocols. Images were acquired at 6X and processed with ImageJ. To quantify red- stained collagen, the scale bar in ImageJ was altered to μm and converted to gray scale. Red- stained collagen was segmented from other color contrasts using color thresholding. The red color threshold was adjusted using a Con and db/db RV sample to determine estimated min and max. The same color threshold was used to quantify the amount of stained collagen for all groups and results were expressed as percent area. The quantification excluded the epicardial fat and perivascular regions.

Oil Red O staining was used to assess the accumulation of neutral lipids. The LV and RV were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature buffer and subsequently cut into 10-μm-thick sections. The frozen sections were equilibrated at 37 °C for 30 min and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the sections were placed in 60% isopropyl alcohol and covered with an Oil Red O working solution for 15 min (Thermo Fisher A12989.14). The sections were sequentially washed thrice with 60% isopropanol and PBS. Images were then visualized under a brightfield microscope and were quantified using ImageJ and analyzed blindly. To quantify red- stained lipid droplets, the scale bar in ImageJ was altered to μm and converted to gray scale. Red-stained droplets are segmented from other color contrasts using color thresholding. The red color threshold was adjusted using a Con and db/db RV sample. The same was done for the LV samples. The same color threshold was used to quantify the amount of lipid stained area for all groups and results were expressed as percent area. Each genotype had at least 3 biological replicates, and images were captured from at least 6 views.

Immunohistochemistry

LV and RV sections in OCT were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissues were permeabilized in cold methanol and 1% triton. Tissues were blocked with 5% BSA. Primary antibodies CD36 (Abcam 124,515), FATP6 (NBP2-81909) were diluted in 1% BSA and incubated overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibody (anti-rabbit Invitrogen, A11012; anti-mouse Invitrogen, A11029) in the dark for an hour. Tissue sections were washed and mounted with DAPI for visualization using Zeiss LSM 980.

Boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY)

Frozen sections (5 μm) were fixed in 10% formalin for 30 min and washed in PBS. The fixed sections were incubated with 4 μM BODIPY 493/503 dye (Invitrogen D3922) for 30 min at room temperature. After washing three times, sections were stained with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI and covered with glass coverslips. The digital images were obtained with a Zeiss LSM 980. Acquired images were quantified for % Area using ImageJ.

Lipidomics

Ventricular lipids were extracted using a biphasic solvent system of cold methanol, methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE), and water with some modifications. Heart samples (LV and RV) (~20–25 mg) was transferred to 2 mL pre-weighted polypropylene tubes containing stainless steel beads (SPEX, Metuchen, NJ) and of LC-MS-grade cold methanol (240 µL). Samples were homogenized in a Spex GenoLyte 1200 homogenizer: 5 cycles at 15 seconds/cycle at 2000 rpm, with a 2 minute break between cycles. Cold MTBE (600 µL) was added to the samples, followed by vortexing and shaking at 4 °C. Phase separation was induced by adding 100 µL LC-MS-grade water followed by vortexing and centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 5 min). The upper organic phase was recovered and evaporated using a Stream of dry nitrogen. Dried lipid extracts were resuspended in a isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1, v/v, 300 µL) mixture. Samples were vortexed (10 s) and centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 2 min) prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS experiments were performed using an Agilent Eclipse C8 (Santa Clara, CA; 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 um particle size) with a Waters I-class UHPLC (Waters, Milford, MA), couple to a Synapt-XS Q-TOF mass spectrometer. The LC solvents were: A; 50% acetonitrile, 50% water, 0.1% formic acid, and B: 90% isopropyl alcohol, 10% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid (all solvents were Fisher Optima LC-MS grade). The initial solvent composition was 20% B, with a linear increase to 35% in 2 minutes, followed by an increase to 52% at 2.5 minutes, linear increase 85% at 11 minutes, a 2.5 minute clean at 99% B, and re-equilibration at 20% B. The flow rate was 0.35 µL/min, with an injection volume of 1 µL. Data was collected at 4 Hz in both positive-ion and negative-ion electrospray modes. Data-dependent-analysis (collision energy 12, 20, 30, 50), and data independent analysis (DIA, “MS^E”, collision energy ramp 20–50) was collected on pooled samples for tandem MS.

MS-Dial 5 [29] was used for data processing; feature finding, alignment, and tandem MS annotation using the MS-Dial built-in lipidomics library. MS1 tolerance for alignment and annotation was set to 0.01 Da, and 0.025 for MS2, with a retention time alignment tolerance of 0.1 min. Statistical analysis to find differential lipids was done in MetaboAnalyst [30] via commonly available methods including principal component analysis (PCA), t-test, and variable importance in projection (VIP) values. It is generally believed that variables satisfying both p < 0.05 and VIP > 1.0 are differential lipid molecules. None of the hypotheses survived multiple-testing correction, thus all analyses are reported using a nominal p-value < 0.05.

The full table of RV and LV lipidomics raw data is provided in Supplementary Material.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Comparisons between WT and db/db mice; Con vs T2D/T1D were conducted using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Data distribution was assessed for normality prior to analysis. Significance was set a priori at α < 0.05.

Results

The temporal dynamics of RV versus LV remodeling in diabetes remain unclear, thus we began by analyzing ventricular structure and function in 8–10 week old db/db mice and their WT controls. By 8 weeks of age, db/db mice are obese, hyperinsulinemic and hyperglycemic [31]. Consistent with prior reports, LV mass and wall thicknesses were not different between db/db and WT mice. Although LV myocytes were mildly hypertrophic, fibrosis and lipid content were not different (Supplemental Fig. 1). LV Ejection Fraction (EF) was preserved and E/e’ and Isovolumic Relaxation Time (IVRT) were unchanged, together suggesting preserved LV function at this age (Supplemental Fig. 2).

However, despite preserved LV function and structure at this early age, we observed differences in RV structure and [15, 16] function. RV mass was higher in the db/db mice compared to WT when normalized by tibia length (RV/TL) (Fig. 1A). db/db mice had a higher RV anterior wall thickness in diastole compared to WT (RVAWd) and a trend towards thicker walls during systole (Fig. 1B, RVAWs, p = 0.06). Surprisingly, RV myocyte cross-sectional area (CSA) was unchanged in the db/db RV (Fig. 1C). However, collagen content was higher in the db/db RV (Fig. 1D) and neutral lipid content was also higher compared to WT (Fig. 1E) as visualized by oil red o staining. By echocardiography (Supplemental Table S1, Fig. 2A), RV fractional area change (FAC), a surrogate for RVEF [32] was lower in the db/db RV compared to WT (Fig. 2B). PA-VTI, a surrogate for RV stroke volume [33], was lower in the db/db RV (Fig. 2C). RV E/e’, indicative of RV filling pressures and diastolic dysfunction, was elevated in the db/db RV (Fig. 2D). We then validated these findings in another model of T2D in dual HFD and low-dose STZ mice that were on the paradigm for 12 weeks. We found at this early stage of T2D that RV FAC and Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (TAPSE) were both lower compared to control mice, indicative of systolic dysfunction (Fig. 2E–G, Tables 1 and 2). Under this same duration of HFD+STZ, the LV retains normal function [5]. Together, these results suggest the presence of early RV dysfunction in T2D, before LV remodeling occurs. To begin to understand how FA may enter the diabetic RV, we quantified gene and protein expression of an alternate receptor fatty acid transporter fatty acid transport protein 6 (FATP6). Unlike in the LV where these regulators are robustly upregulated [36, 37], we found either no change or a downregulation in the db/db RV compared to the WT (Fig. 3H).

Discussion

To date, the pathogenesis of DCM has been understood almost exclusively through the lens of LV pathological remodeling, and current clinical definitions largely fail to recognize the contribution of RV function to this condition. However, accumulating evidence from our group and others [6, 10, 11, 38] indicates that the RV plays an important and independent role in DCM pathophysiology. What has remained unclear is the temporal sequence and mechanisms of RV remodeling relative to the LV. In the present work, we demonstrate that alterations in RV structure and function not only precede LV remodeling but also occur through mechanisms that are distinct from those in the LV. Importantly, we show that these distinct mechanisms persist across several models of T1D and T2D. Together, these findings establish that the LV and RV respond differently to metabolic stress, underscoring the need to consider the RV as a distinct and critical contributor to DCM pathogenesis.

The present work adds to accumulating evidence that indicates that the LV and RV respond differently to metabolic stress in diabetes, with distinct timelines and patterns of dysfunction. We show for the first time that DCM is characterized by early chamber-specific metabolic remodeling. In the db/db model at 8 weeks of age, the RV showed hypertrophy, fibrosis, and significant lipid accumulation, while at this age, the LV remained lipid-sparse and showed no collagen deposition (Fig. 1). This finding is consistent with other reports in models of pathological overload that have demonstrated that the RV has a susceptibility to extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrosis under pathological conditions [39, 40]. Functionally, the db/db RV showed diastolic dysfunction with early signs of systolic impairment. The db/db RV had a higher E/e’ alongside a decreased PA-VTI, a surrogate for RV stroke volume, accompanied by lower RV FAC, showing evidence for progression of systolic dysfunction. In the dual hit HFD+STZ model of T2D, the RV showed systolic dysfunction at 12 weeks of induction of T2D, while the LV retained normal function (Fig. 2). In models of metabolic overload such as the db/db mouse, changes in LV mass and structure occur around 12–14 weeks of age [41] and echocardiographic evidence of LV diastolic dysfunction presents around 16 weeks of age [15, 35]. To our knowledge, a temporal analysis of RV dysfunction in DCM has not yet been reported, though the contributions of the RV to DCM are beginning to gain recognition. Pathological mechanisms of ventricular dysfunction have been recently shown in a genetic model of T1D, where the authors showed RV diastolic dysfunction emerged concomitant with LV progression [38]. In support of the notion that the RV may remodel before the LV, in male subjects with T1D, alterations in RV function preceded functional changes in the LV by echocardiography [13]. In type I diabetic children, subclinical RV systolic and diastolic dysfunction also developed before changes in LV function [14]. Using two different models of T2D, we show that the RV remodels before and independent of LV dysfunction. Recognition of early RV dysfunction may therefore provide a window for earlier detection and intervention in diabetic heart disease before overt LV impairment becomes apparent. These findings identify the RV as an early and metabolically vulnerable target in T2D and suggest that intrinsic differences in substrate utilization, lipid handling, or metabolic flexibility may underlie divergent ventricular remodeling trajectories in DCM.

RV metabolic mechanisms of DCM have been sparsely investigated and nothing is yet known about changes in FA metabolism in RV DCM. In the LV, increased reliance of the diabetic heart on FA has a negative impact on cardiac function and structure [38], suggesting a direct contribution of cardiac FA metabolism to the development of DCM. One prominent change is the high reliance on FA as a fuel. In the insulin-resistant LV, increases in FA uptake are due to translocation of CD36 to the cell membrane [42, 43]. However, unlike the well-established changes in CD36 expression with diabetes in the LV (Supplemental Fig. 3), we show that the diabetic RV fails to upregulate gene or protein expression of CD36 nor localize CD36 at the membrane (Figs. 3 and 4). Similar to unchanged CD36 expression or localization in the db/db and HFD+STZ diabetic RV, we also found that CD36 gene and protein expression were unchanged or downregulated in the T1D pig RV (Fig. 4). Together, although it is clear that CD36 plays a key role in LV metabolic dysfunction during diabetes, we consistently failed to find upregulation of CD36 in the RV, suggesting CD36-independent FA uptake in the RV. Future work should directly test the requirement for CD36 using ventricle-specific gain and loss of CD36 function. Since we were unable to find evidence of CD36 upregulation in multiple models of diabetes, in an effort to understand how FA enters diabetic RV myocytes, we quantified expression of an alternate receptor, a novel member of the fatty acid transport protein family, FATP6. FATP6 is principally expressed in the heart [44], where it is involved in long-chain FA uptake. FATP6 has been suggested to play a role in the pathogenesis of lipid-related cardiac disorders [44] and gain-of-function of FATP6, by T > A gene polymorphism increases the uptake and utilization of FA [45]. We found that the db/db RV had higher expression of FATP6 compared to WT and higher localization of FATP6 at the membrane in the db/db RV. However, we also quantified FATP6 in the T1D pig membrane and found no differences in expression between T1D and Con in either ventricle (Supplemental Fig. 4). It therefore remains unclear whether FATP6 acts as an alternate FA transporter in the diabetic RV and whether this differs across models of diabetes. Targeted studies of FATP6 in the diabetic RV are warranted, though these studies will be challenging due to lack of a cardiac specific knockout model or selective pharmacological tools. Despite these limitations, further assessment of FATP6 in DCM in the RV is warranted.

Once inside the cell, FA must undergo β-oxidation to produce usable energy. Indeed, in the diabetic LV, increased FA uptake is accompanied by enhanced mitochondrial beta oxidation [38]. While we were unable to directly measure FA oxidation in our models, we report that with respect to the enzymes involved in FA oxidation, increased uptake was not accompanied by expression of genes needed for increased oxidation as evidenced by a decreased expression of ACADl, and unchanged ACADm and ACS1 expression in the diabetic RV (Fig. 3H). To uncover mechanisms underlying the limited metabolic flexibility of the RV under stress, we poisted that it would be helpful to understand FA uptake and oxidation in the healthy LV and RV. Indeed, prior work has established that RV mitochondria operate closer to maximal bioenergetic capacity [46] and have higher rates of FA oxidation compared to the LV [39]. In our hands, in the healthy RV, consistent with higher rates of oxidation and higher expression of FA metabolism genes ACS1, ACADm, and ACADl compared to the LV, we also found enrichment of FA transporters CD36 and FATP6. The healthy RV also had a higher uptake of FA as measured by uptake of BODIPY (Supplemental Fig. 5). Together, we propose that these differences in the healthy ventricles position them to differentially respond to metabolic overload. That is, if the healthy RV has basal higher rates of FA uptake and oxidation, perhaps it has a lower ceiling to increase these rates with metabolic overload. Future work aimed at describing healthy RV and LV differences will likely provide valuable insight into how the ventricles respond to pathological load such as DCM.

In T2D, ectopic lipid accumulation in the heart has been associated with cardiac dysfunction, a process termed lipotoxicity [47]. Although recognized for quite some time that lipids negatively impact LV function in DCM, the mechanisms by which lipotoxicity alterse cardiac function remain incompletely understood [48]. We add to this need to better define lipotoxic mechanisms by demonstrating a striking ventricle-specific remodeling of lipid species, with patterns of lipid species in the diabetic RV that are distinct from the diabetic LV (Fig. 5, Supplemental Fig. 6). Surprisingly, most of the identified lipid species in the T1D RV were downregulated diabetic RV including cardiolipins and ether phospholipids. Cardiolipin plays a central role in mitochondrial metabolism by maintaining the architecture and morphology of mitochondrial membranes and by regulating the activity proteins involved in mitochondrial function [49]. While it is not fully clear what downregulation of cardiolipin in the diabetic RV means, we postulate that there might be a poor response of mitochondria to tolerate metabolic overload. Ether lipids are synthesized through peroxisome-dependent pathways and are highly enriched in cardiac membranes, where they regulate membrane architecture and redox balance, both of which are disrupted in diabetes [50, 51]. Decreased ether lipid synthesis is linked to multiple metabolic abnormalities, whereby ether lipids are known to have protective mechanisms against oxidative stress [52]. While not clear how loss of these species directly impacts RV function, it is accepted that the RV has a lower capacity to handle oxidative stress than the LV [53], thus loss of these species may more severely impact RV function. One of the striking findings from our lipidomics studies is that the lipid species identified in the RV showed virtually no overlap with those in the LV. The lipidomic profiling of the LV in T1D revealed coordinated changes consistent with lipotoxicity contributing to DCM. Enrichment of free FA reflects insulin deficiency driven metabolic inflexibility, wherein reduced glucose utilization shifts substrate reliance toward FA, promoting excessive lipid uptake [54, 55] (Supplemental Fig. 5). The heatmap further revealed elevations in long-chain FA and ceramides, consistent with lipid overload and metabolic inflexibility characteristic of DCM [56]. Therefore, while the expected changes of lipid enrichment occurred in the T1D LV, they failed to be mirrored in the diabetic RV and it remains unclear what role different lipid species play in RV DCM. Future work would aim at elucidating lipidomic changes in diabetic models and acknowledge ventricle specific differences to deepen the understanding of FA fuel handling under metabolic overload.

Conclusions

Our findings establish that the RV remodels distinctly and earlier than the LV through unique mechanisms of FA handling in DCM. Unlike the LV, which relies on CD36 for FA uptake, the diabetic RV upregulates FATP6, yet our early findings suggest the RV fails to proportionally upregulate β-oxidation, leading to lipotoxicity and subsequent early functional changes. Lipidomic profiling further reveals RV-specific lipid species, highlighting chamber-specific vulnerability to metabolic stress. Clinically, early RV dysfunction may serve as a sensitive indicator of DCM, offering a window for intervention before LV impairment develops. By defining the RV as a critical and independent contributor to disease progression, this work advances mechanistic understanding of biventricular DCM and identifies new avenues for early detection and precision therapy. Early remodeling of the RV in diabetes supports incorporating RV assessment into routine cardiac evaluation of diabetic patients, which may improve risk stratification and enable earlier therapeutic targeting.
