Local structure investigations on the defects in metal-organic frameworks
Authors: Xueqian Kong
Published: 2026

Recently, an article by Xu et al. was published in Journal of the American Chemical Society regarding the advanced investigations on the defects in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which elucidates that "defects" in MOFs are not mere flaws but can be virtues [1].

In reticular chemistry, defects were once viewed as synthesis failures. However, this paper reinforces the modern paradigm of "defect engineering." In UiO-66—a zirconium-based MOF prized for its stability—defects primarily manifest as missing linkers or missing clusters [[2], [3], [4]]. These imperfections are vital for three reasons: (1) Chemical reactivity: missing linkers expose coordinatively unsaturated Zr4+ sites, which function as Lewis acid centers for catalysis. (2) Enhanced porosity: missing clusters create mesoporous "super-cages" that facilitate the diffusion of large molecules, overcoming the mass-transport limits of a perfect microporous lattice. (3) Electronic tuning: defects alter the electronic environment of the metal node, allowing it to act as a more reactive support for guest species like metal nanoparticles.

Traditionally, defects in MOFs are analyzed via bulk techniques: powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD); thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and N2 adsorption, which only provide an averaged view and fail to capture the "local" coordination chemistry. In this work, the authors move beyond bulk averages and employ a synergistic combination of two highly sophisticated local-structure probes: multinuclear solid-state NMR (SSNMR) [[5], [6], [7], [8]] and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS).

Specifically, by utilizing a diverse suite of nuclei—1H, 13C, 19F, 35/37Cl, and 17O—the team definitively identified the "capping species" that balance the charge at defect sites. The use of 17O and 35/37Cl NMR is particularly impressive, as these signals are challenging to observe and interpret. By in situ heating XAFS analyses, the authors tracked the Zr K-edge in real-time during thermal activation. This "molecular movie" captured the exact moment the coordination number of zirconium decreases as modulators are shed, providing a dynamic view of how the framework transitions from an "as-made" state to a "catalytically active" state.

The study yields several transformative insights: (1) Thermal fingerprinting—The research establishes that trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) modulators are removed at 320 °C, a critical threshold for "unlocking" the MOF's Lewis acid sites. (2) Structural "breathing"—XAFS revealed that the Zr6 cluster is not rigid; it undergoes subtle contraction and expansion in response to the removal of capping ligands, illustrating the flexible nature of "stable" inorganic nodes. (3) Superior catalyst support—The most practical finding is the integration of Pt nanoparticles into the defective framework. The defects act as electronic promoters and anchoring sites, leading to significantly higher activity and stability in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) compared to defect-free versions.

For researchers working in catalysis and energy storage, this work underscores that the location and type of defect-capping species are just as important as the metal-linker combination itself. This work marks the continuous effort to decipher MOF defects by comprehensive SSNMR [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]] as well as other techniques [[14], [15], [16]]. By mastering the structure of defects, we can finally treat MOF defects as precise chemical functionalities rather than random flaws.
