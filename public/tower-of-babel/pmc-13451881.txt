Skeletal Effects After Miniscrew-Assisted Rapid Palatal Expansion on Sagittal and Vertical Dimensions: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Bintao Xie, Lingling Zhang, Yanqin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluated the skeletal impact of miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) in sagittal and vertical planes. We aimed to clarify how this treatment affects the jaw structure of late adolescent and adult patients.MethodsWe searched electronic databases for relevant literature. Study selection, quality appraisal, and data retrieval were completed by two authors independently. Quantitative data synthesis and heterogeneity assessments were executed using a random-effects model within the Review Manager 5.4 software environment.ResultsEleven studies were included, seven of which provided data for the meta-analysis. Regarding sagittal outcomes, MARPE led to significant increments in SNA (mean: 0.79°; 95% CI: 0.08-1.5; P = .03) and ANB (mean: 1.18°; 95% CI: 0.49-1.87; P = .0008). In contrast, the vertical dimensions remained relatively stable, with SN-MP (1.28°; P = .10) and MP-FH (1.09°; P = .44) failing to reach statistical significance. No substantial deviations were identified across any other parameters evaluated in this study.ConclusionsThe meta-analysis demonstrated that while MARPE treatment produces statistically significant sagittal skeletal changes, the magnitude of these changes is relatively small, suggesting a clinically modest impact on maxillary retrusion and mandibular protrusion. However, the limited availability of data hinders a conclusive determination of its impact on vertical dimensions. Resolving this issue requires high-quality prospective longitudinal trials with larger cohorts.

Introduction

As a prevalent craniofacial skeletal anomaly, maxillary transverse deficiency (MTD) is frequently linked to a broad spectrum of clinical complications and malocclusions. Its presence often correlates with conditions ranging from posterior crossbite and dental crowding to more complex skeletal Class II or III discrepancies.1 Furthermore, research suggests that MTD may play a role in the aetiology of temporomandibular joint issues and respiratory disorders, specifically obstructive sleep apnea.1, 2, 3 Notably, the prevalence of MTD in adults ranges from 10% to 18% across different ethnic populations.4, 5, 6 However, addressing MTD in nongrowing patients is particularly challenging, as the midpalatal suture has matured and ossified, making traditional expanders (such as the Haas expander) ineffective in splitting it.

This problem puzzled orthodontists for many years until miniscrew‑assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) was introduced around 2010, gradually replacing surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE).7 Compared with SARPE, MARPE is more efficient, convenient, and minimally invasive. The MARPE device, modified from traditional expanders, requires only the addition of 2-4 miniscrews that are implanted in the palate under local anaesthesia and connected to the expander. These miniscrews, serving as absolute skeletal anchorage, could withstand the substantial expansion forces and transmit them directly to each half of the maxilla so as to separate the sutures. Moreover, one study demonstrated that MARPE presented more skeletal expansion in a relatively parallel pattern, which means fewer detrimental effects on buccolingual tipping and the alveolar supporting teeth.8

Recently, numerous clinical investigations have concentrated on the outcomes of MARPE treatment on posterior teeth, the craniofacial skeleton, and surrounding soft tissues. These studies have also assessed various expander designs with different skeletal patterns,9 anchorage mechanisms,10 stress distribution, displacement of the craniofacial complex,11 risk factors for treatment effects,12 and changes in upper airway morphology and dynamics,13,14 among others. Additionally, research data have indicated that the success of MARPE is often accompanied by the separation of both the midpalatal suture and the circummaxillary sutures, including the pterygopalatine, zygomaticotemporal, and zygomaticomaxillary sutures.15,16 That is, the skeletal changes induced by MARPE are not only limited to transverse expansion but also involve other dimensional changes, as predicted or observed by finite element analysis and clinical studies.17,18 For instance, Cantarella et al19 found that changes in the interzygomatic width were associated with rotation of the zygomaticomaxillary complex. Current evidence remains insufficient regarding the precise biomechanical displacements of the maxillomandibular complex during skeletal expansion. Consequently, this investigation aimed to consolidate available data via a systematic review and meta-analysis, focusing on the sagittal and vertical skeletal responses following MARPE in both late adolescent and adult populations, in order to make more accurate predictions for clinical decision-making.

Methods

The reporting of this meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement framework.20 For auditability, the protocol was assigned the PROSPERO registry number CRD42024539980 prior to the formal implementation of the study.

Inclusion criteria

The PICOS framework – covering participants, interventions, comparisons, outcomes, and research designs – provided the structural basis for defining our study’s inclusion parameters.

Population: Postpeak patients (including late adolescents and adults) diagnosed with MTD, with an average age of over 13 years.

Intervention: Miniscrew‑assisted (or microimplant-assisted) rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) or maxillary skeletal expansion (MSE) performed without surgery, intermaxillary elastics, facemask treatment, or maxillary protraction.

Comparison: Longitudinal assessments comparing post-treatment outcomes against baseline pretreatment measurements (self-controlled design).

Outcome: Quantitative data reporting skeletal changes in either sagittal or vertical dimensions, assessed through validated cephalometric or 3D imaging parameters.

Study design: Randomized controlled trials, retrospective studies, and cohort studies.

Search strategy

Our comprehensive search strategy was applied to PubMed, EMBASE, EBSCO, and Web of Science. To ensure maximum sensitivity, the search queries were constructed using a step-by-step approach. A wide range of synonymous free-text terms and controlled vocabulary were comprehensively combined, explicitly including variants. Detailed parameters and Boolean logical steps for each database search are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Furthermore, the bibliographies of the ultimately included studies and pertinent review articles were manually scrutinized to ensure the inclusion of any missing relevant records.

Study selection

Literature screening was conducted through a multistage process involving two independent investigators (B.T. Xie and L.L. Zhang). Initially, titles and abstracts were reviewed for potential eligibility. Subsequently, the full-text versions of all candidate articles were retrieved and subjected to a rigorous appraisal against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria specifically targeted nonpeer-reviewed content, including conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and book chapters, as well as case reports, finite element analyses, and systematic reviews. Ultimately, only English-language publications were considered. When opinions differed during the review, the two primary authors reached a resolution through discussion or by consulting a third senior investigator (Y.Q. Lu).

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was utilized by two independent reviewers (B.T. Xie and L.L. Zhang) to synthesize key information from the included studies. The extracted data categories encompassed: (1) bibliographic details (author, year, and region); (2) methodological characteristics (study design and sample size); (3) clinical protocols (appliance design and activation sequences); and (4) measurement specifics (timing and diagnostic modalities). All pretreatment and post-treatment skeletal parameters were meticulously recorded and cross-verified. Quality assessment

To evaluate the risk of bias within the included nonrandomized trials, we utilized the RoBANS (Risk of Bias Assessment tool for Nonrandomized Studies) framework. The appraisal encompassed six critical methodological domains: subject enrolment, the influence of confounders, precision in exposure measurement, the implementation of blinding for outcome evaluation, the completeness of reported data, and the presence of selective reporting practices.21 A high risk of bias in the participant selection domain was assigned to studies failing to implement consecutive recruitment. Specifically, the participant selection domain was deemed high risk in the absence of consecutive recruitment. Regarding confounding variables, the assessment centred on sex, age, and skeletal maturation, particularly cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) and midpalatal suture (MPS) stages. For incomplete outcome data, studies failing to provide a baseline characteristic table were classified as high risk. Two investigators independently appraised the risk of bias, with any discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (Y.Q. Lu).

Data synthesis

Quantitative synthesis was executed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen), contingent upon the availability of sufficient, comparable data. To accommodate anticipated methodological and clinical variations, a random-effects framework was adopted. Statistical inconsistency across trials was quantified via the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Additionally, potential reporting biases were evaluated visually through contour-enhanced funnel plots and statistically validated with Egger’s regression test. To evaluate the robustness of the synthesized results and assess the influence of individual trials on the overall effect size, a sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially omitting one study at a time.

Quality assessment

Methodological appraisal results are presented in Figures 2a and b. A high risk of bias regarding participant selection was prevalent, largely stemming from the implementation of nonconsecutive recruitment strategies for the study cohorts. Four studies conducted by Liao et al,22 Lee et al,27 Song et al,17 and Kim et al25 lacked descriptions of the population’s growth characteristics, including CVM and MPS. They were therefore rated as unclear or high risk. In light of the progressive ossification of bony sutures alongside maturation, age-based risk thresholds were applied: studies enrolling younger cohorts (minimum age < 10 years) were categorized as high risk, whereas those involving older participants (minimum age ≥ 12 years) were designated as unclear risk. Furthermore, regarding the integrity of outcome data, a low-risk rating was assigned to three specific studies,22,26,28 owing to their comprehensive provision of baseline demographic characteristics for the study population. If a study did not display this information, the risk would be rated as high. The proportion of other biases was mainly dependent on the age range of the subjects. Meta-analysis

Figure 3a-f illustrate the skeletal sagittal alterations observed following MARPE. A significant 0.79° mean increment in the SNA value (P = .03; 95% CI: 0.08-1.5) was identified. This result indicates that the maxilla’s sagittal position shifted forward in relation to the anterior cranial base. Conversely, the mandibular sagittal position (SNB) remained relatively stable without statistically significant changes (P = .49). Intriguingly, the ANB angle, which reflected the relative position of the maxilla and mandible, increased remarkably by 1.18° (P = .0008, 95% CI: 0.49-1.87). In terms of linear displacement, no statistically significant alterations were observed for the ANS sagittal distance, Pg-Na Perp, or A-Na Perp, yielding P-values of .89, .83, and .14, respectively. Notably, the meta-analyses for all sagittal skeletal parameters exhibited negligible inconsistency, with a calculated heterogeneity (I2) of 0%. Furthermore, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled sagittal outcomes (Supplementary Table S1). For the ANB angle, the results demonstrated high stability; the sequential exclusion of any single study did not alter the statistical significance or the overall direction of the effect size. Conversely, the sensitivity analysis for the SNA angle revealed potential fragility. The sequential omission of specific individual studies – namely, Choi et al29 and Liao et al22 – resulted in a shift of the P-value above the significance threshold (P = .14 and P = .06, respectively). On the other hand, vertical skeletal responses following MARPE are presented in Figure 4a-e. Concerning mandibular rotation, neither the SN-MP nor the MP-FH angles demonstrated significant deviations from baseline (P = .12 and P = .44, respectively). Discussion

By synthesizing evidence from 11 clinical investigations, this study assessed the sagittal and vertical skeletal impact of MARPE in skeletally mature individuals. Of these, seven articles were specifically incorporated into the quantitative meta-analytical portion of the review. By leveraging data from both cephalometric radiographs and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), our analysis revealed a statistically significant expansion of the SNA and ANB angles (eg, 0.79° for SNA and 1.18° for ANB). Given the diverse patient demographics and treatment protocols across the included studies, these pooled estimates may represent an averaged overall tendency rather than a uniform, reproducible biomechanical response to a standardized MARPE intervention. This cautious approach is further corroborated by our sensitivity analysis, which revealed that the statistical significance of the SNA increment is fragile and dependent on specific high-weight studies (eg, Choi et al 29 and Liao et al22). Similarly, the lack of statistically significant changes in vertical skeletal metrics should not be definitively interpreted as true vertical stability. Given the relatively constrained sample sizes and substantial variability across the available literature, the current evidence may simply be insufficient to detect subtle vertical skeletal effects, necessitating a cautious interpretation of these secondary outcomes.

These findings indicate a subtle yet discernible forward positioning of the maxilla. While these statistical increments are evident, their relatively small magnitude suggests they offer only a modest contribution to the overall sagittal correction. Although numerically modest, this favourable skeletal increment effectively reduces the required magnitude of compensatory tooth movement. Furthermore, MARPE can be strategically combined with adjunctive protocols – such as Class III intermaxillary elastics,32 personalized miniscrew insertion,31 or maxillary lateral wall-osteotomy11 – thereby maximizing the orthopaedic correction in nongrowing patients. Hence, recognizing these subtle skeletal alterations remains clinically valuable, particularly when formulating adult camouflage treatment plans or managing borderline skeletal Class III patients, where even minor skeletal improvements can favourably influence the final occlusal outcome.

Distinct from the work led by Kapetanović et al,33 Bi et al,34 and Zeng et al,35 this study focused on the role of MARPE in inducing skeletal changes in both the maxillary and mandibular regions. For the first time, we intended to emphasize the changes in both sagittal and vertical dimensions through a systematic review. In contrast, the studies mentioned above primarily focused on dental or skeletal width increases, success rates, and the thickness or height of the alveolar bone. In this study, the SNA, SNB, and ANB angles were selected as indications of the relative anteroposterior position of the maxilla to the anterior cranial base, the mandible to the anterior cranial base, and the maxilla to the mandible, respectively. In addition, the movement distances of several maxillo-mandibular landmarks, including pogonion, the anterior nasal spine (ANS), and A point, were brought into the meta-analysis. For vertical position, the mandibular plane (MP/SN or MP/FH), lower anterior facial height (ANS-Me), and palatal plane angle (PP-SN) were used to assess potential effects. On the other hand, several studies assessed outcomes using three-dimensional CBCT data. However, due to inconsistent standard coordinates or the lack of initial coordinate data, some of the data could not be combined, which prevented quantitative analysis from being performed.

With the development of miniscrews and advanced measurement technologies, researchers have gradually recognized the role of MARPE in both effective maxillary transverse expansion and modification of the craniofacial complex, especially in nongrowing patients.16 A retrospective study by Cantarella et al19 revealed the movement pattern of the zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC) in the coronal plane following skeletal expansion. Another study from the same team showed that MARPE treatment could cause horizontal rotation of the ZMC, as evidenced by an increase in the zygomaticotemporal angle.36 Similarly, Alan et al37 found that during MARPE, the ZMC of the frontozygomatic suture undergoes both outward and downward rotation, leading to soft tissue changes, including the protrusion of the nasal tip and the elevations of the upper and lower face.

Furthermore, computational simulations revealed that the outward movement of the maxilla, caused by the separation of palatal sutures, might be accompanied by a forward-outward rotation trend, influenced by both the palatal shape and expander design.18 These clinical studies and numerical simulations suggest that MARPE therapy may lead to repositioning of both the maxilla and mandible. Therefore, we speculate that the increase of SNA and ANB may be attributed to anteroposterior and frontal pyramidal expansion of the maxilla. At the same time, because it is difficult to reconstruct the mandible body in nongrowing patients over a short period and the expansion does not significantly alter the mandibular plane angle, the SNB angle does not show significant changes. Importantly, the broad variation in patient age and skeletal maturity across the included studies – ranging from late adolescence to late adulthood – must be carefully considered when interpreting these relatively small skeletal changes. The structural resistance of the midpalatal and circummaxillary sutures naturally increases with advancing skeletal maturity,12 meaning that the biomechanical responses to MARPE are unlikely to be identical across such a wide age spectrum. However, some studies have found that the supporting teeth tend to tip buccolingually during MARPE, leading to an increase in the occlusal height of the maxillary and mandibular dentition, which may result in mandibular clockwise rotation.33,34 Thus, further clinical trials are needed to resolve these disputes.

There are several limitations in this review. First and foremost, one of the primary drawbacks is that all the studies included utilized uncontrolled before-and-after designs, highlighting the paucity of methodologically rigorous prospective investigations within the current evidence base. Secondly, while there is consensus that MARPE relies on palatal miniscrew as the primary anchorage for the overall design, several factors remain unstandardized, including the use of auxiliary anchorage (none, dental, mucosal, or mixed), expansion activation protocols, as well as the number, location, length, diameter, implant depth, and the number of layers penetrating the cortical bone. Additionally, although our meta-analysis demonstrated a low statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) for sagittal parameters, this mathematical consistency does not necessarily equate to direct clinical comparability among the included cohorts. The wide age ranges and varied or unreported skeletal maturation stages (eg, CVMI or CVM) in several primary studies introduce substantial clinical heterogeneity. Consequently, the reported sagittal and vertical effects in this meta-analysis should not be viewed as absolute, reproducible outcomes guaranteed by any single MARPE design. Rather, they serve as an aggregate reflection of a highly heterogeneous clinical landscape. It remains entirely plausible that distinct appliance designs or specific age groups elicit divergent biomechanical responses, the nuances of which are inevitably obscured when data are pooled. Several outcomes use CBCT-based 3D measures but were not pooled due to inconsistencies. We consider suggesting a future standard protocol or coordinate system to address this limitation. These factors may all affect the final pattern of maxillary and mandibular movement. Furthermore, it entails a holistic evaluation of the entire stomatognathic system, which may include the temporomandibular joint,38 eruption of third molars,39 and even mandibular deviation.40 Therefore, future clinical studies should be designed with greater sophistication to assess the impact of these variables accurately.

Conclusions

The meta-analysis demonstrated that MARPE treatment in late adolescents and adults led to improvements in maxillary retrusion and mandibular protrusion, as evidenced by significant increases in SNA and ANB. However, the mandible and vertical skeletal parameters failed to exhibit any statistically notable alterations, likely due to the limited available data. Future clinical trials, particularly prospective studies with long-term and longitudinal monitoring, remain imperative to investigate further the clinical impact of MARPE in postpeak patients over extended periods.
