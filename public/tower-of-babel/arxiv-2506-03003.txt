Newtonian potentials of Legendre polynomials on rectangles have displacement structure
S. Olver

Abstract
Particular solutions of the Poisson equation can be constructed via Newtonian potentials, integrals involving the corresponding Green’s function which in two-dimensions has a logarithmic singularity. The singularity represents a significant challenge for computing the integrals, which is typically overcome via specially designed quadrature methods involving a large number of evaluations of the function and kernel. We present an attractive alternative: we show that Newtonian potentials (and their gradient) applied to (tensor products of) Legendre polynomials can be expressed in terms of complex integrals which satisfy simple and explicit recurrences that can be utilised to exactly compute singular integrals, i.e. , singular integral quadrature is completely avoided. The inhomogeneous part of the recurrence has low rank structure (its rank is at most three for the Newtonian potential) and hence these recurrences have displacement structure. Using the recurrence directly is a fast approach for evaluation on or near the integration domain that remains accurate for low degree polynomial approximations, while high-precision arithmetic allows accurate use of the approach for moderate degree polynomials.



keywords

Newtonian potential, singular integrals, displacement structure.

Introduction

In this paper we consider the
Newtonian potential
and its gradient over the unit square:

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|f(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath},\qquad\iint_{\Omega}\nabla\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|f(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}

where
\Omega:=\{{{\left({x,y}\right)}^{\top}}:-1\leq x,y\leq 1\}
. Newtonian potentials over rectangles and parallelograms can be expressed explicitly in terms of
{\cal L}
via an affine change of variables.
We allow
x
to be anywhere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, including
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\Omega
. When it is far from
\Omega
the kernel is smooth and traditional quadrature techniques are effective. However, as
x
approaches
\Omega
the kernels become increasingly singular, with weak singularities when
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\Omega
.

Computing such integrals has a long history with many effective quadrature schemes, for example
[
3
,
11
,
5
,
1
,
12
,
21
,
2
]
. We refer the reader for
[
21
]
for a recent overview of existing methodology and a detailed description of their limitations for computing the integrals for
{\bf x}
near or on
\Omega
, due to the nearly singular or singular integrals. In
[
21
]
an approach is advocated for computing the integrals via expansions in a monomial basis, whose conditioning can be controlled up to degree 20. In this paper we investigate a closely related alternative: expand
f
in Legendre polynomials

f(x,y)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{kj}\underbrace{P_{k}(x)P_{j}(y)}_{P_{kj}(x,y)}

and compute the integrals

\iint_{\Omega}\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|P_{kj}(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath},\qquad\iint_{\Omega}\nabla\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|P_{kj}(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}

in closed form, which will be related to a simple recurrence relationship. This recurrence relationship can be solved extremely fast, albeit with some issues with ill-conditioning for large
k
or
j
.

To derive the recurrence relationship we express the Newtonian potential as the real part of a complex logarithmic integral

{\cal L}f(z):=\iint_{\Omega}\log(z-(s+{\rm i}t))f(s,t)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t

and its gradient in terms of the real and imaginary part of the Stieltjes integral

{\cal S}f(z):=\iint_{\Omega}{f(s,t)\over z-(s+{\rm i}t)}\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t.

Thus particular solutions to the Poisson equation with the (real-valued) right-hand side
f(x,y)
are given by

\iint_{\Omega}\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|f(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}={\rm Re}\,{\cal L}f(x+{\rm i}y)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{kj}{\rm Re}\,L_{kj}(x+{\rm i}y).

where
L_{kj}(z):={\cal L}P_{kj}(z)
and we identify
{\bf x}={{\left({x,y}\right)}^{\top}}
with
z=x+{\rm i}y
. The gradient can be related to the Stietljes integral via:

\iint_{\Omega}\nabla\log\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}-\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}\|f(\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}){\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$t$\unboldmath}=\begin{pmatrix}{\rm Re}\,\\
-{\rm Im}\,\end{pmatrix}{\cal S}f(x+{\rm i}y)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{kj}\begin{pmatrix}{\rm Re}\,\\
-{\rm Im}\,\end{pmatrix}S_{kj}(x+{\rm i}y)

where
S_{kj}(z):={\cal S}P_{kj}(z)
. The key observation of this article is that
\{L_{kj}(z)\}
and
\{S_{kj}(z)\}
both solve simple recurrence relationships that can be use to compute them
exactly
.

In particular we show the complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals of Legendre polynomials have
displacement structure
. Define the (infinite) matrices

L(z):=\begin{pmatrix}L_{00}(z)&L_{10}(z)&\cdots\\
L_{10}(z)&L_{11}(z)&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},\qquad S(z):=\begin{pmatrix}S_{00}(z)&S_{10}(z)&\cdots\\
S_{10}(z)&S_{11}(z)&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

We show in Theorem
16
that
L(z)
satisfies a Sylvester equation of the form

CL(z)-{\rm i}L(z)C^{\top}=F(z)

where
F(z)
has at most rank
3
and
C
is tridiagonal.
We further show in Lemma
13
that
S(z)
satisfies a Sylvester equation of the form

(zI-B)S(z)-{{\rm i}}S(z)B^{\top}=4{\bf e}_{0}{\bf e}_{0}^{\top},

that is the right-hand side has rank

and

is also tridiagonal.
Displacement structure is a powerful property with substantial numerical implications, see
[

,

,

,

]
.

The paper is structured as follows:

Section
2
: The methodology we develop parallels singular integrals over intervals, in particular it utilises a simple relationship between complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals. Numerical solution of Stieltjes integrals via recurrence relationships has a long history going back to Gautschi
[

Section

satisfy a simple “5-point stencil” recurrence relationship by adapting the technique from intervals. By mimicking the connection between complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals used on intervals, we show that

simultaneously satisfies two “5-point stencil” recurrence relationships with an explicitly constructed inhomogeneous term. Moreover, we can construct the boundary cases
S_{k0}(z)
,

,

, and

Section

: The recurrences can be recast as Sylvester’s equations and the recurrence relationships for

and

.

Section

and

by direct substitution in the recurrence relationship. This proves to be an effective and fast approach to construction that is exact. However, in practice there are errors due to round-off error so it may only be effective for

Section
6
: We conclude by discussing the implications of displacement structure to both computation of Newtonian potentials and their gradient, as well as the potential for generalising to other kernels, geometries, and higher-dimensions.

2
Log and Stieltjes integrals on intervals

A classic result (cf.
[
10
]
) is that Stieltjes integrals of weighted orthogonal polynomials satisfy the same three-term recurrence as the orthogonal polynomials themselves, apart from a simple inhomogeneity. Defining

{\cal S}_{[-1,1]}f(z):=\int_{-1}^{1}{f(t)\over z-t}{\rm d}t

and
S_{k}(z):={\cal S}_{[-1,1]}P_{k}(z)
, the three-term recurrence for Legendre polynomials (assuming
P_{-1}(x)=0
)

\displaystyle xP_{k}(x)\;

\displaystyle={k\over 2k+1}P_{k-1}(x)+{k+1\over 2k+1}P_{k+1}(x)

tells us the three-term recurrence for their Stieltjes integrals:

\displaystyle zS_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}{z-t\over z-t}P_{k}(t){\rm d}t+\int_{-1}^{1}{tP_{k}(t)\over z-t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=2\delta_{k0}+{k\over 2k+1}S_{k-1}(x)+{k+1\over 2k+1}S_{k+1}(x).

Note we also know the initial condition that can be used to kick-off the recurrence,

S_{0}(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}{{\rm d}t\over z-t}=\log(z+1)-\log(z-1),

thus we can deduce
S_{k}(z)
by forward-substitution with the recurrence.

Now consider the complex logarithmic integral

{\cal L}_{[-1,1]}f(z):=\int_{-1}^{1}\log(z-t)f(t){\rm d}t

and define
L_{k}(z):={\cal L}_{[-1,1]}P_{k}(z)
. We first connect the complex logarithmic integral to the Stieltjes integral of a specific ultraspherical polynomial
[
8
, Section 18.3]
. For
\lambda\neq 0,\lambda>-1/2
, the ultraspherical polynomials
C_{n}^{(\lambda)}(x)
are orthogonal with respect to the weight
(1-x^{2})^{\lambda-1/2}
on
[-1,1]
with the normalisation constant
2^{n}(\lambda)_{n}/n!
where
(\lambda)_{n}=\Gamma(\lambda+n)/\Gamma(\lambda)
is the Pochhammer symbol. The definition can be extended to
\lambda=-1/2
, where the weight is no longer integrables by an explicit expression in terms of Hypergeometric functions
[
8
, 18.5.9]
, or more explicitly by the formula

C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(x)=\begin{cases}1&k=0\\
-x&k=1\\
{(1-x^{2})C_{k-2}^{(3/2)}(x)\over k(k-1)}&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

We will denote the Stieltjes integral of ultraspherical polynomials as

S_{k}^{(\lambda)}(z):=\int_{-1}^{1}{C_{k}^{(\lambda)}(t)\over z-t}{\rm d}t.

Proposition 2
.

\displaystyle L_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle=-S_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(z)+\begin{cases}\log(z+1)+\log(z-1)&k=0\\
0&\hbox{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Integrating by parts allows us to relate the complex logarithmic integral to the Stieltjes integral which is more amenable to computation as:

{\cal L}_{[-1,1]}f(z)=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t){\rm d}t\log(z+1)-\int_{-1}^{1}{F(t)\over z-t}{\rm d}t

where
F(x)=\int_{x}^{1}f(s)\,{\rm d}s
. In the case of Legendre polynomials its indefinite integral is given in terms of certain ultraspherical polynomials
[
8
, 18.9.19]
: for
k>0
,

\int_{x}^{1}P_{k}(t){\rm d}t=C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(x)={(1-x^{2})C_{k-1}^{(3/2)}(x)\over k(k+1)}.

Thus, since
\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(t){\rm d}t=0
when
k\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle L_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{1}{C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(t)\over z-t}{\rm d}t=-S_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(z).

For
k=0
we have, since
C_{1}^{(-1/2)}(x)=-x
,

\int_{x}^{1}P_{0}(t){\rm d}t=1-x=1+C_{1}^{(-1/2)}(x)

thus we also have

L_{0}(z)=2\log(z+1)-S_{1}^{(-1/2)}(z)-\int_{-1}^{1}{1\over z-t}{\rm d}t,

which simplifies to the desired expression.

This can be used to deduce a simple recurrence relationship:

Theorem 3
.

The complex logarithmic integral of Legendre polynomials satisfies a three-term recurrence:

\displaystyle zL_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle={k-1\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(z)+{k+2\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(z)+\lambda_{k}(z)

for

\lambda_{k}(z):=\begin{cases}(z-1)\log(z-1)+(z+1)\log(z+1)&k=0\\
-2/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

We shall deduce the recurrence relationship for
L_{k}(z)
using a technique also advocated in
[
18
,
22
]
. We employ the recurrence relationship for ultraspherical polynomials

tC_{k}^{(\lambda)}(t)={k+2\lambda-1\over 2(k+\lambda)}C_{k-1}^{(\lambda)}(t)+{k+1\over 2(k+\lambda)}C_{k+1}^{(\lambda)}(t).

(1)

which remains valid for
\lambda=-1/2
. Note we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(x){\rm d}x\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\begin{cases}1&k=0\\
-x&k=1\\
{(1-x^{2})C_{k-2}^{(3/2)}(x)\over(k-1)k}&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}{\rm d}x=\begin{cases}2&k=0\\
2/3&k=2\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It follows that

\displaystyle zL_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{1}{z-t\over z-t}C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(t){\rm d}t-\int_{-1}^{1}{t\over z-t}C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{cases}z\log(z+1)+z\log(z-1)&k=0\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{1}{1\over z-t}\left({{k-1\over 2k+1}C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(t)+{k+2\over 2k+1}C_{k+2}^{(-1/2)}(t)}\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{cases}z\log(z+1)+z\log(z-1)&k=0\\
-2/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle={k-1\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(z)+{k+2\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(z).

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{cases}\int_{-1}^{1}{1\over z-t}{\rm d}t+z\log(z+1)+z\log(z-1)&k=0\\
-2/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases},

which simplifies to the desired result.

To extend the results to 2D we will need to work with the following variant where we complexify the integration variable:

{\cal M}_{[-1,1]}f(z):=\int_{-1}^{1}\log(z-{\rm i}t)f(t){\rm d}t

and
M_{k}(z):={\cal M}_{[-1,1]}P_{k}(z)
.
This is a subtle change: writing
z=x+{\rm i}y
, for
-1\leq y\leq 1
and
x<0
the integral passes over the branch cut of the logarithm. We nevertheless can relate this to
{\cal L}
:

Proposition 4
.

For
z=x+{\rm i}y
we have

{\cal M}_{[-1,1]}f(z)={\cal L}_{[-1,1]}f(-{\rm i}z)+{{\rm i}\pi\over 2}\begin{cases}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t){\rm d}t&x>0\hbox{\rm\ or }y\geq 1\\
-3\int_{-1}^{1}f(t){\rm d}t&x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }y\leq-1\\
\left(\int_{-1}^{y}-3\int_{y}^{1}\right)f(t){\rm d}t&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\end{cases}

Proof.

We use the formula

\log({\rm i}z)=\log|z|+{\rm i}\arg({\rm i}z)=\log z+{{\rm i}\pi\over 2}\begin{cases}1&-\pi<\arg z\leq\pi/2\\
-3&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

to deduce

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)\log(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)\log(-{\rm i}z-t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+{{\rm i}\pi\over 2}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)\begin{cases}1&-\pi<\arg(-{\rm i}z-t)\leq\pi/2\\
-3&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}{\rm d}t

The result follows by considering the region where
-{\rm i}z-t=y-t-{\rm i}x
is under the three different cases:

1.

If
x>0
then
{\rm Im}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)=-x<0
and hence
-\pi<\arg(-{\rm i}z-t)<0
, whilst if
y\geq 1
we have
{\rm Re}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)\geq y-1\geq 0
and
0\leq\arg(-{\rm i}z-t)\leq\pi/2
.

2.

If
x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }y<-1
then
{\rm Im}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)=-x\geq 0
and
{\rm Re}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)\leq y+1<0
and hence
\pi/2<\arg(-{\rm i}z-t)\leq\pi
. If
y=-1
the integrand only differs at a single point so its value is unchanged.

3.

If
x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1
then for
t<y
we have
{\rm Re}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)>0
whilst for
t>y
we have
{\rm Re}\,(y-t-{\rm i}x)<0
and hence the integrand changes value at the point
y=t
.

Specialising to Legendre polynomials we have the following:

Theorem 5
.

For
z=x+{\rm i}y
we have

\displaystyle M_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle=L_{k}(-{\rm i}z)+{\rm i}\pi\delta_{k0}\begin{cases}1&x>0\hbox{\rm\ or }y\geq 1\\
-3&x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }y\leq-1\\
-1&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad-2{\rm i}\pi\begin{cases}C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{\rm\ otherwise}\end{cases}.

We can deduce a three-term recurrence for
M_{k}(z)
as well:

Corollary 6
.

For
z=x+{\rm i}y
we have

\displaystyle zM_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}{k-1\over 2k+1}M_{k-1}(z)+{\rm i}{k+2\over 2k+1}M_{k+1}(z)+\mu_{k}(z)

for

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(z)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(z-{\rm i})\log(z-{\rm i})+(z+{\rm i})\log_{-}(z+{\rm i})&k=0\\
-2{\rm i}/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad-\begin{cases}2{\rm i}\pi xC_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)&\hbox{$x<0$\hbox{ and } $-1<y<1$}\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases},

where we define the logarithm with the other limit along its branch cut:

\log_{-}z:=\begin{cases}\log|z|-{\rm i}\pi&y=0\hbox{ and }x<0\\
\log z&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Considering the different cases we find that:

\displaystyle(z-{\rm i})\log(z-{\rm i})+(z+{\rm i})\log_{-}(z+{\rm i})\;

\displaystyle=(z-{\rm i})\log(-{\rm i}z-1)+(z+{\rm i})\log(-{\rm i}z+1)

\displaystyle\qquad+\pi\begin{cases}{\rm i}z&\hbox{$x>0$ or $y\geq 1$ }\\
-3{\rm i}z&\hbox{$x\leq 0$ and $y\leq-1$ }\\
-{\rm i}z-2&\hbox{$x<0$ and $-1<y<1$ }\end{cases}.

For the
k=0
case we thus find, using Theorem
5
,

\displaystyle zM_{0}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}(-{\rm i}zL_{0}(-{\rm i}z))+{\rm i}\pi z\begin{cases}1&x>0\hbox{\rm\ or }y\geq 1\\
-3&x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }y\leq-1\\
2y-1&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=2{\rm i}L_{1}(-{\rm i}z)+(z-{\rm i})\log(-{\rm i}z-1)+(z+{\rm i})\log(-{\rm i}z+1)

\displaystyle\qquad+{\rm i}\pi z\begin{cases}1&x>0\hbox{\rm\ or }y\geq 1\\
-3&x\leq 0\hbox{\rm\ and }y\leq-1\\
2y-1&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=2{\rm i}M_{1}(z)+\pi\begin{cases}2y{\rm i}z+2-4C_{2}^{(-1/2)}(y)&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{\rm\ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad+(z-{\rm i})\log(z-{\rm i})+(z+{\rm i})\log(z+{\rm i}).

This reduces to the result when we observe

2y{\rm i}z+2-4C_{2}^{(-1/2)}(y)=2xy{\rm i}-2y^{2}+2-2(1-y^{2})=2xy{\rm i}.

For
k>0
we have, using the three-term recurrence to expand
yC_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)
,

\displaystyle zM_{k}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}(-{\rm i}zL_{k}(-{\rm i}z))-2{\rm i}\pi z\begin{cases}C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{\rm\ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle={\rm i}{k-1\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(-{\rm i}z)+{\rm i}{k+2\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(-{\rm i}z)-2{\rm i}/3\delta_{k1}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\pi\begin{cases}{k-1\over 2k+1}C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(y)+{k+2\over 2k+1}C_{k+2}^{(-1/2)}(y)&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{\rm\ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad-2{\rm i}\pi x\begin{cases}C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)&x<0\hbox{\rm\ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{\rm\ otherwise}\end{cases}

which reduces to the result via Theorem
5
.

Remark 7
.

The proceeding theorem may not apply for complex floating point numbers due to the existence of a negative 0: since
-{\rm i}(0.0+{\rm i}y)=y-0.0{\rm i}
some implementations of the logarithm may be evaluate as if it is below the branch cut. This can be avoided by specialising the implementation of the logarithm for complex floating point numbers.

3
Complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals on squares

We now turn to deducing recurrence relationships for the two-dimensional integrals
S_{kj}(z)
and
L_{kj}(z)
.

3.1
Stieltjes integrals

We begin with deriving a recurrence relationship for the 2D Stieltjes integral

S_{kj}(z):=\iint_{\Omega}{P_{k}(s)P_{j}(t)\over z-(s+{\rm i}t)}{\rm d}s{\rm d}t.

Proposition 8
.

The Stieltjes integral
S_{kj}(z)
satisfies the “5-point stencil” recurrence relationships:

\displaystyle zS_{kj}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j\over 2j+1}S_{k,j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{j+1\over 2j+1}S_{k,j+1}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad+{k\over 2k+1}S_{k-1,j}(z)+{k+1\over 2k+1}S_{k+1,j}(z)+4\delta_{j0}\delta_{k0}.

Proof.

This follows from the three-term recurrence for
S_{k}(z)
. That is:

\displaystyle zS_{kj}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)(-{\rm i}z)\int_{-1}^{1}{P_{j}(t)\over-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s-t}{\rm d}t{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s)S_{j}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s-{\rm i}\int_{-1}^{1}sP_{k}(s)S_{j}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={j\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)S_{j-1}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s+{j+1\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)S_{j+1}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+2\delta_{j0}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s){\rm d}s-{\rm i}{k\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k-1}(s)S_{j}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\qquad-{\rm i}{k+1\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k+1}(s)S_{j}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j\over 2j+1}S_{k,j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{j+1\over 2j+1}S_{k,j+1}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad+{k\over 2k+1}S_{k-1,j}(z)+{k+1\over 2k+1}S_{k+1,j}(z)+4\delta_{j0}\delta_{k0}.

To use this recurrence in practice we need to know initial conditions. It turns out we can deduce these directly in terms of the one-dimensional singular integrals, which are deducible directly from recurrences:

Proposition 9
.

\displaystyle S_{k0}(z)\;

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}{\rm i}(M_{k}(-1-{\rm i}z)-M_{k}(1-{\rm i}z)),

\displaystyle S_{0j}(z)\;

\displaystyle=M_{j}(z+1)-M_{j}(z-1)

Proof.

We use the fact that we have an explicit form for
S_{0}(z)
:

\displaystyle S_{k0}(z)\;

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)S_{0}(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)\left[{\log(1-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s)-\log(-{\rm i}z+{\rm i}s-1)}\right]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle={\rm i}(-1)^{k}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)\left[{\log(-1-{\rm i}z-{\rm i}s)-\log(1-{\rm i}z-{\rm i}s)}\right]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}{\rm i}(M_{k}(-1-{\rm i}z)-M_{k}(1-{\rm i}z)).

Similarly,

\displaystyle S_{0j}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)S_{0}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)\left[{\log(1+z-{\rm i}t)-\log(z-{\rm i}t-1)}\right]{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=M_{j}(z+1)-M_{j}(z-1).

3.2
Complex logarithmic integral

The key idea in the one-dimensional complex logarithmic integrals was their reduction to a Stieltjes integral which lead to a recurrence relationship. Here we observe that one can deploy this same technique on two-dimensional complex logarithmic integrals.

Theorem 10
.

The complex logarithmic integral of Legendre polynomials satisfies two “5-point stencil”-like recurrence relations:

\displaystyle zL_{kj}\;

\displaystyle=\frac{k-1}{2k+1}L_{k-1,j}+\frac{k+2}{2k+1}L_{k+1,j}+{\rm i}\left(\frac{j}{2j+1}L_{k,j-1}+\frac{j+1}{2j+1}L_{k,j+1}\right)+F_{kj}^{(1)}(z),

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2k+1}L_{k-1,j}+\frac{k+1}{2k+1}L_{k+1,j}+{\rm i}\left(\frac{j-1}{2j+1}L_{k,j-1}+\frac{j+2}{2j+1}L_{k,j+1}\right)+F_{kj}^{(2)}(z),

where we take
L_{-1,j}=L_{k,-1}=0
and have

\displaystyle F_{0j}^{(1)}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}{M_{j+1}(z-1)+M_{j+1}(z+1)-M_{j-1}(z-1)-M_{j-1}(z+1)\over 2j+1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu_{j}(z-1)+\mu_{j}(z+1),

\displaystyle F_{10}^{(1)}(z)\;

\displaystyle=-4/3,

\displaystyle F_{kj}^{(1)}(z)\;

\displaystyle=0\qquad\hbox{ for $k\neq 0$ and $(k,j)\neq(1,0)$},

\displaystyle F_{k0}^{(2)}(z)\;

\displaystyle={L_{k+1}(z-{\rm i})+L_{k+1}(z+{\rm i})-L_{k-1}(z-{\rm i})-L_{k-1}(z+{\rm i})\over 2k+1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda_{k}(z-{\rm i})+\lambda_{k}(z+{\rm i})+\beta_{k0}(z)

\displaystyle F_{01}^{(2)}(z)\;

\displaystyle=-4{\rm i}/3+\beta_{01}(z),

\displaystyle F_{kj}^{(2)}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\beta_{kj}(z)\qquad\hbox{ for $j\neq 0$ and $(k,j)\neq(0,1)$}

where

\displaystyle\beta_{kj}(z)\;

\displaystyle=2{\rm i}\pi C_{j+1}^{(-1/2)}(y)\begin{cases}C_{k+2}^{(-3/2)}(x)/3-\delta_{k0}x+\delta_{k1}/3&z\in\Omega\\
-2x\delta_{k0}+2\delta_{k1}/3&x<-1\hbox{ and }-1<y<1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

For the first recurrence relationship we roughly follow the approach of the two-dimensional Stieltjes integral using the recurrence in Theorem
3
:

\displaystyle zL_{kj}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)z\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)\log(z-(s+{\rm i}t)){\rm d}s{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)(z-{\rm i}t)L_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t+{\rm i}\int_{-1}^{1}tP_{j}(t)L_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle={k-1\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)L_{k-1}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t+{k+2\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)L_{k+1}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+{\rm i}{j\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j-1}(t)L_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t+{\rm i}{j+1\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j+1}(t)L_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)\lambda_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t.

Using Corollary
6
we find

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{j}(t)(z-{\rm i}t)\log(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t=zM_{j}(z)-{\rm i}\int_{-1}^{1}tP_{j}(t)\log(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j-1\over 2j+1}M_{j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{j+2\over 2j+1}M_{j+1}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad-{\rm i}{j\over 2j+1}M_{j-1}(z)-{\rm i}{j+1\over 2j+1}M_{j+1}(z)+\mu_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}{1\over 2j+1}M_{j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{1\over 2j+1}M_{j+1}(z)+\mu_{j}(z).

Hence we can simplify:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{j}(t)\lambda_{k}(z-{\rm i}t){\rm d}t=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)\begin{cases}{(z-{\rm i}t-1)\log(z-{\rm i}t-1)\atop+(z-{\rm i}t+1)\log(z-{\rm i}t+1)}&k=0\\
-2/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{-{\rm i}{1\over 2j+1}\left({M_{j-1}(z-1}\right)+M_{j-1}(z+1))\atop+{\rm i}{1\over 2j+1}(M_{j+1}(z-1)+M_{j+1}(z+1))+(\mu_{j}(z-1)+\mu_{j}(z+1))}&k=0\\
-4/3&k=1\hbox{ and }j=0\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

For the second recurrence we use Corollary
6
to reduce the integral:

\displaystyle zL_{kj}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)z\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}(t)\log(z-(s+{\rm i}t)){\rm d}t{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)(z-s)M_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s+\int_{-1}^{1}sP_{k}(s)M_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j-1\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)M_{j-1}(z-s){\rm d}s+{\rm i}{j+2\over 2j+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)M_{j+1}(z-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\quad+{k\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k-1}(s)M_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s+{k+1\over 2k+1}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k+1}(s)M_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)\mu_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j-1\over 2j+1}L_{k,j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{j+2\over 2j+1}L_{k,j+1}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+{k\over 2k+1}L_{k-1,_{j}}(z)+{k+1\over 2k+1}L_{k+1,_{j}}(z)+\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)\mu_{j}(z-s){\rm d}s

where
\mu_{j}
is defined in Corollary
6
. We thus need only simplify the last integral. Note that

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{k}(s)(z-s)\log(z-s){\rm d}s=zL_{k}(z)-\int_{-1}^{1}sP_{k}(s)\log(z-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle={k-1\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(z)+{k+2\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(z)-{k\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(z)-{k+1\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(z)+\lambda_{k}(z)

\displaystyle={-1\over 2k+1}L_{k-1}(z)+{1\over 2k+1}L_{k+1}(z)+\lambda_{k}(z).

Furthermore, if
-1<x<1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{k}(s)\begin{cases}x-s&x<s\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}{\rm d}s=\int_{x}^{1}P_{k}(s)(x-s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=x(C_{k+1}^{(-1/2)}(x)+\delta_{k0})-{k\over 2k+1}\int_{x}^{1}P_{k-1}(s){\rm d}s-{k+1\over 2k+1}\int_{x}^{1}P_{k+1}(s){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=-{1\over 2k+1}C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(x)+{1\over 2k+1}C_{k+2}^{(-1/2)}(x)+x\delta_{k0}-\delta_{k1}/3.

If
x<-1
then

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{k}(s)\begin{cases}x-s&x<s\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}{\rm d}s=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}(s)(x-s){\rm d}s=\begin{cases}2x&k=0\\
-2/3&k=1\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

These combine to give the desired result.

To kick off the recurrence we also need an explicit form for
L_{00}
:

Proposition 11
.

L_{00}(z)=(1-z)M_{0}(z-1)+{\rm i}M_{1}(z-1)+(1+z)M_{0}(z+1)-{\rm i}M_{1}(z+1)-4.

Proof.

This follows from:

\displaystyle L_{00}(z)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\log(z-(s+{\rm i}t))\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t=\int_{-1}^{1}L_{0}(z-{\rm i}t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\left[(1-z+{\rm i}t)\log(z-{\rm i}t-1)+(1+z-{\rm i}t)\log(z-{\rm i}t+1)-2\right]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=(1-z)M_{0}(z-1)+{\rm i}M_{1}(z-1)+(1+z)M_{0}(z+1)-{\rm i}M_{1}(z+1)-4.

Having two different 5-point stencils allows us to eliminate degrees of freedom. In particular, we can deduce inhomogeneous three-term recurrence relationships that give the first row and column:

Corollary 12
.

\displaystyle zL_{k0}(z)\;

\displaystyle={k-2\over 2k+1}L_{k-1,0}(z)+{k+3\over 2k+1}L_{k+1,0}(z)+2F_{k0}^{(1)}(z)-F_{k0}^{(2)}(z)

\displaystyle zL_{0j}(z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm i}{j-2\over 2j+1}L_{0,j-1}(z)+{\rm i}{j+3\over 2j+1}L_{0,j+1}(z)+2F_{0j}^{(2)}(z)-F_{0j}^{(1)}(z)

Proof.

The first part follows from multiplying the first formula in Theorem
10
by 2 and subtracting the second formula, and the second part follows the other way round.

4
Recasting as a Sylvester equation

We can rewrite the recurrence relationhips as (infinite) Sylvester equations. The Stieltjes integral for Legendre polynomials has displacement structure with rank 1:

Lemma 13
.

The (infinite) matrix
S(z)
satisfies the recurrence

zS(z)=BS(z)+{\rm i}S(z)B^{\top}+4{\bf e}_{0}{\bf e}_{0}^{\top}

for the matrix

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1/3&0&2/3\\
&2/5&0&3/5\\
&&3/7&0&4/7\\
&&&4/9&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

The Logarithmic integral for Legendre polynomials satisfies two Sylvester equations for

F^{(\ell)}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}F_{00}^{(\ell)}(z)&F_{10}^{(\ell)}(z)&\cdots\\
F_{10}^{(\ell)}(z)&F_{11}^{(\ell)}(z)&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}:

Lemma 14
.

\displaystyle zL(z)\;

\displaystyle=AL(z)+{\rm i}L(z)B^{\top}+F^{(1)}(z),

\displaystyle zL(z)\;

\displaystyle=BL(z)+{\rm i}L(z)A^{\top}+F^{(2)}(z)

where
B
was defined in Lemma
13
and

\displaystyle A\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
0&0&1\\
&1/5&0&4/5\\
&&2/7&0&5/7\\
&&&3/9&0&\ddots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 15
.

Note that
B
is the multication-by-
x
matrix for Legendre polynomials and
A
is the principle subsection of the multiplication-by-
x
matrix for the ultraspherical polynomials
C_{k}^{(-1/2)}(x)
.

We can combine these two recurrences to deduce a single recurrence where the entire
z
dependence is in the right-hand side:

Theorem 16
.

CL(z)-{\rm i}L(z)C^{\top}=F(z)

where
F(z):=F^{(2)}(z)-F^{(1)}(z)
has at most rank 3 and

C:=A-B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1/3&0&1/3\\
&-1/5&0&1/5\\
&&-1/7&0&\ddots\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The form of the Sylvester equation follows from subtracting the Sylvester equations in the preceding lemma. The fact that
F(z)
has rank 3 follows since dropping the first row and column we find, for
z\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle F(z)[2:\infty,2:\infty]\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{11}(z)&\beta_{12}(z)&\cdots\\
\beta_{21}(z)&\beta_{22}(z)&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=2{\rm i}\pi\begin{pmatrix}C_{3}^{(-3/2)}(x)+1/3\\
C_{4}^{(-3/2)}(x)\\
C_{5}^{-3/2}(x)\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_{2}^{(-1/2)}(y)&C_{3}^{(-1/2)}(y)&\cdots\end{pmatrix}

which has rank 1 (and similarly for
z\notin\Omega
).

6
Conclusion

We have shown that complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals of Legendre polynomials, whose real and imaginary parts contain the Newtonian potential and its gradient, have displacement structure. This leeds to an extremely fast approach for evaluating Newtonian potentials on and near the square, though there are issues with round-off error for large orders.

There are a number of directions for which this result may be useful in real-world computations. One may combine the recurrence-based approach for evaluating Newtonian potentials on and near the square with quadrature-based techniques away from the square, though the precise switch-over point requires further investigation. When combined with
h
-refinement this would result in an effective, fast, and parallelisable approach for computing Newtonian potentials.

An arguably more interesting direction would be utilisation of the displacement structure for faster computations. The structure of our system is very similar to that of the Poisson equation as discretised in
[
9
]
, which was solved efficiently with the Alternative Direction Implicit method. There is also a recent algorithm which avoids inversion
[
4
]
which may also apply to our setting. A hiccup in utilising this result is that whilst our matrices have displacement structure, the right-hand side of the Sylvester equation does not decay rapidly for
z
in the square hence truncation will result in large errors: we need to work with the recurrence in an infinite-dimensional way.
Another alternative is to use mixed precision computation which was introduced for computing an operator arising in fractional calculus which had displacement structure in
[
19
]
. These potential approaches for stable computation would be significantly slower than the unstable direct recurrence, however.

Displacement structure also has implications in terms of utilising the matrices in further computation. It is known that we can perform fast matrix-vector operations
[
15
]
and we further have precise bounds in the decay of the singular values
[
6
,
7
]
. This latter result tells us that we in principle do not need to compute the full matrix, but rather can approximate it by a low-rank matrix which may lead to much more efficient evaluation of Newtonian potentials.

In terms of generalisation of the result we suspect that complex logarithmic and Stieltjes integrals applied to orthogonal polynomials on triangles and other simple two-dimensional geometries have displacement structure. It seems likely there is also displacement structure in higher dimensions, however, the derivation based on complex analysis and Stieltjes integrals is unlikely to generalise. Recent results on computing power law integrals of orthogonal polynomials on intervals
[
13
]
and balls
[
14
]
may facilitate generalisation of displacement structure results, in particular to power law integrals on squares and cubes.
