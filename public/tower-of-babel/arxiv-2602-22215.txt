Graph Your Way to Inspiration: Integrating Co-Author Graphs with Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Model Based Scientific Idea Generation
Pengzhen Xie, Huizhi Liang

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate potential in the field of scientific idea generation. However, the generated results often lack controllable academic context and traceable inspiration pathways. To bridge this gap, this paper proposes a scientific idea generation system called GYWI, which combines author knowledge graphs with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to form an external knowledge base to provide controllable context and trace of inspiration path for LLMs to generate new scientific ideas. We first propose an author-centered knowledge graph construction method and inspiration source sampling algorithms to construct external knowledge base. Then, we propose a hybrid retrieval mechanism that is composed of both RAG and GraphRAG to retrieve content with both depth and breadth knowledge. It forms a hybrid context. Thirdly, we propose a Prompt optimization strategy incorporating reinforcement learning principles to automatically guide LLMs optimizing the results based on the hybrid context. To evaluate the proposed approaches, we constructed an evaluation dataset based on arXiv (2018-2023). This paper also develops a comprehensive evaluation method including empirical automatic assessment in multiple-choice question task, LLM-based scoring, human evaluation, and semantic space visualization analysis. The generated ideas are evaluated from the following five dimensions: novelty, feasibility, clarity, relevance, and significance. We conducted experiments on different LLMs including GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-8B, and Gemini 2.5. Experimental results show that GYWI significantly outperforms mainstream LLMs in multiple metrics such as novelty, reliability, and relevance.



I
Introduction

In the scientific research process, proposing innovative research ideas constitutes a crucial stage. Traditionally, researchers have often required extensive reading of cutting-edge literature and in-depth discussions with relevant colleagues to identify new research directions. However, this process demands considerable time and effort, particularly in fields such as artificial intelligence and medicine where information evolves rapidly and broad knowledge is essential. As knowledge expands and updates at scale, human factors—including cognitive bias and limited prior knowledge—further constrain the ideation process. Consequently, generating forward-thinking and feasible ideas has become increasingly challenging.

With the rapid advancement of large language models, LLMs have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in literature reviews, knowledge extraction, and question-answering tasks; for instance, solving mathematical problems
[
12
,
18
]
, assisting psychological research
[
3
]
, and recommending literature relevant to research directions. With the further refinement of large language models over the past two years and their tighter integration with retrieval techniques, researchers have begun employing LLMs as primary tools for scientific inquiry, marking their emergence as practical instruments within scientific research
[
10
,
9
]
.

However, applying LLMs to scientific idea generation remains challenging. As LLM outputs primarily rely on pre-training data, they often exhibit semantic fluency yet suffer from unverifiable origins and opaque innovation pathways, while remaining susceptible to “hallucinations”
[
7
,
14
,
16
]
. Consequently, generated research ideas frequently lack controllable academic context and traceable inspiration pathways. These shortcomings limit the credibility and practical value of LLM-generated ideas within scientific contexts that prioritize trustworthiness, traceability, and reproducibility.

To mitigate these issues, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
[
6
,
4
]
and Microsoft’s GraphRAG
[
5
,
8
]
provide LLMs with contextual evidence retrieved from external high-quality knowledge bases, which significantly reduces hallucinations and enhances factual substantiation. Related graph-augmented approaches to query-focused summarisation further underline the value of structured evidence for controllable generation. However, simulating the
origins of inspiration
and the
pathways of insight
remains inadequate.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes
GYWI
, a hybrid LLM framework for generating research ideas. The system aims to simulate the process of scientific inspiration, with the task objective of automatically generating novel research ideas based on a given target paper. It comprises the following three key components:

1) Author-centered Knowledge Graph and Inspiration Source Sampling
: Constructs an author knowledge graph based on co-authorship relationships, thematic associations, and community structures (author–paper perspective), drawing on academic graph resources; and designs sampling algorithms utilising neighbouring hops (
exploitation
) and random hops (
exploration
) to form an inspiration-source collection.

2) Hybrid Retrieval via RAG and GraphRAG
: Employs a “depth and breadth” hybrid retrieval mechanism: RAG supplies fine-grained local evidence, while GraphRAG contributes graph-level global connections (entities as nodes, relations as edges), generating the contextual framework.

3) Prompt optimization Strategy
Based on Reinforcement Learning Principles: Automatically fine-tunes generation strategies within the hybrid context to optimise use of retrieved evidence, normalise output structure, and enhance the relevance and reliability of generated ideas (in line with recent progress in reasoning-oriented prompting and multi-step idea development).

For evaluation, we constructed a benchmark dataset based on arXiv papers from 2018–2023 to support multi-dimensional assessment (automated evaluation of The Idea Multiple-Choice Evaluation (IMCQ) objective questions, LLM-based scoring, human evaluation, and semantic-space visualization).
The main contributions of this paper are:

•

author-centered knowledge modeling for controllable and traceable ideation: We propose an external author-centered knowledge graph combined with an inspiration-source sampling algorithm, integrated with retrieval-augmented generation techniques to generate research ideas under a controllable context with traceable inspiration paths, thereby enhancing scientific credibility and traceability.

•

Prompt optimization with reinforcement learning principles: We introduce a prompt-optimization strategy that guides LLMs to perform standardised processing—such as structural adjustment and information filtering—over retrieved content, improving the effectiveness of retrieval-augmented generation for scientific idea generation tasks.

•

Evaluation dataset and framework: We construct an arXiv-based assessment dataset (2018–2023) and a comprehensive evaluation methodology encompassing subjective human assessment, automated LLM scoring, and objective multiple-choice (IMCQ) evaluation.

II
Related Work

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, the use of artificial intelligence to assist scientific research tasks has become a popular research direction. In the early years, researchers began to use knowledge graphs to construct large-scale academic association networks, such as ArnetMiner
[
17
]
and the subsequent MAG
[
11
]
and OAG
[
13
]
proposed by Microsoft, providing researchers with comprehensive information retrieval tools. However, these efforts mainly focused on indexing and retrieval, and did not directly involve the process of generating scientific research ideas.

With the development of LLMs in recent years, researchers have begun to explore the possibility of using LLMs as AI scientists. For example, Lu et al.
[
10
]
discussed the possibility of using LLMs as AI scientists. Similarly, the works of Baek et al.
[
1
]
, Li et al.
[
9
]
and Radensky et al.
[
15
]
also used LLMs to generate scientific research ideas through multi-round dialogue and iterative feedback. However, these methods generally have two limitations. First, they lack academic factual support, making them prone to ‘hallucinations’ and resulting in content with insufficient credibility. Second, they cannot incorporate deep academic connections during the generation process, making it difficult to simulate the ‘flash of inspiration’ process that researchers experience in real-world scenarios, and the generated content often lacks novelty and academic relevance. Addressing these issues, it is crucial to systematically evaluate and fundamentally improve the quality of generated ideas. From early manual evaluations
[
16
]
to strategies combining LLM-assisted assessments, representative work such as AI Idea Bench
[
14
]
has designed multi-dimensional subjective and objective evaluation methods to systematically measure the quality of generated ideas. Despite the increasing maturity of evaluation methods, the fundamental issues on the generation side remain unresolved.

LLM-based idea generation often lacks controllable academic context and traceable inspiration pathways. Existing research also indicates that current studies have not systematically integrated the structured knowledge of academic networks with the generative capabilities of large language models (LLMs), resulting in generated research ideas that lack deep academic connections and show shortcomings in innovation, relevance, and factual grounding. To bridge this gap, this paper proposes the GYWI system, which builds an external author-centered knowledge graph with inspiration-source sampling, forms a hybrid context via a depth and breadth retrieval mechanism (RAG + GraphRAG), and applies a prompt optimization strategy to guide the LLM. The system aims to enhance the academic relevance, innovation, and feasibility of generated ideas, and validates its effectiveness through a multi-dimensional evaluation method.

Knowledge Graph Construction

To simulate the propagation paths of academic inspiration, we constructed an author-centered knowledge graph. In reality, most highly productive authors typically collaborate with a stable group of researchers on similar topics, publishing multiple papers together and forming research communities. Inspiration Source Sampling Algorithm

In reality, inspiration often involves both exploitation and exploration, meaning that academic inspiration may come from highly related academic articles or ideas, or it may come from any knowledge point—even if that knowledge point appears to be unrelated to the target. To simulate this phenomenon, we designed an
inspiration association
and
inspiration jump
sampling algorithm to provide knowledge sources for subsequent inspiration generation. The algorithm primarily consists of two parts:

•

Adjacent Jump Point Sampling:
This part focuses on in-depth exploration of related knowledge points. The algorithm identifies N-order author groups adjacent to
p_{\text{target}}
in the knowledge graph
G
, then samples from these authors’ paper collections to obtain content closely related to the target topic.

•

Random Jump Point Sampling:
This component simulates the ‘eureka moment,’ which is a moment of inspiration. The algorithm randomly samples from the entire paper collection in the knowledge graph, introducing diverse or even seemingly unrelated topics to provide possibilities for stimulating LLM associations.

III-B

Hybrid Retrieval for Depth and Breadth

Based on the inspiration nodes extracted above, we propose a hybrid retrieval strategy that combines RAG and GraphRAG to extract the academic knowledge we need.

•

Depth Content Retrieval via RAG:
Based on the sampling algorithm mentioned above, we perform precise semantic retrieval on relevant nodes in the graph, providing the LLM with the necessary, fine-grained ‘factual fragments’ and ‘innovative fragments’ required for idea generation, thereby enhancing the depth of the input content to the LLM.

•

Breadth Content Retrieval via GraphRAG:
In addition to RAG, we employ GraphRAG technology to further enhance the structure and diversity of generated content. We no longer rely on sampling algorithms but directly use GraphRAG to capture the macro-level structure of the academic network and core relationships between papers through paper titles, generating academic reports based on target papers. This effectively addresses the limitation of RAG, which struggles to retrieve structural and entity relationships between documents in generated content, thereby enhancing the breadth of input content for the LLM.

III-C

Adaptive Prompt Optimization for Idea Generation

To enable LLM to effectively utilize the complex information retrieved through hybrid retrieval, we designed a prompt optimization strategy based on reinforcement learning principles. We found that simple prompt templates used in the past tended to overload the system with information after retrieving complex information through hybrid retrieval, making it difficult to focus. We use a
Prompt_Optimizer
constructed by the LLM to automatically optimise prompts, enabling the LLM to better adapt to the complexity of hybrid retrieval information.

III-C
1
Prompt Optimization Strategy

We optimize the prompt template
P
using LLM-generated scores in five dimensions:

\mathbf{S}(P)=\bigl[s_{\text{novelty}},\;s_{\text{feasibility}},\;s_{\text{clarity}},\;s_{\text{relevance}},\;s_{\text{significance}}\bigr],

and maximize the average score

P^{*}=\arg\max_{P}\;\bar{S}(P),\qquad\bar{S}(P)=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}s_{i}.

To ensure robust evaluation, in each iteration we sample
N_{\text{topic}}
topics and reuse each prompt
R
times; the total number of evaluations is

N_{\text{eval}}=N_{\text{topic}}\times R.

We estimate performance using the sample mean
\bar{s}
and standard error

\mathrm{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{N_{\text{eval}}}},\qquad\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha}=\bar{s}\pm t^{\,1-\alpha/2}_{\,N_{\text{eval}}-1}\cdot\mathrm{SE},

where
s
is the sample standard deviation and
t^{\,1-\alpha/2}_{\,\nu}
is the critical value of the Student’s
t
-distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom. A prompt is updated only if the observed improvement over the current best is statistically significant at level
\alpha
.

In our experiments we set
N_{\text{topic}}=10
,
R=5
, and
\alpha=0.05
. These values are evaluation settings for statistical precision and do not constitute model hyperparameters.

The
Prompt_Optimizer
uses previous prompts, their scores, and structural constraints (e.g., XML format) to refine both the task description and retrieved content. This iterative update process is analogous to policy optimization in reinforcement learning, where the prompt acts as the policy, modifications as actions, and scores as rewards.

Ultimately, we run iterative prompt optimization for 5–10 rounds and stop when the average LLM score no longer improves for two consecutive rounds. Compared with the original prompt (single-document, fixed schema, no explicit ties to retrieved evidence), the optimised prompt consumes a two-layer context (GraphRAG summary plus selected RAG chunks), requires synergy or gap-filling, and adds explicit quality criteria and provenance-aware fields, enabling the LLM to organize and use evidence more effectively. Evaluation Method

To assess the effectiveness of GYWI in generating research ideas, we designed four types of experiments: matching evaluation, LLM-based scoring, human scoring, and semantic space visualization analysis. Additionally, we selected 99 target papers randomly sampled from our 1,000-paper evaluation corpus, and asked four models—GPT–4o, DeepSeek–V3, Qwen3–8B, and Gemini 2.5 Flash—to generate ideas for them, comparing the results with our system.

IV-A
2
Multi-dimensional Scoring Assessment

To assess the overall quality of research ideas generated, we employ a comprehensive scoring system based on five dimensions: novelty, feasibility, clarity, relevance, and significance. Each dimension is scored on a scale from 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest).

LLM-based scoring:
We utilise DeepSeek-V3 for LLM automated scoring, conducting blind evaluations of research ideas from five systems using standardised scoring prompts.

Human scoring:
We used the same method to invite five graduate students from our university, who have relevant domain backgrounds, participated in a blind evaluation. They possess relevant domain knowledge and are capable of assessing the generated research ideas. To ensure a fair and unbiased assessment, the evaluation was conducted in a blind manner, where the identity of the source model for each generated idea was withheld from the evaluators. Details are provided in Appendix
A

For both evaluation methods, the final score is the average of the scores across all dimensions, effectively assessing the system’s overall quality and performance in generating research ideas.

V
Conclusion

This paper proposes GYWI, a system for generating research ideas featuring “controllable context” and “traceable inspiration pathways”. It first constructs an author-centered knowledge graph, forming an external knowledge base through adjacent and random jump point sampling. Subsequently, it employs a hybrid “depth and breadth” retrieval approach to form the context: RAG provides fine-grained content, and GraphRAG, by organising knowledge as a graph (entity nodes, relation edges), supplies structured global connections (causal, hierarchical, co-occurrence) across the knowledge graph.Finally, a prompt optimization strategy based on reinforcement learning principles performs structured abstraction and reconstruction of retrieved content, enabling the model to better leverage relevant information. This facilitates LLM generation of research ideas with enhanced novelty, relevance, and academic feasibility. To validate effectiveness, we constructed an evaluation dataset based on arXiv papers from 2018–2023. Comprehensive assessment employed automated IMCQ objective scoring, LLM performance metrics, human evaluation, and semantic space visualization. Future work may explore incremental and distributed graph construction to improve scalability,
along with online updating mechanisms to enhance adaptability to emerging research trends and real-time data integration.
Moreover, although preliminary experiments demonstrate GYWI’s effectiveness within specific scientific domains,
its cross-domain generalization remains limited.
Future research could extend GYWI by enabling retrieval and fusion across heterogeneous and domain-specific knowledge graphs,

HierVL: Learning Hierarchical Video-Language Embeddings

Abstract:

Video-language embeddings are a promising avenue for injecting semantics into visual representations, but existing methods capture only short-term associations between seconds-long video clips and their accompanying text. We propose HierVL, a novel hierarchical video-language embedding that simultaneously accounts for both long-term and short-term associations. As training data, we take videos accompanied by timestamped text descriptions of human actions, together with a high-level text summary of the activity throughout the long video (as are available in Ego4D). We introduce a hierarchical contrastive training objective that encourages text-visual alignment at both the clip level and video level. While the clip-level constraints use the step-by-step descriptions to capture what is happening in that instant, the video-level constraints use the summary text to capture why it is happening, i.e., the broader context for the activity and the intent of the actor. Our hierarchical scheme yields a clip representation that outperforms its single-level counterpart as well as a long-term video representation that achieves state-of-the-art results on tasks requiring long-term video modeling. HierVL successfully transfers to multiple challenging downstream tasks (in EPIC-KITCHENS-100, Charades-Ego, HowTo100M) in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings.
