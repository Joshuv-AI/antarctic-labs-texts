Dietary Intake and Quality in Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Comparative Study With Controls and the Association With Symptom Severity
Authors: Sanna Nybacka, Esther Colomier, Jóhann Páll Hreinsson, Stine Störsrud, Jan Tack, Hans Törnblom, Magnus Simrén
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) often attribute the onset or worsening of gastrointestinal symptoms to intake of food. Hence, to alleviate symptoms, patients with IBS may avoid triggering foods, potentially impacting their dietary intake and diet quality. This study aimed to compare the habitual diet intake and quality of patients with IBS with controls and to explore the association between dietary habits and symptoms in patients with IBS.METHODS:Patients with IBS were included in 4 clinical studies reporting habitual dietary intakes at baseline. Age- and sex-matched controls representing the general population were derived from the Swedish population-based Riksmaten study. Dietary intakes were assessed using 4-day food diaries. Diet quality was measured using the diet quality index-Swedish national dietary guidelines (DQI-SNR), and diet diversity was scored based on the variety of food groups consumed.RESULTS:The study included 646 patients with IBS and 646 controls (38 ± 14 years, 77% female). Both groups adhered to Nordic nutrition recommendations for macronutrients, except patients consumed fewer carbohydrates. Patients reported eating less carbohydrates, coffee, and dairy products and more fats, lactose-free dairy products, and nuts and seeds compared with controls. Fewer patients had a good diet quality according to the DQI-SNR. In patients, symptom severity and gastrointestinal-specific anxiety were associated with reduced energy and carbohydrate intake, lower diet diversity, and worse diet quality. Poor diet quality was associated with younger age, more severe IBS symptoms, anxiety, and depression.DISCUSSION:Patients with IBS exhibit different dietary patterns compared with controls, with poorer dietary habits linked to more severe symptoms. Understanding food-symptom associations may enhance the optimization and personalization of dietary management for patients with IBS.

INTRODUCTION

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is one of the most prevalent disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI) affecting approximately 4.1% of the global adult population (1). According to the Rome IV diagnostic criteria, IBS is defined by recurrent abdominal pain associated with altered bowel habits (2). Other coexisting symptoms commonly reported by the patients include bloating, abdominal distention, and flatulence. Notably, more than half of the patients with IBS report symptoms to be associated with food intake, underscoring the importance of dietary factors in the multifactorial pathophysiology (3).

The link between food and IBS symptoms has prompted the development of dietary interventions as a key management strategy for the condition (4). However, the effectiveness of various dietary approaches exhibits considerable variability, with between 20% and 58% of patients remaining nonresponsive to dietary treatments (5–8). This variability underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between diet and symptoms in IBS. Complicating matters further, studies on specific food triggers in IBS often yield conflicting results, aside from the well-established impact of fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols (FODMAP)-rich foods (9). Given that responses to dietary treatments can differ widely between individuals, many patients do not experience sufficient symptom relief and may resort to self-management strategies. This can lead to excessive dietary restrictions and impairment in diet quality. Patients with IBS who engage in severe self-restriction and food avoidance may represent a distinct and more severe IBS subgroup characterized by a more severe overall symptom pattern and reduced quality of life (10).

Previous investigations into the habitual dietary patterns of patients with IBS have also yielded inconsistent results, with some studies reporting higher fat intake (11,12) or higher protein intake (13) in patients compared with controls, whereas some studies found no differences in macronutrient intakes (14,15). A meta-analysis showed that reported intake of dietary fiber was lower in patients with IBS vs controls, with high heterogeneity (16). However, data on overall diet quality among patients with IBS remain limited. Emerging evidence suggests a potential association between poorer diet quality and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (12,17,18). However, this should be confirmed in larger studies with comparable control samples.

In light of these considerations, our study aimed to comprehensively assess and compare the habitual diet quality and dietary intake of patients with IBS with general population controls. Specifically, within the IBS cohort, we sought to explore associations between dietary habits and symptoms and to characterize patients exhibiting limited dietary diversity or low diet quality. We hypothesized that poorer diet quality, and a diet lacking diversity, would be associated with more severe IBS symptoms.

METHODS

Participants

In this study, dietary data of individuals diagnosed with IBS assembled from 4 different studies were pooled. Data were collected as part of 2 observational cohort studies in 2010–2020 (cohort 1–2) (19,20) and 2 randomized controlled trials in 2013–2022 (cohort 3–4) (5,6,21). Patients were enrolled at the gastroenterology outpatient clinic of Sahlgrenska University Hospital, Gothenburg, or were recruited on social media platforms. In cohort 3, patients could also be recruited at the Karolinska University Hospital, Stockholm; and Sabbatsbergs Hospital, Stockholm. Patients included before 2016 were diagnosed with IBS according to the Rome III criteria (22) and after 2016 according to the Rome IV criteria (23). Classification into IBS subtypes was performed by the assessment of bowel movements based on the Bristol Stool Form scale (24). Patients with major psychiatric, liver, neurological, or cardiovascular diseases, as well as celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, known food allergies, or other severe GI diseases that could explain GI symptoms, were not eligible. Being pregnant or lactating and having a body mass index <18 kg/m2 or >35 kg/m2 were also part of the exclusion criteria. In cohort 4, participants who adhered to a special diet, e.g., vegan, gluten-free diet, or low FODMAP diet, were excluded. All patients provided written and verbal informed consent in the respective studies. All studies were approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Gothenburg, Sweden, and the 2 clinical trials were registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT02107625 and NCT02970591.

The dietary data for the control sample, matched for age (±3 years) and sex, were obtained from the Swedish national dietary survey in adults, the Riksmaten 2010‒2011 study. This study, conducted by the Swedish Food Agency, has been described in detail elsewhere (25), and the data from the survey have been made publicly available. In summary, the Riksmaten study collected dietary information from 1,797 individuals representing the adult Swedish general population. Participants were selected by Statistics Sweden (SCB) through random sampling from the SCB population register. The randomization process was stratified by gender, age, and geographic location (south, central, and northern Sweden). Initially, 5,003 individuals were invited by post, followed by more detailed information provided by SCB interviewers through phone calls. Those who consented to participate received additional information both by mail and during subsequent phone conversations. The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Uppsala, and all participants provided oral informed consent before enrollment.

Dietary intake

Patients with IBS recorded their dietary intake with 4-day food diaries from Wednesday to Saturday (cohort 1–3) or during the last 4 days of the study screening period (cohort 4). Patients were instructed to consume their habitual diet and record all foods and their amount as accurately as possible in grams or household measures. Data were entered by trained dietitians from the food records into an extended version of the software Dietist XP 3.1, linked to the Swedish food composition database from the Swedish Food Agency (26). This software has a FODMAP database add-on (27), allowing the calculation of the average daily FODMAP intake, excess fructose (average daily fructose subtracted with the glucose intake), galacto-oligosaccharides, fructans, polyols, and lactose.

Controls could record their 4-day food intake either through interview by phone or through a web-based platform into a software called the Riksmaten method (version 04.1), which is linked to the Swedish food composition database. This software enables automated estimations of nutrients and energy. Controls received similar instructions as the patients on how to record their food intake. The start day of the food diary was randomized (Tuesday, Wednesday, Saturday, or Sunday), leading to an equal distribution of weekdays and weekend days. The food composition table from the Swedish Food Agency does not contain information regarding FODMAP content; thus, we were not able to estimate FODMAP intake among the controls. Average daily nutrient intakes were calculated and presented both as crude mean intakes and used as energy-adjusted intakes either by transforming the mean daily intakes in grams to grams per 1,000 kcal or by expressing intakes by their energy percentage (E%), i.e., the percentage of energy derived from the different macronutrients.

All consumed food items were classified into food groups based on a system composed by the Swedish Food Agency, including alcohol, bread, butter, pasta/grains/cereal, coffee, condiments/spices/pickles, dairy products, drinks, eggs, fast food, fish, fruit, juice, lactose-free dairy products, legumes, margarine, meat, mixed dishes, nutritional supplements, nuts and seeds, oil, potatoes, salty snacks, sweet dishes and desserts, tea, vegetarian dishes, and vegetables (25). Average daily intakes of each food group in grams were calculated. Participants who reported unreliable habitual energy intakes, i.e., below 800 kcal or above 4,500 kcal, were excluded. Food group variables were energy-adjusted by transforming the mean daily intake in grams to grams per 1,000 kcal.

Diet quality

A diet index by Drake et al was applied, the diet quality index-Swedish national dietary guidelines (DQI-SNR) (28) to assess the adherence to the Swedish national dietary guidelines based on the Nordic nutrition recommendations. It considered intakes of saturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids, fish, fiber, fruit and vegetables, and sucrose. Adherence scores per component were one (adherent) or zero (nonadherent), summing to a total score of 0 to 6, with 6 indicating excellent adherence (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/AJG/D497). Participants were categorized as having good (4–6), moderate (2–3), or poor (0–1) diet quality.

Diet diversity score

An in-house diet diversity score was used to assess the number of different food groups consumed over the recording days. The original 27 food groups, as previously mentioned, were modified by merging the categories for dairy and lactose-free dairy products, as well as butter and margarine, resulting in a total of 25 food groups. If a participant had reported consuming any food item within a food group, i.e., if the average 4-day intake of a food group was >0 g, a score of 1 was assigned for that particular food group. This resulted in a total score ranging from 0 to 25, with lower scores indicating a more limited, monotonous food intake, whereas higher scores indicate greater diet diversity.

IBS symptom questionnaires

The following questionnaires were completed by participants with IBS within 0–5 days after completing the 4-day food records.

GI symptoms.

IBS symptom severity was assessed using the IBS Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS) measuring the disease burden of IBS, which resulted in a score from 0 (no symptoms) to 500 (29). The 13-item IBS-specific GI symptom rating scale (GSRS-IBS) assessed the severity of different IBS symptoms with a 7-day recall period (30). Higher scores indicated more severe symptoms.

Non-GI symptoms.

Non-GI somatic symptoms were measured using the patient health questionnaire-12 (PHQ-12), which is a modified version of PHQ-15 subtracted with the 3 GI symptoms included (31,32). The 12 items assessed symptoms on a scale from 0 (not bothered at all) to 2 (bothered a lot), with a sum score from 0 to 24 for women and 0 to 22 for men because of an item about menstrual pains.

The hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS), a 14-item self-report screening scale, was used to measure the severity of general anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) (33). Both 7-item scales had a score range of 0–21 with high scores representing increasing severity.

The 15-item visceral sensitivity index (VSI) assessed GI-specific anxiety with items that are scored on a 6-point Likert scale. Low scores referred to strongly agreeing with the statements, and high scores indicated strongly disagreeing (34). High sum scores (0–75) represented more severe GI-specific anxiety.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean values ± SDs and categorical variables as percentages. For the macronutrients, crude values were presented along the guidelines in the Nordic nutrition recommendations. To adjust for potential differences in reported energy intakes between the cohorts, the following analyses were performed using energy-adjusted variables, i.e., the E% or by using the mean daily intakes in grams per 1,000 kcal. Macronutrients, food group intake, and diet diversity score of patients and controls were compared with standardized mean differences analyzed using Cohen's d formula. The effect size was interpreted using Cohen's guidelines, where a small effect size is indicated by d = 0.2−0.4, a medium effect size by d = 0.5−0.7, and a large effect size by d ≥ 0.8 (35).

To study diet quality, we identified the proportion of participants allocated to the 3 DQI-SNR subgroups and the proportion meeting each component cutoff of the DQI-SNR. Differences in proportions between patients and controls were compared with the χ2 test, and for pairwise comparisons, the independent-samples proportions test (Wald H0) was applied. In addition, we compared the 2 groups stratified by sex.

To examine the association between dietary habits and symptoms in the patient population, simple linear regression analyses were conducted. Overall IBS symptom severity (GSRS-IBS and IBS-SSS) and GI-specific anxiety (VSI) were added as outcome variables, and the macronutrients and food groups, DQI-SNR, and the diet diversity score were added as independent variables. Furthermore, simple linear regression models were performed to investigate which patient characteristics were associated with the diet diversity score and the DQI-SNR, respectively. In the regression models, the diet diversity score (linear) or the DQI-SNR (ordinal) was our outcome variable and the independent variables included demographic factors, IBS subtype, IBS duration, IBS symptom severity (IBS-SSS and GSRS-IBS), non-GI somatic symptom severity (PHQ-12), severity of depressive symptoms (HADS-D), GI-specific anxiety (VSI), and severity of anxiety symptoms (HADS-A). The factors that were significantly associated with diet diversity or the DQI-SNR in the simple regression models were included in a multivariable model after multicollinearity was assessed (Spearman r > 0.7).

Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.0.0 (241)), R (version 4.2.2), and R Studio (version 2023.03.0).

RESULTS

We included 646 patients with IBS (mean age: 38 ± 14 years, 77% female) and 646 age- and sex-matched controls (Table 1). The body mass index of patients (24.3 ± 4.2 kg/m2) was similar to controls (24.7 ± 4.5 kg/m2) (P = 0.12). The reported group mean intake of macronutrients was in accordance with the Nordic nutrition recommendations, apart from patients eating less energy from carbohydrates than recommended (Figure 1).

Difference in diet quality between patients with IBS and controls

In total, 19%/52%/29% of the patients and 18%/47%/35% of the controls had poor, moderate, and good diet quality, respectively (P = 0.06). The proportion of patients with good diet quality was lower compared with controls (P = 0.02). Table 3 describes the components of the DQI-SNR and the percentage adhering to the cutoffs of the individual components. More controls adhered to the recommended intake of SFA (P < 0.001), and more patients met the recommendations for sucrose (P < 0.001). When stratified according to sex, differences between patients and controls in SFA adherence were found for both men and women (Figure 3). Difference in sucrose adherence was primarily observed in women with IBS vs controls. In addition, the proportion of women with IBS adhering to the fiber recommendations was lower compared with controls (Figure 3).

Dietary factors associated with symptom severity and GI-specific anxiety in patients with IBS

In simple linear regression analyses, using GSRS-IBS and IBS-SSS as dependent variables, higher intakes of protein, dairy products, vegetables, and total FODMAPs, along with a less diverse dietary pattern, lower total energy intake, and lower intakes of carbohydrates, and sweet dishes and desserts, were associated with more severe IBS symptoms (Table 4). When using VSI as the dependent variable, lower diet quality and diversity, lower total energy intake, and lower intakes of alcohol, fish, sweet dishes, and desserts, as well as higher intakes of protein, drinks, meat, and excess fructose, were independently associated with more severe anxiety (Table 4).

Last, we investigated the characteristics of patients that were associated with diet diversity and diet quality. Using diet diversity score as the outcome variable, younger age, female sex, lower body mass index, more severe IBS symptoms, non-GI somatic symptoms, GI-specific anxiety, and depression were associated with lower diet diversity (Table 5). DISCUSSION

This study aimed to assess the habitual dietary intakes and diet quality of patients with IBS compared with age- and sex-matched controls from the general population. Our results revealed significant differences in macronutrient intake and food group consumption between the 2 groups. Fewer patients exhibited dietary patterns indicative of good diet quality, as determined by the DQI-SNR, compared with controls. Moreover, we observed that lower diet diversity and reduced energy and carbohydrate intake were linked to more severe IBS symptoms. Also, we found that younger age, more severe IBS symptoms, and total anxiety and depression scores were associated with a lower diet diversity and poorer diet quality.

Notably, patients with IBS reported lower energy intake from carbohydrates, falling below the recommended levels, compared with the control group. Surprisingly, despite consuming less carbohydrates, patients reported a higher total energy intake compared with controls. Patients seem to compensate the lower carbohydrate intake by consuming more energy from fat, as previously also noted by Saito et al (11) and Tigchelaar et al (12). This trend suggests that patients may be employing strategies such as carbohydrate restriction to manage their symptoms, which resonates with previous findings, suggesting that a reduced carbohydrate intake might serve as a strategy to alleviate IBS symptoms (6). Several major sources of carbohydrates are high in FODMAP, which could indicate that patients with a more severe symptom pattern were able to identify carbohydrates, potentially because of their FODMAP content, as triggers of symptoms. Some specific high-FODMAP foods and other known dietary triggers were consumed less by patients compared with controls, such as dairy products, bread, sweet dishes and desserts (mostly lactose-containing), and coffee. Patients seemed to substitute dairy products with lactose-free alternatives.

The higher energy intake among patients compared with controls did not align with previous findings, where energy intake was either similar (11,13) or lower (12) compared with controls. However, another study found that the mean total energy intake of patients with IBS exceeds the local estimated average requirements (18). Our finding could be attributed to differences in under-reporting that may have occurred, as the control sample used a self-administered dietary record, which in a validation study was shown to slightly underestimate energy intakes (26). The patients' food diaries were controlled by and entered into the software by trained dietitians, which possibly minimized problems with underreporting of portion sizes. Another factor could be our large sample size, which enabled even minor dissimilarities that may not have any clinical significance, to become statistically significant.

Furthermore, patients were less likely to report good diet quality compared with controls. Similar findings have been observed in the limited number of studies investigating this concept (12). Chaumont et al found in a French population-based study that poor diet quality is associated with more gas-related symptoms, which are common in IBS, and a reduced quality of life (17). Recently, the Mediterranean diet has shown promise as dietary treatment for IBS both in children and adults (36,37), with its positive effects potentially attributed to its good diet quality, including an abundance of vegetables, dietary fiber, fish, use of vegetable oils, and a low intake of saturated fats. These dietary components may beneficially impact gut microbiota composition and help reduce inflammation (38). By promoting the growth of beneficial gut bacteria, a high-quality diet may positively influence the gut-brain axis, potentially improving both mood and GI symptoms. Although the dissimilarities in diet quality between patients and controls were small in our study, it does indicate that diet quality might be of importance for the IBS symptom pattern.

Certain dietary patterns demonstrated associations with severity of IBS symptoms and GI-specific anxiety. A lower total energy intake was associated with more severe IBS symptoms and GI-specific anxiety, and furthermore, lower diet diversity scores correlated with more severe symptom patterns. Diet quality was positively associated with GI-specific anxiety and IBS symptom severity assessed by IBS-SSS (but not with GSRS-IBS). Previous literature has been inconsistent in showing an association between diet quality and symptom severity (12,17). Furthermore, lower intakes of carbohydrates were associated with more severe IBS symptoms assessed with GSRS-IBS. Overall, a more severe symptom pattern was associated with poorer diet quality, less diet diversity, and lower energy intakes in the patient group. This supports the results from a previous study that patients with food avoidance or restriction, who might also suffer from a low diet diversity, may constitute a separate subgroup with an overall more severe symptom pattern (10).

The key strengths of our study include the large patient and sex- and age-matched control sample, which was collected around the same period (except for cohort 4) in the same geographical region. All patients included had their diagnosis of IBS confirmed by a gastroenterologist. We used a locally developed and validated measure of diet quality and validated versions of IBS symptom questionnaires. Nevertheless, we also encountered limitations. The methodology of the diet recording slightly differed between patients and controls, with patients having paper-based 4-day food records and controls having self-administered online 4-day food records, which potentially could affect the accuracy of the reporting. As most food diaries among patients were recorded between Wednesday to Saturday, it may have resulted in slightly more weekend days being captured in patients, which needs to be considered when interpreting the reported intake of energy and alcohol. To cater for differences in the reported energy intakes, we used energy-adjusted variables in all analyses, which enabled more unbiased comparisons of intakes at nutrient and food intake levels. Last, we compared the dietary intake of patients with IBS with individuals from the general population and not healthy controls. Hence, a proportion of the participants from the general population likely also experienced IBS symptoms. Because the population-based study did not include questionnaires about GI symptoms, we were not able to assess the potential presence of IBS in the controls. This is a limitation of the study because the presence of IBS in the control group may have blurred the distinction between the groups, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true differences in dietary intakes between patients with IBS and controls.

In conclusion, this study found differences in dietary patterns between patients with IBS and controls from the general population. A slightly larger proportion of patients reported dietary habits with poor diet quality compared with controls. Within the patient sample, we could identify that reduced energy and carbohydrate intake, lower diet diversity, and lower diet quality were associated with more severe symptoms. Further exploration of food-symptom associations in patients with IBS may pave the way for the development of personalized management strategies tailored to individual dietary preferences and symptom profiles.
