Changed frequency of enterovirus hospitalisations after change of polio vaccination scheme: a nation-wide cohort study from Denmark
Authors: Caroline Klint Johannesen, Neil Alexandre Scheidwasser, Theis Lange, Tyra G Krause, Thea K Fischer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study examined whether ending oral poliovirus vaccination (OPV) increased enterovirus (EV) associated hospitalizations in children. Inactivated poliovirus vaccination (IPV) protects against disease caused by poliovirus. In contrast to OPV, it does not provoke a mucosal defence. Therefore, it is hypothesised that IPV does not offer cross-protection against other EV infections.MethodsThe prevalence and incidence of EV cases were estimated in three cohorts of Danish children. To address the historically sparse diagnosis and registration of EV, we trained supervised machine-learning models in 2017–2018 admission data, validated them against the 2015–2016 cohort and applied the final model to the period 1996–2014. The study was conducted using nation-wide Danish registries on hospital admissions in 1996–2018. Three cohorts of all children living in Denmark born between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 1998 (201 460 live births), 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2004 (325 825 live births) and between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2007 (194 303 live births) were followed in hospital registers until age 12. The outcomes were the prevalence and incidence of EV cases in the three cohorts and the ratio of estimates between the OPV and IPV cohorts.ResultsAmong 201 460 live births in the OPV cohort, 178 EV cases were identified, corresponding to an incidence rate of 6.48 per 100 000 risk years. The incidence rates per 100 000 risk years were 10.64 and 13.81 in the IPV cohorts, with 460 and 326 EV cases, respectively. The incidence rate ratio was 1.64 (95% CI 1.38 to 1.95) and 2.13 (1.78 to 2.56) in the two IPV cohorts compared with the OPV cohort.ConclusionChildren born in Denmark after 2001 had significantly higher rates of EV-related hospitalisations, suggesting potential cross-protective effects of OPV for EV-related hospitalisations. While supporting the hypothesis, the results should not be seen as risk attribution estimates, as the residual impact of diagnostic improvements is unmeasured.

Introduction

Enteroviruses (EVs) are a diverse group of single-stranded RNA viruses that circulate widely and are responsible for a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild febrile illness to life-threatening neurological complications, such as aseptic meningitis, encephalitis and acute flaccid paralysis.1–4 While polioviruses are the most well-known member of the genus, having caused widespread morbidity before the introduction of effective vaccines, numerous non-polio enteroviruses (NPEVs) continue to cause significant disease burden, particularly in young children.56

Poliovirus vaccination strategies historically included oral poliovirus vaccination (OPV), which contains live, attenuated poliovirus and inactivated poliovirus vaccination (IPV). IPV and OPV were often administered in combination, though in some countries one or the other has never been routinely used.78 Vaccination programmes have effectively stopped poliovirus transmission in most parts of the world. The OPV confers strong mucosal immunity as it replicates in the gut.7 However, in particular in areas with low vaccination coverage, the live, attenuated poliovirus can replicate and mutate into a form capable of causing paralysis. To prevent transmission of this vaccine-derived poliovirus, a strategy in the Global Polio Eradication Initiative has been to phase out OPV and, where not already in use, introduce IPV.

Although the protective effects of OPV beyond disease-specific protection against polio are not definitively established, a study from 2009 presents immunological evidence, which suggests cross-reactivity between OPV-induced immune responses and other EVs.9 This raises the possibility that the discontinuation of OPV could have unintentionally altered population-level susceptibility to severe non-polio EV infections.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study that exploits the natural experiment created by Denmark’s 2001 discontinuation of the OPV.10 We hypothesised that the discontinuation of OPV was associated with an increased incidence rate of severe EV-related hospitalisations in cohorts of children receiving IPV only.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a nation-wide, retrospective cohort study using Danish Health Registry data from 1996 to 2018. Denmark is a northern European country with universal, publicly funded healthcare. Reporting follows RECORD guidelines for observational studies and relevant components of the TRIPOD+AI statement for clinical prediction models.1112

EV-coded admissions in children under 18 years increased steadily from 1996 to 2018 in the Danish National Patient Registry (NPR) (figure 1). This rise may reflect improved diagnostics and evolving coding practices rather than true increases in incidence. Retrospective interpretation of EV incidence rates is particularly challenged by diagnostic artefacts, especially in the early 2000s when EV-specific coding was infrequently used, and molecular diagnostics were not yet widespread. We hypothesise that lower EV-coded admissions during this period may not necessarily reflect a lower true incidence but rather underdiagnosis and limited clinical use of specific International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes.

To address this limitation, we trained a supervised machine learning model on modern diagnostic-era data from 2017 to 2018 to identify probable EV-related hospitalisations from 1996 to 2014 based on clinical patterns.

Poliovirus vaccination in Denmark

Poliovirus vaccination schemes were stable in Denmark from the late 1960s until the discontinuation of OPV in 2001. Since 1 August 1969, children living in Denmark were offered a combined diphtheria-tetanus-(inactivated) poliovirus vaccine at 3, 5 and 12 months, with a diphtheria-tetanus booster at 5 years. Between 1968 and 2001, this included OPV at 2, 3 and 4 years.10 After the OPV discontinuation on 1 July 2001, the schedule was added a booster of IPV at age 5, in addition to the IPV offered at 3, 5 and 12 months.13

Children born before 1 July 2001 who had received one or two OPV doses were typically aged 2–4 years in 2001 and completed their vaccination according to the previous programme, with the last OPV dose given at age 4 in 2003 or before.14 Without access to the vaccination registry, we could not consider the vaccination status of individual children or quantify the effect of polio vaccination type on the EV-hospitalisation risk at an individual level. Our retrospective cohort study exploits the changed polio vaccination schemes to consider the effect at the population level. Third-dose polio vaccination coverage ranged from 90% to 99% during the study period, with average coverage levels of 97% (OPV), 97% (IPV 1) and 91% (IPV 2) in the cohorts.15

Cohort definition

Three distinct birth cohorts were constructed in the Danish NPR, based on the offered polio vaccination scheme. The individuals in the patient register were placed in cohorts based on their birth date, and hospitalisations were linked to the birth cohort at the individual level. The individual follow-up length was set at 12 years. Epidemic EV cases, such as the EV-D68 cases 2014, are seen in older children.16 While the majority of endemic EV hospitalisations occur in children <1 year, before an OPV vaccination (if any), our hypothesis was related to the effect of polio vaccinations on EV hospital admissions throughout childhood. All cohorts were open, allowing for migration in and out of the cohort. Migration was accounted for in the population data. To limit misclassification of the exposure, children who turned 2 years old in 2001 were not included in any of the cohorts (figure 2).

Lexis diagram of cohorts and follow-up time, including a follow-up demarcation line at 2018. IPV, inactivated poliovirus vaccination; OPV, oral poliovirus vaccination.

The OPV cohort consisted of children born between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 1998 (201 460 live births). The cohort was offered the OPV in combination with the IPV, the youngest children in the cohort being 2.5 years old at the discontinuation date. The IPV cohort consisted of children born between 1 January 2000, and 31 December 2004 (325 825 live births). This cohort was offered the IPV exclusively, as the oldest child was 1.5 years old at the discontinuation date. Children in the IPV cohort were offered an IPV booster at age 5 years. Children were followed until the age of 12 years, emigration or death. An additional IPV cohort, IPV cohort 2, with children born between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2007 (194 303 live births), was included to assess temporal evolution trends post-OPV discontinuation. Patients in IPV cohort 2 were followed until the age of 12 years, emigration, death or the end of follow-up, whichever came first.

Data sources

The Danish NPR

NPR contains information on all hospital contacts in Denmark.1718 The Danish Health Data Authorities provided us with access to a dataset, including all patients <18 years at the start of admission registered in the NPR with an admission diagnosis included in online supplemental table 1 between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 2018. The registry’s data structure remains consistent across these three time periods; however, after January 2019, the NPR registry underwent restructuring.17–19 Information on patient hospital visits was extracted from the NPR, including the ICD-10 diagnosis.20 Hospital admissions without a discharge date were excluded. Two consecutive admissions were considered as one admission if the time between discharge and the subsequent admission was 1 day or less. Hospital admissions shorter than 12 hours were classified as outpatient visits. Those lasting longer than 12 hours were classified as inpatient hospital admissions.

The national EV surveillance dataset

The national enterovirus surveillance dataset (NESD) was made available for analysis by the WHO National Poliovirus Surveillance Laboratory in the Department of Virology and Microbiological Preparedness at Statens Serum Institut (SSI), Denmark. EV tests were registered here after 1 January 2010, if EV was detected in a sample submitted to the WHO national poliovirus surveillance laboratory at SSI, either through one of the departments of clinical microbiology at Danish hospitals or the routine virus diagnostic laboratory at SSI. Positive EV samples were submitted to SSI for surveillance on a voluntary basis.21

Demographic data

The yearly number of live births and age-specific death rates was provided by Statistics Denmark through the public domain Statbank.dk, specifically from the tables named FOD and DOD.22

Development of the prediction model for retrospective identification of EV admissions

Data

We included all hospital admissions among children living in Denmark (<18 years) who were registered with a contact to the hospital, using any of the ICD-10 codes for population definition listed in online supplemental table 1, including all codes related to infection and infectious diseases. We linked hospital admissions to testing data using the patients’ unique identification numbers, the Danish CPR numbers.23 We defined an EV admission as one with EV-specific ICD-10 codes (table 1) or a positive EV test within 7 days before to 30 days after admission. EV admissions were labelled 1; all other admissions were labelled 0.

We divided all admissions into three temporally distinct datasets: a base dataset (2017–2018) for model development (further split into training and testing sets following a 30/70 partition), a validation dataset (2015–2016) for model evaluation and an application dataset (1996–2014) for retroactive EV admission prediction. No admission appeared in multiple datasets.

The 2017–2018 base dataset contained 220 718 admissions, of which 187 404 admissions had no missing information on the included demographic variables (age in years on date of admission, sex (male/female, gender not available from administrative data), month of birth (1–12), month of admission (1–12) and length of admission in hours). The 2015–2016 validation dataset contained 218 386 admissions (184 966 with complete demographic data). The 1996–2014 application dataset contained 1 358 243 admissions (1 116 557 with complete demographic data).

To address the high-dimensional sparsity inherent in ICD-10 diagnostic coding patterns across hospital admissions, we implemented a systematic feature engineering approach through hierarchical code deconstruction. First, we extracted the alphabetic prefix of each ICD-10 code (chapters A through Z), representing major disease categories, and quantified their frequency distribution within each admission. Second, we isolated the numeric component of each code and encoded its occurrence in another variable. Under this framework, an admission associated with diagnostic codes J19 and J21 would generate the value 2 for the chapter-level feature ‘J’ (diseases of the respiratory system) and 1 for the numeric features ‘19’ and ‘21’, with all remaining deconstructed features assuming null values.

We tested three levels of granularity by grouping the numeric component into 1st (each number 001–999 with their own feature), 10th (001–010 as one feature, 011–020 as one, etc) or 50th (001–050 as one feature, 051–100 as one, etc) percentile sections, yielding three feature sets with 1035, 134 and 55 candidate features, respectively, combined with demographic variables. No biomarker data were included unless captured within ICD-10 codes. We validated and evaluated the models on the 2015–2016 validation dataset and applied the best-performing model to predict probable EV admissions in the 1996–2014 prediction dataset. Model training and evaluation

Three supervised learning models were trained on the base dataset (2017–2018) for all three feature sets (ICD-10 codes aggregated at 1st, 10th and 50th percentile sections): logistic regression, random forest and LASSO regression. All models used fivefold cross-validation. For each model, hyperparameters were optimised using grid search-based cross-validation.

We assessed model performance using area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) with a prediction cut-off set to 50% as the primary performance metric, supplemented with Brier scores. We evaluated false-positive and false-negative rates in the validation dataset, recognising that some false positives likely represented undiagnosed EV cases.

The logistic regression model with 50th percentile ICD-10 aggregation performed best (ROC-AUC 0.863, Brier score 0.1) and was selected for predicting EV cases in the 1996–2014 application dataset. EV test data were not used in the application dataset because laboratory test information was not systematically recorded before 2010. We compared predicted EV admissions with those identified by ICD-10 codes alone.

Results of the model training and evaluation

In the 2017–2018 base dataset of 187 404 admissions without missing information, we labelled 540 (0.3%) EV cases, based on EV-specific ICD-10 codes or positive EV tests. We evaluated the three algorithms across the three feature sets, except LASSO with 1st percentile aggregation, which did not converge. All converged models performed similarly on ROC-AUC, while the test negative rate per 1000 varied (table 2). In the 2015–2016 validation dataset, random forest models performed worse, while logistic and LASSO regression performed comparably in the highest-granularity feature set (table 3). However, LASSO’s lambda value (2.929×10⁻⁶) indicated minimal penalisation, effectively approximating logistic regression. Based on the chosen metrics, the best model appeared to be logistic regression using the 50th sections of ICD-10 codes (see tables 2 and 3).

Methods for cohort comparison

Identification of EV cases

In each cohort, EV cases were identified throughout the follow-up period. No discrepancies were made between first EV infection and reinfections. A hospitalisation was identified as an EV case in the data from 1996 to 2016 if it was either ICD-10 coded (any code throughout admission or a positive EV test related to the admission) or predicted as an EV case by the model. In the 2017–2018 data used to train the model, only ICD-10 coded EV cases were included. As a sensitivity analysis, segregated results from the logistic regression model and the ICD-10 coded identification are in the supplementary material. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses considering a follow-up until 10 years of age (to have a full follow-up in all cohorts) and one considering only hospital admissions of 12 hours or more as EV cases.

Prevalence and incidence rates

Prevalence estimates were based on the number of EV cases in the cohort, per live birth in the cohort, and were not affected by migration or death. Incidence rates were based on the number of EV cases in the cohorts per time at risk, calculated as risk-years, in each cohort. We calculated the time at risk for each cohort, with live births attributing to the first year at risk, and the yearly, age-specific population size up to 12-year-olds contributing a year each for the included risk years of each cohort. This method allows migration and death to change the cohort’s time at risk at a population level.

Statistical analyses

We used log-linear Poisson regression with live births as the offset to estimate the prevalence ratio (PR) of EV cases with the OPV cohort as reference. Log-linear Poisson regression with risk years as the offset was used to estimate the incidence rate ratio (IRR) of EV cases with the OPV cohort as reference. The 95% CIs for the PR and the IRR were computed using Wald CI based on log (IRR). Loss to follow-up was relevant only in cases of migration and was handled by adjusting time at risk at the population level. In an adjusted analysis, sex and age at admission were included in the regression to exclude confounding in the IRR estimate. In sensitivity analyses, the outcome was classified as predicted EV cases only or ICD-10-coded EV cases only.

Implementation

We handled all analysis and data management using R V.4.3.3 (2024-02-29 ucrt) and RStudio V.24.12.0.467, with packages caret (V.7.0-1), riskRegression (V.2025.09.17), tidyverse (V.2.0.0) and data.table (V.1.17.8).24–28

All analyses were conducted on a secure server made available by the Danish Health Data Authority (Sundhedsdatastyrelsen).

Results

The cohorts encompassed 201 460 (OPV cohort), 325 825 (IPV cohort 1) and 194 303 (IPV cohort 2) live births. In the OPV cohort, we found 167 admissions with an EV-specific ICD-10 diagnosis code. The EV admission identification model we developed and applied found 137 admissions with probable EV infection, referred to hereafter as predicted EV admissions. Of the 137 predicted admissions in the OPV cohort, 126 had an EV-specific ICD-10 diagnosis code. The identification methods together identified 178 EV cases when combining the EV cases with ICD-10 diagnostic coding with predicted EV cases. A single vaccine-derived polio case, ICD-10 coded and predicted as an EV admission, in the OPV cohort was counted as an EV case. In the IPV cohorts 1 and 2, 460 and 326 cases were identified using both methods. The latter IPV cohort had a shorter follow-up for the youngest of the included children (table 3).

Overall, the age of admission for the EV cases in the three cohorts was left-skewed, with a median of 1 year, 25% quintile at 0 years and 75% quintile at 3 years of age. The EV cases in the three cohorts were similarly distributed regarding age, sex and LOS, with minor, though significant, differences (table 3). The OPV cohort cases had a higher proportion of admissions longer than 12 hours than the IPV cohorts.

The prevalence of EV per 100 000 live births ranged from 88.36 in the OPV cohort to 141.18 and 167.78 in the IPV cohorts 1 and 2. The PRs were significantly different from 1 at 1.60 (95%CI 1.35; 1.90) and 1.90 (95%CI 1.58; 2.28) between the OPV cohort and the respective IPV cohorts (table 3).

The incidence rate of EV cases per 100 000 risk years differed significantly between the cohorts, with 6.48 in the OPV cohort, 10.64 in the IPV cohort 1 and 13.81 in the IPV cohort 2. Compared with the OPV cohort, the IRR was 1.64 (95% CI 1.38 to 1.95) in IPV cohort 1 and 2.13 (95% CI 1.78 to 2.56) in IPV cohort 2. Adjusting the IRR for age and sex did not change the estimates (data not shown). There was no difference in the proportion of EV cases with a viral CNS diagnosis between the cohorts (table 3), and the age distributions of these EV cases were comparable between the cohorts (data not shown). For sensitivity, the regression analysis for IRR was performed considering predicted EV cases alone and ICD-10 coded EV cases alone. Results of the sensitivity analysis are included in the online supplemental tables 2–4. Both resulted in slightly lower estimates, while showing the same trends in variable distribution, incidence rate and IRR. When children were followed until the age of 6 or 10 years, incidence rates increased, while the still statistically significant IRR decreased slightly. When restricting EV cases to a length of stay of 12 hours or longer, estimates of prevalence and incidence rate were similar to using only predicted EV cases, and the trends in IRR were comparable to the primary analysis.

Discussion

A key challenge in interpreting historical EV trends is the limited diagnostic capacity and infrequent use of pathogen-specific ICD-10 codes in the early 2000s. Indeed, the raw data on EV-coded hospitalisations show an apparent rise in EV-coded hospitalisations over the past two decades and an increased incidence rate in the IPV cohorts. However, this increase could reflect an ascertainment bias due to increased awareness, for example, of certain EV types such as EV-D68, and improvements in testing availability and coding practices rather than a true epidemiological surge. Other possible contributors to the increase include changes in travel, increased virulence and changes in systems such as living conditions, daycare and hospitalisation thresholds, in addition to inherent traits in administrative data. To address this, we trained and validated supervised machine learning models on hospital admissions and positive test results from 2017 to 2018 and applied these models retrospectively to earlier data from 1996 to 2014. This approach enabled the identification of probable EV-related cases that were unlikely to have been explicitly diagnosed between 1996 and 2014. Importantly, while this method increased the number of identified cases relative to the more specific diagnostic codes, it did not eliminate the observed rise over time. After adjustment using machine learning, a residual upward trend in hospitalisations remained, especially after 2010 when diagnostic improvements gained hold in clinical practice.21

This register-based cohort study leveraged Denmark’s 2001 discontinuation of the OPV to evaluate whether a change in mucosal immunity following the transition to an IPV-only vaccination schedule could be shown as an increased incidence rate of EV infections in children in the hospital admission registry. Our results, from hospital registry data with national coverage and a novel application of supervised machine learning to historic admission data, suggest a higher incidence rate of EV-related hospital admissions in cohorts of children vaccinated exclusively with IPV compared with the cohort that received OPV as part of their childhood immunisation schedule.

Consistent with our hypothesis, we found that cohorts of children born after the policy shift, who received IPV only, had a higher prevalence rate and incidence rate of EV admissions than a cohort of children who received both OPV and IPV. These findings support the hypothesis that the mucosal immune protection from OPV potentially extends to NPEVs. While the IRR may be slightly inflated in IPV cohort 2, as the years cut at the end of follow-up are lower-risk years, the tendency was reproduced in the sensitivity analysis. And the prevalence rate estimates, which would be affected by the shorter follow-up time in the opposite direction, showed that the trends in the EV admission increase were robust between the ratio estimates. If the hypothesis holds, a gradually decreasing herd immunity against NPEVs and a similarly gradually increasing circulation, which raise the risk of severe disease, can be a possible explanation for the increased frequency of EV cases in IPV 2.

The IRR should not be interpreted as a risk attribution estimate for IPV-only use, due to the unestimated influence of diagnostic improvement on the incidence of EV cases in Danish hospitals. Of note, some EV cases, especially in children<1 year, may be caused by parechoviruses, a related virus with largely similar hospital admission patterns. Without a pathogen-specific ICD-10 code or test data for parechoviruses, we could not disentangle their proportion of the EV cases.

Our findings are consistent with prior immunological studies suggesting that OPV induces robust mucosal immunity in the gut and oropharynx, which may prevent not only poliovirus infection but also limit replication and transmission of other enteroviruses through cross-reactive immune responses.929 Although evidence for cross-protection is still emerging, recent serological work has demonstrated overlapping antibody profiles between poliovirus and non-polio EVs, lending biological plausibility to our epidemiological observations.30 The findings are also in line with a previous investigation showing an increased incidence of admission to hospital with infectious diseases in a non-OPV cohort of Americans.31

A major strength of this study is the combination of a natural experiment with machine learning-enhanced retrospective case identification. This approach allowed for an adjusted analysis of the association between the withdrawal of OPV and the incidence rate of severe EV infections.

The study has limitations. While machine learning mitigates some of the known diagnostic biases related to EV cases in hospitals, the algorithm is trained on modern data. It assumes that historical hospitalisation patterns reflect similar underlying diagnoses. If clinical practices, diagnostic behaviour or disease presentation changed substantially over time, this assumption may not hold. Notably, the model under-identified EV cases in all cohorts, most severely in the first IPV cohort, where the number of predicted cases is 20% lower than the number of ICD-10-coded EV cases. This discrepancy suggests that the prediction model may exhibit lower sensitivity in certain data contexts. The model is highly specific to the Danish hospital data, and while the methodology is transferable to other settings and data structures, the model itself has low generalisability.

The global shift away from OPV was motivated by the elimination of wild poliovirus from nearly all countries and the risks associated with vaccine-derived strains, and it has clear benefits. However, it may carry unanticipated costs in the form of reduced mucosal immunity and increased risk of severe non-polio EV infection. Our results suggest that long-term enterovirus control strategies may need to account for broader immunological effects of vaccine choice.

Further studies are needed to investigate the findings in other populations, with individual-level data on vaccination and preferably with laboratory confirmation of EV cases in both OPV and IPV populations. Analysis closer in time, with a suitable negative control exposure, could be relevant to explore underlying mechanisms, which could explain the increased EV case incidence in greater detail. Clinical and immunological studies comparing mucosal immunity profiles among children vaccinated with IPV versus OPV in different settings could offer valuable insight.
