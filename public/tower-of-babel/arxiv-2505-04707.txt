Abstract
We present an analysis of the H α \alpha -emitting ionized gas in the warm phase of the NGC 253 outflow using integral field spectroscopy from the Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE). In each spaxel, we decompose H α \alpha , [N ii ], and [S ii ] emission lines into a system of up to 3 Gaussian components, accounting for the velocity contributions due to the disk and both intercepted walls of an outflow cone. In the approaching southern lobe of the outflow, we find maximum deprojected outflow velocities down to ∼ − 500 \sim-500 km s -1 . Velocity gradients of this outflowing gas range from ∼ − 350 \sim-350 to − 550 -550 km s -1 kpc -1 with increasing distance from the nucleus. Additionally, [N ii ]/H α \alpha and [S ii ]/H α \alpha integrated line ratios are suggestive of shocks as the dominant ionization source throughout the wind. Electron densities, inferred from the [S ii ] doublet, peak at 2100 2100 cm -3 near the nucleus and reach ≲ 50 \lesssim 50 cm -3 in the wind. Finally, at an uncertainty of 0.3 0.3 dex on the inferred mass of 4 × 10 5 4\times 10^{5} M ⊙ , the mass-outflow rate of the H α \alpha -emitting gas in the southern outflow lobe is ∼ 0.4 \sim 0.4 M ⊙ yr -1 . This yields a mass-loading factor of η ∼ 0.1 \eta\sim 0.1 and a ∼ 2 % \sim 2\% starburst energy efficiency.



I
Introduction

Galactic winds, or outflows, are key players in the evolution of a galaxy from an active spiral to a quiescent elliptical
(
Veilleux et al. 2005
;
Veilleux et al. 2020
;
Colombo et al. 2020
;
Ellison et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. Starburst-driven outflows arise via stellar feedback from the formation of compact, massive star clusters and supernovae. These processes pump energy and momentum into an expanding bubble of hot (T
\sim 10^{7}
K) gas that sweeps up the surrounding ISM into a shell of shocked material. This shell can be modeled as a bicone exhibiting strong, double-peaked line emission that accounts for both the outflow and the rotation of the galaxy. As it accelerates, the bubble reaches a “blowout” phase where the shocked gas injects its environment with energy and material. In the absence of other drag forces, material moving faster than the escape speed of the galaxy will enrich the surrounding circumgalactic medium with gas, dust, and metals. Material that is unable to escape will accrete back onto the galaxy in a cold “fountain” that may actually spark more star formation
(
Heckman et al. 1990
;
Veilleux et al. 2005
;
Oppenheimer et al. 2010
;
Veilleux et al. 2020
)
. Galactic winds, as described here, function as “ejective” feedback, where star formation fuel is removed from the galaxy. Winds also prevent the accretion of halo gas onto a galaxy by inducing turbulence and shocks, a function known as “preventative” feedback
(
Pandya et al. 2020
;
Thompson & Heckman 2024
)
. Both feedback types are key to stunting the growth of a star-forming galaxy.

NGC 253 is an archetypal starburst galaxy with a circumnuclear star formation rate (SFR) of
2.8
M
⊙
yr
-1

(
Ott et al. 2005
;
Leroy et al. 2015
)
. Gas accretion along the bar orbits triggers shocks, enabling efficient gas collapse and heightened star formation activity in the
200
pc circumnuclear ring. This process manifests in compact, massive “super star clusters”
(
Portegies Zwart et al. 2010
;
Leroy et al. 2018
;
Levy et al. 2022
)
which eventually fuel the galactic superwind
(
Paglione et al. 2004
;
Mills et al. 2021
)
.

At a distance of only
d\sim 3.5
Mpc
(
Rekola et al. 2005
;
Newman et al. 2024
;
Okamoto et al. 2024
, E. Congiu et al. submitted;)
, the physical conditions of the NGC 253 wind can be studied in incredible detail, and has been across the electromagnetic spectrum
(
Ulrich 1978
;
Matsubayashi et al. 2009
;
Westmoquette et al. 2011
;
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Walter et al. 2017
;
Zschaechner et al. 2018
;
Krieger et al. 2019
;
Günthardt et al. 2019
;
Lopez et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. As illustrated in Figure

78^{\circ}

(

. Because of this geometry, the northern outflow suffers from heavy obscuration at optical wavelengths caused by the intervening disk. Indeed, H
\alpha
-emitting gas at T
\approx 5000{-}10^{4}

(
Westmoquette et al. 2011
)
, while both lobes have been detected in the X-ray
(

\approx 10^{7}

and the radio
(

II.1
Continuum Subtraction

Removing the underlying stellar continuum is important in deriving line intensities
(
Bellocchi et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. To subtract the stellar continuum, we use the PHANGS-MUSE data analysis pipeline, which is described in
Emsellem et al. 2022
.
Summarizing, the pipeline first runs a
ppxf
-based
(
Cappellari 2017
)
fitting routine on a Voronoi-binned version of the cube with a reduced number of single stellar population templates recovered from the E-MILES library
(
Vazdekis et al. 2016
)
to obtain a first guess of the kinematics of the stellar populations. Then, it performs a second fit on the Voronoi-binned data with a more extended sample of templates to extract other physical information (like age and metallicity) of the stellar populations. During this run, the kinematics are fixed to those recovered in the first run. Finally, a third fit is performed, this time on the unbinned data. The first sample of stellar templates is used, and the kinematics remain fixed to those identified in the first run of the fitting routine. The final product of this run is a continuum-subtracted cube, which is what we use for the following analysis.

III
Method

To determine the properties of the NGC 253 wind, we must fit each spectrum with multiple Gaussian components. A Gaussian distribution is a good model for the underlying instrumental broadening (i.e., the instrumental line spread function) for MUSE
(
Bacon et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. Many spectral lines show velocity contributions from both the outflow and the rotating disk; in some cases, lines-of-sight may even cut through the front and back walls of the outflow cone, resulting in up to three velocity components per emission line. Thus, we aim to fit each spectrum with one, two, and three “systems of lines.” The components of each system must be fit simultaneously, which also reduces the number of degrees of freedom and ensures a fixed separation between the emission lines.

Our routine relies on the spectral cube handling and fitting capabilities of
pyspeckit
2
2

2

https://pyspeckit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/

(
Ginsburg & Mirocha 2011
;
Ginsburg et al. 2022
)

and
spectral-cube
3
3

3

https://github.com/radio-astro-tools/spectral-cube

(
Robitaille et al. 2016
;
Ginsburg et al. 2019
)
. For this study, we focus on the brightest emission lines: H
\alpha
(
6562.801
Å), [N
ii
] (
6548.05
,
6583.45
Å), and [S
ii
] (
6716.44
,
6730.82
Å). The Gaussian fitter requires initial guesses for each velocity component. Per spaxel, we estimate the disk velocity in two ways: (1) by assuming that the brightest H
\alpha
component emits from the disk and not the outflow; and (2) by adopting a CO(
1{-}0
) velocity model of the disk from
Krieger et al. 2019

\pm 50

-1

-50

-1
assumes the outflow component arises from the approaching southern lobe, while
+50

assumes the outflow component comes from either (1) the back wall of the southern cone or (2) the receding northern lobe. Varying these outflow guesses by
2-4\times

We estimate the initial guesses for the widths (

\sigma=\lambda/R/2.355

III.1
Decomposing emission lines

We now must determine which fit (one, two, or three systems of lines) best models each spectrum. For each fit, we calculate the chi-square (
\chi^{2}
) and obtain the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC):

{\rm BIC}=\chi^{2}+n\times\ln(m),

(1)

where
n
is the number of free parameters (
6
,
12
, and
18
for one, two, and three systems of lines) and
m
is the number of channels (
m=150
). The BIC is a similar diagnostic to the reduced chi-square
\chi_{\rm r}^{2}
(
\chi^{2}
divided by the number of degrees of freedom), but also penalizes models that introduce more parameters. Generally, the smaller the BIC, the better the fit.

We use the BIC to determine on a spectrum-by-spectrum case the number of components needed to decompose the emission lines. We first assume that the one-system-of-lines model is best everywhere and only prefer the two-system model when its BIC value, BIC
2
, is less than BIC
1
. A three-system model is then preferred when BIC
{}_{2}/
BIC
{}_{3}>1.5
. We experiment with different thresholds and visually inspect the resulting maps. We find that generally BIC
{}_{1}/
BIC
{}_{2}>1
works well when preferring
2
components. We take a more conservative approach when determining between
2
and
3
components, because a BIC
{}_{2}/
BIC
3
threshold less than
1.5
tends to result in overfitting, whereas a threshold greater than
1.5
often underfits spectra with obvious outflow components. Being more strict when adopting three components is consistent with
Westmoquette et al. 2011
, who found that most lines can be fitted with one or two components.

The BIC threshold does not always work. In spectra with high signal-to-noise (S/N), the BIC calculation sometimes favors
\geq 2
components when visually it is not apparent that
\geq 2
components are needed. This becomes obvious when the velocity separation between the components is small. Thus, if the velocity separation between multiple components is less than a channel width (i.e.,
<57
km s
-1
), we determine that the spectrum is better fit with the one-system-of-lines model.

Figure
4
illustrates the number of components per spaxel based on the BIC and velocity separation criteria. The majority of spectra in the NW cube can be modeled with one system of lines. There are patches of spaxels that require two- and three-line systems, but it is not immediately evident that regions are truly associated with the northern outflow lobe. In the SE cube, the shape of the outflow cone is already apparent, with the majority of the cone favoring two components. Three components are preferred in patches toward the bottom of the map. We also find some spaxels best fit with three components toward the nucleus of NGC 253, which we suspect may be due to overfitting high S/N regions that were not caught by either the BIC or velocity separation tests.

III.2
Separating components

After determining the number of components that best models each spectrum, we must deduce which components are due to the outflow and which are caused by the rotation of the disk. For those spectra best modeled with one system of lines, the sole velocity component is assigned to the disk. For those with
>2
components, we use the assumption that the brightest H
\alpha
component arises from the disk. We also cross-check this with the CO(
1{-}0
) velocity map from
Krieger et al. 2019
. In the two-system-of-lines fit, the second component is assigned to the “Blue Outflow” map. In the case of three systems of lines, we split up the outflow components based on which is more blueshifted (“Blue Outflow”) and which is more redshifted (“Red Outflow”) with respect to the disk component.

IV.2
Projected Velocities

A galactic wind can be modeled as a truncated cone with the frustum located at the wind-launching site. A schematic of an outflow cone is illustrated in Figure
7

\alpha

\alpha=30^{\circ}
and inclination (purple arc)
i\approx 78^{\circ}

(
Westmoquette et al. 2011
)
. We discuss this geometry and its effects on the observed kinematics in detail in Section
V.1
.

Figure
8

V
Discussion

Our analysis focuses on the well-detected outflow components in the SE. Specifically, we refer to the SE Blue Outflow maps when regarding the southern outflow lobe.

V.1
Deprojected Velocities

The geometry of the southern outflow cone causes projection effects in the kinematics (Figure
7
). Therefore, the outflow velocities discussed in Section
IV.2
are lower limits on the true wind speeds. In this section, we use the discussion in
Bolatto et al. 2021
to calculate the intrinsic, “deprojected” velocities (
v_{\rm deproj}
) along the SE outflow cone. We ignore the thickness of the ionized gas.

Panel (a) in Figure
7
illustrates the geometry of an inclined outflow cone with a semi-opening angle (
\alpha
) that is measured from the cone’s major axis (
\hat{a}
). The vector
\vec{v}_{\rm deproj}
is drawn so that it traces the outer wall of the cone as a function of
\varphi
: the azimuthal angle around the cone’s major axis. If the distribution of material in
\varphi
is uniform, an observer — who sees a 2D projection of the 3D cone (i.e., in the
XZ
plane in Figure
7
b) – will see an excess of emission at
\varphi=0^{\circ},180^{\circ}
, or the cone edges. This geometry leads to the limb-brightening phenomenon seen in both the molecular and ionized phases
(
Bolatto et al. 2021
, see discussion in)
.

\vec{v}_{\rm proj}
is the projection of
\vec{v}_{\rm deproj}
onto the
XY
plane, specifically in the
-y
direction toward the observer [see panel (b)]:
\vec{v}_{\rm proj}=\vec{v}_{\rm deproj}\cdot-\hat{y}=|v_{\rm deproj}|\cos{\theta}.
Here
\theta
takes into consideration both
i
and
\alpha
. Figure
7
a portrays the simplest case in which
\vec{v}_{\rm deproj}
is pointed most toward the observer. This occurs at
\varphi=270^{\circ}
, and means
\cos{\theta}=\cos{(i-\alpha})
. For the part of the cone pointed most away from the observer,
\varphi=90^{\circ}
, and
\cos{\theta}=\cos{(i+\alpha})
. With
i=78^{\circ}
and
\alpha=30^{\circ}
, the projection factor at
\varphi=270^{\circ}
is
|\cos{(i-a)}|=0.67
; for
\varphi=90^{\circ}
, the projection factor is
|\cos{(i+a)}|=0.31
.

At the center of the Blue Outflow map, where we expect the cone most pointed toward us (
\varphi=270^{\circ}
), projected velocities reach average speeds of
v_{\rm proj}\approx 275\pm 70
km s
-1
. This equals average deprojected velocities of
v_{\rm deproj}\sim-400
km s
-1
. Some of the highest projected velocities in this region reach
v_{\rm proj}\sim-350
km s
-1
, or
v_{\rm deproj}\sim-500
km s
-1
.

In the galactic fountain model, gas that does not exceed the escape velocity of the galaxy will accrete back onto the disk
(
Heckman et al. 1990
;
Veilleux et al. 2020
)
. In NGC 253, the hottest (
\sim 10^{7}
K) gas most likely can reach these speeds, as X-ray lobes have been detected out to
\sim 10
kpc
(
Strickland et al. 2000
;
Strickland et al. 2002
;
Mitsuishi et al. 2013
)
. The escape velocity of NGC 253 is estimated to be a few hundreds of km s
-1
up to
\sim~500
km s
-1

(
Heesen et al. 2009
;
Walter et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. Adopting this estimate, it is apparent that a considerable amount of the outflowing H
\alpha
-emitting material could escape NGC 253, especially if the material is indeed accelerating with distance from the midplane, which we explore in Section
V.2
.

The projection of the cone in the plane of the observer may answer a question about component modeling and separation: why do most lines-of-sight
not
detect the back wall of the outflow cone? Without taking geometry into consideration, it is not obvious why there would only be a velocity contribution from the front wall. Panel (c) in Figure
7
illustrates the differences in projection effects from the back wall of the cone versus the front. Measuring the inclination from the rotation axis
\hat{a}
, the angle between
\hat{a}
and the plane of the sky is
90^{\circ}-i=90^{\circ}-78^{\circ}=12^{\circ}
. This means that the back wall of the cone (which is behind the plane of the sky) is only projected at
\alpha-12^{\circ}=30^{\circ}-12^{\circ}=18^{\circ}
toward the observer. Compare this projection to the total
12^{\circ}+30^{\circ}=42^{\circ}
pointed toward the observer from the front wall of the cone, and we can see that the back wall of the cone only produces a small contribution to the overall projected outflow velocity, making it difficult to disentangle from the disk. Therefore, it is expected that the vast majority of the velocity contribution comes from the front wall of the outflow cone.

In a galactic wind, the “cloud crushing” timescale of cold, dense molecular clouds is expected to be less than the time it takes to be accelerated by a hot wind (
t_{\rm cc}<t_{\rm accel}
). How, then, does molecular gas ever survive long enough to escape a galaxy? One possible solution, as brought forth by
Gronke & Oh 2018
;
Gronke & Oh 2020
, is that the time it takes for the hot wind to cool and mix with the cold gas (
t_{\rm cool,mix}
) is less than
t_{\rm cc}
. In this case, the hot gas mixes with the cold gas, cools, and replenishes the cold reservoir. The hot wind carries enough momentum to then entrain the mixed cold reservoir and accelerate it out of the galaxy.

To explore this idea, we seek to compare velocity gradients of both the ionized and molecular phases of the NGC 253 wind. These velocity gradients could provide insight into the acceleration of cold gas by a hot wind. In Figure

Here we see the overlap between the ionized gas and the molecular southwest streamer (SWS); unfortunately, we do not have optical outflow measurements along the area associated with the molecular southeast streamer (SES). Note that the ionized gas measurements along the SWS are not continuous but instead patchy.

We take a series of

V.3
Extinction

In the previous section, we speculate that some of our velocity gradient measurements suffer from non-uniform, high extinction. Similarly, we find challenges in the emission line decomposition that may also be due to high levels of extinction (see Section
IV.2
).

In the top panels of Figure

\alpha

With our kinematics and flux measurements, we may estimate the energetics of the southern NGC 253 wind. We focus on H
\alpha
gas to avoid having to account for metallicity or the ionization parameter. Throughout this section, we use the Blue Outflow measurements for the southern outflow.

V.5.1
Outflow mass

The total H
\alpha
flux in the southern outflow cone (top left panel of Figure
6
) is
F_{\rm H\alpha}=9\times 10^{-13}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
, resulting in a luminosity of
L_{\rm H\alpha}=1.3\times 10^{39}
erg s
-1
(
d=3.5
Mpc). From
Veilleux et al. 2020
the mass of the H
\alpha
-emitting outflow is

M_{\rm out}=3.3\times 10^{8}\frac{C_{\rm e}L_{H\alpha,44}}{n_{\rm e,3}}M_{\odot}.

(3)

This equation is valid for
T_{\rm e}=10^{4}
K.
C_{\rm e}\equiv\langle n_{e}^{2}\rangle/\langle n_{e}\rangle^{2}
is the electron density clumping factor (order unity means uniform density).
L_{H\alpha,44}
is the H
\alpha
luminosity normalized to
10^{44}
erg s
-1
, and
n_{\rm e,3}
is the mean electron density normalized to
10^{3}
cm
-3
.

The electron density derived from the [S
ii
] doublet is sensitive to the ratio between the mean density and
C_{\rm e}
, whereas
n_{\rm e}
in Equation
3
is only the mean density. This means that we cannot use the
n_{\rm e}
from the [S
ii
] doublet in this formalism. Instead, we use the following relation from
Lopez et al. 2025
to derive average densities from H
\alpha
emission, assuming Case B recombination and an optically-thin medium:

n_{\rm e}\simeq 10.9~{\rm cm}^{-3}\left(\frac{I_{H\alpha}}{10^{-5}~{\rm erg~cm^{-2}~s^{-1}~sr^{-1}}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\rm pc}{\Delta s}\right)^{1/2}.

(4)

\Delta s
is the depth of the medium. Because
I_{H\alpha}\propto n_{e}^{2}
, clumpy (high density) regions will dominate the emission and bias toward higher mean densities
(
Lopez et al. 2025
, see Section 2 of)
. We calculate an average H
\alpha
intensity from Figure
6
and assume
\Delta s=40
pc, the thickness of the outflow cone wall found by
Westmoquette et al. 2011
. This yields a mean
n_{\rm e}

\sim 2
cm
-3
(well within the low-density limit of the [S
ii
] indicator).

In practice, the clumping factor
C_{\rm e}
is a difficult quantity to estimate from the data. We gauge a rough value by analyzing the distribution of high-brightness areas in the outflow (Figure
6
). This method determines that high-density structures approximately constitute
\sim 20\%
of the total H
\alpha
-emitting volume (assuming it is a thin wall on the cone surface). This estimate is highly uncertain, and so we test a range of
C_{\rm e}
. We find that
C_{\rm e}=0.1{-}0.4
results in reasonable mass and energetics estimates. Therefore, we assume a median
C_{\rm e}\sim 0.2
. This range of clumpiness is consistent with recent studies of M82, where ionized emission resides in structures ranging from
\sim 0.1-100
pc
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Lopez et al. 2025
)
. Inputting our estimates into Equation
3
, we obtain
M_{\rm out}\sim 4\times 10^{5}
M
⊙
, with significant uncertainty (likely
0.3
dex) due to the effects of clumping.

V.5.2
Outflow energy, momentum, and rates

While the bulk of the momentum is thought to be carried by the cold molecular phase
(
Veilleux et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, we are solely focused on characterizing the momentum and energetics of the H
\alpha
-emitting, ionized phase of the NGC 253 wind. To compute the energetics of the H
\alpha
-emitting wind, we must assume an average deprojected velocity and cone length. In Section
V.1
, we found that
v_{\rm proj}\sim-275
km s
-1
, typical of velocities at the center of the Blue Outflow map, leads to
v_{\rm deproj}\sim-400
km s
-1
. We adopt this deprojected velocity for the following calculations. The projected extent of the outflow cone is about
800
pc, so we take an average deprojected
R_{\rm out}\approx 400/{\rm sin(78^{\circ}})
.

We determine the mass outflow rate
\dot{M}_{\rm out}
of the southern outflow to be
\dot{M}_{\rm out}=M_{\rm out}v_{\rm out}/{R_{\rm out}}\sim 0.4
M
⊙
yr
-1
. With a SFR
=2.8
M
⊙
yr
-1
, the ionized mass-loading factor is
\eta=\dot{M}_{\rm out}/{\rm SFR}\sim 0.1
. This is less than the molecular mass-loading factor
(
Krieger et al. 2019
,
\eta\approx 8-20
for
R\leq 300
pc;)
, which is expected, as the bulk of the outflow mass resides in the cold molecular and neutral phases
(
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Leroy et al. 2015
;
Krieger et al. 2019
)
. Our
\eta
is on the same order as
\eta=0.37
for M82
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
. Additionally, a small survey of photoionized outflows found the relation between the ionized mass-loading factor
\eta
and
M_{\star}
(the mass of a galaxy) to be
\eta\approx 0.76(M_{\star}/10^{10}~M_{\odot})^{-0.43}

(
Chisholm et al. 2017
)
. For an NGC 253 mass of
8\times 10^{11}
M
⊙

(
Karachentsev et al. 2021
)
, we calculate
\eta=0.1
, consistent with our result. Similarly, the MaNGA survey found a relationship between
\eta
and the SFR in H
\alpha
-emitting regions, which would also put
\eta\approx 0.1
at SFR
=2.8
M
⊙
yr
-1

(
Rodríguez del Pino et al. 2019
)
. In summary, our
\eta\sim 0.1
is in line with expected values.

The average kinetic energy of the H
\alpha
-emitting outflow is
E_{\rm out}=0.5M_{\rm out}v_{\rm out}^{2}\sim 6\times 10^{53}
erg. The outflow power is then
E_{\rm out}=0.5\dot{M}_{\rm out}v_{\rm out}^{2}\sim 6\times 10^{47}
erg yr
-1
. According to
Murray et al. 2005
and
Chisholm et al. 2017
, the energy deposition rate due to the starburst (assuming supernovae supply the total energy) is:

\dot{E}_{\rm SN}=3\times 10^{41}\frac{\rm SFR}{1M_{\odot}{\rm yr}^{-1}}~{\rm erg~s^{-1}}.

(5)

In the case of NGC 253,
\dot{E}_{\rm SN}=2.7\times 10^{49}
erg yr
-1
for SFR =
2.8
M
⊙
yr
-1
. Thus, the energy efficiency of the southern wind is a mere
\dot{E}_{\rm out}/\dot{E}_{\rm SN}\sim 2\%
. This efficiency is within the range of efficiencies previously calculated for nearby star-forming galaxies with ionized outflows
(
Chisholm et al. 2017
,
0.9{-}21\%
;)
. We note that the true energy efficiency of the southern outflow may be even lower, as
\dot{E}_{\rm SN}
only accounts for the initial energy supplied by the starburst and not from other sources.

Similarly, we estimate the outflow momentum and momentum rates to be
p_{\rm out}=M_{\rm out}v_{\rm out}\sim 3\times 10^{46}
cm g s
-1
and
\dot{p}_{\rm out}=\dot{M}_{\rm out}v_{\rm out}\sim 1\times 10^{33}
dyn. Following the same assumptions as Equation
5
, the momentum deposition rate due to a starburst is

\dot{p}_{\rm SN}=2\times 10^{33}\frac{\rm SFR}{1M_{\odot}\ {\rm yr}^{-1}}~{\rm dyn}.

(6)

For NGC 253,
\dot{p}_{\rm SN}=5.6\times 10^{33}
dynes, and thus
\dot{p}_{\rm out}/\dot{p}_{\rm SN}\sim 0.2
for the southern outflow.
\dot{p}_{\rm SN}
only accounts for the initial starburst (mainly due to supernovae) and does not include other sources of momentum (e.g., the Sedov-Taylor phase). To include all sources of momentum, we scale the initial momentum rate
\dot{p}_{\rm SN}
:
\dot{p}_{\rm SN,final}=1.3~\dot{p}_{\rm SN}
, assuming a mean number density of
n_{0}=1
cm
-3

(
Kim & Ostriker 2015
)
. This relation results from simulations that assume an inhomogeneous ISM, accounting for the propagation of supernova remnants beyond the initial explosion. Putting it all together, we find
\dot{p}_{\rm out}/\dot{p}_{\rm SN,final}\sim 0.1
. When the ratio between the outflow momentum and the starburst momentum is
<1
, the momentum imparted by the starburst is sufficient enough to drive the outflow without contributions from another energy source (e.g., an AGN). This is consistent with the current picture of NGC 253 being a purely starburst-driven outflow. We note that our definitions of
\dot{M}_{\rm out}
,
\dot{E}_{\rm out}
, and
\dot{p}_{\rm out}
imply that these quantities are time-averaged over the dynamical timescale of the outflow:
t_{\rm dyn,out}\sim R_{\rm out}/v_{\rm out}\sim 1
Myr.

Again, we emphasize that these estimates are highly uncertain (
0.3
dex) due to clumping. Additionally, these numbers only account for the southern lobe of the outflow. If we assume the northern lobe is of a similar mass to the southern lobe, then the mass increases by a factor of 2. Even with a range of clumping factors, the
total
outflow energetics remains consistent with
\dot{p}_{\rm out}/\dot{p}_{\rm SN,final}<1
.

Finally, we must note that our mass estimate of the southern H
\alpha
-emitting outflow is lower than the mass found by
Westmoquette et al. 2011
. The authors used kinematic modeling and calculated
M_{\rm out}=V\rho f
, where
V
is the volume of the outflow modeled as a conic frustum;
\rho
is the density, which is the mass of a proton
m_{\rm p}
multiplied by the electron density
n_{\rm e}
(which they set to
n_{\rm e}

=100
cm
-3
); and
f=0.1
is the filling factor. However, we find that their calculated
M_{\rm out}=6\times 10^{6}
M
⊙
leads to an overestimate of the energetics; specifically, a mass-outflow rate higher than what has been found for M82
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
and a
\dot{p}_{\rm out}/\dot{p}_{\rm SN,final}\sim 2
. While our own mass estimate is uncertain, we believe that the differences between our calculations and that of
Westmoquette et al. 2011
are the assumed electron density and filling factor. Our derived electron density corresponds with a filling factor of
f\sim 0.01
, which is in line with the highly-filamentary nature of the wind and diffuse ionized gas in the Milky Way
(
Berkhuijsen et al. 2006
)
. Following the steps of
Westmoquette et al. 2011
, but instead substituting
n_{\rm e}

\sim 2
cm
-3
(and thus
f\sim 0.01
), the mass and energetics converge with the results of this work.

VI
Summary

In this work, we analyze the kinematics and physical conditions of the NGC 253 superwind by fitting multiple systems of Gaussian profiles to MUSE IFS data cubes. Most spectra can be fit with one- and two-systems of lines, where multiple components indicates velocity contributions from the outflow in addition to the disk. In summary, our results can be interpreted as follows.

1.

Typical projected velocities in the approaching southern outflow cone are between
-150
and
-250
km s
-1
. The bluest part of the cone, assuming it is the region most projected toward the observer, has a maximum projected velocity of
\sim-350
km s
-1
, or a deprojected speed of
\sim-500
km s
-1
. The kinematics are in line with a biconical outflow with limb-brightened edges due to projection effects.

2.

The ionized gas is moving faster with increasing projected distance from the nucleus. On the blue side (negative velocities), the observed projected gradient is
\sim-350
km s
-1
kpc
-1
down the center of the cone and
\sim-550
km s
-1
kpc
-1
along the edge of the cone.

3.

Throughout the outflow, the [N
ii
]/H
\alpha
and [S
ii
]/H
\alpha
ratios exceed nominal thresholds where shock ionization begins to dominate over photoionization from the nuclear star clusters. The highest line ratios are at the edges of the cone, and tend to increase with distance from the nucleus. Models of fast radiative shocks suggest that velocities of
\sim 200{-}300
km s
-1
can account for the observed [N
ii
]/H
\alpha
and [S
ii
]/H
\alpha
ratios.

4.

Assuming
T_{\rm e}\approx 10^{4}
K, electron densities peak around
2100
cm
-3
in the nucleus and quickly reach
\lesssim 50
cm
-3
in the wind. This value is the the low-density limit for our [S
ii
] tracer.

5.

The mass of the southern lobe of the H
\alpha
-emitting outflow is
M_{\rm out}\sim 4\times 10^{5}
M
⊙
with an uncertainty of
0.3
dex. This yields an outflow rate of
\dot{M}_{\rm out}

\sim 0.4
M
⊙
yr
-1
. The mass-loading factor is then
\eta\sim 0.1
, and the energetic efficiency of the starburst is only
\sim 2\%
. If the northern lobe of the outflow is of a similar mass, these estimates increase by a factor of 2 for the total outflow. These energetics are consistent with a wind solely driven by a starburst.
