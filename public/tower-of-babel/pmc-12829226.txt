Retrospective evaluation of the frequency of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth
Authors: Çağlar Yasin KOŞAR, Barış Dağ
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth and to examine which type of odontogenic cyst and anatomical region they most frequently involve.Materials and methodsA total of 100 patients with impacted teeth associated with odontogenic cysts were included in this study. Patients were first grouped according to the type of odontogenic cyst in relation to age and gender. Subsequently, the presence of systemic diseases in relation to odontogenic cysts was evaluated. The impacted teeth associated with the cyst types were examined on panoramic radiographs and classified according to the jaw, anatomical localization, anterior/posterior position of the jaw, and proximity to adjacent anatomical structures. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables. In addition, both cyst size and localization were considered in the proximity analysis to minimize bias and ensure standardization.ResultsThe study included 100 patients aged between 9 and 76 years, of whom 67 were male and 33 were female. Systemic diseases were observed in 14 patients, while 86 patients had no systemic illnesses. Among the included patients, odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth were found in the mandibular posterior region in 74 patients, maxillary anterior region in 16, mandibular anterior region in 6, and maxillary posterior region in 4 patients.ConclusionDentigerous cysts were the most common odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth. Odontogenic cysts related to impacted teeth were more frequently observed in males. The mandibular posterior region was the most commonly affected area.

Introduction

Impacted teeth are defined as teeth that have completed their development but have failed to erupt fully into the oral cavity and are unlikely to do so under normal conditions [1]. The third molars represent the teeth most frequently impacted, with the maxillary canines being the second most common. Impacted teeth have the potential to give rise to various pathological conditions and, due to their proximity to vital anatomical structures, may contribute to the development of additional complications [2]. The extraction of impacted third molars may be indicated for both therapeutic and prophylactic reasons. However, there is no clear consensus in the literature regarding the necessity of surgically removing asymptomatic impacted teeth. The issue remains a topic of ongoing debate, with some authors arguing that prophylactic extraction of asymptomatic third molars is not justified [3]. Therefore, the management of impacted teeth, including the decision to extract or retain them, must be individualized based on the specific clinical circumstances of each patient [4].

An impacted tooth may lead to various complications, including infection, caries in adjacent teeth, periodontal disease, jaw cysts, or tumors. In approximately 16% of cases involving impacted teeth, cystic or neoplastic lesions have been reported in proximity. These lesions are most frequently observed during the second and third decades of life. Although impacted teeth in children and adolescents are rarely associated with pathological changes, the prevalence increases with age [5]. The potential for impacted teeth to undergo cystic or neoplastic transformation is associated with the composition of the dental follicle, particularly remnants of the dental lamina found in the reduced enamel epithelium and connective tissue wall [6].

Jaw cysts are defined as pathological cavities that contain fluid, semi-fluid, or gas, but do not accumulate pus. This definition does not necessarily require the presence of epithelium, thus recognizing that some lesions of non-epithelial origin may be classified as cystic and included in the general classification of cysts [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) published the 5th edition of the Classification of Head and Neck Tumours online in March 2022. This edition provides more detailed subcategories for certain odontogenic tumors and cysts. While the 2017 classification divided jaw cysts into two major categories, the 2022 edition includes all types under the general heading of “Jaw Cysts” without further subdivision [8].

Radicular cysts account for approximately 60% of all odontogenic cysts and represent the most frequently encountered jaw cyst. Chronic inflammation within the periradicular tissues results in the development of a periapical granuloma and promotes the proliferation of the epithelial of Malassez. Radicular cysts are always associated with non-vital teeth and are considered a key radiographic finding in the diagnosis of periapical radiolucent lesions [7].

Dentigerous cysts represent the second most frequently occurring odontogenic cysts in the jaws, following radicular cysts [9]. A dentigerous cyst is a developmental odontogenic lesion that encases the crown of an unerupted tooth and is typically affixed at the cementoenamel junction or the cervical region. It accounts for approximately 20% of all odontogenic cysts. The prevalence of dentigerous cysts in mandibular third molars is about twice that in maxillary canines. Radiographic findings are not definitively diagnostic; however, any pericoronal radiolucency larger than 4–5 mm may indicate cystic transformation [10]. The expansion of dentigerous cysts is modulated by the release of inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), prostaglandins, and collagenases, which contribute to cystic growth and tissue degradation. Radiographically, they usually present as unilocular radiolucent lesions in the coronal region of an unerupted or impacted tooth [11].

Odontogenic keratocysts are developmental cysts of the jaws, known for their rapid growth potential, expansive nature, and tendency to invade adjacent structures [12]. They originate from the dental lamina or enamel organ [13]. Radiographically, they appear as well-circumscribed radiolucent lesions with corticated borders. In 25% to 40% of cases, a keratocyst is associated with an impacted tooth [14]. Odontogenic cysts are a diverse group of lesions arising from odontogenic epithelium, with varying clinical, radiographic, and histopathological characteristics. Understanding their prevalence and distribution in relation to impacted teeth is important for early diagnosis and appropriate management. The present study aims to evaluate the types of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth and to identify their most frequent anatomical locations.

Materials and methods

This retrospective research was granted ethical clearance by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Eskisehir Osmangazi University (Approval No: 52, dated 20.06.2023; Protocol No: 2023 − 191). The study was carried out in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

The study cohort comprised 100 individuals who attended the Faculty of Dentistry at Eskisehir Osmangazi University between May 20, 2013, and June 13, 2023. These participants were identified as having impacted teeth in association with odontogenic cysts.

Inclusion criteria mandated that each participant had preoperative panoramic radiographic imaging and an initial clinical diagnosis indicative of an odontogenic cyst. The radiographs displayed impacted teeth adjacent to pathological lesions. Only patients who had undergone a biopsy followed by histopathological verification of an odontogenic cyst were included in the final analysis.

Cases were excluded if panoramic radiographs were unavailable, impacted teeth were absent, or no cystic pathology was identified. Additionally, patients lacking biopsy confirmation or histopathological records were excluded from the dataset.

Patient assessment

The data set comprised 100 patients with odontogenic cysts linked to impacted teeth. These cases were categorized based on the cyst type, age, and sex of the individuals. The relationship between systemic health conditions and the incidence of odontogenic cysts was also examined. Patient positioning followed manufacturer guidelines to ensure reproducibility.Each lesion was analyzed with reference to its associated impacted tooth on panoramic radiographs. Classification included variables such as affected jaw, anatomical location (anterior or posterior), and proximity to adjacent anatomical landmarks.

Radiographic evaluation

All panoramic images obtained prior to surgical intervention were evaluated. A single observer utilized the Planmeca Romexis Viewer software to examine the radiographs. The assessment focused on the presence of impacted teeth, cyst association, anatomical position, and neighboring structural relationships (Fig. 1). All radiographs were acquired using Planmeca panoramic units (Planmeca ProMax; Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended settings (65 kVp, 6 mA, 16 s exposure time). The same experienced radiology technician performed all imaging procedures, and patient positioning was standardised with the Frankfurt horizontal plane parallel to the floor, ensuring consistent image quality and minimising distortion across the study period.

Histopathological examination

Biopsy specimens previously archived in the pathology department were reviewed for histopathological confirmation of the cyst type. This retrospective evaluation ensured that no new specimens were collected prospectively during the study period.

Statistical analysis

Data encompassing demographic, radiological, and pathological variables were subjected to descriptive statistical analysis via frequency distributions. The Chi-square test was applied to determine the statistical significance of associations between pathological findings and other study parameters. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20.0, with the confidence interval set at 95%. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables before statistical analysis. No a priori power analysis was conducted due to the retrospective nature of the study. All eligible cases meeting the inclusion criteria within the study period were included in the analysis.

Our study was conducted on 100 patients aged between 9 and 76 years who had impacted teeth associated with odontogenic cysts. The mean age was calculated as 37.11 ± 17.86 years. When examining the age distribution, 2 patients (2%) were between 0 and 10 years, 22 patients (22%) between 11 and 20 years, 21 patients (21%) between 21 and 30 years, 15 patients (15%) between 31 and 40 years, 11 patients (11%) between 41 and 50 years, and 29 patients (29%) were over 50 years of age. Regarding gender distribution, 67% (n = 67) of the patients were male, and 33% (n = 33) were female. Histopathological evaluation of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth revealed that 88 patients (88%) had dentigerous cysts, while 12 patients (12%) had odontogenic keratocysts.

When assessing the relationship between odontogenic cyst types and gender, dentigerous cysts were found in 63 males (71.6%) and 25 females (28.4%). Conversely, odontogenic keratocysts were observed in 4 males (33.3%) and 8 females (66.7%). Dentigerous cysts were more prevalent in males, whereas odontogenic keratocysts were more frequent in females. This difference in cyst type distribution by gender was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

The age distribution according to odontogenic cyst type showed that among the 88 dentigerous cyst patients, 2 (2.3%) were aged 0–10 years, 20 (22.7%) were 11–20 years, 16 (18.2%) were 21–30 years, 12 (13.6%) were 31–40 years, 10 (11.4%) were 41–50 years, and 28 (31.8%) were over 50 years old. In the odontogenic keratocyst group, 2 patients (16.7%) were 11–20 years old, 5 (41.7%) were 21–30 years, 3 (25%) were 31–40 years, 1 (8.3%) was 41–50 years, and 1 (8.3%) was over 50 years old. No odontogenic keratocyst cases were observed in the 0–10 years age group. Dentigerous cysts were most commonly seen in patients over 50 years, whereas odontogenic keratocysts were most frequently detected in the 21–30 years age range. However, no statistically significant relationship was found between age and cyst type (p > 0.05, Table 1.).

Regarding systemic diseases, 86% (n = 86) of the patients had no known systemic illness, while 14% (n = 14) had one or more systemic conditions. Among those with systemic diseases, 5 had diabetes mellitus, 7 had hypertension, and 2 had hyperthyroidism. Of the 14 patients with systemic conditions, 11 presented with dentigerous cysts and 3 with odontogenic keratocysts.

When analyzing the anatomical localization of odontogenic cysts, 74% (n = 74) were located in the posterior mandible, 16% (n = 16) in the anterior maxilla, 6% (n = 6) in the anterior mandible, and 4% (n = 4) in the posterior maxilla. Overall, 78% (n = 78) of the cysts were localized in the posterior region of the jaws, while 22% (n = 22) were found in the anterior region.

The relationship between cyst type and anatomical location revealed that dentigerous cysts were localized in the anterior maxilla in 16 cases (18.2%), posterior maxilla in 2 cases (2.3%), anterior mandible in 4 cases (4.5%), and posterior mandible in 66 cases (75%). Odontogenic keratocysts were found in the posterior maxilla in 2 cases (16.7%), anterior mandible in 2 cases (16.7%), and posterior mandible in 8 cases (66.7%). No odontogenic keratocysts were detected in the anterior maxilla. Both cyst types were most frequently localized in the posterior mandible. This association between cyst type and anatomical location was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Regarding the relationship between cyst type and jaw involvement, dentigerous cysts were found in the maxilla in 18 cases (20.5%) and in the mandible in 70 cases (79.5%). Odontogenic keratocysts were located in the maxilla in 2 cases (16.7%) and in the mandible in 10 cases (83.3%). Both cyst types were more commonly found in the mandible; however, this relationship was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

When comparing anterior and posterior localization in relation to cyst type, dentigerous cysts were localized anteriorly in 20 cases (22.7%) and posteriorly in 68 cases (77.3%). Odontogenic keratocysts were localized anteriorly in 2 cases (16.7%) and posteriorly in 10 cases (83.3%). Both cyst types showed a higher frequency in the posterior region compared to the anterior region, with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05).

Finally, the proximity of odontogenic cyst types to anatomical structures was assessed. Among dentigerous cyst cases, 45 (51.2%) showed no proximity to any anatomical structures, 38 (43.2%) were close to the inferior alveolar nerve, 1 (1.1%) near the maxillary sinus, and 4 (4.5%) adjacent to the nasal cavity. In the odontogenic keratocyst group, 4 cases (33.3%) had no proximity to anatomical structures, 6 cases (50%) were near the inferior alveolar nerve, and 2 cases (16.7%) were adjacent to the maxillary sinus. The relationship between cyst type and proximity to anatomical structures was statistically significant (p < 0.05, Table 2).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that dentigerous cysts were the most common type associated with impacted teeth, followed by odontogenic keratocysts. This distribution is consistent with previous studies reporting similar patterns across various populations. When stratified by age and gender, dentigerous cysts predominated in both sexes, with a slightly higher proportion in males, aligning with findings by Güven et al. [3]. The sentence regarding both cyst types being found with impacted teeth has been rephrased to clarify that all included cases were necessarily associated with impacted teeth, ensuring appropriate comparison with broader studies.

One of the most frequently performed procedures in dentoalveolar surgery is the extraction of impacted teeth [15]. Unerupted impacted teeth are often associated with various harmful pathologies such as pericoronitis, caries, periodontitis, cystic formations, undesirable effects on adjacent teeth, pathological root resorption, as well as benign and malignant tumors [16]. Cysts are pathological cavities lined with epithelium, surrounded by connective tissue walls, and filled with fluid. The fluid inside the cyst may be secreted by cells or originate from the surrounding area. In spaces similar to the maxillary sinus where there is no resistance, cysts grow into a spherical shape, while in bone, they grow and adapt to the shape of the hard tissue [17]. Since epithelial residues are more common in the jaws, cysts are more frequently seen in this region [18]. Odontogenic cysts are mostly asymptomatic but can sometimes exhibit aggressive behavior [19]. One study showed that cytokines such as IL-1, IL-2, TNF, and PGE2 play a role in the formation and expansion of odontogenic cysts [20]. The clinical implications of these findings are important for general dental practitioners and oral surgeons. Understanding the distribution patterns of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth can help guide clinical decision-making regarding early diagnosis, appropriate monitoring, and timely surgical intervention. Additionally, these results highlight the need for future prospective, multicentre studies with larger sample sizes to validate and expand upon our findings.

There is no general indication for the surgical removal of all impacted teeth. Some authors advocate for the extraction of asymptomatic impacted teeth to prevent future complications and pathological conditions, while others argue that extraction is unnecessary in the absence of symptoms or pathology [21]. Mettes et al. [22] advocate for the removal of all impacted third molars regardless of symptomatology. In our study, we evaluated impacted teeth that were surgically extracted due to pathological symptoms and associated odontogenic cysts.

Odontogenic cysts are mostly reported in association with or adjacent to impacted teeth [3]. Skinner et al. [23] detected odontogenic cysts adjacent to impacted teeth in 3% of patients. Burgos et al. [24] emphasized that 27% of odontogenic cysts were associated with an impacted tooth. El Gehani et al. [25] found impacted teeth in 53 of the 326 cases they studied. These studies indicate that odontogenic cysts occur with impacted teeth at varying rates.

In studies of jaw cyst incidence in pediatric patients, some researchers noted that dentigerous cysts were the most common odontogenic cysts [19]. In many studies, dentigerous cysts in adults are frequently associated with impacted teeth [26]. In one study on dentigerous cysts, impacted teeth were often detected through radiographic findings [27]. Nuraini et al. [28] examined impacted teeth associated with dentigerous cysts and noted that mandibular third molars are the last to erupt and are frequently impacted due to space limitations, making them more likely to develop dentigerous cysts. Stephens et al. [29] emphasized the overrepresentation of dentigerous cysts with impacted third molars. Odontogenic keratocysts, like dentigerous cysts, are mostly discovered incidentally during radiographic examinations [30]. Studies have shown that odontogenic keratocysts may arise from the pericoronal tissues of impacted third molars [31]. In a study by Chirapathomsakul et al. [14], 31.3% of odontogenic keratocysts were associated with impacted teeth. In another study, 52.8% of odontogenic keratocysts were found to be related to impacted teeth [32]. In our study as well, both cyst types were found together with impacted teeth.

According to findings by Mohammad et al. [33], odontogenic cysts linked to impacted teeth were predominantly found in individuals aged 11 to 20 years, with the lowest incidence in the 41–50 age bracket, and an average age of 25.3 ± 15.2 years. Güven et al. [3] reported a higher mean age of 33.9 years, while Al-Rawi et al. [34] observed increased frequency in patients between the ages of 21 and 40. Similarly, Patil et al. [21]] analyzed data from individuals aged 20–64 and determined a mean of 31.8 years. Although Zor et al. [35] noted an average of 38.8 ± 15.7 years across patients aged 8–67, they did not identify a statistically significant link between age and cyst presence. Conversely, Shoaee et al. [36] found an average age of 25 years and established a meaningful relationship between age and odontogenic cyst occurrence. One investigation emphasized that dentigerous cysts may emerge at any age, yet are most commonly observed among those aged 20–30 [37]. Ledesma-Montes et al. [38] similarly identified odontogenic keratocysts at a higher rate during the second decade of life.

Gender differences in odontogenic cyst prevalence have been noted in the literature. Adaki et al. [6] reported that such cysts are more frequently diagnosed in males than in females. Mohammad et al. [33] reached a similar conclusion, establishing a statistically significant association between gender and the presence of odontogenic cysts in cases involving impacted teeth. In a study of 89 cases, Zor et al. [35] documented 59 male and 30 female patients. Shoaee et al. [36] likewise confirmed a higher incidence among male participants. A Brazilian study encompassing both pediatric and adult patients also demonstrated a statistically relevant connection between gender and cyst type [39]. Corresponding to our findings, Tkaczuk et al. [40] noted that odontogenic keratocysts were more frequently found in females, while dentigerous cysts were predominant among males. Tekkesin et al. [41], in a Turkish cohort, observed a similar trend, identifying dentigerous cysts more frequently in male patients and odontogenic keratocysts in female patients. Daley et al. [42] proposed that the higher incidence in males could stem from anatomical differences, such as smaller jaw dimensions in females, leading to earlier impaction and prophylactic extractions that prevent pathological developments.

Mortazavi et al. [5] identified dentigerous cysts in 65% and odontogenic keratocysts in 27% of cases involving impacted teeth. Similarly, Güven et al. [3] determined that, among 231 patients, 93% of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth were dentigerous cysts, while 7% were keratocysts. Mumtaz et al. [43] observed dentigerous cysts as the most prevalent (68.4%), followed by odontogenic keratocysts (31.6%). Mohammad et al. [33] reported dentigerous cysts in 90.2% and odontogenic keratocysts in 9.8% of 255 patients. Shoaee et al. [36] found 61% dentigerous cysts and 39% keratocysts, while Al-Khateeb et al. [44] noted dentigerous cysts as the most frequent, followed by odontogenic keratocysts, radicular cysts, and paradental cysts. Patil et al. [21] reported dentigerous cysts in 89.3% and keratocysts in 10.7% of 150 patients. Khalesi et al. [45] observed 88.8% dentigerous cysts and 11.2% keratocysts. Saravana et al. [46] also confirmed the predominance of dentigerous cysts. Yıldırım et al. [47] found 85% dentigerous cysts and 15% keratocysts. Another large-scale analysis of 744 cases identified 90.4% dentigerous cysts and 9.6% keratocysts [48], while Karabaş et al. [49] found 73% dentigerous cysts and 27% keratocysts. Our findings (88% dentigerous cysts, 12% odontogenic keratocysts) are in close agreement with the majority of these reports, supporting the consistent observation that dentigerous cysts are by far the most common odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth, whereas odontogenic keratocysts constitute a smaller proportion.In the present study, only dentigerous cysts and odontogenic keratocysts were detected, which is consistent with previous reports indicating that these two cyst types are the most frequently associated with impacted teeth [3, 33, 45]. The absence of calcifying odontogenic cysts likely reflects their relatively low prevalence in such cases rather than a methodological limitation. Additionally, none of the odontogenic keratocyst cases in our series were associated with Gorlin–Goltz Syndrome, and all were sporadic, which is in line with the literature indicating that syndromic OKCs represent a minority of all cases [12, 30].

Mortazavi et al. [5] observed that odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth were most commonly located in the third molars and most frequently in the mandibular posterior region. Güven et al. [3] reported that 68% of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth were localized in the mandible and 32% in the maxilla. Mumtaz et al. [43] observed that cysts were more frequently seen in the mandibular posterior region and not at all in the anterior region. Planinić et al. [50] investigated impacted teeth with odontogenic keratocysts in 75 patients and found them most frequently in the mandibular posterior region and predominantly in the 21–30 age group. Shoaee et al. [36] found 73% of odontogenic cysts in the mandible and 27% in the maxilla, reporting a statistically significant difference based on anatomical localization. Patil et al. [21] found 80% of the cysts in the mandible and 20% in the maxilla among 150 patients. Khalesi et al. [45] found 64.6% of odontogenic cysts in the mandible and 35.4% in the maxilla. Yıldırım et al. [47] found 91% of the cysts in the mandible and 9% in the maxilla. Neville et al. [51] reported that 85% of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth were located in the mandible, particularly in the posterior region. Jones et al. [52] also found that both odontogenic keratocysts and dentigerous cysts were most frequently located in the mandibular posterior region. Tkaczuk et al. [40] similarly reported higher frequencies of both dentigerous and odontogenic keratocysts in the mandible. Pakravan et al. [53] found that 51.8% of odontogenic cysts were located in the mandibular posterior, 31.9% in the maxillary posterior, 15% in the maxillary anterior, and 1.3% in the mandibular anterior region. Mohammad et al. [33] found dentigerous cysts most frequently in the mandibular posterior region and odontogenic keratocysts in the maxillary posterior region. In our study, a significant difference was also observed between odontogenic cysts and anatomical localization.

The mandible and maxilla contain important anatomical structures, and due to the growth potential of odontogenic cysts, they can approach or involve these structures. In our study, a statistically significant relationship was found between the type of odontogenic cyst and its proximity to anatomical structures. One study reported that 2.9% of odontogenic cysts were close to the inferior alveolar nerve and another 2.9% to the maxillary sinüs [54]. Another study found that 74.4% of cases were adjacent to anatomical regions such as the inferior alveolar nerve, maxillary sinus, incisive canal, and nasal fossa [2]. In our study, 51% of odontogenic cysts were found to be near anatomical structures.

In the study by Stoelinga et al. [55], 12 out of 68 cases (17.6%) had odontogenic cysts associated with the inferior alveolar nerve. In our study, 44% of odontogenic cysts were found to be related to the inferior alveolar nerve. A higher rate of proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve was observed for mandibular cysts.

Odontogenic cysts may occasionally grow excessively and cause bone destruction, invading the maxillary sinus [56]. Stoelinga et al. [55] found that 7 of 14 maxillary odontogenic cysts (50%) were close to the sinus. Another study reported that 9.7% of odontogenic cysts were related to the maxillary sinüs [57]. A CBCT case study showed that 33% of cysts in the jaw were near the maxillary sinüs [40]. In a retrospective study by Urrutia et al. [54], 2.9% of cysts were found to have perforated the maxillary sinus. In our study, 3 cases were found to be associated with the maxillary sinus. Pekiner et al. [58] reported that dentigerous cysts could be close to the nasal cavity and may narrow the airway. Ertaş et al. [57] found that 25.8% of odontogenic cysts grew toward the nasal cavity, causing airway narrowing. In our study, only 4 cases were found to be related to the nasal cavity.

Limitation

A larger sample size could have been included in the study. A retrospective study could have included earlier periods. This study has several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design may introduce selection and information bias, as the accuracy and completeness of the data depend on the quality of available patient records. Secondly, although the sample size (n = 100) is adequate for descriptive and comparative analyses, it may not be sufficient to represent rare variants of odontogenic cysts. Thirdly, no power analysis was conducted prior to data collection due to the retrospective design, and all eligible cases during the study period were included. Fourthly, radiographic evaluations were performed by a single observer, which may increase observer bias, and no inter-observer reliability analysis could be performed. Finally, the statistical analysis was limited to descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests; controlling for potential confounders was not feasible in this design.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the understanding of epidemiology and anatomical distribution of odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth. By highlighting the predominance of dentigerous cysts and their frequent anatomical locations, these findings may assist clinicians in diagnosis and surgical planning. Future multicentre studies with larger cohorts are recommended to confirm and expand these observations. Dentigerous cysts were the most common odontogenic cysts associated with impacted teeth. Odontogenic cysts related to impacted teeth were more frequently observed in males. The mandibular posterior region was the most commonly affected area.
