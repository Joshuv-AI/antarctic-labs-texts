Impostor phenomenon and self- reported satisfaction among medical and surgical postgraduate trainees in Sri Lanka
Authors: Manudi Vidanapathirana, Deshan Gomez, Inoshi Atukorala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychological phenomena such as impostor phenomenon (IP) and low work-related satisfaction increase the risk of burnout among medical professionals. The prevalence of these contributory risk factors among South Asian medical professionals has not been examined thus far. This study aims to assess the prevalence, severity and associations of IP, including self-reported satisfaction with training, in a cohort of postgraduate doctors in Sri Lanka.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among medical and surgical postgraduate trainees enrolled at Post Graduate Institute of Medicine, University of Colombo. A self-administered questionnaire including the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale was used for data collection. SPSS version 26 was used for analysis, and associations were analysed using Chi Square and logistic regression.ResultsThe response rate was 75.3% (n = 201). Of the study population, 9% (n = 18) experienced few impostor characteristics, 51.7% (n = 104) experienced moderate impostor characteristics, 38.8% (n = 78) experienced frequent impostor characteristics and 0.5% (n = 1) experienced intense impostor characteristics. The mean IP value was 55.55 (SD-12.03), in the category of moderate IP. Significant associations were seen between IP and sociodemographic factors of being single, lack of friendships outside medicine and improvement in financial status related to medical profession (p < 0.05); academic factors such as satisfaction with pursuing postgraduate training and satisfaction with academic performance (p < 0.05); work-related factors such as early stages of postgraduate training, lack of confidence in communicating with superiors and other specialties, receiving criticism from the team and sense of guilt regarding patient outcomes (p < 0.05). Self-reported satisfaction with postgraduate training was expressed by 79.1% (n = 159) of trainees, with 59.2% (n = 119) self-reporting poor work-life balance. Self-reported satisfaction with training was associated with satisfaction with chosen specialty, self-reported satisfaction with academic performance, good work-life balance, confidence in interpersonal interactions, financial stability and family support (p < 0.05).ConclusionsA significant number of Sri Lankan medical and surgical postgraduate trainees experienced IP. This phenomenon is significantly associated with sociodemographic, academic and work-related factors. Self-reported trainee satisfaction with postgraduate training was high and associated with positive psychosocial factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06290-5.Keywords: Impostor phenomenon, Impostorism, Burn out, Work-related satisfaction, South Asia

Introduction

Impostor phenomenon(IP) or impostorism is a psychological experience that has originally been described among high-achieving individuals [1]. It develops when “high-achieving individuals, despite external measurements of achievement, have a chronic sense of self-doubt paired with a dread of being revealed as a hoax” [2]. IP is due to an inability among these individuals to internalize a sense of competence of skill, and it is detrimental to the affected person’s mental well-being [1, 2].

Medical professionals appear to be at higher risk of impostor phenomenon compared to non-medical professionals [3]. Nationwide research in United States (US) out carried out among 3000 physicians between 2020 and 2021, found that 1 in 4 physicians experienced frequent or severe symptoms of impostor phenomenon [3]. In fact, physicians had an 80% higher relative risk compared to professionals in alternate fields [3]. Additionally, a scoping review carried out in 2019 utilizing 18 studies corroborated these high frequencies by reporting that IP ranged between 22 and 60% among physicians [4].

Postgraduate trainees are at increased risk of impostorism due to internal factors such as constant drive to succeed; and external factors related to training such as placement in academic medical facilities with constant scrutiny, rigid medical hierarchy; and increased responsibility [5]. Sri Lankan postgraduate trainees are constantly subjected to rigorous work demands, and a study among Sri Lankan postgraduate trainees in 2023 has revealed symptoms of depression and anxiety in 11.2% and 8.9% of trainees respectively, both of which were strongly linked to external work stressors [6].

IP has been found to have strong links with a variety of negative psychological experiences in literature [4]. Those with IP were found to experience emotional exhaustion at work, increased work-family conflict and lack of professional fulfilment [7]. Following instances of failure, individuals with IP were found to be more vulnerable to negative feelings of guilt, depression and anxiety [8]. Moreover, recent literature has also drawn links between IP and the sinister phenomena of burnout and suicide [9, 10]. In a postgraduate population, IP can negatively affect learning/ training and lead to inappropriate expectations, difficulty in processing feedback and unnecessary focus on impression management [2]. This is concerning due to the potential negative ramifications on patient safety and health, given that these trainees directly engage in patient care. The negative psychological repercussions of IP mentioned above may be further aggravated by poor work-related satisfaction [8]. IP and poor work-related satisfaction have strong links with each other, and each of them are independently linked to burnout. Despite established correlational links between these phenomena, a causal relationship has not been clearly established in literature [11].

IP has been found to be common among Asian professionals, in populations where Asians were an ethnic minority in predominantly Caucasian cohorts. Factors implicated in increased prevalence were socioeconomic status associated with being a minority, racial discrimination and parenting style subjected to in childhood, including authoritarian parenting [12, 13]. However, there are currently no studies on IP among Asian medical professionals, resident in their native countries. Such studies will help determine whether being Asian is associated with IP, when the confounders and implications of being a minority or migrant are removed.

This study aimed to assess the prevalence, severity and associated factors of IP and self-reported work satisfaction among medical and surgical postgraduate trainees in Sri Lanka.

Methods

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among medical and surgical postgraduate trainees enrolled at Post Graduate Institute of Medicine (PGIM), University of Colombo. The PGIM is the only institute for postgraduate medical education and training in Sri Lanka. The study was conducted from September 2023 to January 2024 among trainees who had completed at least 6 months of postgraduate training. A period of 6 months was allowed to remove stress associated with an upgraded role, which may have impacted results. Trainees on maternity leave or leave for > 1 month at the time of the study were excluded. Trainees were invited to participate via email, and responses were recorded using a Google Form.

The study instrument consisted of a self-administered questionnaire comprising of three sections.

i)Section 1 was used to gather socio demographic details of participants.

Section 1 was used to gather socio demographic details of participants.

ii)Section 2 consisted of the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) [14]. This is an internationally validated questionnaire, with proven validation among medical professionals. It consists of 20 questions with a score of 1–5 given for each question. The answers were related to the frequency of experiencing IP and were graded as 1- ‘not at all true’ to 5- ‘very true’.

Section 2 consisted of the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) [14]. This is an internationally validated questionnaire, with proven validation among medical professionals. It consists of 20 questions with a score of 1–5 given for each question. The answers were related to the frequency of experiencing IP and were graded as 1- ‘not at all true’ to 5- ‘very true’.

iii)Section 3 was an investigator-developed questionnaire for evaluation of associated factors of impostor phenomenon. It consisted of factors categorized under several topics such as academic and work-related factors. These factors were identified after a careful literature review of related studies [2, 4, 5, 11]. This questionnaire was tested on 10 postgraduate trainees in paediatrics to ensure reliability.

Section 3 was an investigator-developed questionnaire for evaluation of associated factors of impostor phenomenon. It consisted of factors categorized under several topics such as academic and work-related factors. These factors were identified after a careful literature review of related studies [2, 4, 5, 11]. This questionnaire was tested on 10 postgraduate trainees in paediatrics to ensure reliability.

Categorical variables were described as percentages and numerical variables were described using mean, median and standard deviation. IP was assessed using the cumulative CIPS score. The lowest and highest scores that a participant could obtain were 20 and 100 respectively. Respondents were categorized as those having few impostor characteristics (score less than 40), moderate impostor characteristics (score between 41 and 60), frequent impostor characteristics (score between 61 and 80) and intense impostor characteristics (score more than 80) [7]. Correlates were analysed using Chi Square, and logistic regression applied where necessary. When assessing associations, severity of IP among respondents was categorized into 4 groups based on the categories given above.

Data analysis was done using Version 26 of the IBM SPSS statistical software. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of Faculty of Medicine, University of Colombo (EC-23-057).

Results

The response rate for this study was 75.3%. Study participants included 201 postgraduate trainees enrolled in medicine, surgery, and their related subspecialties. The mean age of the study population was 32.4 years (SD-2.23). Of the study participants, 66.2% (n = 133) were from medicine and medical subspecialties, and the remainder (33.8% (n = 68)) were from surgery and surgical subspecialties. Registrars constituted 81.1% (n = 163) of the study population, and senior registrars constituted 18.9% (n = 38). Impostor phenomenon among the study population

Of the study population, 9% (n = 18) experienced few impostor characteristics, 51.7% (n = 104) experienced moderate impostor characteristics, 38.8% (n = 78) experienced frequent impostor characteristics, and 0.5% (n = 1) experienced intense impostor characteristics. The mean CIPS value was 55.55 (SD-12.03) which fell into the category of moderate impostor characteristics.

Associations of impostor phenomenon

For assessment of associations, severity of IP among respondents was categorized into 4 groups as ‘few’, ‘moderate’, ‘frequent’ and ‘intense’ impostor characteristics. Respondents with frequent and intense impostor characteristics were combined into one category, due to the low number in the category of ‘intense’ impostor characteristics.

Several sociodemographic, academic and work-related factors were found to be significantly associated with IP. Sociodemographic factors significantly associated with IP were being single (p = 0.048), lack of friendships outside medicine (p = 0.01) and improvement in financial status related to medical profession (p < 0.01). Academic factors significantly associated with IP were satisfaction with pursuing postgraduate training (p = 0.02) and satisfaction with academic performance (0 = 0.036). Work-related factors significantly associated with IP were early stages of postgraduate training (p < 0.01), lack of confidence in communicating with superiors and other specialties (p = 0.02, p < 0.01), receiving criticism from the team (p = 0.03) and sense of guilt regarding patient outcomes (p < 0.01).

There were several significant associations for self-reported satisfaction with postgraduate training. Work factors associated with training-related satisfaction were self-reported satisfaction with chosen specialty, academic performance and confidence in interpersonal interactions (p < 0.01). Social factors associated with training-related satisfaction were self-reported work-life balance and financial and social stability (p < 0.01).

Objective markers of academic performance, such as merit ranks and honours qualifications did not appear to be significantly associated with training-related satisfaction. Receiving either validation or criticism, or experience of guilt regarding patient outcomes also did not have a significant association with training-related satisfaction.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence, severity and determinants of IP and examined self-reported satisfaction amongst Sri Lankan postgraduate trainees. It is the first study in the South Asian region to examine these important determinants of psychological wellbeing, and sheds new light on IP among Asian medical professionals when the confounder of the ‘minority experience’ is removed. This study showed that moderate, frequent and intense impostor features were collectively seen in 91% (n = 186) of the study population. There were several sociodemographic, academic or work-related factors associated with IP in this population, some of which had not been previously examined in other studies. There was self-reported satisfaction with postgraduate training in 79.1% (n = 159) of trainees, though 59.2% (n = 119) reported experiencing poor work-life balance.

Prevalence of IP

This study showed that 91% of the population experience moderate, frequent or intense impostor characteristics, with only 9% experiencing few impostor characteristics. Our findings surpass the prevalence of IP reported in previously published studies from other countries. As reported in a scoping review in 2020, the frequency of IP ranged between 22 and 60%, among physicians and physicians-in-training [4]. The mean CIPS scores of the studies included in this review ranged from 45 [15] to 61.2 [16]. Two studies that were not included in this scoping review also revealed similar frequencies of IP among postgraduate doctors. A US study among 269 residents of four different specialties (family medicine, paediatrics, anaesthesiology and general surgery) found that 62.7% of residents experienced IP, independent of the specialty, with a mean CIPS score of 66.4 [17]. Another study in 2021 among 144 surgical residents revealed that 76% of surgical residents experienced IP, with a mean CIPS score of 71.3 among White residents, and 68.3 among non-White residents [2].

It appears that the mean CIPS score in our population is equivalent or lower compared to other studies, despite a higher prevalence of IP. This may be due to the heterogeneous methods used in defining IP in different studies. A lot of studies on IP have used a CIPS score cut-off of 62 to categorize respondents as those with and without IP [4]. This practice is now outdated due to the fact that IP is a non-dichotomous spectrum, and the cut-off method overlooks respondents with moderate degrees of IP. Therefore, we categorized respondents as those having ‘few’, ‘moderate’, ‘frequent’or íntense’ impostor characteristcs, as originally suggested in CIPS, and used the frequencies of the latter 3 categories to calculate the prevalence of IP in our population.

Despite this heterogeneity, it is evident that the prevalence of IP in our population is undoubtedly high. Both personal and environmental factors pertaining to the study participants may have contributed to this high prevalence. The authors speculate that the innate, high-achieving nature of those enrolled in postgraduate training may render them vulnerable to IP. The susceptibility of high-achieving individuals to IP due to their innate nature was hypothesized in a US study among surgical residents, which despite a high prevalence of IP, failed to identify significant environmental associations [3]. The vulnerable innate nature alluded to in this study, may be mirrored by the current study population of postgraduate trainees, which is a population of high-achieving individuals with strong internal motivation. These features are evident from the fact that 90.04% of the study population graduated with honours in their primary medical qualification, and 82.6% reported personal motivation for career development as the reason for enrolling in postgraduate training. Furthermore, this study population may also have continual exposure to previously established environmental risk factors of IP. Recognized drivers of IP such as work cultures that prioritize precision over process, constant scrutiny, rigid medical hierarchy, practicing at an academic medical facility, change in job role and increased responsibility may be applicable to this population of postgraduate doctors enrolled in patient care at busy, tertiary care hospitals in Sri Lanka [3, 4]. Therefore, both personal and environmental factors may have driven the high prevalence of IP that was reported.

Additionally, the authors wonder whether the high prevalence may also reflect a potentially higher susceptibility of South Asians to IP. Previous studies on IP that included Asians, were studies in which the predominant segment of the study population was Caucasian. The prevalence of IP among Asians included in these studies may have been affected by the negative psychosocial consequences of the “minority or migrant experience”, which may have had confounding effects on IP [8, 9]. Therefore, although a higher risk of IP is demonstrated among Asian professionals in Caucasian studies, it is not clear whether this is due to a true inherent predisposition to IP or due to confounding by the minority experience [8, 9]. Research conducted among Asian populations in their native environment, is therefore important to remove this confounder.

South Asians are privy to both predisposing and protective factors of IP. The authoritarian parenting style, more commonly used in Asian households is a predisposing factor for IP since these individuals, in their childhood learn to attribute their achievements to the external factor of parental pressure [12, 18]. Authoritarian parenting also subliminally teaches children to seek validation from a controlling parent, and once these children become adults, they continue to tie their self-worth with approval from others or feel like an impostor otherwise [19]. In contrast, greater family connectedness and social support inherent in South Asian communities, may afford protection against IP by increasing one’s self-worth [12, 13]. The majority of our study population had protective social factors and evidence of adequate social support. Of the participants, 78.1% were married or in a relationship, and 65.7% lived with some form of family, either partner or parents or both. Additionally, 72.1% self-reported having adequate emotional support from the partner during difficult times and 92% self-reported receiving the same from family. It is therefore, curious that this study population which appeared to have reasonable social and emotional protection against IP, experienced a high prevalence of IP. This begs the question of whether it can be solely explained away by the personal and environmental factors described above, or whether the South Asian ethnicity had a role to play.

Associations of IP

Several sociodemographic, academic and work-related factors were found to be significantly associated with IP. Sociodemographic factors significantly associated with IP were being single, lack of friendships outside medicine and improvement in financial status related to medical profession. The first two factors are reflective of poor social and emotional support, which has been an established risk factor for IP in previously published studies [20]. The authors speculate that the improvement in financial status related to medical profession may be reflective of poorer baseline economic conditions, and upward social mobility has been shown to be a predictor of IP in one study [21].

Academic factors significantly associated with IP were satisfaction with pursuing postgraduate training and satisfaction with academic performance. It is surprising that IP was significantly associated with these subjective markers, as the ability to feel satisfaction reveals an ability to internalize gratification for external circumstances, and the primary pathology in IP is an inability to internalize success. Since high-achieving, driven individuals may not like to admit discontentment with career choices that signal progression, we wonder whether social desirability bias may have led to false, positive reporting of self-reported satisfaction. If this did in fact play a role in altering the results obtained in terms of satisfaction, its association with IP may not be reliable.

Work-related factors significantly associated with IP were early stages of postgraduate training, lack of confidence in communicating with superiors and other specialties, being criticized by the team and sense of guilt regarding patient outcomes. On a related note, there was no association seen with confidence in communicating with peers, or allied healthcare staff. From this it appears, that interaction with superiors or colleagues outside the immediate team may be more likely to trigger feelings of IP in susceptible individuals, where one’s fear of being discovered as a fraud may be more severe. Being at the early stage of training and a sense of guilt have previously been linked to IP, and in terms of the latter, it is difficult to establish which is causative [13, 22].

An alternate explanation for the high prevalence of IP, and its associations, is that impostorism, could in fact be an interpersonal strategy, rather than a true self-held perception [23]. It may be utilized by high-achieving individuals to convey a particular social identity to others, particularly in new roles, so that expectations are less, and blame is consequently lighter in case of failure [23]. It is possible that trainees in early stages of training, trainees who lacked confidence in communication, trainees subjected to criticism and trainees who experienced guilt regarding patient outcomes may have significant associations with IP as identified above, because IP may be a subconscious coping mechanism for these individuals who might like to ward off blame by suggesting that others simply expected too much of them [23].

Self-reported satisfaction with training

Most trainees (79.1%) reported satisfaction with their decision of postgraduate training, with 89.6% (n = 180) reporting satisfaction with their decision of the chosen specialty. However, despite the high levels of work-related satisfaction, 59.2% (n = 119) of trainees felt that they did not have good work-life balance.

A similar study from the United Kingdom assessed satisfaction with training among junior doctors from 2012 to 2015 [23]. This study was designed based on the premise that trainee morale seemed to be deteriorating in the recent past [24]. Surprisingly, an overall satisfaction with training was reported by approximately 80% of trainees, despite waning of morale [24]. This begs the question of whether trainee morale has stronger links to alternate factors other than training-related satisfaction. Structuring of the healthcare system, high workload with long work hours and inadequate remuneration may be factors that contribute to low trainee morale [25].

Trainee-reported satisfaction with postgraduate training was associated with protective socio-financial and work-related factors in this study. Though trainees who had self-reported satisfaction with their academic performance also experienced training-related satisfaction, there was no link between self-reported satisfaction with academic performance and objective markers of academic performance. This supports the hypothesis that contentment independent of external factors, may have a role to play in personal satisfaction.

Strengths

The major strengths of this study are that it assesses previously unexplored important psychological phenomena among postgraduate medical professionals in South Asia and identifies associations for these phenomena. The authors suggest that IP needs to be recognized as a common psychological phenomenon among Sri Lankan postgraduate trainees and further qualitative research needs to be designed to identify innate associations. In order to reduce the burden of IP on trainees, the authors recommend incorporation of an appropriately designed IP-related training module into the curriculum, availability of adequate psychological support for trainees through PGIM (e.g. counsellors/ psychologists/ psychiatrists), career guidance for junior doctors when choosing postgraduate specialities, enabling of balanced, non-critical shared reflections on patient outcomes to deter a sense of personal guilt, equitable distribution of work among postgraduate trainees and other doctors at teaching hospitals, and improvement of trainees’ social skills and psychological awareness through appropriately designed modules.

Additionally, this study identifies certain factors, which may potentially improve work-related satisfaction among trainees. While the potential for improvement assumes a causal correlation, the nature of the said factors, makes it prudent to alter them for the better regardless of the existence of causality. These modifiable factors that may improve satisfaction with training are appropriate selection of training specialty, financial and social support, and better structuring of the healthcare system with provisions for improved work-life balance. Taking measures to rectify these factors and providing support to trainees-at-risk may improve their quality of life and the service they provide.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is non-inclusion of postgraduate trainees in non-clinical specialties. Secondly, although the CIPS scale has been validated in Asian healthcare professionals, the lack of validation in an exclusively South Asian population was a limitation in our study, which may have affected the results. Thirdly, this study was unable to examine complex psychological and socio-environmental features due to its quantitative nature.

Conclusion

Impostor phenomenon among Sri Lankan medical and surgical trainees is common with significant sociodemographic, academic and work-related associations. Most trainees self-reportedly had poor work-life balance despite high self-reported satisfaction with postgraduate training.
