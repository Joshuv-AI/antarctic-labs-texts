M2 macrophage-derived extracellular vesicle/polydopamine hydrogel attenuates diabetic periodontitis-induced bone loss via the NEK7/NLRP3/IL−1β pathway
Authors: Yuzhao Cheng, Pengyu Zhao, Na Zhang, Jiehua Guo, Jiao Niu, Fenglan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathological progression of diabetic periodontitis is closely associated with macrophage functional imbalance induced by a high-glucose environment. High glucose significantly upregulates IL-1β and other pro-inflammatory factors by activating NLRP3 inflammasomes in macrophages, exacerbating periodontal tissue inflammation and suppressing osteoblast activity. Extracellular vesicles derived from M2-type macrophages (M2-EVs) effectively inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and ameliorate macrophage polarisation imbalance through their natural inflammatory targeting affinity and miR-23a-3p-mediated negative regulation of NEK7. The M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel developed in this study enables controlled release via a dynamic network structure, significantly reducing NLRP3 and IL-1β expression in periodontal tissues while promoting bone marrow stromal cell (BMSC) osteogenic differentiation through reparative macrophage-mediated secretion of osteoinductive factors, ultimately achieving periodontal bone repair and regeneration. This strategy establishes a novel multifunctional biomaterial platform for diabetic periodontitis treatment by integrating immune modulation and bone regeneration mechanisms, demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34266-y.Keywords: Diabetic periodontitis, M2-EVs, miR-23a-3p, Bone repair and regenerationSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Immunology

Introduction

Diabetic periodontitis represents a severe complication of diabetes mellitus, characterised by exacerbated inflammatory responses, accelerated tissue destruction, and a significantly elevated risk of periodontal tissue collapse1. Current research demonstrates that the complex interplay between metabolic dysregulation induced by hyperglycaemia and chronic low-grade inflammation collectively aggravates periodontal tissue damage in diabetic patients, presenting distinct clinical challenges2,3. Notably, conventional periodontal therapies frequently fail to achieve satisfactory outcomes in diabetic individuals, underscoring the urgent need for novel treatment strategies targeting the dual pathological mechanisms of inflammation and metabolic dysregulation inherent to this condition4,5. In this context, in-depth investigation of key molecular pathways associated with diabetic periodontitis—such as NF-κB signalling and cellular apoptosis—has emerged as a critical avenue for achieving targeted therapeutic interventions6.

In the molecular pathogenesis of diabetic periodontitis, the NLRP3 inflammasome, IL-6 and TNF-α form an intricately interconnected inflammatory regulatory network that plays a pivotal role in disease progression7,8. Specifically, hyperglycaemia and metabolic dysregulation activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, inducing caspase-1 activation which subsequently cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 to generate biologically active inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-189,10. The resulting IL-1β further stimulates the NF-κB signalling pathway, promoting transcription and release of IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby amplifying the inflammatory cascade11. TNF-α reciprocally activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, establishing a positive feedback loop that exacerbates inflammatory responses - representing the primary interaction mechanism among these factors12. IL-6 and TNF-α synergistically promote periodontal tissue destruction through distinct pathways. IL-6 facilitates inflammatory cell infiltration via the JAK/STAT signalling pathway while stimulating osteoclast differentiation, ultimately leading to alveolar bone resorption. Concurrently, TNF-α enhances matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression (particularly MMP9) through NF-κB and MAPK pathways, accelerating degradation of periodontal ligament and alveolar bone13. Furthermore, both cytokines promote macrophage polarisation towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype, inducing further release of inflammatory mediators (including IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) and establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation14. Collectively, these mechanisms demonstrate a defined NLRP3-IL-6-TNF-α axis in diabetic periodontitis: hyperglycaemia initially activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering IL-1β release which activates NF-κB to upregulate IL-6 and TNF-α expression, ultimately driving inflammatory exacerbation and bone destruction. TNF-α and IL-6 subsequently feedback to enhance NLRP3 inflammasome activation, creating persistent, self-sustaining inflammation that resists natural resolution.

In the complex and critical physiological process of immune regulation, macrophage polarisation acts as a regulatory switch and plays a decisive role in the progression of periodontitis. Macrophages, as important members of the immune system, possess high plasticity and functionality15. Under different microenvironmental stimuli, they can polarise into distinct phenotypes and perform varied immune functions. When NLRP3 is continuously activated, it serves as an invisible trigger, inducing macrophages to polarise towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype16,17. Once polarisation occurs, numerous proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 are released. These pro-inflammatory cytokines accumulate locally in periodontal tissues, further activating surrounding immune cells and amplifying the inflammatory response18,19. This disrupts the relatively balanced immune microenvironment20. Therefore, understanding the mechanism of macrophage polarisation in diabetic periodontitis is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, hydrogel-encapsulated extracellular vesicles have demonstrated significant advantages in the biomedical field by improving vesicle stability, enabling precise controlled release, enhancing targeting capability, promoting tissue repair and increasing biocompatibility21. Extracellular vesicles can carry various bioactive molecules including proteins, lipids and nucleic acids, which influence the biological behaviour of target cells through mediating intercellular communication22. M2 macrophage extracellular vesicles (M2-EVs) exhibit notable advantages in treating diabetes-associated periodontitis, particularly regarding immunomodulation, tissue repair, targeted delivery, low immunogenicity and potential for combination therapies23,24. M2-EVs contain abundant functional miRNAs (such as miR-23b-3p and miR-378a-3p) and cytokines (including IL-10 and TGF-β)25. These molecules can suppress activation of key pro-inflammatory signalling pathways (such as NF-κB, p38 MAPK and JAK/STAT) through miRNA-gene interactions, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses and improving the local periodontal microenvironment. Importantly, M2-EVs facilitate macrophage polarisation from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory phenotypes during tissue repair processes, which is crucial for managing diabetic periodontitis24. As unique natural nanomaterials, M2-EVs possess intrinsic macrophage-targeting capabilities at inflammatory sites and can transmit biological signals to both adjacent and distant cells, enabling more precise therapeutic action at lesion sites with enhanced efficacy. Being naturally occurring nano-sized membrane vesicles, M2-EVs demonstrate low immunogenicity and high biocompatibility, minimising immune reactions and side effects while improving patient tolerance and treatment safety. For instance, combining M2-EVs with hydrogels enables sustained drug release, optimising drug concentration and duration at target sites26,27. Polydopamine (PDA), a biomimetic polymer with strong adhesive properties, excellent biocompatibility and low toxicity, safely degrades in vivo and can form stable nano-coatings around M2 macrophage surfaces. This protective coating shields extracellular vesicles from degradation in the physiological environment, prolongs their circulatory half-life and consequently enhances therapeutic outcomes28.

In this study, we developed an innovative M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel system that significantly reduced NLRP3/IL-1β levels through overexpression of miR-23a-3p in M2-EVs, ameliorated the inflammatory microenvironment induced by pro-inflammatory macrophages, and facilitated macrophage transition from pro-inflammatory to reparative phenotypes using extracellular vesicle-mediated cell reprogramming technology. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that this hydrogel system could attenuate macrophage pyroptosis, effectively alleviate inflammatory responses in diabetic periodontitis, improve the immune microenvironment, and promote periodontal tissue regeneration.

Methods

Extraction and culture of BMSCs

The isolation procedure for bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) was conducted as follows: two-week-old male Wistar rats were selected, and their femurs and tibias were flushed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Hyclone, USA) to obtain bone marrow tissue. The isolated BMSCs were then cultured in α-MEM medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution (Hyclone, USA).

M2-EVs/PDA Preparation

The cell line we utilized RAW264.7 cells were obtained from(Ubigene Biosciences, Guangzhou). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in exosome-depleted serum medium and induced with 50 ng/mL IL-4 (Peprotech, China) for 48 h to obtain M2-type macrophages. Following culture completion, the supernatant was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation at 300 g for 10 min, 2000 g for 10 min, and finally 10,000 g for 30 min. For further purification of M2-type macrophage extracellular vesicles (M2-EVs), the supernatant underwent two additional ultracentrifugation steps at 120,000 g for 70 min at 4 °C (Optima, USA). All isolated M2-EVs were preserved in ultrapure water at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Dopamine (2 mg/mL) was dissolved in 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 8.5) and stirred at room temperature for 4 h until the solution turned dark brown. M2-EVs-loaded PDA nanoparticles (M2-EVs/PDA) were prepared through self-polymerisation of dopamine monomers followed by overnight incubation with M2-EVs at 4 °C. The characteristics of M2-EVs and M2-EVs/PDA, including their morphology, concentration and size distribution, were analysed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; G2 Spirit Biotwin, Tecnai, USA) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight, Malvern, UK). Additionally, Western blotting was performed to identify specific marker proteins expressed on the M2-EVs surface.

Uptake and endocytosis of M2-EVs by cells

DiI-labelled M2-EVs were introduced to macrophages and BMSC surfaces in confocal dishes and incubated for 24 h to facilitate cellular uptake. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS buffer and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Solebo, China) for 15 min. Cellular localisation of M2-EVs was subsequently visualised using laser scanning confocal microscopy (A1+/A1R+; Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Preparation of loaded M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel

Gelatin powder (2.5 g) was dissolved in 25 ml of PBS and stirred at 50 °C until complete dissolution. Methacrylic anhydride (6 ml) was added dropwise to the gelatin solution and stirred at 50 °C for 5 h in the dark. The solution was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min to remove unreacted methacrylic anhydride. The GelMA supernatant was collected, diluted five-fold with preheated PBS, transferred to 14 kDa dialysis bags, and dialysed against deionised water at 40 °C for 1 week. The dialysed GelMA solution was freeze-dried, and the resulting lyophilised powder was reconstituted in deionised water at 60 °C with stirring until a clear solution was obtained. To prepare the GelMA hydrogel prepolymer solution, 0.25% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was added as a photoinitiator. Subsequently, 150 µg/ml M2-EVs/PDA was incorporated into the GelMA prepolymer solution, which was then transferred to a mould and photocrosslinked under a 405 nm ultraviolet lamp (3 W) for 30 s to form the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel. The surface morphology of both GelMA and M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogels was examined using scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi, Japan).

Biosafety assessment

For the live/dead staining assay, cells were treated with M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA for either 24–48 h before being stained with calcein AM/propidium iodide (PI) and examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany). Haemolysis experiments were performed following standard protocols. Rat erythrocytes were diluted 1:20 in PBS, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with various concentrations of nanoparticles in the red blood cell suspension. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation (100 × g, 10 min) and its absorbance at 405 nm was measured to quantify haemolysis levels using a standard calculation formula.

Macrophage co-culture with BMSC cells

Cell migration assays were performed using a transwell system (Corning, NY, USA) with matched 6-well plates containing 0.4 μm pore size membranes that permitted small molecule and secreted factor passage while preventing cellular migration. The upper chamber received 1 ml of macrophage culture medium containing M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA extract, with macrophages counted and adjusted to a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/ml. Simultaneously, 2 ml of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) suspension at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/ml was added to the lower chamber of the 6-well plate. The complete system was incubated in a humidified chamber for 24 h.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Gibco, Montclair, USA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations in the supernatant were quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked for 2 h before overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Following three careful washes with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 at room temperature, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution, Proteintech, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualised using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA), with band intensities quantified using ImageJ software. Detailed antibody dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan). Reverse transcription was performed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) to synthesise complementary DNA (cDNA). The cDNA was then subjected to quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis using TB Green Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) on a real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH and U6 served as internal controls for mRNA and miR-23a-3p quantification respectively. All primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1 and were synthesised by Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan, China).

Alkaline phosphatase and Alizarin red S staining

Cells were cultured in osteogenic induction medium containing 10 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 µM ascorbic acid for 7 days. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was assessed using a BCIP/NBT ALP staining kit, with colour intensity quantified using an ALP detection kit. For alizarin red S (ARS) staining, cells were fixed with 4% PFA after 14 days of osteogenic induction culture and stained with 2% ARS solution (pH 4.2) for 20 min to evaluate mineralised matrix deposition. The staining process was terminated by washing with deionised water. Stained cells were visualised under an inverted microscope (Olympus). For quantitative analysis, ARS-bound deposits were dissolved in hexadecylpyridinium chloride and absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies against iNOS at 4 °C for 14 h. The samples were then treated with either goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (H + L) or FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (H + L) secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature under light-protected conditions. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min, and fluorescent signals were visualised using an A1+/A1R + laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Animal surgical procedures

This study was conducted in strict compliance with the guidelines of Ethics Committee of Shanxi Province People’s Hospital (EC Approval No.: N0.159). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. Prior to study commencement, experimental animals underwent a one-week acclimatisation period to adapt to the laboratory environment. To ensure stable diabetic conditions, blood glucose levels in db/db male mice were monitored weekly throughout the experiment, with only those maintaining continuous glucose levels ≥ 13.88 mmol/L selected for subsequent procedures. For mice anesthesia, a 0.3% solution of sodium pentobarbital (prepared with normal saline) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.1–0.2 mL/10 g of body weight (equivalent to 30–60 mg/kg). Periodontitis was induced by ligating the maxillary second molar (M2) with 4 − 0 silk sutures around the gingival margin for two weeks in diabetic mice. Following ligature removal, mice were randomly and blindly allocated into four groups (n = 10 per group): (1) Control group − 3-month-old db/m male mice without periodontitis; (2) Control + P group − 3-month-old db/m male mice with periodontitis; (3) DM + P group − 3-month-old db/db male mice with periodontitis; (4) DM + P + group treated with M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA. At weeks 1 and 4 post-treatment, mice were humanely sacrificed. Euthanasia of mice was performed using the CO₂ inhalation method. A CO₂ trachea was connected, and CO₂ was perfused at a rate of 10% − 30% of the cage volume per minute to minimize animal suffering, which meets ethical requirements. Periodontal tissues were immediately collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 7 days, and decalcified in 10% EDTA for 2 weeks. Histological analysis employed H&E staining for tissue morphology evaluation, while Masson’s trichrome staining assessed collagen fibre deposition. Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence results were graphically presented to facilitate data interpretation.

Microcomputed tomography analysis

After a 4-week treatment period, mice were humanely euthanised to assess bone regeneration at the defect site through micro-CT imaging. Vertical bone loss was quantified by measuring the distance between the palatal lateral cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar bone crest. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the alveolar bone adjacent to the maxillary second molar, extending from the mesial root of the first molar (M1) to the distal root of the second molar (M2), and ranging from the CEJ to 0.5 mm below the root apex. Within this ROI, key bone microstructural parameters were evaluated, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Quantitative data are presented as means ± standard deviation from at least three independent replicates. The Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed normal distribution of all datasets (p > 0.05). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA for multiple groups and Student’s t-tests for two-group comparisons. A threshold of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.The n = 3 refers to independent biological replicates, and describe the experimental setup in more detail to illustrate how these replicates were obtained.

Result

Preparation and characterisation of M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with IL-4 to induce polarisation into alternatively activated M2 macrophages(The identification of the M2 phenotype of RAW264.7 cells was presented in Fig.S1 of the Supplementary Materials.), from which M2-EVs were isolated through differential centrifugation (Fig. 1A). The purified extracellular vesicles were incubated with polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles in buffer overnight at 4 °C to ensure complete integration. Transmission electron microscopy revealed both M2-EVs and M2-EVs/PDA as circular vesicles with characteristic bilayer membranes. Nanoparticle tracking analysis determined average particle sizes of 193 nm for M2-EVs and 200 nm for M2-EVs/PDA(Fig. 1B). Electrostatic interactions between negatively charged PDA (-26.37 mV) and M2-EVs (-12.48 mV) facilitated their combination, with zeta potential measurements confirming this interaction through a significant shift to -20.0 mV for the M2-EVs@PDA composite (Fig. 1C). The encapsulation efficiency and release rate of M2 - EVs with PDA were presented in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S2A) and the stability of M2-EVs, M2-EVs/PDA in the hydrogel(Fig. S2B). DiI-labelled M2-EVs were internalised by both macrophages and BMSCs, with more pronounced perinuclear localisation observed in macrophages within 24 h (Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis identified the presence of M2-EVs markers CD206, TSG101 and CD63, while confirming the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum protein calnexin in M2-EVs (Fig. 1E). The M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA was prepared by dissolving 10% (w/v) methacrylated gelatin (EFL-GM-60, Suzhou, China) and 0.25% (w/v) LAP in PBS, followed by visible light (405 nm) irradiation for 20 s (Figs. 1F, G). Both hydrogel formulations demonstrated sustained M2-EVs release while preserving bioactivity, though the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA group exhibited enhanced release kinetics under periodic near-infrared irradiation due to its photothermal responsiveness (Fig. S3). Degradation profiles were similar for both hydrogels, with complete degradation occurring within approximately 60 h. The PDA component improved hydrogel adhesion, minimising detachment risk while permitting controlled replacement (Fig. S4). Scanning electron microscopy of freeze-dried, platinum-sputtered samples confirmed hydrogel microstructure (Fig. 1H). These results collectively demonstrate successful fabrication of functional M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogels.

NLRP3 is activated in the late stage of diabetic periodontitis healing

Macrophages in the immune microenvironment play a crucial role in the healing stage of diabetic periodontitis, with their polarisation and function directly influencing the repair process of periodontal tissue. For effective tissue repair, macrophages must transition from a pro-inflammatory to a reparative state to suppress inflammation and promote healing. However, high glucose levels disrupt this transition, leading to the persistence of pro-inflammatory macrophages and delayed tissue repair. To investigate this further, we examined the in vivo effects in db/db diabetic periodontitis mice by extracting periodontal tissues seven days after ligation with 4 − 0 silk sutures (Fig. 2A). Macrophage expression was evaluated via immunofluorescence using F4/80⁺/iNOS co-localisation. The results demonstrated that DM + P mice exhibited a higher number of labelled pro-inflammatory macrophages compared to control periodontitis mice (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, co-staining with NLRP3/IL-1β revealed significantly elevated expression levels in diabetic periodontitis mice relative to the control periodontitis group (Fig. 2C). Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis of RNA expression in periodontal tissues showed a marked increase in mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory macrophage marker genes (iNOS and CD86) in diabetic periodontitis mice, alongside enhanced local release of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6), exacerbating inflammation (Fig. 2D). Given these findings, it is imperative to promptly correct the excessive inflammatory response in macrophages to prevent prolonged inflammation during the healing phase of diabetes-associated periodontitis. Consequently, we further explored the molecular mechanisms through which M2-EVs mitigate inflammation.

M2-EVs improve inflammatory microenvironment by inhibiting NLRP3 via miR-23a-3p

The membrane-derived surface components of M2-EVs confer high selectivity and molecular precision, enabling specific fusion with macrophages. Furthermore, macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles (M2-EVs) contain diverse immunomodulatory molecules, including cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β and miRNAs(Fig.S5)29,30. Through miRNA-gene interaction networks, M2-EVs deliver these bioactive components to target cells, inhibiting key pro-inflammatory signalling pathways (NF-κB, p38 MAPK, and JAK/STAT) and exerting synergistic immunomodulatory effects. To examine M2-EVs-mediated macrophage reprogramming, we analysed microRNA expression profiles in M2-EVs. Existing evidence indicates that miR-23a-3p negatively regulates NEK7, consequently suppressing NLRP3 activation. We therefore investigated whether M2-EVs could inhibit NLRP3 activation (Fig. 3A). The chronic inflammatory microenvironment in hyperglycaemia-induced periodontitis promotes macrophage polarisation. Accordingly, we employed LPS-supplemented high-glucose medium to simulate diabetic conditions in subsequent experiments (Fig. 3B). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed decreased expression of NEK7, NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β following M2-EVs treatment (Fig. 3C).

To verify whether M2-EVs could trigger an anti-inflammatory response in pro-inflammatory macrophages, we incubated M2-EVs at different concentrations (50, 100 and 150 µg/mL) with M1 macrophages in serum-free medium for 24 h. The results showed that 50 µg/mL M2-EVs treatment reduced iNOS and CD86 mRNA levels, as well as TNF-α and IL-6 inflammatory factor expression (Fig. 3D). To reduce reagent usage, we further experimented with 100 µg/mL of M2-EVs.

To maintain functional activity in vivo, macrophages must sustain an anti-inflammatory state. We therefore evaluated the persistence of M2-EVs-mediated anti-inflammatory effects using immunofluorescence staining. Results demonstrated significantly reduced fluorescence intensity of iNOS, a specific proinflammatory macrophage marker (Fig. 3E). Flow cytometric analysis revealed substantially lower CD86 expression in the M2-EVs group compared to the LPS + HG group (Fig. 3F), with corresponding protein levels showing consistent patterns (Fig. S6). In this study, only a negative control group was set for flow cytometry (shown in Fig. S7), and an FMO (fluorescence minus one) control group was not set. This may be a shortcoming of this paper. These findings indicate that M2-EVs potentially modulate macrophage polarisation by downregulating inflammatory mediators including NLRP3, thereby establishing an immunologically favourable microenvironment conducive to subsequent periodontal bone regeneration.

M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA enhances BMSC adhesion, proliferation and osteogenic differentiation

The biocompatibility of GelMA and M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA were comprehensively assessed through live/dead cell staining and haemolysis assays. As demonstrated in Fig. 4A, both the control group and M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA-treated group showed minimal macrophage apoptosis, with cell viability evidenced by predominant green fluorescence. The haemolysis rates for M2-EVs and M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA groups measured 1.37% and 1.87% respectively (Fig. 4B), confirming excellent haemocompatibility. Under identical hyperglycaemic inflammatory conditions, experiments were performed using an indirect co-culture system of BMSCs with macrophages supplemented with M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA extract (Fig. 4C). Fluorescence microscopy revealed comparable BMSC adhesion in both GelMA, M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA and control groups after 1 h, with most adherent cells exhibiting spherical or elliptical morphology (Fig. 4D). Following 4 and 24 h of culture, BMSCs maintained adhesion to M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA surfaces while demonstrating characteristic triangular and polygonal morphology, accompanied by extensive branching and filiform pseudopodia formation.

The co-culture system of BMSCs with macrophages supplemented with M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA extract was maintained for 7 and 14 days (Fig. 4C), followed by ALP and ARS staining evaluation. Results demonstrated significantly stronger ALP staining intensity and higher enzymatic activity in the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA group compared to controls. ARS staining, both qualitatively and quantitatively, corroborated these findings, with the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA group exhibiting more extensive mineralised nodule formation, indicative of enhanced osteogenic potential (Fig. 4E, F). qRT-PCR analysis of osteogenic marker genes (OPN, ALP, OSX and OCN) revealed significantly elevated mRNA expression levels in the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA-treated macrophages versus controls (Fig. 4G). Generally, ALP and OPN reflect early osteogenic differentiation capacity, while OCN and OSX reflect late-stage osteogenic capacity38. These observations collectively indicate that M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA enhances BMSC osteogenic differentiation by macrophage-mediated modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment under diabetic conditions.

M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA improves periodontal bone loss in diabetic mice

By establishing a db/db diabetic mouse model of periodontitis, the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA during the repair phase of periodontitis was evaluated (Fig. 5A). Further immunohistochemical analysis of inflammatory responses in murine periodontal tissues revealed a significant reduction in NLRP3 expression within the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA treatment group (Fig. 5B).

H&E staining revealed significant thickening and structural disorganisation of the epithelial layer in the diabetic periodontitis group. This group also exhibited marked inflammatory cell infiltration, including purple-stained eosinophils and red-stained neutrophils, indicative of severe periodontitis. Compared with the control group, M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA treatment significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Both groups displayed only minor tissue destruction, substantial improvement in periodontitis, and a notable slowing of inflammation progression (Fig. 5C). Masson staining demonstrated denatured collagen fibres in the diabetic periodontitis group, with numerous irregularly arranged and sparsely distributed muscle fibres (stained red). Following M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA treatment, the number of disorganised muscle fibres decreased significantly. Concurrently, dense, well-aligned collagen fibres (stained blue) emerged, indicating improved periodontal tissue healing and reduced inflammation (Fig. 5C). Micro-CT analysis revealed no significant difference in alveolar bone loss between the control and M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA groups after four weeks of treatment. However, bone regeneration was more pronounced in the M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA group compared to the DM + P group (Fig. 5D). The corresponding quantitative results are presented in Fig. 5F. Alveolar bone loss was quantified by measuring the linear distance from the distal cementoenamel junction (CEJ) of the first molar to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) (Fig. 5E). The results confirmed that db/db mice treated with M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA exhibited enhanced alveolar bone regeneration.

Discussion

In diabetic periodontitis, elevated glucose levels activate NLRP3 inflammasomes in macrophages, leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory factors including IL-1β and consequently exacerbating periodontal tissue inflammation. Hyperglycaemia inhibits the activity of periodontal fibroblasts and osteoblasts, reduces synthesis of collagen glycosaminoglycans and bone matrix, and impairs periodontal tissue renewal and remodelling31. NLRP3 inflammasome activation further worsens this immune response imbalance, resulting in diminished periodontal tissue healing capacity. Recent years have seen growing attention to the role of NLRP3 inflammasomes in metabolic disease pathophysiology. The strong inflammation-metabolism correlation highlights the NLRP3 inflammasome’s function as a key inflammatory response regulator in metabolic disorders32. Accumulating evidence indicates NLRP3 inflammasome involvement in chronic diabetic wound inflammation. Furthermore, Zhang et al. demonstrated that macrophages isolated from human diabetic wounds exhibit elevated NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β expression levels33, consistent with our preliminary animal study findings. Our research reveals macrophage pyroptosis occurrence in diabetic periodontitis, suggesting its potential pathogenic role. This finding not only significantly contributes to understanding disrupted bone immune homeostasis but also enables promising future investigations in this field, indicating potential for further discoveries and breakthroughs.

NEK7 (NIMA-related kinase 7) serves as a crucial regulator in NLRP3 inflammasome activation, facilitating oligomerisation and inflammasome assembly through direct binding to the LRR domain of NLRP3. This mechanism operates independently of NLRP3 initiation or rotational activation, instead functioning as a molecular switch to control inflammasome activation34. The NEK7-NLRP3 axis significantly contributes to diabetic periodontitis progression35. Within the diabetic microenvironment, elevated potassium efflux (resulting from tissue damage-induced alterations in cell membrane permeability) enhances NEK7 phosphorylation via JNK kinase, thereby exacerbating NLRP3 inflammasome activation and accelerating periodontal tissue destruction36. While current NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors demonstrate partial efficacy in suppressing this activity, their limited specificity for NLRP3 may lead to non-specific interactions with related proteins or signalling pathways, potentially causing undesirable side effects or interfering with normal physiological functions37. Consequently, developing small-molecule inhibitors or polypeptides targeting NEK7 to disrupt its interaction with NLRP3 represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

M2-EVs inherit macrophage membrane surface components and demonstrate natural affinity for inflammatory targets. This intrinsic property protects them from rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Yuan et al. demonstrated that M2-EVs can reprogramme macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, primarily through content-mediated cross-talk with macrophages38. As anticipated, experimental investigation revealed that miR-23a-3p in M2-EVs negatively regulates NEK7 (a mitotic serine/threonine kinase) by binding to the 3’UTR region39. In this study, M2-EVs were combined with PDA to form nanoparticles. Results demonstrated that at 100 µg/ml concentration, these nanoparticles effectively reduced NEK7, NLRP3 and IL-1β production in cellular experiments while alleviating macrophage-mediated inflammation. Consequently, this approach improves the immune microenvironment in periodontitis and creates favourable conditions for periodontal reconstruction. Growing evidence suggests that active immune regulation can enhance tissue regeneration and repair. Macrophages play particularly crucial roles in damaged tissue regeneration40. During the initial phase, proinflammatory responses mediated by M1 macrophages facilitate tissue repair through cellular debris clearance. In the subsequent phase, M1 macrophages transition to M2 phenotype, secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, TGF-β) that promote cellular proliferation, differentiation and tissue remodelling41. Recent studies indicate that phenotypically distinct macrophages interact with osteoclasts and osteoblasts at multiple levels to regulate bone homeostasis42. We clearly demonstrate that proinflammatory macrophage polarisation is essential for osteoclast activation and initial mesenchymal stem cell induction. These macrophages are crucial for initiating the inflammatory phase in normal bone homeostasis. However, in late-stage diabetic periodontitis healing, proinflammatory macrophages fail to transition to reparative phenotypes, creating an imbalance between proinflammatory and reparative macrophages that impairs osseointegration. Studies confirm that prolonged inflammation (> 1 week) in diabetic wounds delays healing, indicating that early conversion to reparative macrophages could prevent chronicity43. Our results demonstrated that M2-EVs reduced iNOS and CD86 expression in Macrophages within 24 h, establishing a stable osteoimmune microenvironment through macrophage inflammatory modulation and consequently enhancing BMSC osteogenesis. Research indicates that reparative macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines along with osteogenic inducer BMP-2. BMP-2 binding to its receptor induces Smad1 phosphorylation, facilitates Runx2 nuclear translocation, and subsequently upregulates osteogenic factor expression including ALP and OCN44, which aligns with our findings. Notably, reparative macrophages exhibit significantly greater BMP-2 secretion capacity compared to proinflammatory macrophages, thereby promoting M2 cell osteogenic differentiation. Beyond BMP-2, TGF-β serves as another crucial mediator in osteoblast formation during bone homeostasis45,46. The developed M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel outperforms conventional hydrogels in managing diabetic periodontitis microenvironments. Its injectable dynamic network adapts to irregular periodontal defects while maintaining wound surface adherence, enabling controlled M2-EVs release alongside wound protection. In vivo experiments confirmed this strategy not only reduces NLRP3 levels in periodontal macrophages and ameliorates inflammatory microenvironments, but also enhances periodontal bone repair and regeneration, presenting a promising therapeutic approach for diabetes mellitus.

Our study admittedly has certain limitations, particularly its exclusive focus on macrophage and osteoblast alterations within diabetic inflammatory conditions. Future investigations should prioritise examining response mechanisms in additional cell types to achieve more comprehensive understanding of the underlying processes. Furthermore, the application potential of the developed M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel could be expanded to other inflammatory bone diseases to validate its therapeutic efficacy more extensively. The hydrogel system may also undergo further optimisation and enhancement to achieve controlled drug release applications.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates activation of the NEK7/NLRP3/IL-1β inflammatory signalling axis in macrophages during the post-healing phase of diabetic periodontitis, which regulates macrophage pyroptosis and inhibits osteogenesis. We developed an injectable M2-EVs/PDA/GelMA hydrogel incorporating both M2-EVs and PDA components. This hydrogel effectively reduces excessive NLRP3 accumulation in diabetic periodontitis while establishing an anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic microenvironment. These findings provide novel insights into cementum regeneration and repair mechanisms in diabetic periodontitis, advancing our understanding of disease pathogenesis and offering innovative therapeutic strategies for bone tissue engineering applications.
