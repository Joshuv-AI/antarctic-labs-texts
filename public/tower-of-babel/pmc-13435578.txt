Associations of the atherogenic index of plasma and its modified indices with the incidence and progression of cardiovascular-liver-metabolic multimorbidity: a prospective cohort study from UK Biobank
Authors: Qida He, Mengtong Sun, Jiazhen Yao, Yu Wang, Yueping Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and its modified indices integrate atherogenic dyslipidaemia with overall or central adiposity and have been associated with cardiovascular-liver-metabolic (CLM) diseases. However, their associations with cardiovascular-liver-metabolic multimorbidity (CLMM) remain unclear. This study examined the relationships of AIP and its modified indices with the incidence and progression of CLMM and further explored whether selected biomarkers explained part of these associations.MethodsA total of 370,258 participants without baseline CLM diseases were included in this prospective cohort analysis. AIP and its seven modified indices were evaluated: AIP, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR, AIP-ABSI, AIP-WWI, AIP-BRI, and AIP-VAI. Multivariable Cox models were applied to evaluate associations between these indices and both the incidence and progression of CLMM. Exploratory mediation analyses were further conducted to estimate the contributions of inflammatory, hepatic, and renal biomarkers. Predictive ability was evaluated using C-index, net reclassification improvement, and integrated discrimination improvement.ResultsDuring the median 16.0-year follow-up, 8646 participants developed CLMM, and each of the eight indices was associated with a higher risk of incident CLMM, with fully adjusted hazard ratios per standard deviation (SD) increase ranging from 1.18 for AIP-VAI to 1.68 for AIP and AIP-BMI. Consistent associations were also observed for CLMM progression, particularly for AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR. Per 1-SD increase in each index, the risks of transition from a healthy state to the first CLM disease increased by 38%, 36%, and 37%, the risks of progression from the first disease to CLMM increased by 28%, 26%, and 27%, and the risks of progression from CLMM to triple diseases increased by 20%, 17%, and 18%, respectively. Exploratory mediation analyses indicated that biomarkers of systemic inflammation and impaired liver and kidney function jointly accounted for 28.28 to 45.45% of the observed associations. All indices improved predictive performance, with AIP-BRI and AIP-BMI showing the highest overall performance, followed by AIP-WC and AIP-WHtR.ConclusionsHigher AIP and its modified indices were independently associated with increased risks of both incident CLMM and its progression. These indices may help improve CLMM risk stratification and early identification of individuals at elevated risk.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03225-w.Keywords: Cardiovascular-liver-metabolic multimorbidity, Atherogenic index of plasma, Incidence, Progression, Prediction, Mediation, UK Biobank

Introduction

Driven by population aging and the growing burden of chronic diseases, multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions, has emerged as a major public health concern worldwide [1–3]. Among the different multimorbidity patterns, cardiometabolic (CMM) and cardiovascular-renal-metabolic (CRMM) multimorbidity have been extensively studied [2, 4–8]. With the global surge of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), it is increasingly recognized that hepatic steatosis and cardiovascular disease share a profound relationship, prompting the proposal of the cardiovascular-liver-metabolic (CLM) syndrome concept [9, 10]. These conditions frequently cluster together and share common pathophysiological mechanisms. However, prior research has predominantly focused on isolated endpoints, such as the incidence of CVD, T2DM or MASLD, largely overlooking the incidence and dynamic progression of CLM multimorbidity (CLMM) [4–8]. Therefore, a critical need exists to identify cost-effective and reproducible indicators capable of tracking CLMM trajectories, thereby facilitating targeted prevention strategies and refining clinical management.

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated by taking the logarithm of the ratio of triglycerides (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), is a simple marker reflecting atherogenic dyslipidaemia and unfavorable lipoprotein profiles [11]. While AIP effectively captures lipid-related metabolic dysfunction, evaluating lipid profiles or obesity in isolation often yields a fragmented risk assessment. Recent evidence suggests that integrating lipid metabolic factors with anthropometric measurements provides a far more complete assessment of cardiometabolic risk, including AIP-body mass index (AIP-BMI), AIP-waist circumference (AIP-WC), AIP-waist-to-height ratio (AIP-WHtR), AIP-body roundness index (AIP-BRI), AIP-a body shape index (AIP-ABSI), AIP-weight-adjusted waist index (AIP-WWI), and AIP-visceral adiposity index (AIP-VAI) [12–16]. While these indices have been associated with diverse cardiovascular outcomes in large population studies [12–16], their specific associations and predictive values regarding the risk of incident CLMM remain unclear. Furthermore, existing studies primarily focused on the association between these indicators and the incidence of a single CLM disease, which obscured the overarching trajectory of disease progression. The specific roles of AIP and its modified indices in the incidence and progression trajectory of CLMM remain to be clarified. Although prior studies have suggested that chronic inflammation and multi-organ dysfunction may underlie the relationship of AIP and its modified indices with CMM and CRMM [4, 7], whether these biomarkers are involved in the observed associations with CLMM remains unclear.

Using data derived from the UK Biobank, this study aimed to: (1) examine the associations of AIP and its modified indices with the incidence of CLMM; (2) compare their predictive ability for CLMM risk; (3) evaluate whether selected biomarkers explained part of the associations; and (4) delineate their roles in CLMM progression trajectories.

Methods

Study participants

Participants were obtained from the UK Biobank, a large population-based cohort of middle-aged and older adults, as previously described [17]. Briefly, baseline assessments were conducted between 2006 and 2010 and included more than 500,000 UK residents aged 40–69 years.

Among 501,936 UK Biobank participants, 1296 were excluded because they were lost to follow-up. Of the remaining 500,640 participants, 74,841 were excluded because of missing data on AIP and its modified indices. We further excluded 37,621 participants with prevalent cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, or metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease at baseline, leaving 388,178 participants free of baseline CLM diseases. After excluding an additional 17,920 participants with missing covariate data, 370,258 participants were retained for the main analyses. For the exploratory mediation analyses, a further 13,429 participants without available mediator data were excluded, leaving 356,829 participants. Assessment of AIP and its modified indices

At the baseline visit, venous blood samples were obtained from all participants. Biochemical measurements, including triglycerides and HDL-C, were performed using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 chemistry analyser, and detailed information on laboratory procedures and quality assurance procedures has been provided by the UK Biobank (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/refer.cgi?id=1227). Baseline anthropometric data, including height, weight, and WC, were collected during the initial physical examinations. Besides, seven indicators of BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, ABSI, WWI, and VAI were selected to measure general or central obesity. Thus, the final analysis included eight AIP and its modified indices, including AIP and its extensions (AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR, AIP-BRI, AIP-ABSI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-VAI). All indices were computed according to previously validated formulas [12–16], as follows:

AIP-VAI in male = AIP × WC (cm) / (39.68 + (1.88 × BMI [kg/m2])) × (TG [mmol/L]/1.03) × (1.31/HDL-C [mmol/L]) or AIP-VAI in female = AIP × WC (cm) / (36.58 + (1.89 × BMI [kg/m2])) × (TG [mmol/L]/0.81) × (1.52/HDL-C [mmol/L]);

AIP-VAI in male = AIP × WC (cm) / (39.68 + (1.88 × BMI [kg/m2])) × (TG [mmol/L]/1.03) × (1.31/HDL-C [mmol/L]) or AIP-VAI in female = AIP × WC (cm) / (36.58 + (1.89 × BMI [kg/m2])) × (TG [mmol/L]/0.81) × (1.52/HDL-C [mmol/L]);

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was incident cardiovascular-liver-metabolic multimorbidity (CLMM). CLMM was defined as the development, during follow-up, of at least two of the following conditions: CVD, T2DM, and MASLD [10]. The date of CLMM onset was assigned as the date on which the second of these three conditions was first diagnosed, while the first CLM disease referred to the earliest diagnosis among the three. We categorized the co-occurrence of two conditions as double CLM, and the co-occurrence of all three as triple CLM. Incident cases were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes: IHD (I20-I25) and stroke (I60-I64) for CVD, E11 for T2DM, and K75.8 or K76.0 for MASLD [4, 7, 18]. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of baseline assessment to the date of incident outcome, death, or the end of follow-up (August 31, 2025), whichever came first. Individuals with CVD, T2DM, MASLD, or other liver-related diseases (K70.2, K70.3, K70.4, K74.0, K74.1, K74.2, K74.6, K76.6, and I85) at baseline were excluded.

Assessment of biomarkers

Blood biomarker assays in the UK Biobank were conducted under rigorous quality-control procedures [19]. Based on previous literature, we identified a panel of candidate biological markers that may account for part of the observed associations between AIP and its modified indices and CLMM risk, including markers of systemic inflammation, liver function, and renal function [4, 20, 21]. The inflammatory panel included C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count, monocyte count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count. Liver-related biomarkers comprised alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total bilirubin, total protein, and albumin. Biomarkers reflecting renal function included cystatin C, creatinine, urate, and urea. Further information is presented in Table S1.

Definition of covariates

Covariates were selected according to prior studies [4, 8, 13, 22], biological plausibility, and data availability in UK Biobank, included sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, and clinical factors. Demographic and lifestyle variables comprised age, sex, race and ethnicity, educational attainment, smoking status, frequency of alcohol consumption, physical activity level, and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI). Clinical covariates included self-reported hypertension and cancer, use of antihypertensive or lipid-lowering medications, and family history of cardiometabolic disease. Adequate physical activity was defined as fulfilling any of the following: at least 75 min per week of vigorous activity, at least 150 min of moderate activity weekly, an equivalent combination of the two, moderate activity on five or more days each week, or vigorous activity on at least one day each week [23]. The TDI measures area-based socioeconomic deprivation by combining information on social class, employment, car availability, and housing, with larger scores indicating greater deprivation [24]. Missingness for covariates ranged between 0 and 2.1%, with the lowest proportion for age and the highest for physical activity (Table S2). Detailed definitions of these covariates are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

For baseline comparisons, participants were classified according to incident CLMM status and quartiles of AIP. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Group differences were evaluated using t tests or analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Associations of indices with the risks of CLMM, double CLM diseases, and triple CLM diseases were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with follow-up time as the underlying time scale. Effect estimates are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in each index. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. No substantial violation of the proportional hazards assumption was detected. Covariates were introduced sequentially in three models: Model 1 included sociodemographic variables; Model 2 further incorporated lifestyle factors; and Model 3 additionally included clinical variables. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare crude cumulative incidence across quartiles. Potential nonlinear associations were further examined by restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on the fully adjusted Cox models. To further characterize the nonlinear associations suggested by the restricted cubic spline analyses, threshold effect analyses were performed. Inflection points were identified using a profile likelihood approach, and two-piecewise Cox regression models were fitted to estimate the associations below and above each inflection point [25].

To further characterize the role of AIP and its modified indices during CLMM progression, multi-state models were fitted. Compared with conventional competing risk approaches, this framework allows assessment of associations across successive disease transitions and prognosis stages. In line with prior studies, nine transition types were defined (Fig. 2): (A) from a healthy state to a first CLM disease (n = 62,962; 17.00%), including (A1) transition to CVD (n = 39,623; 10.70%), (A2) transition to T2DM (n = 19,024; 5.14%), and (A3) transition to MASLD (n = 4315; 1.17%); (B) from a first CLM disease to double CLM diseases (n = 8646; 13.7%), including (B1) CVD to double CLM diseases (n = 4039; 10.19%), (B2) T2DM to double CLM diseases (n = 3913; 20.57%), and (B3) MASLD to double CLM diseases (n = 694; 16.08%); and (C) from double CLM diseases to triple CLM diseases (n = 618; 7.15%). For participants who entered more than one disease stage on the same date, the onset date of the preceding stage was assigned as 0.5 days earlier than that of the subsequent stage, consistent with previous reports. For example, the onset date of the first CLM disease was defined as 0.5 days before the date of double CLM diseases.

To explore whether selected biomarkers explained part of the observed associations, exploratory mediation analyses were performed. Following previous analytical strategies [4, 7], candidate circulating biomarkers were screened using a two-step procedure. First, multivariable linear regression models were applied to assess associations between AIP and its modified indices and individual biomarkers. Second, Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations of these biomarkers with CLMM risk. Biomarkers that were significant in both steps and showed concordant directions of association were entered into the exploratory mediation analyses [26, 27]. Mediation was performed using the CMAverse R package (version 0.1.0) under a regression-based framework for Cox proportional hazards models [25, 27–29]. The total effect (TE), natural direct effect (NDE), natural indirect effect (NIE), and proportion mediated (PM) were estimated on the HR scale. The PM was calculated as HRDE × (HRIE − 1) / (HRDE × HRIE − 1). Statistical inference was based on 1000 bootstrap resamples to derive 95% CIs and P values [27]. All biomarker variables were standardized before analysis.

To assess whether AIP and its modified indices improved risk prediction beyond established risk factors, two nested models were constructed according to previous studies [4, 7, 30, 31]. The reference model included all covariates in Model 3, and the extended model additionally incorporated AIP and each modified index separately. As a supplementary analysis, TG and HDL-C were separately added to the reference model for comparison. Incremental predictive value was evaluated using the net reclassification index (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and Harrell’s C-index [32, 33]. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis were used to evaluate model calibration and net clinical benefit for the conventional model and models additionally including each AIP-related index. In addition, the discriminatory ability of the eight indices for incident CLMM was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) [34]. Pairwise differences in AUCs between indices were compared using the DeLong test.

Stratified analyses were performed by age at baseline (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years) and sex (female vs. male) to identify potentially susceptible subgroups, and likelihood ratio tests were used to examine effect modification. Furthermore, a series of sensitivity analyses was performed to examine the robustness of the main findings. First, to reduce the possibility of reverse causation, the analyses were repeated after excluding participants who developed CLMM within the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up. Second, missing covariate values were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Third, the competing risk of death before CLMM occurrence was addressed using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models. Fourth, to reduce potential misclassification of undiagnosed MASLD at baseline, we additionally excluded participants with probable baseline MASLD, defined as fatty liver index (FLI) ≥ 60 plus the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [30, 35]. Fifth, to assess the potential influence of additional lipid-related confounding and lipid-lowering treatment, we further adjusted for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and repeated the analyses after excluding participants who reported lipid-lowering medication use at baseline. Sixth, given the distribution of AIP-VAI, we repeated the analyses using Yeo-Johnson-transformed AIP-VAI [36, 37]. Seventh, to further assess the robustness of the incremental predictive performance analyses, we used an expanded clinical reference model that included all covariates in the primary conventional model and additionally adjusted for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), LDL-C, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Eighth, for the robustness of the multistate analyses, we performed additional sensitivity analyses using alternative 1-day and 7-day intervals instead of the 0.5-day interval for participants who entered different disease states on the same date. We also repeated the analyses after excluding all participants who entered different disease states on the same date. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1. A two-sided P value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance, whereas in biomarker-related analyses, significance was based on false discovery rate-adjusted P values < 0.05.

Results

Study participants

The analysis included 370,258 participants from the UK Biobank (mean age, 56.08 years; SD, 8.08), among whom 55.61% were female. Compared with participants who did not develop CLMM, those who developed CLMM tended to be older and less educated, have higher TDI scores, report insufficient physical activity, and have a greater likelihood of smoking. In addition, they more often had hypertension and cancer, reported greater use of antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medications, and more often had a family history of cardiometabolic disease (all P < 0.001). Associations of AIP and its modified indices with incident CLMM

Over 16.0 years of median follow-up (interquartile range [IQR]: 14.4–17.0 years), a total of 8646 incident CLMM cases (2.34%) were identified, including 8028 cases of double CLM diseases and 618 cases of triple CLM diseases. As shown in Fig. 3, Kaplan-Meier analyses showed that participants in the highest quartile of the eight AIP and its modified indices had a significantly higher cumulative hazard of CLMM than those in the lower quartiles (all log-rank P < 0.001). In multivariable-adjusted analyses, all eight indices were significantly associated with the incidence of CLMM (Table 2). Specifically, the fully adjusted (Model 3) HRs (95% CIs) for per SD increase were 1.68 (1.65–1.72) for AIP, 1.68 (1.64–1.71) for AIP-BMI, 1.66 (1.62–1.69) for AIP-WC, 1.67 (1.63–1.70) for AIP-WHtR, 1.57 (1.54–1.59) for AIP-BRI, 1.66 (1.62–1.69) for AIP-ABSI, 1.18 (1.17–1.19) for AIP-VAI, and 1.66 (1.63–1.70) for AIP-WWI. Similar results were observed for double CLM diseases, with all indices being significantly associated with higher risk, ranging from 18 to 66%. For triple CLM diseases, the HRs (95% CIs) for each 1-SD increment in AIP, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR, AIP-BRI, AIP-ABSI, AIP-VAI, and AIP-WWI were 1.93 (1.77–2.10), 1.90 (1.77–2.05), 1.88 (1.74–2.03), 1.90 (1.76–2.05), 1.72 (1.62–1.81), 1.89 (1.74–2.05), 1.20 (1.17–1.23), and 1.90 (1.75–2.06), respectively (all P < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analyses further indicated significant nonlinear associations between these eight indices and CLMM risk (all P for nonlinearity < 0.01; Fig. 4). Given the significant nonlinear associations observed, additional threshold effect analyses were performed. The identified inflection points and segment-specific HRs are shown in Table S5. Significant threshold effects were observed for all eight AIP-related indices, with all P values for the log-likelihood ratio test < 0.001.

Role of AIP and its modified indices in the trajectory of CLMM progression

Among individuals without any CLM disease at baseline, 62,962 (17.00%) developed first CLM disease, with 8646 (13.73%) further progressing to CLMM, and 618 (7.15%) progressing to triple CLM diseases (Fig. 2). In multistate models, all eight AIP and its modified indices were significantly associated with a higher risk of CLMM progression, with the strongest associations observed for AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR (Table 3). For AIP-BMI, each 1-SD increase was associated with higher risks of transition from healthy to first CLM disease (HR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.37–1.39), from first CLM disease to CLMM (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.25–1.30), and from CLMM to triple CLM diseases (HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.11–1.29). Correspondingly, for AIP-WHtR, the HRs (95% CIs) were 1.37 (1.36–1.38), 1.27 (1.24–1.29), and 1.18 (1.10–1.28), respectively. For transitions from the healthy state to a single CLM disease (all P < 0.05; Table 4), these indices showed the strongest associations with incident T2DM, with HRs (95% CIs) of 1.85 (1.83–1.87) for AIP-BMI and 1.83 (1.81–1.86) for AIP-WHtR. For transitions from a specific first CLM disease to CLMM (all P < 0.05; Table 4), each 1-SD increase in AIP-BMI, AIP-WHtR, and AIP-WC was associated with HRs (95% CIs) of 1.45 (1.41–1.50), 1.43 (1.39–1.47), and 1.42 (1.38–1.46), respectively, among participants with CVD. Similar patterns were observed for the other indices.

Mediating effects of blood biomarkers in the associations of AIP and its modified indices with incident CLMM

The study further evaluated the associations of these eight indices with 17 candidate biomarkers and assessed the relationships between these biomarkers and incident CLMM. Biomarkers that remained significant and directionally concordant across the two-step selection procedure were retained for subsequent exploratory mediation analyses (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05; Tables S6 and S7). Figure 5 presents the mediated proportions of these biomarkers in the associations between AIP-related indices and incident CLMM, while detailed estimates and corresponding 95% CIs for the TE, NDE, NIE, and PM are shown in Table S8. Overall, these biomarkers collectively ranged from 28.28% for AIP-BMI to 45.45% for AIP-VAI, with all total mediation effects being statistically significant. Among systemic inflammation markers, CRP and neutrophil count accounted for the largest proportions of the observed associations across most AIP and its modified indices, explaining approximately 4.59–5.19% of the associations. In comparison, WBC accounted for smaller proportions of the associations (1.66–1.83%), whereas monocyte count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count showed relatively limited explanatory proportions. Among liver function markers, ALT consistently accounted for the largest estimated proportion of the observed associations for most AIP-related indices, ranging from 9.18 to 10.30%, followed by ALP and GGT. In comparison, AST and albumin accounted for relatively smaller proportions. Total bilirubin generally did not show statistically significant explanatory proportions for most indices. Among renal function markers, urate accounted for the largest estimated proportion of the observed associations for most AIP-related indices, ranging from 10.51 to 11.65%, whereas cystatin C accounted for moderate proportions ranging from 4.12 to 4.47%, and urea showed limited evidence of an explanatory role. Notably, the largest overall proportion was observed for AIP-VAI, for which the estimated proportions accounted for by CRP, neutrophil count, ALT, and urate were 7.24%, 7.88%, 18.91%, and 25.24%, respectively, with an overall proportion of 45.45%.

Predictive performance of AIP and its modified indices for CLMM incidence

ROC curve analyses showed modest discriminatory ability for incident CLMM, with the highest AUC observed for AIP-BRI (AUC = 0.704), followed by AIP-BMI (AUC = 0.702) and AIP-WHtR (AUC = 0.701) (Fig. S2). Pairwise comparisons using DeLong’s test showed that AIP-BRI had statistically higher AUCs than the other indices (all P < 0.001) (Fig. S3). Incremental predictive analyses based on the fully adjusted conventional model further demonstrated that all eight AIP and its modified indices significantly improved CLMM risk discrimination and reclassification (all P < 0.001; Table 5). Numerically, the greatest increase in Harrell’s C-index was observed after adding AIP-BRI (0.806, 95% CI 0.802–0.811), followed by AIP-BMI (0.805, 95% CI 0.801–0.809) and AIP-WC (0.804, 95% CI 0.800-0.808), compared with 0.775 (95% CI 0.770–0.780) for the conventional model alone. Similarly, AIP-BRI yielded the largest NRI (0.232, 95% CI 0.208–0.259). For IDI, the numerically largest improvement was observed for AIP-BMI (0.040, 95% CI 0.030–0.050), whereas AIP, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR each yielded an IDI of 0.039. In additional analysis, adding TG or HDL-C to the conventional model modestly improved predictive performance, with C-index values of 0.783 and 0.787, respectively (Table S9). Calibration plots suggested acceptable model fit, while decision curve analysis supported the additional net clinical benefit of incorporating AIP-related indices into the conventional model (Figs. S4 and S5).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Stratified analyses by age and sex further showed that the positive associations between these indices and CLMM risk were present in all subgroups, with relatively stronger associations observed in participants aged < 60 years and in women, indicating possible effect modification (Tables S10 and S11). After FDR correction for multiple comparisons, all associations in the primary Cox and multistate transition analyses remained statistically significant (Tables S12–S14). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings. Excluding participants who developed CLMM during the first 2 years or 5 years of follow-up did not materially alter the associations, and all eight AIP-related indices remained significantly associated with incident CLMM (Tables S15 and S16). The findings were also consistent after multiple imputation for missing covariates. Compared with the primary complete-case analysis, the effect estimates were slightly attenuated but remained statistically significant for all eight AIP-related indices, with HRs for incident CLMM ranging from 1.18 to 1.63 in the imputed analysis versus 1.18 to 1.68 in the primary analysis (Table S17). After accounting for the competing risk of death, the associations between AIP and its modified indices and incident CLMM remained materially unchanged (Table S18). After applying the additional exclusion of participants with probable baseline MASLD based on FLI and cardiometabolic risk criteria, all eight AIP-related indices remained positively associated with incident CLMM (Table S19). The associations also persisted after further adjustment for LDL-C and after excluding participants who reported lipid-lowering medication use at baseline (Tables S20 and S21). AIP-VAI remained significantly associated with incident CLMM after Yeo-Johnson transformation (Table S22). For the multistate analyses, consistent results were obtained when alternative 1-day and 7-day intervals were used for participants who entered different disease states on the same date, as well as after excluding all participants with same-day disease-state entries (Tables S23–S25). For predictive performance, using the expanded conventional model also yielded results consistent with the main analysis, and AIP and its modified indices continued to improve discrimination and reclassification for incident CLMM (Table S26).

Discussion

Our study of 370,258 participants from the UK Biobank showed that all eight AIP and its modified indices were independently associated with an increased risk of incident CLMM. Among them, AIP-BMI, AIP-WHtR, and AIP-WC showed the strongest associations with incident CLMM. Multistate modeling further suggested that these indices were relevant not only to disease onset but also to subsequent progression across different stages of CLMM. Specifically, for transitions from healthy status to a first CLM disease, AIP-related indices showed their strongest associations with incident T2DM; the highest estimates were observed for AIP-BMI (HR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.83–1.87) and AIP-WHtR (HR, 1.83; 95% CI, 1.81–1.86), followed by AIP-WC (HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.78–1.83). For transitions from a specific CLM disease to CLMM, the strongest associations were observed for progression from CVD to CLMM. In addition, biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation, liver dysfunction, and renal impairment jointly accounted for a substantial proportion of the observed associations, with the total mediated proportion ranging from 28.28 to 45.45%. Finally, these indices also demonstrated improved predictive performance for CLMM risk, with AIP-BRI and AIP-BMI showing relatively better performance than the original AIP.

Our findings extend the emerging literature on multimorbidity. Recent UK Biobank studies have shown that these indices are relevant to the incidence of CMM and CRMM [38–41]. However, those multimorbidity frameworks did not explicitly integrate liver disease. Emerging evidence has proposed that CVD, liver-related disorders, and T2DM should be considered as an interconnected disease spectrum requiring integrated management [9, 10]. In the present study, we provide prospective evidence showing that elevated AIP and its modified indices were associated with a higher risk of incident CLMM, thereby addressing an important gap in the current literature. Our results suggested that each 1-SD increase in these indices was associated with an 18–68% higher risk of CLMM. Among them, AIP-BMI, AIP-WHtR, and AIP-WC showed consistently stronger associations, with larger effect estimates than the other indices. The positive association between higher AIP and its modified indices and incident CLMM is biologically plausible and is broadly consistent with prior AIP-focused studies on cardiometabolic outcomes. Recent work has shown that cumulative AIP exposure is associated with cardiometabolic multimorbidity, while cumulative changes in AIP-derived obesity indices are associated with incident cardiovascular disease [14, 38]. In parallel, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have linked elevated AIP to greater coronary artery disease risk, severity, and poorer cardiovascular prognosis [42, 43].

Our results add to this literature by demonstrating that the association is not restricted to single cardiovascular outcomes and persists when liver disease is incorporated into a multimorbidity trajectory model. Although numerous studies have explored the relationship between AIP and its modified indices and individual CLM conditions, including CVD, chronic liver diseases, and T2DM [44–46], most of that research has described disease status at a single point in time. Such designs offer limited insight into how these indices may reflect the temporal sequence of disease accumulation. To address this gap, our study applied a multi-state modeling framework to systematically characterize how these indices relate to CLMM progression. Our results showed that, for transitions from the healthy state to a first CLM disease, AIP and its modified indices were most strongly associated with incident T2DM. Among these indices, AIP-WC and AIP-WHtR showed the most consistent associations across transitions, suggesting that the combination of atherogenic dyslipidaemia with measures of abdominal adiposity, particularly when standardized for body size, may best reflect the chronic metabolic milieu that drives sequential disease accumulation. In addition, AIP-WC showed the strongest disease-specific associations from CVD-to-CLMM transitions, implying that absolute abdominal adiposity may be especially relevant when excess visceral fat directly accelerates dysglycaemia, hepatic steatosis, and secondary metabolic deterioration after CVD. This interpretation is compatible with the recognized role of abdominal obesity as a driver of insulin resistance and ectopic fat deposition and with prior studies showing the added clinical value of AIP-derived obesity composites [14, 47].

The exploratory mediation analyses provide preliminary evidence that inflammatory, hepatic, and renal biomarkers statistically accounted for part of the observed associations between AIP-related indices and CLMM. These biomarkers accounted for approximately 28.28–45.45% of the observed associations. Across most indices, CRP and neutrophil count accounted for the largest proportions among inflammatory biomarkers; ALT, ALP, and GGT showed the largest explanatory proportions among hepatic biomarkers; and urate and cystatin C showed the largest explanatory proportions among renal biomarkers. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting associations of higher AIP with chronic kidney disease, early liver injury, hyperuricaemia, and inflammation-related cardiometabolic risk. For example, a national cohort of 9112 middle-aged and older adults reported that both AIP and hs-CRP were independently associated with incident diabetes, and exploratory mediation analyses further showed that hs-CRP mediated 5.7% of the association, supporting a role for chronic low-grade inflammation in AIP-related metabolic progression [48]. Similarly, another prospective cohort study reported a joint association of elevated AIP and hs-CRP with incident CVD, with 6.6% of the association explained by hs-CRP [49]. In addition, a longitudinal analysis has shown that higher AIP predicts incident hyperuricaemia [50]. Collectively, these studies provide contextual support for our biomarker-based findings and suggest that the observed associations between AIP-related indices and CLMM may partly reflect inflammatory, hepatic, and renal-metabolic biomarker patterns. Nevertheless, given the observational nature of the current mediation analysis, these pathways should still be interpreted cautiously, and further mechanistic and causal studies are needed to determine the biological sequence of these pathways.

In addition to their etiological relevance, AIP and its modified indices provided additional prognostic information for CLMM risk prediction. After incorporation into the conventional model, these indices yielded statistically significant improvements in C-index, NRI, and IDI, suggesting improved discrimination and risk reclassification. However, the AUCs of individual indices were modest and the absolute increase in C-index was limited. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as supporting the use of AIP-related indices as standalone diagnostic tools. Rather, they may serve as complementary markers for population-level risk stratification, particularly for identifying individuals at elevated risk who may benefit from closer monitoring or preventive assessment.

Several strengths of this study merit emphasis. The analysis was based on a large prospective cohort, enabling robust assessment of both incident CLMM and disease progression. We comprehensively compared eight AIP and its modified indices and integrated conventional survival analyses, multistate modeling, exploratory mediation analyses, and prediction analyses within a single framework. The consistency of the findings across these complementary approaches enhances confidence in the overall robustness of the findings. This study also has limitations. First, the observational design does not allow causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Second, AIP and its modified indices and mediators were assessed at baseline only, so temporal changes during follow-up were not captured. Third, disease ascertainment relied mainly on hospital and registry records, which may have introduced some degree of misclassification, particularly for MASLD. Fourth, UK Biobank is subject to healthy volunteer bias, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Participants in UK Biobank are generally healthier and less socioeconomically deprived than the general population, and therefore the absolute incidence of CLMM may be underestimated [51, 52]. Although this selection bias may have a smaller impact on internal exposure–outcome associations than on disease prevalence or incidence estimates, our findings may not be directly generalizable to other populations. Further validation in more representative and ethnically diverse cohorts is needed. Fifth, residual confounding from lipid-lowering therapies cannot be completely excluded, although our findings remained consistent after further adjustment for LDL-C and exclusion of baseline lipid-lowering medication users. Finally, the mediation analyses should be interpreted as exploratory because baseline measurements of both AIP-related indices and biomarkers preclude clear temporal ordering between exposure and mediator, and unmeasured confounding between biomarkers and outcomes may have influenced the estimated indirect effects. Future studies with repeated longitudinal biomarker measurements are needed to better clarify these temporal and potential mechanistic pathways.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that elevated AIP and its modified indices were independently linked to greater risks of incident CLMM and its progression across successive disease stages. Among these indices, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR appeared to be the most consistently associated indices with longitudinal disease progression, whereas AIP-BRI showed relatively higher discriminatory performance for incident CLMM. In exploratory mediation analyses, markers of systemic inflammation and liver and kidney dysfunction accounted for part of the observed associations. These findings suggest that AIP-derived composite indices may serve as simple and accessible markers for CLMM risk stratification and early identification of higher-risk individuals.
