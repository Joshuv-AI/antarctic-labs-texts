On inequalities between norms of partial derivatives on convex domains
Alexander Plakhov, Vladimir Protasov

Abstract
We consider inequalities between L p L_{p} -norms of partial derivatives, p ∈ [ 1 , + ∞ ] p\in[1,+\infty] , for bivariate concave functions on a convex domain that vanish on the boundary. Can the ratio between those norms be arbitrarily large? If not, what is the upper bound? We show that for p = 1 p=1 , the ratio is always bounded and find sharp estimates, while for p > 1 p>1 , the answer depends on the geometry of the domain.

1. Introduction

We consider a convex compact domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and the class
{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}
of continuous concave functions
u(x,y)

on
\Omega
such that
u\rfloor_{\,\partial\Omega}=0
and the
gradient
\nabla u(x,y)\,=\,(u_{x},u_{y})

is uniformly bounded over all points
(x,y)\,\in\,int\,\Omega
where it is defined. Since concave continuous functions are absolutely
continuous on
\Omega
, the gradient is defined a.e. and belongs to
L_{p}(\Omega)

for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
. For arbitrary
f\in L_{p}(\Omega)
, we use the standard notation

\bigl\|f\bigr\|_{p}\ =\ \left(\int_{\Omega}|f(x,y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy\right)^{1/p},\ p\in[1,+\infty);\qquad\bigl\|f\bigr\|_{\infty}\ =\ {\rm ess\,sup}_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|f(x,y)|.

We are interested in the value

K_{p}\ =\ K_{p}(\Omega)\quad=\quad\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\Omega}}}{{u\neq 0}}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{p}}{\|u_{y}\|_{p}}\,.

(1)

It will be shown that in the case
p=1
the value
K_{1}(\Omega)

is always finite and can be efficiently estimated.
For
p>1
, the value
K_{p}(\Omega)
can be infinite or finite depending on the geometry of the domain
\Omega
. We obtain upper bounds for
K_{p}
in terms of
geometric characteristics of
\Omega
. For
p=\infty
, this value is found explicitly
for every domain. The problem of computing the optimal
domains that minimize
K_{p}
under some natural assumptions is formulated.
It is interesting that for all
p>1
, neither the Euclidean ball nor any other
domain with a smooth boundary is optimal (Theorems
6
and
8
).
On the other hand, in
L_{1}
, the Euclidean ball is optimal,
along with infinitely many other optimal domains.

For
p=2
, the problem of estimating
K_{p}
is applied to the study of the
surfaces of smallest resistance in the Newton aerodynamical problem
[
6
]
.
Estimating the value
K_{p}

can also be attributed to the so-called inequalities between derivatives,
which is an important branch of the approximation theory known from the classical
results of Bernstein, Kolmogorov, Landau, Nikolsky, Calderon, Zygmund, etc. Such inequalities involve
norms of derivatives of various orders in different functional spaces.
See
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
7
]
for an extensive bibliography.

Remark 1
.

Without the concavity condition on the function
u
,
the value
K_{p}
is infinite for every convex domain
\Omega
.
To see this it suffices to
take an arbitrary nonzero
C^{1}
function
u_{0}(x,y)
that vanishes on
\,\partial\Omega

and multiply it by a highly oscillating function
\varphi(x)
, for example,
by
\varphi_{N}(x)=\cos\,Nx
. Then
the ratio of norms of partial derivatives for the
function
u_{N}(x,y)\,=\,u_{0}(x,y)\varphi_{N}(x)

tends to infinity as
N\to\infty
.

Remark 2
.

The value
K_{p}
admits an intuitive interpretation. Suppose we are interested in measuring the average slope of a hill. The hill has a convex shape and rests on the flat base
\Omega
, hence its upper surface is the graph of a function
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}
. Of course, it is reasonable to take
\|u\|_{p}
as the average measure. Suppose, however, that we are limited in our actions and can only measure slope from north to south or from east to west, thus obtaining
\|u_{x}\|_{p}
or
\|u_{y}\|_{p}
. The question is, how large is the discrepancy between these quantities, expressed by their ratio
K_{p}
?

The ratio
\frac{\|u_{x}\|_{p}}{\|u_{y}\|_{p}}
can be made arbitrarily large or arbitrarily small by replacing
u(x,y)
with
u(kx,y)
, where the coefficient
k
tends to infinity or to zero. Accordingly, the domain of
u
is uniformly stretched
k
times along the
x
-axis. This means that the quantity
K_{p}(\Omega)
can be made arbitrarily large and the quantity

k_{p}(\Omega)\quad=\quad\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\Omega}}}{{u\neq 0}}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{p}}{\|u_{y}\|_{p}}

(2)

can be made arbitrarily small just by stretching and compressing
\Omega.
However, the ratio
\frac{K_{p}(\Omega)}{k_{p}(\Omega)}
is of interest. We pose the following Minimax problem:

Problem 1
.

Find

r_{p}\ =\ \inf_{\Omega}\frac{K_{p}(\Omega)}{k_{p}(\Omega)}.

In this paper we find a solution to this problem for
p=\infty
:
r_{\infty}=1
,   and a partial solution for
p=1
:
r_{1}\leq 4
.   The solution for
1<p<\infty
is unknown.

2. The case
{\boldsymbol{p}}=1

We are going to show that
K_{1}(\Omega)\,\leq\,2\,\frac{w_{x}}{w_{y}}
,
where
w_{x},w_{y}
are, respectively, the horizontal and vertical sides of the circumscribed
rectangle of
\Omega
. Moreover, this estimate in general cannot be improved, i.e.,
it is sharp for some domains
\Omega
. Sometimes we consider an extension
of
{\mathcal{U}}_{\Omega}
to the class of arbitrary
concave functions on
\Omega
vanishing on
\partial\Omega
.
In this case
u
may be discontinuous on the boundary and its partial derivatives
u_{x},u_{y}

are understood in the sense of distributions.

Theorem 1
.

For every
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}
, we have

K_{1}\ =\ \sup_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}\quad\leq\quad 2\ \frac{w_{x}}{w_{y}}\ ,

(3)

where
w_{x},w_{y}
are the sidelengths of the rectangle circumscribed around
\Omega

with the sides parallel to the
x
and
y
-axes respectively.
Inequality (
3
) becomes an equality if and only if
\Omega
contains
a vertical segment with ends on the horizontal sides of the circumscribed rectangle.

This theorem immediately gives an above estimate for Minimax problem
1
. Indeed,

\frac{K_{1}(\Omega)}{k_{1}(\Omega)}\ =\ \frac{\sup_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}}{\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}}\ =\ \sup_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}\,\sup_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\ \frac{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}\ \leq\ 2\ \frac{w_{x}}{w_{y}}\ 2\ \frac{w_{y}}{w_{x}}\ =\ 4.

(4)

It follows that
r_{1}\leq 4.

This theorem will be obtained as a corollary of Theorems
2

and Theorem
4
proved below. Then in Theorem
5

we estimate the ratio between the maximal and minimal
L_{1}
-norms
of directional derivatives
\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}
over all possible pairs of
unit vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
.

We begin with auxiliary results.
For arbitrary
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we denote by
\bigl[a(t),b(t)\bigr]

the segment of intersection of
\Omega
with the horizontal line
y=t
and

m(t)\,=\,\max\limits_{x\in[a,b]}u(x,t)
on this segment.
If this line does not intersect
\Omega
, then the segment is empty and
m=0
.

Lemma 1
.

For every
y
, we have
\int_{a}^{b}\,\bigl|u_{x}(x,y)\bigr|\,dx\,=2m
, where
a,b,m
are
a(y),b(y)
and
m(y)

respectively.

Proof
. Since for every
y
, the univariate function

u(\cdot\,,\,y)
is concave,
its derivative is non-increasing. Hence,

u_{x}(x,y)\geq 0
for
x<\tau
and
u_{x}(x,y)\leq 0
for
x>\tau
, where

x=\tau
is a point of maximum. Therefore,

\int_{a}^{\tau}|u_{x}(x,y)|\,dx\,=\,\int_{a}^{\tau}u_{x}(x,y)\,dx\,=\,u(\tau,y)-u(a,y)=m-0=m
.
Similarly,
\int_{\tau}^{b}|u_{x}(x,y)|\,dx\,=\,\int_{\tau}^{b}-u_{x}(x,y)\,dx\,=\,u(\tau,y)-u(b,y)=m-0=m
. Hence,
\int_{a}^{b}\,|u_{x}(x,y)|\,dx\,=\,m+m\,=2m
.

\Box

Lemma
1
immediately implies

Proposition 1
.

Let a segment
[c,d]
of the axis
OY
be the projection of the set
\Omega
. Then
\|u_{x}\|_{1}\,=\,\int_{c}^{d}\,2m(y)\,dy
.

Proposition
1
guarantees, in particular, that the supremum and infimum of the value

\|u_{x}\|_{1}

over a bounded set of functions
u
are always attained in distributions.
Let us denote by
w=d-c
the length of the projection of
\Omega

to
OY
. Every point
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\Omega
with the second coordinate equal to
c

is called
lower
, a point
{\boldsymbol{d}}\in\Omega
with the second coordinate equal to
d

is
upper
.

Now we are ready to formulate the main result.

Theorem 2
.

For every
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}
, we have;

wM\ \leq\ \|u_{x}\|_{1}\ \leq\ 2wM\,,

(5)

where
\max\limits_{(x,y)\in\Omega}u(x,y)=M
.

Proof
. Since for every
y\in[c,d]
, we have
m(y)\leq M
,
Proposition 1 implies that
\|u_{x}\|_{1}\leq\int_{c}^{d}\,M\,dx\,=\,2(d-c)M
, which proves the upper bound. To establish the lower bound, we apply
the concavity of
u
: let
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\Omega
be a point of maximum of
u
; then
\ u\bigl((1-t){\boldsymbol{c}}+t{\boldsymbol{z}}\bigr)\,\geq\,(1-t)u({\boldsymbol{c}})\,+\,t\,u({\boldsymbol{z}})\,=\,tM
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
Hence, the maximum of
u
on the horizontal line passing through the point

(1-t){\boldsymbol{c}}+t{\boldsymbol{z}}
is at least
tM
.
The same is true for every point
(1-t){\boldsymbol{d}}+t{\boldsymbol{z}}
of the segment
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{d}}]
.
Now Proposition 1 yields
\|u_{x}\|_{1}\,=\,\int_{c}^{d}\,2m(x)\,dx\,=\,\int_{c}^{z}\,2m(y)\,dy\,+\,\int_{z}^{d}\,2m(y)\,dx\,\geq\,2(z-c)\int_{0}^{1}\,tM\,dt\,+\,2(d-z)\int_{0}^{1}\,tM\,dt\,=\,(d-c)M
.

\Box

Both bounds for the value
\|u_{x}\|_{1}
in (
5
) are sharp as the following
theorem states:

Theorem 3
.

The lower bound in (
5
) is attained if and only if there exists a point of maximum
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\Omega
of
u
and lower and upper points
{\boldsymbol{c}}
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}
of
\Omega
respectively
such that the function
u
is linear on each of the two segments
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{c}}]
and
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{d}}]

and every point of those segments is a point of maximum for the restriction of
u

to the horizontal line passing through it.

The upper bound can be attained only in distributions.
It occurs if and only if there are
lower and upper points of
\Omega

such that an open segment between them consists of points of maximum of
u
.

Proof
.
If
\|u_{x}\|_{1}=2wM
, then every horizontal cross-section of
\Omega

intersects the set
S
of points of maxima of
u
, which is impossible
for cross-sections close to the lower and upper ones.
If
u
is discontinuous, then
this equality holds in distributions
precisely when all horizontal cross sections, except of the extreme ones, intersect
S
.
Since
S
is convex, this means that it contains an open interval connecting some lower and upper points of
\Omega
.

From the proof of the lower bound in (
5
) it
follows that
\|u_{x}\|_{1}=wM
if and only
if
\ u\bigl((1-t){\boldsymbol{c}}+t{\boldsymbol{z}}\bigr)\,=\,(1-t)u({\boldsymbol{c}})\,+\,t\,u({\boldsymbol{z}})\,=\,tM
for every
t\in[0,1]
, i.e.,
u
is linear on the segment
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{c}}]
.
Furthermore,
the maximum of
u
on the horizontal line passing through the point

(1-t){\boldsymbol{c}}+t{\boldsymbol{z}}
must be equal to
tM
, i.e., must be achieved
at this point. This completes the proof for the segment
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{c}}]
.
For the segment
[{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{d}}]
, the proof is the same.

\Box

Remark 3
.

Theorem 1 implies that for every convex domain
\Omega
, the lower
bound in (
5
) is achieved on a certain admissible function
u

and the upper bound is achieved in distributions on another function.
A question arises can they be achieved on the same function but for different
directional derivatives? To attack this problem we need to introduce some further notation.

The intersection of
\Omega
with its line of support is called an
edge
of
\Omega
.
One-point edges a precisely the extreme points of
\Omega
. We say that a one-point edge
is
parallel
to a given line if it is an intersection of
\Omega
with a line of support parallel to this line.
For an arbitrary unit vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}
, we consider the
directional derivative
u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}=(\nabla u,{\boldsymbol{h}})
.
We call
the width of
\Omega
parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}

a pair of lines of support parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and also the distance between them.

Corollary 1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
be arbitrary unit vectors and

w_{i}
be the width parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}
; then

\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}\quad\leq\quad 2\ \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}\,.

(6)

The equality can be achieved only in the sense of distributions.
This occurs precisely when
u
is constant on a line
connecting two points
{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{d}}
on the opposite edges parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}
,
linear on each half-plane about the line
{\boldsymbol{c}}{\boldsymbol{d}}
and vanishing
on the lines of support parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
. In this case
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
is parallel to
{\boldsymbol{c}}{\boldsymbol{d}}
.

From Corollary
1
follows the following result generalizing the estimate (
4
) for Minimax problem
1

\frac{\sup_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}}{\inf_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}}\ =\ \sup_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}\ \sup_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}\ \leq\ 2\ \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}\ 2\ \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}\ =\ 4.

Proof of Theorem
1
. We apply Corollary
1
to
the unit coordinate vectors
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}
.

\Box

Corollary
1
gives the geometric conditions on the domain
\Omega
and directions
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
under which the inequality

\sup_{u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}}\,\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}\quad\leq\quad 2\ \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}\,.

(7)

becomes an equality.

Corollary 2
.

For a given convex domain
\Omega
and
non-collinear unit vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
, the inequality (
7
) becomes an equality
if and only if
there is a straight line parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
intersecting two
edges of
\Omega
parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}
.

Now let us turn to the uniform bounds over all directions
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
.
Denote the biggest and the smallest widths of
\Omega
by
w_{\max}
and
w_{\min}

respectively.
Applying (
6
) we obtain

Theorem 4
.

For a given convex domain
\Omega
, we have

\sup_{u,{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\,\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}\quad\leq\quad 2\,\frac{w_{\max}}{w_{\min}}\ .

(8)

Theorem
4
yields the conditions under which the upper bound (

)
is sharp.

Theorem 5
.

For a convex domain
\Omega
, the inequality (
8
) becomes an

if and only if there are the greatest and the smallest width
of
\Omega

with orthogonal directions. If this is the case, then

{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
are parallel to those directions respectively.

Proof.

For each maximal width, the corresponding opposite edges possess
a common perpendicular with ends on
\Omega
. By Corollary 1, if those lines are parallel
to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}
, then the segment
{\boldsymbol{c}}{\boldsymbol{d}}
must be their common perpendicular
parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
.

\Box

Corollary 3
.

If the inequality (
8
) becomes an equality, then
the directions
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
are orthogonal and correspond to the
maximal and minimal widths of
\Omega
.

Example 1
.

If
\Omega=\bigl\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\ x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\bigr\}
is a disc, then there exists a unique, up to rotations and normalization, function realizing (in distributions)
the biggest ratio
\frac{\|u_{x}\|_{1}}{\|u_{y}\|_{1}}=2
.
This is
u(x,y)=1-|x|
.

Example 2
.

If
\Omega
is a non-isosceles triangle,

\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{1}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{1}}\,=\,\frac{2a}{h}

3. The case
\ {\boldsymbol{p}}\ \mathbf{\in\,(1,+\infty)}

If
1<p<\infty
, then the supremum of the ratio between
\|u_{x}\|_{p}
and
\|u_{y}\|_{p}

is finite for some domains
\Omega
and infinite for others. The main result of this section, Theorem
6
, gives a criterion for deciding between those two cases.
To formulate it we need to introduce some notation.
The
tangent cone
to
\Omega
with the vertex at
\xi\in\partial\Omega
is the intersection of all closed half-planes containing
\Omega
and bounded by lines of support through
\xi
.
A point
\xi\in\partial\Omega
is called
regular
, if there is only one line of support to
\Omega
through
\xi
, and
singular
otherwise. Equivalently,
\xi
is regular, if the tangent cone to
\Omega
with the vertex at
\xi
is a half-plane, and singular otherwise.

Consider a tangent cone to
\Omega
with the vertex at a singular point of
\partial\Omega
.
Any straight line that intersects the tangent cone by its vertex is called an
angular
support line.

Figure 1:
In figure (a), both vertical lines of support to
\Omega
are angular, while in figure (b), both lines are not angular: the left line is tangent, and the right one is half-tangent to
\Omega
.

Theorem 6
.

For a domain
\Omega
, we have
K_{p}<\infty

if and only if both vertical (that is, parallel to the
y
-axis) lines of support
of
\Omega
are angular.

Proof
.
(Necessity. If at least one vertical line of support is not angular,
then
K_{p}=\infty
).

Denote by
l_{\alpha}
the line of support with the outward normal
(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)
,
-\pi<\alpha\leq\pi
, and by
\Pi_{\alpha}
the closed half-plane bounded by
l_{\alpha}
and containing
\Omega
. By the hypothesis, one of the lines
l_{0}
,
l_{\pi}
is not angular; let it be
l_{0}
. Fix a value
0<\varphi<\pi/2
and set

\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega^{\prime}_{\varphi}=\cap_{|\alpha|\geq\varphi}\Pi_{\alpha};

see Fig.
2
. Since
l_{0}
is not angular, there is an open arc of the boundary
\partial\Omega
contained in the interior of
\Omega^{\prime}
. All lines of support at points of this arc correspond to angles in
(-\varphi,\,\varphi)
. Choose a point
\xi
on the arc, and denote
r=\text{dist}(\xi,\partial\Omega^{\prime})>0
.

Take
\varepsilon>0
and denote

D=D(\varphi,\varepsilon):=\{(x,y):\ \text{dist}\big((x,y),\xi\big)\leq r/2,\ \,\text{dist}\big((x,y),\partial\Omega\big)\geq\varepsilon\}\cap\Omega

(see Fig.
2
). Let
u=u_{\varphi,\varepsilon}
be the smallest concave function satisfying
u\rfloor_{\partial\Omega}=0
and
u\rfloor_{D}=1
. Each point of the graph of
u
belongs

(i) either to the relative interior point of
D\times\{1\}
;

(ii) or to a plane through a point of
\partial D\times\{1\}
and a point of
(\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\Omega^{\prime})\times\{0\}
;

(iii) or to a plane through a point of
\partial D\times\{1\}
and a point of
(\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega^{\prime})\times\{0\}
.

Correspondingly,
\Omega
is the disjoint union of three sets,
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, where
\Omega_{0}
is the interior of
D
, and
\nabla u\rfloor_{\Omega_{0}}=0
;
\arg(\nabla u)\not\in[-\varphi,\,\varphi]
at each regular point of
\Omega_{1}
;  and
\arg(\nabla u)\in[-\varphi,\,\varphi]
at each regular point of
\Omega_{2}
.

Figure 2:
Here
\Omega
is the upper half-circle, and
\Omega^{\prime}
contains
\Omega

Thus, one has

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dxdy=I_{1}+I_{2},

where

I_{j}=I_{j}(\varphi,\varepsilon)=\int_{\Omega_{j}}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dxdy,\quad j=1,\,2.

For regular points of
\Omega_{1}
one has
|\nabla u|\leq\dfrac{1}{r/2}
, hence

I_{1}\leq|\Omega|(2/r)^{p}.

Here and in what follows,
|\Omega|
means the area of
\Omega
.

Further, the set
\Omega_{2}
contains the layer

of width
\varepsilon
between

and
\partial\Omega
. The area of this layer is of the order of
\varepsilon
, and
|\nabla u|=1/\varepsilon
at all points of the layer, therefore

I_{2}\geq\int_{L}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dxdy\sim\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{p}}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

On the other hand, at each regular point of
\Omega_{2}
one has
|u_{y}|\leq\tan\varphi\,|u_{x}|
, hence

|\nabla u|^{p}=\big(u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2}\big)^{p/2}\leq(1+\tan^{2}\varphi)^{p/2}u_{x}^{p}=\frac{1}{\cos^{p}\varphi}\,u_{x}^{p},

and

\int_{\Omega_{2}}u_{x}^{p}\,dxdy\geq\cos^{p}\varphi\int_{\Omega_{2}}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dxdy=\cos^{p}\varphi\,I_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\int_{\Omega_{2}}u_{y}^{2}\,dxdy\leq(1-\cos^{p}\varphi)\,I_{2}.

Fix
\varphi
(and therefore,

) and let
\varepsilon\to 0
; then
I_{2}
goes to infinity, and
I_{1}
is bounded, and therefore, the integrals

I_{1x}:=\int_{\Omega_{1}}u_{x}^{p}\,dxdy\quad\text{and}\quad I_{1y}:=\int_{\Omega_{1}}u_{y}^{p}\,dxdy

are also bounded. It follows that

\frac{\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{p}\,dx\,dy}{\int_{\Omega}u_{y}^{p}\,dx\,dy}=\frac{\int_{\Omega_{1}}u_{x}^{p}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega_{2}}u_{x}^{p}\,dx\,dy}{\int_{\Omega_{1}}u_{y}^{2}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega_{2}}u_{y}^{2}\,dx\,dy}\geq\frac{I_{1x}+\cos^{p}\varphi\,I_{2}}{I_{1y}+(1-\cos^{p}\varphi)\,I_{2}},

hence the lower partial limit of the ratio

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\,\frac{\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{p}\,dx\,dy}{\int_{\Omega}u_{y}^{p}\,dx\,dy}\geq\frac{\cos^{p}\varphi}{1-\cos^{p}\varphi}.

Since
\varphi
can be made arbitrarily small, the limit of the ratio is
+\infty
. This proves the part of necessity of Theorem
6
.

(Sufficiency. If both vertical lines of support are angular,
then
K_{p}<\infty
).

Without loss of generality it may be assumed
that
\Omega
has the extreme left point at the origin
(0,0)
.
Its extreme right point
has coordinates
(w_{x},c)
, where
w_{x}>0
is the length of the projection
of
\Omega
to the
x
-axis.

After the linear change of variables

x\ =\ \frac{w_{x}}{2}\,\tilde{x}\,,\qquad y\ =\ \frac{c}{2}\,\tilde{x}\ +\ \tilde{y}

(9)

the domain
(x,y)\in\Omega

is mapped to a domain
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\in\tilde{\Omega}
with the extreme
left point
(0,0)
and extreme right point
(2,0)
. The Jacobian of this transform is equal to
\frac{w_{x}}{2}

and
u_{y}\,=\,u_{\tilde{y}},\ u_{x}\,=\,\frac{2}{w_{x}}\,u_{\tilde{x}}\,-\,\frac{c}{w_{x}}\,u_{\tilde{y}}
. Therefore,

\bigl\|u_{y}\bigr\|_{p}\ =\ \bigl(w_{x}/2\bigr)^{1/p}\bigl\|u_{\tilde{y}}\bigr\|_{p}\,;\qquad\bigl\|u_{x}\bigr\|_{p}\ \leq\ \bigl(w_{x}/2\bigr)^{1/p}\left(\frac{2}{w_{x}}\,\bigl\|u_{\tilde{x}}\bigr\|_{p}\ +\ \frac{|\,c\,|}{w_{x}}\,\bigl\|u_{\tilde{y}}\bigr\|_{p}\right)\,,

where the
L_{p}
-norms of
u_{\tilde{x}},u_{\tilde{y}}
are computed over the
domain
\tilde{\Omega}
. Thus,

\frac{\|u_{x}\|_{p}}{\|u_{y}\|_{p}}\quad\leq\quad\frac{2}{w_{x}}\,\frac{\|u_{\tilde{x}}\|_{p}}{\|u_{\tilde{y}}\|_{p}}\ +\ \frac{|\,c\,|}{w_{x}}\ .

(10)

Thus,
it suffices to prove the theorem for the domain
\tilde{\Omega}
.
To simplify the notation
we will assume that the original domain
\Omega
has the extreme left and
right angular points
(0,0),\,(2,0)
,
so we shall not use tildes. This way we obtain an upper bound
for
\frac{\|u_{\tilde{x}}\|_{p}}{\|u_{\tilde{y}}\|_{p}}
and then substitute it to (
10
).

Let
\Omega
be bounded below and above by the graphs of functions
\varphi_{1}(x)

and
\varphi_{2}(x)
respectively. So,
\varphi_{1}
is convex,
\varphi_{2}

is concave on the segment
[0,2]
and both those functions
vanish at the ends of the segment.

We estimate the ratio of
L_{p}
-norms of
u_{x}
and
u_{y}
on the
left part
\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega\,=\,\bigl\{(x,y)\in\Omega:\,0\leq x\leq 1\bigr\}
, then the same argument can be applied to the right part. In other words, we
prove that
\|u_{x}\|_{L_{p}(\frac{1}{2}\Omega)}\leq M_{p}\|u_{y}\|_{L_{p}(\frac{1}{2}\Omega)}

for some constant
M_{p}
.
Then the same argument gives the same
estimate for the right part of
\Omega
. Summing two parts, we get

\|u_{x}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq M_{p}\|u_{y}\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}
, and hence
K_{p}\leq M_{p}
.
We will use the short notation
\|u\|=\|u\|_{L_{p}(\frac{1}{2}\Omega)}
, and
similarly for
u_{x},u_{y}
. We begin the the following key lemma.

Lemma 2
.

At every point

(x,y)\in\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega
where
u
is differentiable, we have
\,\bigl|u_{x}\bigl|\,\leq\,\frac{1}{x}\,\bigl(\,u\,-\,y\,u_{y}\bigr)
.

Proof
. Denote by
L(x,y)
the tangent plane to the
graph of the function
u
at the point
(x,y)\in\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega
; see Fig.
3
. The equation of this
plane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in L(x,y)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\ z^{\prime}-z\ =\ (x^{\prime}-x)\,u_{x}(x,y)\ +\ (y^{\prime}-y)\,u_{y}(x,y)\,,

where
z\,=\,u(x,y)
.
Since
u
is concave, it follows that

L(x,y)
is located above
the graph. i.e.,
z^{\prime}\,\geq\,u(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
At the point
(0,0)
, we have
u=0
and therefore,

z^{\prime}\geq 0
. Substituting the point
(0,0,z^{\prime})
to the equation of the plane, we get

z+(-x)u_{x}+(-y)u_{y}\,=\,z^{\prime}\geq 0
. Thus,

\,u\,-\,x\,u_{x}\,-\,y\,u_{y}\,\geq\,0
and so,

u_{x}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{x}\,\bigl(\,u\,-\,y\,u_{y}\bigr)
. This proves the lemma in the case
u_{x}(x,y)\geq 0
.

If
u_{x}(x,y)<0
, then we
apply the same argument to the
right angular point
(2,0)
, where we also have
z^{\prime}\geq 0
.
Substituting the point
(2,0,z^{\prime})
to the equation of
L(x,y)
, we obtain

z+(2-x)u_{x}+(-y)u_{y}\,\geq 0
,
which implies
u_{x}\,\geq\,\frac{1}{2-x}\,\bigl(\,-u\,+\,y\,u_{y}\bigr)
.
Consequently,
|u_{x}|\,=\,-u_{x}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{2-x}\,\bigl(\,u\,-\,y\,u_{y}\bigr)
. The right hand side must be positive,
and since
x\leq 1
, we have

2-x\geq x
, which implies
|u_{x}|\,\leq\,\frac{1}{x}\,\bigl(\,u\,-\,y\,u_{y}\bigr)
. This completes the proof of the lemma.

.

Now we continue the proof of Theorem
6
. Lemma
2
yield that

\bigl\|u_{x}\bigr\|\ \leq\ \Bigl\|\frac{1}{x}\,u\Bigr\|\ +\ \Bigl\|\frac{y}{x}\,u_{y}\Bigr\|\,,

(11)

where we use the short notation for the functions

\frac{1}{x}\,u\,=\,\frac{1}{x}\,u(x,y)\,,\ \frac{y}{x}\,u\,=\,\frac{y}{x}\,u(x,y)
. We estimate the two terms in the
right hand side of (
11
) separately, beginning with the the second one.

The convex domain
\Omega
lies
between two its tangents to
\Omega
at the point
(0,0)
.
Consequently, for every
(x,y)\in\Omega
, we have
\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\leq\frac{y}{x}\leq\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(0)
.
In the notation
m=\max\{|\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(0)|\,,\,|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(0)|\}
, we get

\bigl|\frac{y}{x}\bigr|\,\leq\,m
and therefore,

\Bigl\|\,\frac{y}{x}\,u_{y}\,\Bigr\|\ \leq\ m\,\|u_{y}\|\,.

(12)

To estimate the
first term
\bigl\|\frac{1}{x}\,u\bigr\|
we use the generalized Poincaré inequality
(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
]
): for an absolutely continuous function
v

on
[0,1]
with derivative from
L_{p}
and with
v(0)=v(1)=0
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}|v(t)|^{p}\,dt\quad\leq\quad C_{p}\,\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{v}(t)|^{p}\,dt\,,

(13)

where
C_{p}
is a fixed constant. The precise value of this constant is found in
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
.
After the change of variables
y=(1-t)\varphi_{1}(x)\,+\,t\varphi_{2}(x)
, we obtain

\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}|v(y)|^{p}\,dy\quad\leq\quad C_{p}\,\left|\varphi_{2}(x)-\varphi_{1}(x)\right|^{p}\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}|\dot{v}(y)|^{p}\,dy\,.

Since the function
\varphi_{2}(x)-\varphi_{1}(x)
is concave in
x
, it follows that

\varphi_{2}(x)\ -\ \varphi_{1}(x)\ \leq\ \bigl(\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(0)-\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(0)\bigr)\,x\,\ \leq\ 2\,m\,x.

Thus,

\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}|v(y)|^{p}\,dy\quad\leq\quad C_{p}\,(2mx)^{p}\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}|\dot{v}(y)|^{p}\,dy\,.

We have

\Bigl\|\,\frac{1}{x}\,u\,\Bigr\|^{p}\ =\ \int_{0}^{1}\,\frac{dx}{x^{p}}\,\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}\,u^{p}\,\,dy\quad\leq\quad\int_{0}^{1}\,\,\frac{dx}{x^{p}}\ C_{p}(2mx)^{p}\,\,\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}\,|u_{y}|^{p}\,\,dy\ =\

=\ C_{p}2^{\,p}m^{p}\,\int_{0}^{1}dx\,\int_{\varphi_{1}(x)}^{\varphi_{2}(x)}\,|u_{y}|^{p}\,\,dy\quad=\quad C_{p}2^{\,p}m^{p}\,\bigl\|\,u_{y}\,\bigr\|^{p}\,.

Thus,
\bigl\|\,\frac{1}{x}\,u\,\bigr\|\,\leq\,2\,(C_{p})^{1/p}\,m\,\bigl\|\,u_{y}\,\bigr\|
.
Combining this with inequality (
12
) and
substituting to (
11
), we finally obtain

\bigl\|u_{x}\bigr\|\quad\leq\quad m\,\bigl(2\,(C_{p})^{1/p}\ +\ 1\bigr)\,\bigl\|u_{y}\bigr\|\,.

(14)

Thus, if the domain
\Omega
has the left and the right
angular points
(0,0)
and
(2,0)
respectively, then
for every
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\,\Omega}
,
we have
\frac{\|u_{x}\|_{p}}{\|u_{y}\|_{p}}\,\leq\,M_{p}\,\,=\,m\,(2\,(C_{p})^{1/p}\ +\ 1)
.
Therefore,
K_{p}(\Omega)\ \leq\ M_{p}
, which completes the proof.

\Box

If the domain

Problem 4
.

Find
K_{p}(\Omega)
for an arbitrary domain
\Omega
.

A natural question arises for which domain the constant

is minimal?
Since a linear contraction of
\Omega
with respect to the

-axis
enlarges

, the problem has to be formulated after a normalization of the sizes of
\Omega

along the coordinate axes, for example, by the condition that
\Omega
has equal projections.

Problem 5
.

Which domain inscribed in a square with sides parallel to the coordinate axes has the minimal
constant
K_{p}(\Omega)
?

Note that for
p>1
, neither the circle nor other domains with smooth boundaries give the answer to Problem
5
, since
by Theorem
6
, for such domains,
K_{p}=\infty
. On the other hand,
for

, the circle is optimal (Example
1
), however, there are many other optimal figures.

4. The case
\ {\boldsymbol{p}}\ \mathbf{=\ \infty}

This remaining case admits an explicit expression for
K_{p}(\Omega)
. A simple formula for
K_{\infty}(\Omega)

is provided by Theorem
8
below. Note that for all
p\in[1,+\infty)
, we do not have formulas but only estimates for
K_{p}
.

Let
\Omega
be bounded below and above by the graphs of functions
\varphi_{1}(x)
and
\varphi_{2}(x)
,
x\in[a,\,b]
, respectively. Thus,
\varphi_{1}
is convex and
\varphi_{2}
is concave.
Thus,
\Omega=\{(x,y):x\in[a,\,b],\ \varphi_{1}(x)\leq y\leq\varphi_{2}(x)\}
. Denote as above
m=\max\{|\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x)|:\,i\in\{1,2\},\ x\in[a,\,b]\}.
Clearly, the maximum is attained at one of the points
x=a
or
x=b
. If both vertical lines of support are angular then
m<\infty
and
\varphi_{1}(a)=\varphi_{2}(a),\ \varphi_{1}(b)=\varphi_{2}(b)
. If, otherwise, one of the vertical lines of support is not angular, then either
m=\infty
or one of the differences
\varphi_{2}(a)-\varphi_{1}(a),\ \varphi_{2}(b)-\varphi_{1}(b)
is positive.

If the intersection of
\Omega
with the line
y=y_{0}
is a non-degenerate interval and
(x_{0},y_{0})
is one of its endpoints, we let the derivative
u_{x}
at
(x_{0},y_{0})
to be the one-sided derivative
\frac{d}{dx}\Big\rfloor_{x=x_{0}}u(x,y_{0})
. In the similar way we define the derivative
u_{y}
at the boundary points
(x,\varphi_{i}(x)),\ i\in\{1,2\},\ x\in(a,\,b)
.

Theorem 8
.

We have
K_{\infty}=m<\infty
, if both vertical lines of support are angular, and
K_{\infty}=\infty
otherwise.

Note that it may happen that one or two vertical lines of support are not angular, while
m<\infty
; see Fig.
4
(b). In this case, obviously,
K_{\infty}=\infty\neq m
.

Proof
is based on the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
a<x_{0}<b
and
y_{0}=\varphi_{i}(x_{0})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. In other words, the point
(x_{0},y_{0})
lies on the graph of the function
\varphi_{i}
, but does not coincide with one of its endpoints. Then

-\frac{u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})}{u_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})}\ \in\ [\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0}-0),\ \varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0}+0)].

Proof
.
The equation
z=u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})(x-x_{0})+u_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})(y-y_{0})
determines a plane of support to the graph of
u
at
(x_{0},y_{0},0)
. The line of intersection of this plane with the horizontal plane
z=0
is given by
u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})(x-x_{0})+u_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})(y-y_{0})=0
, consequently

-\frac{u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})}{u_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})}\ =\ \frac{y-y_{0}}{x-x_{0}}.

This line lies outside the domain
\Omega
, hence it is supporting to the graph of
\varphi_{i}
in the horizontal plane, and so,
\frac{y-y_{0}}{x-x_{0}}\in[\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0}-0),\ \varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0}+0)]
.

\Box

Lemma 4
.

The value
\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}\,=\,{\rm ess\,sup}_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|
is attained on the boundary of
\Omega
, that is,

\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}\ =\ \sup_{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|.

The same formula is true when
u_{x}
is substituted with
u_{y}
.

Assume that
\varphi_{1}(a)=\varphi_{2}(a)
and
\varphi_{1}(b)=\varphi_{2}(b)
, and denote by
A
and
B
the left and right extreme points of
\Omega
, that is,
A=(a,\varphi_{1}(a))
and
B=(b,\varphi_{1}(b))
; then
\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}
is attained on
\Omega\setminus\{A\cup B\}
, that is,

\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}\ =\ \sup_{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega\setminus\{A\cup B\}}|u_{x}(x,y)|.

Proof.

Let us first show that

{\rm ess\,sup}_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|\ =\ \sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega^{\prime}}|u_{x}(x,y)|,

(15)

where
\Omega^{\prime}
is obtained by taking away the horizontal lines through
A
and
B
from
\Omega
, if
\varphi_{1}(a)=\varphi_{2}(a)
and
\varphi_{1}(b)=\varphi_{2}(b)
, and
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega
otherwise.
It suffices to arrange, for all
\varepsilon>0
and
(x,y)\in\Omega^{\prime}
, a set of points in
\Omega
with positive measure for which
u_{x}
lies in the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
u_{x}(x,y)
.

Indeed, there exists a regular point
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
arbitrarily close to
(x,y)
with
u_{x}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
arbitrarily close to
u_{x}(x,y)
.
In turn, there is a neighborhood of
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
such that in all its regular points

u_{x}
is sufficiently close to
u_{x}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Formula (
15
) is proved.

It remains to show that
\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|
is attained on
\partial\Omega
, that is,

\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|=\sup_{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|,

and if
\varphi_{1}(a)=\varphi_{2}(a)
and
\varphi_{1}(b)=\varphi_{2}(b)
then

\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega}|u_{x}(x,y)|=\sup_{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega\setminus\{A\cup B\}}|u_{x}(x,y)|.

Indeed, take a regular point
(x_{0},y_{0})
in
\Omega^{\prime}
; at one of the endpoints of the segment
\Omega\cap\{y=y_{0}\}
(let it be
(x_{1},y_{0})
) it holds
|u_{x}(x_{1},y_{0})|\geq|u_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})|
.

This finishes the proof of Lemma
4
for
u_{x}
. The proof for
u_{y}
is similar.

. First consider the case when both vertical lines of support to

Remark 4
.

In the case
p=\infty
one easily determines the solution of Minimax problem
1
. Indeed,

\frac{K_{\infty}(\Omega)}{k_{\infty}(\Omega)}\ =\ \sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\Omega}}}{{u\neq 0}}}\ \frac{\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}}{\|u_{y}\|_{\infty}}\ \ \sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\Omega}}}{{u\neq 0}}}\ \frac{\|u_{y}\|_{\infty}}{\|u_{x}\|_{\infty}}\ =\ \sup\Big|\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|\ \sup\Big|\frac{dx}{dy}\Big|\ =\ \frac{\sup\big|\frac{dy}{dx}\big|}{\inf\big|\frac{dy}{dx}\big|},

where the suprema and the infimum are taken over all graphs of functions forming
\partial\Omega
. Clearly, the minimum of the latter quantity is attained when
\Big|\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|
is constant, that is,
\Omega
is a parallelogram with equal slopes of the sides. Thus,

\inf_{\Omega}\,\frac{K_{\infty}(\Omega)}{k_{\infty}(\Omega)}=1.

In a similar way, the more general relation can be derived for nonzero
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
,

\inf_{\Omega}\ \frac{\sup_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{\infty}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{\infty}}}{\inf_{u}\frac{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}}\|_{\infty}}{\|u_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}}\|_{\infty}}}\ =\ 1,

and the infimum is attained when
\Omega
is a parallelogram with the sides parallel to
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{h}}_{2}
.
