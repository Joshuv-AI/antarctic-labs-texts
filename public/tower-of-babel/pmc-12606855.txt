Effects of different antioxidants on exercise-induced oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingfeng Chen, Jiahao Ding, Chunlong Han, Qi Liu, Li Wang, Wenlong Ma, Zeyu Liang, Yang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise-induced oxidative stress and muscle damage are key factors affecting athletes’ performance and recovery. This meta-analysis aims to assess the effects of different antioxidants on post-exercise muscle damage and oxidative stress in athletes, providing evidence-based support for the development of effective exercise nutrition strategies.MethodsThis meta-analysis strictly followed the PRISMA guidelines. It systematically retrieved the Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of antioxidants on post-exercise muscle damage and oxidative stress in athletes. Primary outcomes encompassed total antioxidant status (TAS), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), superoxide dismutase (SOD), thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), and lactate (LA). Secondary outcomes were interleukin-6 (IL-6), malondialdehyde (MDA), creatine kinase (CK), and nitrates (NOx). Effect sizes were combined via standardized mean difference (SMD). Heterogeneity tests, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses were performed, as well as publication bias evaluations.ResultsTwenty-six RCTs involving 505 athletes were incorporated. The overall analysis indicated that antioxidant supplementation significantly decreased post-exercise LA (SMD: -1.25; 95% CI: -2.06, -0.43; I²=81.7%, p < 0.001) and CK (SMD: -1.88; 95% CI: -2.98, -0.78; I²=86.2%, p < 0.001) levels in athletes. However, the overall effects for TAS, TBARS, SOD, GPx, NOx, IL-6, and MDA exhibited no statistical significance. All outcomes, whether showing significant or nonsignificant effects, demonstrated varying degrees of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses revealed that region, sex distribution, study type, and athletes’ professional level were important sources of heterogeneity for indicators like LA and SOD. Funnel plot analysis for publication bias revealed no significant asymmetry. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of major results.ConclusionsThe current study suggests that antioxidant supplementation can effectively reduce post-exercise LA and CK levels in athletes. This reduction may help promote fatigue recovery and alleviate muscle damage. However, the overall effects of antioxidant supplementation on inflammatory biomarkers and other oxidative stress are unclear and are modulated by factors such as region, sex, professional level, and study design. Future research should concentrate on individual differences and more refined intervention regimens to optimize antioxidant use in exercise.Trial registrationPROSPERO, CRD42023460244.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01381-2.Keywords: Antioxidants, Oxidative stress, Muscle damage, Athletes, Meta-analysis

Background

Oxidative stress refers to the pathophysiological process in which the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) surpasses the body’s ability to neutralize them via its antioxidant mechanisms, resulting in oxidative damage to biomacromolecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids [1]. In skeletal muscle, ROS and RNS are produced whether during resting basal metabolism or exercise-induced muscle contraction. At low concentrations, these substances act as signaling molecules involved in the modulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant enzyme expression, helping the body adapt to training [2]. However, when the intensity or duration of exercise exceeds the body’s intrinsic antioxidant defense capacity, ROS accumulate in large amounts, causing lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and DNA oxidation damage. This is manifested by significant increases in thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, changes in mitochondrial membrane permeability, and disruptions in intracellular calcium homeostasis, resulting in decreased muscle contractile force, increased fatigue, and delayed post-exercise recovery [3]. Furthermore, this exercise-induced oxidative stress can also cause elevated plasma levels of creatine kinase (CK) and lactate (LA), which are linked to muscle protein breakdown and immune function suppression. This remarkably increases the risk of injury and infection in athletes during high-intensity training or competition [4].

The body’s endogenous antioxidant system incorporates glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). This system maintains intracellular redox homeostasis by decomposing hydrogen peroxide, superoxide anion, and hydroxyl radicals into harmless substances [5]. However, available evidence has revealed that, although routine training can increase SOD and GPx activity in athletes, these enzymes still have difficulty eliminating the damaging effects of excess ROS under high-intensity or prolonged loading conditions [6]. Therefore, to counteract the limitations of intrinsic defense mechanisms during vigorous exercise, antioxidant supplementation has emerged as a prominent research focus for mitigating oxidative stress in recent years [7]. Common exogenous antioxidants, including vitamins C (VC) and E (VE), coenzyme Q10, and various bioflavonoids such as rhodiola rosea, can act by directly scavenging free radicals or regenerating antioxidant enzyme substrates [8]. Prior studies have demonstrated that VC significantly reduces post-exercise MDA and TBARS levels, shortens recovery time, and alleviates muscle soreness [9]. Rhodiola rosea reduces the release of exercise-induced inflammatory factors, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), and improves total antioxidant status (TAS) and iron-reducing antioxidant capacity in plasma [10]. Nevertheless, findings on the effects of exogenous antioxidant supplementation in athletes are inconsistent due to significant heterogeneity and large variations in the type of athlete, professional level, and outcome measurement time points. Some studies have reported no significant changes in oxidative stress indicators after supplementation. These inconsistencies have made further clinical applications and recommendations controversial [11, 12]. This study is important because exercise-induced oxidative stress increases the risk of injury and infection in athletes, potentially impairing performance and career longevity. Effective antioxidant strategies could mitigate these risks and inform evidence-based nutritional interventions.

Most existing systematic reviews focus on a single exercise pattern or a common supplement. There is a lack of comprehensive, systematic assessments of athlete populations that integrate multiple antioxidant types and multidimensional metrics [13]. Although systematic reviews can summarize the research lineage, they cannot quantify the effect size. Exploring potential moderating variables through statistical methods is also difficult [14]. In contrast, a meta-analysis quantitatively synthesizes the results of multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) via statistical indicators, including 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and standardized mean differences (SMDs). Meta-analysis effectively improves sample size and test efficacy, as well as investigating the effects of different exercise types, geographic regions, and professional levels on effect sizes through heterogeneity tests (I², Q-tests) and subgroup analyse [15]. Additionally, funnel plots evaluate the publication bias, thereby enhancing the results’ reliability and reproducibility and providing a solid evidence-based foundation for exercise nutrition intervention strategies [16].

This is the first meta-analysis to compare the effects of different types of antioxidants on post-exercise oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes. This meta-analysis focused on athletes from various exercises and systematically included RCTs of oral antioxidant supplements, including VC and VE, flavonoids, and berry extracts. Primary outcomes encompassed TAS, TBARS, SOD, GPx, and LA. Secondary outcomes were CK, nitrates (NOx), IL-6, and MDA. The present study employed meta-analysis to assess the overall effects of antioxidants on post-exercise oxidative stress and muscle damage. The sources of heterogeneity were examined via subgroup analyses, such as region, sex, professional level, and study design. This study aims to provide high-quality, evidence-based recommendations for nutritional interventions for athletes, as well as quantitative guidance for coaches and exercise medicine professionals in developing personalized antioxidant strategies.

Methods

The present meta-analysis followed the stringent methodological guidelines established in the Cochrane Handbook for Interventional Systematic Reviews. It was performed per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension statement to ensure reporting transparency [17]. To enhance transparency, this meta-analysis was registered in the PROSPERO database (National Institute for Health and Care Research, CRD42023460244).

Search strategies

A retrieval was conducted across five databases: Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Scopus up to April 2025, encompassing publications in all languages. To include only the highest-quality evidence, the retrieval was limited to articles featuring RCT designs. Core terms for the retrieval were ‘anti-oxidant effect’, ‘exercise’, and ‘dietary supplement’ (details are presented in Supplementary Table S1). To capture recent findings, ongoing RCTs presented at World Congresses of Sports Medicine within the last five years were examined. Moreover, reference lists of all included studies were scrutinized to prevent possible omission.

Eligibility criteria

Studies meeting the criteria below were included: study design as an RCT; fulfillment of the full treatment course with any type of antioxidant supplement. Studies meeting the following criteria were excluded: letters; observational studies (including cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs); personal opinions; case reports; book chapters; investigations with limited sample sizes; duplicate publications; studies with unsuitable endpoint reporting, and those with ambiguous baselines. These criteria refined article selection for this meta-analysis.

The participant, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) criteria guiding this study were:

P: Athletes of any type, including those engaged in aerobic, anaerobic, and resistance training;

P: Athletes of any type, including those engaged in aerobic, anaerobic, and resistance training;

I: Intervention groups receiving any type of antioxidant supplement, e.g., VC, VE, coenzyme Q10;

I: Intervention groups receiving any type of antioxidant supplement, e.g., VC, VE, coenzyme Q10;

C: Control groups receiving a placebo or a standard diet without specific antioxidant supplementation;

C: Control groups receiving a placebo or a standard diet without specific antioxidant supplementation;

O: Primary outcomes were TAS, TBARS, SOD, GPx, and LA. Secondary outcomes included CK, Nox, IL-6, and MDA.

O: Primary outcomes were TAS, TBARS, SOD, GPx, and LA. Secondary outcomes included CK, Nox, IL-6, and MDA.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently collected the specified data from eligible studies: sample size, study title and first author, sex distribution, intervention, athlete type, professional level, and primary and secondary outcomes. When multiple publications originated from the same trial, the most recent data were incorporated. Discrepancies during data extraction were resolved through discussion. A third researcher was involved, when necessary, to resolve discrepancies and verify data consistency.

Quality evaluation

Two independent researchers employed the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) tool to assess the quality of eligible studies. The ROB tool evaluates bias across seven domains: allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, random sequence generation, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias. Any discrepancies during the evaluation were arbitrated by a third researcher to establish agreement and reduce potential bias. ROB figures were generated via Review Manager 5.3 software for a visual summary of the evaluation outcomes.

Data integration and analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted via Stata software (V14.0, U.S.). For dichotomous data, effect sizes were quantified via risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% CIs. For continuous data (e.g., TAS), effect sizes were quantified via SMDs and corresponding 95% CIs. For continuous outcomes, means and standard deviations (SDs) were acquired from studies or derived from published data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given the clinical and methodological heterogeneity among included RCTs regarding antioxidant type, dose, intervention duration, exercise modality, and athlete characteristics, it is reasonable to assume that differences in true effect sizes among these studies may not simply be due to sampling error. Additionally, this meta-analysis aimed to generalize the results to a broader athlete population rather than being limited to the specific studies included. Thus, a random-effects model was utilized for primary analyses to more accurately estimate the mean effect size across studies and reflect interstudy variability. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and assessed via the Q test. I² values less than 50% generally indicate low heterogeneity. Values between 50% and 75% indicate moderate heterogeneity, and values greater than 75% indicate high heterogeneity. Heterogeneity test results were utilized to characterize the degree of interstudy variation, not as the sole basis for model selection.

To explore possible sources of the heterogeneity observed, subgroup analyses for selected outcomes were performed. Stratified analyses were conducted according to geographical region, sex, professional level, and study type based on available data from included studies. Funnel plot analysis was performed to evaluate potential publication bias and small-study influences. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for each outcome to evaluate the robustness.

Results

Search strategy

The retrieval, screening, and selection process is visually depicted in Fig. 1. These processes adhered to the PRISMA guidelines. A preliminary search identified 3,992 candidate studies. Following duplicate removal and abstract screening, 60 studies proceeded to full-text evaluation. Fourteen studies were removed because of ineligible outcomes, interventions, or study populations (n = 13), unavailable baseline data (n = 19), or unsuitable study design (n = 2). Ultimately, the current meta-analysis incorporated 26 RCTs [18–43].

Flow diagram of included studies

Basic characteristics of eligible studies

All included studies focused on the impact of various antioxidants on post-exercise oxidative stress responses in athletes. General characteristics of these studies are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Exercise programs and measurement time points are detailed in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The 26 RCTs encompassed 505 participants and involved 52 distinct interventions.

Regarding participant sex, 24 RCTs focused exclusively on male subjects, while 2 included both male and female participants. Based on athletes’ professional level, 22 studies investigated professional athletes, and 4 involved amateur athletes.

The types of antioxidants involved in these RCTs were diverse, incorporating vitamins (e.g., VC/VE/β-carotene complex), polyphenols (anthocyanins, green tea polyphenols, purple grape juice, etc.), coenzymes and bioactive molecules (coenzyme Q10, melatonin, L-carnosine), botanical extracts, fatty acids, micronutrients, and substances with specific functionalities (e.g., beetroot juice). Exercise modalities primarily consisted of team ball sports (soccer, basketball, volleyball, etc.) and endurance events (rowing, running, cycling/triathlon), with additional studies focusing on rugby and resistance training. This reflected the diversity of the study samples. The majority of included studies originated from Europe.

Primary outcomes

TAS

Twelve RCTs examined TAS, revealing scattered effect sizes with substantial heterogeneity (I²=67.9%, p = 0.000), which was potentially due to varied antioxidant types and supplementation durations (Fig. 2). The overall effect was not statistically significant for post-exercise TAS levels between the antioxidant and control groups (SMD: 0.06; 95% CI: −0.42, 0.53). Subgroup analysis by geographical region indicated possible regional differences in responses: studies from the Americas showed a significant positive effect, while studies from Europe did not. Subgroup analyses by sex, study type, and athlete level showed no significant differences (Supplementary Figure S1). However, these findings are limited by study quality and population representation, as most participants were primarily European male professionals.

Meta-analytic forest plot for the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise TAS in athletes

TBARS

Four RCTs involved TBARS. The overall difference in post-exercise TBARS levels was not statistically significant between the control and antioxidant supplementation groups (SMD: −0.23; 95% CI: −0.65, 0.19). No significant interstudy heterogeneity (I²=0.0%, p = 0.874) (Fig. 3) was demonstrated by the heterogeneity test. Subgroup analyses according to geographical region, sex distribution, study type, and athlete professional level did not reveal statistically significant differences in the effect of antioxidant supplementation on TBARS between these subgroups (Supplementary Figure S2).

Meta-analytic forest plot for the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise TBARS in athletes

SOD

Eleven RCTs addressed SOD, revealing scattered effect sizes with significant heterogeneity (I²=76.2%, p = 0.000), which was potentially due to varied antioxidant types and supplementation durations (Fig. 4). The overall effect was not statistically significant for post-exercise SOD levels between the antioxidant and control groups (SMD: 0.05; 95% CI: −0.56, 0.66). Subgroup analysis by study design indicated possible differences: crossover RCTs showed a significant reduction in SOD levels with antioxidant supplementation, while parallel RCTs did not consistently reach statistical significance. Subgroup analyses by geographical region, sex, and athlete level showed no significant differences (Supplementary Figure S3). Subgroup analyses by antioxidant type (e.g., vitamins vs. polyphenols) and duration (short-term [< 14 days] vs. long-term [≥ 14 days]) showed no significant differences where data permitted. Nevertheless, these findings are limited by study quality, population representation (primarily European male professionals), and SOD biomarker suitability.

Meta-analytic forest plot for the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise SOD concentration in athletes

GPx

Nine RCTs addressed GPx, revealing scattered effect sizes with significant heterogeneity (I²=79%, p = 0.000), which was potentially due to varied antioxidant types and supplementation durations (Fig. 5). The overall effect was not statistically significant for post-exercise GPx levels between the antioxidant and control groups (SMD: −0.14; 95% CI: −0.87, 0.59). Subgroup analyses by geographical region, sex, study type, and athlete level showed no significant differences (Supplementary Figure S4). However, these findings are limited by study quality, population representation (primarily European male professionals), and GPx biomarker suitability.

Meta-analytic forest plot for the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise GPx concentration in athletes

LA

Nine RCTs investigated the effects of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise LA levels. A pooled analysis indicated a statistically significant reduction in LA compared with controls (SMD: −1.25; 95% CI: −2.06, −0.43). However, the reliability of this finding is limited by substantial interstudy heterogeneity (I²=81.7%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). Subgroup analyses by geographical region, sex distribution, study design, and athlete professional level revealed variations in the observed effects. For instance, a reduction in LA was more evident in male and professional athletes, and in studies conducted in Europe, whereas crossover RCTs did not show significant effects and presented lower heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses by antioxidant type (e.g., vitamins vs. polyphenols) and duration (short-term [< 14 days] vs. long-term [≥ 14 days]) showed no significant differences where data permitted. Nevertheless, the persistence of high heterogeneity within subgroups indicates that these exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution. Due to small sample sizes, results in amateur athletes and mixed-sex cohorts remain inconclusive (Supplementary Figure S5). Overall, antioxidant supplementation may be associated with lower post-exercise LA levels. However, the considerable inconsistency across studies substantially limits the confidence in this conclusion, highlighting the need for further well-designed trials.

Meta-analytic forest plot for the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise LA in athletes

Secondary outcomes

Eight RCTs examined the effect of antioxidant supplementation on post-exercise CK levels in athletes. The overall difference in post-exercise CK levels was statistically significant between the control and antioxidant supplementation groups (SMD: −1.88; 95% CI: −2.98, −0.78), suggesting that antioxidant supplementation can reduce post-exercise CK levels in athletes. The heterogeneity test revealed significant interstudy heterogeneity (I²=86.2%, p = 0.000) (Supplementary Figure S6).

Pooled results for the remaining secondary outcomes – NOx (four RCTs; I²=86.3%, p = 0.000; SMD: 1.65; 95% CI: −0.06, 3.35), IL-6 (five RCTs; I²=79.9%, p = 0.000; SMD: 0.28; 95% CI: −0.08, 0.64), and MDA (five RCTs; I²=79.6%, p = 0.001; SMD: 0.99; 95% CI: −0.08, 2.07) – indicated non-significant differences between the control and antioxidant supplementation groups (Supplementary Figures S7 - S9).

Publication bias

Funnel plot analyses revealed no significant asymmetry across all outcomes, suggesting negligible publication bias in the selected studies (Supplementary Figures S10 and S11).

Quality evaluation

The Cochrane ROB tool was utilized to evaluate the quality of included studies. The findings are summarized in Supplementary Figure S12. Of the 26 RCTs, four were assessed as low risk. One study was rated as high risk owing to insufficient participant and personnel blinding and improper allocation concealment procedures. The remaining 22 studies demonstrated some methodological concerns across specific bias domains.

Sensitivity analysis

To verify the robustness of the primary analysis results, sensitivity analyses were conducted for all included outcomes. A leave-one-out approach was applied, and the results indicated that the magnitude, direction, and statistical significance of pooled effect sizes for all outcomes remained largely unchanged after removing any single study (i.e., the significant effects for LA and CK, and the non-significant effects for other outcomes, were maintained). This suggested that the major findings of this study were robust and not unduly influenced by the results of any single study or specific study characteristics (Supplementary Figure S13).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This meta-analysis systematically evaluated the impact of various antioxidants on post-exercise oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes. We synthesized and analyzed multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to summarize their role in recovery from high-intensity exercise. Our rigorous analysis revealed the complexity and significant uncertainty of the effects of antioxidant interventions [44]. While funnel plots did not suggest significant publication bias, a quality evaluation of the included studies highlighted a high ROB, a critical limitation that undermines the reliability of our findings.

LA, a critical indicator of anaerobic metabolism intensity and exercise fatigue [44], demonstrated a significant reduction with antioxidant supplementation post-exercise. This suggests that antioxidants might improve post-exercise energy metabolism efficiency or accelerate LA clearance, thus promoting fatigue recovery. However, this finding is accompanied by extremely high heterogeneity (I2 >80%), raising significant concerns about the consistency of the evidence. Our subgroup analyses indicated that this heterogeneity could be linked to region, sex, study type, and athlete professional level. LA reduction was more pronounced in male and professional athletes and in studies from Europe. Parallel RCTs also showed a greater tendency toward significant LA decreases, whereas crossover RCTs did not. While these findings imply a potential role for antioxidants in regulating LA metabolism, the substantial inconsistency in the data suggests that this effect is not universal, but rather, is influenced by various factors. The pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. CK, an intracellular enzyme, exhibits elevated plasma levels after exercise, typically indicating damage to skeletal muscle cell membranes and increased permeability [45]. The present results demonstrated that antioxidant supplementation significantly reduced post-exercise CK levels, consistent with previous studies [46]. One potential mechanism is the mitigation of oxidative stress-induced damage to muscle membrane structures, thereby reducing CK release into the bloodstream. This finding has practical value for guiding athletes’ recovery interventions. However, it is important to note the high heterogeneity observed (I2 >70%). The significant pooled effect should be understood as a tendency in the available literature, rather than a definitive and consistent outcome [47]. For most other oxidative stress and inflammation biomarkers, the overall analysis did not reveal consistent, statistically significant improvements, and a high level of heterogeneity was prevalent.

The overall effect on TAS, a broad indicator of antioxidant capacity, was not significant. However, subgroup analyses suggested that region might be an important influencing factor. This finding has also been seen in other meta-analyses on related topics55–57. While subgroup analyses suggested region as a potential influencing factor, this non-significant finding should be interpreted carefully. It does not allow for a strong conclusion about the mechanism. Rather, it simply suggests that the supplementation with exogenous antioxidants within a short timeframe or after reaching a certain threshold, does not simply add to the body’s complex and highly regulated endogenous TAS [48]. The body may maintain redox balance through intricate negative feedback mechanisms or by prioritizing the regulation of other antioxidant defenses [49]. Similarly, TBARS, a marker of lipid peroxidation, showed no significant change in the overall analysis. The pooled effect size of MDA even demonstrated a positive, non-significant trend. However, the interstudy heterogeneity for TBARS was low, indicating that different studies reached relatively consistent conclusions on this specific indicator. This may imply that antioxidant supplementation has limited effects on lipid peroxidation products produced immediately after exercise or that the measurement time point did not capture the most significant clearance effect.

For endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and GPx, the overall effects were also not significant, and significant heterogeneity was present in both. These enzymes are core components of the body’s redox defense system. As these enzymes are core components of the body’s finely regulated redox defense system, their activity is not usually increased by direct exogenous supplementation [50, 51], which primarily functions by scavenging free radicals. Thus, the lack of significant elevation in their activity is an expected finding, as the supplements likely enhance non-enzymatic defenses or indirectly reduce enzyme consumption by alleviating oxidative stress, rather than directly stimulating synthesis [52]. Specifically, study design type (parallel RCTs vs. crossover RCTs) was identified as an important source of heterogeneity. Antioxidant supplementation indicated a statistically significant decrease in SOD in crossover RCTs. This suggests that subjects’ baselines or exercise adaptation may be more controlled in crossover designs, revealing that antioxidants may indirectly affect enzyme expression or activity in specific circumstances, such as reducing oxidative stress-induced enzyme overconsumption. However, this effect is not significant overall.

Furthermore, the NOx and IL-6 levels, reflecting nitrogen oxide products and inflammatory responses, respectively, did not exhibit statistically significant overall changes, and both demonstrated high heterogeneity. This suggests that the effects of antioxidants on exercise-induced inflammation and nitrogen oxidative stress may not be as direct or universal as those on LA and CK. Oxidative stress and inflammation are closely intertwined biological processes, but antioxidants may primarily act on the early stages of oxidative stress, having limited impact on subsequent complex inflammatory cascades [53]. Compared with previous studies, this meta-analysis quantified the effect sizes of different antioxidants on multiple oxidative stress and muscle damage indicators by integrating a large number of RCTs.

Furthermore, it preliminarily explored the sources of heterogeneity via subgroup analyses, providing a more comprehensive perspective for understanding the complexity and uncertainty of this field. Several prior meta-analyses have examined the effects of exercise or specific supplements (such as VC, VE, or specific botanical extracts) on antioxidant status or muscle damage, but the results often differ depending on the included studies, exercise modalities, and outcome indicators [54–56]. When assessing the effects on muscle damage markers (such as CK), the results of these meta-analyses were also not entirely consistent, which may be related to differences in the region, athlete level, and measurement time point of CK in the included studies [57, 58]. In assessing the impact on muscle damage markers (such as CK), the current study found that antioxidants significantly reduced post-exercise CK levels. This finding is consistent with some previous meta-analyses, emphasizing its potential benefits in muscle damage recovery [59, 60]. Particularly, this study indicated for the first time that antioxidant supplementation significantly reduced post-exercise LA levels in athletes. This result has been less often emphasized as a core finding in previous meta-analyses. The importance of LA reduction for exercise performance and recovery is self-evident, opening up new perspectives for the application of antioxidants in exercise [61, 62]. However, the significant heterogeneity of LA results also suggests that this benefit is not universally applicable, but is constrained by multiple factors.

Strengths and limitations

The current meta-analysis presents several notable strengths. First, as the first meta-analysis comparing the effects of different types of antioxidants on post-exercise oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes, it provided comprehensive evidence for this field. The current study considered not only primary outcomes such as TAS, TBARS, SOD, GPx, and LA, but also a variety of secondary outcomes, including CK, NOx, IL-6, and MDA, to provide a more detailed and thorough assessment. Second, this study rigorously followed the PRISMA guidelines for literature retrieval, screening, and data extraction, ensuring the systematic and transparent nature of the research. The inclusion of only RCTs effectively reduced the risk of confounding bias and increased the level of evidence. Importantly, detailed subgroup analyses were performed, thoroughly exploring possible sources of heterogeneity such as region, sex, professional level, and study design. This helps explain inconsistencies in existing research results and provides clues for future research directions. Sensitivity analysis further enhanced the reliability of the major findings.

Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations.

First, the extremely high heterogeneity observed across most outcomes (I2 values consistently > 70%) means that the pooled estimates are unreliable and that the findings are not broadly applicable. While subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the reasons for this inconsistency, they remain largely unexplained. The poor quality of the included studies is a major concern. Most trials were rated as having an unclear or high ROB in key domains, which compromises the overall certainty of our conclusions. Additionally, the limited external validity of our findings must be noted. Almost all included studies were conducted on male professional athletes from Europe. Therefore, our conclusions cannot be confidently generalized to women, amateur participants, or other populations. Future research must address this lack of diversity. Finally, the selection and interpretation of certain biomarkers require careful consideration. Endogenous enzymes such as SOD and GPx are not expected to show a direct response to exogenous supplementation. The non-significant findings should be interpreted as a reflection of their complex regulation, rather than a failure of the supplement itself.

Secondly, supplementing with antioxidants significantly reduces exercise-induced LA and CK levels. However, the duration of supplementation (short-term vs. long-term) does not have a statistically significant impact on this effect. Short-Term (< 14 days) and long-term (≥ 14 days) antioxidant supplementation both significantly decrease LA and CK levels. This is confirmed by their “P-value for effect,” which is less than 0.05 for both markers and is particularly low for CK, indicating a highly significant effect (p < 0.001). These results suggest that antioxidant intervention is effective in mitigating muscle damage and improving metabolic stress.

Although both short- and long-term supplementation are effective, the difference in their outcomes is not significant. The “P-value for subgroup difference,” which describes the difference between the two durations, is 0.73 for LA and 0.86 for CK, both of which are well above the 0.05 threshold for significance. This suggests that a short-term supplementation period is sufficient to reduce LA and CK, and extending supplementation beyond 14 days does not yield a statistically significant additional benefit. Across all duration groups, antioxidant supplementation did not significantly impact TAS, SOD, or GPx, as all “P-values for effect” were greater than 0.05. This implies that, while supplements may impact markers of muscle damage (e.g., CK), they may not sufficiently alter the overall levels or activity of these core antioxidant enzymes in the blood.

For individuals engaged in physical exercise, if the primary goal is to quickly reduce post-exercise LA and CK levels, a short-term antioxidant supplementation strategy is both effective and sufficient. There is no strong evidence suggesting that extending the supplementation period beyond 14 days provides a superior effect (Supplementary Figure S14).

Third, different types of antioxidants (vitamins, plant extracts/polyphenols, and others) are equally effective at reducing LA and CK. There is no statistically significant difference in performance among them. Vitamins, plant extracts/polyphenols, and other types of antioxidants all demonstrated a highly significant effect in lowering LA and CK levels (all “P-values for effect” < 0.01). This suggests that multiple antioxidant pathways are likely involved in mitigating post-exercise muscle damage and metabolic stress. In the “P-value for subgroup difference” column, the p-values for all pairwise comparisons are well above 0.05. For instance, regarding CK, the effect of vitamins was almost identical to that of “other” types (p = 1.00). This reveals a critical insight: based on this dataset, no single type of antioxidant is significantly superior to the others. The effects of common vitamins (e.g., vitamins C and E) and popular plant polyphenols are comparable in terms of their impact on these key markers.

Consistent with the duration analysis, all types of antioxidants failed to produce a significant change in the levels of TAS, SOD, and GPx (all “P-values for effect” >0.05). This reinforces the earlier point that exogenous antioxidant supplementation may primarily act on markers of damage rather than having a limited effect on the overall homeostasis of the endogenous antioxidant system.

When choosing an antioxidant to combat exercise-induced muscle damage, it is unnecessary to be overly concerned with selecting a specific type, such as vitamins versus plant extracts. According to this data, their effects are equivalent. Therefore, the choice can be based on factors such as cost, availability, or personal preference, rather than on the supposed superiority of one type over another in reducing LA or CK (Supplementary Figure S15).

In conclusion, while this meta-analysis revealed that antioxidant supplementation may reduce post-exercise LA and CK levels in certain athlete populations, the overall evidence is marked by profound inconsistency and methodological limitations. The high heterogeneity and the poor quality of the included studies mean that our findings cannot support a blanket recommendation for antioxidant use. Future research, particularly high-quality, methodologically sound RCTs that include diverse populations, is urgently needed to provide a more definitive answer.

Clinical significance and future research directions

Our results achieve the goals of the introduction by quantifying effects and identifying moderators, though heterogeneity limits generalizability. This advances exercise nutrition by guiding personalized protocols. First, the positive effect of antioxidant supplementation on significantly reducing post-exercise LA and CK levels suggests that antioxidants may serve as an effective strategy to aid in the recovery of athletes who engage in high-intensity or prolonged exercise and are prone to muscle damage and fatigue. Antioxidants may help accelerate muscle recovery, reduce post-training or post-competition discomfort, and potentially enhance athletic performance. This has practical value for optimizing athletes’ training cycles and reducing the risk of injury. Second, although no significant improvement was observed for most oxidative stress markers, this does not mean that antioxidants are completely ineffective. It suggests that antioxidants may not directly ‘eliminate’ oxidative stress but rather ‘offset’ its negative effects by enhancing the body’s defense system. Alternatively, obvious effects may only be evident within specific time frames, at certain dose levels, or in particular populations. More detailed research is required to clarify this.

Future research should focus on the following aspects. First, higher-quality, more standardized RCTs are required to unify the types, doses, intervention regimens, and exercise modalities of antioxidants in order to reduce interstudy heterogeneity. Specifically, more studies are needed on different exercises (e.g., track and field, ball games, and strength training) to clarify the load parameters of exercise intervention. Second, the mechanisms of action of different antioxidants, especially how they act synergistically in athletes’ bodies or exert effects through non-enzymatic pathways, should be thoroughly investigated. Studies should focus on the effects of antioxidants on cell signaling pathways, gene expression, and protein modification, rather than on changes in oxidative stress products alone. Third, prospective studies should evaluate the cumulative effects of long-term antioxidant supplementation on athletes’ health and performance rather than just acute responses. Finally, given the impact of factors such as region and professional level on antioxidant effects in this study, future research should consider these demographic and exercise characteristics and conduct more targeted studies in different populations and environments to provide more individualized guidance.

Conclusions

This study is the first to comprehensively evaluate how different antioxidant supplements affect post-exercise oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes. Our findings reveal that antioxidant supplementation significantly reduces post-exercise LA and CK levels. This suggests a positive role for antioxidants in accelerating fatigue recovery and reducing muscle damage. These results offer valuable evidence to support the use of antioxidants for athletes and provide a scientific basis for developing personalized supplementation strategies. However, the overall impact of antioxidant supplementation is complex and influenced by various factors. While antioxidants demonstrate certain benefits, their effects on oxidative stress and muscle damage in athletes are context-dependent.

Future research should focus on precise investigations of individual differences, specific types of antioxidants, dosages, and exercise modalities. This approach will not only contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the potential mechanisms of antioxidants but also help in providing more targeted and personalized recommendations for athletes. Our study aims to offer evidence-based guidance and emphasizes the need for tailored approaches based on individual athlete factors.
