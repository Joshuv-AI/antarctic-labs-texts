When all directed cycles have length three
Paul Seymour

Abstract
We give a construction to build all digraphs with the property that every directed cycle has length three.



1
Introduction

A
digraph
(in this paper) means a directed graph without loops or parallel edges (but we permit directed cyles of length two). A digraph is
\ell
-cyclic
if all its
directed cycles have length
\ell
.
This paper is an exploration of the land of
3-cyclic digraphs. We will give a construction for all 3-cyclic digraphs, and look at
some natural subclasses. Some of the easier results work for general values of
\ell
.

This question arose from trying to extend a theorem of Thomassen
[
5
]
. This said:

1.1

Let
G
be an acyclic digraph, and let
S=\{a,b\}
be its set of sources, and
T=\{c,d\}
its set of sinks.
Suppose that
S\cap T=\emptyset
, and every vertex not in
S\cup T
has in-degree and out-degree both at least two. Then exactly one of the following holds:

•

there are vertex-disjoint dipaths
P,Q
of
G
, where
P
is from
a
to
c
and
Q
is from
b
to
d
;

•

G
can be drawn in a closed disc with
a,b,c,d
drawn in the boundary in order.

(A shorter proof of a slightly more general theorem is given in
[
3
]
.) We can think of this as saying that if
G

is drawn in a rectangle with crossings allowed, with the sources on the north side and sinks on the south side of the rectangle,
so that
a,b,c,d
are in order in the boundary of the rectangle, then either
we can rearrange the drawing to eliminated the crossings, without moving the sources or sinks, or there are paths
P,Q
as in the
theorem that show this is impossible. Now consider what happens if we glue the north side of the rectangle to the south side,
making an annulus, and making the identifications
a=d
and
b=c
. We start with a drawing with crossings in the annulus, and we would
like to rearrange the drawing (by some continuous deformation, remaining within the annulus at eash stage) to get rid of the crossings; and we
can do so if and only if every directed cycle winds exactly once around the annulus. But there is a “cut” across the annulus passing
through only two vertices, and it is not clear whether this is important. Could the following be true?

1.2

Conjecture:
Let
G
be a strongly 2-connected digraph drawn (possibly with crossings) in an annulus
\Sigma
,
such that every directed cycle has winding number one around the annulus in the natural sense. Then
G
can be drawn in
\Sigma

without crossings, such that every directed cycle has winding number one.

This would be a pretty extension of Thomassen’s theorem, but we cannot prove it.

Drawing in an annulus is the same as drawing
in the plane
without using the origin; and if we have such a drawing, each edge
e
of
G
subtends an angle at the origin (a
w(e)
fraction of a full
rotation, say), and for
each directed cycle
C
,
\sum_{e\in E(C)}w(e)=1
. Let us say a digraph
G
is
weightable
if we can assign a real weight

w(e)
to each edge

, such that
\sum_{e\in E(C)}w(e)=1
for each directed cycle
C
. Being weightable is equivalent to being
drawable (possibly with crossings) in an annulus as in the hypothesis of
1.2
. So, which digraphs are weightable?

In the final section we will show that this is true
if and only if no subgraph of
G
is a “weak
k
-double cycle” with
k\geq 3
(for the definition, see
[
4
]
), using
a proof virtually identical to the proof in
[
4
]
, working with the real field instead of
GF(2)
.
So we can characterize these digraphs
by excluded
subdigraphs, and hence obtain a co-NP characterization. What about an NP-characterization, can we give a construction for all weightable digraphs? For strongly 2-connected digraphs,
1.2
would give such a construction,
but that remains open. (Here is one curious fact about them, however: every weightable digraph admits a weighting
w
that that is
\{0,1\}
-valued. We give the proof in the final section.)

So we look for evidence, simple cases of
1.2
that we can check.
For instance, what if all the numbers
w(e)
are equal, that is,
G
is
\ell
-cyclic (for some
\ell
)? Unfortunately, this provides
no confirmation for
1.2
, because there are no such digraphs: one can show that no
\ell
-cyclic digraph is strongly 2-connected.
(That is easy: if
G
has minimum out-degree at least two, construct a directed walk
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,
where for each
i>\ell
,
v_{i}\neq v_{i-\ell}
. When this repeats a vertex
we have found a cycle of length different from
\ell
.) At the moment, that is the extent of progress on
1.2
: we are stuck on
obtaining a general construction for all weightable digraphs, and even for those that are strongly 2-connected.

But what about
\ell
-cyclic digraphs, can we understand them? Since
\ell
-cyclic digraphs cannot be strongly 2-connected, this suggests that there might be a
structure theorem for them; can all
\ell
-cyclic digraphs be built from simple ones by some assembly operations? This is so when

\ell=2
(every strongly-connected 2-cyclic digraph is obtained from a tree by replacing each edge by a directed cycle of length two — thanks to Stephen Bartell for this observation),
so the first interesting case is when
\ell=3
, the topic of this paper. We will give such a construction when
\ell=3
. In joint work with
Bartell, we tried to do the same when
\ell=4

If

is a digraph without directed cycles of length two, let

be the graph obtained by forgetting the direction of the edges; that is,

Clearly a digraph

\ell
-cyclic if and only if all its strong components are

-cyclic

First, let us say that

Second, a more brutal restriction:

One might hope for a construction for all
\ell
-cyclic digraphs, somehow building them out of classes of
\ell
-cyclic digraphs

and in acyclic digraphs

composition operation. Let us say an edge

if either

We will prove:

1.3

Every unbreakable 3-cyclic digraph can be made by special edge sums, starting from 3-pinched digraphs.

If

,

.
Let us say a

is a directed cycle of length three.
Take a graph consisting of a cycle

of even length, and an extra vertex

adjacent to every vertex of

We call the digraph we obtain a

We say

is

if no subdigraph is a diwheel.
We will see that general unbreakable 3-cyclic digraphs can be made by piecing together on small cutsets
digraphs that are either 3-pinched or diwheel-free, so let us try to understand those that are diwheel-free.

If
G
is 3-cyclic, there are several equivalent descriptions of when

is diwheel-free:

1.4

Let

be an unbreakable 3-cyclic digraph. Then the following are equivalent:

•

G
is diwheel-free;

•

admits a 3-ring
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
such that each of the graphs

•

•

“3-cyclic” is part of the hypothesis in the above; being buildable in the way described does not guarantee that
G
is 3-cyclic.
But here is one that does. Let us say that a digraph
G
is
safely buildable
if

can be constructed by starting with a tricycle,
and repeating the following

with degree 2 (so in-degree and out-degree both one);

.

1.5

For a digraph
G
, the following are equivalent:

•

G
is unbreakable, 3-cyclic and diwheel-free;

•

is safely buildable.

It is tempting to conjecture that all
unbreakable 3-cyclic diwheel-free digraphs are 3-annular, but that is false. The digraphs in Figure

1.6

If

is an unbreakable digraph, then

is 3-annular if and only if it contains no diwheel and none of the four branchers.

We conclude this section by proving the statement above about
\ell
-rings:

1.7

Let

be an integer. If

has length a multiple of

if and only if

Proof.
The “if” part is clear, and we must prove “only if”. Suppose then that

It follows that the length of every directed closed walk is also a multiple of
\ell
(for instance, by induction on the length of

are directed walks from

be the set of vertices

is an

If
G

\ell
-cyclic and strongly connected, then for every edge

to

3.3

•

•

We claim first that there exist

be the components of

, where

That takes us a step closer to

:

3.4

Every unbreakable 3-cyclic digraph can be made by special edge sums, starting from 3-pinched digraphs
and unbreakable 3-cyclic diwheel-free digraphs.

Proof.
Let

be an unbreakable 3-cyclic digraph; we prove by induction on

that

can be made by special edge sums, starting from 3-pinched digraphs and unbreakable 3-cyclic diwheel-free digraphs. If

4

In this section we prove

4.1

Let

Suppose that there is a partition of

For each

Let

Let

4.4

4.5

•

•

. Consequently
G
is safely buildable. This proves
4.6
.

This has some pleasing consequences:

•

If
G
is safely buildable, then it follows inductively that it admits a 3-ring
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
in which each of

G^{-}[A_{1}\cup A_{2}],G^{-}[A_{2}\cup A_{3}],G^{-}[A_{3}\cup A_{1}]
is a tree. Since any digraph that admits such a 3-ring is clearly diwheel-free,
this shows the equivalence of the first two bullets of
1.4
.

•

If
G
is safely buildable, then it can certainly be built according to the third bullet of
1.4
; and since digraphs bulit in this way are diwheel-free (because diwheels have minimum degree three), this shows the equivalence of the first and third bullets of

1.4
.

•

Every unbreakable 3-cyclic digraph
G
can be built by adding ears, of length one or two, starting
from a tricycle, by
2.2
; and so has at least
3|G|-3
edges. If
G
is in addition diwheel-free, then it can be built just by adding ears of length two, and so has exactly
3|G|-3
edges. Thus an unbreakable, 3-cyclic digraph
G
is diwheel-free
if and only if it has exactly
3|G|-3
edges. This shows the equivalence of the fourth bullet of
1.4
with the others.

5
3-annular digraphs

Where do the 3-annular digraphs fit in the general picture? One way to answer this is to consider what happens as we build
G
as in

1.5
.
As we add a new vertex
v
, it is made adjacent to some previous vertex
u
that had degree two; let us call
u
the “parent” of
v
.
This defines a tree
T
say.
T
could be any tree (except the vertex
r

must have degree one), but
G
is 3-annular if and only if
T
is a caterpillar (that is, some path of
T
with one end
r

contains all vertices with degree at least two in
T
). This can be derived from the equivalence of the first two bullets of

5.1

below, but we omit the proof.

Finally, we would like to give a characterizations of the 3-annular digraphs. The
branchers
are the two digraphs of Figure
3
,
together with the two digraphs obtained from these by reversing the direction of all edges.

Figure 3:
Two of the branchers

If
uv
is an edge of a digraph
G
, its
activity
is the number of components of
G^{-}\{u,v\}
that have more than one vertex.
We have the following, which implies
1.6
:

5.1

Let
G
be an unbreakable diwheel. Then the following are equivalent:

•

G
is 3-annular;

•

G
is 3-cyclic and diwheel-free, and every edge has activity at most two;

•

G
is 3-cyclic and diwheel-free, and contains none of the four branchers.

Proof.
The four branchers are not 3-annular, so the first bullet implies the third. Next, we show that
the third implies the second. To see this, observe that if

B
is a non-singleton component
G^{-}\setminus\{u,v\}
for some edge
uv
, then there is a tricycle consisting of
u,v
and a vertex
b\in B
; and since
|B|>1
,
b
has a neighbour in
B
, and
4.1
implies that there is a tricycle containing
b
, one of
u,v
, and some other
vertex of
B
. This gives two cases, depending whether
ub
or
vb
is in the second tricycle. If
uv
has activity at least
three, then the same holds for three choices of
B
, and so
G
contains one of the four branchers.

It remains to show that the second bullet implies the first, and we prove this by induction on
|G|
. Let
G
be unbreakable,
3-cyclic and diwheel-free, such that every edge has activity at most two. We may assume that
|G|\geq 4
.
By
4.5
, we can choose an edge
t_{1}t_{2}
of
G
such that
G^{-}\setminus\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
has more than one component, and at
most one of them is not a singleton. If all of them are singletons, the result holds, so we assume that
B
is the
unique non-singleton component of
G^{-}\setminus\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
. Choose
t_{3}\in X
. As in the proof of
4.6
,
there is a unique
b\in B
making a tricycle with
t_{1}t_{2}
; and we may assume that
t_{1}
has degree two in
G\setminus X
.
Moreover,
H=G\setminus X
is unbreakable, 3-cyclic and diwheel-free, and so admits a 3-annular drawing, from the inductive hypothesis.
From the hypothesis, the edge
bt_{1}
has activity at most two, and since
\{t_{2}\}\cup X
is the vertex set of a
non-singleton component of
G^{-}\setminus\{b,t_{1}\}
, it follows that
H\setminus\{t_{1},t_{2},b\}
has at most non-singleton component. Since
H
admits
a 3-annular drawing, and
H\setminus\{t_{1},t_{2},b\}
has at most non-singleton component, it follows that we can rearrange the drawing
so that
t_{1},t_{2},b
are the vertices of the outermost tricycle. But then we can add
X
to the drawing, and make a 3-annular drawing of
G
.
This proves
5.1
.

6
More about weightable digraphs

In this section we prove the claims made in the introduction about weightable digraphs. If
G
is a digraph, a
weighting
is a
map
w:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{e\in E(C)}w(e)=1
for every directed cycle
C
(
\mathbb{R}
is the set of real numbers.)
Thus
G
is weightable if and only if it admits a weighting.
We begin with:

6.1

If
G
is weightable, then it admits a weighting that is integer-valued.

Proof.
We may assume by induction on
|G|
that
G
is unbreakable. Choose a maximal unbreakable subdigraph
H
such that there is a weighting
w
of
G
satisfying
w(e)\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
e\in E(H)
. (
\mathbb{Z}
is the set of integers.)
Suppose that
H\neq G
; then, by
2.2
, there is an ear
P
in the sense of
2.2
, from
u
to
v
say. Since
H
is unbreakable,
there is a directed path
Q
of
H
from
v
to
u
. Let
C
be the directed cycle
P\cup Q
, and let the vertices of
P
be

u=p_{1}\hbox{-}\cdots\hbox{-}p_{n}=v
in order. Let
e_{i}
be the edge
p_{i}p_{i+1}
for
1\leq i<n
, and for
1\leq i\leq n
let
P_{i}
be the subpath of
P
from
u=p_{1}
to
p_{i}
. Choose
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

maximal such that there is a weighting
w
of
G
satisfying that
w(e)\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
e\in E(H)\cup E(P_{i})
(this is
possible since we may take
i=0
if necessary). By the maximality of
H
it follows that
i\leq n-1
; and if
i=n-1
, then since

w(C)=1
and
w(e)\in\mathbb{Z}
for each edge
e\in E(C)
except
e_{n-1}
, it follows that
w(e_{n-1})\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a contradiction to the definition of
i
. So
i\leq n-2
. Define
w^{\prime}:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}
by:
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)
for each
e\in E(G)
not
incident with
p_{i+1}
;
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)-w(e_{i})
for each edge
e

with head
p_{i+1}
; and
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)+w(e_{i})
for each edge with tail
p_{i+1}
. Then
w^{\prime}
is a weighting, and

w^{\prime}(e)\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
e\in E(H)\cup E(P_{i+1})
, contradicting the choice of
i
. This proves that
H=G
, and so proves
6.1
.

6.2

If
G
is weightable, it admits a weighting that is
\{0,1\}
-valued.

Proof.
We may assume that every edge is in a directed cycle, since we may delete any edges not in directed cycles.
By
6.1
, there is an integer-valued weighting
w
. Define
\phi(w)
to be the sum of
w(e)
over all
e\in E(G)
with

w(e)<0
; and choose
w
to maximize
\phi(w)
.
Suppose that
\phi(w)<0
, and choose
uv\in E(G)
with
w(uv)<0
. Let
X
be the set of all vertices
x
such that there is
a directed path
P
of
G
from
v
to
x
where
w(e)\leq 0
for all edges
e\in E(P)
. Thus
v\in X
, and
u\notin X
, since adding
uv
to such a path
from
v
to
u
would give a directed cycle with negative total weight. Let
A
be the set of all edges with tail in
X
and head in

V(G)\setminus X
, and
B
the set of edges with head in
X
and tail in
V(G)\setminus X
. So
uv\in B
; and
w(e)\geq 1
for each
e\in A
,
from the definition of
X
and since
w
is integer-valued. Define
w^{\prime}:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}
by:
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)
for each

e\in E(G)\setminus(A\cup B)
;
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)-1
for each
e\in A
; and
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)+1
for each
e\in B
. Then
w^{\prime}
is an
integer-valued weighting, and
\phi(w^{\prime})>\phi(w)
, a contradiction. This proves that
\phi(w)=0
, and so
w(e)\geq 0
for every edge
e
.
Since every edge is in a directed cycle, and
w
is a weighting, it follows that
w(e)\in\{0,1\}
for each edge
e
. This proves
6.2
.

Next, we will characterize the minimal digraphs that are not weightable. The proof is almost exactly the same as a proof in

[
4
]
, except that there we were characterizing the minimal digraphs that were not weightable modulo two; that is, those that did not admit a map
w:E(G)\to\{0,1\}
such that
w(C)
is odd for every directed cycle
C
. That proof is quite long, and great swathes of it
need minimal or no changes, so we will just indicate the changes needed in the proof of
[
4
]
.

Let
k\geq 2
, and let
G
be the digraph with vertex set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
and edges
v_{i}v_{i+1}
and
v_{i+1}v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
, reading subscripts modulo
k
. We call
G
a
k
-double-cycle
. (A 2-double-cycle is just a directed cycle of length two.)
A
weak
k
-double-cycle
is a digraph formed by the union of
k
directed cycles
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
arranged in circular order, where
each vertex belongs to at most two of
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
, and
C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}
is a directed path for
1\leq i\leq k
(reading subscripts
modulo
k
) and
C_{i}
is vertex-disjoint from
C_{j}

if
j\neq i-1,i,i+1
modulo
k
. In
[
4
]
we showed that a digraph is weightable modulo two if and only if no subdigraph
is a weak
k
-double-cycle where
k\geq 3
and odd. Here we will show that:

6.3

A digraph
G
is weightable if and only if no subdigraph is a weak
k
-double-cycle with
k\geq 3
.

If
C
is a directed cycle, let
{\bf C}
be its characteristic vector in
\mathbb{R}^{E(G)}
; so
{\bf C}_{e}=1
if

e\in E(C)
and
{\bf C}_{e}=0
otherwise. We view
{\bf C}
as a column vector.
Let us say a
real directed cycle basis
of a digraph
G
is a set
\mathcal{C}
of directed cycles such that:

•

for every directed cycle
D
of
G
,
{\bf D}
is a linear combination of the vectors
{\bf C}\;(C\in\mathcal{C})
; and

•

the vectors
{\bf C}\;(C\in\mathcal{C})
are linear independent.

The proof of Proposition (2.1) in
[
4
]
shows without any change
that:

6.4

Every strongly connected digraph
G
has a real directed cycle basis
\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover, if
A\subseteq E(G)
is such that
G\setminus A
is strongly connected, then
\mathcal{C}
can be chosen
such that each edge of
A
is in precisely one cycle of
\mathcal{C}
.

Moreover, the later part of the proof of theorem (4.1) in
[
4
]
(the section from step (4) onwards) shows the following
(although this is not stated explicitly in that paper):

6.5

Let
G
be a strongly 2-connected digraph. If for every
F\subseteq E(G)
such that
G\setminus F
is strongly connected, there is a
directed cycle containing
F
, then
G
is a
k
-double-cycle for some
k\geq 2
.

Proof of
6.3
.

We mimic the proof of theorem (4.1) in
[
4
]
, which we call the “old proof”.
Suppose that
G
is a minimal counterexample. As in the old proof, it follows that
G
is strongly connected and
|G|\geq 4
.
The argument in step (2) of the old proof also works (except the weightings in the proof are in the sense of this paper),
and we deduce that
G
is strongly 2-connected. Step (3) of the old proof needs some adjustment. We need to show:

(1)
If
F\subseteq E(G)
is such that
G\setminus F
is strongly connected, then there is a directed cycle of
G
containing
F
.

By
6.4
, there is a real directed cycle basis
\mathcal{C}
such that every edge in
F
belongs to exactly one cycle of

\mathcal{C}
. Let
{\bf M}
be the real matrix with columns indexed by
\mathcal{C}
and where the column indexed by
C\in\mathcal{C}
is
the vector
{\bf C}
. Since the columns of
{\bf M}
are linearly independent, there is a vector
{\bf w}\in\mathcal{R}^{E(G)}
such that

{\bf M}^{T}{\bf w}={\bf 1}_{\mathcal{C}}
, where superscript
T
means transpose, and
{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{C}}
is the all-ones vector in
\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Since
G
is not weightable, there is
a directed cycle
D
such that
{\bf D}^{T}{\bf w}\neq 1
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is a real directed cycle basis, there is a
vector

{\bf f}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}}
such that
{\bf M}{\bf f}={\bf D}
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the set of cycles
C\in\mathcal{C}

with
{\bf f}_{C}\neq 0
; let
G^{\prime}
be the union of the cycles in
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
(and consequently
D
is a cycle of
G^{\prime}
); and let
{\bf N}
be the submatrix of
{\bf M}
formed by the columns

{\bf C}
with
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Thus
{\bf N}{\bf f}={\bf D}
, and
{\bf N}^{T}{\bf w}={\bf 1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
, where

{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
is the all-ones vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
.
Suppose that
G^{\prime}\neq G
, and so
G^{\prime}
is weightable, from the minimality of
G
. Hence
there is a vector

{\bf w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{E(G)}
such that
{\bf N}^{T}{\bf w^{\prime}}={\bf 1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
, and
{\bf D}^{T}{\bf w}^{\prime}=1
.
Consequently
{\bf N}^{T}({\bf w}^{\prime}-{\bf w})={\bf 0}
, and
{\bf D}^{T}({\bf w}^{\prime}-{\bf w})\neq 0
. But this contradicts that

{\bf N}{\bf f}={\bf D}
, and so proves that
G^{\prime}=G
. Let
e\in F
, and let
C
be the unique member of
\mathcal{C}
that contains

Since

, it follows that
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, and so
f_{C}\neq 0
. Since
{\bf M}{\bf f}={\bf D}
, it follows that

.
This proves (1).

From (1) and
6.5

G
is a
k
-double-cycle for some

. Since the 2-double-cycle is weightable, we deduce that

.
