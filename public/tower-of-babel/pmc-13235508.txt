Lessons learned from three approaches to digital pathology system validation at a large academic medical institution
Authors: Tara Krishnan, Jamie Garrett, Jonathan Henriksen, Daisy Zhen, Luis Gonzalez-Cuyar, Kareem Hosny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe University of Washington Department of Pathology validated whole-slide imaging (WSI) for clinical diagnostic use in 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Subsequently, the project was expanded to address shortcomings in the initial validation and to include the adoption of new technologies and organizational restructuring goals.DesignWe describe three validation approaches: Rapid (3-week emergency deployment), Selective (single-subspecialty focused), and Big-Bang (simultaneous multi-subspecialty inclusion) methods utilized to validate digital pathology at our institution. Each method was selected based on the availability of resources, timeline constraints, and institutional needs at the time.ResultsWSI for clinical use was successfully validated in each approach and eventually across all specialties. The rapid neuropathology validation achieved 100% accuracy in 3 weeks in the setting of an urgent pandemic response, however, it did not meet all updated CAP validation recommendations. The selective genitourinary service validation project achieved 96.45% concordance following all 2021 CAP WSI validation guidelines, but was time and labor-intensive, stressing a single specialty. The simultaneous multi-subspecialty validation achieved 95.16% concordance while distributing the work effort. When combined, the system-wide concordance reached 95.78%.ConclusionThis descriptive article highlights our institution's varying experiences with WSI validation. Success was dependent on matching the validation approach to the available infrastructure, timeline requirements, and clinical/organizational needs.Keywords: Digital Pathology Validation, WS, Operational approaches

Background

At the University of Washington Medical Center (UWMC), the COVID-19 pandemic necessitated the rapid adoption of remote diagnostic capabilities for the neuropathology service at our secondary site, Harborview Medical Center (HMC).1 Following this initial success, two main factors drove department-wide expansion: the advancement of whole-slide imaging (WSI) technology making clinical implementation feasible, and our organization's push to unify services across UWMC's large reference laboratory network. This article describes our experiences with rapid, selective, and big-bang validation approaches.

Multiple validation approaches have been described in the literature since the Aperio ePathology system became the first system approved for clinical diagnosis in 2017.2, 3, 4 These studies have demonstrated high diagnostic concordance between WSI and glass slides, and challenges in aligning the validation approach, infrastructure capacity, and institutional priorities are not unique to our setting. Hanna et al. described a comprehensive framework for integrating digital pathology into clinical practice at a large academic cancer center, emphasizing that infrastructure maturity must precede full clinical deployment.5 Retamero et al. demonstrated that a complete, simultaneous transition across a multicenter hospital network is achievable when staffing and scanning capacity are sufficient.6 Fraggetta et al. documented a rapid, pandemic-driven fully digital transition at a community hospital, drawing lessons directly applicable to resource-limited settings.7 At the international scale, Bychkov et al. conducted an intercontinental survey of digital pathology adoption across Europe and Asia, documenting the heterogeneity in implementation pace, regulatory environment, and infrastructure investment that our three-approach framework also reflects.8 Temprana-Salvador et al. similarly described the system-wide DigiPatICS transformation across an eight-hospital network, highlighting the logistical and workforce challenges of phased versus simultaneous rollout.9 Taken together, this global literature underscores that there is no single optimal implementation pathway—the choice of approach depends on the intersection of institutional readiness, regulatory requirements, workforce training capacity, and clinical urgency, all of which varied across our three validation phases.

UWMC's digital pathology infrastructure evolution

UWMC's digital pathology infrastructure evolved across the three validation phases:

Initial rapid phase: Minimal WSI infrastructure existed before the rapid validation method in 2020. The department had acquired a single Leica Biosystems Aperio ScanScope AT2 and implemented the Aperio eSlide Manager image management system (IMS) many years before managing digital scans limited to teaching, research, and conferences. Digital pathology staffing included a single informaticist coordinator and scanning technicians who operated the digital slide scanner during batch testing downtimes. The rapid validation leveraged this existing hardware and staff. Aperio eSlide Manager had not been interfaced with the laboratory information system (LIS), PowerPath, at the time of the validation.

Selective expansion phase: This approach built upon the rapid validation foundation, utilizing a new Aperio ScanScope GT450 system and an interface between a recently implemented Epic Beaker LIS and Aperio eSlide Manager to expand WSI validation to the genitourinary service at HMC as well. Established imaging technicians and workflows allowed the team to focus on case curation, validation design, and pathologist training, but the overall validation was limited to a single subspecialty due to limited capacity.

Big-Bang phase: By this point, UWMC had added a digital pathology lab, acquired new Aperio ScanScope GT450 scanners, established WSI workflows, and expanded digital pathology staffing to a team of five employees including informatics-trained pathologists. This facilitated simultaneous, multi-specialty validation and was the most efficient of the three approaches.

Validation methodology framework

All three approaches followed core CAP WSI validation principles while adapting to specific constraints:

Case selection: Representative cases were selected to reflect specimen distributions over a typical 4-month cycle at HMC/UWMC. Attention was taken to include a mix of both straightforward and diagnostically challenging cases (i.e., small biopsies and larger resection specimens were selected to include a mix of benign, borderline, and malignant cases). The selection was designed to imitate the actual clinical variation of the subspeciality workflow.

Quality control: Slide scanning quality checks were incorporated into the imaging process, including review of scan snapshots to ensure all tissue on slides was captured, review of images for quality issues, and rescanning when necessary.

Pathologist training: Pathologists were trained in individual and group sessions that included a variety of topics such as using the WSI software, monitor setting optimization, and digital workflow integration.

Concordance assessment: Systematic comparison of digital and glass slide diagnoses using an internally validated quality concern scoring (QCS) metric, used in our department for other QA/QI-related work and aforementioned validations for remote intraoperative frozen section consultation with telepathology systems:

Rapid approach

Context: In the face of the unprecedented COVID-19 pandemic, and to mitigate potential patient care interruptions, our department was tasked with rapidly validating telepathology services via the Leica Aperio ScanScope AT2 slide imaging system combined with the Aperio eSlide Manager IMS. This was undertaken in just 3 weeks using limited digital pathology resources. However, the neuropathology department had several years of prior experience utilizing the Aperio ScanScope LV1 telepathology system, which features WSI-like slide navigation with live imaging of slides, due to previous near-daily remote intraoperative consultations. One of the neuropathologists had participated in internal validations of the LV1 system for this purpose. Whereas Aperio eSlide Manager had been available to pathologists and the shared staff technicians had been oriented to and operating the AT2 scanner for years before the pandemic, we had not formalized and documented a training procedure for either role. Computer monitor specifications were not defined.

Implementation: 30 neuropathology cases (93 glass slide preparations) from a readily available teaching library were digitized and independently examined by board-certified neuropathologists and NP fellows (total number = 4) to establish a reference diagnosis. The participants reviewed every neurosurgical case and the associated slides to render their final diagnoses which were then compared to the reference diagnosis. The internal QCS metric was used to grade discrepancies; however, the results were binned into scores 0–2 for concordant (no discrepancy and minor discrepancy) and 3–5 for discordant (moderate and major discordancy). The study found no major or minor diagnostic discrepancies in either the attending or fellow groups for all case types.

The Leica Aperio ScanScope AT2 scanner and Aperio eSlide Manager IMS did not have a formalized set of policies and procedures for providing training to imaging technicians and pathologists. This required the rapid development of documented training procedures for imaging technicians, who operate the slide scanner or perform image management roles and for pathologists who access case images in the IMS. Following this, role-based training was provided in either in-person sessions or in online group sessions, and training was documented in our lab documentation control system.

The existing digital infrastructure facilitated the swift implementation of a validated digital pathology system over a 3-week validation period, allowing the NP service to resume operations quickly and effectively.

Minimum infrastructure for this approach comprised a single existing slide scanner, an IMS, one coordinating informaticist, and limited scanning technicians shared with another lab service. This is consistent with the minimal viable configuration described for early-stage enterprise digital pathology deployments, wherein a pre-existing WSI system and limited IT support can suffice when pathologist experience with the platform already exists.10

The AT2 slide scanner required technicians to review pre-scan snapshots to confirm or adjust scan settings for each slide, including a QC step to ensure all tissue on the slide was scanned. The lack of automated image assignment by LIS-derived slide barcodes and an interface between the LIS and IMS required technicians to perform manual data entry to organize slide images into cases. Post-scan image QC steps included reviewing each image in the IMS, spot-checking five 20× fields of view per image to inspect quality issues such as focus, image striping, or image compression issues. The average technician hands-on time per slide was 56 s per slide (n = 93 slides) and included steps to clean and load slides (22 s), review scan previews to adjust settings (23 s), review resulting images for QC issues (focus, striping, compression artifacts) (5 s), and perform image assignment with data entry in the IMS (6 s). The number of slides rescanned determined from image quality reviews was not recorded during the validation study. The manual steps were a source of labor in the cost of technician hands-on time, which limited the scalability of this infrastructure. Whereas we recorded no issues, the lack of LIS integration was identified as a source of potential errors from mis-entered data with patient-safety concerns in the form of mistyped case numbers, patient information, or accidental merging of images from different cases into a single case. The scanner records correct information in slide label snapshots, and the IMS displays them to pathologists while reviewing images, however, incorrect image metadata and image organization could lead to confusion or even result in reporting a diagnosis to an incorrect patient record.

Results: 100% diagnostic concordance achieved across all participants and case types. Based on a follow-up QA survey, two neuropathologists described moderate dissatisfaction in the IMS viewing experience.

Selective expansion approach

Context: The rapid approach established WSI workflows and validation history at HMC, allowing easy expansion to the genitourinary (GU) pathology service which is based at the same site. The GU service had previous experience with digital pathology in their multidisciplinary conference presentations, in developing digital teaching libraries, and in using the technology for collaboration on research study slide sets. The department had recently migrated to Epic Beaker LIS, installed an interface between Epic Beaker and Aperio eSlide Manager, and acquired a new Aperio ScanScope GT450, which required validation. The validation was designed to isolate the work to a small group, including the digital pathology medical director, digital pathology informaticist coordinator and one technician, all pathologists from one subspecialty, and a subspecialty fellow to ease coordination complexities. At the same time, limited informatics support and stressing a single service resulted in a lengthy validation timeline.

Implementation: 62 GU cases were selected to include a variety of specimen types and complexity from cases accessioned in 2020 following the updated 2021 CAP guidelines.11 These guidelines included three strong recommendations: using a validation set of at least 60 cases, establishing >95% concordance between digital and glass slides for the same observer (i.e., intraobserver variability), and maintaining a washout period of two weeks. Four pathologists were assigned 7–20 cases (based on time on service) that they had previously signed out on glass to maintain intraobserver variability. A washout period of >one year was used, well beyond the CAP's recommended two-week washout period. In this study, we continued to use the internally validated QCS metric (used in our department for other QA/QI-related work) (Table 1) to grade the noted discrepancies, however, removed the binning of results into concordant/discordant results and instead assigned minor, moderate, and major discrepancies appropriately. The minimum infrastructure required for this approach included an upgraded slide scanner (Aperio GT450), at least one trained informatics coordinator, established scanning technician workflows, and LIS integration with the IMS for automated image to case matching. A minimum computer monitor specification was defined in the validation protocol (Resolution: 1920 × 1080, Color depth: 24-bit, Luminance/Brightness: ≥ 300 Cd/m^2, Contrast Ratio: ≥1000:1) and the informatics coordinator evaluated each pathologist's office to confirm their displays matched the specification. The LMS/IMS integration in the Selective approach more closely mimicked real-world data flow than the Rapid protocol. The workflow started with the LIS slide orders, which issued outbound scan orders to the digital pathology IMS interface. Then technicians scanned slides, resulting in images filed to the IMS automatically matched to case metadata.

Big-Bang approach

Context: This final phase was largely driven by an institutional push to unify WSI validation across the remainder of the UWMC system. The goal was to build upon the existing digital pathology validations, include an expansion of the digital pathology lab into the UWMC-Montlake site, and validate the remaining surgical pathology subspecialties (>80% of the department volume), which are based at UWMC in an efficient and timely manner. The new UWMC digital pathology lab included multiple Aperio ScanScope GT450 slide scanners, which were configured to save slide image data to the same instance of Aperio eSlide Manager with existing Epic Beaker LIS integration. We use the term “Big-Bang” to describe an integrated validation method, where all subspecialties are validated simultaneously and tested at once.

Implementation: 62 anatomic pathology cases representing a diverse case volume (autopsy, gynecology, bone/soft tissue, ENT, breast, cardiac, and renal) were selected. Cases were selected from specimens accessioned in 2020 following a typical 4-month program Quality Assurance cycle. Pathologists were again assigned to review cases they had previously signed out based on time on service to maintain intraobserver concordance. The minimum infrastructure required for this approach was substantially greater than the prior two phases: a dedicated digital pathology team of 5 (1 informatics pathologist +1 informaticist +1 scanning manager +2 technicians), multiple high-throughput scanners, centralized image storage, and LIS integration. This aligns with published frameworks characterizing large-scale digital pathology operations, which emphasize that simultaneous multi-specialty deployment necessitates mature staffing, storage, scanning capacity, and institutional IT infrastructure.12, 13

UWMC and HMC network infrastructure are directly connected by high-speed fiber despite being located across the city from each other. This allows our IT services to manage datacenter infrastructure which delivers centralized application and data management services across sites. For example, the Epic medical record system is hosted centrally and accessed at all UW Medicine sites. This enabled quick expansion of the digital pathology lab infrastructure by adding new scanners at UWMC while connecting them to the existing Aperio eSlide Manager server infrastructure and LIS interface.

Results: 95.16% diagnostic concordance was achieved. Notably, >60% of overall discrepancies were attributed to system unfamiliarity rather than image quality limitations, such as limited knowledge of software magnification/resolution capabilities. Some noted that the biggest issue was simply the cognitive load associated with adapting to a new technology while simultaneously trying to make diagnostic decisions, though this reportedly improved with use over time. Whereas training was provided according to the previously established training procedures, a one-on-one training session was conducted between each pathologist with a discrepancy diagnosis and the medical director of informatics to familiarize the pathologist with the IMS, identify the error, provide hands-on training, review the use of the IMS, and answer any questions the pathologist may have. In addition, routine software training drop-in sessions (for both trainees and pathologists) were added to address any specific issues pathologists experienced. Overall, our study demonstrated that diagnostic accuracy using WSI is comparable to traditional glass slide microscopy across a diverse range of pathology specialties. We also administered a pathologist satisfaction survey, in which 7 of 30 pathologists reported moderate dissatisfaction with the IMS viewing experience. As this validation represents an initial step toward implementing a fully digital pathology workflow, longitudinal data on turnaround time and cost savings are not yet available. Following full implementation of the digital workflow, we plan to collect longitudinal data on turnaround time, cost savings, quality of care, and pathologist satisfaction. We also acknowledge that this study is descriptive in nature and was not designed for formal statistical comparison between diagnostic approaches.

Discussion

In the United States, WSI has mainly been used for research or educational purposes. In recent times, however, as slide scanning and storage technology has improved, the use case for WSI in primary diagnosis has been well described both for general surgical pathology practices and specific subspecialties such as dermatopathology and breast pathology.14, 15

Where prior publications largely described the endpoint of mature, fully operational digital pathology programs, this manuscript documents the longitudinal, institution-specific journey across three discrete validation phases. Rather than presenting a snapshot of a fully realized infrastructure, we describe how each successive approach was constrained and enabled by the resources available at that specific moment in time, and how those resources evolved incrementally.

The Aperio ScanScope AT2 and Aperio GT450 represent meaningfully different generations of WSI hardware, and the transition between platforms across our three validation phases warrants explicit discussion. The AT2, deployed exclusively during the rapid neuropathology phase, is an older-generation scanner capable of imaging at both 20× and 40× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel at 40×) with a throughput of 50 slides per hour at 20× and 20 slides per hour at 40× normalized to a 15 mm × 15 mm scan area per slide. It is also capable of being upgraded to accommodate large-format whole-mount slides with the investment of an additional slide stage adapter. The GT450, introduced for the selective subspecialty and big-bang phases, represents a substantially higher-throughput platform: it achieves approximately 81 slides per hour at 40× (0.26 μm/pixel) normalized to a 15 mm × 15 mm scan area per slide with a 450-slide autoloader, centralized network-based scanner management via a dedicated Scanner Admin Manager server, autonomous tissue selection, and real-time automatic focusing technology that reduces technician hands-on time and reduces rescan rates. Notably, the GT450 is limited to 40× scanning only and cannot process whole-mount slides, which may influence subspecialty case selection and should be considered when generalizing our workflow to other departments. With multiple GT450 units operating concurrently during the big-bang phase, both intra- and inter-instrument variability become relevant: published performance data report intra-system agreement of 97.1% and inter-system agreement of 96.3% for the GT450 DX platform, indicating high but not absolute image consistency across units.4 The cross-phase transition from AT2 to GT450 introduces an additional source of inter-instrument variability that is difficult to control for retrospectively; differences in image rendering, color fidelity, and scanning resolution between platforms could theoretically influence pathologist diagnostic confidence and contribute to the variation in concordance rates observed across our three approaches. Future prospective studies should standardize scanner platform, where feasible or explicitly account for inter-scanner variability when comparing concordance outcomes across validation cohorts.

Regarding the diagnostic discrepancies, the selective approach yielded a 3.55% discordance rate (96.45% concordance) and the big-bang approach a 4.84% rate (95.16% concordance). Though both met the CAP's 95% concordance threshold for clinical WSI validation, it is important to acknowledge that 95% is a regulatory floor, not a quality ceiling. At this threshold, approximately 3 of the 62 cases in the big-bang cohort represented diagnostic discrepancies between glass slide and WSI review. The clinical significance of these discrepancies depends critically on their QCS grade distribution; nearly all cases fell into the minor (QCS 1–2) category. Although each validation project employed slightly different methodologies and resources, the results were consistently reliable and scalable, evolving in tandem with the growth of our digital pathology infrastructure. This approach could be beneficial to smaller or non-academic labs for generalization.

Critically, each successive validation approach was not undertaken in isolation but built meaningfully upon the foundation established by its predecessor. The rapid neuropathology validation, though conducted under pandemic-driven urgency, established the core WSI workflows at HMC including procedural training and use of slide scanning hardware, technician roles, and basic digital workflow. The selective GU validation then leveraged this framework, along with upgrades to the scanner model and LIS to IMS integration, to develop a validation protocol that adhered to updated 2021 CAP WSI validation guidelines, streamlined image acquisition, and implement more rigorous quality control processes, while simultaneously deepening pathologists' familiarity with digital review. By the time the big-bang approach was undertaken, the department had accumulated not only mature technical infrastructure but also a trained workforce and a cohort of pathologists with growing comfort and confidence in WSI-based diagnosis. This cumulative progression in infrastructure, staff expertise, and pathologist adaptability was integral to the success of the comprehensive multi-specialty rollout and should be recognized as a key feature of institution-wide digital pathology adoption.

Future potential

WSI implementation has established the foundation for AI-augmented diagnostic tools at our institution. Following FDA approval of the first AI-assisted pathology software in 2021 for prostate cancer detection, machine learning and deep learning approaches have enabled the development of tools for biomarker quantification (Ki67, PD-L1), micro-metastases identification, and biopsy triage for immunohistochemistry ordering.16

Our eventual transition toward a fully digital practice will leverage these AI capabilities to support pathology workflows while maintaining essential human expertise for clinical context application and nuanced interpretation. These technologies are designed to augment rather than replace pathologist judgment in comprehensive patient care.

Conclusion

Digital pathology implementation requires strategic alignment between the desired validation method and the resources available; the choice of a specific approach can be influenced by multiple factors (project objectives, funding resources, informatics expertise, pathologists' engagement, etc.). Our three-pronged framework demonstrates that successful WSI adoption is achievable across diverse settings, from emergent deployment during times of critical need to simultaneous system-wide rollouts. As AI-assisted workflows continue to advance, comprehensive WSI validation can establish a foundation to enable pathology practices to leverage these tools effectively while maintaining essential human expertise for nuanced clinical interpretation and patient-centered care.
