Introduction

Immunoglobulin light-chain (AL) amyloidosis is a life-threatening systemic disorder in which cardiac involvement is the principal determinant of prognosis.1,2 In patients with multiple myeloma (MM), cardiac amyloid infiltration may masquerade as hypertensive remodelling or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenocopies, delaying recognition.3-5 We report a patient with immunoglobulin A lambda (IgA-λ) MM who developed progressive heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), in whom integration of biomarkers and multimodality imaging enabled a high-confidence working diagnosis of AL cardiac amyloidosis when tissue diagnosis or scintigraphic subtyping was impractical.1,6

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man with a long history of mucocutaneous bleeding presented in 2020 with progressive dyspnoea and peripheral oedema. Physical examination revealed signs of congestion with ascites and pleural effusions. Laboratory testing demonstrated anaemia and thrombocytopenia consistent with immune thrombocytopenic purpura in the setting of MM, together with markedly elevated NT-proBNP.

Transthoracic echocardiography (Figure 1) showed concentric left ventricular (LV) wall thickening (septal thickness ∼13 mm) with preserved LV ejection fraction (59%) and elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (∼49 mmHg), consistent with HFpEF physiology. Serial electrocardiograms (Figure 2) demonstrated borderline QRS voltages in the limb leads with non-specific ST-segment-T-wave changes, illustrating the absence of classic low-voltage findings despite increased wall thickness.

Bone marrow examination (Figure 3) confirmed IgA-λ MM. Initial therapy with corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, and thalidomide improved bleeding manifestations. In 2022, the patient experienced decompensated heart failure with NT-proBNP peaking at 7164 pg/mL. According to the revised 2012 Mayo staging system, incorporating NT-proBNP and cardiac troponin, the patient was classified as Stage II, indicating an intermediate cardiac risk profile.7

Bone marrow pathology at the time of multiple myeloma diagnosis. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated hypercellular marrow with marked infiltration of atypical plasma cells, consistent with plasma cell dyscrasia. Immunohistochemical staining showed monoclonal light-chain restriction (λ), supporting the diagnosis of immunoglobulin A lambda multiple myeloma. These findings provided the haematological basis for subsequent development of light-chain–related cardiac involvement.

Clone-directed therapy with bortezomib-cyclophosphamide-dexamethasone (PCD) (bortezomib ∼1.3 mg/m2 subcutaneously on Days 1, 8, and 15; cyclophosphamide 300–500 mg orally on Days 1–14; dexamethasone 20–40 mg weekly) was initiated for two cycles, alongside optimized heart failure therapy. In 2024, owing to rising myeloma markers and suboptimal adherence, treatment was escalated to KRd (carfilzomib 20→36 mg/m2 on Days 1–2, 8–9, and 15–16; lenalidomide 10–15 mg on Days 1–21; dexamethasone 20–40 mg weekly), with three cycles completed and ongoing at the last follow-up.

Repeat echocardiography in late 2024 demonstrated persistent LV wall thickening and pulmonary hypertension, with small pericardial effusion showing partial improvement. Cardiac magnetic resonance (Figure 4) revealed diffuse non-ischaemic late gadolinium enhancement with concentric ventricular thickening and bi-atrial enlargement, supportive of an infiltrative cardiomyopathy. In the MM context, bone-avid scintigraphy was not relied upon for diagnostic confirmation. Considering the markedly abnormal free AL burden, HFpEF with LV thickening, serosal effusions, and diffuse CMR findings, a working diagnosis of highly likely AL cardiac amyloidosis was adopted. Despite persistent structural abnormalities, NT-proBNP improved to 3431 pg/mL with multidisciplinary management.

Discussion

Diagnostic challenges in multiple myeloma

In MM, HFpEF with increased LV wall thickness and modest electrocardiography (ECG) voltage changes may mimic hypertensive heart disease or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenocopies, delaying recognition of AL cardiac amyloidosis.3-5 This case demonstrates that absence of classic low-voltage ECG findings does not exclude AL cardiac involvement.1,8,9

Limitations of bone-avid scintigraphy in light-chain cardiac amyloidosis

Bone-avid scintigraphy is valuable for diagnosing transthyretin amyloidosis but is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosing AL amyloidosis.1,6 In patients with MM, scintigraphy may be negative or misleading; thus, a negative scan cannot exclude AL, and positive uptake does not reliably confirm it.3,6

Management priorities and chemotherapy-related cardiotoxicity

Management focused on maintaining euvolaemia with cautious use of vasodilators, reflecting the preload-sensitive physiology of amyloid cardiomyopathy.1 Although proteasome inhibitors, such as carfilzomib, are associated with heart failure risk,10 several features supported AL cardiac amyloidosis as the dominant mechanism in this case3,10: heart failure symptoms preceded intensified chemotherapy, CMR demonstrated a diffuse infiltrative pattern rather than focal toxic injury, and NT-proBNP improved with combined clone-directed and heart-failure therapy.2,7 Nevertheless, chemotherapy-related cardiotoxicity cannot be completely excluded and warrants close monitoring.

Overall, this case highlights three key teaching messages: (i) AL cardiac amyloidosis should be suspected in MM patients with HFpEF and LV thickening even without classic ECG features, (ii) bone-avid scintigraphy has important limitations in this context, and (iii) early multidisciplinary management can improve biomarkers despite persistent structural disease. The absence of endomyocardial or surrogate tissue biopsy remains a major limitation, and the diagnosis is therefore framed as ‘highly likely’ rather than histologically confirmed AL cardiac amyloidosis. Similar diagnostic challenges of AL cardiac amyloidosis secondary to MM have also been described in previously published case reports and case series.11-15 Alternative explanations, including hypertensive remodelling or chemotherapy-related cardiotoxicity, cannot be fully excluded, although integrated clinical and imaging findings make these less likely. Older age and frailty further complicate diagnosis and management of cardiac amyloidosis.16 And MM with secondary amyloidosis may present with diverse extracardiac manifestations17

Conclusion

In patients with MM, integration of cardiac biomarkers and CMR enables timely recognition of AL cardiac amyloidosis and guides management when biopsy or scintigraphy is impractical. This case underscores real-world nuances beyond guideline algorithms, including non-classic ECG and imaging features, non-diagnostic scintigraphy, and discordance between biochemical and structural cardiac response.1,8,9
