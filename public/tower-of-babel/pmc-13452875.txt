Escherichia coli DH5α-loaded fenugreek extract: an effective anticancer combination against human breast cancer cell lines
Authors: Hatim M. Hamadnalla, Hoda E. Mahmoud, Omayma M. Sadek, Ahmed S. Sultan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Recently, bacteria-based delivery systems have emerged as promising strategies for targeted cancer therapy due to their biocompatibility, low systemic toxicity, and ability to overcome drug resistance. In this study, bacterial ghosts (BGs) derived from Escherichia coli DH5-α were successfully prepared using a modified sponge-like reduced protocol, achieving approximately 92% reduction in optical density and releasing 246.3 ± 5.46 µg/mL DNA and 2.5 ± 0.054 mg/mL proteins, confirming efficient cytoplasmic evacuation. Heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) was prepared via thermal inactivation. Ethanolic extract of fenugreek seeds (Trigonella foenum-graecum) was obtained with a yield of 14% and characterized by GC–MS, identifying 26 bioactive compounds, including linoleic acid ethyl ester (19.39%) and β-sitosterol (14.14%). LC–MS/MS analysis further confirmed the presence of key metabolites such as trigonelline, diosgenin, orientin, and vitexin. The extract was successfully loaded into BGs and HKEc with loading capacities of 14 µg/mg and 11.6 µg/mg, and entrapment efficiencies of 21% and 13.4%, respectively. In vitro release studies demonstrated sustained release behavior from BGs, with only 14% released after 4 h at pH 7.4 compared to 90% for free extract, indicating effective controlled delivery. Cytotoxicity analysis using the WST-1 assay revealed enhanced anticancer activity of FEE-loaded BGs, with IC₅₀ values of 1.2 mg/mL in MDA-MB-231 and 0.87 mg/mL in ZR-75–1 cells, compared to 2.2 and 1.74 mg/mL for free extract, respectively. Mechanistically, treatment resulted in significant downregulation of STAT3, mTOR, and β-catenin, alongside upregulation of STAT5, and a twofold increase in caspase-3 activity, confirming apoptosis induction. Overall, these findings highlight E. coli-derived bacterial ghosts as an effective and low-cost delivery platform for plant-derived anticancer compounds, offering enhanced therapeutic efficacy and controlled release for breast cancer treatment.Key points• Escherichia coli ghosts are safe, versatile carriers with high drug-loading capacity.• E. coli ghosts enhance natural extract delivery, improving anticancer efficacy and targeting.• Fenugreek extract-loaded E. coli ghosts modulate key cancer pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13963-2.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be the most prevalent disease diagnosed in women globally, and it is the primary cause of cancer-related death in women. With an expected 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths in 2020 alone, breast cancer is responsible for almost 30% of all new cancer cases among women worldwide, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) (Sung et al. 2021). The increasing prevalence of breast cancer worldwide puts tremendous strain on healthcare systems and emphasizes the need for efficient, widely available therapies. Many obstacles still exist, such as resistance to current therapy, recurrence, and the negative side effects of traditional treatments, even if survival rates have increased as a result of early detection and therapeutic breakthroughs (Abouzeid et al. 2025). Surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and occasionally targeted treatments or immunotherapy are used in the treatment of breast cancer. Although survival rates have significantly increased as a result of these treatments, they frequently carry serious disadvantages. Despite their effectiveness, radiation and chemotherapy have serious systemic adverse effects, including organ damage, nausea, and immunosuppression. These treatments frequently fail to target malignant regions specifically, harming healthy cells and promoting the emergence of new cancers (Waks and Winer 2019).

Natural medicines, especially those from plant origin, have attracted a lot of interest since they can target several pathways implicated in the development of cancer while posing comparatively little risk to healthy cells. Many plant-based substances have been investigated for their anticancer potential like vincristine and paclitaxel (Taxol) drugs (Popovici et al. 2026). These substances frequently show selective toxicity, which minimizes adverse effects by efficiently eliminating cancer cells while sparing healthy cells (Cragg and Pezzuto 2016). The discovery of a number of intriguing substances with various modes of action is fueling the growing interest in plant-based cancer treatments. These include the induction of apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, suppression of angiogenesis, inhibition of metastasis, and modification of important oncogenic signaling pathways. Trigonella foenum-graecum, or fenugreek, is one of these plant-derived substances that has garnered a lot of interest because of its wide range of biological activities, which include anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties (El Bairi et al. 2017).

The medicinal properties of fenugreek seeds are attributed to a range of bioactive substances, including flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, and polyphenols. These phytoconstituents have strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and recently anticancer effects (Idris et al. 2021). Fenugreek seed extracts show specific cytotoxicity against breast cancer cells, such as the commonly used MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, according to several preclinical investigations. For instance, it has been demonstrated that fenugreek extract inhibits cell growth, stops the cell cycle at the G1 phase, and causes apoptosis in breast cancer cells via activating caspase-dependent pathways (Alsemari et al. 2014; Khoja 2013). In addition to these benefits, fenugreek has been demonstrated to have chemopreventive efficacy by lowering tumor size and incidence in experimental models of mammary carcinogenesis. These results offer a compelling case for looking into fenugreek as a possible treatment for breast cancer (Amin et al. 2005).

According to previous studies, phytochemicals extracted from fenugreek, especially diosgenin, can cause mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis in breast cancer cells by activating caspases. Furthermore, it has been discovered that fenugreek extracts block important proteins involved in cell cycle progression, such as cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases, resulting in cell cycle arrest and decreased proliferation of tumor cells (Idris et al. 2021). Furthermore, fenugreek has been demonstrated to control a number of critical signaling pathways linked to the invasion, metastasis, and survival of cancer cells. For instance, fenugreek extracts have been shown to inhibit the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, which is often dysregulated in breast cancer and plays a role in drug resistance, cell survival, and proliferation (McGrowder et al. 2020). Fenugreek’s potential as an anticancer drug is further supported by its capacity to suppress the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is crucial for inflammation and cancer spread (Salam et al. 2023). Fenugreek may be a useful tactic for addressing the problems of resistance and recurrence connected with traditional medicines by focusing on several biochemical pathways implicated in cancer. The medicinal efficacy of fenugreek is diminished by the rapid excretion of many of its phytochemicals, which are not easily absorbed into the bloodstream. Furthermore, it may be challenging to extract and purify particular active chemicals for therapeutic usage due to the complexity of fenugreek’s phytochemical composition (Vittal et al. 2026). Novel drug delivery methods are required to increase the stability, solubility, and bioavailability of chemicals produced from fenugreek in order to get around these restrictions (Hu et al. 2025).

Innovative carriers that can improve the therapeutic efficacy of chemicals generated from plants have been developed as a result of recent advancements in drug delivery technologies. Because of their capacity to efficiently encapsulate and distribute bioactive substances, bacterial-based delivery systems such as bacterial ghosts and heat-killed bacteria have drawn interest. According to Langemann et al. (Langemann et al. 2010), bacterial ghosts are the empty cell walls of bacteria produced by controlled lysis that have lost their cytoplasmic contents but nevertheless maintain their structural integrity and immunostimulatory qualities. Because they can encapsulate and protect therapeutic substances while facilitating their uptake by target cells, these empty bacterial envelopes can function as extremely effective drug delivery vehicles. It has been demonstrated that a variety of medicinal substances, such as proteins, tiny compounds, and nucleic acids, can be transported by bacterial ghosts, which are produced from Gram-negative bacteria (Tabrizi et al. 2004). These ghosts offer a number of benefits as drug delivery vehicles, such as high loading capacities, biocompatibility, and immune system stimulation potential. Bacterial ghost surface features, including as lipopolysaccharides and protein antigens, can facilitate the cellular absorption of encapsulated medicines and target particular organs more effectively (Tabrizi et al. 2004). Furthermore, it has been shown that bacterial ghosts have immunomodulatory properties, which can aid in stimulating the body’s immunological response a useful aspect of cancer immunotherapy (Paukner et al. 2006). Heat-killed bacteria have also been investigated as medication delivery systems, in addition to bacterial ghosts. Heat-killed bacteria can be used in vaccines because they maintain their surface antigens and structural integrity (Pandey et al. 2022).

The present study aimed to develop and evaluate E. coli bacterial ghosts (EcGhs) as an innovative carrier for fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE) to enhance its anticancer efficacy against human breast cancer cells. We specifically compared the performance of EcGhs and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc), assessing their drug-loading capacity, release behavior, and in vitro cytotoxicity. Our hypothesis posited that E. coli ghosts, due to their preserved surface morphology, would offer superior FEE loading efficiency, sustained release, and improved anticancer activity compared to heat-killed cells. The preservation of surface recognition structures was supported by indirect evidence from light microscopy and TEM analyses, which confirmed preservation of the cell envelope and overall surface morphology, from which their functional implications were inferred.

To validate this hypothesis, we devised a systematic experimental workflow that encompassed the preparation of bacterial ghosts, physicochemical characterization, evaluation of FEE loading and release under physiological and tumor-mimicking conditions, and a mechanistic investigation of the anticancer effects.

Material and methods

Bacterial strain

E. coli DH5-α ATCC 68368 was used throughout this study. The strain was cultivated in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) broth medium and incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 100 rpm for 18 h.

Preparation of E. coli ghosts

E. coli ghosts were prepared according to the method reported by Amara et al. 2013, with minor modifications including the exclusion of CaCO₃ from the protocol. Briefly, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and MGC minimum growth concentration (MGC) for sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were determined using a microdilution technique. Cell viability was assessed using resazurin (0.015%) as a metabolic indicator (Abd El-Baky et al. 2018).

Sponge-like reduced protocol

Based on the determined MIC of NaOH and MGC of SDS, a sponge-like reduced protocol was designed for E. coli ghost (EcGh) formation. A 9900 µl E. coli suspension (adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard) was treated with 50 µl SDS (5 mg/mL) and 50 µl NaOH (5 mg/mL), followed by incubation at 37 °C under shaking conditions (100 rpm) for 24 h (Amara et al. 2013; Amro et al. 2014). After treatment, the bacterial ghosts were collected by centrifugation at 672 × g for 5 min and washed twice with sterile 0.85% NaCl solution. The concentration of released DNA and proteins in the supernatant were quantified using a Nanodrop® Spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA) at 260 and 280 nm, respectively. The relative reduction in optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was calculated using Eq. (1) to assess cell content release (Hajam et al. 2015).

Preparation of heat-killed E. coli

E. coli cells were harvested by centrifugation at 672 × g for 5 min, washed twice with sterile 0.85% NaCl solution, and resuspended in 10 mL of 0.85% NaCl. The bacterial suspension was the subjected to thermal inactivation at 70 °C for 1 h (Nakai et al. 2021). Following heat treatment, the suspension was centrifuged again at 672 × g for 5 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 1 mL normal saline. To confirm complete inactivation, 50 µL of the heat-treated suspension was plated on LB-agar and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. the absence of colony growth confirmed the non-viability of the heat-killed E. coli (HKEc).

Microscopic and viability examination

The structural integrity of both E. coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) was evaluated using light microscopy (Olympus CX31) following staining with toluidine blue (He et al. 2022), nigrosine (Villarino et al. 2000), and safranin (Ma et al. 2021). Briefly, bacterial samples were first collected and prepared on clean glass slides. A small volume of each sample was placed onto the slide and allowed to air-dry. The dried samples were then stained separately using toluidine blue and safranin. For toluidine blue staining, the dye was applied to the fixed smear and left for a few minutes before gentle washing and air-drying. Nigrosine staining was performed by mixing the bacterial suspension with the stain and spreading it to form a thin film, followed by air-drying without fixation to allow visualization of intact versus disrupted cells. Safranin staining was applied to detect structural differences, where the stain was added to the smear, left for a short period, then rinsed and dried. After staining, all samples were examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX31) to observe cellular morphology, membrane integrity, and the presence or absence of intracellular contents.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL JSM-1400 PLUS) was further employed to examine cellular morphology and confirm the preservation of the outer membrane structure (Abdelnaby et al. 2022). Briefly, bacterial samples were fixed, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded according to standard TEM preparation protocols. Thin sections were then prepared and placed on grids, followed by imaging to observe the outer membrane structure and confirm the formation of bacterial ghosts compared to heat-killed cells. In addition, to verify complete loss of bacterial viability, treated samples were cultured on LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, as previously described (Saleh et al. 2023).

Preparation and characterization of Trigonella foenum-graecum (fenugreek) seed extract

Extraction procedure

T. foenum-graecum (fenugreek) seeds were purchased from a local market in Alexandria, Egypt. The authenticity and purity of the seeds were confirmed by Drs. M. M. Ibrahim and G. A. EL-Gaaly, experts in this field.

A total of 100 g of fenugreek seeds were thoroughly washed with distilled water and shade-dried for 14 days. The dried seeds were ground into fine powder using a laboratory grinder and extracted via cold maceration using 50% ethanol (1:10 w/v) for 72 h at room temperature. The resulting mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrate was air-dried at ambient temperature until complete solvent evaporation. Phytochemical screening and spectroscopic characterization

The ethanolic extract of T. foenum-graecum (FEE) was subjected to various qualitative phytochemical analysis following standard protocols to determine the presence of tannins, alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, and anthraquinones (Baxter et al. 1998; Sofowora & Traditional Medicinal in Africa. 2nd Ed. Spectrum Books Ltd., 1993).

UV–visible spectrophotometric analysis (Agilent, USA) was performed to detect characteristic absorbance peaks. The chemical composition was further profiled using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS; Agilent 7890 A, USA) following the method of Hadi and Hussein 2016 (Hadi and Hussein 2016), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Bruker Alpha ECO ATR), and liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–ESI–MS/MS). Additionally, the fluorescence characteristics of the FEE were examined using photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy and by visual observation under UV light at 365 nm to assess its suitability for imaging and tracking in later formulation studies.

LC–MS/MS analysis

The LC–ESI–MS/MS profiling of T. foenum-graecum ethanolic extract was conducted to identify its major bioactive constituents (Fatima et al. 2022). The analysis was performed using an ExionLC AC system (USA) coupled with a SCIEX Triple Quad 5500+ MS/MS instrument (Singapore) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data acquisition and peak identification were carried out using MS-Dial software version 4.70 against the ResPect spectral library.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Ascentis® Express 90 Å C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 µm). The mobile phases consisted of (A) 5 mM ammonium formate (pH 8 for negative ionization and pH 3 for positive ionization) and (B) LC-grade acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: 5% B (0–1 min), linear increase to 100% B (1–20 min), hold at 100% B (20–25 min), then return to 5% B (25.01–30 min). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 µL.

In the negative ionization mode (ESI⁻), the source parameters were as follows: curtain gas, 25 psi; ion spray voltage, −4500 V; source temperature, 500 °C; and ion source gases 1 and 2, both at 45 psi. MS1 scans were recorded over an m/z range of 100–1000 Da, and MS2 scans were performed using a declustering potential of −80 V and a collision energy of −35 eV.

Loading of FEE into E. coli ghosts and heat-killed E. coli

The loading of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE) into E. coli ghosts (EcGh) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) was carried out following the method described for 5-fluorouracil loading into E. coli ghosts, with slight modifications to enhance loading efficiency (Alanazi et al. 2020; Youssof et al. 2019). The modifications introduced in this study are summarized as follows. Briefly, FEE was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a concentrated stock solution, which was subsequently diluted to obtain a working solution at a final concentration of 20 mg/mL, then 50 mg of EcGh or HKEc was suspended in 5 mL of FEE (20 mg/mL) and incubated for 24 h at room temperature (28 °C) out of light with continuous stirring. After incubation, the FEE-loaded EcGh and HKEc were collected by centrifugation at 3914 × g for 5 min, followed by three washes with 0.85% NaCl to remove unbound extract and to minimize residual DMSO. The obtained FEE-loaded preparations were stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Characterization of FEE-loaded E. coli ghosts and heat-killed E. coli

The successful loading of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE) into E. coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) was confirmed both qualitatively and quantitatively. Qualitative assessment was conducted by digital photography, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LCSM; Leica TCS SPE, Leica LAS X interface) at excitation wavelengths of 488 nm and 555 nm, and the results were compared with unloaded (blank) EcGhs and HKEc. In vitro release of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE)

The in vitro release of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE) from the formulations was evaluated under simulated physiological (pH 7.4) and tumor microenvironment (pH 6.7) conditions (Danyuo et al. 2019). Release experiments were performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 5 mL) at 37 °C under gentle agitation (100 rpm). For the free extract, 0.5 mL of FEE solution was sealed in a VISKING® dialysis bag (molecular-weight cut-off = 12–14 kDa) and immersed in 5 mL of release medium. In contrast, the loaded ghost (LEcGhs) and heat-killed cells (LHKEc) were directly dispersed in the medium without dialysis.

At predetermined intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h), 1 mL of the release medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed buffer to maintain sink conditions. The concentration of released FEE in each aliquot was determined spectrophotometrically (Agilent Tech., USA) at λmax = 270 nm. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SD. Stability study of FEE

The stability of free FEE and FEE entrapped within EcGhs and HKEc was assessed at 37 °C, 4 °C, and −20 °C. The stability of the entrapped extract was monitored based on the photoluminescence (PL) intensity measured using a PerkinElmer LS 55 fluorescence spectrometer (Yuan et al. 2010). Measurements were recorded every 7 days for one month, and the relative fluorescence intensity was compared to the initial value to evaluate FEE retention and degradation resistance.

Cell culture

We bought the triple-negative MDA-MB-231 and estrogen-positive ZR-75–1 breast cancer cell lines from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA). Dulbecco’s modified basic media (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin were used to maintain these cell lines at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Cell cytotoxicity (cell proliferation reagent Wst-1) of MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75–1 cell lines

The cytotoxic effects of the tested samples were evaluated using the WST-1 colorimetric assay (Roche Applied Science, Germany), which measures the metabolic activity of viable cells. Briefly, MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75–1 breast cancer cell lines were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 µL of complete culture medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 to allow cell attachment.

After incubation, cells were treated with various concentrations of FEE, LEcGhs, and LHKEc, EcGhs and HKEc at (0–2.5 mg/mL) for 24 h. Untreated cells served as the negative control. Following treatment, 10 µL of WST-1 reagent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The conversion of the tetrazolium salt WST-1 to formazan by mitochondrial dehydrogenases in viable cells was measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm (reference wavelength 630 nm) using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the following equation:

Western blotting analysis

Cells were used in a 6-well plate for a Western blot examination. Cells were lysed at 4 °C for 1 h using a 1% NP–40 buffer that contained a 1× cocktail of phosphate inhibitors (phosphatase inhibitor cocktail I and II, Sigma) and protease inhibitors (Complete Mini, Roche, Mannheim, Germany). After centrifuging the cell lysate at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. The BCA assay (Pierce, Rockford, IL) was used to measure the protein concentration in the supernatant. Sample were denatured by heating them to 98 °C for 5 min after being combined in a 1:1 ratio in Laemmli buffer.10%Tris-SDS- PAGE gels (Bio Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) were used to separate 30 µg of protein for 1 h at 100 V. Proteins were isolated and electrophoretically deposited onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) at 380 mA for 1 h to perform western blotting. Using specific monoclonal primary antibodies for Stat-3, β-catenin, mTOR, and Stat-5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), western blot analysis was performed. Protein loading was normalized using the expression of β-actin (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

The membranes were blocked with TBS plus 5% nonfat milk (20mM Tris – HCl, pH 7.6, 137 mM NaCl) and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution for antibodies (1–1000). The membranes were then incubated once more for one hour in the appropriate horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA). For detection, an ECL kit was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Amersham, Buckinghamshire, UK), and Quantity One software was used to measure the corresponding relative density of STAT3, β-catenin, mTOR, and STAT5 bands (Khalil et al. 2015).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5 software. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and results were analyzed using Student’s t-test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. All experiments were repeated at least three times. Densitometric analysis of Western blots was performed using Quantity One software (Bio-Rad).

Results

Bacterial ghost preparation

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum growth concentration (MGC) of both sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were determined to be 5 mg/mL Following chemical treatment, E. coli cells released 246.3 ± 5.46 µg/mL of DNA and 2.5 ± 0.054 mg/mL of proteins, as quantified by NanoDrop® spectrophotometry. Moreover, a significant reduction of approximately 92% in optical density (OD600) was observed, indicating effective cell lysis and cytoplasmic content release.

Characterization of EcGhs and HKEc

No visible bacterial growth was detected on LB agar plates after 48 h of incubation, confirming the complete loss of viability in both E. coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) preparations.

Light microscopy

Microscopic examinations of EcGhs and HKEc stained with safranin, toluidine blue, and nigrosine revealed distinct structural differences compared with native E. coli cells (Fig. 1a–i). The EcGhs maintained their overall cell wall integrity while appearing empty and translucent, consistent with the absence of intracellular contents. Additionally, their outer membrane retained a negative surface charge, typical of ghost cells. Transmission electron microscopy

TEM micrographs confirmed the successful formation of E. coli ghosts and differentiation from HKEc (Fig. 1j–l). The EcGhs appeared completely devoid of cytoplasmic and genetic material, exhibiting intact, continuous cell envelopes with clear internal voids, characteristic of ghost structures. Conversely, the HKEc cells displayed preserved intracellular contents, including cytoplasmic and nucleoid material, while maintaining a cohesive rod-shaped morphology and unbroken cell walls.

Fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE)

Extraction yield and physical characteristics

The cold maceration of T. foenum-graecum seed powder with 50% ethanol resulted in a 14% yield of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE). The obtained extract exhibited a greasy yellow–brown color and emitted a distinct honey-like odor, indicating the presence of volatile phytoconstituents and essential oils.

Phytochemical screening

Qualitative phytochemical analysis of FEE revealed the presence of several bioactive constituents, including saponins, flavonoids, tannins, anthraquinones, and alkaloids (Supplementary Table S1). These compounds are known for their pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities.

UV–visible spectroscopic analysis

The UV–visible absorption spectrum of FEE was recorded in the range of 200–800 nm using an Agilent spectrophotometer (USA). The spectrum exhibited three prominent absorbance peaks at 225 nm, 270 nm, and 370 nm (Supplementary Fig. S1a), corresponding to the presence of phenolic, flavonoid, and conjugated aromatic compounds, respectively.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectral analysis

The FTIR spectrum of the fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE) (Supplementary Fig. S1b) revealed a wide range of absorption bands corresponding to various functional groups associated with bioactive phytoconstituents. A broad band at 3927.7 cm⁻1 indicated N–H stretching, while a strong peak at 3436.4 cm⁻1 corresponded to O–H stretching vibrations of alcohols and phenolic compounds. Medium and weak peaks observed at 2998.1 cm⁻1 and 2916.2 cm⁻1 were attributed to C–H stretching of alkenes and alkanes, respectively. Additional weak absorption at 2588.7 cm⁻1 represented S–H stretching of thiols, whereas the presence of carbon dioxide and isocyanate groups was confirmed by peaks at 2319 cm⁻1 (O = C = O) and 2270.8 cm⁻1 (N = C = O). Distinct bands at 2222.6 cm⁻1 and 2097.4 cm⁻1 indicated nitrile (C≡N) and isothiocyanate (N = C = S) functional groups, respectively, while multiple peaks between 1991.4 and 1909.5 cm⁻1 were associated with allenic (C = C = C) linkages. The aromatic nature of the extract was evident from the band at 1663.8 cm⁻1, and the peaks at 1437.4 cm⁻1 and 1408.5 cm⁻1 corresponded to O–H bending of carboxylic acids and alcohols. Furthermore, absorptions at 1312.2 cm⁻1 and 1042.5 cm⁻1 were assigned to C–O stretching of aromatic esters and CO–O–CO stretching of anhydrides, respectively. The fingerprint region displayed additional peaks at 955.8 cm⁻1 (C = C bending of alkenes), 897.9 cm⁻1 (C–H bending of 1,2,4-trisubstituted benzene derivatives), 700.5 cm⁻1 (benzene ring substitution), and 671.6 cm⁻1 (C–Br stretching of halo compounds). Collectively, these findings confirm that FEE contains diverse phytochemical classes such as phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, and fatty acids, contributing to its broad biological potential.

Fluorescence properties of fenugreek ethanolic extract (FEE)

Photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL) analysis of FEE at a concentration of 5 mg/mL revealed two prominent emission peaks at 485 nm and 535 nm (Supplementary Fig. S1c). Additionally, FEE exhibited strong green fluorescence when exposed to a UV lamp at 365 nm (Supplementary Fig. S1e).

Functionalization of E. coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc)

Functionalization of EcGhs and HKEc with FEE was achieved by simple incubation at room temperature for 24 h. The loading capacities were determined to be 14 µg FEE/mg EcGhs and 11.6 µg FEE/mg HKEc, while the entrapment efficiencies were 21% and 13.4% for EcGhs and HKEc, respectively.

In contrast to STAT5, STAT3 is more frequently constitutively activated in breast cancer; however, STAT5 activation can reverse its oncologic consequences and act as a differentiation marker for early initiation of breast cancer (Sultan et al. 2008, 2005). Figure 7a (i, iii) and b (i, iii) show that STAT5 protein expression was upregulated after 48 h of FEE and LEcGh treatments, compared to untreated control cells.

β-Catenin is a multipurpose protein essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis. Abnormally high expression of β-catenin is implicated in various cancers (Shang et al. 2017). As shown in Fig. 7a (i, iv) and b (i, iv), western blot analysis revealed that β-catenin protein expression was downregulated 48 h after treatment with FEE and LEcGh compared to untreated control cells, suggesting that FEE and LEcGh treatments inhibited cell viability in both MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-1 cells, as evidenced by the changes in β-catenin expression levels. The mTOR is a member of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase related kinase (PIKK) family and is expressed in the majority of mammalian cells (Hare and Harvey 2017).

When mTOR complexes are activated, tumors gain a significant growth advantage due to enhanced protein synthesis and suppression of autophagy (Hare and Harvey 2017). It has generally been reported that increased mTOR signaling accelerates tumor progression and is commonly linked to poorer patient survival outcomes. (Xu et al. 2014). The western blot data in Fig. 7a (v, vi) and b (v, vi) show that treatment with FEE and LEcGh for 48 h results in mTOR downregulation compared to untreated cells, suggesting that both MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-1 cells underwent cell growth inhibition due to a possible cross-talk among the inhibition of STAT3, mTOR, and β-catenin signaling expression following FEE and LEcGh treatments. On the other hand, STAT5 up-regulation, as a differentiation factor, in treated cells might enhance cell–cell differentiation and play an antagonistic effect against STAT3’s role in the treated breast cancer cells after FEE and LEcGh treatments for 48 h (Sultan et al. 2005, 2008). after activating mTOR through the PI3K/AKT pathway and also enhances β-catenin signaling via the Wnt pathway.

Caspase-3 activity and apoptosis induction after treatment of FEE and LEcGh

The activation of a cascade of intracellular cysteine proteases, known as caspases, is a major process in apoptosis (Thornberry 1997). Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) is one of the substrates that caspase-3 can cleave upon proteolytic activation by upstream caspases. The characteristic morphological feature is believed to be that caspase-3 is a generic mediator of normal and stress-induced apoptosis due to the variety of its substrates (Datta et al. 1997). To investigate whether FEE, EcGh, and LEcGh could activate caspase-3 in treated MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75–1 cells, we assayed caspase-3 activity following the manufacturer’s protocol under the “Materials and methods” section. Our data revealed that LEcGh resulted in a significant increase in caspase-3 activity, showing a two-fold rise in both treated MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-1 cells compared to untreated cells after 48 h. As illustrated in Fig. 7C(i, iii), our data confirmed that LEcGh induced apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-1 cells in a caspase-dependent manner. LEcGh treatment induced PARP-1 activation as shown in Fig. 7C (ii, iv), indicating that the treatment of the breast cancer cells with LEcGh for 48 h induced apoptosis.

Discussion

The development of bacterial ghost (BG)-based systems as innovative carriers for natural bioactives has recently attracted growing attention due to their safety, biocompatibility, and ability to preserve the biological activity of sensitive compounds (Chen et al. 2021). In the present study, Escherichia coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc) were successfully prepared and evaluated as delivery vehicles for the ethanolic extract of T. foenum-graecum (FEE). The rationale behind this approach stems from the synergistic potential of combining the structural robustness and targeting ability of bacterial envelopes with the pleiotropic pharmacological effects of fenugreek phytochemicals. This design aims to improve drug stability, bioavailability, and controlled release—overcoming the major limitations associated with crude plant extracts in therapeutic applications (Zhang et al. 2024). Fenugreek (T. foenum-graecum) has long been recognized for its anticancer potential, attributed to its rich composition of steroidal saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenolic acids that exhibit antiproliferative, antioxidant, and pro-apoptotic effects in several cancer cell lines (Mir et al. 2019).

The efficiency of bacterial ghost formation was confirmed through comprehensive physicochemical and morphological analyses. The marked reduction (≈92%) in optical density at 600 nm, together with the significant release of intracellular DNA and protein, indicates efficient cytoplasmic evacuation and loss of cellular integrity while maintaining the outer membrane framework. These findings are consistent with prior reports on chemically induced BGs demonstrating that cytoplasmic content can be removed without compromising envelope morphology (Saleh et al. 2023). Moreover, the absence of bacterial growth after incubation validated the full loss of viability, confirming the biosafety of the produced EcGhs (Ali et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2022).

Thermal treatment was selected as the inactivation method in the current study to conserve the surface structures of the bacteria while disabling its internal metabolism and retarding its ability to replicate (Rabiei et al. 2019). The prepared HKEc were recognized firstly by the lack of growth on LB plates after 48 h at 37 °C incubation.

Microscopy confirmed the formation and integrity of EcGhs and HKEc. Safranin, toluidine blue, and nigrosine staining (Fig. 1a–i) revealed that EcGhs retained their intact outer walls but lost cytoplasmic content, indicating successful envelope preservation. Safranin confirmed the presence of intact cell walls in all samples. Toluidine blue distinguished live (blue-stained) bacteria from nucleic acid–free EcGhs (unstained). Negative staining verified the retention of negative surface charges, a key factor for cellular uptake and electrostatic interactions with bioactive compounds (Chen et al. 2021). TEM imaging (Fig. 1j–l) revealed hollow ghost cells—lacking internal contents but with smooth, continuous envelopes (~600 nm wide, 2 µm long, internal volume 0.565 μm3). This balance of porosity and rigidity is key for molecule encapsulation and stability. In contrast, HKEc retained its cytoplasmic content and intact envelopes, likely resulting in different diffusion behavior. This supports traditional views of BGs as non-denatured envelopes ideal for drug delivery (Abdullah et al. 2023; Saleh et al. 2023).

The anticancer potential of fenugreek seed extract is likely attributed to its rich content of bioactive compounds, including saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids, tannins, and anthraquinones. In this study, the ethanolic extract of T. foenum-graecum seeds (FEE) yielded 14% via cold maceration, consistent with previously reported extraction efficiencies (Bukhari et al. 2008; Idris et al. 2021). Phytochemical screening confirmed the presence of multiple bioactive classes (Supplementary Table S1), supporting its reported anticancer properties (Ahmad et al. 2022). Spectroscopic analyses further validated these findings: UV/Vis absorption at 225, 270, and 370 nm indicated conjugated systems characteristic of flavonoids and other polyphenols (Supplementary Fig. S1a), while FTIR spectra revealed functional groups such as N–H, O–H, and C–H, reflecting the chemical diversity of the extract (El-Bahy 2005). GC–MS profiling revealed 26 major compounds (Fig. 2a, Table 1), such as n-hexadecanoic acid and lupeol, which are noted for their anticancer properties. Collectively, these results indicate that FEE’s bioactivity may stem from the combined action of several phytochemicals, underlining its promise as a natural anticancer agent and warranting further study of its mechanisms. The LC–MS/MS analysis of the ethanolic extract of Trigonella foenum-graecum revealed the presence of a diverse range of bioactive secondary metabolites belonging primarily to flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, amino acid derivatives, and phenolic acids. Both the positive and negative ionization modes provided complementary information, with major chromatographic peaks corresponding to trigonelline, orientin, vitexin, vitexin 2″-O-rhamnoside, kaempferol derivatives, diosgenin, trillin, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and 4-hydroxyisoleucine (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). These compounds have been previously reported as characteristic constituents of fenugreek and are largely responsible for its broad pharmacological spectrum (Gupta et al. 2024; Morshidi et al. 2025; Raju and Rao 2012; Yang et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2025a).

Trigonelline, a notable alkaloid identified in the extract, is linked to strong antidiabetic and neuroprotective effects by modulating glucose metabolism and reducing oxidative stress (Gupta et al. 2024). Additionally, diosgenin and its glycoside trillin, which are major steroidal saponins in fenugreek seeds, are recognized for their anticancer, hypolipidemic, and hepatoprotective effects. Diosgenin is also an important precursor in steroidal drug synthesis and has been found to induce apoptosis in several cancer cell lines (Raju and Rao 2012).

Flavonoid glycosides such as orientin, vitexin, and kaempferol derivatives were also abundant in the extract. Orientin and vitexin, both C-glycosyl flavones, contribute significantly to the antioxidant and cytoprotective potential of fenugreek by scavenging reactive oxygen species and modulating inflammatory signaling pathways (Yang et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2025a). Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside (astragalin) and kaempferol-3,7-di-O-glucoside are flavanols glycosides with reported anticancer and anti-inflammatory properties, likely contributing to the extract’s observed bioefficacy (Morshidi et al. 2025; Shabbeer et al. 2009).

In addition to flavonoids and saponins, the presence of phenolic acids such as caffeic acid and p-coumaric acid enhances the antioxidant potential of the extract. These phenolics are well-documented for their free radical scavenging, antimicrobial, and chemopreventive effects, providing synergistic protection against oxidative damage (Amin et al. 2005). The amino acid derivative 4-hydroxyisoleucine, a unique metabolite in fenugreek, exhibits insulinotropic activity and contributes to the plant’s hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic effects (Broca et al. 1999).

Collectively, the LC–MS/MS profile of T. foenum-graecum confirms that its pharmacological activities stem from the synergistic action of multiple secondary metabolites with complementary mechanisms. These findings align with prior reports that fenugreek’s bio efficacy particularly in metabolic disorders and cancer prevention is attributed to its complex phytochemical composition combining saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenolic acids (Dong et al. 2025)The identification of such compounds underscores the therapeutic potential of fenugreek extract as a source of multifunctional bioactive compounds for nutraceutical and pharmaceutical applications.

Additionally, PL scanning of FEE at 5 mg/mL concentration revealed peaks at 485 nm and 535 nm (Fig. 2d), allowing for potential real-time tracking of drug-loaded formulations, offering insights into their biodistribution and cellular interactions.

In this study, we successfully encapsulated the fluorescent ethanolic extract (FEE) into both E. coli ghosts (EcGhs) and heat-killed E. coli (HKEc), achieving loading capacities of 14 µg/mg and 11.6 µg/mg, respectively. FEE-loaded EcGhs and HKEc (LEcGhs and LHKEc) exhibited a distinct dark creamy coloration compared to their unloaded counterparts, providing a preliminary visual indication of successful loading (Fig. 3). Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) further confirmed incorporation, as LEcGhs and LHKEc displayed dual fluorescence, emitting at 612 nm (red) and 535 nm (green) (Fig. 4), highlighting their potential as theranostic systems that combine therapeutic and imaging functionalities. Transmission electron microscopy corroborated these findings, revealing an increase in the cell wall thickness of loaded EcGhs without structural compromise (Fig. 3b, d), consistent with previous observations in prodigiosin-loaded Lactobacillus acidophilus ghosts (Saleh et al. 2023). TEM analysis of LHKEc (Fig. 3f, h) showed uniform distribution of FEE throughout the cells, suggesting interactions with both cytoplasmic contents and the membrane, aligning with reports of anticancer agent binding in heat-inactivated L. plantarum (Abdullah et al. 2023). These results collectively demonstrate the successful loading and structural preservation of FEE within bacterial carriers, supporting their potential for combined therapeutic delivery and imaging applications.

The in vitro release profiles of free FEE, LEcGhs, and LHKEc were investigated under physiological (pH 7.4) and mildly acidic (pH 6.7) conditions (Fig. 5a and b), simulating normal and tumor microenvironments, respectively. The release pattern highlights the influence of carrier type on FEE diffusion and overall release behavior.

Free FEE experienced a rapid initial burst followed by near-complete release within 72 h, typical of molecules diffusing unimpeded by a matrix. In contrast, both LEcGhs and LHKEc exhibited sustained release profiles, suggesting that the bacterial envelope functions as a diffusion barrier that modulates drug release. The ghost structures’ intact but empty envelopes promote gradual, steady diffusion while maintaining stability (Gupta et al. 2024; Raju and Rao 2012).

A modest increase in FEE release was observed at pH 6.7 compared with pH 7.4. This may be attributed to the increased solubility and partial protonation of certain phenolic and flavonoid constituents of fenugreek extract under mildly acidic conditions, which can slightly enhance diffusion. However, the overall differences between the two pH conditions were limited, suggesting that the formulations do not exhibit strong pH-triggered release behavior. Similar pH-dependent trends have been reported in other natural extract-based delivery systems, although the extent of responsiveness varies depending on matrix composition (Paukner et al. 2004; Xie et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2025b).

Moreover, LEcGh showed slightly more sustained release than LHKEc, which may be related to differences in membrane integrity and permeability pathways within the bacterial ghost structure (Raju and Rao 2012). This controlled release behavior supports the potential of both systems as biocompatible drug carriers for sustained delivery, rather than as strongly pH-responsive systems.

FEE-loaded EcGhs and HKEc, as shown in Table 3, preserved the encapsulated extract’s fluorescence and chemical stability far better than free FEE. Both maintained over 70% fluorescence during refrigerated and frozen storage, whereas free FEE degraded rapidly. LEcGhs kept 100% fluorescence for a week at both 4 °C and 37 °C, and LHKEc retained 90%. At −20 °C, both carriers held full fluorescence for 2 weeks before it declined, making freezing the best storage option. This improved stability is attributed to the protective bacterial envelope, consistent with previous findings on bacterial ghost systems for the delivery of drugs and vaccines (Alanazi et al. 2020). Overall, these results confirm that EcGhs and HKEc significantly enhance the stability of FEE, supporting their potential as reliable carriers for long-term storage and therapeutic applications. It is worth mentioning that there is a strong need to develop new alternative therapies for the prevention and treatment of breast cancer. Fenugreekis a bioactive chemical source that has anti-cancer and other pharmacological benefits (Al-Timimi 2019; Almalki and Naguib 2022; Khalil et al. 2015).

Both MDA-MB-231 and ZR-75-1 cell lines were chosen for the current study because they have different phenotypes and genotypes with varying capacities of differentiation. This investigation aims to assess the effects of various treatment groups (FEE, EcGh, LEcGh, HKEc, and LHKEc) on the two breast cancer cell lines.

Cytotoxicity in these groups was evaluated using the WST-1 calorimetric assay, which measures metabolic activity and is suitable for analyzing cell proliferation, cell viability, and cytotoxicity. We found that the viability of both ZR-75-1 and MDA-MB-231 cells decreased in a concentration-dependent manner. This is consistent with earlier research showing variability in cell line sensitivity. Our findings also support reports that the extract is more toxic to cancer cells than to healthy cells, likely because cancer cells are more susceptible to flavonoid-induced cell death. Furthermore, when FEE was encapsulated within E. coli ghosts (LEcGhs), cytotoxicity increased significantly, as evidenced by a decrease in IC₅₀ from 2.2 mg/mL to 1.2 mg/mL. In comparison, FEE alone had only modest cytotoxic effects, indicating that our delivery system enhances targeted activity against the breast cancer cells tested.

To investigate the main reason for the inhibition in cell viability, which should be related to apoptosis induction and inhibition of several oncogenic signaling pathways. One of the most studied oncogenic transcription factors in recent decades, STAT3 is closely linked to the development, spread, metastasis, chemoresistance, and immune evasion of cancer (Huynh et al. 2019), in addition to controlling several target oncogenes (J.-h. Ma et al. 2020).

Several studies have shown that STAT3 can decrease the apoptosis of breast cancer cells by upregulating cyclin D-1, c-myc, and bcl2, suggesting a possible role for STAT3 in cell cycle and survival (Kohandel et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2020). Moreover, STAT3 activated by the IL-6/JAK2 pathway can inhibit caspase-dependent apoptosis, which is linked to the Bax/Bcl-2 system (Xie et al. 2019). According to Vundavilli et al., combination therapy targeting both mTOR and STAT3, as identified by a Bayesian inference framework, may be the most effective approach for breast cancer treatment (Vundavilli et al. 2019). In line with this, our study demonstrated that treatment with LEcGh in both breast cancer cell lines resulted in a significant downregulation of STAT3 expression compared to controls. Additionally, recent evidence has emphasized the significance of STAT5 in cancer development. Moving beyond the traditional perspective, it is now understood that tumor survival, growth, metastasis, and resistance to anticancer treatments are influenced by aberrant STAT3 signaling—but not by STAT5—primarily due to Stat3’s constitutive activation or deletion. However, studies have shown that Stat5 activation contributes to cell differentiation and promotes homotypic adhesion in breast cancer (Sultan et al. 2008).

Previously, we have shown that STAT5 functions as a tumor suppressor (Sultan et al. 2008). Our data showed increased expression of STAT5, a factor known to function as a tumor suppressor, following treatment with FEE and LEcGh in both breast cancer cell lines compared to the control group. As a result, the oncogenic effects of STAT3—which is frequently constitutively activated in breast cancer—may be counteracted by the tumor-suppressing actions of STAT5.

β-Catenin is another factor that may help explain how LEcGh treatment could induce apoptosis by inhibiting cell growth. As a multifunctional protein, β-catenin is essential for physiological homeostasis, but its abnormal overexpression is linked to several diseases, including breast cancer (King et al. 2012; Shang et al. 2017). Early carcinogenesis is caused by the aberrant regulation of the transcription factor β-catenin, a crucial component of the Wnt signaling pathway, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Zhang and Wang 2020).

Increasing evidence indicates that deregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway contributes to the development and progression of certain solid tumors and hematological malignancies (Zhang and Wang 2020). β-catenin has been identified as a poor prognostic marker in several cancers, notably breast cancer, due to its increased activity (Zhao et al. 2014). In our study, treatment with LEcGh significantly reduced β-catenin expression in both breast cancer cell lines when compared to controls, including free and empty BG groups.

The mTOR pathway is a critical signaling cascade implicated in breast cancer progression (Hare and Harvey 2017) reported that increased expression of mTOR is associated with worse outcomes for breast cancer patients. Studies investigating genetic alterations within the pathway reveal that most modifications occur upstream of mTOR, leading to increased signaling activity through mTOR (Moschetta et al. 2014). PI3Ks play a crucial role in activating mTOR through Akt and the TSC1/2 complex. Alterations in PI3Ks can lead to excessive mTOR signaling, a phenomenon commonly observed across multiple malignancies (Moschetta et al. 2014). Our data in the present study showed that m-TOR is down-regulated after treatment with LEcGh for 48 h in both tested cell lines.

To investigate if the inhibition in the cell viability and downregulation of the three main oncogenic signals in the tested breast cancer cells due to apoptosis induction, caspase-3 activity is determined. Activation occurs via specific proteolytic cleavage, which removes the prodomain and allows the large and small subunits to assemble into the enzyme’s catalytic domain. Caspase-3 is a central effector of apoptosis, activated by both extrinsic (death ligands) and intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathways, and is also involved in tissue differentiation, regeneration, and neural development. Elevation of caspase-3 activity is a strong indication of apoptosis. Our results show that LEcGh treatment leads to a twofold increase in caspase-3 activity in both breast cancer cell lines compared to FEE or the control, suggesting a significant induction of apoptotic processes. This difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Notably, no previous studies have reported on the cytotoxicity of functionalized FEE-EcGh.

We analyzed PARP1 cleavage, a key nuclear enzyme that primarily functions as a transferase and plays a crucial role in DNA repair via the Base Excision Repair (BER) pathway. Our results highlight the importance of PARP1 in the fundamental processes of tumorigenesis and cancer progression. Elevated PARP1 levels have been observed in many cancers, with the protein detected in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of cancer cells. Assessing PARP1 expression is vital for evaluating the efficacy, therapeutic potential, and adverse effects of clinically used inhibitors. In our study, LEcGh treatment resulted in a significant increase in PARP1 activity—threefold in MDA-MB-231 cells and twofold in ZR-75-1 cells—compared to controls. This increase likely indicates greater DNA damage in rapidly dividing cells, which requires enhanced PARP1-mediated DNA repair. The activity increase was statistically significant (P < 0.05) versus controls.

The cross-talk between β-catenin and STAT3 leads us to focus on the possible link between the major tested oncogenic signals in the tested breast cancer cells. In several cancer types, including breast cancer, β-catenin and the JAK/STAT3 axis have been shown to interact. Phosphorylated STAT3 (pSTAT3) increases certain transcriptional activity, which amplifies oncogenic signaling.

Tumor aggressiveness and stem-like characteristics are enhanced by this collaboration, which encourages the overexpression of target genes such cyclin D1, c-Myc, and surviving. On the other hand, in some situations, STAT3 may use SOCS-mediated feedback loops to control β-catenin signaling adversely.

Mammary gland differentiation and tumor growth are associated with STAT5, another member of the STAT family, which is often hyperactivated in breast cancer (Chen and Xue 2025; Li et al. 2024). A key integrator of cellular metabolism, proliferation, and survival, mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) is often overexpressed in breast cancer.

The main way that mTOR and β-catenin signaling interact is through AKT mediated suppression of GSK3β, which stabilizes β-catenin and increases transcriptional activity.

Furthermore, mTOR can regulate STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby linking inflammatory and nutrient-sensing pathways in the maintenance of cancer stem cells. Conversely, it has been shown that mTOR inhibition alters STAT5 activity and downstream translational control via 4E-BP1. The interconnected nature of these signaling cascades highlights a cooperative network driving breast cancer growth, where pharmacological targeting of β-catenin, STAT3/5, or mTOR can disrupt several oncogenic nodes simultaneously, inhibiting cell growth and inducing apoptosis (Bou Antoun and Chioni 2023; Gao et al. 2023).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the ethanolic extract (FEE) of T. foenum-graecum (fenugreek), when delivered using bacterial ghosts (BGs), showed strong anticancer effects on breast cancer cell lines ZR-75-1 and MDA-MB-231. Compared to the crude extract, FEE-loaded BGs significantly improved cytotoxic efficacy, reflected in decreased IC50 values. This delivery method facilitated greater cellular uptake and sustained release of FEE bioactive compounds by providing an efficient, biocompatible platform for targeted therapy. Mechanistically, FEE-loaded BGs notably suppressed key oncogenic pathways, including mTOR, β-catenin, and STAT3, which are vital for tumor growth, survival, and metastasis. Furthermore, FEE-loaded BGs enhanced Stat5 expression, a differentiation factor and tumor suppressor, in both treated cell lines. This suggests the treatment may promote differentiation after substantial inhibition of cell growth and proliferation. Collectively, these findings emphasize the potential of bacterial-based drug delivery systems as new vehicles for administering plant-derived anticancer agents. Loading FEE phytochemicals into our promising delivery system might enable the creation of safe and targeted therapies for various cancers. Further experiments are needed to investigate the main effects of the selected components of the fenugreek extract using this promising delivery system in vivo.
