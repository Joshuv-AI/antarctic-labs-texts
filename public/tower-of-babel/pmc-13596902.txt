X‑ray FLASH RT inhibits lung cancer bone metastasis via activating CD4⁺ T-cell‑mediated antitumor immunity with modulation of Th17 cell response
Authors: Yao Huang, Tao Wu, Wen Yang, Yuan-yuan Liang, Yun-fei Sun, Chao-fan Ma, Ruo Tang, Rui-ci Liang, Xue-ting Zheng, Hui-wen Luo, Cheng-he Ding, Xiao-an Li, Yan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLung cancer is characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis, with 50%–70% of patients presenting local or distant metastasis at diagnosis, among which bone metastasis is the most common type. Radiotherapy is the standard approach for managing cancerous bone metastasis pain. Here, we report the first application of X-ray FLASH radiotherapy (RT) in treating lung cancer bone metastasis in mice. Using an intratibial LLC-luc cell injection model, mice were treated with FLASH RT (94 Gy/s) or conventional RT (CONV RT, 0.1 Gy/s) via a petal accelerator developed by the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics. The results demonstrated that X-ray FLASH RT and CONV RT exhibited comparable efficacy in suppressing tumor growth, and both may alleviate osteolytic lesions in mice with lung cancer bone metastasis through CD4⁺ T cell-dependent antitumor responses. However, FLASH RT further attenuated bone destruction by inhibiting osteoclastogenesis, remodeling the bone-tumor microenvironment, and reducing tumor invasion, with lower systemic toxicity. Notably, unlike CONV RT, FLASH RT instigates a distinct DNA damage response that drives oxygen depletion, thereby attenuating ROS accumulation. This mechanistic cascade sequentially suppresses cGAS-STING-NF-κB axis activation and dampens the ensuing inflammatory response. In addition, we observed a reduction in Th17 cell differentiation following FLASH RT compared to CONV RT, which may contribute to the differential efficacy of these two radiation modalities in attenuating lung cancer bone metastasis. Collectively, X-ray FLASH RT provides a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with bone metastases.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a group of malignant tumors characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis and is the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide. 1 Distant metastasis is the leading cause of death in lung cancer patients, with 50%–70% of lung cancer patients presenting with local or distant metastases at the time of diagnosis, of which bone metastases (BM) are the most common clinical malignancy. 2-4 Skeletal complications resulting from tumor BM, referred to as skeletal-related events (SREs). SREs can impair patients' functional independence, erode health-related quality of life, and diminish overall survival. 5 Surgery, radiotherapy (RT), bisphosphonates, and chemotherapy are currently the common clinical treatments. RT has become the standard pain relief method for patients with cancer BM, 6 with about one-third of patients experiencing complete pain relief. However, while RT effectively kills tumor cells, it also damages healthy tissues, and its associated side effects may limit the efficacy and reduce patients' quality of life. 7

Recent studies have shown that ultrahigh dose rates (>40 Gy/s) RT can effectively control tumors while significantly reducing radiation damage to normal tissues, a phenomenon known as the “FLASH effect”. 8-10 This finding provides a promising opportunity to improve the effectiveness of RT and has rapidly become a research hotspot in the field of RT. However, the mechanism of FLASH RT is still unclear, and researchers have proposed various hypotheses to explain this effect. 11 Whether it has the same effect on different types of tumors and whether its mechanism of action is the same needs to be explored by further research.

To date, the FLASH effect has been investigated using various radiation sources worldwide, including electron beams, 9 , 10 , 12 proton beams, 13-15 and X-ray. 16 , 17 Low-energy electrons are primarily applicable to superficial tumors and small animal models, but their limited penetration depth restricts their use in treating deep-seated tumors. In contrast, high-energy electrons and X-rays possess superior tissue penetration and are considered more suitable for clinical applications. 18 , 19 However, due to limitations in energy deposition and X-ray conversion efficiency, the development of high-energy X-ray FLASH systems remains technically challenging and has progressed slowly. 19 Our team has developed a high-energy X-ray FLASH system based on a high-power rhodotron accelerator. This system is capable of achieving average dose rates of ≥100 Gy/s at a distance of 0.8 m and ≥800 Gy/s at 0.06 m from the target. It features a high duty cycle, high conversion efficiency, and excellent beam quality. By repeatedly accelerating electrons to near-light speed within the same acceleration cavity and directing them onto a high-Z target to generate X-rays via bremsstrahlung. Additionally, it supports multi-energy extraction, providing a robust platform for deep-tumor FLASH treatment and studies of dose-dependent biological effects.

In this study, after constructing a mouse lung cancer bone metastasis model, mice were subjected to FLASH RT (94 Gy/s) and CONV RT (0.1 Gy/s), respectively, using a MV X-ray-based petal accelerator built by the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics. The results showed that X‑ray FLASH RT exerted antitumor effects by activating CD4⁺ T cells and exhibited comparable efficacy to CONV RT in inhibiting tumor growth. However, FLASH RT was more effective in alleviating osteolytic lesions caused by lung cancer bone metastasis in mice and caused fewer side effects. Notably, compared with CONV RT, FLASH RT induces oxygen exhaustion via a unique DNA damage response, thereby reducing ROS levels, inhibiting activation of the cGAS‑STING‑NF‑κB pathway, suppressing inflammatory responses, and ultimately inhibiting Th17 cell differentiation to alleviate bone metastasis. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the application of X‑ray FLASH RT in the treatment of lung cancer bone metastasis.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

Luciferase-tagged Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC-luc) were purchased from Xiamen Immocell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Fujian, China). All cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Procell System, China) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell System, China). 5% CO2 and 37 °C were maintained throughout the incubation.

An in vivo model for lung cancer bone metastasis

C57BL/6J mice (female, 6–8 weeks, 18–20 g) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) and housed at the Animal Experiment Center of Mianyang Central Hospital. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All mice were bred and housed in specific pathogen-free conditions under controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and exposed to a constant 12 h light‒dark cycle in the animal facilities at Laboratory Animal Center of Mianyang Central Hospital. Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of freshly prepared 2% tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg) for deep anesthesia. Loss of pedal withdrawal reflex was confirmed prior to euthanasia by cervical dislocation. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Mianyang Central Hospital (File No. S20250312-01). Mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and placed in the supine position. The hind limbs were shaved, and the surgical site was disinfected three times with 75% ethanol using cotton balls. 20 μL LLC-luc cell suspension (5 × 107 cells/mL, 1 × 106 cells in total) was drawn into an insulin syringe. The knee joint was flexed to expose the patellar ligament, and the needle was inserted into the medullary cavity at the proximal intercondylar fossa along the long axis of the femur. The cell suspension was slowly injected; the needle was held in place for 10 seconds to prevent backflow, then slowly withdrawn. The surgical site was cleaned with sterile saline-moistened cotton swabs and disinfected with povidone-iodine. Mice were subsequently housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility under standard conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Activity status and the inoculation site were monitored daily until tumor cells proliferated and established tumors in the bone marrow cavity. Progression of tumor growth and dissemination to the bone was followed by measuring photon flux of bioluminescent imaging (BLI) signals using a Tanon-ABL X6 imaging system (Tanon) after intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg D-Luciferin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China).

X-ray FLASH irradiator

X-rays are adopted to irradiate tumor cells in this study, generated via the bremsstrahlung effect of electron beams. The accelerator was independently developed by the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics. It delivers electron energies of 6/7/8/9 MeV with an adjustable average current ranging from 0.1 μA to 3 mA. Since the stainless steel target cannot withstand high power dissipation, the duty cycle is limited to 32%, resulting in a maximum average current of 3 mA. The energy spread is less than 1%, and the peak beam power reaches approximately 85 kW. The target material is 304 stainless steel, as listed in Table S1. The electron beam used in this work is identical to that in conventional radiotherapy, except for the average dose rate and temporal structure. Electron beams are accelerated horizontally. Prior to reaching the target, the beams are deflected by a 90° magnet and strike the target vertically. FLASH radiotherapy is realized by reducing the distance between mice and the target, with a separation of approximately 15 cm. Three lead bricks equipped with collimators are arranged between the target and mice. Under FLASH RT conditions, the system achieves an average dose rate of 94 Gy/s, while in CONV RT mode, the dose rate is approximately 0.1 Gy/s. In both modes, the dose rate and total delivered dose can be modulated by adjusting the pulse width, repetition frequency, and irradiation time.

Bone metastases radiation therapy

A total of 15 d after tumor inoculation, 150 mg/kg D-Luciferin was intraperitoneal injected. BM were monitored using BLI in vivo. The experimental unit was the single animal. All mice were divided into three groups (Control, FLASH RT, and CONV RT, n = 12 per group) so that the BLI signal in each group was approximately 105 p/s/cm2/sr. For accurate identification, each mouse will be assigned a unique ear tag. Mice were brought to the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics for radiotherapy. The tibia of the inoculated tumors was irradiated to 50 Gy at FLASH RT or CONV RT dose rates. Table S1 summarizes the physical parameters used for both FLASH and CONV dose-rate irradiations. In the same irradiator, a single top-down beam was used with the animal in the supine position, with parameter adjustments to achieve two distinct treatment modalities. After anesthesia induction with 2% tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg), mice were immobilized using a customized fixation device to achieve a source-to-surface distance (SSD) of 66 mm. For CONV and FLASH dose irradiation, the field of view size is 10 × 10 mm2. During lower limb target irradiation in mice, conformal lead shielding was applied to precisely block excess dose to non-target regions. Consequently, tissues outside the target region were completely spared from irradiation. The irradiation field and dose distribution were experimentally measured and calibrated using radiotherapy verification films. The films were scanned at three minutes and 24 hours after irradiation, respectively. The dose obtained at three minutes was used for dose monitoring in the experiment, including detecting beam stability and the consistency of the dose within the experimental group. The dose obtained within 24 hours is used for dosimetric analysis, which can calculate the dose in the target area and the dose uniformity within the radiation field.

Tumor growth assessment

Tumors were inoculated into the tibial bone marrow rather than forming solid masses; therefore, tumor volume could not be directly measured. Tumor growth delay was assessed by the following two methods: (a) measuring leg circumference with a soft ruler twice weekly, and (b) detecting the average value of signals by BLI imaging system once weekly. Mice gained weight with tumor progression and lost weight upon effective radiotherapy, body weight changes were also used as a supplementary indicator of tumor growth delay.

Blood routine and biochemical index detection

Blood samples were collected through the tail vein 1 d before radiotherapy and 7 and 14 d after radiotherapy, blood routines were compared using an automatic animal hematology analyzer (URIT-5160Vet, Guilin, China). On 14 d after radiotherapy, blood samples were collected from orbital venous plexus. An automatic biochemical analyzer (URIT-8021AVet, Guilin, China) was used to detect the liver and kidney function indexes in mice.

Flow cytometry

Mice in each group were sacrificed at 7 and 14 d after radiotherapy, respectively. The spleen of each mouse was removed and ground, and erythrocytes were removed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (SolarBio, Beijing, China). The single-cell suspension, containing 1 × 106 cells, was incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) for 10 minutes at room temperature for Fc blocking. Add 1 μL of BD Horizon Fixable Viability Stain 575 V Stock Solution for each 1 mL of cell suspension and then flow cytometry staining was performed. The antibodies were incubated in a mixed manner, with all antibodies diluted at a ratio of 1:1000. The staining antibodies used are listed in Table S2. After re-washing, stained cells were analyzed using FACS VerseTM (BD, NJ, USA). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo V10.9.0 (FlowJo, OR USA).

Micro-CT analysis

Upon sacrifice of bone metastasis mice at 14 d after radiotherapy, tibia was collected and fixed in 4% buffered formalin for 2 d and were scanned on a micro-CT scanner (PINGSENG, Jiangsu, China). The 3D models were reconstructed by Recon, and the region of interest ROI was analyzed using Avatar software, with the same region selected for all samples to calculate the following parameters: BMD, BV/TV, Tb. Th, SMI, Tb. N, Tb.Pf and Tb.Sp.

Bone histomorphometric analysis

A total of 14 d after radiotherapy, bone transfer mice were sacrificed and tibiae were collected, fixed in 4% buffered formalin for 2 d followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA for 3–4 weeks, and 6 μm sections were prepared on a rotation microtome. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated. The samples were next performed H&E and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the standard protocol. For TRAP staining, the bone sections from 4 mice per group were obtained, and 3 fields of view were randomly selected in the bone marrow and cartilage for photography. The number of osteoclasts in each section was counted separately using Image J software. For IHC staining, the endogenous peroxidase of sections was inactivated by incubation with 3% H2O2 for 10 min. Then the sections were incubated with 1:100 diluted TTF1 antibodies (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C overnight. Images were photographed under a bright-field microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image J software was used for statistical analysis of area positive-stained.

Multiplex cytokine, chemokine analysis and ALP analysis in serum

On 14 d after radiotherapy, blood samples were collected from orbital venous plexus. Cytokine and chemokine levels were evaluated with a Luminex 200 system (Luminex) using a panel of 31 mouse cytokines (LX-MultiDTM-31, R&D system, Minnesota, USA) in accordance with the recommendations of the manufacturer. All other steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions in LabEx of UNIV (Shanghai, China). Samples were then analyzed on a Bio-Plex MAGPIX (Bio-Rad, California, USA) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Serum ALP levels were determined using ALP activity assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

RNA sequencing

On the 7th day after radiotherapy, spleens were harvested from mice in each group, and total RNA was extracted using standard protocols. Illumina RNA library construction, library purification, and transcriptome sequencing were performed according to instructions provided by Shanghai Genechem Sequencing Company (Shanghai, China). Differential expression analysis between the two comparison groups was performed using DESeq2 software (version 1.16.1), followed by enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

Sequential multiplex staining was conducted using tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technology (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Sections underwent four sequential rounds of staining, each comprising: overnight incubation at 4 °C with one primary antibody (CD3, cat. no. GB150004; CD4, cat. no. GB15064; CD8, cat. no. GB15068; and IL-17A, cat. no. ER1902-37), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation and TSA staining with a fluorophore-conjugated tyramide. Antibody elution was performed between rounds to eliminate cross-reactivity. Finally, sections were counterstained with DAPI and treated with an autofluorescence quencher. For each sample, five random fields of view per section were examined and imaged. Positively stained regions were semi-quantified using Image J software, and relative expression levels were derived following data normalization. Statistical comparisons were then conducted on the normalized values to evaluate differential expression.

Real-time quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from spleen tissue using the TRIzol method (Chomczynski and Sacchi 1987). 20 In a 2 mL centrifuge tube, an equal volume of spleen tissue homogenate (500 μL) and TRIzol reagent (500 μL) were combined for total RNA isolation. The resulting RNA pellet was dissolved in 50 μL of nuclease-free sterile water and stored at −80 °C until further use. RNA concentration was measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometry. cDNA was obtained by taking 3 μg of RNA for reverse transcription according to the FOREGENE reverse-transcription kit manual. qRT-PCR was performed with 100 ng of RNA using the ForeColor™-(Blue) SYBR Green Kit (FOREGENE, Chengdu, China) in technical triplicates. Gene expression was normalized to that of GAPDH (or β-actin) and analyzed by the ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were provided in Table S3.

Western blotting assay

Spleen tissues were snap-frozen and homogenized on ice in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5× Omni-Easy™ Protein Sample Loading Buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai China), denatured by boiling for 10 minutes, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NFATC1, calcineurin A, IL-17A, and GAPDH. After washing off unbound antibodies, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Detection was performed using the enhanced chemiluminescence method (Abbkine, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, exposure was carried out with the iBright FL1000 Automated Gel Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the results were analyzed using ImageJ software. The expression levels of target proteins were normalized relative to those of the internal reference protein GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

All outcome assessors and the statistician were blinded to group allocation using randomly coded samples/data. All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 by one-way ANOVA. The significance level assumed at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

X-ray radiotherapy in bone metastasis mouse model

To establish a mouse model of lung cancer BM, LLC-luc cells were intratibially injected into female C57BL/6 mice on day 0. Body weight and leg circumference were measured every 5 d. On day 15, tumor-bearing mice underwent BLI signal value, and were subsequently stratified into three groups (Control, FLASH RT, and CONV RT) with comparable mean bioluminescence intensities. Mice were subjected to corresponding radiotherapy treatments according to their experimental grouping. Body weight and leg circumference were monitored twice weekly post-irradiation. At 7- and 14-d post-treatment (corresponding to days 22 and 29 post-tumor inoculation, respectively), therapeutic efficacy was evaluated by BLI, after which mice were euthanized and tissue samples were harvested (Figure 1a). Mice were then treated with either FLASH RT or CONV RT using an accelerator independently developed by the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics. The figure illustrates the layout of the target region. Films are separately attached to mice to verify the absorbed radiation dose (Figure 1b). Irradiation was performed using lead bricks with a diameter of 10 mm. For the treatment setup, the placement order from bottom to top was as follows: lead bricks, acrylic plates, films, and the tibias of mice (Figure 1c). The results showed that 14 d after tumor inoculation, the mice showed swelling in the leg with an average BLI signal value of about 105 p/s/cm2/sr (Figure 1d), positive lung cancer marker TFF1 (Figure 1e), and osteolytic lesions (Figure 1f), suggesting that the mouse lung cancer BM model was successfully constructed. The results indicated that the total dose delivered was approximately 50 Gy (±2.5 Gy) (Figure 1g and h), with excellent dose homogeneity. Film grayscale value confirmed that normal tissues outside the target volume received no additional radiation dose. Beam orientation and field size were strictly controlled, ensuring precise dose delivery to the target.

X-ray FLASH RT is equivalent to CONV RT in controlling tumor growth

Before radiotherapy, mice in all groups exhibited gradual weight gain due to tumor growth. Following irradiation, mice in the treatment groups initially lost weight but subsequently recovered toward normal levels, whereas mice in the Control group continued to gain weight due to unchecked tumor progression. Notably, a sharp weight drop was observed in the irradiated groups on day 4 after radiotherapy (day 19 after tumor inoculation), which may be attributable to radiation-induced toxicity. However, the magnitude of weight loss was less pronounced in the FLASH RT group compared to the CONV RT group, suggesting that FLASH RT may be associated with reduced side effects (Figure 2a). The leg circumference of mice in the Control group increased continuously, whereas that in the radiotherapy groups decreased gradually, with no significant difference observed in tumor control efficacy between the FLASH RT and CONV RT groups (Figure 2b and c). After radiotherapy, the tumor growth of mice was inhibited, and the mean value of BLI signal decreased, while the Control group mice continued to rise (Figure 2d–f). No statistically significant difference was detected between FLASH RT and CONV RT in terms of tumor growth inhibition at any of the evaluated time points (p > 0.5).

X-ray improves hematologic indexes and liver and kidney functions in bone metastases mice and FLASH RT toxicity is lower than CONV RT

Blood routine revealed that the Control mice exhibited elevated levels of multiple blood parameters at the early stage (day 14 after tumor inoculation), consistent with a systemic inflammatory response induced by tumor burden and indicative of active antitumor immunity. As the disease progressed and mice aged, immune function gradually declined. At the late stage of the experiment, multiple hematological parameters decreased below normal reference ranges, including RBC, HGB, and LYM, suggesting advanced tumor-associated bone marrow suppression and immune exhaustion. Compared with the Control group, blood indexes—RBC, WBC, HGB, PLT and LYM—in the irradiation group progressively recovered toward baseline levels over the course of treatment (Figure 3a–e). Furthermore, by day 14 post-radiotherapy, serum hepatic and renal function biomarkers (e.g. TBA, AST, UREA, and CREA) in the irradiation group had essentially normalized, indicative of therapeutic benefit and resolution of acute radiation toxicity. Nevertheless, statistically significant differences persisted between the irradiation and control groups (Figure 3f and g).

Compared with the Control group, RBC, WBC, HGB, PLT, and LYM gradually recovered to normal in the radiotherapy group after X-ray treatment and did not cause any damage to liver and kidney functions. Compared with the CONV RT group, the blood indexes of the FLASH RT group were closer to normal (Figure 3a–e), suggesting that FLASH RT had less killing effect on RBC, WBC, and LYM and fewer side effects. Compared with the Control group, the serum liver and kidney functions of mice in the experimental group basically returned to normal 14 d after radiotherapy (Figure 3f and g).

X-ray FLASH RT significantly mitigates bone damage and osteolytic lesions in bone metastases mice

Micro-CT results showed that X-rays attenuated osteolytic lesions induced by BM of lung cancer in mice (Figure 4a). Three-dimensional models were reconstructed using Recon software. BV/TV values, a commonly used index for evaluating bone mass, were elevated in mice receiving radiotherapy, but there was a significant difference between FLASH RT and CONV RT (Figure 4b). Both radiotherapy modalities decreased Tb. Pf values, but FLASH RT values were lower (Figure 4c). The H&E results showed that FLASH RT was more able to reduce the pathological damage of mouse tibias. In the Control group, the bone tissue structure was unclear, and extensive bone erosion and bone fragments were seen. Focal cell proliferation was mostly seen in the bone marrow cavity, lymphocyte infiltration, and bone marrow bruising was mostly seen. A large amount of cell proliferation was seen in the bone marrow cavity of bone tissues in the CONV RT, a small amount of lymphocyte infiltration, and bone fragments were seen. Cell proliferation and connective tissue hyperplasia were also seen in the bone marrow cavity of bone tissues in the FLASH RT, but there was less infiltration of inflammatory cells, and bone fragments with bone erosion were not detected (Figure 4d). Osteoclast activity was assessed by TRAP staining. After mice were treated with radiotherapy, osteoclasts were reduced at the bone marrow and bone joints in both experimental groups of mice (Figure 4e–g). Elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) suggests abnormal bone metabolism and is an important indicator of BM. Compared with CONV RT, the serum ALP levels of mice treated with FLASH RT were significantly decreased (Figure 4h), indicating a more potent inhibitory effect on tumor bone metastasis.

X-ray FLASH RT modulates T-cell immunity and inflammatory cytokine profile to suppress bone metastasis progression

Various types of immune cells were screened by flow cytometry as shown in Figure 5a. Compared with the Control group, both the FLASH RT and CONV RT groups exhibited a significant increase in total CD3⁺ T-cell counts at 7- and 14-d post-irradiation, indicating that radiotherapy induced a systemic T-cell immune response. Among these, the proportion of CD4⁺ T cells was notably elevated, while the proportion of CD8⁺ T cells decreased, suggesting that CD4⁺ helper T cells are relatively resistant to radiotherapy and are preferentially activated, whereas CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells may decrease due to higher radiation sensitivity or exhaustion (Figure 5b–i).

A total of 31 cytokines and chemokines were detected using the Luminex 200 system. IL-1beta, TNF-alpha, IFN-gamma and IL-6 (Figure 5j), GM-CSF (Figure 5m), and chemokines, including CX3CL1, CXCL12, CXCL13, CXCL16, CCL3, CCL7, CCL11, CCL12, CCL17, and CCL24 were decreased in RT group of mice (Figure 5k and l). The results suggest that X-ray RT inhibits BM by inhibiting osteoclast activity and regulating the tumor microenvironment, which mainly includes regulating immune cells and inhibiting tumor cell migration and invasion. There were no differences in the remaining cytokines (p > 0.05). Notably, compared with CONV RT, FLASH RT significantly reduced the expression level of IL-6 (p < 0.05), and this difference may be closely related to the subsequent Th17 cell differentiation mechanism.

X-ray FLASH RT-induced antitumor effects accompanied by altered CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration

Transcriptomic sequencing data analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm revealed significant differences in Th17 cell infiltration between the FLASH RT and CONV RT groups (Figure 6a). To validate these findings, mouse splenic tissues were collected at 7 and 14 d post-irradiation for mIHC corroborating the results of immune infiltration analysis and flow cytometry. At day 7 post-irradiation, both FLASH RT and CONV RT groups exhibited a significant increase in total CD3⁺ T-cell counts compared with the Control group, accompanied by a marked elevation in CD4⁺ T-cell proportion and a concomitant decrease in CD8⁺ T-cell proportion (Figure 6b–f). At day 14 post-irradiation, the CD4⁺ T-cell proportion remained significantly elevated, whereas the CD8⁺ T-cell proportion was reduced, both showing significant differences compared with the Control group (Figure 6g–k). These findings were consistent with the flow cytometry data, further corroborating that X-ray FLASH RT mediates antitumor efficacy through enhanced CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration. Notably, at day 7 post-irradiation, the proportion of Th17 cells in the FLASH RT group was significantly lower than that in the CONV RT group (p < 0.05), suggesting potential mechanistic differences in Th17 cell activation between the two radiotherapy modalities (Figure 6f).

RNA-seq identifies differential gene expression patterns between FLASH RT and CONV RT

RNA-seq revealed the mechanisms of X-ray RT and guided the discovery of FLASH RT. Mice spleens were collected 7 d after radiotherapy for RNA-seq. To reveal the gene expression differences between radiotherapy group and the control group, we performed pairwise analysis of the samples and generated volcano plots. The results showed that a total of 4012 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the control group and the FLASH RT group, including 1323 significantly upregulated genes and 2189 significantly downregulated genes. Between the control group and the CONV RT group, 7273 DEGs were detected, with 3422 genes significantly upregulated and 3851 genes significantly downregulated. Meanwhile, 5790 DEGs were found between the FLASH RT group and the CONV RT group, comprising 2938 upregulated genes and 2852 downregulated genes (Figure 7a).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that, compared with the Control group, genes related to immune regulatory functions were upregulated in the radiotherapy groups, particularly those associated with T-cell immune responses. This finding was consistent with the increased number of CD4 + T cells observed in the radiotherapy groups by flow cytometry. Conversely, genes related to the cell cycle were downregulated, suggesting that ionizing radiation affects cells by modulating cell cycle progression and inducing various forms of cell death. 21 Consequently, genes associated with cell cycle and proliferation were downregulated in the radiotherapy groups. Genes related to immunomodulation were upregulated in both RT groups, while some signaling pathway (NF-kappa B)-related genes showed upregulation and some signaling pathway (FoxO)-related genes showed downregulation (Figure 7b and c). The organism may develop resistance to radiotherapy due to apoptotic escape and cycle alterations. 22 , 23 Inhibition of the p53 pathway and enhanced expression of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) both lead to apoptosis evasion. 24 Notably, compared with CONV RT, FLASH RT up-regulated genes associated with the p53 signaling pathway and down-regulated genes associated with the NF-kappa B signaling pathway; apoptosis was inhibited, and radioresistance was reduced. Furthermore, at 7 d post-radiotherapy, genes associated with Th17 cell differentiation were downregulated in the FLASH RT group (Figure 7c), which was corroborated by the mIHC results (Figure 6f). This further suggests that FLASH RT and CONV RT may differ in their ability to activate Th17 cells.

Reactome functional enrichment analysis revealed that both radiotherapy groups exhibited downregulated expression of genes associated with the cell cycle. Compared with the Control group, the CONV RT group upregulated genes related to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), whereas the FLASH RT group upregulated genes associated with cytokines. Furthermore, compared with CONV RT, the FLASH RT group showed upregulation of cell cycle-related genes and downregulation of genes involved in TCR signaling and Ca2⁺ pathways, which is relevant to Th17 cell differentiation (Figure 7d and e).

Previous findings suggested potential differences in the regulation of Th17 cell differentiation between FLASH RT and CONV RT, so we further validated this using Western blotting. Mechanistically, due to its unique DNA damage response, FLASH RT can reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels by inducing oxygen exhaustion, thereby inhibiting the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, impairing NF-κB activation, and ultimately suppressing the inflammatory response. 25 The suppression of the inflammatory response further downregulates IL-6/STAT3 signaling. As a key cytokine for Th17 differentiation, the downregulation of IL-6 expression hinders the formation of the NFATC1-RORγt transcription complex, thereby inhibiting IL-17A transcription and Th17 cell differentiation. As pivotal orchestrators of the bone metastatic microenvironment, Th17 cells drive RANKL-dependent osteoclast genesis via IL-17A secretion and reshape the bone immune milieu, thereby promoting osteolysis and facilitating metastatic bone colonization. 26 Therefore, our findings suggest that FLASH RT and CONV RT may differentially affect Th17 cell differentiation, with FLASH RT associated with reduced RANKL‑mediated osteoclastogenesis, a potential contributor to its distinct efficacy in attenuating lung cancer bone metastasis. Meanwhile, FLASH RT can activate systemic immunity, promote CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration, and enhance CD4⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses, ultimately achieving effective elimination of tumor cells (Figure 8f). These observations regarding Th17 cells may serve as a valuable reference for further investigating the mechanistic differences between FLASH RT and CONV RT in suppressing lung cancer bone metastasis.

Discussion

Lung cancer is a class of malignant tumors with rapid development and poor prognosis, and 50%–70% of lung cancer patients are prone to bone metastasis, which affects mortality and quality of life. Radiotherapy has been an effective method for treating advanced tumors. 27 FLASH RT, as a novel radiotherapy modality, not only has anti-tumor effects but also has low toxicity to normal tissues. 28 The clinical application of FLASH RT has great potential and has the potential to revolutionize the treatment of cancer. However, to date, the radiobiological mechanism of FLASH RT and its effect on the body's immune environment are not clear. 29

In this study, we constructed a mice lung cancer BM model and then performed RT with X-ray, which is the first time that X-ray FLASH RT was used in a mouse lung cancer bone metastasis study. We focused on the therapeutic effects of X-ray FLASH RT and CONV RT on lung cancer BM. The two RT modalities were found to be equivalent in controlling tumor growth, which is consistent with what has been reported previously, 10 , 30 but FLASH RT was more able to attenuate osteolytic lesions caused by tumor BM. Previous studies have reported that mice may experience transient weight loss following either total-body or localized irradiation, which is associated with acute radiation toxicity. 31 In the present study, mice in the treatment groups exhibited a sharp weight drop on day 4 post-radiotherapy, followed by gradual recovery toward baseline levels. Notably, the magnitude of weight loss was less pronounced in the FLASH RT group compared to the CONV RT group, indicating reduced side effects with FLASH RT. The Control mice exhibited deviations in hematological parameters from normal physiological ranges due to tumor burden. Following radiotherapy, mice in the treatment groups showed gradual normalization of hematological indices, with the FLASH RT group demonstrating values closer to normal than the CONV RT group. Collectively, blood routine and blood biochemistry analyzes indicate that FLASH RT effectively attenuates radiation-induced systemic toxicity and exerts protective effects on hepatic and renal function compared with CONV RT. The results of multiple cytokine assays showed that the two RT groups had similar effects in changing the tumor microenvironment, inhibiting tumor growth and attenuating tumor BM. However, the specific mechanism of these chemokines in tumor BM needs further study.

Current researchers have proposed the oxygen depletion hypothesis, DNA damage, and immune-related mechanisms. 32 , 33 However, regarding the oxygen depletion hypothesis, recent studies have shown that low oxygen consumption during RT at ultra-high dose rates does not fully explain the FLASH effect seen in healthy tissues. 34‐36 DNA damage is affected by a variety of factors, but DNA damage and repair induced by different dose rates vary widely. 37 , 38 Therefore, the underlying mechanisms of FLASH effects have not been fully elucidated and additional experimental studies and clinical investigations are needed to confirm them. So far, studies on the effect of FLASH RT on tumor immune microenvironment infiltration are lacking. In this study, immune cell infiltration in splenocytes was analyzed by RNA‑seq using Cibersort, and verified by flow cytometry, mIHC, and enrichment analysis. X‑ray radiotherapy exerted antitumor effects by activating CD4⁺ T cells in both groups. Notably, FLASH RT suppressed NF‑κB‑dependent RORγT expression, and the inflammatory microenvironment required for Th17 differentiation via oxygen depletion and immunomodulatory effects triggered by its unique physical dose rate properties, ultimately inhibiting Th17 cell differentiation. This was in sharp contrast to the pro‑inflammatory and Th17‑promoting environment induced by CONV RT. Th17 cells and their signature cytokine IL-17A are pivotal orchestrators of the bone metastatic microenvironment. IL-17A drives RANKL-dependent osteoclastogenesis by upregulating RANKL and suppressing OPG in osteoblasts and mesenchymal stromal cells, thereby disrupting the RANKL/OPG equilibrium and accelerating bone resorption. 39 Concurrently, IL-17A sensitizes osteoclast precursors through enhanced RANK receptor expression and modulates autophagic survival pathways. Furthermore, the Th17 axis fosters an immunosuppressive bone niche by skewing the Th17/Treg balance and responding to TGF-β released during bone resorption, ultimately attenuating cytotoxic T-cell responses and establishing a permissive microenvironment for metastatic colonization. 40

Theoretically, RT has the effect of killing all tumor cells, but it may result in a diminished or ineffective effect because many cells are radiation resistant. Mechanisms by which the body develops radioresistance include cell cycle regulation and evasion of apoptosis, among others. 22 In addition, DNA-protein cross-links and double-strand breaks (DSBs) are the most lethal forms of ionizing radiation-induced DNA damage, which is repaired mainly by two mechanisms: non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR). 41 The G2 and M phases of the cell cycle are the more radiation-sensitive phases. Therefore, blocking cells in G2/M phase increases radiosensitivity. 42 FLASH RT group down-regulated genes associated with the G2 and M phases of the cells and increased radiosensitivity. Compared with CONV RT, FLASH RT was more capable of repairing DNA damage. In addition, p53 pathway of FLASH RT was activated, NF-κB expression was decreased, apoptosis was inhibited, and radioresistance was decreased.

Although the present study demonstrates that both FLASH RT and CONV RT can elicit CD4⁺ T-cell-dependent antitumor immune responses, whether the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of FLASH RT is strictly contingent upon CD4⁺ T cells remains to be directly validated. Future investigations should employ CD4⁺ T-cell-depleted mouse models followed by FLASH RT administration to ascertain the indispensable role of CD4⁺ T cells in FLASH RT-mediated antitumor effects. Furthermore, while our preliminary findings suggest that FLASH RT may suppress Th17 cell differentiation via the ROS-cGAS/STING/NF-κB signaling axis, thereby attenuating tumor bone metastasis, this proposed mechanism warrants further confirmation through genetic knockout/knockin models and pathway-specific pharmacological inhibitor studies.

In conclusion, we demonstrated for the first time that X-ray FLASH RT and CONV RT were equivalent in controlling tumor growth, but FLASH RT was more able to attenuate osteolytic lesions due to tumor bone metastases. This provides a potential treatment modality for patients with tumor BM. FLASH RT exerts antitumor effects accompanied by activation of CD4⁺ T-cell-mediated immune responses. Furthermore, our preliminary mechanistic findings suggest that the attenuated tumor bone metastasis observed with FLASH RT, relative to CONV RT, may be partially mediated through dampened activation of the cGAS-STING-NF-κB signaling axis, which could alleviate inflammatory responses and subsequently restrain Th17 cell differentiation. While these observations provide initial insights into the protective effects of FLASH RT, they remain exploratory and require further functional validation to establish definitive causal relationships.
