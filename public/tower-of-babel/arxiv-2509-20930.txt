Learners are Almost Free Compact Closed
Mitchell Riley

Abstract
The category of learners has a pleasant symmetric formulation when the morphisms are considered up to a coarser equivalence than the one originally described in the paper "Backprop as Functor". A quotient of this modified category gives a new construction of the free compact closed category on a symmetric monoidal category.



In this note I expand on an observation made at the end
of
[
16
]
and further discussed
online
[
17
]
. If the definition of a
learner
[
8
]
is tweaked slightly so that
learners are considered identical under a coarser equivalence relation
than before, a striking symmetry in the definition is revealed.

This new category comes quite close to being compact closed. There is
a strict symmetric monoidal involution and each object has a cup and
cap morphism connecting it to its “dual” under this involution.
However, the snake equations fail and so these are not true duals.
Quotienting the category to force the equations to hold results in a
compact closed category, and we end the note by showing that it is in
fact the
free
compact closed category on the starting symmetric
monoidal category.

Compact closed categories support a more powerful diagrammatic
calculus than bare symmetric monoidal categories, so this work may
make it easier to design and reason about learners compositionally.
The operation that dualises a learner does not appear to have been
previously noticed and there is much to investigate. As asked by David
Spivak, what is the dual of a neuron?

Extensional Learners

Learners are a categorical construction used in a compositional
approach to supervised learning and
backpropagation
[
8
,
3
]
. We first recall their definition.

Definition 1.1

(
[
8
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

For sets
A
and
B
, a
learner

A\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,B
is a “parameter” set
P

together with a triple of functions
I:P\times A\to B
and

U:P\times A\times B\to P
and
r:P\times A\times B\to A
,
considered up to isomorphism of parameter sets. That is, two learners

(P,I,U,r)
and
(P^{\prime},I^{\prime},U^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are identified whenever there
is a bijection
f:P\to P^{\prime}
with

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(f(p),a)

\displaystyle=I(p,a)

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(f(p),a,b)

\displaystyle=f(U(p,a,b))

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(f(p),a,b)

\displaystyle=r(p,a,b)

Learners expose their parameter sets
P
to the outside world (up to isomorphism), and two
learners with non-isomorphic parameter sets are considered different
even if they ‘behave the same’ on all input data
A
and
B
. Cribbing
some terminology from type theory, we will label these learners

intensional
: their identity depends on the specific choice of
parameter set even if it is not observable through their input-output
behaviour. (Unfortunately this clashes with the meaning of
intensional/extensional as used in

[
11
]
.)

The equivalence relation on learners has a clear generalisation to any
category with finite products, and in this generality:

Proposition 1.2

(
[
8
, Proposition 2.4]
)
.

Intensional learners in any category with finite products form a
symmetric monoidal category
\mathbf{IntLearn}_{\mathscr{C}}
. ∎

The data underlying a learner
A\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,B
with fixed parameter set
P

has an appealing symmetric description, via the chain of isomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(P\times A,B)\times\mathscr{C}(P\times A\times B,P)\times\mathscr{C}(P\times A\times B,A)

\displaystyle\cong{}

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(P\times A,B)\times\mathscr{C}(P\times A\times B,P\times A)

\displaystyle\cong{}

\displaystyle\int^{Q:\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}(P\times A,Q)\times\mathscr{C}(P\times A,B)\times\mathscr{C}(Q\times B,P\times A)

\displaystyle\cong{}

\displaystyle\int^{Q:\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}(P\times A,Q\times B)\times\mathscr{C}(Q\times B,P\times A)

The quotient up to isomorphism of
P
can then be expressed as an
additional coend over the core of
\mathscr{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{IntLearn}(A,B)=\int^{P:\Core(\mathscr{C})}\int^{Q:\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}(P\times A,Q\times B)\times\mathscr{C}(Q\times B,P\times A)

Three generalisations immediately suggest themselves:

•

replace the former coend with one over all of
\mathscr{C}
rather than
only the isomorphisms,

•

replace
\times
with an arbitrary symmetric monoidal product

\otimes
, and,

•

allow the repeat occurrences of
A
and
B
to be different
objects, as in the category of optics
[
2
,
16
]
.

We arrive at our generalised definition of learner.

Definition 1.3
.

For objects
A,A^{\prime},B,B^{\prime}
of a symmetric monoidal category
\mathscr{C}
, an

extensional learner

(A,A^{\prime})\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,(B,B^{\prime})
is an element of

\displaystyle\mathbf{Learn}_{\mathscr{C}}((A,A^{\prime}),(B,B^{\prime})):=\int^{P,Q:\mathscr{C}}\mathscr{C}(P\otimes A,Q\otimes B)\times\mathscr{C}(Q\otimes B^{\prime},P\otimes A^{\prime})

The extensional learner represented by a pair of maps

f:P\otimes A\to Q\otimes B
and

g:Q\otimes B^{\prime}\to P\otimes A^{\prime}
will be written

(f\mid g):(A,A^{\prime})\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,(B,B^{\prime})
.

Because these are our focus, we omit ‘extensional’ and call these
simply
learners
.

When specialised back to the case of sets and binary products, this
definition unwinds to a coarser notion of equivalence between a pair
of intensional learners. Two learners
(P,I,U,r)
and

(P^{\prime},I^{\prime},U^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are identified by this new definition when there is
a (not-necessarily-bijective) function
f:P\to P^{\prime}
and an
intermediate function
\hat{U}:P^{\prime}\times A\times B\to P
such that

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(f(p),a)

\displaystyle=I(p,a)

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(f(p),a,b)

\displaystyle=r(p,a,b)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(f(p),a,b)

\displaystyle=U(p,a,b)

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(p^{\prime},a,b)

\displaystyle=f(\hat{U}(p^{\prime},a,b))

That is, the relationship of
f
to
I
and
r
is the same as before,
but for
U
we require a diagonal filler in the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\times A\times B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{\prime}\times A\times B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f\times A\times B}

Lemma 1.4
.

Intensionally equivalent learners are extensionally equivalent.

Proof.

The bijection
f:P\to P^{\prime}
satisfies the first two equations of
extensional equivalence by assumption. For the intermediate function

\hat{U}:P^{\prime}\times A\times B\to P
, take

\hat{U}(p^{\prime},a,b):=U(f^{-1}(p^{\prime}),a,b)
, then

\displaystyle\hat{U}(f(p),a,b)

\displaystyle=U(f^{-1}(f(p)),a,b)=U(p,a,b)

\displaystyle f(\hat{U}(p^{\prime},a,b))

\displaystyle=f(U(f^{-1}(p^{\prime}),a,b))=U^{\prime}(f(f^{-1}(p^{\prime})),a,b)=U^{\prime}(p^{\prime},a,b)

∎

2
Duality

For extensional learners, there is an obvious involution

(-)^{*}:\mathbf{Learn}\to\mathbf{Learn}^{\mathsf{op}}
that comes into view by virtue of
the new symmetrical formulation: just switch the two components!

Proposition 2.1
.

There is a (strict) involutive symmetric monoidal functor

(-)^{*}:\mathbf{Learn}\to\mathbf{Learn}^{\mathsf{op}}
given by
(A,A^{\prime})^{*}:=(A^{\prime},A)

and
(L\mid R)^{*}:=(R\mid L)
.

Proof.

On objects, we have

\displaystyle\left((A,A^{\prime})\otimes(B,B^{\prime})\right)^{*}=(A\otimes B,B^{\prime}\otimes A^{\prime})^{*}=(B^{\prime}\otimes A^{\prime},A\otimes B)=(B^{\prime},B)\otimes(A^{\prime},A)=(B,B^{\prime})^{*}\otimes(A,A^{\prime})^{*}

Preservation of identity, composition and tensor of morphisms is
clear by observing that their definitions are exactly symmetric
between each component.
∎

The resulting strictness of this functor is why it is convenient to
switch the objects in the right component of

(A,A^{\prime})\otimes(B,B^{\prime}):\equiv(A\otimes B,B^{\prime}\otimes A^{\prime})
.
