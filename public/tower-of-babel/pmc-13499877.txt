Indirect Comparison of the Efficacy of Zanubrutinib vs Ibrutinib and Acalabrutinib in Treatment-Naive Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia: 6-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Mazyar Shadman, Talha Munir, Sheng Xu, Keri Yang, Leyla Mohseninejad, Grace Guthrie, Jamie Hirata, Marcus Lefebure, Rhys Williams, Constantine S. Tam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionZanubrutinib, acalabrutinib, and ibrutinib each demonstrated improved investigator-assessed progression-free survival (PFS-INV) and overall survival (OS) versus chemotherapy-based regimens in treatment-naive chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) in the phase 3 trials SEQUOIA (NCT03336333), ELEVATE-TN (NCT02475681), and RESONATE-2 (NCT01722487), respectively. In the absence of head-to-head studies, this analysis compared the efficacy of zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and vs acalabrutinib in treatment-naive CLL via indirect naive comparisons.MethodsEligibility criteria were aligned to improve cross-trial comparability. The zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib comparison included COVID-19-adjusted PFS-INV and OS in arm A of SEQUOIA (n = 241; median follow-up, 73.4 months) and the ibrutinib arm of RESONATE-2 (n = 136; median follow-up, 88.5 months). The zanubrutinib vs acalabrutinib comparison included PFS-INV and OS in subgroups of patients without del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations from arm A of SEQUOIA (n = 215; median follow-up, 73.6 months) and the acalabrutinib arm of ELEVATE-TN (n = 156; median follow-up, 74.5 months).ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar for each comparison. Zanubrutinib significantly prolonged PFS-INV (hazard ratio [HR], 0.65; 95% CI, 0.44–0.97; P = 0.0330) and showed a trend toward improved OS (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.36–1.01; P = 0.0532) vs ibrutinib. At the 72-month landmark, PFS-INV and OS rates were significantly higher with zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib, with a risk difference of 15.3% (95% CI, 8.3–22.2%) for PFS and 9.9% (95% CI, 3.0–16.7%) for OS. Compared with acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib trended toward improved PFS-INV (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.52–1.11; P = 0.1553) and OS (HR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.41–1.06; P = 0.0836). The 72-month landmark PFS-INV and OS rates were significantly higher with zanubrutinib vs acalabrutinib, with a risk difference of 9.9% (95% CI, 3.0–16.9%) for PFS-INV and 8.8% (95% CI, 2.0–15.5%) for OS.ConclusionThese findings inform the long-term comparative efficacy of Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors and suggest that zanubrutinib may confer sustained PFS and OS benefits compared with ibrutinib and acalabrutinib in similar treatment-naive CLL populations.Graphical abstract and video available for this article. What was learned from the study? The current analyses of clinical data based on extended follow-up in patients with TN CLL provided evidence suggesting a benefit in overall and progression-free survival with zanubrutinib vs other covalent inhibitors, which increased over time.Findings from this indirect treatment comparison provided exploratory insights into the comparative efficacy of covalent BTK inhibitors in TN CLL that, when interpreted in the context of a naive comparison, can help inform clinical decision-making to improve long-term patient management.

Introduction

Over the last decade, the clinical treatment landscape for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) has rapidly evolved from use of chemotherapy, such as chlorambucil, to chemoimmunotherapeutic approaches that combine cytotoxic agents with immune modulators, to the current paradigm that is increasingly dominated by novel treatments that target critical CLL pathways [1].

Therapies developed to inhibit the B-cell lymphoma 2 protein further transformed the management of CLL beyond chemoimmunotherapy, and the introduction of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors provided additional benefits in extending both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with treatment-naive (TN) and relapsed/refractory (R/R) CLL [1, 2].

The first-generation BTK inhibitor, ibrutinib, at 420 mg once-daily, demonstrated improved PFS and OS over chlorambucil in TN patients with CLL without del(17p) aberrations over 10 years of follow-up in the phase 3 RESONATE-2 study [3]; however, off-target effects of ibrutinib are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular adverse events (AEs), including hypertension, hemorrhage, and atrial fibrillation [4–6]. The second-generation BTK inhibitor, acalabrutinib, at 100 mg twice daily, also demonstrated improved PFS in TN patients with CLL after 6 years of follow-up in the phase 3 ELEVATE-TN study of acalabrutinib plus obinutuzumab (arm A) vs acalabrutinib monotherapy (arm B) vs chlorambucil plus obinutuzumab (arm C) [7].

Zanubrutinib, a next-generation BTK inhibitor, was designed to be highly potent and selective, providing complete and sustained BTK occupancy with fewer off-target AEs than other BTK inhibitors [8, 9]. In biochemical assays evaluating BTK inhibition, the potency (IC50) of zanubrutinib (0.5 nM) is three-fold higher than that of ibrutinib (1.5 nM) and 10-fold higher than that of acalabrutinib (5.1 nM) [9]. In the phase 3 SEQUOIA study in TN patients with CLL/SLL without del(17p), zanubrutinib monotherapy (arm A) at 160 mg twice daily showed a statistically significant improvement in PFS and higher OS rates compared with bendamustine plus rituximab after 6 years (72 months) of follow-up [10].

The introduction of BTK inhibitors has fundamentally transformed the clinical management of CLL [11, 12]; however, the lack of head-to-head trials with new treatment modalities and the rapid emergence of new clinical data have paradoxically posed challenges for clinicians striving to make informed, evidence-based decisions to optimize long-term outcomes in TN patients with CLL. In R/R CLL, zanubrutinib demonstrated PFS superiority vs ibrutinib in the ALPINE study, and acalabrutinib demonstrated noninferior PFS vs ibrutinib in the ELEVATE-RR study; however, no direct comparisons between covalent BTK inhibitors have been conducted in patients with TN CLL [13, 14]. Furthermore, heterogeneity in patient demographic and clinical characteristics, such as age, sex, and baseline mutation status, influence patient outcomes and may pose an additional challenge to the generalizability of individual trial results [15]. Given the lack of direct comparative evidence, clinicians increasingly rely on data from indirect treatment comparison (ITC) analyses to inform therapeutic decision-making. While population-adjusted indirect comparisons (PAICs) are preferred in most scenarios, they may lose validity during extended follow-up (e.g., ≥ 5 years) in trials in which patient numbers can markedly decline. With broadly similar patient populations across each study comparison, an unadjusted naive ITC approach can be a viable and appropriate alternative to adjusted approaches when the latter methods would not be feasible because of extended follow‑up leading to substantial reductions in evaluable patients [16].

To that end, the current exploratory analysis evaluated the longer-term efficacy of zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and acalabrutinib in TN patients with CLL from arm A of SEQUOIA, the ibrutinib arm of RESONATE-2, and the acalabrutinib monotherapy arm of ELEVATE-TN, respectively, using extended follow-up via a naive ITC approach.

Methods

Investigator-assessed PFS (PFS-INV) and OS outcomes in zanubrutinib-treated patients in SEQUOIA (NCT03336333) [10] were evaluated vs ibrutinib-treated patients in RESONATE-2 (NCT01722487) [3] and acalabrutinib-treated patients in ELEVATE-TN (NCT02475681) [7]. The study designs and methodology for these studies have been previously reported [3, 7, 17].

PAIC approaches, such as a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) and a simulated treatment comparison (STC), are preferred methods relative to a naive ITC when comparing data from different trials [18]; these methods require sufficient covariate representation and/or stable sample sizes over time to generate unbiased adjusted estimates. In CLL trials with extended follow-up of > 5 years, the number of patients at risk may be too low, limiting the accuracy of and confidence in adjusted ITC approaches [16, 19]. For this study, a naive comparison approach was used because of potential instability of other structured and adjusted analytic approaches, given the diminished sample size with extended follow-up [20]. A previously published ITC comparing zanubrutinib (SEQUOIA) vs acalabrutinib plus venetoclax (AMPLIFY) in patients with TN CLL [21] provided conditional support for the effectiveness of a naive comparison, given sufficient similarity between the clinical populations. In those analyses, when evaluating clinically similar patient populations from each trial, the results of the naive comparison were robustly similar to those of the supportive analyses, which included anchored and unanchored MAIC approaches, each supported by multiple sensitivity analyses [21].

Differences in trial design and eligibility criteria were considered when assessing cross-trial comparability. The zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib comparison included zanubrutinib-treated patients from arm A of SEQUOIA (n = 241), with a median follow-up of 73.4 months [10], and ibrutinib-treated patients from RESONATE-2 (n = 136), with a median follow-up of 82.7 months [3]. The proportions of patients with TP53 mutations in SEQUOIA arm A and RESONATE-2 were comparable (Table 1); therefore, no restrictions based on TP53 mutation status were required for the comparison of zanubrutinib and ibrutinib. The ELEVATE-TN trial reported outcomes in a subgroup of patients without del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations. Patients with del(17p) were excluded from arm A of SEQUOIA. As TP53 mutation is a strong adverse prognostic marker in CLL, and to align with the ELEVATE-TN population, patients with TP53 mutation in the zanubrutinib arm in SEQUOIA were also excluded. This resulted in 215 patients without del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations from arm A of SEQUOIA (median follow-up, 73.6 months [10]) and 156 acalabrutinib-treated patients without del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations from ELEVATE-TN (median follow-up, 74.5 months [7]).

For both indirect comparisons, individual patient data (IPD) from SEQUOIA were used. Pseudo-IPD for the ibrutinib and acalabrutinib comparisons were reconstructed from digitized PFS-INV and OS Kaplan-Meier curves from the respective published studies [3, 7, 22]. Visual inspection of the reconstructed pseudo-IPD was performed to validate the accuracy of the digitization of the original Kaplan-Meier curves. Additionally, quantitative validation was conducted for the reconstructed comparator datasets, including source-file traceability, reconstructed sample size/event/censoring summaries, alignment with published or recorded numbers at risk, and consistency of reconstructed KM landmark estimates with digitized published KM curves. Across datasets with available numbers-at-risk information, the mean absolute discrepancy was 1.7 patients across the assessed time points; larger discrepancies were concentrated in the late follow-up tail regions. Across datasets with digitized KM coordinates, reconstructed landmark estimates showed close agreement with the digitized curves, with a mean absolute difference of 0.49%.

Statistical Analysis

PFS-INV and OS outcomes were compared between treatment groups using the Kaplan-Meier method with hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% CIs. P-values were calculated using a Cox proportional hazards regression model; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Patients who discontinued treatment without documented progression or death were censored at their last adequate disease assessment, consistent with standard PFS definitions. Testing for the proportional hazard assumption was conducted by examining Schoenfeld residuals and performing a formal test for correlations between scaled Schoenfeld residuals and time; these tests did not indicate any evidence of violation of this assumption. Landmark PFS-INV and OS rates in each treatment group and risk differences between groups were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The 95% CIs for risk differences were calculated using a Wald-type statistic; statistical significance was inferred when 95% CIs did not include the null value.

As immunoglobulin heavy chain variable (IGHV) mutation status is a critical prognostic biomarker and predictive factor [23], PFS-INV and OS outcomes with zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and vs acalabrutinib were compared with those in subgroups of patients with and without IGHV mutations.

Ethics committee approval was not required for these post hoc analyses. The SEQUOIA, RESONATE-2, and ELEVATE-TN trials were approved by the relevant ethics committees and adhered to the ethical principles of Good Clinical Practice, the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines, and the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients provided written informed consent to participate in each study [3, 7, 17].

Results

Zanubrutinib Versus Ibrutinib

Baseline demographic and clinically important disease characteristics were well balanced between zanubrutinib and ibrutinib in the SEQUOIA and RESONATE-2 trials, respectively (Table 1). Clinically relevant genetic parameters, including IGHV mutation status (46.6% vs 43%), TP53 mutations (6.5% vs 9%), and del(17p) (0.8% vs 0%) and del(11q) (17.8% vs 22%) abnormalities, were similar between the SEQUOIA and RESONATE-2 groups.

Zanubrutinib significantly prolonged PFS-INV compared with ibrutinib (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.44–0.97; P =0.0330) (Fig. 1A). Patients treated with zanubrutinib trended toward improved PFS-INV compared with acalabrutinib-treated patients (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.52–1.11; P = 0.1553) (Fig. 1C). OS showed a similar trend in favor of zanubrutinib over acalabrutinib but did not reach significance (HR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.41–1.06; P = 0.0836) (Fig. 1D).

Estimated landmark rates for PFS and OS were comparable between zanubrutinib and acalabrutinib treatment groups at earlier time points but favored zanubrutinib with longer follow-up (Fig. 2C; Supplemental Table S2). At the 72-month landmark, the PFS-INV rate was 74% in zanubrutinib-treated patients (95% CI, 67.1–79.7%), which was significantly higher than in acalabrutinib-treated patients (64%), with a 72-month PFS risk difference of 9.9% (95% CI, 3.0–16.9%) (Fig. 2C). The OS landmark rate at 72 months was also significantly higher with zanubrutinib (85.0%; 95% CI, 79.3–89.2%) compared with acalabrutinib (76%), with a 72-month OS risk difference of 8.8% (95% CI, 2.0–15.5%) (Fig. 2D).

PFS and OS Outcomes by IGHV Mutation Status

In patients with unmutated IGHV, zanubrutinib showed prolonged PFS and OS compared with both ibrutinib and acalabrutinib, with separation in the Kaplan-Meier curves expanding over time (Supplemental Figure S1). Landmark analyses evaluating clinically meaningful time points demonstrated increasingly greater differences in PFS and OS with continued follow-up (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables S3 and S4). At the 72-month landmark, zanubrutinib trended toward improvement in PFS vs ibrutinib, with a risk difference of 7.7% (95% CI, −2.4–17.9%). Compared with acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib showed significant improvement in PFS-INV at 72 months, with a risk difference of 11.4% (95% CI, 2.7–20.0%). The magnitude of improvement was larger with zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and acalabrutinib in patients with mutated IGHV, with significant differences in both PFS-INV and OS rates (Fig. 3; Supplemental Figure S2; Supplemental Tables S3 and S4). The 72-month risk difference for PFS was 19.4% (95% CI, 6.8–31.9%) for zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and 14.8% (95% CI, 4.2–25.3%) for zanubrutinib vs acalabrutinib. Zanubrutinib was also associated with significantly higher OS rates than ibrutinib (risk difference, 11.7%; 95% CI, 0.1–23.4%) and acalabrutinib (risk difference, 17.6%; 95% CI, 6.6–28.7%) at the 72-month landmark.

Discussion

The CLL clinical landscape has rapidly evolved in the last 15 years. Targeted therapies have largely replaced chemoimmunotherapy, with meaningful advances in patient outcomes with the introduction of each new treatment modality (Supplemental Figure S3). However, comparative data on the efficacy of novel therapies are necessary to inform treatment decisions in clinical practice [1]. In the absence of direct head-to-head evidence, indirect comparisons have become increasingly important in providing the comparative evidence used by clinicians, regulators, and reimbursement agencies [24]. Given the shared mechanism of action of covalent BTK inhibitors, we conducted naive indirect comparisons to evaluate the long-term efficacy of the next-generation zanubrutinib compared with the first- and second-generation BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib and acalabrutinib, respectively).

In this exploratory analysis, zanubrutinib was associated with significantly prolonged PFS and a trend toward significantly improved OS compared with ibrutinib in TN patients with CLL without del(17p). Zanubrutinib was also associated with numerically greater improvements in PFS and OS compared with acalabrutinib in TN patients with CLL without del(17p) and/or TP53 mutations. However, time-averaged analyses (e.g., HR estimates derived from Kaplan-Meier curves) may lack sensitivity and obscure clinically important differences between therapies, particularly in CLL trials, where the absence of early events can dilute effect sizes and treatment effects often become more pronounced later in follow-up [25].

In contrast, landmark-based assessments may provide additional insights when estimating late-emerging treatment differences when comparative therapies may show delayed separation, offering complementary data that can improve the evaluation of long-term patient outcomes [25–27]. In the current naive ITC analysis, treatment differences between zanubrutinib and ibrutinib, and between acalabrutinib and ibrutinib, generally became evident at approximately 3 years of follow-up and were sustained thereafter. These results suggested progressively durable disease control among long-term event-free patients treated with zanubrutinib that seemed to be more pronounced over time (Fig. 2). At the 72-month landmark analysis, zanubrutinib was associated with statistically significant differences in OS and PFS. Any inferences must be interpreted with caution as they reflect condition comparisons at prespecified time points, and this study was not specifically designed to evaluate temporal patterns of treatment effects.

However, progressively durable disease control with zanubrutinib is biologically consistent with its pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) profile. Clinical PK/PD studies demonstrate that, unlike ibrutinib or acalabrutinib, twice-daily zanubrutinib dosing maintains near-complete BTK occupancy (≥ 95%) across the 24-h dosing interval [9], which is associated with sustained suppression of B-cell receptor signaling and durable disease control [28, 29].

Given the clinical relevance of IGHV mutation status, a subgroup analysis was conducted. Outcomes by IGHV mutation subgroups were generally consistent with the overall findings, suggesting that landmark PFS-INV and OS differences favored zanubrutinib compared with both ibrutinib and acalabrutinib, with these differences appearing to increase over time. These results were based on smaller patient samples and may be more vulnerable to confounders and should be considered in this light.

Confidence in the results and inferences of any single ITC analysis is strongest when consilient with other independent studies that use other analytic approaches and data sources. In patients with TN CLL, the results of an MAIC by Kittai et al. that compared acalabrutinib from the ELEVATE-TN trial and zanubrutinib from SEQUOIA showed no significant difference between the 36-month PFS rate with acalabrutinib (86%; 95% CI, 78–91%) vs zanubrutinib (84%; 95% CI, 79–88%) [30]. Although the populations compared in that MAIC analysis slightly differ from the current study, the findings closely align with the 36-month landmark PFS rates observed in the current analysis (zanubrutinib, 86%; acalabrutinib, 83%) (Fig. 2). This supports the robustness of the current results that analyzed outcomes using extended patient follow-up. Importantly, the authors noted that MAIC-based matching had little impact on their analysis results [30]. Matching in the MAIC resulted in only a modest reduction (24%) in effective sample size at the 36-month follow-up, reflecting the overall similarity in baseline patient characteristics between these analyses. The current results also align with an ITC analysis that utilized multiple analytic strategies to compare treatment with zanubrutinib vs acalabrutinib plus venetoclax in patients with TN CLL using data from SEQUOIA and AMPLIFY, respectively. In that study, naive, anchored MAIC, and unanchored MAIC approaches showed consistent results when evaluating clinically similar patients [21].

The overall results of the current ITC analysis also align well with other published literature, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and real-world evidence studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis that synthesized data from both single-arm and comparator clinical trials and real-world studies suggested the benefits of zanubrutinib vs other covalent BTK inhibitors in both overall response rate and PFS [31]. Other meta-analyses that included studies in patients with TN CLL similarly reported significant improvements in various efficacy outcomes (e.g., overall response rate, complete response, PFS) with zanubrutinib vs ibrutinib and acalabrutinib monotherapies [32, 33]. While each of these analytical approaches provides additional evidence about the comparative efficacy or effectiveness of the different BTK inhibitors, they must be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity in CLL patient populations and treatment settings across studies, which limits direct comparability between treatments.

In the context of the limitations associated with cross-trial comparisons, the current results suggest that zanubrutinib vs other covalent BTK inhibitors offered improvements in both OS and reduced risk of disease progression, and these benefits continually increased during the 6-year extended follow-up. These notable results are consistent and should be interpreted in the context of the transformational changes that targeted therapies, particularly BTK inhibitors, have brought to patients with TN CLL, improving life expectancy to approach that of the general population [34, 35]. These revolutionary improvements in patient outcomes will require a concomitant systematic holistic evolution in the approach to managing patients with CLL. While CLL is already conceptualized as a disease of older patients, improved survival will continue to increase the median age and distribution of patients with CLL [34]; therefore, clinicians will need to adapt to provide greater support tailored to an increasingly older patient population [34]. As modern CLL management increasingly yields prolonged survival, consideration of the persistent male predominance (approximately 60–65% men vs 35–40% women), differences in baseline life expectancy, and associated sex-based variation in disease biology and treatment tolerance will be essential in optimizing personalized CLL care [36]. Potential challenges include managing patients with CLL who have higher risks of various comorbidities, including cardiovascular and renal disease, increased risk of infections, and central nervous system disorders that are commonly associated with aging [34]. These factors will need to be integrated into patient management paradigms to navigate not only the impact of comorbidities themselves but also the complexities of polypharmacy to reduce the risk of adverse drug-drug interactions and pill burden, which can decrease treatment adherence [34]. There are limited data regarding the impact of simplifying treatment regimens in CLL, but a single-site study of once vs twice-daily zanubrutinib suggested it may improve treatment adherence, reduce administration burdens, and decrease polypharmacy complexities [37].

As advances in the CLL clinical landscape have included new approaches to fixed-duration treatment regimens using targeted therapies (e.g., BCL2 inhibitors), the current results should be interpreted in the context of differing treatment paradigms. Continuous treatment regimens can provide sustained pathway inhibition and durable disease control [12, 38]. Conversely, fixed-duration regimens allow for treatment cessation and may reduce long-term treatment burden but rely on achieving sufficient early depth of response to maintain disease control following therapy discontinuation [38]. While the present study focused on comparing BTK inhibitors within a continuous duration treatment framework, these insights may help contextualize the relative contributions of different BTK inhibitors when used in fixed-duration strategies.

Importantly for clinical trial evaluation and regulatory assessment, PFS is increasingly valuable as a clinical outcome in its own right, beyond its role as a surrogate for OS. Disease progression itself is becoming a clinically meaningful endpoint, strongly associated with patient impairment and disability, reduced quality of life, and increased disease-related anxiety [39, 40]. The benefits of prolonging PFS and reducing the negative impacts associated with disease progression may be understated by landmark assessments, which may not fully capture the cumulative negative impact of disease progression over time, as recently recognized in other chronic disorders [41].

With the goal of prolonged disease control and the considerable improvement in patient outcomes provided by targeted therapies, understanding clinical differences in long-term benefits between targeted treatments is becoming an increasingly important part of clinical decision-making. The current findings suggest that in TN patients with CLL, zanubrutinib may provide long-term benefits compared with ibrutinib and acalabrutinib. Future independent research is warranted to confirm and advance the results of these analyses.

These analyses were based on a naive indirect comparison rather than an adjusted approach, as PAICs can be unstable and less accurate when the number of patients at risk declines markedly, which is characteristic of extended follow-up in CLL trials [42]. In these conditions, naive indirect comparisons that analyze clinically similar patient populations can be more methodologically defensible and provide greater confidence when interpreting ITC study results.

This study was exploratory, and any inferences should be considered in the context of limitations commonly associated with naive ITC approaches, including lack of randomization across comparisons, cross-trial differences based on trial design and differences in patient characteristics, concomitant therapies, and the time periods when these trials were conducted [43]. Although population restriction was used to enhance comparability between trials, differences in baseline characteristics may still limit the interpretation of these results. Differences in the collection and reporting of baseline patient characteristics and demographic data across trials, including race, ethnicity, gender, and other key clinical variables, may have introduced heterogeneity and limited cross-trial comparability. The lack of consistent reporting of demographic data across trials may limit the generalizability of these findings to more diverse patient populations [15]. Differences in radiologic assessment intervals required for confirmation of progression may have varied across trials. As PFS outcomes are dependent on the schedule of tumor evaluations, differences in imaging intervals may introduce bias in PFS estimations, where less frequent assessments, as observed in RESONATE-2; this may delay the detection of progression events and artificially prolong PFS compared with the more frequent assessments seen in ELEVATE-TN and SEQUOIA. Additionally, because IPD was not publicly available for ELEVATE-TN and RESONATE-2, pseudo-IPD for acalabrutinib and ibrutinib was reconstructed from digitized Kaplan-Meier curves. This process can be susceptible to digitization errors, censoring assumptions, and differences in reporting or measurement of survival outcomes across studies [22].

Naive indirect comparisons, when interpreted in the context of methodologic limitations, offer informative clinical insights that complement alternative analytic approaches. Cross-trial variability limits inferences from indirect comparison of treatments among trials; however, the patient populations of SEQUOIA, ELEVATE-TN, and RESONATE-2 in the current analysis had comparable baseline characteristics after applying eligibility restriction. Moreover, although transitivity cannot be tested in unstructured comparisons, there were no major differences in outcome definitions or assessment methods between trials, follow-up durations were comparable, and all treatments assessed shared a common mechanism of covalent BTK inhibition, supporting the robustness of the indirect comparison. Given the potential for loss of effective sample size with weighting-based adjustment methods and the challenges of fully capturing all prognostic and predictive factors when IPDs are unavailable, naive indirect comparisons with eligibility restrictions to harmonize populations may offer informative insights into comparative efficacy.

While these results provide important new insights that may help clinicians better understand potential BTK inhibitor treatment options, future studies comparing time to next treatment, incidence of Richter transformation, and safety profiles would further enhance clinical decision-making. Data on time to next treatment and Richter transformation were not consistently reported across the studies and subgroups included in this analysis and therefore could not be reliably evaluated. Additionally, the comparison of safety profiles was not included in this study. While naive ITC approaches in certain scenarios can be clinically informative in evaluating safety outcomes, this analysis was focused on hypothesis-generating insights into the efficacy of long-term continuous BTK inhibitor treatment in patients with TN CLL; comparative assessments of safety are warranted for future research [44, 45].

Conclusion

This analysis supports the growing benefits of zanubrutinib in OS and PFS over time, potentially differentiating it from other covalent BTK inhibitors in the frontline CLL setting. Differences in OS and PFS rates increased with extended follow-up, suggesting a sustained benefit with zanubrutinib compared with ibrutinib and acalabrutinib. In the absence of direct comparative evidence, this analysis provides a transparent and complementary perspective on the long-term comparative efficacy of BTK inhibitors in TN CLL that, when interpreted in the context of cross-trial variation, can help inform clinical decision-making to improve long-term CLL management.
