Factors influencing high expectations in patients undergoing corneal transplantation
Authors: Ayixianmuguli Wufuer, Xiaodi Liu, Jiamei Ma, Suling Tan, Qi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study conducted a single-center cross-sectional analysis of 120 patients undergoing corneal transplantation to explore preoperative visual function expectations and contributing factors. Utilizing self-designed questionnaires and validated scales, significant item score variations were observed, highlighting higher expectations for independent daily activities and near-vision tasks. Multivariate analysis identified educational level, ophthalmic history, and neuroticism as independent influencers. A binary logistic regression model exhibited strong discrimination (AUC 0.858) and good calibration, validated through internal methods. Decision curve analysis favored the model, and ROC analysis pinpointed 0.293 as the optimal threshold for identifying high expectations. In the outpatient setting, thresholds of 0.20–0.30 are recommended for screening and trigger purposes, while 0.40 is suggested for referrals and resource allocation. Overall, patients displayed varied preoperative expectations, with educational level, prior ophthalmic history, and neuroticism notably impacting these expectations. The predictive model, in conjunction with decision curve and ROC-based optimization, provides actionable thresholds for enhancing preoperative communication and management. Multicenter validation and longitudinal studies are advised for further model refinement and evaluating long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44105-3.

Introduction

Corneal transplantation, as a critical intervention for various blinding corneal disorders, has been widely applied in the treatment of advanced stages of conditions such as corneal dystrophy, corneal opacity, and endothelial decompensation1. With continuous advancements in surgical techniques, donor cornea quality, and perioperative management, the overall postoperative recovery of visual function has improved substantially2. However, clinical practice has revealed a frequent mismatch between patients’ subjective expectations regarding postoperative visual outcomes and the actual therapeutic results. This discrepancy may lead to reduced postoperative satisfaction, strained physician-patient communication, and even negative emotional responses3–5. Particularly in the preoperative phase, some patients—due to limited understanding of the procedure or idealized perceptions of corneal transplantation—tend to develop elevated or even unrealistic visual function expectations5. If such individuals are not effectively identified and addressed before surgery, it may negatively impact their postoperative rehabilitation behavior, treatment adherence, and overall subjective experience6. Therefore, accurately assessing and managing preoperative expectations has become an increasingly critical aspect of perioperative care in corneal transplantation.

Preoperative visual function expectations represent patients’ subjective predictions regarding visual improvement after surgery, shaped by their disease experience, cognitive evaluation, and psychological state. These expectations exhibit substantial individual variability7. Evidence suggests that visual expectations are influenced not only by disease severity and physician counseling but also by sociodemographic characteristics, previous ophthalmic history, educational level, and psychological traits5. Among personality traits, the neuroticism dimension has shown the most pronounced moderating effect on visual function expectations; individuals with higher levels of neuroticism tend to exhibit heightened worry and idealized anticipation regarding future outcomes8,9. At the same time, those with a higher educational level are more inclined to actively seek medical information and form stronger expectations about surgical outcomes4. Although some studies have preliminarily explored these influencing factors, most remain at the descriptive level. There is still a lack of systematic predictive tools, and no quantitative model has been developed to facilitate early clinical identification of patients with “high expectations”.

In recent years, predictive modeling techniques have been widely adopted in clinical risk assessment and decision-making processes10. Through multivariate regression analysis, internal cross-validation, and performance evaluation methods such as the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and Brier score, it is possible to construct interpretable models with strong generalizability11,12. Furthermore, the incorporation of decision curve analysis (DCA) enables evaluation of a model’s net clinical benefit across a range of probability thresholds, thereby assessing its practical value in real-world settings13. On this basis, optimization of decision thresholds using ROC curves and the Youden index can provide scenario-specific triggers—such as for preoperative education, risk screening, or individualized follow-up—enhancing both the clinical applicability of the predictive tool and the efficiency of resource allocation14,15. Although these methods have been extensively applied in oncology and chronic disease management, research remains scarce in the context of preoperative expectation management for corneal transplantation, particularly regarding models integrating psychological traits and ophthalmic history.

This study aimed to determine the overall level and item-level distribution of preoperative visual function expectations in patients scheduled for corneal transplantation, to identify key influencing factors, and to detect individuals with elevated expectation levels. It further sought to propose a clinically applicable predictive model and corresponding decision thresholds for identification and management purposes. For the first time, the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) was incorporated into preoperative expectation assessment for corneal transplantation, integrating both psychological and ophthalmic history data to provide an evidence-based foundation for patient education and risk communication. The findings are expected to offer a theoretical framework for developing patient-centered, individualized perioperative management strategies and to support the creation of predictive screening tools aimed at improving postoperative satisfaction and intervention effectiveness. Additionally, the multi-threshold strategy proposed in this study allows for flexible trade-offs between screening sensitivity and resource utilization across different clinical goals, highlighting both its scientific value and practical significance.

Materials and methods

Participants and ethics

This study was a single-center, observational, cross-sectional investigation. Adult patients scheduled to undergo corneal transplantation in the Department of Ophthalmology at Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital between September 2023 and August 2024, and who met the inclusion criteria, were consecutively enrolled. Sample size was calculated using PASS 27.0 software for a binary outcome regression model. Parameters were set as α = 0.05 (two-sided) and statistical power (1–β) = 0.80. Following the principle of having at least 10 outcome events per predictor variable, and considering three key independent variables—educational level, ophthalmic history, and Eysenck Personality Questionnaire-Neuroticism (EPQ-N)—the minimum required number of events was ≥ 30. Assuming an event rate of 25%, the total required sample size was n ≥ 30 /0.25 = 120. Additionally, the Kendall empirical rule (12 scale items × 10 cases per item) also supported a minimum sample size of n ≥ 120. Taking into account both methods, along with the study’s cross-sectional design, on-site questionnaire collection with real-time verification, and the anticipated low rate of missing data or dropouts, the final sample size was set at 120 participants.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) undergoing corneal transplantation for the first time; (2) aged ≥ 18 years; (3) completion of at least primary education (educational level); (4) full consciousness and normal communication ability; and (5) provision of written informed consent voluntarily. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of severe organic diseases involving the heart, lungs, or kidneys; (2) undergoing other organ transplantation simultaneously; (3) a history of psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment; and (4) refusal to participate in the study (Fig. S1).

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Ethics Approval No.: KY2023080139).

Data sources and variable definitions

Participants completed a self-administered general information questionnaire, which was independently designed and included 10 demographic and disease-related variables, such as age, gender, educational level, occupation, monthly income, and prior ophthalmic history (Table S1). A preliminary pilot test yielded a content validity index of 0.92 for the questionnaire.

Preoperative expectations regarding postoperative functional outcomes were assessed using a three-level scale—“slight improvement,” “moderate improvement,” and “substantial improvement”—and subsequently converted into numerical scores (Table S2). The visual function expectations scale covered 12 functional items, categorized into three domains: (1) Independent daily activities: identifying signs, recognizing faces, navigating steps/curbs, and cooking; (2) Near-vision tasks: reading small/large print, reading books/newspapers, completing forms/signing, and performing fine work; (3) Recreational and physical activities: watching television, engaging in leisure activities, and participating in sports. Each item recorded both an expectation score (used to calculate median and interquartile range [IQR]) and an expected level of improvement (frequencies and percentages for each of the three levels).

Psychological traits were assessed using the Chinese short form of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire-Revised (EPQ-RSC), which includes four subscales: psychoticism (P), extraversion-introversion (E), neuroticism (N), and the lie scale (L), with a total of 48 items16,17 (Table S3). For this study, only the P, E, and N subscales were retained for personality trait analysis; the L scale, used as a validity index, was excluded from statistical analyses. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the EPQ-RSC in this study was 0.785.

Actual visual function was assessed five weeks postoperatively using the Chinese version of the Visual Function Index-12 (VF-12-CN), a culturally adapted revision of the original VF-14. This assessment served a supplementary purpose: to evaluate the correlation between preoperative expectations and actual early postoperative functional outcomes (the “expectation-reality gap”). Consequently, these postoperative data were used solely for validation analysis and were not included as primary outcome measures in the construction of the preoperative predictive model. This scale comprises 12 items covering both near and distance vision, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no difficulty, 5 = unable to perform). Total scores range from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater visual impairment18,19. In this study, the VF-12-CN demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.863 and a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.821.

Data collection procedures

Trained research personnel administered the questionnaires after patients had completed routine preoperative evaluations and received standard education. The study’s objectives, procedures, and privacy protections were explained, and informed consent was obtained before questionnaire distribution. Participants with adequate literacy completed the questionnaire independently, while those with limited educational level or impaired vision were assisted through one-on-one interviews conducted by the researchers. All questionnaires were collected on-site and immediately reviewed for completeness; any missing items were promptly addressed and filled in. To facilitate the secondary correlation analysis, a brief telephone follow-up was conducted at five weeks postoperatively. Participants were sent an electronic link to the VF-12-CN questionnaire and reminded to complete it independently in a quiet environment (Fig. S2).

Statistical methods

General principles

All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at α = 0.05. A threshold of p < 0.10 was used for candidate variables entering multivariate models. Prior to analysis, data consistency and missingness were examined. A complete-case approach was primarily adopted; when the missing rate for key independent variables exceeded 5%, multiple imputation was conducted for robustness testing.

Descriptive analysis and item-level differences

Continuous variables were presented as IQR or mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were reported as n (%). Differences in expectations across the 12 items within each participant were assessed using the Friedman test.

Analysis of influencing factors

Preoperative total expectation score was treated as a continuous outcome. Univariate linear regression was first applied to screen potential predictors (educational level, occupation, monthly income, ophthalmic history, and EPQ-N), using p < 0.10 as the inclusion threshold. All candidate variables were then simultaneously entered into a multivariate linear regression model. Regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), p-values, and model explanatory power (R²) were reported. Independent variables were stratified or dummy-coded as needed. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, and residuals were examined for normality and homoscedasticity.

Predictive modeling and internal validation

Participants with preoperative expectation scores ≥ Q3 (51.225) were defined as having “high expectations.” A logistic regression model was constructed using educational level, ophthalmic history, and EPQ-N as predictors. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination (area under the curve, AUC) and calibration (Brier score, Hosmer-Lemeshow decile test, calibration plot, and calibration slope). Internal validation was conducted using the bootstrap method (primary analysis: B = 200; for publication, B ≥ 1000 is recommended), and optimism-corrected AUC values were reported.

Clinical utility (DCA)

Net benefit was calculated to assess clinical utility and compared against the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a threshold range of pt = 0.05–0.75. Key net benefit (NB) values were reported for pt = 0.20, 0.30, and 0.40. Clinical impact curves (CIC) and net reduction in unnecessary interventions (NRNI) were provided to quantify the efficiency of resource allocation.

ROC-based threshold optimization and diagnostic trade-offs

The optimal decision threshold was identified using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive/negative likelihood ratios (LR±), false-positive rate (FP/n), and false-negative rate (FN/n) were calculated and reported at thresholds of pt = 0.20, 0.293, 0.30, and 0.40 to support scenario-specific threshold recommendations.

Results

General patient characteristics

A total of 120 consecutive patients scheduled for corneal transplantation at the Department of Ophthalmology, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region People’s Hospital between September 2023 and August 2024 were enrolled in this study (Table 1). Among them, 68 were male (56.7%) and 52 were female (43.3%). The median age was 54 years (IQR: 40–68; range: 26–78), indicating a predominantly middle-aged and elderly population. Educational level was distributed as follows: primary school in 24 patients (20.0%), junior high school in 36 (30.0%), senior high school/technical secondary school in 30 (25.0%), and college degree or above in 30 (25.0%). Occupational background included non-manual/service work in 54 patients (45.0%), manual labor in 42 (35.0%), and retired or unemployed status in 24 (20.0%). Monthly household income was reported as < 3,000 CNY in 30 patients (25.0%), 3,000–4,999 CNY in 30 (25.0%), 5,000–7,999 CNY in 36 (30.0%), and ≥ 8,000 CNY in 24 (20.0%). A total of 56 patients (46.7%) reported a prior ophthalmic history.

At the item level, tasks directly related to independent daily activities elicited the highest expectations. For example, both “watching television” and “recognizing various signs” had the highest expectation scores—each with a median of 10.0 (IQR: 8.0–12.0). The proportion of patients anticipating “moderate or substantial improvement” for these tasks reached 86.7% and 83.3%, respectively, reflecting a strong desire to regain basic visual information processing abilities. Similarly, “reading small print” and “performing fine tasks” received relatively high expectation scores of 9.0 (IQR: 7.0–11.0), with 80.0% and 78.3% of patients, respectively, expressing moderate-to-high expectations for improvement (Fig. 1A, B).

In contrast, expectations were lower for items related to recreation and sports. Both “participating in sports” and “engaging in recreational activities” had median scores of 5.0 (IQR: 4.0–6.0), with 46.7% and 50.0% of patients, respectively, anticipating moderate or substantial improvement. Additionally, near-vision functions such as “reading newspapers and books” and “completing forms/signing” received expectation scores of 8.0 (IQR: 6.0–10.0), with 75.0% and 73.3% of patients, respectively, expecting clear functional improvement (Fig. 1A, B).

In summary, patients prioritized visual functions directly related to independent daily activities, followed by near-distance precision tasks. In comparison, expectations for improvement in recreational and physical activities were more moderate.To further assess the clinical implications of these expectations, we analyzed the relationship between total preoperative expectation scores and actual 5-week postoperative visual function (VF-12-CN). Pearson correlation analysis revealed no significant linear correlation (r = -0.112, p = 0.223). Furthermore, when patients were stratified into “High Expectation” (score ≥ 51.225) and “Normal Expectation” groups, the Mann-Whitney U test indicated no statistically significant difference in postoperative VF-12-CN scores between the two groups (Median [IQR]: 28.0 [24.0–34.0] vs. 29.0 [25.0–35.0]; Z = -0.584, p = 0.559). This disconnect suggests that elevated preoperative expectations do not necessarily predict better functional recovery, highlighting the potential risk of an expectation-reality gap.

Analysis of influencing factors of preoperative visual function expectations

Univariate analysis revealed that educational level, occupation, monthly income, prior ophthalmic history, and neuroticism (N) scores were associated with the total score of preoperative visual function expectations (all p < 0.10). When these variables were simultaneously entered into a multivariate linear regression model, the explained variance for the total expectation score was 43.2% (R² = 0.432). The final retained independent influencing factors were educational level, ophthalmic history, and neuroticism (Table 3).

In summary, higher educational attainment, a history of ophthalmic disease, and elevated neuroticism were independently associated with greater preoperative visual function expectations, with educational level showing the strongest effect. These findings suggest that patients with high educational levels, prior ophthalmic conditions, or elevated neuroticism may be more likely to hold elevated expectations. Early identification of such individuals during preoperative counseling may enable stratified expectation management and targeted communication, thereby informing subsequent threshold strategies and follow-up planning.

Internal validation and calibration performance

Using “total preoperative expectation score ≥ Q3 = 51.225” as the positive outcome definition and a sample size of n = 120, a predictive model for “high expectations” was constructed based on educational level, ophthalmic history, and EPQ-N. Model discrimination and calibration were systematically evaluated.

In terms of discrimination, the apparent AUC was 0.858. After bootstrap internal validation, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.818, yielding a difference of approximately 0.04. This indicated that the model maintained a stable, moderately high level of discriminative ability even under small-sample conditions (Fig. 3A). Regarding calibration, the Brier score was 0.127, indicating low overall prediction error (Fig. 3B). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded χ² = 8.81, df = 8, p = 0.359, showing no significant misfit. The decile-based calibration plot demonstrated close agreement between predicted probabilities and observed positive rates. The median calibration slope from bootstrap validation was 1.173, suggesting a slight conservative tendency in predicted probabilities (i.e., shrinkage toward the mean), though this remained within acceptable bounds.

In summary, the predictive model demonstrated publication-level performance in both discrimination and calibration. Its robustness following internal validation supports its use as a reliable foundation for subsequent analyses of clinical net benefit (DCA) and threshold optimization.

DCA

To evaluate the clinical net benefit of the model under various intervention thresholds, a DCA was constructed based on individual predicted probabilities and compared against two baseline strategies: “Treat-all” and “Treat-none” (Fig. 4). The results showed that, across the threshold range of 0.05–0.75, the model consistently provided higher net benefit than either baseline strategy, indicating that it could offer additional clinical value over a wide range of practical application scenarios.

Taking commonly used outpatient management thresholds as examples: When the threshold was set at 0.20, the model yielded a net benefit of 0.156, outperforming Treat-all (0.063) and Treat-none (0). At thresholds of 0.30 and 0.40, the model’s net benefits were 0.127 and 0.097, respectively—both superior to the corresponding values for Treat-all (− 0.071 and − 0.250) and Treat-none (0 in both cases). Given the prevalence of “high expectations” in this study population was 25%, setting the threshold between 0.20 and 0.40 allowed for the reduction of unnecessary intensified counseling or follow-up reminders while also minimizing the risk of missed identification—demonstrating strong clinical feasibility (Fig. 4).

In summary, the threshold range of 0.20–0.40 achieved an optimal balance between net benefit and resource utilization, making it a recommended decision range for expectation management in outpatient settings. Moreover, this range retains sufficient flexibility for adjustment according to institutional priorities and clinical context.

ROC-based threshold optimization and trade-off between misclassification rates

After confirming that the model demonstrated acceptable discriminatory performance and clinical net benefit, we further conducted threshold optimization and performance trade-off analysis based on the ROC curve (Fig. 5A). The optimal threshold, determined using the Youden index, was 0.293. At this point, sensitivity was 0.833, specificity 0.800, PPV 0.581, and NPV 0.935. The LR + was 4.167, and the LR- was 0.208. The overall FP/n was 0.150, and the FN/n was 0.042.

In outpatient settings where the priority is to “identify high-expectation individuals and minimize missed cases,” a lower threshold of 0.20 yielded higher sensitivity (0.933) and a lower FN/n (0.017), but at the cost of reduced specificity (0.589) and an elevated FP/n (0.308). Conversely, raising the threshold to 0.40 significantly improved specificity (0.911) and reduced the FP/n to 0.067, but decreased sensitivity to 0.567 and increased the FN/n to 0.108 (Fig. 5B).

In summary, for goals such as screening or educational triggers, a threshold in the 0.20–0.30 range is recommended to enhance sensitivity while maintaining an acceptable FP/n. For targeted referrals or resource-focused interventions, a threshold closer to 0.40 may be considered to improve specificity. Overall, the range of 0.20–0.40 constitutes a practical and actionable threshold band: lower values (around 0.20) are more appropriate in screening-oriented contexts, while higher values (around 0.40) are preferable in precision management settings. This approach enables a balanced trade-off between risk and benefit under varying resource constraints and aligns with the net-benefit range demonstrated in the DCA.

Discussion

This study focused on the preoperative visual function expectations of patients scheduled to undergo corneal transplantation. The results indicated an overall high level of expectation, with marked heterogeneity across functional items. Notably, expectations were highest for tasks related to independent daily activities and near-vision tasks. Consistent with previous findings in cataract and refractive surgery populations3,20, our study further confirmed the common tendency toward idealized perceptions among patients undergoing vision-related procedures. This may be attributed to informational asymmetry regarding postoperative recovery in corneal transplantation, highlighting the need to avoid a “one-size-fits-all” approach to preoperative counseling. The observed item-level variability underscores the importance of granular patient education—namely, designing tailored communication strategies for specific functional domains to enhance cognitive accuracy and patient satisfaction.

Through multivariate linear regression analysis, educational level, prior ophthalmic history, and EPQ-N score were identified as independent influencing factors associated with high preoperative expectations. Unlike prior studies that have primarily focused on static demographic variables such as sex and age21, the present study introduced psychological scale variables, incorporating personality dimensions into expectation prediction—an extension with theoretical significance. The link between neuroticism and elevated expectations is well supported in psychological literature. Individuals high in neuroticism tend to exhibit emotional instability and heightened anxiety, potentially leading to irrationally inflated expectations regarding surgical outcomes. Specifically, patients with high neuroticism scores may exhibit attentional bias towards desired outcomes and impaired risk perception, leading them to use “high expectations” as a psychological coping mechanism to manage preoperative anxiety. It is crucial to note that this psychological construct of expectation formation is distinct from the objective physiological recovery potential determined by surgical success. The impact of educational level was also validated. While individuals with higher education levels often possess better access to medical information and greater confidence in recovery, in the context of generalized health messaging, this may also result in overestimation of expected outcomes. Meanwhile, a prior ophthalmic history may reflect a “deferred expectation effect,” where patients accumulate long-term dissatisfaction with impaired vision and place disproportionate hope on postoperative recovery20,22. These findings contribute to the identification of high-expectation, high-risk subgroups and support the development of targeted preoperative interventions.

In terms of model development, this study established a predictive model for high preoperative expectations based on the three identified variables. The model demonstrated good discriminative performance (AUC = 0.858; optimism-corrected AUC = 0.818) and calibration (Brier score = 0.127; Hosmer-Lemeshow test, p = 0.359). In contrast to previous models that primarily focused on predicting postoperative satisfaction, this study shifted the modeling process to the preoperative assessment phase, reflecting a forward-thinking approach to predictive strategy. Importantly, the variables included in the model are highly accessible and interpretable in routine clinical practice, which enhances its practical applicability and potential for implementation in real-world outpatient settings. The use of the bootstrap method further reinforced the model’s robustness and internal consistency.

In evaluating model performance, DCA showed that across the probability threshold range of 0.05–0.75, the model consistently provided higher net benefit than both Treat-all and Treat-none strategies, confirming its clinical utility under varying decision thresholds. Based on ROC curve analysis and the Youden index, the optimal threshold was identified as 0.293. Additionally, the study proposed a multi-threshold strategy: a 0.20–0.30 range for low-barrier screening that prioritizes sensitivity and minimizes missed cases, and a ~ 0.40 threshold for high-specificity referrals. Compared to traditional single-threshold approaches, this flexible “strategic band” allows dynamic adjustment based on different management goals, enhancing the model’s adaptability and operational efficiency in clinical use.

This study offered notable innovations in both methodology and application. First, it was the first to incorporate EPQ-based personality traits into the domain of visual expectation management, providing a new perspective for psychobehavioral modeling. Second, by integrating DCA and a multi-threshold strategy, the model balanced precision in management with efficient resource allocation. Third, the findings support the inclusion of psychological factors in the management pathway for visual surgeries, laying empirical groundwork for future interventions targeting patient expectations. Collectively, these contributions enrich the scope of preoperative assessment in corneal transplantation and offer a transferable research framework for other ophthalmic procedures.

Applying this predictive model in routine clinical practice, particularly in busy outpatient settings, faces practical challenges such as significant time constraints and variability in patient health literacy. To address this, we propose a tiered, risk-adapted counseling strategy. Utilizing the model’s multi-threshold capability, a lower threshold (0.20–0.30) can serve as an automated screening trigger to flag “high-risk” patients for intensive education, while standard care is provided to low-risk groups, thereby optimizing resource allocation.

From a clinical translational perspective, the predictive model developed in this study could be integrated into preoperative outpatient assessments to enable early identification of patients with high expectations. In combination with electronic medical record systems, the model could be embedded as an automated alert tool to assist healthcare providers in flagging key individuals and adjusting communication strategies during preoperative education. Moreover, the predicted risk scores could inform decisions on follow-up frequency and the timing of psychological interventions, thereby supporting a personalized and multidisciplinary perioperative management pathway. Nursing staff could also use the predictive results to develop customized educational materials aimed at improving patients’ comprehension and emotional adjustment, ultimately enhancing both patient satisfaction and postoperative adherence.

Despite its theoretical depth and practical relevance, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-center cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, it lacks external validation, and caution is warranted when generalizing the findings. Second, although psychological instruments such as EPQ-N demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability, their assessment may be influenced by timing and emotional states; future studies could consider incorporating emotional scales (e.g., SAS/SDS) for joint modeling. Third, variables such as social support and self-efficacy were not included, which may have led to the omission of important influencing factors.Fourth, although we assessed objective visual function using the VF-12-CN, this study did not collect direct data on postoperative subjective satisfaction. Given our finding that high expectations were not correlated with superior functional outcomes (p > 0.05), the potential impact of unmet expectations on patient satisfaction remains a critical area. Future studies should incorporate validated satisfaction scales to better evaluate the clinical consequences of these expectation gaps.Fifth, the study cohort included different surgical types (PK, DALK, DSAEK) with varying visual recovery trajectories. Due to limited subgroup sample sizes (DALK, n = 36, DSAEK, n = 18, both,<40), we could not perform stratified analyses to isolate the specific impact of surgical type on expectation formation. Future research could build on this model through multicenter, longitudinal cohort studies and leverage artificial intelligence technologies to develop more adaptive and dynamic visualization tools. Such efforts would facilitate a shift in the management of visual function expectations from passive response to proactive intervention.

Conclusion

Among the 120 patients scheduled for corneal transplantation at our hospital, the overall level of preoperative visual function expectations was high, with expectations primarily focused on independent daily activities and near-vision fine tasks, while those related to leisure and sports were relatively modest. Higher educational level, the presence of ophthalmic history, and elevated EPQ-N scores were identified as independent influencing factors associated with elevated preoperative expectations. A predictive model for “high expectations” was constructed based on these variables and demonstrated good internal performance. Within a threshold range of 0.05–0.75, the model consistently yielded greater net benefit compared to Treat-all and Treat-none strategies, as shown by DCA. Considering the ROC-based threshold trade-offs, a range of 0.20–0.30 is suitable for screening and education triggers that prioritize sensitivity and minimize missed identifications, while a threshold near 0.40 is more appropriate for precise referral, where resource allocation and specificity are emphasized. Embedding this model and threshold framework into preoperative communication and follow-up processes may reduce unnecessary interventions while minimizing the risk of overlooking patients with high expectations, thereby enhancing the standardization and operational feasibility of expectation management. Finally, it must be emphasized that this predictive tool is intended to augment, not replace, the clinician’s role. Personalized counseling by ophthalmologists remains the cornerstone of managing patient expectations and ensuring accurate prognostic perception. While the model identifies ‘at-risk’ individuals, the empathy and professional judgment inherent in the doctor-patient interaction are irreplaceable for bridging the gap between expectation and reality. However, due to the limitations of a single-center, cross-sectional design and the lack of external validation, further multicenter and longitudinal studies are warranted to assess the long-term relationship between preoperative expectations and postoperative satisfaction or functional recovery, and to refine the model further (Fig. 6).
