Inverse eigenvalue problem for discrete Schrödinger operators of a graph
Anzila Laikhuram, Jephian C.-H. Lin

Abstract
A discrete Schrödinger operator of a graph G G is a real symmetric matrix whose i , j i,j -entry, i ≠ j i\neq j , is negative if { i , j } \{i,j\} is an edge and zero if it is not an edge, while diagonal entries can be any real numbers. The discrete Schrödinger operators have been used to study vibration theory and the Colin de Verdière parameter. The inverse eigenvalue problem for discrete Schrödinger operators of a graph aims to characterize the possible spectra among discrete Schrödinger operators of a graph. Comparing to the inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph, the answers turn out to be more limited, and several restrictions based on graph structure are given. Using the strong properties, analogous versions of the supergraph lemma, the liberation lemma, and the bifurcation lemma are established. Using these results, the inverse eigenvalue problem for discrete Schrödinger operators is resolved for each graph with at most 5 5 vertices.



Keywords:

Inverse eigenvalue problem, discrete Schrödinger operator, strong spectral property, Jacobi matrix, Colin de Verdière parameter

AMS subject classifications:

05C50, 15A18, 15B57, 65F18.

1
Introduction

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. A
generalized adjacency matrix
of
G
is an
n\times n
real symmetric matrix whose off-diagonal
i,j
-entry is nonzero whenever
\{i,j\}
is an edge, where the diagonal entries can be any real number. The set of all generalized adjacency matrices of
G
is denoted as
\mathcal{S}(G)
. The
inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph
G
(IEP-
G
) aims to characterize all possible spectra of matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. It has been an active research area. We refer the reader to the monograph
[
23
]
and the references therein.

A
discrete Schrödinger operator
of
G
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
whose off-diagonal entries are zero or negative
[
7
,
19
]
. A
weighted Laplacian matrix
of
G
is a discrete Schrödinger operator
A
with
A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}

[
7
,
15
]
. The set of discrete Schrödinger operators and the set of weighted Laplacian matrices of
G
are denoted by
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
, respectively. These families play important roles in vibration theory.

A graph
G
can be viewed as a mass-spring system, where vertices are masses and edges are springs. See, e.g., the monograph
[
18
]
for more details. Its vibration behavior is governed by the differential equation
M\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}
, where
M
is a diagonal matrix determined by the weights of the masses,
\mathbf{x}
is a vector of displacements, and
L
is a weighted Laplacian matrix based on the stiffness of the springs. The solutions of this equation can be found through the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
M^{-\frac{1}{2}}LM^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, which is a discrete Schrödinger operator.

Due to the connection to vibration theory and physics, the inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph
G
originally focuses on discrete Schrödinger operators. Let
P_{n}
and
C_{n}
be the path and cycle on
n
vertices, respectively. Hochstadt
[
22
]
called a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(P_{n})
a
Jacobi matrix
if its off-diagonal entries are zero or positive; that is
-A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(P_{n})
. Hochstadt showed that
\operatorname{spec}(A)
and
\operatorname{spec}(A(1))
have to be strictly interlacing, and any strictly interlacing
\operatorname{spec}(A)
and
\operatorname{spec}(A(1))
lead to a unique Jacobi matrix
A
, if exists. Here
A(1)
stands for the principal submatrix of
A
by removing the first row and column. After that, Hald
[
21
]
and Gray and Wilson
[
20
]
independently introduced algorithms to construct the Jacobi matrix with prescribed spectral data. In 1980, Ferguson
[
17
]
called a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(C_{n})
a
periodic Jacobi matrix
if its off-diagonal entries are zero or positive; that is
-A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(C_{n})
. Ferguson characterized all possible spectra of a periodic Jacobi matrix and introduced an algorithm for constructing the matrix from prescribed spectral data. Moreover, Boley and Golub
[
8
]
had a survey on the inverse eigenvalue problems on
-A
with
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. These fruitful results laid the groundwork for the inverse eigenvalue problem. However, it appears that the attention of the IEP-
G
has been shifted to
\mathcal{S}(G)
, and the research on
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
seems discontinued.

On the other hand, Colin de Verdière
[
12
,
11
]
defined a graph parameter
\mu(G)
as the maximum multiplicity of the second smallest eigenvalue among all discrete Schrödinger operators of
G
with some non-degeneracy condition called the strong Arnold property. Despite its linear algebraic definition,
\mu(G)
is proved to possess many nice topological behaviors. For example, it is minor monotone, which means
\mu(G)\leq\mu(H)
if
G
is a minor of
H
. Also, it is shown that
\mu(G)\leq 3
if and only if
G
is a planar graph. See
[
24
]
for a survey.

Motivated by these groundbreaking studies, this paper investigates the
inverse eigenvalue problem for the discrete Schrödinger operators of a graph
(IEPS), which aims to characterize all possible spectra among matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. The rest of this section is used to discuss basic properties of
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and summarize known results.
Section

2
introduces new results about
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
in several graph families.
Section

3
revisits the strong spectral property and establishes the analogues versions of the supergraph lemma, the liberation lemma, and the bifurcation lemma for
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Moreover, we establish
Corollary

3.10
allowing us to increase each multiplicity by one, which works only for
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
but not
\mathcal{S}(G)
. Finally, we resolve the IEPS for all graphs with at most
5
vertices in
Section

4
.

1.1
Preliminaries

We first introduce some notation to be used throughout the paper. Let
A
be a matrix. We use
A[\alpha,\beta]
for the submatrix of
A
induced on rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
, and
A(\alpha,\beta)
for the submatrix of
A
obtained by removing rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
. When
\alpha=\beta
, we use
A[\alpha]=A[\alpha,\alpha]
and
A(\alpha)=A(\alpha,\alpha)
as the shorthand. When a graph
H
is used as an index set, it means the vertex set
V(H)
. We use the notation
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,\lambda_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
for the spectrum of
A
, where each of the distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
has multiplicity
m_{i}
. When
\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{q}
, we say
\mathtt{m_{1}\cdots m_{q}}
is the
ordered multiplicity list
of
A
. We say a spectrum or an ordered multiplicity list is
allowed
in
\mathcal{S}(G)
if there is a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
with the given spectral property; otherwise, we say it is
forbidden
. An ordered multiplicity list is said to be
spectrally arbitrary
if any spectrum with the ordered multiplicity list is allowed in the same set of matrices. The definition naturally extends to
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and other set of matrices. Finally, we say an eigenvalue is
simple
if its multiplicity is one, while it is
multiple
if its multiplicity is more than one.

For two graphs
G
and
H
,
G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H
stands for the disjoint union of
G
and
H
, and
G\vee H
stands for the join of
G
and
H
. For a vertex
i
,
N_{G}(i)
stands for the neighborhood of
i
in
G
. When the context is clear,
N(i)
is used instead.

Matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
demonstrates several special properties, in contrast to matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. Here we summarize some immediate ones.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Then
\lambda I-A
is a nonnegative matrix when
\lambda
is large enough. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
), the largest eigenvalue of
\lambda I-A
is simple. Equivalently, we have the following observation, which is one of the major differences between the spectral behaviors of matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and that in
\mathcal{S}(G)
.

Observation 1.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Then the smallest eigenvalue of
A
is simple, and the corresponding eigenvector can be chosen to be entrywise positive.

On the other hand, matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and the adjacency matrix share the same diameter bound for the number of distinct eigenvalues. Let
q(A)
be the number of distinct eigenvalues of
A
. Let
\operatorname{diam}(G)
be the diameter of
G
, that is, the largest distance between two vertices. It is known that
\operatorname{diam}(G)+1\leq q(A)
if
A
is the adjacency matrix of
G
; see, e.g.,
[
10
]
. In fact, the same proof also works for any matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Then
\operatorname{diam}(G)+1\leq q(A)
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
\mathsf{Kite}
be the graph obtained from
C_{4}
by adding a chord and appending a leaf on a vertex of degree
2
; see
Table

2
. According to
[
4
]
, the ordered multiplicity list
122
is allowed in
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{Kite})
. However, by
Theorem

1.2
,
4=\operatorname{diam}(\mathsf{Kite})+1\leq q(A)
for any
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Kite})
. Therefore,
122
is forbidden in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Kite})
.

Next, we justify that
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
is indeed the right focus in studying the inverse problem on vibration. In the equation
M\ddot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}
,
M
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are positive, so the equation is equivalent to
\ddot{\mathbf{y}}=-M^{-\frac{1}{2}}LM^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{y}
, where
\mathbf{y}=M^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}
. Therefore, it is natural to focus on matrices of the form
M^{-\frac{1}{2}}LM^{-\frac{1}{2}}
such that
L\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
M
is a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal entries. It is well known that the weighted Laplacian matrix
L
of a connected graph is positive semidefinite with
\operatorname{null}(A)=1
; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
7
]
. Therefore, every matrix of the form
M^{-\frac{1}{2}}LM^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is congruent to
L
and is a singular positive semidefinite matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
.

On the other hand, let
A
be a singular positive semidefinite matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
for a connected graph
G
. By
1.1
,
\operatorname{null}(A)=1
and the eigenvector of
A
with respect to
0
can be chosen to be a positive vector
\mathbf{v}
. By choosing
M^{\frac{1}{2}}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{v})
as the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are
\mathbf{v}
, or equivalently,
M=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{v}^{\circ 2})
, where
\mathbf{v}^{\circ 2}
is the vector obtained from
\mathbf{v}
by taking the square on each entry, we may easily compute

M^{\frac{1}{2}}AM^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{1}=M^{\frac{1}{2}}A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}.

Thus,
L=M^{\frac{1}{2}}AM^{\frac{1}{2}}
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
.

Observation 1.4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then every singular positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
has a unique decomposition
M^{-\frac{1}{2}}LM^{-\frac{1}{2}}
such that
L\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
M
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are positive.

In general, for every matrix
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
,
A-\lambda_{\min}I
is a singular positive semidefinite matrix, where
\lambda_{\min}
is the smallest eigenvalues of
A
. Therefore, solving the inverse eigenvalue problem in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and solving the inverse eigenvalue problem for singular positive semidefinite matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
are equivalent.

In fact, early results on the inverse problem focus more on
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Hald
[
21
]
and Gray and Wilson
[
20
]
independently solved the IEPS for paths.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
20
,
21
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be a multiset of real numbers. Then
\Lambda=\operatorname{spec}(A)
for some
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(P_{n})
if and only if elements in
\Lambda
are distinct.

On the other hand, Ferguson
[
17
]
solved the IEPS for cycles using a discrete version of Floquet theory.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be a multiset of real numbers such that
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. Then
\Lambda=\operatorname{spec}(A)
for some
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(C_{n})
if and only if

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}<\lambda_{4}\leq\lambda_{5}<\cdots.

These results are based on algorithmic constructions of a matrix from given spectral data. Recent development of the IEP-
G
utilizes the strong properties to construct new matrices implicitly.

A matrix

is said to have the
strong spectral property
(SSP) if
X=O
is the only real symmetric matrix that satisfies
A\circ X=O
,
I\circ X=O
, and
AX-XA=O
. The SSP was introduced in
[
5
]
and motivated by the strong Arnold property used in the Colin de Verdière parameter
[

,
11
]
. These strong properties have been powerful tools in constructing new matrices with desired spectral properties
[
5
,
4
,
23
]
.

Remark 1.7
.

Note that
Theorems

1.5
and
1.6
did not concern about the SSP. However,
[
29
]
showed that every matrix in
\mathcal{S}(P_{n})
has the SSP, and so are those in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(P_{n})
. In contrast, some matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(C_{n})
do not have the SSP.

We first review some results using the SSP. Here we call
H
a
spanning supergraph
of
G
if
H
is a supergraph of
G
and
V(H)=V(G)
.

Theorem 1.8

(Supergraph lemma
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
H
a spanning supergraph of
G
. If
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has the SSP, then there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(H)
with the SSP such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
. Moreover,
A^{\prime}
can be chosen so that
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
is arbitrarily small.

A follow-up paper
[
4
]
generalized the supergraph lemma into the liberation lemma, which works for matrices without the SSP. Here
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x})
is the index set on which
\mathbf{x}
is nonzero. The SSP verification matrix
\Psi
of
A
is a matrix whose rows are indexed by
E(\overline{G})
, which will be introduced in
Section

3
.

Theorem 1.9

(Liberation lemma
[
4
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph,
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, and
\Psi
the SSP verification matrix of
A
. Suppose
\mathbf{x}
is a vector in the column space
\operatorname{Col}(\Psi)
such that rows of
\Psi
outside
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x})
are linearly independent. Then there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G+\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x}))
with the SSP such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
. Moreover,
A^{\prime}
can be chosen so that
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
is arbitrarily small.

While the supergraph lemma and liberation lemma guarantee a matrix with the same spectrum and a nearby pattern, the bifurcation lemma guarantees a matrix with the same pattern and a nearby spectrum.

Theorem 1.10

(Bifurcation lemma
[
16
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
with the SSP. Then there is
\epsilon>0
such that for any
M
with
\|M-A\|<\epsilon
, a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G)
exists with the SSP and
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(M)
.

There are yet more applications of the strong properties; see, e.g.,
[
23
]
. In
Section

3
, we will introduce the analogous versions of these lemmas for the discrete Schrödinger operators.

2
Graph families

In this section, we study the spectral behaviors of the discrete Schrödinger operators for several graph families. We start with complete graphs and trees.

2.1
Complete graphs

The
Givens rotation matrix

R_{i,j;\theta}
of order
n
is obtained from the identity matrix of order
n
by replacing the principal submatrix induced on
i
and
j
(in order) with the
2
-dimensional rotation matrix

R_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

The Givens rotations have been used to solve the inverse eigenvalue problem for complete graphs
[
6
]
. We follow the similar proof but pay extra attention to make sure the off-diagonal entries are negative.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with two vertices
i
and
j
such that
N(i)\setminus\{j\}\supseteq N(j)\setminus\{i\}
. Define
H
as the graph obtained from
G
by adding edges from
j
to each vertex in
N(i)\cup\{i\}\setminus\{j\}
if the edge was not present. If
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
such that either
i\sim j
in
G
or
a_{i,i}<a_{j,j}
when
i\nsim j
in
G
, then the matrix
A^{\prime}=R_{i,j;\theta}AR_{i,j;\theta}^{\top}
is a matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(H)
that is similar to
A
for any
\theta>0
small enough.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
i=1
and
j=2
. Let
n
be the order of
G
. Thus, we may write

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&A_{1,2}\\
A_{1,2}^{\top}&A_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }R_{i,j;\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{\theta}&O_{2,n-2}\\
O_{n-2,2}&I_{n-2}\end{bmatrix}

such that
A_{1,1}
is of order
2
. Consequently,

R_{i,j;\theta}AR_{i,j;\theta}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{\theta}A_{1,1}R_{\theta}^{\top}&R_{\theta}A_{1,2}\\
A_{1,2}^{\top}R_{\theta}^{\top}&A_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
N(i)\setminus\{j\}\supseteq N(j)\setminus\{i\}
, each nonzero column
\mathbf{v}
in
A_{1,2}
meets the patterns

\begin{bmatrix}-\\
-\end{bmatrix}\text{ or }\begin{bmatrix}-\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, for
\theta
small enough,
R_{\theta}\mathbf{v}
is a entrywise negative vector.

Suppose
i\sim j
. Then the fact that
A_{1,1}\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{2})
implies
R_{\theta}A_{1,1}R_{\theta}^{\top}
is in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{2})
when
\theta
is small enough. Suppose
i\nsim j
and
a_{i,i}<a_{j,j}
. Then by direct computation

\displaystyle R_{\theta}A_{1,1}R_{\theta}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,i}&0\\
0&a_{j,j}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,i}\cos^{2}\theta+a_{j,j}\sin^{2}\theta&(a_{i,i}-a_{j,j})\sin\theta\cos\theta\\
(a_{i,i}-a_{j,j})\sin\theta\cos\theta&a_{i,i}\sin^{2}\theta+a_{j,j}\cos^{2}\theta.\end{bmatrix}

With
a_{i,i}<a_{j,j}
and
\sin\theta\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta
, the matrix
R_{\theta}A_{1,1}R_{\theta}^{\top}
is in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{2})
when
\theta>0
is small enough.
∎

Roughly speaking, the idea of
Lemma

2.1
is that, if the
i
-th row is “more negative” than the
j
-th row, then the Givens rotation on
i,j
with a
\theta>0
small enough leads to another discrete Schrödinger operator through similarity.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
be a multiset of real numbers with
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. Then
\Lambda
is the spectrum of some matrix
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{n})
if and only if
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
D
be the diagonal matrix whose
i,i
-entry is
\lambda_{i}
. Then
\operatorname{spec}(D)=\Lambda
. By applying
Lemma

2.1
with
i=1
and
j=2,\ldots,n
sequentially, there is a matrix
A\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{n})
with
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\Lambda
. Note that we can always choose
\theta>0
small enough to make sure that
a_{1,1}
is always the unique minimum element on the diagonal.
∎

2.2
Trees

The signature similarity techniques have been used in many places in the inverse eigenvalue problem
[
9
,
4
]
and sign pattern problem
[
13
]
. We include the statement and the proof for completeness.

A
signature matrix
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are either
1
or
-1
. Note that the inverse of a signature matrix is itself. If
A
and
B
are two matrices with
B=DAD
, then we say
A
and
B
are
signature similar
.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. For any spanning tree
T
of
G
, there is a signature matrix
D
such that
DAD
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
whose entries corresponding to
E(T)
are negative.

Proof.

Let
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}
and
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
. Label the vertices of
T
with
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the induced subgraph
T_{i}
of
T
on
\{1,\ldots,i\}
is a tree for each
i
. Necessarily,
i
is a leaf in
T_{i}
for any
i\geq 2
; otherwise,
T_{i-1}=T_{i}-i
would be disconnected. We let
d_{1}=1
and will choose
d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}
in order. For
i=2,\ldots,n
, let
j
be the unique neighbor of
i
in
T_{i}
. Then choose
d_{i}
so that
d_{i}d_{j}
and
a_{i,j}
have the same sign. Note that
\{i,j\}
is an edge, so
a_{i,j}\neq 0
; also,
j
is a vertex of
T_{i}
, so
j<i
and
d_{j}
was chosen. By doing so, the matrix
DAD
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
whose entries corresponding to
E(T)
are negative.
∎

Consequently, the standard inverse eigenvalue problems on trees are equivalent to the inverse eigenvalue problems of discrete Schrödinger operators of trees.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
T
be a tree and
\Lambda
a multiset of real numbers. Then
\Lambda
is the spectrum of some matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(T)
if and only if
\Lambda
is the spectrum of some matrix in
\mathcal{S}(T)
.

Proof.

Note that
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(G)
. Also, by
Proposition

2.3
, every matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
is similar to a matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. Therefore, the two sets have the same possible spectra.
∎

The IEP-
G
is solved for path
[
20
,
21
]
, generalized stars and double stars
[
26
]
, but remains open for general trees.

2.3
Unicyclic graphs

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
G
be a unicyclic graph whose cycle has odd length. If a spectrum is allowed in
\mathcal{S}(G)
such that the largest eigenvalue is multiple, then the spectrum is allowed in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. Let
p
be the product of all the entries corresponding to the unique cycle. Note that any signature similarity does not change the product
p
. If
p<0
, then
A
is signature similar to a matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
since the cycle has odd length, and the smallest eigenvalue is simple by
1.1
. If
p>0

-A

2.5
Graphs with a cut-vertex

Let
A
be a real symmetric matrix and

an index. It is known that

|\operatorname{null}(A)-\operatorname{null}(A(v))|\leq 1.

Equivalently, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

|\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)-\operatorname{mult}_{A(v)}(\lambda)|\leq 1.

Here we consider the multiplicity as
0
if
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue. Many works have been done to study the change of the nullity or the multiplicity after removal of a row and a column; see, e.g.,
[
31
,
32
,

,
3
,
1
]
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
A
be a real symmetric matrix whose columns and rows are indexed by

for some graph

. Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and

. Then

is called

•

a
\lambda
-upper

\operatorname{mult}_{A(v)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)+1
,

•

a
\lambda
-neutral

\operatorname{mult}_{A(v)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)
, and

•

a
\lambda
-downer

\operatorname{mult}_{A(v)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{mult}_{A}(\lambda)-1
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and

a cut-vertex of
G
. Let

. Then

has to be a
\lambda
-upper vertex if
\lambda
is the smallest eigenvalue of
A[H]
for some component

of
G-v
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a component of
G-v
such that
\lambda
is the smallest eigenvalue of

. By
1.1
,
\lambda
is simple and the eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
of

with respect to

can be chosen to be entrywise positive. Thus,

cannot be orthogonal to

is not in the column space of

∎

3
Strong spectral property

Recall that a real symmetric matrix
A
is said to have the SSP if
X=O
is the only real symmetric matrix that satisfies
A\circ X=O
,
I\circ X=O
, and
AX-XA=O
.

We first check that signature similarity preserves the SSP.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
D
be an signature matrix. Then
A
has the SSP if and only if
DAD
has the SSP.

Proof.

Since
A
and
DAD
have the same based graph,
A\circ X=O
if and only if
DAD\circ X=O
. On the other hand,
AX-XA=O
if and only if

DAXD-DXAD=DADDXD-DXDDAD=O.

Also,
X=O
if and only if
DXD=O
.
∎

Thus,
Theorem

2.4
is naturally extended.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
T
be a tree and
\Lambda
a multiset of real numbers. Then
\Lambda
is the spectrum of some matrix in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(T)
with the SSP if and only if
\Lambda
is the spectrum of some matrix in
\mathcal{S}(T)
with the SSP.

Next we see that the supergraph lemma naturally extends to matrices in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
. The only differences between
Theorem

1.8
and
Theorem

3.3
are that the new nonzero entries need to be negative and that the negative entries in the original matrix need to stay negative. Indeed, these are natural consequence of the classical supergraph lemma.

Theorem 3.3

(Supergraph lemma with signs)
.

Let
H
be a spanning supergraph of
G
. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has the SSP. Then there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(H)
such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
,
A^{\prime}
has the SSP, and
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
can be chosen arbitrarily small. Moreover, the sign of each entry of
A^{\prime}
on
E(H)\setminus E(G)
can be chosen arbitrarily, while other off-diagonal entries have the same signs as those of
A
correspondingly.

Proof.

The original proof of the supergraph lemma
[
5
]
actually says more than its statement. According to its proof, the entries of
A^{\prime}
corresponding to
E(H)\setminus E(G)
can be chosen to be arbitrary real numbers. Thus, we may choose them to be small values with any prescribed signs. Since
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
can be chosen to be small, off-diagonal entries other than
E(H)\setminus E(G)
preserves their signs.
∎

We remark that the fact that the new nonzero entries can be chosen arbitrarily is also used in
[
28
]
to guarantee a matrix with any prescribed spectrum and a generic eigenbasis.

By
[
5
, Theorem 34]
, the direct sum
A\oplus B
of two matrices
A
and
B
has the SSP if and only if both
A
and
B
have the SSP and they share no common eigenvalue. This leads to an immediate application.

Example 3.4
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
a set of distinct real numbers. By
[
5
, Theorem 34]
, the diagonal matrix
D
with diagonal entries
\Lambda
has the SSP. Since
G
is a spanning supergraph of
\overline{K_{n}}
and
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(K_{n})
, there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
with the SSP and
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\Lambda
.

Next we review some background of the verification matrix to introduce the liberation lemma. The SSP verification matrix
\Psi
of a matrix
A
was first introduced in
[
4
]
. It is known that the left kernel of
\Psi
is trivial if and only if
A
has the SSP. We use the equivalent statement in
[
4
, Proposition 7.7]
as the definition. Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
M
an
n\times n
matrix. We adopt the notation that
\operatorname{vec}_{E(\overline{G})}(M)
is the vector recording entries of
M
corresponding to
\{(i,j):\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G}),\ i<j\}
, and that
\operatorname{vec}_{E_{o}}(M)
is the vector recording entries of
M
corresponding to
E_{o}=\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. Here the entries are ordered lexicographically.

Definition 3.5

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. The
SSP verification matrix
of
A
is a
|E(\overline{G})|\times\binom{n}{2}
matrix whose rows are indexed by
\{(i,j):\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G}),\ i<j\}
in the lexicographical order such that the
(i,j)
-row is
\operatorname{vec}_{E_{o}}(AX_{i,j}-X_{i,j}A)
. Here
X_{i,j}
is the symmetric matrix whose
i,j
-entry and
j,i
-entry are
1
and other entries are
0
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
be the topological closure of
\mathcal{S}(G)
. That is,
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
allows those entries corresponding to edges to be zero. Moreover, let
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(G)
be the matrices in
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
with diagonal entries all zero. Thus, the condition
A\circ X=O
is equivalent to
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(\overline{G})
when
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, while
A\circ X=O
and
I\circ X=O
are equivalent to
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(G)
. Based on this, Lin, Oblak, and Šmigoc
[
30
]
introduced an extension of the SSP.
Let
G
be a graph and
G^{\prime}
a spanning supergraph of
G
. A matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
is said to have the
strong spectral property with respect to
G^{\prime}
if
X=O
is the only real symmetric matrix that satisfies
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G^{\prime}})
and
AX-XA=O
.

One of the challenges for using the liberation lemma is to find the vector
\mathbf{x}
. Lin, Oblak, and Šmigoc
[
30
]
extended the notion of the SSP and introduced the liberation set, which is proved to be the same as
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x})
for some valid
\mathbf{x}
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. A nonempty set of edges
\beta\subseteq E(\overline{G})
is called an
SSP liberation set
of
A
if and
only if
A
has the SSP with respect to
G+\beta^{\prime}
for all
\beta^{\prime}\subset\beta
with
|\beta^{\prime}|=|\beta|-1
.

It is shown that
[
30
, Proposition 2.6]

\beta
is an SSP liberation set if and only if
\beta=\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x})
for some vector
\mathbf{x}
valid for the liberation lemma (
Theorem

1.9
).

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. An
annihilator
of
A
is a matrix
X
such that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
AX-XA=O
. Define
\mathcal{R}(A)
as the set of annihilators of
A
, which is a subspace in the space of symmetric matrices
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Many definitions and results can be rephrased in the language of annihilators.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G
be a graph,
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, and
\Psi
the SSP verification matrix of
A
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a spanning supergraph of
G
. Then the following hold.

(1)

A
has the SSP if and only if
\mathcal{R}(A)=\{O\}
.

(2)

A
has the SSP with respect to

if and only if
\mathcal{R}(A)\cap\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G^{\prime}})=\{O\}
.

(3)

\beta
is an SSP liberation set if and only if
\mathcal{R}(A)\cap\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G+\beta^{\prime}})=\{O\}
for all
\beta^{\prime}\subset\beta
with
|\beta^{\prime}|=|\beta|-1
.

(4)

A real symmetric matrix

is in
\mathcal{R}(A)
if and only if
\operatorname{vec}_{E(\overline{G})}(X)^{\top}\Psi=\mathbf{0}^{\top}
.

(5)

A vector
\mathbf{x}
is in the column space
\operatorname{Col}(\Psi)
if and only if
\left\langle\mathbf{x},\operatorname{vec}_{E(\overline{G})}(X)\right\rangle=0
for all
X\in\mathcal{R}(A)
.

Theorem 3.8

(Liberation lemma with signs)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. Suppose
\beta\subseteq E(\overline{G})
is a liberation set and
\mathbf{x}
a vector with

such that
\left\langle\mathbf{x},\operatorname{vec}_{E(\overline{G})}(X)\right\rangle=0
for all

. Then there is a matrix

such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
,

has the SSP, and
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
can be chosen to be arbitrarily small. Moreover, the sign pattern of

is the same as that of
\mathbf{x}
on
\beta
and the same as that of

on other off-diagonal entries.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be the SSP verification matrix of

. Since
\beta
is an SSP liberation set, rows of
\Psi
outside

[

. On the other hand, the
\mathbf{x}
with the given assumptions is a vector in
\operatorname{Col}(\Psi)
by

3.7
. By the liberation lemma (

1.9
), there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G+\beta)
such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
,

has the SSP, and
\|A^{\prime}-A\|
can be chosen to be arbitrarily small.

According to the proof of the liberation lemma (

),

is obtained by perturbing

toward the direction
\mathbf{x}
on
\beta
. Therefore, the sign pattern of

on

is the same as that of
\mathbf{x}
. When the perturbation is small enough, all off-diagonal entries other than

∎

\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
, respectively. We may assume that
\{\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{q}\}
is orthonormal.

Since both
A
and
B
have the SSP,
\mathcal{R}(A\oplus B)
is an
(h-1)
-dimensional subspace spanned by elements of the form
f(\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\top})
such that
\mathbf{a}_{i}
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}
correspond to the same eigenvalue, where

f(Y)=\begin{bmatrix}O&Y\\
Y^{\top}&O\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\beta
be the set of edges from the last vertex of
P_{p}
to every vertex of
H
, which corresponds to those entries in the bottom row of
Y
. Note that each of
\mathbf{b}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{h}
appears exactly once in the spanning set. Also, by zero forcing the last entry of
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is always nonzero; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
. Thus,
\operatorname{vec}_{\beta}(R)
for
R\in\mathcal{R}(A\oplus B)
are nonzero multiple of
\mathbf{b}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{h}
, respectively. Let
\mathbf{x}
be the vector indexed by
E(\overline{P_{p}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H})
and whose entries corresponding to
\beta
are
\mathbf{b}_{1}
and zero otherwise. Then
\left\langle\mathbf{x},\operatorname{vec}_{E(\overline{P_{p}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H})}(R)\right\rangle=0
for all
R\in\mathcal{R}(A\oplus B)
.

On the other hand, we claim that
\beta
is an SSP liberation set. Let
Q
be the matrix whose rows are
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{h}
. Since
\{\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{h}\}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis of
H
, the matrix
Q
is orthogonal. This implies
Q^{\top}=Q^{-1}
and
\det(Q)=\pm 1
. With
Q^{\top}=Q^{-1}=\pm Q^{\rm adj}
, we know all
(h-1)\times(h-1)
minors of
Q
without using the row of
\mathbf{b}_{1}
are nonzero. Therefore, by vanishing any
h-1
entries on
\beta
, the only remaining matrix in
\mathcal{R}(A\oplus B)
is
O
. That is,
\beta
is a liberation set by
Proposition

3.7
. By
Theorem

3.8
, there is a matrix
A^{\prime}
with the desired spectrum, with the SSP, and
A^{\prime}\in\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
.
∎

Theorem 3.11

(Bifurcation lemma with signs)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
with the SSP. Then there is
\epsilon>0
such that for any
M
with
\|M-A\|<\epsilon
, a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G)
exists with the SSP and
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(M)
. Moreover,
A^{\prime}
and
A
have the same off-diagonal sign pattern.

Proof.

Let
A^{\prime}
be the matrix with
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(M)
in the classical bifurcation lemma (
Theorem

1.10
). Since
A^{\prime}
is a small perturbation of
A
, the off-diagonal sign pattern stays the same.
∎

5
Concluding Remarks

Through the data in
Section

4
, we see that the inverse problems on
\mathcal{S}(G)
and that on
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
behave differently in many different aspects, beyond the basic properties of a
Z
-matrix such as
1.1
and
1.2
. For example,
122
is allowed in
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{Bnr})
,
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{Dart})
, and
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{Kite})
; in contrast, it is allowed only in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Dart})
yet forbidden in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Bnr})
and
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Kite})
for different reasons. Cycles remain a mysterious case where the ordered multiplicity list
\mathtt{1\cdots 2\cdots 1}
is allowed if and only if
2
occurs at the even position by
Theorems

1.6
and
3.9
. Such restriction is not observed on other graphs, and it is worthy to explore more hidden properties from a cycle. Notably, whether all allowed spectrum in
\mathcal{S}(C_{n})
can be realized by an matrix with the SSP in
\mathcal{S}(C_{n})
remains an open problem. Similarly, whether all spectrum satisfying
Theorem

1.6
can be realized by a matrix with the SSP in
\ddot{\mathcal{S}}(G)
is of interest as well.

On the other hand, the SSP again plays an important role in the study of IEPS. There are other strong properties, such as the strong Arnold property (SAP) and the strong multiplicity property (SMP); see, e.g.,
[
5
,
4
,
23
]
. We note that the supergraph lemma with signs (
Theorem

3.3
), the liberation lemma with signs (
Theorem

3.8
), and the bifurcation lemma with signs (
Theorem

3.11
) naturally extend to SAP and SMP, so there are more potentials for these signed versions.
