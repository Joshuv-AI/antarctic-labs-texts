Prospective association of the triglyceride-glucose index and triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio with anxiety risk in a community-based cohort: a UK Biobank study
Authors: Huiming Ren, Tian Zhao, Zhongyue Lv, Guoming Xie, Hongpeng Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo investigate whether the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, as clinical surrogates of insulin resistance, predict the risk of anxiety, and to examine the potential mediating role of plasma proteins.DesignProspective cohort study.SettingCommunity-based population cohort in the United Kingdom.ParticipantsA total of 412 767 participants aged 40–69 years without anxiety at baseline and with available data on TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio were included. Among them, 43 991 participants had proteomic data with 2923 unique plasma proteins measured.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the incidence of first-episode anxiety. The main exposures were TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio. Mediation analysis was conducted to assess the contribution of plasma proteins.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.8 years, 21 616 cases of first-episode anxiety were identified. Higher quintiles of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio were significantly associated with increased anxiety risk (p trend <0.001). Each one SD increase in TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with an 8% and 9% higher risk of anxiety, respectively. Mediation analysis showed that 36 proteins jointly explained 64.0% and 74.9% of the associations of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety, respectively, with asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGR1) and protease serine 8 (PRSS8) identified as key mediators.ConclusionsElevated baseline TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio are associated with an increased risk of anxiety, and this association may be mediated by ASGR1 and PRSS8.Keywords: Anxiety disorders, PSYCHIATRY, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Anxiety is one of the most common mental disorders and the second leading mental health-related cause of disability-adjusted life years worldwide. In 2019, 45.82 million people worldwide will suffer from anxiety, which seriously interferes with their normal daily life and work.1 Anxiety symptoms usually begin in childhood or adolescence, but public awareness of anxiety disorders is low and only 27.6% of patients with anxiety seek treatment,2 delaying accurate diagnosis and treatment. Early recognition and prevention of anxiety symptoms is therefore crucial.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a state of reduced sensitivity and responsiveness of the body to the action of insulin. Patients with anxiety often have IR.3 Excessive anxiety can lead to hyperfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, significantly increased cortisol levels and IR. At the same time, poor lifestyle choices caused by anxiety can exacerbate IR. Cross-sectional studies have shown that IR helps to elucidate the pathological processes associated with anxiety.4 However, the longitudinal association between baseline IR levels and anxiety risk remains unclear. The hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp is the gold standard measurement for assessing IR. However, the complexity of the method limits its clinical use. In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio have been used as surrogate markers of IR and have high clinical feasibility, which is expected to establish their association with anxiety risk.

Previous studies have indicated that circulating proteins may act as mediators linking metabolic disturbances and psychiatric outcomes. For example, a large-scale proteomic analysis in the UK Biobank (46 165 individuals with baseline plasma proteomics) identified 157 proteins associated with incident depression, emphasising the potential role of proteins in mediating metabolic and mood-related changes.5 Proteomic changes have also been documented in plasma of poststroke depression patients, particularly involving lipid metabolism and immunoregulatory pathways.6 Moreover, intervention studies in schizophrenia demonstrated that exercise-induced changes in metabolic and proteomic profiles correspond to psychiatric improvement.7 Building on this foundation, our study hypothesises that proteins may similarly mediate the association between IR-related surrogates and the future onset of anxiety.

UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project is a consortium of diverse and population-sized proteomic data using Olink proteomics techniques to characterise the plasma proteomic characteristics of 54 219 participants in the UK Biobank.8 Proteins are the direct performers of life activities. Link proteomics can detect a large number of proteins from trace biological samples, covering 100% of signalling pathways, and then provide an explanation for pathological changes in disease. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no study on the extent to which proteins mediate the association between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety.

Therefore, to investigate the relationship between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and the risk of anxiety in Europeans, we conducted a longitudinal analysis of TyG index, TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety in 412 767 participants followed for 13.83 years using UK Biobank data. The mediating role of a number of proteins in the relationship between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety was also assessed.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

The UK Biobank is a prospective community-based cohort that collected genetic and blood samples, lifestyle and environmental exposure data from half a million volunteers aged 40–69 years at 22 assessment centres in the UK between 2006 and 2010 and has been tracking information from their health records for decades.9 Of the 502 415 participants, after excluding 73 344 who had no data on TyG index or TG/HDL-C ratio and 16 304 who had anxiety at baseline, 412 767 individuals were included in the analysis and began follow-up (figure 1). Anxiety as an outcome, including phobic and other anxiety disorders, is coded as 130 904 and 130 906 in UK Biobank (online supplemental table S1). The first episode of anxiety after 31 March 2017 was reviewed to determine the date of first postbaseline anxiety.

Flowchart of participant enrolment. HDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol; TG, triglyceride; TyG, triglyceride-glucose.

Data collection

We collected potential confounders at baseline, including age, sex, ethnicity, current smoking and drinking status, physical activity, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index, blood pressure and medication (aspirin, insulin, antihypertensive medication and cholesterol-lowering medication). The Townsend deprivation index, a measure of the socioeconomic circumstances of a population, has a significant impact on mental health.10

Peripheral venous blood was collected from participants according to UK Biobank study procedures. Serum biochemical tests measured urate, glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-sensitivity C reactive protein, creatinine concentrations and glycated haemoglobin was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography analysis. The TyG index was calculated as ln (triglycerides (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2), and the TG/HDL-C ratio was calculated as TG (mg/dL) divided by HDL-C (mg/dL). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was assessed using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creativity equation.11 Chronic kidney disease was defined as an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. In addition, a number of comorbidities were assessed, including type 1 diabetes (UK Biobank code 130706), type 2 diabetes (code 130708), hypertension (code 131286) and retinopathy (codes 41 270 and 41280) (online supplemental table S1). Details of these assessments are found in the UK Biobank online protocol (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified by quintile according to TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio, respectively. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SE and trends across quintiles were assessed using generalised linear regression analysis. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and tested for trends across quintiles using the Cochran-Armitage χ2 test for trend.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate multivariable-adjusted HRs and 95% CIs for incident anxiety at the end point. Three models were constructed: model 1 adjusted for age and sex, model 2 further adjusted for ethnicity, smoking, drinking, physical activity and Townsend deprivation index. Model 3 was further adjusted for body mass index, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, urate, glycated haemoglobin, high-sensitivity C reactive protein, eGFR, aspirin, insulin, antihypertensive medication and cholesterol-lowering medication. P values were tested for linear trends in the quintile HRs for TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio using the median within each quintile as the predictor. In addition, to determine whether there was a non-linear dose-response relationship between multivariable-adjusted TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and CVD risk, we constructed a restricted cubic spline with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th and 95th percentiles and trimmed 1% of the highest and lowest TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio observations.

Variables such as sex, age, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, smoking, alcohol consumption, Townsend deprivation index, diabetes, chronic kidney disease and medication use were adjusted for in the subgroup analysis. Given the effects of triglycerides and glucose on anxiety, we also performed prospective associations of anxiety with triglycerides and glucose. In addition, six sets of sensitivity analyses were performed. We analysed whether individuals with an early onset anxiety disorder within the first 2 years after enrolment were excluded, or whether multiple imputation was used to account for missing values at baseline, or whether missing values were excluded, or whether model 3 was further adjusted for covariates such as diastolic blood pressure, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and creatinine, or when disease was defined by an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code, or when patients with cancer were excluded, whether the associations between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety would change. Missing values were assumed to be missing at random, and sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded consistent results. Anxiety was defined as F40 and F41 in the ICD, 10th Revision, and comorbidities included dyslipidaemia (E78), type 1 diabetes (E10), type 2 diabetes (E11), hypertension (I10-I15), retinopathy (E10.3, E11.3, E12.3, E13.3, E14.3, H28.0, H33, H35.3, H36.0, H40-H42) (online supplemental table S1).

UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project, which used the antibody-based Olink Explore 3072 proximity extension assay to perform proteomic analysis of plasma samples collected from 54 219 UKB participants, measuring 2941 protein analytes and capturing 2923 unique proteins.8 Of the 54 219 participants, 43 991 were also included in the analyses of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety. Pearson correlation analysis was first used to assess the association of proteins with the TyG index, and Cox regression was used to investigate their association with anxiety. A p value (FDR corrected) <5×10–5 was set as the threshold for statistical significance. The selected proteins were then analysed using the publicly available SAS macro % mediate to investigate whether the proteins play a mediating role and explain the relationship between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety. The mediation model was adjusted for confounders as in the Cox proportional hazards model 3.

Data were analysed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina) and R V.4.3.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), and statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed p values with significance determined at a level below 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Table 2 outlines the baseline characteristics of the entire study cohort (N=412 767), stratified by TyG index quintiles, while analogous data for TG/HDL-C ratio quintiles are presented in online supplemental table S2. The study population had a mean age of 57.1 years and was 53.4% female, with the majority (94.2%) identifying as white. The recorded baseline prevalences for dyslipidaemia, type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes and hypertension were 3.3%, 1.0%, 9.2% and 27.4%, respectively.

The median (IQR) TyG index was 8.68 (8.54–8.83) and the median TG/HDL-C ratio was 2.42 (2.02–2.91). Elevations in both the TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio correlated with a cluster of demographic and clinical factors. Specifically, higher quintiles were more likely to include males, older individuals and those of white ethnicity or lower socioeconomic status. These groups also exhibited less favourable lifestyle profiles (eg, current smoking, lower physical activity), higher adiposity and blood pressure and an adverse cardiometabolic biomarker profile—characterised by elevated urate, glycated haemoglobin, glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-C, hs-CRP and creatinine, alongside lower HDL-C and eGFR. A positive gradient was observed across the higher quintiles for the use of medications such as aspirin, insulin, antihypertensives and lipid-lowering agents. A parallel increase was noted in the prevalence of several conditions, including retinopathy, chronic kidney disease, dyslipidaemia, diabetes (both type 1 and type 2) and hypertension (table 1, online supplemental table S2).

Association of baseline TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety

During a median follow-up of 13.83 years (IQR: 13.08–14.55), 21 616 (5.24%) patients developed first-episode anxiety. Cox regression analysis showed that higher TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio were significantly associated with increased risk of anxiety after adjustment for age and sex, and after further adjustment for race, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity and the sociodemographic factors of the Townsend deprivation index. After full adjustment, the HRs (95% CIs) for the risk of anxiety in the top quintile compared with the bottom quintile of the TG index were 1.09 (1.04–1.14), 1.13 (1.08–1.18), 1.14 (1.09–1.2) and 1.24 (1.18–1.3), respectively. For the TG/HDL-C ratio in the top quintile compared with the bottom quintile, the HRs were 1.06 (1.02–1.11), 1.10 (1.05–1.14), 1.14 (1.09–1.19) and 1.23 (1.17–1.29), respectively. There was a clear trend towards an increased risk of anxiety across quintiles of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio (p for trend <0.001). Each 1 SD increase in TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with an 8% and 6% increase in the risk of anxiety, respectively (tables2 3). For phobic and other anxiety disorders, the associations of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety risk also remained significant (online supplemental tables S3–S6). Multivariable adjusted restricted cubic spline analysis showed no evidence of a non-linear association between TyG index and anxiety (p-non-linear=0.199) (figure 2). However, there was a non-linear association between TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety (p-non-linear <0.001).

Further subgroup analyses of participants were performed after adjustment for the same variables included in model 3 in table 2. The results showed that gender, age, systolic blood pressure, smoking, aspirin and insulin use had significant interactions with the TyG index on anxiety risk. Specifically, females, age ≤60 years, systolic blood pressure ≤120 mm Hg, non-smokers, non-aspirin users and non-insulin users had a greater increase in anxiety risk than males, age >60 years, systolic blood pressure >120 mm Hg, smokers, aspirin users and insulin users (table 4). In addition, further analysis showed that a high triglyceride level was a risk factor for anxiety, and a high triglyceride level increased the risk of anxiety more than a moderate or low triglyceride level. This effect of triglycerides on anxiety was more pronounced as blood glucose levels increased (figure 3).

Mediation analysis

There were 36 proteins that were significantly correlated with TyG index and also had an effect on anxiety risk (online supplemental table S7). Mediation analysis showed that the joint mediating effect of 36 proteins accounted for 64.0% and 74.9% of the total effect of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio on anxiety, respectively (table 6). Among them, the effect of TyG index on anxiety was related to the mediating effect of 26 proteins, while the effect of TG/HDL-C ratio on anxiety was related to the mediating effect of 8 proteins. The effects of asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGR1), protease serine 8 (PRSS8) and adrenomedullin (ADM) on TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio were 67.3% and 30.1%, 52.6% and 42.8% and 29.0% and 31.7%, respectively. TNF receptor superfamily member 10A (TNFRSF10A) was mediated by 12.6% and 16.7% and integrin subunit beta 2 (ITGB2) by 13.1% and 6.0%. These results suggest that specific proteins play an important mediating role in the effects of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio on anxiety risk. In particular, ASGR1 and PRSS8 were the most prominent mediators and played a significant role in the effects of these two indicators on anxiety, as did ADM, TNFRSF10A and ITGB2.

Discussion

We found that elevated TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio were associated with an increased risk of anxiety in a large population-based cohort from the UK Biobank. After adjustment for relevant covariates, the risk of anxiety increased by 8% and 9% for each SD increase in TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio, respectively. The association between TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio and anxiety was mainly mediated by ASGR1, PRSS8, ADM, TNFRSF10A, ITGB2 and other proteins.

IR, especially in the brain, is the main cause of neuroplasticity defects in patients with metabolic disorders, which further induces mitochondrial and dopamine dysfunction and is an important pathophysiological mechanism leading to anxiety, depression and other diseases.12,15 TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio, as surrogate indicators of IR, have been confirmed to be associated with the risk of a variety of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.16,18 The cross-sectional study also found an association between the TyG index and depression.19 This is the first study to investigate the association of the TyG index and the TG/HDL-C ratio with anxiety, which is a common mental disorder along with depression. As the TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio increase, the risk of anxiety increases. Similarly, studies have shown that obesity,20 diabetes,21 polycystic ovary syndrome22 and others are often associated with or exacerbate the risk of anxiety, and IR is known to be the common pathological basis of these diseases. This suggests that if we can intervene in the early stage of insulin sensitivity decline, the incidence of anxiety can be effectively reduced, and the data of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio are easily obtained.

Among the 2941 proteins measured in the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project from the cardiometabolic, inflammation, neurology, oncology and other panels, the effects of TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio on anxiety can be partially explained by some proteins. For example, prostasin (PRSS8) is a serine protease that may be involved in the glucose-dependent physiological regulation of insulin secretion through the epidermal growth factor and its receptor signalling pathway in pancreatic beta cells.23 In addition, both animal and human studies have shown that overexpression of PRSS is associated with dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism.24 Previous studies have described the mechanism of protein-mediated genetic risk for disease based on data from circulating proteins in human blood and genetic data, and the PRSS8 gene has been shown to be a candidate gene associated with Alzheimer’s disease.25 In addition, the ASGR1 protein is an asialoglycoprotein receptor that plays a key role in cholesterol excretion and metabolism. Inhibition of ASGR1 protein can promote cholesterol efflux and reduce lipid deposition in the blood and liver.26 ADM has been associated with mental health problems such as stress, anxiety and depression.27 ADM has a neuroprotective effect that helps to maintain homeostasis in normal or stressed states and to reduce anxiety behaviour.28 These findings provide a direction for the study of related mechanisms or disease targets.

The strength of this study is that it is a prospective study using a large sample population from the UK Biobank, which, to our knowledge, is the first study of the association between the TyG index and TG/HDL-C and anxiety risk, and additionally uses Olink Proteomics data to explain the role of protein mediation in this association. The limitations of our study are, first, that the UK Biobank population was derived from within the United Kingdom, which may affect the applicability to other ethnic groups. Moreover, UK Biobank participants tend to be healthier, better educated and predominantly of European ancestry compared with the general population, which may introduce a healthy volunteer bias and limit the representativeness of the cohort. Thus, caution is warranted when generalising our findings to other populations or ethnic groups. Second, the glucose data in the UK Biobank were random (non-fasting) blood glucose rather than fasting blood glucose, which may have introduced measurement error, although we adjusted the model for glycated haemoglobin. Finally, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, and causal inferences should be made with caution.

In conclusion, our study showed that the increase in TyG index and TG/HDL-C ratio, two surrogate markers of IR, can predict the increased risk of anxiety, and this association is mainly mediated by ASGR1, PRSS8, ADM, TNFRSF10A, ITGB2 and other proteins.
