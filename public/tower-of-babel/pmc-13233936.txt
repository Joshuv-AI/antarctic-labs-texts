Attitudes Toward Deprescribing Among Community-Dwelling Adults: A Cross-Sectional Study in Community Pharmacies
Authors: Elodie Marcellaud, Tassadit Merabtine, Jérémy Couturas, Zeinab Tarhini, Hugo Célèrier, Simon Ageorges, Préscillia Alves Gomes, Achille Tchalla, Édouard Desvaux, Jérémy Jost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to identify patient-related factors associated with attitudes toward deprescribing and to explore perceived barriers and facilitators among community-dwelling adults.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted in 60 community pharmacies across the Haute-Vienne and Dordogne regions of France. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 55 years, with at least one chronic condition and taking five or more chronic medications. Pharmacies were selected using a random cluster sampling method stratified by geographic area. Consecutively recruited patients completed an anonymous self-administered questionnaire assessing their attitudes toward deprescribing, perceived medication burden, and potential barriers or facilitators. Sociodemographic and clinical data were also collected. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with acceptance of deprescribing.ResultsAmong the 505 included patients (mean age 71.9 ± 9.0 years), 24.6% were reluctant to deprescribe. The main reasons for reluctance were the perceived effectiveness of their treatment (56.5%) and the fear of symptom recurrence (35.5%). Sleep disorders (odds ratio [OR] 0.51, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.33–0.78; p = 0.002) and urinary incontinence (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.96; p = 0.048) were significantly associated with refusal of deprescription. Perceived medication overload (OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.12–2.95; p = 0.015) and restrictive treatment burden (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.07–2.89; p = 0.026) were significantly associated with acceptance of deprescription.ConclusionAttitudes toward deprescribing were positive among community-dwelling adults. Medication burden was associated with acceptance, whereas sleep disorders and urinary incontinence were associated with reluctance. Patient perceptions and clinical conditions should be considered when implementing deprescribing interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-026-01298-6.

Key Points

Attitudes toward deprescribing were positive among community-dwelling adults with polypharmacy.Medication burden was associated with acceptance of deprescribing, while certain clinical conditions were associated with reluctance.Considering patient perceptions and concerns may be important in shared decision-making regarding deprescribing.

Introduction

The prevalence of polypathology increases with age and often requires multiple medications, commonly defined as polypharmacy. This situation raises the risk of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), drug–drug interactions, and prescribing cascades, where side effects are misinterpreted as new conditions [1–4]. Psychotropic medications, including benzodiazepines, antidepressants, and Z-drugs, are frequent contributors to adverse events in older adults.

Polypharmacy increases the likelihood of ADRs by 12–18% for each additional medication, and is implicated in up to 25% of hospital admissions and 10% of emergency department visits [5]. In geriatric care, as many as 75% of ADR-related hospitalizations are considered potentially preventable [6]. In France, recent data show that 46.7% of individuals aged 75 years and older are exposed to polypharmacy (defined as 5–9 medications) and 25.2% to hyper-polypharmacy (≥ 10 medications) [7]. With the proportion of older adults aged 75 + years projected to double by 2070, reducing medication-related harm has become a public health priority [8].

Deprescribing, a supervised, patient-centered process of discontinuing inappropriate medications, has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate the risks associated with polypharmacy [9]. It has been shown to improve clinical outcomes, reduce hospitalizations, and lower healthcare costs [10, 11]. However, successful deprescribing depends not only on clinical judgment but also on patient engagement and attitudes toward deprescribing. Barriers such as fear of symptom recurrence, medication dependence, and mistrust in prescribers have been widely reported [12–14].

Several studies conducted in hospital and primary care settings have reported that a large majority of patients are willing to consider deprescribing when recommended by their physician [14–17]. However, willingness appears to vary depending on medication classes, with lower acceptance reported for drugs associated with symptomatic relief or dependence (e.g., benzodiazepines) and higher acceptance for preventive treatments such as statins or antihypertensives. Despite these findings, existing studies remain heterogeneous and often focus on specific populations or settings, limiting the generalizability of results. To our knowledge, few studies have explored patient-related determinants of attitudes to deprescribe across multiple therapeutic classes in community pharmacy settings.

Community pharmacies represent a key point of contact within the healthcare system, particularly for community-dwelling adults receiving long-term treatments. Pharmacists are increasingly involved in medication reviews, identification of drug-related problems, and collaboration with physicians to optimize pharmacotherapy. As such, they are well positioned to identify barriers and facilitators to deprescribing in routine care. However, data on patients’ attitudes toward deprescribing in this setting remain scarce.

A preventive approach targeting middle-aged adults may also be relevant, as early identification of potentially inappropriate long-term treatments (e.g., prolonged proton pump inhibitor use) may help limit future iatrogenic risks and medication burden.

In this context, a better understanding of patient-related determinants of attitudes toward deprescribing may help identify individuals more likely to engage in deprescribing discussions and support more targeted interventions in routine care.

This study aimed to identify patient-related factors associated with attitudes toward deprescribing and to explore perceived barriers and facilitators among community-dwelling adults.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a cross-sectional study in 60 community pharmacies from two French departments (Haute-Vienne and Dordogne) between 25 March and 16 August 2024. The Dordogne and Haute-Vienne are two departments located in South-Western France. In 2021, Dordogne had a population of 413,730, with 13.8% aged 75 years or older, while Haute-Vienne counted 371,691 inhabitants with a comparable proportion of older adults, reflecting a pronounced demographic aging in these predominantly rural areas [18–20].

To ensure territorial representativeness, each department was stratified into three demographic areas (urban, semi-urban, rural) based on population size, population density, and the presence of a hospital. Participating pharmacies were selected within these areas.

Patients were approached during routine dispensing visits and invited to complete an anonymous, self-administered questionnaire. Individual informed consent was obtained from each patient. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of a University Hospital (No 471-2021-127).

Participants

Eligible participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 55 years, treated for at least one chronic disease and receiving five or more chronic medications. Patients with cognitive impairment, inability to understand or complete the questionnaire, institutionalization, or who declined participation were excluded. Cognitive impairment was not formally assessed using a standardized tool. Exclusion was based on the pharmacist’s clinical judgment, particularly the patient’s ability to understand the study information and complete the questionnaire independently.

Patients were recruited during routine pharmacy visits. All eligible patients presenting to the selected pharmacies to collect their chronic medications during the study period were invited to participate by the community pharmacist. Eligibility was assessed by the pharmacist at the time of dispensing, based on available information such as age, number of chronic medications, and known medical history when applicable.

Sample Size Calculation

A sample size calculation was performed to determine the required number of patients. Based on published data indicating that 38% of adults are reluctant to deprescribe, and assuming a 5% alpha risk with a 5% margin of error, the minimum sample size needed was estimated at 362 participants [15].

Sampling Method

A stratified cluster sampling design was used, in which each cluster corresponds to a community pharmacy. Within each department, three demographic areas were defined based on population size, density per square kilometer (km2), and the existence of a hospital in the area. Urban areas consisted of towns with a referral hospital and a population density >200 inhabitants/km2, semi-urban areas were defined as towns with a hospital and a population density between 60 inhabitants/km2 and 199 inhabitants/km2, and rural area were towns with a population density < 59 inhabitants/km2 and no hospital within the area.

In each demographic area, 10 community pharmacies were randomly selected from the national registry of community pharmacies, resulting in 30 pharmacies per department (60 in total). Participation agreements were obtained from pharmacy owners before inclusion. Participating pharmacists received standardized instructions and support to ensure consistent patient recruitment and questionnaire administration. Participants were recruited in community pharmacies during routine visits by community pharmacists.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a structured, anonymous, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review and reviewed by a multidisciplinary team including clinical pharmacists, a geriatrician, and an internal medicine physician.

The questionnaire consisted of 15 closed-ended items covering socio-demographic characteristics, clinical history, and current medication use. It also included items assessing medication adherence, attitudes toward deprescribing (classified as positive or negative), predefined reasons underlying these attitudes, and the identification of specific medications that participants reported they would consider stopping.

Medication adherence was assessed using the Girerd questionnaire, a validated 6-item self-reported scale with dichotomous (yes/no) responses [21].

Items related to deprescribing were specifically developed for this study based on a literature review conducted in Medline/PubMed, which identified commonly targeted medication classes as well as patient-related barriers and facilitators to deprescribing [9, 12, 13, 16, 22–26]. The following keywords were used: "deprescription", "ceasing prescribing", "stop prescribing", "inappropriate prescription", "patient barriers to deprescribing", and "deprescribing barriers".

The questionnaire was pre-tested in a small sample of ten participants to assess clarity, comprehension of the items, and completion time. Average completion time was approximately 10 minutes.

The questionnaire was distributed by email or hand-delivered to all primary care pharmacies included in this study. Patients could complete the questionnaire at the pharmacy counter during dispensing, at home and return it later, or with pharmacist support in a confidential counselling area. Completed questionnaires were placed by participants into sealed envelopes to ensure confidentiality and were later opened and analyzed by an independent investigator.

Collected Variables

The questionnaire included closed-ended items with the exception of age and collected data on several domains. Demographic characteristics included age and sex (male/female). Health-related variables comprised self-reported comorbidities categorized according to predefined conditions (cardiovascular disease, diabetes, mood or anxiety disorders, hypertension, gastrointestinal disorders, sleep disorders, hypercholesterolemia, constipation, urinary incontinence), the number of chronic medications, the type of medications classified by therapeutic class (antihypertensive agents, beta-blockers, diuretics, lipid-lowering agents, antidiabetic agents, proton pump inhibitors, laxatives, antidepressants, anxiolytics, hypnotics, medications for urinary incontinence), and history of falls (yes/no). History of falls was assessed by asking participants whether they had fallen in the past 12 months (yes/no).

Medication adherence was assessed using the Girerd questionnaire [21]. Adherence was categorized as good (0 positive responses), minor non-adherence (1–2 positive responses), or poor adherence (≥ 3 positive responses). Knowledge of chronic medications was assessed using a self-reported item evaluating participants’ awareness of the indication of their treatments, categorized as all, most, some, or none.

Attitudes toward deprescribing were assessed using a hypothetical scenario in which participants were asked whether they would agree to stop one of their regular medications if recommended by their physician (yes/no). Attitudes were classified as positive or negative based on this response. Participants were then asked to select one or more predefined reasons supporting their answer, distinguishing between perceived barriers and facilitators to deprescribing. Finally, participants were asked to indicate one or more medications they would consider stopping, which were recorded according to their therapeutic class.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons in univariate analyses were tested using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Quantitative variables were assessed for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test; normally distributed variables were compared using Student’s t test, otherwise the Mann–Whitney U test. ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons across more than two groups.

A multivariate backward stepwise logistic regression model was applied to identify variables associated with positive attitude toward deprescribing (yes/no). Variables with p value < 0.25 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariate model as a screening criterion, in line with commonly accepted practices, and not as a threshold for statistical significance. Clinically relevant variables identified in the literature were retained regardless of statistical significance. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors.

Data entry was performed using Excel 2016, and statistical analyses were conducted with Jamovi version 2.6.44.0.

The distribution of patients across the demographic groups was relatively balanced: 25.5% resided in urban areas, 38.6% in semi-urban areas, and 35.9% in rural areas (Table 1).

Patients had an average of three comorbidities from the list provided in the questionnaire. The most common medical conditions were hypertension (65.7%), hypercholesterolemia (50.5%), and heart disease (42.0%). The most frequently prescribed drugs were antihypertensives (69.7%) and statins (52.9%). Most patients were taking five chronic medications (38.4%).

Self-reported medication adherence was high (93.3%). Most patients reported knowing the indication for all their medications (65.0%). However, 41.6% felt that they were taking too many medications, and 37.4% found their chronic treatments restrictive. Additionally, 24.6% of patients were resistant to deprescribing (Table 1). However, among these patients, 50.0% indicated that they would consider stopping at least one chronic medication under specific conditions. Specifically, 25.7% of patients reported that they would accept discontinuation of lipid-lowering medications, 15.4% of hypnotics, and 14.7% of antihypertensive drugs if recommended by their general practitioner.

Subgroup analysis comparing participants who accepted versus those who refused deprescribing revealed several significant differences. Refusal to deprescribe was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of sleep disorders (p = 0.003) and constipation (p = 0.025), as well as increased use of laxatives (p = 0.022). Male sex was positively associated with acceptance of deprescribing (p = 0.025). Acceptance was also more frequent among patients with hypercholesterolemia (p = 0.028) and those receiving antidiabetic therapy (p = 0.018). Moreover, participants who accepted deprescribing were more likely to report perceptions of excessive medication burden (p = 0.001) and treatment-related constraints (p < 0.001), in contrast to those who declined (Table 1).

In a subgroup analysis by age, subjects aged over 75 years had more comorbidities than younger patients (p = 0.004). Heart diseases (p < 0.001), urinary incontinence (p < 0.001), and constipation (p = 0.009) were more prevalent in the older group. These individuals were more likely to be treated with beta-blockers (p = 0.022), loop diuretics (p = 0.004), laxatives (p = 0.005), and urinary antispasmodics (p = 0.012) and were at greater risk of falls (p < 0.001). Participants under 75 years of age were more likely to be taking five drugs, compared with more than nine for those aged > 75 years (p < 0.001). Additionally, participants > 75 years were less familiar with their treatments than younger participants (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Facilitators and Barriers to Deprescribing

The main factors facilitating deprescribing among participants were the perception that their medication was unnecessary (25.2%), the desire for their doctor to reduce the number of medications taken (29.7%), and a high level of trust in their general practitioner (29.1%).

Conversely, the main barriers to deprescribing included belief in the effectiveness of the medication (56.5%), fear of becoming ill again after stopping it (35.5%), and previous negative experiences related to discontinuing a treatment (15.3%).

Discussion

The patients included in the study had a mean age of 71.9 years (± 9.0 years) and a history of cardiovascular conditions, including hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and cardiovascular diseases. This cohort reflects the general French population, where cardiovascular disease remains highly prevalent and the second leading cause of mortality [27]. Age-stratified analysis revealed that individuals aged 75 years and older had a higher burden of comorbidities, such as constipation, urinary incontinence, and cardiovascular disease, were at greater risk of falls, used more medications, and had less knowledge about their treatment regimens. This profile was also more frequently observed in rural areas. Self-reported adherence was high (93.3%) but may have been overestimated due to social desirability and recall biases.

In our study, 75.4% of patients expressed a positive attitude toward deprescribing. Reeve et al. reported that over 90.0% of individuals had a positive attitude toward stopping medications if advised by their physician [17]. Age did not appear to influence this perception, with 89.0% of older adults expressing a positive attitude toward discontinuing medication. However, real-world acceptance is often lower. Two studies involving structured deprescribing with informed patient consent reported divergent results: 82% in Israel and 33% in the United States [28, 29]. Several patient-related factors may contribute to this variability, including concerns about symptom relapse, fear of deterioration, and perceived medication necessity. Medication type is also crucial. The study with the lowest acceptance rate highlighted resistance to deprescribing benzodiazepines and opioids likely due to dependence. In a study on long-term benzodiazepine use, fewer than 20.0% of participants were able to reduce their dosage [30]. Conversely, acceptance rates for discontinuing antihypertensive medications reached up to 80.0% [31]. Proton pump inhibitors are also frequently identified as candidates for deprescribing. However, patients are often reluctant to discontinue them, and their long-term use can be difficult to address in routine clinical practice.

Our findings were consistent: the highest proportions of patients reporting a positive attitude toward deprescribing concerned lipid-lowering agents (25.7%), hypnotics (15.4%), and antihypertensives (14.7%). Interestingly, statins were more frequently accepted for deprescribing than antihypertensives, possibly reflecting the sociocultural context in France, where skepticism toward statins, especially in primary prevention, is widespread. Studies have shown that patients often distrust statins, citing concerns about side effects or questioning their necessity [32]. Public debate and media coverage may have reinforced this perception [33]. Long-term use of antidiabetics (p = 0.018) and laxatives (p = 0.022) were also associated with a more positive attitude toward deprescribing. Among factors associated with deprescribing, sleep disturbances (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.33–0.78; p = 0.02) and urinary incontinence (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.34–0.96; p = 0.048) were significantly associated with a refusal to deprescribe. Conversely, feeling overmedicated (OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.12–2.95; p = 0.015) and perceiving treatment as restrictive (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.07–2.89; p = 0.026) were significantly associated with acceptance of deprescribing. This finding may reflect a greater acceptance of deprescribing medications prescribed for asymptomatic conditions, such as hypercholesterolemia or diabetes, compared with treatments for symptomatic conditions (e.g., sleep disorders or urinary incontinence), which have a more immediate and direct impact on daily quality of life.

Facilitators of deprescribing included patients’ feeling of being over-medicated (p = 0.001) and feeling burdened by treatment (p < 0.001). Trust in the GP was the most frequently cited reason for acceptance, consistent with existing literature emphasizing the GP’s pivotal role in therapeutic decision making. Studies have shown that deprescribing interventions are more effective when GPs are directly involved [34, 35]. Conversely, a study in Quebec found that older patients were less inclined to accept deprescribing when led primarily by pharmacists [14].

Although this study was conducted in a rural French setting, the structure of primary care, with general practitioners and community pharmacists playing central roles, reflects many international care models. Furthermore, key determinants of deprescribing acceptance, including trust in healthcare providers, perceived medication burden, and symptom control needs, are likely consistent across diverse cultural and health systems contexts. These considerations support the broader applicability of our findings.

In France, community pharmacists play an active role in medication management through structured services such as medication reviews and preventive care consultations. They contribute to identifying drug-related problems, including iatrogenesis, and can identify situations where deprescribing may be appropriate, working in collaboration with general practitioners to support therapeutic adaptations. Although this role was not specifically assessed in our study, it represents an important component of primary care that may influence deprescribing practices in routine settings.

These findings align with a recent overview of 48 systematic reviews assessing deprescribing interventions in older adults, which concluded that these strategies generally reduce medication burden and are well tolerated, although their impact on clinical outcomes such as hospitalization or mortality remains uncertain due to methodological heterogeneity [36]. The success of deprescribing depends on the healthcare setting, personalization, shared decision-making, and interprofessional collaboration. Structured, patient-centered approaches supported by multidisciplinary teams yield the highest acceptance. This study adds to existing literature by providing a large, community-based evaluation of deprescribing attitudes among older adults living independently and receiving primary care. Unlike prior research mostly focused on hospitals or institutions, this work explores patient perspectives in real-world ambulatory care, where most chronic prescriptions are initiated and maintained.

Taken together, our results highlight the importance of structured approaches to deprescribing that take into account patient perceptions and treatment burden. Deprescribing should evolve from an occasional intervention into a systematic, anticipatory process embedded in routine care, particularly during care transitions. Institutional protocols should promote regular medication reviews, backed by healthcare professional training in pharmacotherapy, overprescribing, deprescribing, and benefit–risk reassessment. Interprofessional collaboration, especially involving clinical pharmacists, is critical to ensure safe implementation. Digital decision-support tools integrated into electronic health records may facilitate real-time deprescribing opportunities. Transparent communication and shared therapeutic goals with patients and caregivers are also essential to improve adherence and engagement. Finally, deprescribing offers ecological benefits by reducing pharmaceutical waste and the environmental impact of drug production and disposal, an argument that may further support patient acceptance.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations. First, responses were self-reported and anonymous, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Second, although patients were recruited consecutively, we did not document the number of individuals who declined participation, nor their reasons (e.g., lack of time, disinterest, perceived irrelevance). As a result, the response rate could not be calculated. This limits our ability to assess how representative respondents were of the broader pharmacy-attending population. The absence of these data also reinforces a likely selection bias, as individuals more open to discussing medication-related issues may have been more inclined to participate, potentially inflating the observed positive attitudes toward deprescribing. However, the lack of direct interaction with the research team likely minimized the risk of a Hawthorne effect, as participants were not influenced by the presence or expectations of investigators during questionnaire completion. Third, this study assessed attitudes toward deprescribing in a hypothetical context, without reference to specific clinical situations or individualized therapeutic proposals. As such, participants’ responses may differ from actual decisions made in real-life clinical settings, where the perceived benefits and risks of discontinuing a specific medication are more concrete. Fourth, the questionnaire focused exclusively on patients’ perspectives and did not assess potential differences in acceptance of deprescribing according to the healthcare professional involved (e.g., general practitioner versus pharmacist). Given that the study was conducted in community pharmacies, this limits our ability to explore the specific contribution of pharmacists in shaping patients’ attitudes toward deprescribing. Fifth, certain medication classes known to be inappropriate for older adults, such as NSAIDs, were not captured. Laxative use may also have been reported irrespective of whether it reflected continuous treatment (e.g., opioid-induced constipation) or occasional symptomatic use. Sixth, sleep disorders were not formally defined for participants, which may have resulted in some misclassification between physiological sleep changes and pathological sleep disorders. Finally, the study was primarily designed to ensure inclusion of an adequate number of participants for robust analysis, rather than to estimate a participation rate.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It included a large sample of community-dwelling older adults, allowing robust quantitative analysis. The inclusion of a substantial proportion of participants aged 75 years and older, often underrepresented in similar studies, enhances the clinical relevance of the findings. Additionally, it offers valuable insights into patients’ perceptions of deprescribing in French primary care, where such data are limited. Finally, the structured assessment of patient-reported barriers and facilitators also provides a useful basis for developing targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Identifying patient characteristics associated with a more positive attitude toward deprescribing may help healthcare professionals better target individuals who are more likely to engage in deprescribing discussions. This is particularly relevant during structured medication reviews, where prioritizing patients who are more receptive could facilitate implementation and improve the efficiency of deprescribing interventions.
