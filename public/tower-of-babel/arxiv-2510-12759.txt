Rate of convergence to equilibrium for the heated string
Tomasz Cieślak, Jacek Jendrej, Christian Stinner

Abstract
. In the present manuscript, we calculate the exponential rate of convergence of the heated string system (a mixed-type hyperbolic-parabolic system of PDEs) towards the equilibrium, independently of the initial data. As a by-product of our analysis, we obtain an enhanced time decay of the solution. The main tool of our reasoning consists of asymptotic analysis at the Fourier side of the linearized problem in the spirit of Kato. The latter method fits very well to the estimates obtained earlier for the system in [ 2 ] . The matching between the linear problem and the nonlinear original system requires delicate estimates of the nonlinear terms at the Fourier side as well as careful spectral analysis.

1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the asymptotic studies of the mechanics of the heated string. The model we consider to describe the heated string is a 1d thermoelasticity system of PDEs given by the most natural (and simplest) Helmholtz free energy:
\theta\log\theta-\theta+\mu\theta u_{x}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}
.
Under such a choice of the Helmholtz free energy, also picking up the Fick (Fourier) law, one arrives at the hyperbolic-parabolic mixed type system of PDEs describing the phenomenon, the details can be found for instance in
[
9
]
.

To be more precise, we study the problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-u_{xx}=\mu\theta_{x},&\mbox{in }(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi),\\
\theta_{t}-\theta_{xx}=\mu\theta u_{tx},&\mbox{in }(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi),\\
u(\cdot,0)=u(\cdot,\pi)=\theta_{x}(\cdot,0)=\theta_{x}(\cdot,\pi)=0,&\mbox{in }(0,\infty),\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\;u_{t}(0,\cdot)=v_{0},\;\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}>0,\end{cases}

where
u(t,x)
denotes the position of a string fixed at the ends and
\theta(t,x)
is its temperature. Moreover,
v_{0}
is the
initial velocity and
\mu
a material constant. W.l.o.g. we restrict ourselves to the spatial interval
(0,\pi)
, which seems to
be appropriate for Fourier expansion. We study the problem under the following regularity assumptions on initial data:

(1.2)

u_{0}\in H^{2}(0,\pi)\cap H^{1}_{0}(0,\pi),\;v_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(0,\pi),\;\theta_{0}\in H^{1}(0,\pi).

Under such a regularity of initial data, it is known that the unique solution to (
1.1
) exists for any time
t>0
, see
[
1
]
.
Moreover, in
[
2
]
it was shown that for any initial data in the regularity class (
1.2
) (plus the positivity of initial temperature), the solution converges as time goes to infinity to the flat string with uniformly distributed temperature. In the present paper we study the rate of the latter convergence. As a by-product, we obtain the time decay enhancement for the solution to (
1.1
). Our main theorem reads

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(u,\theta)
be the solution to (
1.1
) for initial data satisfying the regularity assumptions (
1.2
), with positive initial temperature
\theta_{0}
. Assume
s\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
. Then,
u
converges to
0
in
H^{1+s}(0,\pi)
,
(u_{t},\theta)
converges to
(0,\theta_{\infty})
in
H^{s}(0,\pi)
, and all the convergences are exponential in time, where the number
\theta_{\infty}
is the constant that will be defined in (
2.2
) in Section
2
.

The exponential decay results from the coupling between the wave component and the heat component at the linear level.
We will show that, at high Fourier modes, the coupling induces an effect analogous to including a standard damping term in a wave equation, which yields exponential decay at uniform rate of all Fourier modes (hence no gain of regularity).

Let us comment on the proof. Our method is an asymptotic analysis type calculation (in the spirit of the Kato book
[
4
]
) on the Fourier side.
We make use of the convergence of the solution to the equilibrium in
H^{s}(0,\pi)
for
s\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
, which is a consequence of the convergence result in
[
2
]
, the precise statement is given in Section
2
. The Fourier expansion of the problem (
1.1
) is given in Section
3
. Next, in Section
4
, we analyze the spectral properties of the linearization of (
1.1
) at the equilibrium. Moreover, the first steps of the argument matching the linear estimates and the original nonlinear problem are taken (it requires the estimates of the exact order of convergence of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the linearized system), some linear algebra methods, including the Gershgorin circles to analyze the eigenvalues, are used. Having prepared the framework for the matching between the linear and the original problem, we furnish it with the estimates (the time-dependent variant of weighted
l_{2}
estimates of sequences of Fourier expansions) in Section
5
. Finally, our asymptotic construction closes up in Section
6
in a proper fixed point Banach contraction argument. In Section
7
, we use the previous construction to prove the main theorem.

Finally, let us compare our result to the already existing in the literature. It was Slemrod
[
9
]
, who first considered the nonlinear 1d thermoelasticity model. However, the system of PDEs studied in
[
9
]
does not cover (
1.1
). Though, seemingly more general, due to the considered assumptions, the system in
[
9
]
does not allow the nonlinear term in the heat equation to depend on
\theta
in a way, which would allow the positivity of
\theta
. Moreover, under the assumptions in
[
9
]
, the second principle of thermodynamics does not hold (unlike in our system, see
[
2
]
, where the quantitative version of the second principle of thermodynamics is used in the proof of convergence of the solution towards equilibrium). Still, under his restrictive assumptions, Slemrod shows local in time existence of solutions. Next, he also shows, that when starting close to the equilibrium, solutions exist for any time and converge to the equilibrium.

The articles
[
3
]
and
[
8
]
address the system of PDEs very similar to the one studied in
[
9
]
. Again, the structural assumptions exclude applicability to (
1.1
) (or a similar system of PDEs satisfying positivity of temperature and second principle of thermodynamics). But, in those restrictive models, additional information on the rate of convergence to equilibria is obtained, still, only for initial data starting close to equilibrium. In
[
8
]
, the polynomial rate of convergence for the Dirichlet problem in both, temperature and displacement, is obtained. In
[
3
]
, the exponential rate holds, this time for the Neumann problem in temperature. Again, the initial data need to be close to equilibrium in quite a restrictive regularity class. And, as we already mentioned, the system studied in
[
3
]
does not overlap with our system (
1.1
). It is also important to mention the work
[
6
]
of Muñoz Rivera, where the linearization of our system, however, under Dirichlet data, is studied. The exponential rate then follows (but the problem is then much simpler and the smart use of an energy method gives the desired rate of convergence). To summarize, we faced a completely new and quite challenging problem. We had to employ a refined asymptotic analysis technique, including the perturbation of linear unbounded operators and match it with the non-trivial estimate obtained in
[
2
]
.

Interpolating between (

Assume that initial data

be the unique solution to (

(u_{x},u_{t},\theta)

The above enhancement of the convergence result in
[

is crucial for our estimates, as will be seen in our proof, where we require the convergence to hold in

,

Notice that as a straightforward consequence, we see that

(2.4)

\int_{0}^{\pi}\theta(t,x)dx\rightarrow\pi\theta_{\infty}\mbox{ as }t\to\infty,

2.2
).

At the end of this section, let us comment on one of the consequences of our main Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

The whole energy

, defined in (

3.
Fourier expansion

In view of the boundary conditions in (
1.1
) we use the Fourier expansion

(3.1)

u(t,x)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{n}(t)\sin(nx),\qquad\theta(t,x)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\cos(nx),

for
(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,\pi]
. Inserting this into (
1.1
) and using the Cauchy product of series, we get for

(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=u_{tt}-u_{xx}-\mu\theta_{x}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin(nx)\left(\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{u}_{n}(t)+\mu n\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\right),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\theta_{xx}-\mu\theta u_{tx}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\mu\left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\cos(nx)\right)\left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}n\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\cos(nx)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\mu\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{n-k}(t)\cos((n-k)x)k\hat{u}_{k}^{\prime}(t)\cos(kx).

In view of

\cos(y\pm z)=\cos(y)\cos(z)\mp\sin(y)\sin(z),

we have

\cos(y)\cos(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(y+z)+\cos(y-z)\right),\quad y,z\in\mathbb{R}.

Inserting this into the previous identity, rearranging the terms, and using that
\cos
is even, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{n-k}(t)k\hat{u}_{k}^{\prime}(t)\big(\cos(nx)+\cos((n-2k)x)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)-\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{n-k}(t)k\hat{u}_{k}^{\prime}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg(\cos(0x)\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l}(t)l\hat{u}_{l}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left[\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l+n}(t)l\hat{u}_{l}^{\prime}(t)+\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l}(t)(l+n)\hat{u}_{l+n}^{\prime}(t)\right]\bigg).

Altogether, as
\cos(nx)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
\sin(nx)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are linearly independent, we obtain the system of ODEs

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)=-n^{2}\hat{u}_{n}(t)-\mu n\hat{\theta}_{n}(t),\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N},\\
\hat{\theta}_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l}(t)l\hat{u}_{l}^{\prime}(t),\quad t\in(0,\infty),\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)=-n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\hat{\theta}_{n-k}(t)k\hat{u}_{k}^{\prime}(t)+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l+n}(t)l\hat{u}_{l}^{\prime}(t)\\
\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l}(t)(l+n)\hat{u}_{l+n}^{\prime}(t),\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

In particular, we note that when time goes to
\infty
,

(3.3)

\pi\hat{\theta}_{0}(t)\rightarrow E(0),\quad\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\rightarrow 0,\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, this is a consequence of (
2.4
) and (
2.2
).

4.
Perturbation for linear system

We write
\theta=a+\psi
for some constant
a\in(0,\infty)
and linearize (
1.1
) to obtain

(4.1)

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-u_{xx}=\mu\psi_{x},&\mbox{in }(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi),\\
\psi_{t}-\psi_{xx}=\mu au_{tx},&\mbox{in }(0,\infty)\times(0,\pi).\end{cases}

Using Fourier expansion, in view of (
3.1
) and
\theta=a+\psi
we have

(4.2)

\psi(t,x)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\psi}_{n}(t)\cos(nx)=\hat{\psi}_{0}(t)+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)\cos(nx),\;\hat{\theta}_{0}=a+\hat{\psi}_{0}.

Inserting this and (
3.1
) into (
4.1
), we have

0=\psi_{t}-\psi_{xx}-\mu au_{tx}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\cos(nx)\left(\hat{\psi}_{n}^{\prime}(t)+n^{2}\hat{\psi}_{n}(t)-a\mu n\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\right).

As
\hat{\psi}_{n}=\hat{\theta}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for the first PDE of (
4.1
) we get again the first ODE in (
3.2
).
Hence, for this linearization we have the system of ODEs

(4.3)

\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t)=-n^{2}\hat{u}_{n}(t)-\mu n\hat{\theta}_{n}(t),\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N},\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\prime}(t)=-n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n}(t)+a\mu n\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}(t),\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

With

(4.4)

u_{t}(t,x)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\sin(nx)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{v}_{n}(t)\sin(nx)

we get the first order linear system

(4.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}n\hat{u}_{n}\\
\hat{v}_{n}\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=A_{n,a}\begin{pmatrix}n\hat{u}_{n}\\
\hat{v}_{n}\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N},

with

(4.6)

A_{n,a}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&n&0\\
-n&0&-\mu n\\
0&a\mu n&-n^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

With this notation, the nonlinear system (
3.2
) corresponds to the first order system

(4.7)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}n\hat{u}_{n}\\
\hat{v}_{n}\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=A_{n,a}\begin{pmatrix}n\hat{u}_{n}\\
\hat{v}_{n}\\
\hat{\theta}_{n}\end{pmatrix}+g_{n},\quad t\in(0,\infty),\;n\in\mathbb{N},

with

(4.8)

\begin{split}&g_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
g_{3,n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{where}\\
&g_{3,n}:=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\hat{\theta}_{n-k}k\hat{v}_{k}+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l+n}l\hat{v}_{l}\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\hat{\theta}_{l}(l+n)\hat{v}_{l+n}+\mu(\hat{\theta}_{0}-a)n\hat{v}_{n}.\end{split}

Next we study the linear system (
4.5
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a>0
, and
A_{n,a}
defined in (
4.6
). Then any eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of
A_{n,a}
satisfies

\mathrm{Re}\,(\lambda_{n})<0
.

Proof.

Allowing for later use also complex vectors, for
y:=(n\hat{u}_{n},\hat{v}_{n},\hat{\theta}_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, we define

E_{n}(y):=|n\hat{u}_{n}|^{2}+|\hat{v}_{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{a}|\hat{\theta}_{n}|^{2}.

Restricting to the case
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have
E_{n}\geq 0
and
E_{n}(y)=0
only for
y=0
as well as

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\nabla E_{n}(y)\cdot A_{n,a}y

\displaystyle=2n\hat{u}_{n}n\hat{v_{n}}+2\hat{v_{n}}(-n^{2}\hat{u}_{n}-\mu n\hat{\theta}_{n})+\frac{2}{a}\hat{\theta_{n}}(a\mu n\hat{v}_{n}-n^{2}\hat{\theta}_{n})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2n^{2}}{a}\hat{\theta}_{n}^{2}\leq 0.

Hence,
E_{n}
is a Liapunov function for (
4.5
) and thus
y=0
is stable for (
4.5
) and any eigenvalue

\lambda_{n}
of
A_{n,a}
satisfies
\mathrm{Re}\,(\lambda_{n})\leq 0
by
[
7
, Theorems 2 and 3 in Section 2.9]
.

Assume for contradiction that there is an eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of
A_{n,a}
such that
\mathrm{Re}\,(\lambda_{n})=0
. Then there is a
corresponding eigenvector
y\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
z(t):=e^{\lambda_{n}t}y
,
t\geq 0
, is a solution to (
4.5
). The
definition of
E_{n}
and
|e^{\lambda_{n}t}|=1
imply

E_{n}(z(t))=E_{n}(y)=E_{n}(\mathrm{Re}\,(y))+E_{n}(\mathrm{Im}\,(y)),\qquad t\geq 0,

and hence

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\dot{E}_{n}(\mathrm{Re}\,(y))+\dot{E}_{n}(\mathrm{Im}\,(y))=-\frac{2n^{2}}{a}\left(|\mathrm{Re}\,(\hat{\theta}_{n})|^{2}+|\mathrm{Im}\,(\hat{\theta}_{n})|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2n^{2}}{a}|\hat{\theta}_{n}|^{2}.

As
E_{n}(z(t))
is constant, we have

0=\frac{d}{dt}E_{n}(z(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\dot{E}_{n}(y)=-\frac{2n^{2}}{a}|\hat{\theta}_{n}|^{2},

and hence
\hat{\theta}_{n}=0
. In view of
A_{n,a}y=\lambda_{n}y
, the third component now implies
\hat{v}_{n}=0
and
then we get
\hat{u}_{n}=0
from the second component. But then
y=0
, a contradiction, as

is an eigenvector. Hence,
the claim is proved.

Remark 4.2
.

An alternative proof could be obtained by applying the Routh-Hurwitz criterion.

Our aim is to study projections of the solutions to (
4.7
) with respect to appropriate directions and start by
studying the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of

(4.9)

A_{n,a}^{\ast}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-n&0\\
n&0&a\mu n\\
0&-\mu n&-n^{2}\end{pmatrix}

for large
n
. In what follows, we shall use the enhanced version of the Gershgorin theorem (see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 4]
), stating that if the Gershgorin disks (that are defined below) divide into the disjoint unions of disks, then the eigenvalues (calculated with their multiplicities) must lie in the separated unions of Gershgorin disks. In our case, the matrix is
3\times 3
, and as we shall see, one of the Gershgorin disks is separated from the union of other two, for large enough
n
. Hence, one of eigenvalues will lie in the single separated disk, while the other two will lie in the separated double.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{j,n}
,
j=1,2,3
, of
A_{n,a}^{\ast}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, have the following properties.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a>0
, and
A_{n,a}^{\ast}
defined in (
4.9
). Then there are
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c>0
such that, for any

n\geq n_{0}
,
A_{n,a}^{\ast}
has three simple eigenvalues
\lambda_{j,n}
,
j=1,2,3
, and we have

|\lambda_{1,n}-(-n^{2}+a\mu^{2})|\leq\frac{c}{n^{2}}
,
|\lambda_{2,n}-(-ni-\frac{a\mu^{2}}{2})|\leq\frac{c}{n}
, and

|\lambda_{3,n}-(ni-\frac{a\mu^{2}}{2})|\leq\frac{c}{n}
.

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
A_{n,a}^{\ast}=(a_{kj})
and

D_{k,n}:=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\>:\>|z-a_{kk}|\leq\sum\limits_{j\neq k}|a_{kj}|\Big\},\quad k=1,2,3,

the Gershgorin disks. Then we have
D_{3,n}=\overline{B(-n^{2},\mu n)}
,
D_{1,n}=\overline{B(0,n)}
, and

D_{2,n}=\overline{B(0,n(1+a\mu))}
. Hence,
there is
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D_{3,n}\cap(D_{1,n}\cup D_{2,n})=\emptyset
for all
n\geq N_{1}
. Hence, by Gershgorin’s
theorem for any
n\geq N_{1}
exactly one eigenvalue
\lambda_{1,n}
of
A_{n,a}^{\ast}
is
contained in
D_{3,n}
, while the other two
eigenvalues
\lambda_{2,n}
and
\lambda_{3,n}
(complex conjugated if they are not in
\mathbb{R}
, as the matrix is of real
entries) are contained in
D_{1,n}\cup D_{2,n}
.

Assume for contradiction that there is
n\geq N_{1}
such that
\lambda_{2,n}\in\mathbb{R}
. As
\lambda_{2,n}\in D_{1,n}\cup D_{2,n}
, we have
|\lambda_{2,n}|\leq n(1+a\mu)
. The
characteristic polynomial of
A_{n,a}^{\ast}
is

\displaystyle p_{n}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det(A_{n,a}^{\ast}-\lambda I)=\det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&-n&0\\
n&-\lambda&a\mu n\\
0&-\mu n&-n^{2}-\lambda\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\lambda(-\lambda(-n^{2}-\lambda)+a\mu^{2}n^{2})-n(-n(-n^{2}-\lambda)-0)

\displaystyle=-n^{2}\lambda^{2}-\lambda^{3}-a\mu^{2}n^{2}\lambda-n^{4}-n^{2}\lambda

\displaystyle=-(\lambda^{3}+n^{2}\lambda^{2}+n^{2}(a\mu^{2}+1)\lambda+n^{4}).

In view of
\lambda_{2,n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
|\lambda_{2,n}|\leq n(1+a\mu)
, there is
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that for any
n\geq N_{2}

we have

p_{n}(\lambda_{2,n})\leq-n^{4}+n^{3}\left[(1+a\mu)^{3}+(a\mu^{2}+1)(1+a\mu)\right]\leq-\frac{n^{4}}{2}<0.

Hence, for
n\geq N_{2}
this is a contradiction and in that case we have

\displaystyle 0

5.
Estimates of nonlinear terms

Here we estimate the nonlinear terms appearing in (
4.8
) and (
4
) in some weighted
l^{2}
-spaces. These
estimates correspond, on the Fourier side, to the
H^{s}
estimates on the original variables and will be used in the next section to set up an appropriate fixed point argument.

We first recall Young’s inequality for the discrete convolution. For sequences
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and

\mathcal{B}=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
the discrete convolution is

(\mathcal{A}\ast\mathcal{B})_{n}=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n-k}b_{k},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then, we have Young’s inequality

\|\mathcal{A}\ast\mathcal{B}\|_{l^{r}}\leq\|\mathcal{A}\|_{l^{p}}\|\mathcal{B}\|_{l^{q}}\quad\mbox{for }p,q,r\in[1,\infty],1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}.

Setting
a_{n}=b_{n}=0
for
n\leq 0
, we get in particular

(5.1)

\begin{split}\left\|\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{n-k}b_{k}\Big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\|_{l^{r}}&\leq\|(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{l^{p}}\|(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{l^{q}}\\
&\mbox{for }p,q,r\in[1,\infty],1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}.\end{split}

Setting in addition
d_{l}=b_{-l}
,
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get for
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k=-l

\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}a_{l+n}b_{l}=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{-1}a_{n-k}b_{-k}=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{-1}a_{n-k}d_{k}=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n-k}d_{k}

and an application of Young’s inequality implies

(5.2)

\begin{split}\left\|\Big(\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}a_{l+n}b_{l}\Big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\|_{l^{r}}&\leq\|(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{l^{p}}\|(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|_{l^{q}}\\
&\mbox{for }p,q,r\in[1,\infty],1+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}.\end{split}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\sup\limits_{t>0}\int_{0}^{t}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\Big(|(a^{(1)}_{3,l}-a^{(2)}_{3,l})(\sigma)|l|a^{(1)}_{2,l}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+|a^{(2)}_{3,l}(\sigma)|l|(a^{(1)}_{2,l}-a^{(2)}_{2,l})(\sigma)|\Big)d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\sup\limits_{t>0}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\alpha\sigma}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\Big(l^{s}e^{\alpha\sigma}|(a^{(1)}_{3,l}-a^{(2)}_{3,l})(\sigma)|l^{s}e^{\alpha\sigma}|a^{(1)}_{2,l}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+l^{s}e^{\alpha\sigma}|a^{(2)}_{3,l}(\sigma)|l^{s}e^{\alpha\sigma}|(a^{(1)}_{2,l}-a^{(2)}_{2,l})(\sigma)|\Big)d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\alpha\sigma}d\sigma\;\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\bigg[\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\Big(l^{s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{\alpha t}|(a^{(1)}_{3,l}-a^{(2)}_{3,l})(t)|\Big)\Big(l^{s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{\alpha t}|a^{(1)}_{2,l}(t)|\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\Big(l^{s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{\alpha t}|a^{(2)}_{3,l}(t)|\Big)\Big(l^{s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{\alpha t}|(a^{(1)}_{2,l}-a^{(2)}_{2,l})(t)|\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{4\alpha}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|a^{(1)}_{2,n}(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|(a^{(1)}_{3,n}-a^{(2)}_{3,n})(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{\mu}{4\alpha}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|(a^{(1)}_{2,n}-a^{(2)}_{2,n})(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|a^{(2)}_{3,n}(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{4\alpha}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}c_{2}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|A^{(1)}_{j,n}(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}c_{2}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|(A^{(1)}_{j,n}-A^{(2)}_{j,n})(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{\mu}{4\alpha}\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}c_{2}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|A^{(2)}_{j,n}(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}c_{2}\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2s}\sup\limits_{t>0}e^{2\alpha t}|(A^{(1)}_{j,n}-A^{(2)}_{j,n})(t)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\mu c_{2}^{2}}{2\alpha}\,R\,\|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}-\mathcal{A}^{2}\|_{X}.

Hence, we have

(6.37)

\sup\limits_{t>0}|(\xi^{(1)}-\xi^{(2)})(t)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}-\mathcal{A}^{2}\|_{X}\quad\mbox{if }R\leq\frac{\alpha}{9\mu c_{2}^{2}}\,.

Combining this with (
6.33
), (
6.36
), and (
6.3
), we conclude that

(6.38)

\begin{split}&\|(\Psi^{(1)}_{1},\Psi^{(1)}_{2},\Psi^{(1)}_{3},\xi^{(1)})-(\Psi^{(2)}_{1},\Psi^{(2)}_{2},\Psi^{(2)}_{3},\xi^{(2)})\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}^{(1)}-\mathcal{A}^{2}\|_{X}\\
&\mbox{if }R\leq R_{0}:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{4(12c_{2}c_{3}+54c_{2}^{2}c_{3}c_{4})},\frac{1}{4(12c_{2}c_{5}+54c_{2}^{2}c_{5}c_{4})},\frac{\alpha}{9\mu c_{2}^{2}}\right\}.\end{split}

Hence,
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction on

with this choice of

has a unique fixed

by Banach’s fixed point theorem.
∎
