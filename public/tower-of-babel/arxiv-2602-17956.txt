Abstract.

We approach multivariate mode estimation through Gibbs distributions and introduce GERVE (Gibbs-measure Entropy-Regularised Variational Estimation), a likelihood-free framework that approximates Gibbs measures directly from samples by maximizing an entropy-regularised variational objective with natural-gradient updates. GERVE brings together kernel density estimation, mean-shift, variational inference, and annealing in a single platform for mode estimation. It fits Gaussian mixtures that concentrate on high-density regions and yields cluster assignments from responsibilities, with reduced sensitivity to the chosen number of components. We provide theory in two regimes: as the Gibbs temperature approaches zero, mixture components converge to population modes; at fixed temperature, maximisers of the empirical objective exist, are consistent, and are asymptotically normal. We also propose a bootstrap procedure for per-mode confidence ellipses and stability scores. Simulation and real-data studies show accurate mode recovery and emergent clustering, robust to mixture overspecification. GERVE is a practical likelihood-free approach when the number of modes or groups is unknown and full density estimation is impractical.

Keywords.

Mode estimation; Modal clustering; Variational methods; Gibbs measures; Annealing; Bootstrap.

Introduction

Understanding the structure of an unknown probability distribution
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, observed through samples, is a central task in statistics. Modes, the local maxima of
p
, reveal data high-density regions. Detecting multiple modes exposes heterogeneity, supports clustering and segmentation, and offers alternative explanations in inverse problems
(
Carreira-Perpiñan, 2006
;
Chacón, 2015
)
. Recently, it has also emerged as a preliminary requirement for new efficient sampling methods of multimodal high-dimensional distributions
(
Noble et al., 2025
)
.

Classical approaches to mode estimation are commonly grouped into three main families. The first comprises geometric methods that detect high-density regions from the sample
(
Chernoff, 1964
;
Dalenius, 1965
;
Venter, 1967
;
Sager, 1978
)
. These methods are simple and robust but tend to degrade in high dimensions. The second fits a smooth density and then locates its modes
(
Parzen, 1962
;
Loftsgaarden and Quesenberry, 1965
;
Kronmal and Tarter, 1968
;
Samworth, 2018
;
Liu and Ghosh, 2020
)
. This route is principled but suffers from smoothing bias and high computational cost. The third consists of mean-shift algorithms that ascend the gradient of a kernel density estimate (KDE) without requiring fitting a global model
(
Fukunaga and Hostetler, 1975
;
Comaniciu and Meer, 2002
;
Carreira-Perpiñan, 2007
;
Arias-Castro et al., 2016
)
. These methods are efficient but bandwidth sensitive and can miss well-separated modes. To our knowledge, no existing method simultaneously targets multiple density modes, provides valid uncertainty quantification, and scales with dimension via simple algorithmic updates, without requiring a full density estimate
(
Genovese et al., 2016
;
Ameijeiras-Alonso et al., 2021
)
.

We propose a different perspective: we recast the task of mode estimation into that of approximating a Gibbs measure from samples. Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function and fix a temperature parameter
\omega>0
. Define

g_{\omega}(d{\mathb{x}})=\frac{\exp(f({\mathb{x}})/\omega)}{Z_{\omega}(f)}d{\mathb{x}},\qquad\text{with~}Z_{\omega}(f):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\exp(f({\mathb{y}})/\omega)d{\mathb{y}},

and assume
Z_{\omega}(f)<\infty
. We assume that
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has finite Lebesgue measure. The Gibbs measure
g_{\omega}
preserves the modes of
f
and is the maximiser, among the unconstrained family of distributions, of the entropy-regularised variational objective
(
Donsker and Varadhan, 1976
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[f({\mathb{X}})\right]+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q),\qquad\text{with~}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q)=-\int_{\mathcal{S}}q({\mathb{y}})\log q({\mathb{y}})d{\mathb{y}}.

(1)

We introduce
GERVE
(
Gibbs-measure Entropy-Regularised Variational Estimation
), a likelihood-free variational framework that approximates the Gibbs measure associated with a density
f:=p
, in a Gaussian-mixture family from i.i.d. samples
{\mathb{X}}_{1:N}\sim p
, where
p
is nonnegative, supported on a subset of
\mathcal{S}
. Since
p
is a density, but not analytically available, we leverage the identity
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[p({\mathb{X}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[q({\mathb{X}})\right]
to target, with natural-gradient updates, the maximisation of an empirical version of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
averaging the variational density
q
over the observed samples:

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}(q)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q({\mathb{X}}_{i})+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q).

(2)

This estimation problem requires fundamentally different treatment from optimisation settings where
p
(or
f
) can be evaluated: we develop asymptotic theory for maximisers, establish consistency and asymptotic normality, and introduce bootstrap-based inference for per-mode uncertainty quantification, extending recent computational techniques from variational optimisation
(
Khan and Rue, 2023
;
Le Minh et al., 2025
)
to a statistical estimation methodology.

By fitting Gaussian mixtures to the Gibbs density, GERVE unifies ideas from mean-shift, kernel density estimation, entropy annealing, and variational inference within one algorithmic paradigm. The variational formulation is well-suited to multimodal and multivariate settings, where geometric or purely nonparametric mode estimators often struggle. The entropy term induces repulsion between mixture components, enabling exploration of the modal landscape, while annealing (
\omega\rightarrow 0
) concentrates mass at the global modes. This allows overcomplete mixtures to automatically adapt to the effective number of modes without prior specification.

Our main contributions are:

1.

Variational formulation for mode estimation.
We cast mode estimation as Gibbs-measure approximation from samples, enabling mode finding and uncertainty quantification when the number of modes is unknown and density estimation is impractical.

2.

Asymptotic theory for multimodal recovery.
We establish two complementary theoretical regimes: As
\omega\to 0
, the variational mixture concentrates on the population modes. At fixed
\omega>0
, empirical maximisers are consistent and asymptotically normal.

3.

Bootstrap uncertainty for modes.
We provide per-mode confidence ellipses and stability scores via a matched-mode bootstrap with theoretical guarantees.

4.

Algorithmic framework.
Natural-gradient updates with annealing recover multiple modes, estimate local curvature, and yield cluster assignments without pre-specifying the number of groups. A fixed-covariance special case recovers the mean-shift algorithm, while learned covariances adapt to anisotropy and reduce bandwidth sensitivity.

5.

Evidence on simulations and data.
We show accurate mode recovery and competitive clustering, robustness to mild overcompleteness, and a hotspot analysis with uncertainty on UK road-collision data.

Our approach is related to modal clustering
(
Menardi, 2016
;
Chacón, 2020
)
, which defines clusters as basins of attraction of density modes. GERVE uses variational approximation, yielding clusters that locally agree with basins near modes under mild conditions (Suppl.
S1.4
), while providing computational efficiency and natural uncertainty quantification.

2
Mode seeking with annealed Gaussian mixtures

For a measurable function
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, the associated Gibbs measure
g_{\omega}
preserves its modes.
The goal of finding the modes of
f
can then be turned into that of approximating
g_{\omega}
, which in turn can be achieved through a variational approximation principle.
The classical variational identity

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)=\omega\log Z_{\omega}(f)-\omega\mathrm{KL}\left(q\|g_{\omega}\right),

with
\mathrm{KL}\left(q\|g_{\omega}\right):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}q({\mathb{x}})\log(q({\mathb{x}})/g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}}))d{\mathb{x}}
,
implies that
g_{\omega}
uniquely maximises the objective
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
when
Z_{\omega}(f)<\infty

(
Donsker and Varadhan, 1976
)
. Restricting the search for
q
in a tractable variational family gives an approximation for
g_{\omega}
by minimisation of
\mathrm{KL}\left(q\|g_{\omega}\right)
.

As a natural choice for approximating multimodal landscapes, we study how Gaussian mixtures approximate
g_{\omega}
when
\omega
decreases to
0
. Since
g_{\omega}
is defined only on
\mathcal{S}
, admissible variational distributions are required to have support in
\mathcal{S}
. We therefore work with mixtures of truncated Gaussians. The next result, proved in Supplementary Material
S2.2
, describes how optimal mixtures concentrate on the global modes when the number of components
K
is at least the number of global modes
I
.

Theorem 2.1

(Gaussian mixture concentration on global modes)
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathcal{S})
and bounded on
\mathcal{S}
, and for each mode in
\{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\}_{i=1}^{I}
,
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{S})
and
{\mathb{H}}_{i}:=-\nabla^{2}f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\succ 0
. Consider
\mathcal{Q}
to be the variational family of Gaussian mixtures truncated on
\mathcal{S}
, with a fixed number of components
K\geq I
and upper-bounded covariances. Let
q_{\omega}^{\star}
denote any maximiser of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
in
\mathcal{Q}
. Choose disjoint neighborhoods
U_{1},\dots,U_{I}
of the modes and let
U_{0}:=S\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}U_{i}
.

Then, as
\omega\to 0
:

(i)

We have
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\omega}^{\star}\|g_{\omega})\rightarrow 0
. In particular,
q_{\omega}^{\star}(U_{0})\rightarrow 0
, and, for each neighborhood
U_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,I
,

q_{\omega}^{\star}(U_{i})\rightarrow c_{i},\qquad\text{where}\,\,c_{i}\propto(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{-1/2},\,\sum_{i=1}^{I}c_{i}=1.

(ii)

For all
i=1,\dots,I
, the conditional laws of
q_{\omega}^{\star}
and
g_{\omega}
on
U_{i}
satisfy
\mathrm{KL}\big(q_{\omega}^{\star,U_{i}}\|g_{\omega}^{U_{i}}\big)\rightarrow 0
. In particular, the mean and covariance of
q_{\omega}^{\star,U_{i}}
satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\omega}^{\star,U_{i}}}[{\mathb{X}}]={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}+o(1),\qquad\mathrm{Cov}_{q_{\omega}^{\star,U_{i}}}({\mathb{X}})=o(1).

This result shows that Gaussian mixtures provide accurate approximations of the Gibbs measure and place their mass on all global modes of
g_{\omega}
, thus of
f
, under annealing whenever
K\geq I
. The local shape of
g_{\omega}
at a particular mode
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
has been studied in Proposition 3 of
Le Minh et al., 2025
. There, the optimal Gaussian approximation in a neighborhood of
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
is of the form
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\omega},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\omega})
satisfying:

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\omega}={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}+o(\sqrt{\omega}),\qquad{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\omega}=\omega{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}+o(\omega).

These expansions clarify how Gibbs measures can be used for mode seeking. As
\omega
decreases, mixture components sharpen and their means drift toward the global modes of
f
. The temperature parameter governs the trade-off between exploration (through smooth and diffuse mixtures at large
\omega
) and exploitation (concentrated components at small
\omega
). A decreasing
\omega
schedule allows starting in an exploration regime and gradually refining mode localisation. We next introduce a statistical formulation in which
f
is unobserved and only accessible through samples drawn from it.

3
Sample-based mode estimation and modal clustering

We observe i.i.d. samples
{\mathb{X}}_{1:N}\sim p
supported on a bounded domain
\mathrm{supp}(p):=\{{\mathb{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:p({\mathb{x}})>0\}
and we choose
\mathcal{S}
with finite Lebesgue measure such that
\mathrm{supp}(p)\subset\mathcal{S}
. Replacing
f
with
p
, we distinguish the
population
and
empirical
objectives respectively referring to
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}(q)
defined by (
1
), with
f:=p
, and (
2
).

Instead of performing optimisation on truncated distributions on
\mathcal{S}
, GERVE maximises
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
over untruncated Gaussian mixture families on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
while all integrals in the objective are kept over
\mathcal{S}
. Supplementary Material
S1.2
discusses that if
\mathcal{S}
is taken large enough, then the objective gap between the truncated and untruncated distributions is uniformly small, and the truncated and untruncated maximisers coincide asymptotically under the
M
-estimation theory
(
van der Vaart, 1998
)
. This simplifies optimisation, as it removes parameter-dependent normalisers in the natural-gradient updates (details in Section
4.1
).

For a single Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, we consider the compact parameter set

\Theta:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}},-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}):\lVert{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\max},\sigma_{\min}^{2}{\mathb{I}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\preceq\sigma_{\max}^{2}{\mathb{I}}\Bigr\}.

(3)

Proposition
S1.1
shows that if the outside mass
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=1-\int_{\mathcal{S}}q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}
is uniformly small on
\Theta
, then the objective gap is uniformly small. The same reasoning applies component-wise to mixtures on the parameter set
\Theta_{K}

\Theta_{K}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{K-1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}):\lvert v_{k}\rvert\leq v_{\max},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\in\Theta\Bigr\},

(4)

with bounded logits
v_{k}:=\log(\pi_{k}/\pi_{K})
, which prevent degeneracies while preserving flexibility (Suppl.
S1.2
, eq. (
S3
)). In fact, throughout,
\Theta_{K}
shall be understood as its restriction to the closure
\overline{\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}}
of the lexicographically ordered parameter space

\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
(defined in Suppl.
S1.3
), that enforces a canonical labelling and thus provides sufficient
identifiability for our technical results.

In the multimodal optimisation setting, the main source of bias is due to the entropy-induced repulsion in mixtures. We deliberately exploit this repulsion early in training to mitigate mode collapse, a known issue in variational Gaussian mixtures
(
Wu et al., 2019
;
Jerfel et al., 2021
;
Soletskyi et al., 2025
)
. The induced bias progressively diminishes under annealing.

The following theorem establishes that GERVE remains statistically consistent at fixed
\omega=\omega_{0}>0
, as
N\to\infty
. An asymptotic normality result is also derived. These results are stated for Gaussian mixtures in
\Theta_{K}
(eq. (
4
)), but can be applied to single Gaussians in
\Theta
(eq. (
3
)) as a special case. The proof is provided in Supplementary Material
S2.3
.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymptotics of empirical maximisers)
.

At fixed
\omega_{0}>0
, if
p
is bounded and continuous on
\mathcal{S}
, then a population maximiser
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}
of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{0}}
exists on
\Theta_{K}
. If it is unique, then any empirical maximiser
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{\omega_{0},N}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{0},N}
satisfies, as
N\to\infty
:

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{\omega_{0},N}\xrightarrow{P}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}.

Furthermore, if
{\mathb{H}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}:=-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{0}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\succ 0
, then

\sqrt{N}\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\omega_{0},N}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\big)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{N}(0,{\mathb{W}}_{\omega_{0}}),

where
{\mathb{W}}_{\omega_{0}}:=\big({\mathb{H}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\star}\big)^{-1}{\mathb{V}}_{\omega_{0}}\big({\mathb{H}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\star}\big)^{-1}
, and
{\mathb{V}}_{\omega_{0}}:=\operatorname{Var}\big(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{X}})\big)\big|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}
.

Remark
.

maximiser uniqueness is subtle in mixture models. In particular, unless the parameter space is restricted so as to rule out degeneracies, a component with vanishing weight can have its associated mean and covariance drift without affecting the mixture density, and if several components share the same
({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
then redistributing weights within that tied group also leaves the density unchanged. Consequently, convergence at the parameter level cannot be guaranteed in general, even when the induced densities converge to a maximiser.
To formalise consistency without uniqueness, one can replace the uniqueness assumption in Theorem 3.1 by the weaker requirement that

\mathcal{D}_{\omega_{0}}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{0}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

is nonempty and compact. The set-valued argmax theorem
(
van der Vaart, 1998
, Cor. 5.58)
then yields

\mathrm{dist}\bigl(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{\omega_{0},N},\mathcal{D}_{\omega_{0}}\bigr)\xrightarrow{{P}}0,\qquad\mathrm{dist}({\mathb{x}},\mathcal{A})=\inf_{{\mathb{a}}\in\mathcal{A}}\|{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{a}}\|.

The asymptotic normality statement, however, holds only under local identifiability, for example
{\mathb{H}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\succ 0
, which rules out duplicated components at the maximiser.
In practice, non-uniqueness can be helpful: redundant components may be absorbed as some weights shrink or as component means coalesce near prominent modes, so that an overcomplete mixture adapts to the effective number of modes without the user pre-specifying
K
.

As demonstrated by Theorem
2.1
, optimal variational mixtures concentrate on the modes of
p
as
\omega\to 0
. With the consistency guaranties of Theorem
3.1
, GERVE therefore defines a mode estimator: Gaussian mixtures
q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}
simultaneously capture multiple modes under annealing.

Beyond mode estimation, GERVE naturally induces a clustering structure.
With possibly overcomplete mixtures, components are attracted toward distinct
modes as entropy decreases. Redundant components either vanish or collapse, so the
effective number of clusters adapts automatically. Unlike likelihood-based Gaussian mixtures clustering, GERVE targets density modes under annealing.

In practice, once fitted, cluster assignments are straightforward
via responsibilities (posterior component probabilities).
This connects to
modal clustering

(
Menardi, 2016
;
Chacón, 2020
)
, where clusters are attraction basins of density modes. Classical approaches rely on nonparametric density estimates and gradient flows, while GERVE uses a parametric variational approximation. The resulting clusters need not coincide exactly with attraction basins, but they agree locally near each mode under mild conditions, see Supplementary Material
S1.4
for a statement and a counterexample.
This yields adaptive modal clustering without tuning
K
. A caveat is that annealing emphasises dominant modes: secondary modes may disappear as
\omega\to 0
, so retaining them requires stopping at a positive temperature or imposing weight lower bounds.

GERVE algorithm and variants

4.1
Variational optimisation with natural gradients

The empirical objective
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
(eq. (
2
)) can be optimised over
\Theta
or
\Theta_{K}
using natural gradients
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}):={\mathb{F}}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
, which incorporates the geometry of the variational parameter space through the Fisher information matrix
{\mathb{F}}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
(
Amari, 1998
).
When
q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
belongs to an exponential family with sufficient statistic
{\mathb{T}}({\mathb{X}})
, the natural gradient equals the gradient in expectation parameters
{\mathb{M}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}[{\mathb{T}}({\mathb{X}})]
:

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\nabla_{\mathb{M}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}).

(5)

This identity allows computation of natural gradients without explicit estimation and inversion of the Fisher matrix. It also holds for untruncated Gaussian mixtures via the Minimal Conditional Exponential Family representation (MCEF;
Lin et al., 2019
).

1
describes natural gradient ascent on

with a temperature schedule

over iterations

. Convergence guarantees for stochastic natural gradient ascent are stated in Supplementary Material

: at fixed-

); with

, stationary sets can be tracked with slow schedules, i.e.
\lvert\omega_{t+1}-\omega_{t}\rvert=o(\rho_{t})
where

). In GERVE, a projection step can be added to ensure the updates remain in the compact space

Algorithm 1

GERVE (general form)

4.2
Variant A: Equivalence to Gaussian mean-shift

Consider the family of Gaussian distributions with a fixed isotropic covariance
\sigma^{2}{\mathb{I}}
,
\{q_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},\sigma^{2}{\mathb{I}}),{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. For a large enough
\mathcal{S}
, we have
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\approx 0
, so the natural-gradient step can be simplified into:

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}+\frac{\rho_{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t})q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{t}}({\mathb{X}}_{i}).

(6)

Using a batch
\{{\mathb{X}}^{(t)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
gives an unbiased estimator of the RHS (see Suppl.
S3.2
for the derivation).
Denoting by

p_{h}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi_{h}({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
the KDE with a Gaussian kernel
\varphi_{h}
with bandwidth
h
, the following result states that GERVE recovers the mean-shift algorithm when
h=\sigma^{2}
.

Proposition 4.1

(Equivalence to Gaussian mean-shift)
.

Update rule (
6
) performs gradient ascent on
p_{h}
, and normalising the step
\rho_{t}=p_{h}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{-1}
yields the classical mean-shift fixed-point update:

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi_{h}({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}){\mathb{X}}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{h}({\mathb{X}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t})}.

Viewing fixed-covariance GERVE as mean-shift puts us under classical KDE theory. Operationally,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
is a particle ascending the smoothed density
p_{h}
, and the (fixed) covariance
h{\mathb{I}}
is the bandwidth: large
h
leads to smooth and global but biased updates, while small
h
yields sharp but noisy updates, sensitive to sampling.

This regime is thus a well-understood nonparametric baseline that grounds richer GERVE variants. This can typically be used to set the covariance bounds in learned-covariance variants of GERVE. For example, if
p
is
\mathcal{C}^{3}
, the gradient of the Gaussian KDE has bias
O(h^{2})
and fluctuation
O_{p}\big((Nh^{d+2})^{-1/2}\big)
, yielding the optimal plug-in rate
h\asymp N^{-1/(d+6)}

(
Parzen, 1962
;
Romano, 1988
;
Tsybakov, 1990
;
Genovese et al., 2016
)
.

4.3
Variant B: Gaussian mixture GERVE

When considering Gaussian mixtures represented by
\Theta_{K}

q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1})

\displaystyle{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}

Learned covariances adapt to anisotropy and converge to positive limits at fixed temperature. As
\omega\rightarrow 0

\omega

\omega\to 0

4.4
Complexity and practical guidelines

With
T

B

K

S3.4

We now summarise practical guidance for hyperparameter selection. For the temperature schedule, the initial value of
\omega
should be large so that mixture components repel and cover
\mathcal{S}
. The temperature should then decrease to a target value

\omega^{\dagger}=0

\omega^{\dagger}=\omega_{0}

\omega_{0}>0

For stepsizes, a safe choice satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions

Compactness constraints require explicit bounds for the covariance parameters in

(eq. (

8
Conclusion

GERVE is a likelihood-free variational approach to mode estimation and modal clustering. It optimises an entropy-regularised objective with natural gradients under annealing, fitting mixtures that concentrate on high-density regions and induce clusters without density estimation. As temperature goes to zero, components align with population modes and inherit local curvature. At fixed temperature, empirical maximisers exist, are consistent, and satisfy a central limit theorem. A matched-mode bootstrap procedure yields per-mode confidence sets.

Empirically, GERVE recovers modal structure and adapts the effective number of groups as temperature decreases, with redundant components merging or vanishing. Diagonal-covariance variants preserve most of the behaviour at lower cost, and an ablation study shows that annealing, moderate component overspecification, and short burn-in phases are useful levers.

GERVE is most suitable when uncertainty in mode locations is important, when the cluster count is misspecified, or when modal clustering is preferable to partition-based grouping. For problems where the number of clusters is known and computational speed is paramount, established task-specific clustering algorithms remain preferable. Three limitations merit attention. First, modal clustering may misalign with the group structure that matters in an application. Modes define clusters by attraction basins, but scientific groupings may depend on other criteria, so agreement is not guaranteed. Second, the optimisation procedure can converge to local rather than global maxima. Careful initialisation, temperature schedules, and overcomplete mixtures reduce this risk but do not completely eliminate it. Third, computation can be costly with full covariance matrices in high dimensions. In our experiments, using diagonal covariances allows one to control the cost while preserving mode separation.

The theory and experimental results position GERVE as a principled statistical framework for likelihood-free modal inference. By unifying modal clustering, mean-shift, variational inference, and annealing, GERVE provides both algorithmic tools and theoretical guarantees within one platform. Looking ahead, three directions are natural. First, develop data-driven annealing schedules with principled stopping. Second, establish pathwise guarantees for empirical maximisers and for the validity of the bootstrap procedure under annealing. Third, characterise mode-component correspondence and label stability as temperature varies. Pursuing these directions will link the annealing procedure to stronger statistical guarantees and broaden the method’s practical reach.

S1.1
Notation and setup

We work on a domain
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

Bold symbols denote vectors/matrices (e.g.,

Given a measurable

We approximate
g_{\omega}
on
\mathcal{S}
with a tractable

\mathcal{S}

(i) a single Gaussian
q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})

(ii) a

Mixture weights are parameterised by logits

{\mathb{M}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}[{\mathb{T}}({\mathb{X}})]
:

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\nabla_{\mathb{M}}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})]\;.

Let
\{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\}_{i=1}^{I}
denote the set of global maximisers (“modes”) of
f
(or of
p
when
f=p
) on
\mathcal{S}
.
At a nondegenerate mode
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
(i.e.
\nabla^{2}f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\prec 0
), define the positive-definite curvature

{\mathb{H}}_{i}:=-\nabla^{2}f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\succ 0.

For symmetric matrix
A
, we write
\mathrm{eig}_{\min}(A),\mathrm{eig}_{\max}(A)
for extremal eigenvalues.
Norms are Euclidean for vectors and operator norms for matrices unless noted.

For a set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{A})
denotes its interior,
\partial\mathcal{A}
its boundary, and
\mathcal{A}^{c}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{A}
its complement. For an element
{\mathb{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
\mathrm{dist}({\mathb{x}},{\cal A}):=\inf_{{\mathb{a}}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\lVert{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{a}}\rVert_{2}
. For
r>0
, let
B({\mathb{x}},r)
denote the ball centred on
{\mathb{x}}
with radius
r
.

S1.2
Truncation equivalence and compact sets

In the optimisation setting
(
Le Minh et al., 2025
)
, the target
f
is known or directly evaluable, so
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}[f({\mathb{x}})]
is computable. In GERVE,
f
is replaced by an unknown density
p
and we observe only i.i.d. samples
{\mathb{X}}_{1:N}\sim p
. We suppose that
p
is smooth, bounded, and supported on a bounded domain
\mathrm{supp}(p)\subset\mathcal{S}
.

Using symmetry,

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}p({\mathb{x}})q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{X}})\right]+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}),

so the first term is an expectation under
p
. Let
P_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\mathb{X}}_{i}}
be the empirical measure. A natural plug-in estimator replaces
\mathbb{E}_{p}[q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{X}})]
by
\mathbb{E}_{P_{N}}[q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{X}})]
, yielding the empirical variational objective

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{N}}\left[q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{X}})\right]+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}).

(S1)

This is the learning objective optimised by GERVE.

Optimisation on untruncated Gaussians.

Since
p
is supported on
\mathcal{S}
rather than on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the corresponding Gibbs measure is defined only on
\mathcal{S}
, and one might a priori restrict
q
to
\mathcal{S}
.
q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
denotes the untruncated Gaussian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with natural parameter
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Define

\psi_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}.

The
\mathcal{S}
-truncated version is
q^{\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}({\mathb{x}}):=q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{x}})/\psi_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

Optimising directly over
q^{\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
is cumbersome because
\psi_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
varies with the parameter. The next result (proven in Section
S2.5.1
) shows that on a large
\mathcal{S}
, optimising over the untruncated family while integrating over
\mathcal{S}
incurs only a controlled error on a compact set.

To define such a compact parameter space for Gaussians, fix a mean bound
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\max}<\infty
and eigenvalue bounds
0<\sigma_{\min}^{2}<\sigma_{\max}^{2}<\infty
, and consider the compact set of natural parameters

\Theta:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}},-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}):{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\sigma_{\min}^{2}{\mathb{I}}\preceq{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\preceq\sigma_{\max}^{2}{\mathb{I}},\lVert{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\max}\Bigr\}.

For
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta
, let
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
be the mean and covariance matrix encoded by
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

For notational convenience, let

F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}),\qquad G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q^{\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}),

for
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta
.
Let
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}):=1-\psi_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
,
\varepsilon_{\max}:=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta}\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})<1
, and
p_{\max}=\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}p({\mathb{x}})
.
In the following proposition, we bound
\left\lvert G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\right\rvert
uniformly on
\Theta
.

Proposition S1.1

(Truncation gap)
.

Let
C_{q}=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta}\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\big|\log q_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathb{x}})\big|<\infty
.
Define, for
t\in[0,1)
,

\delta_{\omega}(t):=p_{\max}t+\omega\big\lvert\log(1-t)\big\rvert+\omega C_{q}t.

Then for all
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta
,

\big\lvert G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\big\rvert\leq\delta_{\omega}\big(\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\big),

(S2)

and

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta}\big\lvert G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\big\rvert\leq\delta_{\omega}(\varepsilon_{\max}).

This bound implies that if
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
stays away from
\partial\mathcal{S}
, the error from using untruncated Gaussians is negligible. Now, we derive a tail bound and, combined with an argmax stability argument, we obtain the next theorem, proven in Section
S2.5.2
.

Theorem S1.2

(Truncation equivalence on a bounded support)
.

Let
\delta_{\omega}(\cdot)
be as in Proposition
S1.1
.
For any fixed
\omega>0
, if there is a margin
M_{0}>0
such that

\mathrm{dist}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\partial\mathcal{S})\geq M_{0}\sqrt{\mathrm{eig}_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}))}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta
, then for some
C>0
,

\varepsilon_{\max}\leq Ce^{-M_{0}^{2}/2},\qquad\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta}\big|G_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\big|\leq\delta_{\omega}(\varepsilon_{\max}).

Moreover, let
m_{\omega}(\rho):=F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\omega}^{\star})-\sup_{\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\omega}^{\star}\|\geq\rho}F_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
be the value gap.
Consequently, if
m_{\omega}(\rho)>0
for some
\rho>0
,
then every maximiser of
G_{\omega}
lies in
B({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\omega}^{\star},\rho)
whenever
\delta_{\omega}(\varepsilon_{\max})<m(\rho)/3
. In particular, if the maximiser of
F_{\omega}
is unique and in
\mathrm{int}(\Theta)
, then under the same conditions, the maximiser of
G_{\omega}
is also unique and
\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Theta}G_{\omega}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega}
.

Case of mixtures.

Theorem
S1.2
is stated for single Gaussians. For mixtures, we have to apply Proposition
S1.1
componentwise under the same spectral bounds and control the entropy term via per-component bounds. Uniform control of the mixture log-density
\log q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
additionally requires avoiding degenerate behaviours (e.g., vanishing weights with unbounded parameters), which can be done by staying on a compact set of “natural” parameters.

Indeed, although mixtures are not an exponential family, they lie in the minimal conditional exponential family (MCEF;
Lin et al., 2019
), which admits analogous natural representation. We consider the compact set given by equation (
4
), that we recall here:

\Theta_{K}:=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{K-1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}):\lvert v_{k}\rvert\leq v_{\max},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\in\Theta\Bigr\},

where
v_{k}=\log(\pi_{k}/\pi_{K})
. To preserve the compactness induced by
\Theta
, we impose additional box constraints
\lvert v_{k}\rvert\leq v_{\max}
on the log-ratios, which implies a uniform floor on the weights:

\pi_{k}\geq\frac{1}{1+(K-1)e^{2v_{\max}}}>0\qquad\text{for all }k\in[K].

However, choosing

v_{\max}\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-(K-1)\Bigr)

(S3)

allows a designated subset of components to have weights as small as
\varepsilon
simultaneously while respecting the box constraints.
As
v_{\max}\to\infty
, the set of feasible weights approaches the simplex, so the constraint becomes non-restrictive in practice. Typically, setting
v_{\max}=6
is already sufficient to allow all weights to be smaller than
10^{-5}
.

In brief, for mixtures, we follow the same convention as above: we optimise over untruncated mixtures while integrating over
\mathcal{S}
. Under the margin condition
\varepsilon({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})=o(1)
(e.g.,
\mathrm{dist}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},\partial\mathcal{S})\geq M_{0}\sqrt{\mathrm{eig}_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})}
with
M_{0}
large), replacing truncated by untruncated components perturbs
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
by
o(1)
uniformly, and so leaves maximisers unchanged asymptotically.

S1.3
Lexicographic order on
\Theta

Fix a one-to-one coordinate map
\tau:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}
we write
a\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}b

if there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\lambda}\}
such that
a_{i}=b_{i}

for all
i<j
and
a_{j}<b_{j}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}\in\Theta
we write
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}

if
\tau({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}\tau({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime})
.
We define the lexicographically ordered parameter space for
K
-component mixtures by

\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}=\Bigl\{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{K-1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K})\in\Theta_{K}:{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{2}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}\cdots\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}\Bigr\}.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{K}
denote the permutation group on
\{1,\dots,K\}
.
The mixture model is invariant under relabelling of components, in the sense that for every

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
and every
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=({\mathb{v}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K})\in\Theta_{K}
there exists a relabelled parameter

\sigma\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}:=\bigl({\mathb{v}}^{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(K)}\bigr)\in\Theta_{K},

such that the mixing weights are permuted accordingly,

\pi({\mathb{v}}^{\sigma})=(\pi_{\sigma(1)}({\mathb{v}}),\dots,\pi_{\sigma(K)}({\mathb{v}}))
,
and

f(\cdot;\sigma\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

Assume additionally that the component parameters are pairwise distinct, that is,
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}\neq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
Then, for every
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}
, there exists a unique permutation
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
such that

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}}:=\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}},\qquad f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

In particular, whenever a maximiser (or stationary point) exists over
\Theta_{K}
for a permutation-invariant criterion,
there is an equivalent maximiser (or stationary point) lying in
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
.
Thus, without loss of generality, one may restrict uniqueness arguments to
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
.

Furthermore, suppose that the model is identifiable up to label switching on
\Theta_{K}
, namely,

f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime})\Longrightarrow\exists\,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}:\ {\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}=\sigma\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}.

Then the parametrisation is identifiable on
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
, in the sense that

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
and
f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime})
imply
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}
.

To justify the existence and uniqueness of
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
, observe first that because
\tau
is one-to-one and

\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}
is a strict total order on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}
, the induced relation
\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}

on
\Theta
is also a strict total order. In particular, for any
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}
in
\Theta
, exactly one of

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}
or
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
holds.

Fix
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=({\mathb{v}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K})\in\Theta_{K}
and assume that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}\neq{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
Define the rank of component
i
by

r(i):=1+\bigl|\{j\in\{1,\dots,K\}:\ {\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}\}\bigr|.

Since the
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{i}
are pairwise distinct and the order is total, the ranks
\{r(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}
are exactly

\{1,\dots,K\}
. Hence there exists a unique permutation
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
such that

r\bigl(\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(k)\bigr)=k,\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

or equivalently,

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(1)}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(2)}\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}\cdots\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(K)}.

Define the ordered representative

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}}=\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\bigl({\mathb{v}}^{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}(K)}\bigr).

By construction,
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}}\in\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
.
Uniqueness of
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
follows because the sorting permutation of a list of distinct elements under a strict total order
is unique (see, e.g.,
Cormen et al., 2009
, Sec. 8.1
).

By the assumed relabelling invariance, for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
we have
f(\cdot;\sigma\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
,
and in particular
f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
.
Consequently, any permutation-invariant criterion
Q
(for instance,

Q({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=\int\psi({\mathb{x}},f({\mathb{x}};{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}))\,d{\mathb{x}}
or
Q({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f({\mathb{X}}_{i};{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
, or any other function of

f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
) satisfies

Q({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{ord}})=Q({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

Hence, if
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}
is a maximiser of
Q
over
\Theta_{K}
, then
\bigl({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}\bigr)^{\mathrm{ord}}
is also a maximiser and lies
in
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
.

Finally, to prove identifiability on
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
, let
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
satisfy

f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})=f(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime})
. By identifiability up to label switching, there exists
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
such that

{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}=\sigma\cdot{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
, and therefore

({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{K})=({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(K)}).

But both
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K})
and
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{K})
are strictly increasing under
\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}
.
Since the
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
are distinct, the set
\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}\}
admits a unique strictly increasing ordering.
Therefore the only permutation
\sigma
for which
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{\sigma(K)})
is strictly increasing is

\sigma=\mathrm{id}
. It follows that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{k}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
for all
k
, and hence
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
.
This shows that the parametrisation is identifiable on
\Theta_{K}^{\mathrm{lex}}
.

S1.4
Modal basins-responsibility cells agreement

To investigate the link between GERVE’s induced clustering and modal clustering, we compare two partitions near each mode
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
of a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
density: the responsibility cells
A_{k}(\theta)
of an isotropic Gaussian mixture

q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathb{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}q_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},\sigma^{2}}(x)
, with
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{K})
, and the modal basins
\{\mathcal{B}_{i}\}
of
p
.
We give simple, local conditions under which they agree on inner neighborhoods of the modes.

Let
\{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\}_{i=1}^{I}
be isolated modes of
p
in
\mathrm{supp}(p)\subset\mathcal{S}
.
For
r>0
, write
B_{i}(r):=B({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star},r)
the ball centred on
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
and with radius
r
, and
\tilde{r}:=r/2
.
Weights satisfy
\pi_{k}\in[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]
with
\pi_{\min}>0
,
\sum_{k}\pi_{k}=1
.
For all
k\in[K]
, define posterior component responsibilities
\mathsf{resp}_{k}({\mathb{x}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\dfrac{\pi_{k}q_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k},\sigma^{2}I}({\mathb{x}})}{\sum_{j}\pi_{j}q_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j},\sigma^{2}I}({\mathb{x}})}
and
cells
\mathcal{A}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\{{\mathb{x}}:\mathsf{resp}_{k}({\mathb{x}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq\mathsf{resp}_{j}({\mathb{x}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\forall j\in[K]\}
. For
i\in[I]
, the modal basin
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is the set of all initial points
{\mathb{x}}
such that the solution
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)
of the gradient-ascent flow
\dot{{\mathb{x}}}=\nabla p({\mathb{x}})
with
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(0)={\mathb{x}}
satisfies
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)\rightarrow{\mathb{x}}^{\star}_{i}
as
t\to\infty

Assumption S1.3

(Local strong log-concavity and separation)
.

For each
i
there exists
r_{i}>0
and
\kappa_{i}>0
such that

-\nabla^{2}\log p({\mathb{x}})\succeq\kappa_{i}{\mathb{I}}
for all
{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(r_{i})
, and the closed balls

\overline{B_{i}(r_{i})}\subset\mathrm{supp}(p)
are pairwise disjoint.

Lemma S1.4

(Basin containment)
.

Under Assumption
S1.3
,
B_{i}(r_{i})\subset\mathcal{B}_{i}
for each
i
.

Proof of Lemma
S1.4
.

Let
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)
be a trajectory of
\dot{{\mathb{x}}}=\nabla p({\mathb{x}})
with
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(0)\in B_{i}(r_{i})
. Choose the Lyapunov function
V({\mathb{x}})=\lVert{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rVert^{2}/2
.

On
\mathrm{supp}(p)
, using
\dot{{\mathb{x}}}=\nabla p({\mathb{x}})=p({\mathb{x}})\nabla\log p({\mathb{x}})
, we have

\dot{V}({\mathb{x}})=\langle\dot{{\mathb{x}}},{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rangle=p({\mathb{x}})\langle\nabla\log p({\mathb{x}}),{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rangle.

Since
\nabla\log p({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})=\nabla p({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})=0
, strong log-concavity (Assumption
S1.3
) implies,
for
{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(r_{i})\setminus\{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\}
,

\langle\nabla\log p({\mathb{x}}),{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rangle\leq-\kappa_{i}\|{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\|^{2}<0,

thus, with
m_{i}=\inf_{{\mathb{x}}\in\overline{B_{i}(r_{i})}}p({\mathb{x}})>0
,

\dot{V}({\mathb{x}})\leq-2\kappa_{i}p({\mathb{x}})V({\mathb{x}})\leq-2\kappa_{i}m_{i}V({\mathb{x}}).

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have

V(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t))\leq V(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(0))\exp(-2\kappa_{i}m_{i}t),

(S4)

as long as
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)
is in
B_{i}(r_{i})
.

Finally, we prove by contradiction that
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)\in B_{i}(r_{i})
for all
t\geq 0
. Suppose the existence of
T_{\mathrm{out}}=\inf_{t>0}\{t:\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)\not\in B_{i}(r_{i})\}<\infty
. In this case, by continuity of
{\mathb{x}}
, we have
\lVert\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(T_{\mathrm{out}})-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rVert=r_{i}
.

But since
V(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(0))<r_{i}^{2}/2
, (
S4
) gives for
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{out}})
,

V(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t))<\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{2}\exp(-2\kappa_{i}m_{i}t)<\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{2}.

This implies
\|\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\|<r_{i}
for all
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{out}})
, and this contradicts
\lVert\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(T_{\mathrm{out}})-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\rVert=r_{i}
.

Thus, we have
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)\in B_{i}(r_{i})
for all
t\geq 0
and (
S4
) ensures
V(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t))\rightarrow 0
as
t\to\infty
, hence
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(t)\to{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
and we conclude that
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}(0)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
.
∎

Assumption S1.5

(One-per-mode configuration)
.

There is a configuration
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{I})
with exactly one centre

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})
for each
i
, and no other centres in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}B_{i}(r_{i})
.
Therefore, by convention, we relabel components so that centre
i
lies in
B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})
and write
\mathcal{A}_{i}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for the
i
th cell.

Assumption S1.6

(Local margin)
.

There exists
\Delta>0
such that for all
i
and all
{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})
,

\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\|\geq\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|+\Delta\qquad\forall j\neq i.

Remark 1
.

If
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})
and

\mathrm{dist}\big(B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i}),\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\}_{j\neq i}\big)>0
, then

\Delta:=\inf_{{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})}\min_{j\neq i}(\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\|-\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|)>0
.

Assumption S1.7

(Scale separation)
.

There exists
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\sigma\leq\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2\log\Psi(\varepsilon)}},\qquad\text{where }\Psi(\varepsilon):=\frac{(I-1)\pi_{\max}}{\varepsilon\pi_{\min}}>1.

Theorem S1.8

(Responsibility on balls)
.

Under Assumptions
S1.5
-
S1.7
, for each
i
,

B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})\subset\mathcal{A}_{i}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\qquad\text{and}\quad r_{i}({\mathb{x}};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{-1},\quad\forall{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i}).

Proof of Theorem
S1.8
.

Fix
i
and let
{\mathb{x}}\in B_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})
. By Assumption
S1.6
,

\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\|-\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|\geq\Delta
and

\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}\|+\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|\geq 2\|{\mathb{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|+\Delta
. Combining with Assumption
S1.7
, this gives

S1.5
Natural-gradient convergence

Under the Robbins–Monro and bounded iterate conditions, we can show that stochastic natural-gradient ascent (SNGA) is consistent. Convergence is obtained via a direct application of the ODE method for stochastic approximation
(
Borkar, 2008
, Chp. 2.1)

Assumption S1.10
.

Consider the update

{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t}+\rho_{t}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t},

(S5)

where
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t}
is an unbiased stochastic estimate of the natural gradient

\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t})
(equivalently, the gradient in the expectation-parameter space for exponential families or mixtures thereof). Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t})
.

Assume:

(A1)

(
Robbins–Monro
)
\sum_{t}\rho_{t}=\infty
,
\sum_{t}\rho_{t}^{2}<\infty
.

(A2)

(
Mean field
)
\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
is locally Lipschitz and of at most linear growth.

(A3)

(
Noise
)
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t-1})
and
\mathbb{E}\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t}\|^{2}\leq C(1+\|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t-1}\|^{2})
.

(A4)

(
Stability
) The iterates
({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
are a.s. bounded.

Theorem S1.11

(Convergence of SNGA at fixed
\omega
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\omega}=\{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}:\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})=0\}
. Under Assumption

, we have, as

\mathrm{dist}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t},\mathcal{S}_{\omega})\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Equivalently, every limit point of
({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t})
is almost surely stationary.

Remark 3

(Natural-gradient regularity)
.

For exponential families and mixtures thereof (treated as MCEF models), the “natural gradient = expectation gradient” identity (eq. (
5
)) ensures that
\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
inherits Lipschitz continuity from
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}
on compact

, without explicit inversion of the Fisher information matrix.

Under annealing, conditions on the schedule are sufficient to guarantee convergence. The following corollary stems from standard two-timescale results for stochastic approximation (
Kushner and Yin, 2003
, Chp. 8.6;
Borkar, 2008
, Chp. 6.1).

Corollary S1.12

(Convergence of SNGA under annealing)
.

Suppose that under Assumption
S1.10
for each fixed
\omega>0
, and that

({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\omega)\mapsto\widetilde{\nabla}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega,N}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
is jointly continuous on bounded sets.

1.

Stagewise schedule.
Let
\omega_{1}>\omega_{2}>\cdots\rightarrow 0
.
If at stage

the run with fixed
\omega_{j}

(and the next stage is warm-started at that iterate),

\mathrm{dist}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\mathcal{S}_{\omega_{j}})\to 0
as

.

2.

Continuous schedule.
If
\omega_{t}\rightarrow 0
satisfies

\lvert\omega_{t+1}-\omega_{t}\rvert=o(\rho_{t})

\mathrm{dist}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t},\mathcal{S}_{\omega_{t}})\to 0

Remark 4

(GERVE’s convergence)
.

GERVE performs optimisation in a compact parameter space (
\Theta
for single Gaussians,
\Theta_{K}
for Gaussian mixtures), so the iterates must be projected to ensure that they remain in this space. In this case, the above convergence results are not directly applicable. However, in practice, projection is not always needed, typically when the compact space contains the solution to the unconstrained optimisation problem.

To investigate the properties of the stability scores, we write their mathematical definition.
Let
\Phi(G)

s_{j}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}F_{j}(P_{N}^{\ast(\ell)})
. In other words,

s_{j}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{\exists\text{ a matched component in }U_{j}\text{ for the }\ell\text{-th bootstrap fit}\}.

Let
\mathbb{E}^{\ast}
and

denote the conditional expectation and variance given
{\mathb{X}}_{1:N}
. The following proposition, proven in Section
S2.4.7
, establishes statistical guarantees on the stability scores.

Proposition S1.13

(Consistency of stability scores)
.

Let us index the stability scores

by

. Under Assumptions
S2.10
and

and with

chosen to have a positive margin at

(Lemma

), define

Then:

(i)

(Conditional LLN).
For each

, conditionally on the data,

(Large-

S1.6.3
Handling local optima

Although
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{0}}
is nonconvex in general, in practice we use the following guardrails to mitigate the issue:

1.

Annealing + overcompleteness.
We use an overcomplete model, and we start from a higher smoothing level and continue down to
\omega_{0}
. Small components are pruned. This reliably enlarges the global basin explored by GERVE.

2.

Stability scores.
Per-mode stability scores
s_{j}
are monitored, with values near 1 indicating robust recovery. For example, as a diagnostic, we can flag
s_{j}<0.5
as potentially problematic and
s_{j}<0.3
as likely unstable.

3.

Multiple initialisations.
We can run several random starts at
\omega_{0}
and keep the best solution (by objective).

S2.1
Preliminaries: Empirical process properties

In this section, we first prove that the classes of Gaussian distributions and GMM with bounded covariances are Glivenko–Cantelli (GC), and admit integrable envelopes. We recall

, converges uniformly to its expectation
Pf=\mathbb{E}_{P}[f({\mathb{X}})]
, in probability:

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert P_{N}f-Pf\right\rvert\xrightarrow{P}0\text{ as }N\to\infty.

We say that
\mathcal{F}
admits an integrable envelope

if
F({\mathb{x}})\geq\lvert f({\mathb{x}})\rvert
for all
f\in{\cal F}
, with
\mathbb{E}_{P}[F({\mathb{X}})]<\infty
.

-component mixtures with components in
{\cal F}

\mathcal{F}^{(K)}=\Big\{f({\mathb{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}f_{k}({\mathb{x}}):{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in\Delta_{K},f_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\Big\},

where
\Delta_{K}
is the
K-1
-dimensional simplex. Then
\mathcal{F}^{(K)}
is GC with envelope
KF\in L_{1}(P)
.

Proof of Lemma
S2.3
.

We need to prove that
KF
is an integrable envelope and that
\mathcal{F}^{(K)}
is totally bounded in
L_{1}(P)
.
First,

\lvert f({\mathb{x}})\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}f_{k}({\mathb{x}})\right\rvert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\lvert f_{k}({\mathb{x}})\rvert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert f_{k}({\mathb{x}})\rvert\leq KF({\mathb{x}}),

hence
\mathbb{E}_{P}[KF]<\infty
, and
KF
is an envelope.

The total boundedness of
\mathcal{F}^{(K)}
comes from the total boundedness of
\mathcal{F}
:

1.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded, there exists a finite
\varepsilon/2
-net
\{g^{(1)},\dots,g^{(J_{\varepsilon})}\}
in
L_{1}(P)
. For each
f_{k}
, denote by
g^{(j_{k})}
an
\varepsilon
-approximation.
For each
g^{(j_{k})}
,
\|g^{(j_{k})}\|_{L_{1}(P)}\leq\|g^{(j_{k})}-f_{k}\|_{L_{1}(P)}+\|f_{k}\|_{L_{1}(P)}=\varepsilon/2+\|F\|_{L_{1}(P)}=F_{\varepsilon}
.

2.

Approximate
\pi\in\Delta_{K}
using a finite grid
\Pi_{\eta}
of mesh
\eta=\varepsilon/(2KF_{\varepsilon})
and denote by
\tilde{\pi}_{k}
the closest element of
\Pi_{\eta}
to
\pi_{k}
.

3.

Compute

\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}f_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\pi}_{k}g^{(j_{k})}\right\rVert_{L_{1}(P)}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\lVert f_{k}-g^{(j_{k})}\rVert_{L_{1}(P)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}F_{\varepsilon}\lvert\pi_{k}-\widetilde{\pi}_{k}\rvert.

The two terms are bounded by
\varepsilon/2
, so the sum is bounded by
\varepsilon
.

Thus
\mathcal{F}^{(K)}
is totally bounded, hence GC.
∎

Corollary S2.4

(GC for bounded-covariance Gaussian mixtures)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the class of Gaussian distributions with covariance eigenvalues in
[\sigma_{\min}^{2},\sigma_{\max}^{2}]
and unconstrained means.
For fixed
K
, the class of
K
-component Gaussian mixtures with those covariance bounds is GC under
P
and has an envelope in
L_{1}(P)
.

Proof of Corollary
S2.4
.

In the proof of Lemma
S2.2
, we proved the sufficient conditions to apply Lemma
S2.3
with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}
, which proves in turn the result above.
∎

Finally, we present the
P
-Donsker property. It upgrades GC to a uniform CLT, which yields rates for empirical averages over
\mathcal{F}
and underpins our asymptotic normality results. Consider a probability measure
P
and some i.i.d. samples
{\mathb{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathb{X}}_{N}
of a random variable
{\mathb{X}}\sim P
. A class of functions
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
P
-Donsker if the empirical process

G_{N}
defined as
G_{N}f\coloneqq\sqrt{N}(P_{N}-P)f
for
f\in{\cal F}
,
viewed as a random element in
\ell_{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
, converges in distribution to a tight mean-zero Gaussian process
G_{P}
with covariance

\mathrm{Cov}\big(G_{P}f,G_{P}g\big)=P\left[(f-Pf)(g-Pg)\right],\qquad f,g\in\mathcal{F}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is
P
-Donsker if a functional CLT holds uniformly over
f\in\mathcal{F}
. A sufficient condition is that
\mathcal{F}\subset L_{2}(P)
admits an
L_{2}(P)
envelope and has a finite bracketing entropy integral, for example

\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{\log N_{[\,]}\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P)\big)}d\varepsilon<\infty,

where
\log N_{[\,]}\big(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F},L_{2}(P)\big)
denotes the
\varepsilon
-bracketing entropy for
{\cal F}
in
L_{2}(P)
, see
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Chapter 2.5.

Lemma S2.5

(GC and Donsker properties for Gaussian mixtures and their gradients)
.

For fixed
K
and compact
\Theta_{K}
, the classes

\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}:{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}\}
,

\mathcal{Q}^{(u)}_{\nabla}=\{[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{u}:{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}\}
for
u=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
and
\mathcal{Q}^{(u,v)}_{\nabla^{2}}=\{[\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{uv}:{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}\}
for
u,v=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})

are
P
-Donsker (hence Glivenko–Cantelli) with envelopes in
L_{2}(P)
.

Proof of Lemma
S2.5
.

First, we prove that
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\nabla}
admit envelopes in
L_{2}(P)
, then we use the fact that they are Lipschitz to exhibit their Donsker property.

Step 1.
L_{2}
envelopes.

On set
\Theta_{K}
, by continuity of Gaussian densities and compactness of
\Theta_{K}
, there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}<\infty
(depending only on
\Theta_{K}
) such that, for all
{\mathb{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}
, for all
u,v=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
,

\begin{split}&0\leq q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})\leq C_{0},\qquad\lvert[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})]_{u}\rvert\leq C_{1}(1+\|{\mathb{x}}\|+\|{\mathb{x}}\|^{2}),\\
&\lvert[\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})]_{uv}\rvert\leq C_{2}(1+\|{\mathb{x}}\|+\|{\mathb{x}}\|^{2}+\|{\mathb{x}}\|^{3}+\|{\mathb{x}}\|^{4}).\end{split}

Hence an envelope for
{\cal Q}
is
Q({\mathb{x}})\equiv C_{0}\in L_{2}(P)
. For
u=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
, since
P
is supported on a bounded set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is
R<\infty
with
\|{\mathb{X}}\|\leq R
a.s., and thus an envelope for
{\cal Q}^{(u)}_{\nabla}
is

Q_{\nabla}({\mathb{X}})\equiv C_{1}(1+R+R^{2})
a.s. In particular
Q_{\nabla}\in L_{2}(P)
, therefore

{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u)}_{\nabla}
admits an
L_{2}(P)
envelope. Similarly, for
u,v=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
, an envelope for
{\cal Q}^{(u,v)}_{\nabla^{2}}
is

Q_{\nabla^{2}}({\mathb{X}})\equiv C_{2}(1+R+R^{2}+R^{3}+R^{4})
a.s. and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u,v)}_{\nabla^{2}}
also admits an
L_{2}(P)
envelope.

Step 2. Lipschitz into
L_{2}(P)
.

Fix
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\Theta_{K}
and set
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+t({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
,
t\in[0,1]
.
By the mean value theorem in Banach spaces,

q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\rangle dt,

so

\|q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}-q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\|\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{K}}\|\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\prime}}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq\|Q_{\nabla}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\|.

By the same argument applied to
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
and
\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
and using that all second derivatives

\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})
and third derivatives
\nabla^{3}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{x}})
are uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{S}\times\Theta_{K}
, since Gaussian mixture densities have continuous second and third derivatives and
\Theta_{K}
ensures bounded means and eigenvalues bounded away from
0
and
\infty
,
there exists
C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2}<\infty
with

\|[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{u}-[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{u}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq C^{\prime}_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\|,\quad\|[\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{uv}-[\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{uv}\|_{L_{2}(P)}\leq C^{\prime}_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\|,

for all
u,v=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
.
Thus all maps
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mapsto q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mapsto[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{u}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mapsto[\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}]_{uv}
are Lipschitz into
L_{2}(P)
with
L_{2}
envelopes.

Step 3. Donsker.

By the Euclidean parametric-Lipschitz bracketing bound
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Thm. 2.7.11)
, the
L_{2}(P)
bracketing numbers of
\mathcal{Q}
,
{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u)}_{\nabla}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u,v)}_{\nabla^{2}}
grow at most polynomially on compact
\Theta_{K}
.
Therefore, the bracketing integral is finite, and the bracketing Donsker theorem
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
, Chp. 2.5)
yields that
\mathcal{Q}
,
{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u)}_{\nabla}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{(u,v)}_{\nabla^{2}}
are
P
-Donsker, for all
u,v=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
.
∎

S2.2
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We prove Theorem
2.1
, establishing the consistency of the optimal variational solution
q_{\omega}^{\star}:=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)
, where
\mathcal{Q}
is the family of truncated Gaussian mixtures of
K\geq I
components with upper-bounded covariances, as
\omega\rightarrow 0
. We proceed in three steps. First, we identify the asymptotics of the Gibbs measure
g_{\omega}
via a Laplace expansion near each mode of
f
(Prop.
S2.6
and
S2.7
). Then, we construct a truncated mixture
\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}
on
\mathcal{S}
such that
\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}||g_{\omega})\rightarrow 0
(Prop.
S2.8
). This implies
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\omega}^{\star}||g_{\omega})\rightarrow 0
, so finally, combining with the Laplace expansions of
g_{\omega}
, we deduce the claims of Theorem
2.1
.

Setup

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathcal{S})
be bounded with (finitely many) nondegenerate global modes
\{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\}_{i=1}^{I}
, and let
f^{\star}:=\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\in{\cal S}}f({\mathb{x}})=f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})
. Nondegeneracy means
{\mathb{H}}_{i}=-\nabla^{2}f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\succ 0
, for all
i\in[I]
. These modes are isolated and in the interior of
\mathcal{S}
, so there exist disjoint open, bounded neighborhoods
U_{1},\dots,U_{I}\subset\mathcal{S}
of the
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
such that each
U_{i}
contains no other critical point of
f
. Without loss of generality, assume that
f
is strictly positive on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}U_{i}
. Define
U_{0}:=\mathcal{S}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}U_{i}
.
The restricted objective over a family
\mathcal{Q}
is

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q}[f({\mathb{X}})]+\omega\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q),\qquad q\in\mathcal{Q}.

Here, the family
\mathcal{Q}
is the
\mathcal{S}
-truncated Gaussian mixture family with
K\geq I
components, and all eigenvalues of covariance matrices in
(0,\sigma_{\max}^{2}]
. Recall that for any Gaussian mixture
q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write its
\mathcal{S}
-truncation

q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{x}}):=\frac{q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{x}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{x}})}{\int_{\mathcal{S}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{y}})d{\mathb{y}}}.

Let
q_{\omega}^{\star}
denote any choice of maximiser of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(q)
in
\mathcal{Q}
, for a fixed
\omega>0
. To simplify the notation, we denote the conditional distributions of
q_{\omega}^{\star}
and
g_{\omega}
on the regions
U_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,I
, respectively by
\varphi_{i,\omega}
and
\psi_{i,\omega}
, defined as follows:

\varphi_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}}):=q_{\omega}^{\star,U_{i}}({\mathb{x}})=\frac{q_{\omega}^{\star}({\mathb{x}})\mathds{1}_{U_{i}}({\mathb{x}})}{\alpha_{i,\omega}},\qquad\psi_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}}):=g_{\omega}^{U_{i}}({\mathb{x}})=\frac{g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})\mathds{1}_{U_{i}}({\mathb{x}})}{\gamma_{i,\omega}},

(S6)

where

\alpha_{i,\omega}:=\int_{U_{i}}q_{\omega}^{\star}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}},\qquad\gamma_{i,\omega}:=\int_{U_{i}}g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}.

(S7)

Step 1: Laplace expansion and local Gibbs measure properties

We start by applying Laplace’s method around each isolated mode
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
of
f
to the unrestricted Gibbs optimiser
g_{\omega}
.
The two following propositions characterise the shape of the Gibbs measure under annealing.

Proposition S2.6

(Laplace expansion near a mode)
.

Fix
i\in[I]
and consider an open neighborhood
U_{i}
of mode
{\mathb{x}}_{i}
. Then, as
\omega\rightarrow 0
,

\int_{U_{i}}\exp\{f({\mathb{x}})/\omega\}d{\mathb{x}}=e^{f^{\star}/\omega}(2\pi\omega)^{d/2}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{-1/2}\big(1+o(1)\big),

and for any bounded continuous function
h
,

\int_{U_{i}}h({\mathb{x}})g_{\omega}(d{\mathb{x}})=h({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})c_{i}(\omega)+\frac{\omega}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\big({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\nabla^{2}h({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\big)c_{i}(\omega)+o(\omega),

where

c_{i}(\omega)=c_{i}+o(1),\qquad c_{i}:=\frac{(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{-1/2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}}.

Moreover,
g_{\omega}\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{I}c_{i}\delta_{{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}}
weakly, and there exists
C,\eta>0
such that
g_{\omega}(U_{0})\leq Ce^{-\eta/\omega}
.

Proof of Proposition
S2.6
.

The proof uses the classical Laplace method (
Hwang, 1980
; also see
Le Minh et al., 2025
, Proof of Thm. 2
): in
U_{i}
, write the Taylor expansion of
f({\mathb{x}})
at
{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}
,

f({\mathb{x}})=f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})+R_{i}({\mathb{x}}),

with
R_{i}({\mathb{x}})\leq L_{i}\|{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\|^{3}
, where
L_{i}>0
depends on
U_{i}
. Then

\int_{U_{i}}\exp(f({\mathb{x}})/\omega)d{\mathb{x}}=\exp(f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})/\omega)\int_{U_{i}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\omega}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})+\frac{1}{\omega}R_{i}({\mathb{x}})\Big\}d{\mathb{x}},

and standard dominated convergence in Gaussian coordinates yields the first display. Summing over
i
and normalising gives the weights
c_{i}(\omega)\to c_{i}
and the weak limit. The second display follows from the same expansion applied to
\psi
(up to second order) and Gaussian moment calculations. The last statement can be established since the modes are isolated: there exists
\eta>0
such that

\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\in U_{0}}f({\mathb{x}})\leq f^{\star}-\eta,

so there exists
C>0
such that

g_{\omega}(U_{0})\leq Ce^{-\eta/\omega}.

∎

Proposition S2.7
.

For
i\in[I]
and
\psi_{i,\omega}
defined in equation (
S6
), there exists
\eta>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}]={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}+O(e^{-\eta/\omega}),\qquad\mathrm{Cov}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}({\mathb{X}})=\omega{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}+O(e^{-2\eta/\omega}).

Proof of Proposition
S2.7
.

(i) Expectation.
Consider the coordinate functions
h_{j}({\mathb{x}})=x_{j}
, for all
j\in[d]
. These functions are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with
h_{j}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})=x_{i,j}^{\star}
and
\nabla^{2}h_{j}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{*})\equiv 0
.
Apply Proposition
S2.6
to
h=h_{j}
:

\int_{U_{i}}x_{j}g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}=x_{i,j}^{\star}c_{i}(\omega)+o(\omega).

Next, Proposition
S2.6
also implies
\gamma_{i,\omega}=g_{\omega}(U_{i})=c_{i}(\omega)+o(e^{-\eta/\omega})
. So

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[X_{j}]=\frac{\int_{U_{i}}x_{j}g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}}{\gamma_{i,\omega}}=\frac{x_{i,j}^{\star}c_{i}(\omega)+o(\omega)}{c_{i}(\omega)+o(e^{-\eta/\omega})}.

Since
c_{i}(\omega)\rightarrow c_{i}>0
, the denominator is bounded away from
0
and we can divide: for all
j\in[d]
,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[X_{j}]=x_{i,j}^{\star}+O(e^{-\eta/\omega}).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}]={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}+O(e^{-\eta/\omega}).

(ii) Covariance.
Next, consider the centred second moment functions
h_{jk}({\mathb{x}}):=(x_{j}-x_{i,j}^{\star})(x_{k}-x_{i,k}^{\star})
, for all
(j,k)\in[d]^{2}
. These functions are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with
h_{jk}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})=0
and

\left(\nabla^{2}h_{jk}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\right)_{\ell m}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{\ell}\partial x_{m}}\left((x_{j}-x_{i,j}^{\star})(x_{k}-x_{i,k}^{\star})\right)|_{{\mathb{x}}={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}}=\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{km}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell},

where
\delta_{\cdot\cdot}
is the Kronecker symbol, i.e.
\nabla^{2}h_{jk}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})
is the matrix with entries
(j,k)
and
(k,j)
set to one and all other entries set to zero.
Hence, by symmetry of
{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}
,

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\nabla^{2}h_{jk}({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\right)=\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\right)_{jk}+\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\right)_{kj}=2\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\right)_{jk}.

Apply Proposition
S2.6
to
h_{jk}
:

\int_{U_{i}}h_{jk}({\mathb{x}})g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}=\omega\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\right)_{jk}c_{i}(\omega)+o(\omega).

Again, we can divide by
\gamma_{i,\omega}=c_{i}(\omega)+o(e^{-\eta/\omega})
:

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[(X_{j}-x_{i,j}^{\star})(X_{k}-x_{i,k}^{\star})^{T}]=\frac{\int_{U_{i}}h_{jk}({\mathb{x}})g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})d{\mathb{x}}}{\gamma_{i,\omega}}=\omega\left({\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}\right)_{jk}+O(e^{-\eta/\omega}).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[({\mathb{X}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})({\mathb{X}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}]=\omega{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}+O(e^{-\eta/\omega}).

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}({\mathb{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[({\mathb{X}}-\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}])({\mathb{X}}-\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}])^{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[({\mathb{X}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})({\mathb{X}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}]-(\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}]-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})(\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}]-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T},

where we have already shown that
\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}[{\mathb{X}}]-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}=O(e^{-\eta/\omega})
, so

\mathrm{Cov}_{\psi_{i,\omega}}({\mathb{X}})=\omega{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}+O(e^{-2\eta/\omega}).

∎

Step 2: Competitor mixture and KL convergence

For
\omega>0
, define the Gaussian distributions, for
i\in[I]
:

\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\mathb{x}};{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star},\omega{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1}).

These Gaussian dsitributions approximate locally the Gibbs measure at each mode.
We define the associated mixture with Laplace weights

\widetilde{q}_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{I}c_{i}\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}}),

where
c_{i}\propto(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{-1/2}
and
\sum_{i}c_{i}=1
.
We use the truncated mixture
\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}:=\widetilde{q}_{\omega}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}/\int_{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{q}_{\omega}
as a competitor for
q_{\omega}^{\star}
in
\mathcal{Q}
. In this step, we prove that
\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}
converges in KL to
g_{\omega}
.

Proposition S2.8
.

Let
\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}:=\widetilde{q}_{\omega}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}/\int_{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{q}_{\omega}
be the truncation of
\widetilde{q}_{\omega}
on
\mathcal{S}
. Then,
\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}\in\mathcal{Q}
and, as
\omega\to 0
,

\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{q}^{\mathcal{S}}_{\omega}\|g_{\omega})\longrightarrow 0.

To prove Proposition
S2.8
, we derive an intermediate lemma.

Lemma S2.9
.

Let
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{q}_{\omega}\|g_{\omega}):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{q}_{\omega}\log\widetilde{q}_{\omega}/g_{\omega}
. As
\omega\to 0
,

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{q}_{\omega}\|g_{\omega})\longrightarrow 0.

Proof of Lemma
S2.9
.

We first compute the KL divergence for a single Gaussian
\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}
and show
that it converges to a finite constant. We then use the disjoint-mode structure
to prove that the mixture
\widetilde{q}_{\omega}
is asymptotically indistinguishable
from
g_{\omega}
in KL.

Step 1. Log-ratio for a single component.

Fix
i
. On
U_{i}
we write

f({\mathb{x}})=f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})+\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}}),\qquad|\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}})|\leq C\|{\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}\|^{3}.

By definition,

g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})=\frac{\exp\{f({\mathb{x}})/\omega\}}{Z_{\omega}},\qquad\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}})=(2\pi\omega)^{-d/2}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{1/2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\omega}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})\Bigr).

From Proposition
S2.6
,

Z_{\omega}=e^{f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})/\omega}(2\pi\omega)^{d/2}\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

so, for
{\mathb{x}}\in U_{i}
,

\displaystyle\log g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{f({\mathb{x}})}{\omega}-\log Z_{\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})}{\omega}-\frac{1}{2\omega}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})+\frac{\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}})}{\omega}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{f({\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})}{\omega}-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\omega)-\log\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}\Bigr)+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\omega}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})+\frac{\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}})}{\omega}-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\omega)-\log\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}\Bigr)+o(1).

On the other hand,

\log\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}})=-\frac{1}{2\omega}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})^{T}{\mathb{H}}_{i}({\mathb{x}}-{\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star})-\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\log\det{\mathb{H}}_{i}.

Subtracting gives

\displaystyle\log\frac{\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}({\mathb{x}})}{g_{\omega}({\mathb{x}})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}})}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det{\mathb{H}}_{i}+\log\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}\Bigr)+o(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta_{i}({\mathb{x}})}{\omega}-\log c_{i}+o(1),

where

c_{i}=\frac{(\det{\mathb{H}}_{i})^{-1/2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{I}(\det{\mathb{H}}_{j})^{-1/2}}.

For
{\mathb{X}}\sim\widetilde{q}_{i,\omega}
, write
{\mathb{X}}={\mathb{x}}_{i}^{\star}+\sqrt{\omega}{\mathb{Y}}

with
{\mathb{Y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\mathb{H}}_{i}^{-1})

\frac{\Delta_{i}({\mathb{X}})}{\omega}=O\bigl(\sqrt{\omega}\|{\mathb{Y}}\|^{3}\bigr).

Since
{\mathb{Y}}
has finite moments of all orders,

as
\omega\to 0
.
Dominated convergence on
U_{i}
yields, as
\omega\to 0

Step 2. KL for the mixture
\widetilde{q}_{\omega}
near the modes.

Define

We show that the limiting constant

cancels mode by mode

Fix

and for each

(S8)

On

Existence of a population maximiser

Proof of (i).

On the compact
\Theta_{K}

S2.3.2
Consistency of the empirical maximisers

The proof of this result consists of two steps: the derivation of a uniform law of large numbers, due to the Glivenko–Cantelli (GC) property of the Gaussian mixture family in

(see App.

S2.3.3
Asymptotic normality of the empirical maximisers

The asymptotic normality result is derived from a Taylor expansion and the Donsker property on the Gaussian mixture family but also on the gradients. Lemma

Proof of (iii).

By Lemma

S2.4.1
Preliminaries: Bounded-Lipschitz metric

Let
(\mathcal{S},d)
be a metric space. For
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
define

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}|f({\mathb{x}})|,\qquad\mathrm{Lip}(f)=\sup_{{\mathb{x}}\neq{\mathb{y}}}\frac{|f({\mathb{x}})-f({\mathb{y}})|}{d({\mathb{x}},{\mathb{y}})}.

For two measures
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{S}
, the bounded-Lipschitz (BL) metric is

d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}):=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1}\Big|\textstyle\int fd\nu-\int fd\nu^{\prime}\Big|,\qquad\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\mathrm{Lip}(f).

Convergence in
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
implies weak convergence
(
Dudley, 2002
)
, denoted by
\xrightarrow{w}
. We use it to state bootstrap results compactly.

Proof of Lemma
S2.13
.

(i)
By Lemma
S2.11
, for all
{\mathb{x}}\in\mathcal{S}
, the map
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mapsto\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{x}})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
\mathcal{V}
,
\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\mathcal{V}}\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{x}})\|\leq F_{1}({\mathb{x}})
and
\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\mathcal{V}}\|\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{x}})\|\leq F_{2}({\mathb{x}})
for some bounded functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Therefore, for any
G\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
, the maps
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\mapsto G([\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}]_{u})
, for
u=1,\dots,\dim(\Theta_{K})
, have derivatives
(G([\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}]_{u1}),\dots,G([\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}]_{u\dim(\Theta_{K})}))
and are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
\mathcal{V}
. This implies that
S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
, and

S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=0,\qquad\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{0}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})={\mathb{A}}\prec 0,

where
{\mathb{A}}:=-{\mathb{H}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\star}
is invertible by Assumption
S2.10
.

Furthermore, since
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
is continuous in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
, there is
r>0
such that on the closed ball
\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)\subset\mathcal{V}
,

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)}\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-{\mathb{A}}\rVert\leq\frac{1}{3\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert}.

(S10)

Now, define for
G\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
,
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
,

T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}):={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

To prove (i), we would like to apply the Banach fixed-point theorem (
Zeidler, 1995
, Th. 1.A
) to
T_{G}
, for all
G
in some neighborhood of
P
, in the set
\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
. For this, we need to verify a self-mapping property and a contraction property, which hold for a sufficiently small neighborhood of
P
: we consider the neighborhood
\mathcal{T}
formed by
G
such that

\|G-P\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq\min\left(\frac{1}{3\|{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\|},\frac{r}{3\|{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\|}\right).

Contraction property.

S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
\mathcal{V}
, so for any
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2}
in
\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
, the mean-value theorem gives

S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1})-S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2})=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}})({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2})

for some
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
on the segment between
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2}
. Therefore,

T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1})-T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2})=({\mathb{I}}-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}))({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2}).

This means

\lVert T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1})-T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2})\rVert\leq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)}\lVert{\mathb{I}}-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert\times\lVert{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2}\rVert.

(S11)

Furthermore,

\lVert{\mathb{I}}-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert\leq\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert(\lVert{\mathb{A}}-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert+\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert).

Then, by definition of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
,

\|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\|=\Big\|(G-P)(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{2}q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\Big\|\leq\|G-P\|_{\mathcal{F}}.

For all
G\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
\lVert G-P\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leq 1/(3\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert)
, so

\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Combining this with (
S10
), we have

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)}\lVert{\mathb{I}}-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})\rVert\leq\frac{2}{3},

so in (
S11
), we have

\lVert T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1})-T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2})\rVert\leq\frac{2}{3}\lVert{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{2}\rVert,

(S12)

which proves contraction for
G\in\mathcal{T}
.

Self-mapping property.
Since
S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=0
,

T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}=-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=-{\mathb{A}}^{-1}(G-P)(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}).

By definition of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
,

\Big\|(G-P)(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}})\Big\|\leq\|G-P\|_{\mathcal{F}}.

Hence, for
G\in\mathcal{T}
,

\|T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\|\leq\|{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\|\|G-P\|_{\mathcal{F}}\leq\frac{r}{3}

Thus, using the contraction property (
S12
), for any
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
,

\|T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\|\leq\|T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\|+\|T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\|\leq\frac{2r}{3}+\frac{r}{3}\leq r,

and this ensures that
T_{G}(\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r))\subset\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)
, the self-mapping property for
G\in\mathcal{T}
.

Conclusion.
Finally, by the Banach fixed-point theorem for
T_{G}
, for every
G
in the neighborhood
\mathcal{T}
of
P
, there exists a unique fixed point
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(G)\in\overline{B}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},r)\subset\mathcal{V}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(G)=T_{G}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(G))
is equivalent to
S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(G))=0
. This gives (i).

(ii)
Let
t_{n}\to 0
and
h_{n}\to h
in
\ell_{\mathrm{lin}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
under
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
. Set
G_{n}:=P+t_{n}h_{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}:=\Phi(G_{n})={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(G_{n})
. Our objective is to show that

D\Phi_{P}(h):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(G_{n})-\Phi(P)}{t_{n}}=({\mathb{H}}_{\omega_{0}}^{\ast})^{-1}h(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\ast}_{\omega_{0}}}).

First, the sequence
(h_{n})
is bounded in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}
, so
\lVert G_{n}-P\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=\lvert t_{n}\rvert\lVert h_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\to 0
, and
G_{n}\in\mathcal{T}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
, so by (i), we have
0=S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n})
. Furthermore, since we also have
S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=0
, then

0=\left(S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n})-S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\right)+\left(S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\right).

By linearity, the second difference is

S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=t_{n}h_{n}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}).

For the first term, since
S(G,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
, the mean-value theorem says that there exists
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n}
on the segment between
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}
such that

S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n})-S(G_{n},{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}).

Thus, we have the relation

0=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})+t_{n}h_{n}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}).

(S13)

On the other hand, the fixed-point and the contraction properties of
T_{G}
for all
G\in\mathcal{T}
, proven in (i), imply that
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}
. Indeed, we have

\begin{split}\lVert{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\rVert&=\rVert T_{G_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n})-T_{P}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\rVert\\
&\leq\rVert T_{G_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n})-T_{G_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\rVert+\rVert T_{G_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-T_{P}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\rVert\\

hence

\begin{split}\lVert{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}\rVert&\leq 3\rVert T_{G_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})-T_{P}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}})\rVert\\
&\leq 3\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert\left\lVert(G_{n}-P)(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}})\right\rVert\\
&\leq 3\lVert{\mathb{A}}^{-1}\rVert\lVert G_{n}-P\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\\
&\rightarrow 0.\end{split}

This also implies

. Furthermore, we have

\begin{split}\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})-{\mathb{A}}\rVert&\leq\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})\rVert+\lVert\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(P,\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})-{\mathb{A}}\rVert\rightarrow 0\end{split}

because the first term is

and the second term goes to
0
by continuity of
\partial_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}S(P,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
at
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}
.
This means

\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n})\to{\mathb{A}}=-{\mathb{H}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}},

(S14)

so there is some
N

S13

\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}{t_{n}}=-(\partial_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}S(G_{n},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}_{n}))^{-1}h_{n}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\star}_{\omega_{0}}}).

(S15)

Finally, as

in

(S16)

Proof of Lemma

.

Let
\Pi
be the finite set of injections
\pi:\{1,\dots,K_{0}\}\hookrightarrow\{1,\dots,K\}
and define

Each
\mathsf{cost}_{\pi}
is continuous in
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
(composition of continuous maps).

On
\mathcal{U}_{\pi}

\pi
, hence

which is continuous there because each
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
is continuous.

Equip
\Pi
with the lexicographic order
\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}
: for
\pi,\rho\in\Pi
, write
\pi\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}\rho
if there exists
j^{\star}
with

\pi(j)=\rho(j)
for all

and
\pi(j^{\star})<\rho(j^{\star})

Here, each set
\{\mathsf{cost}_{\pi}\leq\mathsf{cost}_{\eta}\}
is closed and each

\{\mathsf{cost}_{\pi}<\mathsf{cost}_{\rho}\}
is open, so
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
is Borel.
On
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
, the tie-break selects
\pi
, hence the same formula for
\mathcal{M}
holds and
\mathcal{M}
is continuous there.

Finally,

\Theta_{K}=\Big(\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal{U}_{\pi}\Big)\cup\Big(\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\Big).

is a finite Borel partition on whose pieces

is continuous.

\mathcal{M}
is Borel measurable on
\Theta_{K}
.

Algorithm 4

Gaussian mixture GERVE (with mini-batches)

Derivation details.

The natural-gradient update rules are:

\displaystyle v_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle=v_{k,t}+\rho_{t}\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}},

\displaystyle{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle={\mathb{S}}_{k,t}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t}+\rho_{t}\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}_{k}^{(1)}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}},

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}{\mathb{S}}_{k,t}+\rho_{t}\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}_{k}^{(2)}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}.

We rearrange them to obtain:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t}+\rho_{t}{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}^{-1}(\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}^{(1)}_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}+2(\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}^{(2)}_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}){\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t}),

\displaystyle{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle={\mathb{S}}_{k,t}-2\rho_{t}\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}^{(2)}_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}},

\displaystyle v_{k,t+1}

\displaystyle=v_{k,t}+\rho_{t}\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{t}}.

We apply the chain rule to express the gradients w.r.t. the expectation parameters as gradients w.r.t.
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
and
{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}_{k}^{(1)}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}\left(\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})-2(\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})){\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\right),

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathb{m}}_{k}^{(2)}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

These gradients are

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})\right)+\omega_{t}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}}{N}{\mathb{S}}_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})+\omega_{t}\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}),

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})\right)+\omega_{t}\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}}{2N}{\mathb{S}}_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})({\mathb{X}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{T}{\mathb{S}}_{k}-{\mathb{I}})q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})+\omega_{t}\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

Given that
\pi_{K}=1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}
, we also have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega_{t},N}({\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})\right)+\omega_{t}\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}({\mathb{X}}_{i})-q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}}({\mathb{X}}_{i}))+\omega_{t}\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}).

Combining these expressions, we find the Gaussian mixture GERVE update rules.

Entropy derivatives (for Monte-Carlo estimation).

The entropy derivatives do not admit a closed form, but they admit an integral representation. For the derivatives w.r.t.
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
and
{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}
, the following expressions can be used:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathb{S}}_{k}^{-1}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}}{2}{\mathb{S}}_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}}[(({\mathb{X}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})({\mathb{X}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{T}{\mathb{S}}_{k}-{\mathb{I}})\log q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{X}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{X}})]

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\pi_{k}{\mathb{S}}_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}}[({\mathb{X}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})\log q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{X}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{X}})].

Finally, for the derivative w.r.t.
\pi_{k}
, we use:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\pi_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}}[\log q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{X}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{X}})]-\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}}}[\log q_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}({\mathb{X}})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathb{X}})].

S3.4
Computational considerations

Recall GERVE’s complexity for Gaussian mixtures with
K
components (Sec.
4.4
):

•

Full covariances:
O(d^{3}BKT)
.

•

Diagonal, isotropic or fixed covariances:
O(dBKT)
.

Simplified families sometimes retain most of the algorithm’s qualitative behaviour but improve scalability.

This is comparable to related methods:

•

Mean-shift mode-seeking.
A single run of classical mean-shift with fixed, spherical bandwidths and mini-batches costs
O(dBT)
. Running from
K
initialisations (to target
K
modes) yields
O(dBKT)
, comparable to fixed-covariance GERVE. However, unlike GERVE, these independent trajectories lack a repulsion mechanism and may fail to explore the full modal structure.

•

Mean-shift clustering.
Since mean-shift clustering launches one trajectory per data point, the cost is
O(dNBT)
for spherical bandwidths. This can be substantially more expensive than GERVE, which only launches
K
trajectories to find the cluster centroids.

•

EM for Gaussian mixtures (GMM-EM).
The EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al., 1977
;
McLachlan and Peel, 2000
)
with full covariances has cost
O\big((d^{2}B+d^{3})KT\big)

per run, due to matrix inversions and determinant computations. For diagonal covariances, it reduces to
O(dBKT)
, which is comparable to fixed-covariance GERVE. In contrast, GERVE uses unified natural-gradient steps rather than alternating E- and M-steps, and does not require likelihood maximisation, making it applicable in likelihood-free settings.

Model.

We use the same triangle configuration with means at

Metrics.

Each method is replicated

K

\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}=(\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}_{K})
.
We assess performance in terms of mode estimation using the following metrics:

•

Mode recovery
(
\mathsf{MR}_{\epsilon}
): number of true modes
\{{\mathb{u}}_{i},i\in[I]\}
recovered within a strict tolerance
\epsilon
:

\mathsf{MR}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{{\mathb{u}}})=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathds{1}\left\{\min_{k\in[K]}\big\|\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}_{k}-{\mathb{u}}_{i}\big\|_{2}<\epsilon\right\}.

We set
\epsilon=10^{-2}
, a small value relative to
\sigma=\sqrt{0.1}
.

•

Hungarian matching sum
(
HM
): minimum linear assignment cost between true and estimated modes,

\mathsf{HM}(\widehat{{\mathb{u}}})=\min_{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big\|\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}_{\tau(i)}-{\mathb{u}}_{i}\big\|_{2},

i.e., the minimum total distance over injective maps (
K\geq I
)
\tau:\{1,\dots,I\}\hookrightarrow\{1,\dots,K\}
(one distinct estimate per true mode). This can be computed via the Hungarian algorithm
(
Kuhn, 1955
)
.

•

Nearest-neighbor sum
(
NN
): aggregate distance from each estimate to the closest true mode,

\mathsf{NN}(\widehat{{\mathb{u}}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\min_{i\in[I]}\big\|\widehat{{\mathb{u}}}_{k}-{\mathb{u}}_{i}\big\|_{2}.

(iii) Annealing:

Appendix S6
Details on the UK collision case study

Data normalisation and processing.

We apply GERVE on a normalised space : the Greater London window
[-0.54,0.33]\times[51.28,51.70]
(longitude-latitude) into a centred rectangle
[-0.7,0.7]\times[-0.35,0.35]
preserving the aspect ratio and with area
\approx 1
. This transformation is applied to the coordinates of the data points. To smoothen the underlying distribution
p
, we generate artificial data points uniformly in the region between the normalised rectangle and the
[-2,2]\times[-2,2]
square, with density
1000
times smaller than the mean density of the data in the normalised rectangle.

GERVE hyperparameters.

We target the top
H=10
hotspots. We fit an overcomplete mixture with
K=2H=20
.

•

Temperature.
We fix the stopping temperature at
\omega^{\dagger}=1
. This choice follows the elbow of the resolved-mode curve represented in Figure
S10
and described in Sec.
7
. The same
\omega^{\dagger}
is used for the bootstrap refits.

•

Step sizes.
The step-size schedule is
\rho_{t}=0.2t^{-0.1}
.

•

Initialisation.
Component means use
k
-means++ centroids on the working coordinates. Initial covariances are
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k,1}=\sigma_{1}^{2}{\mathb{I}}
with
\sigma_{1}^{2}=5\times 10^{-3}
.

•

Covariance bounds.
We constrain
\sigma_{\min}^{2}=1\times 10^{-5}
and
\sigma_{\max}^{2}=1\times 10^{-2}
.

•

Domain.
Optimisation is performed on
\mathcal{S}=[-2.05,2.05]\times[-2.05,2.05]
. Mapping to British National Grid is used only for reporting distances and ellipses.

•

Entropy term.
We use
B=100
Monte Carlo samples per iteration to estimate the entropy contributions.

•

Early stopping.
We cap iterations at
T=10{,}000
. We stop early if the following hold at three consecutive checks, evaluated every 10 iterations:

\big\|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t+1}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k,t}\big\|_{2}<10^{-2}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\big\|{\mathb{S}}_{k,t+1}-{\mathb{S}}_{k,t}\big\|_{F}}{\big\|{\mathb{S}}_{k,t}\big\|_{F}}<10^{-1}\quad\text{for all }k.

•

Pruning and merging.
A component is pruned if

\mathrm{eig}_{\max}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})>\sigma_{\min}^{2}+\beta\big(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{\min}^{2}\big),

with
\beta\approx 0.018
, so that the threshold equals
10\sigma_{\min}^{2}
.
Components means within
0.005
(
\approx 200
meters in the British National Grid EPSG:27700) are merged.

•

Bootstrap settings.
We use
L=500
resamples at
\omega^{\dagger}
. Bootstrap modes are matched to the baseline with a Hungarian assignment and adaptive gates around each baseline mode as described below.

Figure S10:
Diagnostic for choosing
\omega
: resolved modes after successive pruning (dashed) and merging (solid) for values of
\omega
(in log-scale). The elbow at
\omega^{\dagger}=1
maximises resolved modes before decline.

Mode matching and per-mode ellipses.

We
z
-score coordinates using the baseline mode centres. For mode
k
, we compute
\mathrm{nn}_{k}
as the minimum distance to any other baseline mode. The gate is
\tau_{k}=\mathrm{clip}(\eta\mathrm{nn}_{k},\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max})
with defaults
\eta=0.35
,
\tau_{\min}=0.08
,
\tau_{\max}=0.22
. Since
\eta<0.5
, gates stay inside midpoints between modes, which prevents cross-assignments. The clips avoid degenerate gates when modes are extremely close or very isolated. After Hungarian assignment, pairs with distance larger than
\tau_{k}
are rejected. Accepted matches form the bootstrap cloud for mode
k
, from which we compute stability
s_{k}
and the 95% chi-square confidence ellipse.

To obtain confidence regions in meters, we project coordinates to the British National Grid (EPSG:27700). For each resolved mode
k
, let
\{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
denote the matched bootstrap centres. We report 95% confidence ellipses from the empirical covariance
\widehat{V}_{k}=\operatorname{Var}^{\ast}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}_{k}^{(\ell)})
: if
\mathrm{eig}_{1}\geq\mathrm{eig}_{2}
are eigenvalues of
\widehat{V}_{k}
and
{\mathb{e}}_{1}
is the principal eigenvector, then the semi-axes are
a=\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{2;1-\alpha}\mathrm{eig}_{1}}
and
b=\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{2;1-\alpha}\mathrm{eig}_{2}}
, with angle
\mathrm{atan2}({\mathb{e}}_{1,x},{\mathb{e}}_{1,y})
(in degrees) relative to Easting.

Figure
S11
shows, for each baseline mode, the cloud of matched bootstrap centres, the adaptive gate used for matching, and the resulting confidence ellipse, illustrating mode stability and localisation uncertainty.
