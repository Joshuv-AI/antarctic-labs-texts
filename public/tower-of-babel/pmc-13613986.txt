Bioethics and Medical Litigation in France: An Exploratory Study
Authors: Rania Nader, Henri‐Corto Stoeklé, Louisa Smail Shehba, Pascal Jazat, Christian Hervé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims and ObjectivesMedical litigation is a matter of particular concern for hospitals. We decided to examine whether bioethics might offer additional insights into these disputes.MethodWe conducted a highly exploratory and descriptive study in empirical bioethics, relying exclusively on a sample of scientific articles addressing medical litigation in France and primarily derived from real‐world healthcare settings. Analysis proceeded manually in three stages: (i) a content analysis of the sample; (ii) a thematic analysis of the collected information; and (iii) a bio‐ethical analysis of the identified themes and codes, and collected information.ResultsWe ultimately derived three broader themes, presented in descending order of importance: “Legal Claims”, “Poor Practices”, and “Specific Laws”. Informed by these themes, but also associated codes and collected information, we may already infer several ethical tensions between responsibility―but understood here as a fundamental moral value rather than a simple legal obligation to account for one's professional actions―and a wide range of health and life science practices, most of which relate to surgical interventions to dis day. However, it remains difficult to determine whether these tensions reflect a single macro‐bio‐ethical issue―that is, an ethical tension or dilemma between health and/or life science practices and moral values and/or standards at a societal or country level―or rather a set of distinct micro‐bio‐ethical issues―arising at the level of given individuals or institutions.ConclusionBioethics warrants closer engagement with such disputes, insofar as they might embody bio‐ethical issues of sufficient significance.

Introduction

In France, a “contentieux médical” in French, or a “medical litigation” in English, refers to a specific set of legal procedures designed to adjudicate medical disputes between two individuals or between an individual and a legal entity before the competent judicial authority [1]. In other words, within the French legal framework, a “contentieux” constitutes a dispute that has progressed to formal judicial proceedings; therefore, not all disputes reach this stage. Medical litigation is of particular significance for hospitals, as it may trigger a series of legal processes that vary in severity and in their potential consequences for all parties involved. Such cases are typically managed by the hospital's legal department, alongside other disputes or claims concerning the provision of healthcare services. In this context, the executive management of our hospital adopted an innovative approach by bringing together the Legal Department and the Ethics Department to examine whether bioethics might offer additional insights into these disputes. This initiative was undertaken through a highly exploratory and descriptive study in empirical bioethics, following the protocol template outlined by Stoeklé and Hervé in Health Research Policy and Systems in June 2025 [2].

Method

According to the typology of empirical bioethics research approaches proposed by Stoeklé and Hervé in The New Bioethics in July 2026 [3], the research approach was primarily qualitative, descriptive, and macro‐bio‐ethical. The research team included three principal co‐investigators and two additional co‐investigators. Two principal co‐investigators are bioethicists, while the third is an administrator with experience in the subject matter. The two additional co‐investigators are legal professionals. It should be note that this article substantially draws on the section of the master's thesis in health law completed by the first co‐author that relied on a sample of scientific articles primarily derived from real‐world healthcare settings. The thesis was prepared during a 3‐month internship in the hospital's ethics department between May and August 2025. It should also be noted that the initially submitted manuscript and accompanying tables have since undergone extensive revisions in response to the constructive comments and suggestions provided by reviewers and editors.

Sampling of scientific articles was conducted manually using Google Scholar, and discontinued upon reaching thematic saturation; with saturation assessed with regard to the inferred themes rather than the codes. We included articles, published primarily in Open Access (N.B., to ensure transparency and facilitate access to our sources for peers, colleagues and stakeholders), between 2005 and 2025, in either French or English, that addressed medical litigation in France. Information was manually collected and processed using Microsoft Office and subsequently stored in hospital professional accounts. The information consisted of references and abstracts. All information supporting the findings of this study are available within the paper. Analysis proceeded manually in three stages: (i) a global content analysis of the sample based on the collected information; (ii) an inductive and reflexive thematic analysis involving the coding of the collected information, and the grouping of codes into broader themes informed by that information; and (iii) a preliminary and reflexive bio‐ethical analysis informed by the themes, codes, and information―the results of which are finally presented in the Discussion rather than in the Results section owing to the highly preliminary and reflexive nature of this third level of analysis. Both manuscript and tables were written manually in French, but the translation into English―and the subsequent English language revisions―were performed automatically using Microsoft 365 Copilot.

Results

Our sample comprises 28 scientific articles (n = 28), including 22 written in French (Table 1, articles #1–22), and 6 in English (Table 1, articles #23–28). The French‐language articles are predominantly published in academic journals specializing in law, with a smaller proportion appearing in journals focused on health, as well as in the humanities and social sciences. In contrast, the English‐language articles are published exclusively in health‐related journals. Notable methodological differences emerge between the two language groups. French‐language articles primarily employ strictly legal or qualitative approaches, whereas English‐language articles rely almost exclusively on quantitative methods, particularly statistical techniques. Taken together, this body of literature offers general and/or specific descriptive insights into medical litigation in France. However, its comprehensiveness cannot be asserted due to the sampling strategy employed.

Discussion

We could reasonably expect this sample to consist mostly of scientific articles published in specialized, French‐language legal journals (Table 1). At least initially, medical litigation is undeniably a legal matter, typically with country‐specific legal nuances. It therefore makes sense that researchers in France would primarily publish their findings in such journals. What we did not necessarily expect, however, might be the predominance of the theme “Legal Claims” over that of “Poor Practices”. After all, one or more instances of poor practice—real or alleged, intentional or unintentional—are usually the first explicit cause of this type of complaint, as suggested by the strong presence of the code “medical accident”. Yet it seems that legal consequences of poor practices could attract more interest than poor practices themselves. That seems entirely appropriate given the topic. Still, it would be just as valuable to pay more attention to ways of preventing the causes of such litigation—chiefly poor practice, even when presumed and/or unintentional.

Informed by these themes, codes and information, we may already infer several ethical tensions between responsibility―but understood here as a fundamental moral value rather than a simple legal obligation to account for one's professional actions―and a wide range of health and life science practices. Notably, two medical specialties, namely digestive surgery (Table 2a, “Malpractice Claims, Digestive Surgery” and “Nosocomial Infection, Digestive Surgery”) and orthopedic and trauma surgery (Table 2a, “Medical Accident, Orthopedic and Trauma Surgery” and “Nosocomial Infection, Orthopedic and Trauma Surgery”), are represented by more than one combination of codes. By contrast, other identifiable specialties are represented by only a single combination, namely neurosurgery (Table 2a, “Medical Accident, Neurosurgery”) and otorhinolaryngology (Table 2a, “Nosocomial Infection, ENT (Otolaryngology)”), to which transplantation may also be added (Table 2a, “Transplant, Post‐Operative Complications”). In other words, most of the identified practices relate to surgical interventions to this day.

It remains difficult to determine whether these tensions reflect a single macro‐bio‐ethical issue―that is, an ethical tension or dilemma between health and/or life science practices and moral values and/or standards at a societal or country level―or rather a set of distinct micro‐bio‐ethical issues―arising at the level of given individuals or institutions. This distinction is far from trivial, as the challenge may then lie not in resolving a single bio‐ethical issue but several, albeit closely related, ones. This is, in our view, one of the main limitations of the present study (N.B., beyond the non‐exhaustive nature of the sampling strategy, or the highly reflexive nature of the inductive thematic and preliminary bio‐ethical analysis), likely because our analysis relied on information, namely published analyses of primary and/or secondary data, rather than on the data themselves. Furthermore, empirical material directly consisting of data, and gathered across multiple centers, would appear better suited to the implementation of a normative approach (N.B., indeed, as explained in the Methods section, and consistently applied throughout the study, we restricted our analysis to a descriptive approach, namely the identification and description of one or more bio‐ethical issues, without proposing specific solutions or recommendations based on the application of any particular ethical theory).

Conclusion

Admittedly, medical litigation has not traditionally been regarded as falling within the domain of bioethics. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that bioethics warrants closer engagement with such disputes, insofar as they may embody bio‐ethical issues of sufficient significance. One might envisage addressing these issues through a two‐tiered approach: first, through local ethics committees, and second, through empirical bioethics studies. In this respect, local ethics committees may operate primarily in the short term, whereas empirical bioethics studies may contribute over the longer term. In hospital settings, for example, when such issues are, explicitly or implicitly, raised by healthcare professionals and/or members of executive management, the role of the local ethics committee would be to bring together the stakeholders involved, or potentially involved, in these issues in order, first, to clarify their nature and origins and, second, to develop solutions or recommendations that are both appropriate and broadly acceptable. The role of empirical bioethics studies would then be to deepen understanding of these issues, thereby refining, complementing, and potentially generalizing the solutions or recommendations proposed in practice.
