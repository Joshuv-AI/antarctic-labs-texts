Chronic adrenal infarction presenting as an incidentaloma: Three-year radiological follow-up
Authors: Gabriela Vieira Bon, Alessandro Severo Alves de Melo, Ana Beatriz Winter Tavares, Juliana Werneck Coelho, Aline Barbosa Moraes, Giselle Fernandes Taboada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This is a descriptive observational study conducted at a University Hospital. We report the case of a 34-year-old woman with a right adrenal mass detected incidentally on computed tomography, presenting atypical radiological characteristics that raised suspicion of malignancy. Hormonal investigation ruled out hypersecretion, and the patient underwent adrenalectomy. Histopathological examination revealed chronic adrenal infarction. Retrospective review of medical records identified a previous episode of severe COVID-19 during pregnancy, and re-evaluation of the computed tomography scan performed at that time allowed us to document the evolution of the adrenal infarction from the acute phase to the formation of a chronic mass over 3 years. The case illustrates an unusual presentation of adrenal infarction as an incidentaloma and highlights the importance of clinical-radiological correlation in the differential diagnosis of adrenal masses. Data collection was performed through review of medical records.

Introduction

Adrenal incidentaloma (AI) affects 2% of the general population, with prevalence increasing with age, reaching up to 10% in subjects older than 70 years [[1], [2], [3]]. The evaluation of an AI aims to determine both the presence of inapparent hormonal hypersecretion and the potential for malignancy [1]. While nonfunctioning adrenal adenomas represent the most frequent etiology, awareness of rarer causes is essential for clinicians and radiologists managing these patients.

We report an unprecedented cause of AI: a chronic unilateral adrenal infarction with anatomopathological correlation. Although adrenal infarction is a recognized cause of abdominal pain, particularly in pregnant women and patients with severe COVID-19, to the best of our knowledge, there has been no previous description of the radiological 3-year follow-up of an unilateral adrenal infarction presenting as an incidentaloma.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old female presented in April 2024 with a 1-week history of right-sided lower back pain, described as a tight, constricting sensation of severe intensity radiating to the right hypochondrium. The pain was accompanied by high fever (39°C/102.2°F), chills, nausea, vomiting, and oliguria. Physical examination revealed a Body Mass Index of 34 kg/m², blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, cervical acanthosis nigricans, and unremarkable heart and lung auscultations. No acne, violet stretch marks, hirsutism, or facial plethora were observed. The abdomen was painful on palpation of the right hypochondrium and flank, with notable right costovertebral angle tenderness.

Computed tomography of the abdomen (Fig. 1) revealed right kidney stones and expansile nodular formation in the right adrenal gland. The mass measured 3.9 cm in its longest axis and exhibited soft tissue density, with precontrast attenuation of 40 HU (Fig. 1). Following contrast administration, the lesion showed lobulated contours and peripheral enhancement with no washout as it maintained the same density across all phases (Fig. 1). These nonspecific features, notably a precontrast density higher than 10 HU and a peripheral enhancement pattern, raised concern for the potential malignant nature of the right adrenal incidentaloma. Ceftriaxone, tamsulosin and analgesia were prescribed for complicated kidney stones and the patient recovered well, with spontaneous kidney stones expulsion after 9 days. Due to the suspicious radiological features, patient underwent right adrenalectomy in May 2024. Histological examination revealed extensive adrenal chronic diffuse infarction, fibrosis, and calcification forming a mass measuring 6.5 × 6.0 cm. Remaining viable adrenal tissue corresponded to approximately 20% of the gland and showed no malignancy (Fig. 2). Discussion

This report highlights an unprecedented cause of adrenal incidentaloma: chronic unilateral adrenal infarction presenting in a patient with a past history of severe COVID-19 during pregnancy, raising important considerations regarding pathophysiology, diagnosis, and clinical follow-up. To our knowledge, the appearance of an adrenal infarction in a long-term chronic phase has not been previously described. Prior reports have mainly focused on descriptions of the acute phase or short-term follow-up (4-6 months), describing either resolution or residual atrophy of the affected adrenal gland. The present radiological description not only enhances the understanding of the natural history of the lesion but also provides valuable information for distinguishing chronic infarction from other adrenal masses in clinical practice.

Abdominal pain is a frequent complaint during pregnancy and is often associated with a wide differential diagnosis. Identifying the cause is particularly challenging mainly due to physiological body changes such as organ displacement and altered laboratory parameters [4,5]. Nonhemorrhagic adrenal infarction (NHAI) is rarely suspected in this setting, with fewer than 1 hundred cases related during pregnancy in literature. Most of these describe the acute phase and lack long term imaging follow-up [6,7]. Moreover, even if suspected, NHAI diagnosis remains challenging due to imaging limitations in pregnancy, particularly concerns about accuracy and fetal radiation exposure [4,6].

The adrenal glands have a unique vascular architecture that predisposes them to infarction. They receive a rich arterial supply from 3 major arteries but drain through a single central vein. This configuration makes them vulnerable to blood stasis and subsequent thrombosis in the setting of impaired venous drainage or increased arterial inflow [5]. Furthermore, pregnancy itself is a hypercoagulable state, with increased levels of prothrombotic factors such as fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII, X and von Willebrand, together with decreased free protein S [6]. These changes contributes to increased thrombogenic risk, including adrenal infarction.

Additionally, emerging postpandemic evidence indicates that COVID-19 infection disrupts endothelial function and the coagulation cascade, further predisposing to thrombotic events [7]. In a retrospective cohort of 219 patients with severe COVID-19, imaging findings suggestive of adrenal infarction were identified in 23% of cases, predominantly bilateral, reinforcing the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and adrenal vascular events. Notably, this study did not specifically address pregnant patients [7]. In the present case, the patient experienced severe COVID-19 during pregnancy, which likely amplified the thrombotic risk and contributed to adrenal infarction. Remarkably, the infarction was not identified in the acute phase during hospitalization for COVID-19, but was retrospectively diagnosed as a chronic lesion on follow-up imaging. The presence of fibrosis and calcification on histopathology supports this hypothesis and reinforces the importance of long-term surveillance of patients with risk factors for adrenal vascular events. This case illustrates how unrecognized adrenal infarction may subsequently manifest as AI.

Considering the increasing number of patients recovering from severe COVID-19, it is plausible that adrenal infarction is underdiagnosed and should be considered more frequently in the differential diagnosis of AI, especially in individuals with a history of severe systemic illness [8,9].

Histopathological examination revealed extensive chronic diffuse infarction, surrounded by fibrosis with multiple calcification foci, forming a voluminous mass. These findings align with Fox's [10] classification of adrenal infarction, specifically type 2 pattern in the chronic phase (>10 days), characterized by extensive ischemic necrosis with minimal hemorrhage, structural disorganization, and thick fibrous tissue extending into necrotic areas. Notably, the calcification observed in our case represents a previously undocumented evolution in the literature, likely related to the extended 3-year follow-up period. The imaging evolution from acute infarction (2021) to chronic calcified mass (2024) provides the first radiological documentation of long-term adrenal infarction progression, as prior reports by Woolley [11] and Fox [10] focused primarily on acute histopathological changes. This case offers unique insight into the natural history of adrenal infarction, demonstrating how acute venous thrombosis can evolve into a calcified pseudotumoral mass that mimics malignancy on imaging, emphasizing the importance of clinical history in evaluating adrenal incidentalomas.

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of considering chronic adrenal infarction in the differential diagnosis of AI, particularly in patients with a history of severe infection or hypercoagulable states. Its uniqueness lies in the long term follow-up, which provided radiological evidence of the acute and chronic phases of the infarction, underscoring the value of serial imaging in elucidating disease progression. The documentation of this evolution adds to the limited literature linking pregnancy complications and COVID-19 to adrenal vascular events. Physicians should be aware of the delayed presentation of adrenal infarction and consider prior systemic inflammatory or thrombotic events as potential contributors to adrenal pathology. Further studies are warranted to investigate the prevalence and long-term outcomes of adrenal infarction in post-COVID-19 patients, with the ultimate goal of improving diagnostic strategies and patient care.
