Percutaneous Removal of Cardiac Papillary Fibroelastomas
Authors: Robert Seby, Goyal Umadat, Samantha Espinosa, Prajwal Reddy, Pragnesh Parikh, Jordan Ray, Melanie C. Bois, S. Michael Gharacholou, Kyle W. Klarich, Abdallah El Sabbagh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Papillary fibroelastomas (PFEs) are primary cardiac tumors associated with significant embolic risk. Surgical excision or medical therapy with antiplatelets and/or anticoagulants have been the treatment options for symptomatic patients. This paper reports our early experiences of percutaneous removal of PFE using mechanical aspiration techniques.

Cardiac papillary fibroelastomas (PFEs) are common primary cardiac tumors and are associated with stroke and systemic embolism.1 Conventional treatment for PFEs has been surgical excision.2 Patients who are not surgical candidates are conservatively managed with antiplatelets and/or antithrombotics, but it remains unclear if this lowers embolic risk because PFEs can embolize in 2 ways: tumor embolization or adherent thrombus embolism.1 We report a series of early experience with removal of PFEs using percutaneous mechanical aspiration (PMA) techniques. All patients were deemed to be high or prohibitive risk for surgery, and all procedures were performed using cerebral embolic protection. Case 1

A 77-year-old woman with a history of transcatheter mitral valve repair who presented with recurrent stroke was found to have a small 3-mm PFE (Figure 1A). Given the patient’s history of stroke, removal of the PFE was recommended using a Penumbra Lightning 12 (PL12) (Penumbra Inc). This device is an engine-based catheter that generates modest, intermittent aspiration forces and was the only device available at the time of the procedure with adequate length to reach the PFE via a retrograde approach. Right femoral arterial access was preclosed and upsized to a 12-F Medtronic FlexCath (Medtronic) sheath, through which the PL12 was introduced. Through the left femoral artery, a 25-mm EN Snare (Merit Medical) was introduced and used to grip the distal tip of the PL12 and improve steering of the aspiration catheter (Figure 1B). Under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance, the snared catheter was aligned with the PFE and advanced toward it with active aspiration (Figures 1C and 1D). Despite adequately engaging the PFE, repeated aspiration attempts were unsuccessful, likely due to its strong adhesion to the aortic valve and modest aspiration forces generated by the device. Case 2

A 65-year-old woman with a history of coronary artery disease presented with a recurrent stroke. TEE showed a mobile 6- × 7-mm mass attached to the atrial side of the anterior mitral valve leaflet consistent with PFE (Figures 2A and 2B). A decision was made to proceed with PMA via a transseptal puncture using PL12. The PL12 was introduced transseptally through a steerable 12-F Medtronic FlexCath and aligned with the PFE (Figure 2C), and aspiration was initiated (Figures 2D to 2F). Multiple aspiration runs resulted in a reduction in the size of the PFE to 2.5 × 3.5 mm (Figure 2G). The residual PFE and stalk appeared to be well adhered to the mitral valve and could not be removed despite repeated aspiration. Case 3

An 81-year-old woman with lung cancer and previous chemoradiation underwent a routine TTE which revealed a 12- × 10-mm aortic PFE (Figure 3A). Given the high embolic risk, she was referred for PMA. At the time of the procedure, the F18 AngioVac (Angiodynamics) was available, an additional device long enough for retroaortic approach. The F18 AngioVac system provides continuous flow aspiration via an extracorporeal membrane oxygenation circuit that draws blood through a filter to trap aspirated specimens followed by blood return to the body via a separate access return cannula. A 22-F sheath was used for femoral access with preclosure. An 18-F return cannula was placed in the femoral vein. After establishing the flow circuit, the F18 Angiovac cannula was advanced to the ascending aorta and circuit flow was initiated at low speed (Figures 3C and 3D). Given the relatively firm adhesion of the PFE stalk, an upfront snare from a separate arterial access was inserted to help cut the stalk while concomitantly aspirating, similar to a previously described technique.3 The aspiration catheter was positioned in proximity to the PFE, and continuous aspiration was initiated. This allowed locking the mobility of the PFE and facilitated deployment of the 25-mm Gooseneck snare (Medtronic) around its stalk, while mitigating distal embolization by aspirating potential debris that may theoretically dislodge during manipulation of the PFE (Figures 3E to 3H). Circuit speeds were ramped up, the snare tightened to cut the stalk, albeit challenging to visualize on transesophageal echocardiography, and the PFE was successfully aspirated (Figures 3I and 3J). The specimen in saline had typical sea urchin-like morphology consistent with PFE (Figures 3N and 3O). Postaspiration imaging revealed moderate to severe aortic regurgitation, confirmed both on imaging and hemodynamics, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. The mechanism of injury was likely mechanical from the snare pulling the aortic leaflet tip and the challenge with visualizing the snare position with respect to the stalk of the PFE (Figures 3K and 3L). Given the hemodynamic stability, and the moderate to severe quantification, a decision was made to defer transcatheter aortic valve replacement. There was no material collected in the retrieved cerebral embolic protection device. However, the patient noted worsening dyspnea on exertion in the following weeks, and underwent successful transcatheter aortic valve replacement using an oversized self-expanding valve using 26-mm Evolut FX (Medtronic) in the setting of pure aortic regurgitation and lack of calcium (transcatheter aortic valve replacement planning parameters: aortic valve annulus area: 320 mm2, annular perimeter: 66 mm, sinotubular junction diameter: 27 mm, sinotubular junction height: 24 mm, sinus of Valsalva diameter 30 × 28 × 27 mm, left coronary height: 13 mm and right coronary height: 18 mm, aortic valve calcium score: 0) 4 weeks after PMA (Figure 3M). Case 4

A 64 year-old male with history of coronary artery disease status post coronary artery bypass surgery and rheumatoid arthritis was found to have a 9 x 6 mm PFE on TEE (Figure 4A). Given the embolic risk associated with the PFE, and given his high surgical risk, a decision was made to proceed with PMA with AngioVac F18 via retrograde approach. The device, technique and approach were similar to Case 3 (Figure 4B to 4D) with some exceptions. 2 snares were used in this case: a 25 mm Gooseneck snare was used as a cutting snare and was connected to a Bovie pencil, and a 25 mm Ensnare was used to snare the F18 aspiration catheter to provide more flexion and reach given the horizontal nature of the patient's aorta. Once the F18 was aligned with the PFE and aspiration flows were turned on at low speeds, the 25 mm Gooseneck snare inside a 6 Fr JR4 guide catheter was wrapped around the PFE and tightened around the stalk (Figures 4E and 4F). The aspiration speeds were ramped up and the stalk was cut using the electrified snare that was connected to the bovie at a setting of 70 Watts cutting mode. The PFE was successfully removed and retrieved. Case 5

An 85-year-old woman with recurrent strokes was found to have a chordal PFE on TEE (10 × 13 mm) (Figures 5A and 5B). PMA was recommended, and a 3-dimensional printed model (Figure 5C) confirmed a retroaortic approach was feasible. A similar technique as in case 3 was planned using F18 AngioVac and concomitant snaring of the stalk. Flows were initiated at low speeds in the ascending aorta, opening the aortic valve and allowing delivery of the F18 AngioVac funnel into the left ventricle toward the chordal mass. With low continuous suction flowing, the F18 was advanced in proximity to the PFE. It was noted however that at this low flow speed, the cannula successfully aspirated the mass before delivering the snare around the stalk (Figures 5D to 5H) or ramping up the flow speeds. There were no procedural complications, and no material was found in the retrieved cerebral embolic protection device. Discussion

This case series of the early experience of percutaneous removal of PFEs showed the following: 1) small PFEs <10 mm are challenging to remove due to their firm adhesion and difficulty visualizing the catheter-mass interaction on intraprocedural imaging; 2) aspiration of large PFEs >10 mm without snaring and cutting the stalk may result in decreasing the size of the PFE, which in turn makes it difficult to remove; 3) large PFEs >10 mm can be feasibly removed using continuous flow aspiration and an upfront snare around the stalk to cut it; and 4) the strength of the adhesion of the stalk can be variable and its amenability to aspiration alone without snare-cutting the stalk can be unpredictable.

Cardiac PFEs present a risk for cerebrovascular events at rates of 6% at 1 year and 13% at 5 years.1 Due to these risks, surgical resection of PFEs has been recommended. In a study by Mazur et al,2 PFE removal was feasible with no operative mortality, however there was an associated 2% postoperative stroke rate as well as 8.8% rate of needing additional valve surgery. Moreover, there was 15.8% rate of recurrence of PFE after surgical resection. In another study, up to 17% of patients with PFE were managed medically with antithrombotics with unknown risk reduction.1 Therefore, there may be an unmet need for percutaneous options for PFE removal.

Percutaneous removal of PFEs was previously described in 2 case reports: both of which used noncontinuous flow aspiration devices in large left-sided PFEs and highlighted the high embolic risk associated with these devices and techniques, with one case showing moderate amount of material in the cerebral embolic protection device3 and another sustaining a stroke.4 The current case series revealed the early lessons and technical evolution that allowed removal of PFEs while minimizing distal embolization through the introduction of continuous flow aspiration. Technical challenges with percutaneous removal of PFEs are associated with their anatomic characteristics, including variability in size and strength of stalk adhesion and the risk for embolization. Based on this early experience, larger PFEs (>10 mm) appear more favorable for percutaneous removal given their ease of visualization. Continuous flow aspiration is recommended to minimize distal embolization and generate adequate aspiration forces. The use of the F18 AngioVac in the current case series was novel and facilitated percutaneous removal of PFE using continuous flow aspiration to mitigate the risk of distal embolization and provide strong aspiration forces. The F18 has a long reach and a cannula size compatible with transfemoral retroaortic approach in patients with vessel size of 8 mm or more. Its use, compared with noncontinuous flow aspiration devices, allows manipulation and snaring of the PFE with ongoing continuous flow aspiration that would allow aspiration of any debris. It would also generate higher aspiration forces compared with other devices, which is sometimes required for PFE removal. The strength of the PFE stalk may be unpredictable and as a result we recommend an upfront use of a snare in all percutaneous removal of PFEs. This is especially important in large PFEs because aspiration alone can decrease the size of the mass without severing its connection to the underlying structure, making it harder to aspirate the residual small mass. The use of electrocautery applied on the snare may help facilitate cutting the stalk without requiring forceful tensioning of the snare; however, the risk of injury to the aortic valve with this is also unknown, especially because visualization of the exact location of the tightened snare, whether around the stalk or the aortic leaflet tip, is challenging. Conclusions

Percutaneous removal of PFEs is feasible using continuous-flow aspiration. Large PFEs are more favored for percutaneous removal and adherence strength of the stalk to endocardial surfaces varies and may require loop-snaring as an adjunct to aspiration.
