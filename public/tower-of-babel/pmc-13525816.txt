Research Progress of Molecular H2 in Mediating Immune Tolerance via Regulating Tregs—from the Perspective of the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine
Authors: Xiaolin Ding, Wenjie Tang, Yumei Wang, Jinfeng Tian, Jingfei Shi, Youzhen Wei, Chao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Mary E. Brunkow, Fred Ramsdell, and Shimon Sakaguchi in recognition of their pioneering contributions to the field of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and peripheral immune tolerance—defining Tregs as the core regulatory subset maintaining immune homeostasis and identifying the transcription factor FOXP3 as decisive for Treg development and functional maintenance, which lays the foundation for elucidating the mechanisms of immune imbalance-related diseases. As a novel bioactive molecule, molecular hydrogen (H2) possesses advantages of antioxidation, low toxicity, and multiple administration routes. Preliminary and accumulating studies suggest that H2 may protect Tregs’ survival, enhance their proliferative activity and immunosuppressive function by inhibiting oxidative stress, regulating inflammatory signaling pathways (eg, NF-κB, TGF-β/Smad), and modulating apoptotic pathways. Integrating the core findings of the Nobel Prize, this review systematically summarizes the molecular mechanisms underlying H2-mediated Treg regulation, collates disease-specific evidence in autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, tissue injury repair, and other scenarios, analyzes the heterogeneous characteristics of Treg regulation mediated by different administration routes, and dissects current research limitations and clinical translation bottlenecks. It is important to emphasize that the available clinical support for H2-mediated Treg regulation is restricted to single case reports and small-scale exploratory open-label studies, which represent hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming evidence. Finally, this review outlines the development direction of H2 combined with Treg-targeted strategies, noting that the current evidence remains preliminary and inconclusive, providing a new theoretical basis and technical insights for the precise treatment of immune-related diseases.

Introduction

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded to Mary E. Brunkow, Fred Ramsdell, and Shimon Sakaguchi for their foundational work on Tregs and immune tolerance, underscores the pivotal role of this research field in basic and translational immunology. This award marks a critical stage in the translational progression of Treg research from laboratory discovery to clinical application.

In 1995, Sakaguchi first identified the CD4⁺CD25⁺ T cell subset in mice, demonstrating that its depletion induces fatal multiorgan autoimmune diseases and thus establishes Tregs as the core “gatekeeper” of peripheral immune tolerance. Subsequently, the teams of Brunkow and Ramsdell independently discovered that the transcription factor FOXP3 serves as the master regulatory factor governing Treg development and functional maintenance. Foxp3 mutations cause the scurfy phenotype in mice and immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked syndrome in humans, further demonstrating the indispensable role of Tregs in maintaining immune homeostasis.

Since these landmark discoveries, research on Treg biology has continued to deepen.1 Modern immunology has clearly established that Tregs are not a functionally homogeneous and static population but exhibit high heterogeneity, plasticity, and functional specialization within the tissue microenvironment.2 Under pathological conditions such as chronic inflammation, metabolic disorders, or tumors, some Tregs may lose FOXP3 expression and functional stability, transforming into pro-inflammatory “ex-Treg” cells that further exacerbate immune imbalance.3 Therefore, maintaining Treg stability and functionality—rather than merely focusing on cellular quantity—has become the core goal of targeted immune intervention.

However, current therapeutic strategies targeting Tregs still face numerous clinical challenges.4 For instance, Treg adoptive cell therapy is limited by insufficient cell sources, low in vitro expansion efficiency, and poor in vivo persistence; low-dose IL-2 can selectively expand Tregs but carries the risk of concomitant effector T cell activation; immunomodulators such as glucocorticoids and vitamin D lack tissue specificity and frequently induce systemic adverse effects.5 These limitations highlight an urgent demand to develop safe, precise, and clinically accessible strategies for Treg modulation.

A key unmet need in Treg-targeted therapy is to identify cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory agents that can stabilize Treg function without disrupting systemic immune homeostasis. Oxidative stress and exaggerated inflammatory signaling are well-recognized drivers of Treg instability and dysfunction under diverse pathological conditions. This provides a sound biological rationale for exploring redox-modulating molecules characterized by high safety and low immunotoxicity.

Against this critical unmet need, molecular H2, an emerging redox-modulating and cytoprotective bioactive gas, has emerged as a promising candidate for immunoregulation and Treg protection.6 H2 possesses unique advantages such as selective scavenging of hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), absence of cytotoxicity, free penetration across biological membranes (including the blood-brain barrier), and convenient administration via multiple routes including inhalation, HRW ingestion, or H2-saline injection.7 Given its capacity to preferentially target oxidative stress and exaggerated inflammation, two core pathological drivers of Treg instability, H2 represents a biologically plausible, safe, and effective modulator of Treg-dependent immune regulation. In recent years, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that H2 may not only protect Tregs against oxidative-stress-induced damage but also enhance Tregs stability and immunosuppressive function through indirect mechanisms such as remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment, regulating macrophage polarization, and modulating gut-microbiota-related metabolism.8 Notably, H2-mediated regulation of key signaling pathways including NF-κB, TGF-β/Smad, PI3K-AKT, and Nrf2 is closely associated with Treg development, survival, and function.9–12

Apart from molecular H2, recent multidisciplinary studies have reported that multiple synthetic nanomaterials and small-molecule compounds also exhibit prominent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-apoptotic-regulating pharmacological properties, offering abundant candidate resources for the development of immunomodulatory agents.13–15 Laser-synthesized platinum nanoparticles and graphene-oxide nanoparticles display powerful antioxidant capacity validated via the DPPH assay, together with antimicrobial and tumor-suppressing bioactivities.13,14 Novel pyrazoline-derived small-molecule compounds also show favorable molecular-interaction characteristics predicted by DFT calculations and molecular docking, exhibiting potential antitumor pharmacological value.15 Nevertheless, it should be pointed out that most of these material-based pharmacological investigations are currently restricted to in vitro cell or microbiological experiments; systematic research exploring how these agents regulate Treg-mediated immune tolerance remains limited. Compared with these novel synthetic agents, molecular H2 exhibits distinctive advantages including high biosafety, membrane-permeability, and diverse administration approaches, rendering it worthy of further in-depth exploration as a Treg-targeted immunomodulator.

In this review, we focus exclusively on exogenous molecular H2 administered via inhalation, HRW ingestion, or H2-saline injection; endogenously generated H2 produced by gut microbiota falls outside the scope of the present discussion. Accumulating preclinical and preliminary clinical observations suggest that exogenous H2-mediated Treg modulation holds particular promise for autoimmune disorders (including Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, and rheumatoid arthritis), chronic intestinal inflammation, transplant-associated immune rejection, and maternal-fetal immune-tolerance-related complications. Nevertheless, an ongoing academic debate exists regarding the primary mode of action of molecular H2: one dominant hypothesis posits that H2 acts mainly as a selective antioxidant that directly neutralizes cytotoxic reactive oxygen species such as hydroxyl radicals; an alternative viewpoint holds that H2 functions predominantly as a signaling-modulating molecule that indirectly reshapes intracellular signaling cascades, gene-expression profiles, and redox-sensitive pathways, rather than merely acting as a radical scavenger. This review integrates evidence supporting both perspectives without adopting a one-sided stance, and discusses how these two proposed mechanisms may jointly contribute to Treg immune homeostasis.

Landmark Discovery in Peripheral Immune Tolerance: Biological Significance of Treg

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Japanese scientist Shimon Sakaguchi and American scientists Mary E. Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell in recognition of their landmark contributions to elucidating peripheral immune tolerance mechanisms—identifying Tregs and their core regulatory gene Foxp3, deciphering the key regulatory mechanism underlying immune system “self-tolerance”, and laying a solid theoretical foundation for therapeutic advances in autoimmune diseases, malignancies, organ transplantation, and other immune-related disorders. This pivotal discovery redefined the regulatory logic of immune homeostasis, advancing immunological research from a “central tolerance-dominated” paradigm to a new framework featuring coordinated central and peripheral immune tolerance. Its scientific value spans basic research and clinical translation, profoundly reshaping the understanding of immune imbalance-associated disorders and optimizing corresponding therapeutic intervention strategies.

Cellular Basis of Peripheral Immune Tolerance: Discovery and Functional Confirmation of Treg

Peripheral immune tolerance constitutes a core physiological mechanism that prevents endogenous autoimmune attacks, and the discovery of Tregs provides an essential cellular and molecular foundation for elucidating this critical immunological mechanism.16 Prior to the formal identification of Tregs, the immunological community generally held that immune tolerance depended predominantly on central tolerance, represented by negative selection of autoreactive lymphocytes within the thymus. However, accumulating clinical observations and animal model evidence demonstrated that numerous autoreactive T cells could escape central clonal deletion, migrate to peripheral tissues, and nonetheless fail to trigger autoimmune pathology, thereby implying the existence of compensatory peripheral tolerance machinery.17 This longstanding “peripheral tolerance puzzle” remained unsolved for decades, until Sakaguchi’s groundbreaking study in 1995 achieved this pivotal breakthrough.

In 1995, Sakaguchi et al first identified the CD4⁺ T-cell subset expressing the IL-2 receptor α chain (CD25) (ie, CD4⁺CD25⁺ Tregs) and confirmed their key role in maintaining immune tolerance: depletion of this cell population in mice induced multiorgan autoimmune diseases, while adoptive transfer of Tregs effectively restored immune tolerance. Their mechanism primarily involves suppressing effector T-cell activation in an antigen-nonspecific manner, thereby limiting excessive immune responses directed against self- and foreign antigens.18 This discovery not only revealed Tregs’ core role as a “brake” of peripheral tolerance but also provided a novel perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. From 1995 to 2001, research within this field focused on refining Treg phenotypic characteristics, clarifying tissue-distribution patterns, and verifying functional specificity. Studies confirmed that Tregs do not constitute a homogeneous population; their enrichment at inflammatory sites is strongly correlated with the efficiency of tolerance maintenance.19 Meanwhile, the IL-2-STAT5 signaling pathway was identified as a key regulatory axis for Treg survival and proliferation, offering the first potential target for subsequent strategies aimed at targeted modulation of Treg function.20 Additionally, studies from this period initially uncovered the molecular mechanisms through which Tregs exert inhibitory effects by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, challenging the early simplistic view that suppression occurs solely through direct cell-to-cell contact. These findings laid the groundwork for comprehensively deciphering the immunoregulatory network of Tregs and facilitated the shift in therapeutic strategies for autoimmune diseases from generalized “immunosuppression” toward “immune reconstitution”.21,22

In 2001, the teams of Brunkow and Ramsdell further uncovered the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying Treg function through studies on “scurfy” mutant mice.23 These mice succumb to death within weeks of birth owing to an X-linked recessive mutation, which is characterized by uncontrolled CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation, multiorgan tissue infiltration, and hypercytokinemia. Using positional-cloning techniques, the group identified the pathogenic gene as Foxp3, which encodes a transcription factor belonging to the forkhead/winged-helix family. In scurfy mice, a frameshift mutation in Foxp3 leads to deletion of its critical DNA-binding domain. Subsequent investigations confirmed that mutations within the human FOXP3 gene cause immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked syndrome, establishing FOXP3 not merely as the “master regulatory factor” for Treg development and functional maintenance but also as an indispensable core regulator of immune homeostasis. After 2001, Treg research entered an expansive phase characterized by in-depth exploration of molecular mechanisms and advancing translational applications. In 2004, several laboratories independently verified that forced Foxp3 expression can reprogram conventional CD4⁺ T-cells into functional Tregs, solidifying its status as the “molecular switch” governing Treg-lineage differentiation.24–26 Subsequent work further revealed that FOXP3 shapes the suppressive phenotype of Tregs by modulating the expression of downstream target genes including CTLA-4 and GITR, offering precise molecular targets for the intentional modification of Treg function.27,28 At the translational level, FOXP3-based Treg-cell-engineering technologies have advanced rapidly. CAR-Treg therapy enables precise targeted regulation at lesion sites via chimeric-antigen-receptor expression, showing promising efficacy in clinical trials for conditions such as type 1 diabetes mellitus and organ-transplant rejection, while circumventing the broad-spectrum adverse effects associated with conventional immunosuppressive regimens.29–31 Meanwhile, multiple innovative research avenues have continued to emerge, including FOXP3 isoform modulation, the induction of functional Tregs by macromolecular agents (eg, the GLP-1-receptor agonist semaglutide), small-molecule compounds (eg, the PD-L1 small-molecule inhibitor PCC0208025, also known as BMS202), and Chinese patent medicines (eg, Yuzhi Zhixue Granules). These advances deliver new strategies for tackling major obstacles including tumour immune evasion and refractory autoimmune disorders, marking a critical leap from fundamental mechanistic discovery toward clinical translational implementation.32–35

Sakaguchi et al identified the CD4⁺CD25⁺ Treg subset and characterized its immunosuppressive functions, uncovering for the first time the core cellular effector unit underlying peripheral immune tolerance; the teams of Brunkow and Ramsdell cloned the Foxp3 gene through studies on “scurfy” mice, confirming it as the “master regulatory factor” governing Treg development and functional maintenance, which advanced the mechanistic understanding of Treg-associated regulatory pathways at the molecular level (Figure 2). Figure 2 systematically illustrates the Nobel Prize-winning discoveries that establish the Treg-FOXP3 axis as the molecular foundation of peripheral immune tolerance. Physiologically, Tregs comprise two major subgroups: tTregs maturing within the thymus, and pTregs generated from naive CD4⁺ T cells in secondary lymphoid tissues. By integrating cellular-level and molecular-level evidence, these figures highlight why stabilizing FOXP3 expression and preserving Treg function constitute rational therapeutic targets, a reasoning that directly underpins the subsequent evaluation of H2 as a Treg modulator within this review. These two sequential landmark discoveries collectively established the central position of Tregs and FOXP3 within the immune-tolerance regulatory network. They not only resolved the long-standing “peripheral tolerance puzzle” but also built a comprehensive theoretical framework spanning Treg cellular phenotypes to molecular regulation, providing a key theoretical basis and technical support for further deciphering the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases and developing Treg-targeted precise therapeutic strategies. Given their profound scientific value and translational significance, these breakthroughs were recognized by the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Heterogeneity, Plasticity of Tregs and the Immune-Homeostasis Regulatory Network

Tregs do not constitute a functionally homogeneous cell population; instead, they form a highly complex, dynamic, and multilayer-regulated cellular network.36 Their functional integrity directly governs whether the organism can efficiently clear pathogenic agents while preventing autoimmune-mediated tissue injury.37 In recent years, alongside deepening research advances, Treg heterogeneity and plasticity, as well as their central network function in immune-homeostasis regulation, have been progressively uncovered, yielding key molecular targets for deciphering disease mechanisms and developing innovative therapeutic interventions.

Heterogeneity of Treg: Functional Specialization and Subset Division

Tregs account for approximately 5%–10% of total peripheral blood CD4⁺ T lymphocytes.38 Based on developmental origin, surface marker profiles, and functional specialization, Tregs can be further categorized into distinct functional subsets, including nTreg, iTreg, and Tr1 cells (Figure 4).39 Figure 4 illustrates the major Treg subgroups classified by origin, phenotype, and function, clearly distinguishing thymus-derived nTregs from peripherally induced iTreg/Tr1 cells. This classification clarifies Treg subset specificity, as H2 exerts differential regulatory effects on discrete Treg populations. nTregs mature within the thymus and constitute the predominant Treg subset.41 Their development depends on high-affinity interactions between TCRs and self-peptide-MHC complexes on thymic stromal cells, alongside activation of the IL-2-STAT5 signaling cascade.42,43 Mature nTregs exhibit high expression of the transcription factor FOXP3 and the IL-2 receptor α chain (CD25), together with constitutive expression of co-inhibitory molecules including CTLA-4 and GITR.44–46 With stable and robust suppressive capacity, nTregs exert immune regulation mainly via direct cell-to-cell contact (eg, CTLA-4-mediated suppression of antigen-presenting cell activity) and the secretion of inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, acting as critical “sentinels” for sustaining systemic immune tolerance.47,48

In contrast, iTregs differentiate from naive CD4⁺ T cells within peripheral tissue microenvironments, a process dependent on the presence of key cytokines including TGF-β and IL-2.49,50 Compared with nTregs, iTregs display relatively unstable FOXP3 expression, and their functionality and stability rely heavily on persistent microenvironmental stimuli.51,52 iTregs are particularly enriched in mucosal tissues (eg, the intestines and lungs) and exert essential functions in suppressing local inflammatory responses, maintaining mucosal immune homeostasis, and mediating oral immune tolerance.53

Tr1 cells constitute another important subset of peripherally induced iTregs. Their most distinctive characteristic is the absence of canonical FOXP3 expression, accompanied by robust secretion of large quantities of IL-10.54 The differentiation of Tr1 cells generally requires the combined activity of IL-10 and IL-27.55 These cells mediate potent immunosuppressive effects predominantly via IL-10 secretion and fulfill unique biological functions in the regulation of allergic responses, autoimmune disorders, and tumour immune evasion.56–58

Plasticity of Tregs: Dynamic Balance Between Stability and Transformation

The suppressive capacity of Tregs is not static; their phenotype and function display substantial plasticity under specific microenvironmental conditions. This phenomenon is chiefly manifested in three dimensions: functional impairment, phenotypic conversion, and the modulatory influence of metabolic reprogramming (Figure 5).59,60 Multiple microenvironmental cues drive Treg phenotypic plasticity, including pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-23), hypoxic stress, and metabolic reprogramming. Figure 5 illustrates the linkage between Treg plasticity and metabolic switching between OXPHOS and glycolysis. This mechanism is highly relevant to H2-mediated bioeffects, given that H2 modulates mitochondrial function and oxidative-stress responses. It should be noted that direct experimental evidence demonstrating H2-driven metabolic reprogramming in Tregs remains insufficient, constituting an important knowledge gap within this research field. Under pathological conditions including chronic inflammation, persistent infection, and the tumor microenvironment, some Tregs may downregulate FOXP3 expression and lose suppressive capacity, a pathological phenotype defined as “Treg exhaustion” or functional dysfunction.63–65 Furthermore, under the stimulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-23, specific Tregs can completely abrogate their regulatory activity, concurrently acquiring the ability to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-17 and IFN-γ and adopt effector profiles reminiscent of Th17 or Th1 cells.66 Among these inflammatory mediators, IL-23 further stabilizes the ex-Treg-Th17-like phenotype and exacerbates Treg functional transformation.67 This phenotypic switch from a “regulatory” to an “effector” state amplifies local inflammatory cascades, thereby driving the initiation and progression of autoimmune disorders (eg, systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis) and malignancies.68–70

Apart from inflammatory cytokines, the hypoxic microenvironment represents another critical extrinsic factor that impairs Treg stability.71 Hypoxia-triggered accumulation of HIF-1α can drive glycolytic reprogramming within Tregs, down-regulate FOXP3 expression, and promote Treg phenotypic conversion toward effector-like profiles.72 Treg plasticity is tightly linked to cellular metabolic status. Quiescent Tregs predominantly utilize OXPHOS to meet energy demands, a metabolic pattern compatible with their stable suppressive phenotype.73 Nevertheless, following activation or within inflammatory microenvironments, Tregs undergo metabolic reprogramming and shift toward glycolytic metabolism.74 This metabolic transition is essential for Treg proliferation, migration, and effector-function execution. Mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive ROS production, or dysregulated lipid metabolism can upset Treg metabolic homeostasis, consequently compromising their stability and suppressive capacity and increasing susceptibility to phenotypic transformation.75 Accordingly, metabolic reprogramming acts as a central regulatory hub governing Treg plasticity.

Treg-Mediated Immune Homeostasis Regulatory Network: Multidimensional Precise Regulation

Tregs constitute a dynamic regulatory network modulated by genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and microenvironmental cues. This multilayered regulatory system comprises four core dimensions: the molecular regulatory core, cytokine signaling cascades, metabolic and redox modulation, and tissue microenvironmental regulation (Figure 6). Figure 6 systematically summarizes the four-dimensional framework governing Treg function, including genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and microenvironmental control. This integrated theoretical framework rationalizes the multi-pathway working mechanism of H2 elaborated in subsequent sections, rather than simply regarding H2 as a single-target immunomodulatory agent. This sophisticated and multilayered regulatory network provides abundant intervention entry points for Treg-targeted therapy, including stabilizing Treg identity, promoting functional differentiation, reversing immune dysfunction, and exploiting cellular plasticity for engineered Treg modification. An in-depth understanding of this comprehensive regulatory network is of fundamental significance for developing next-generation therapeutic strategies against autoimmune disorders, chronic inflammatory diseases, malignancies, organ transplant rejection, and other immune-related disorders. At the molecular regulatory level, the transcription factor FOXP3 acts as the master regulator governing Treg development and functional maintenance. Its expression and stability are tightly controlled through multilayered epigenetic and transcriptional mechanisms, including DNA methylation dynamics (eg, demethylation of the TSDR region), histone modification patterns, and the delicate equilibrium between diverse transcriptional co-activators (eg, NFAT and STAT5) and co-repressors.76–78 Any perturbation that compromises FOXP3 expression or protein stability directly induces Treg functional impairment.

At the cytokine-signaling-network level, cytokines function as essential signaling molecules that modulate the Treg regulatory network. The IL-2-STAT5 cascade constitutes a vital positive-regulatory axis for sustaining Treg survival, proliferation, and stable FOXP3 expression.79 By contrast, pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α predominantly exert inhibitory effects upon Treg function and drive their conversion toward effector-like phenotypes via activation of downstream signaling cascades such as STAT3 and NF-κB.80

At the metabolic-and-redox-regulation level, as outlined above, the metabolic state of Tregs is intimately coupled to their functional properties. The equilibrium among metabolic pathways including glycolysis, OXPHOS, and fatty-acid oxidation, together with intracellular ROS abundance, collectively governs Treg cell-fate decisions.81–83 For example, the mTOR signaling cascade, a master controller of cellular metabolism, facilitates glycolytic reprogramming and proliferation within activated Tregs; nevertheless, aberrant over-activation of this pathway can compromise their suppressive capacity.84,85

At the tissue-microenvironment level, Treg function displays pronounced tissue specificity, which is tightly linked to the local microenvironment where these cells reside. Cytokine repertoires, nutrient availability, and intercellular crosstalk within distinct tissues (eg, the intestines, skin, and adipose tissue) collectively sculpt the phenotypic and functional specialization of Tregs, allowing them to efficiently adapt to and modulate regional immune responses.86–88

Notably, nTregs and iTregs also display striking differences in their epigenetic landscapes and metabolic signatures. nTregs are distinguished by stable demethylation within the Foxp3-TSDR region, an epigenetic hallmark that guarantees sustained FOXP3 expression and functional stability. In comparison, iTregs maintain partial TSDR methylation, which renders their FOXP3 expression far more plastic and vulnerable to down-regulation under inflammatory or metabolic stress conditions. Metabolically, nTregs depend predominantly on constitutive OXPHOS and fatty-acid oxidation to satisfy cellular energy demands, whereas iTregs show greater susceptibility to glycolytic reprogramming and ROS-triggered metabolic perturbation. These epigenetic-metabolic disparities underpin the differential responsiveness of nTregs and iTregs toward extrinsic modulatory stimuli, including oxidative stress, inflammatory cues, and metabolic interventions.

The AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling axis, a critical hub for mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic reprogramming, may represent a key pathway through which H2 exerts differential effects on Treg subsets. Notably, nTreg rely more heavily on constitutive oxidative phosphorylation and exhibit stable Foxp3 expression, whereas iTreg demonstrate greater metabolic flexibility and are more susceptible to glycolytic reprogramming under inflammatory conditions. Given that H2 can modulate AMPK activity and mitochondrial function,89 it is plausible that nTreg and iTreg exhibit differential sensitivity to H2-mediated protection. Future studies should systematically compare H2 effects on these subsets using subset-specific markers (eg, Helios, Neuropilin-1 for nTreg) and assess whether H2 preferentially stabilizes Foxp3 expression in iTreg through epigenetic modifications at the TSDR region.

Mechanisms Underlying H2-Mediated Regulation of Tregs

To prevent redundant mechanistic explanations within subsequent disease-related chapters, this section first summarizes four core mechanisms whereby H2 modulates Treg stability, survival, and functional performance: (1) mitigating oxidative stress and scavenging excess ROS to shield Tregs from apoptosis and mitochondrial impairment; (2) dampening pathological NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling to diminish pro-inflammatory mediators that destabilize FOXP3 expression; (3) tuning the Fas-FasL apoptotic axis and down-regulating pro-apoptotic miRNAs (miR-21, miR-210) to sustain the stability of the Treg cell pool; and (4) reversing dysregulated metabolic reprogramming (glycolysis-to-OXPHOS shift) to preserve the immunosuppressive phenotype of Tregs. These conserved mechanisms operate across multiple disease settings and will not be reiterated in full in Disease-Specific Evidence for H2-Mediated Treg Modulation: Preclinical Mechanisms and Exploratory Clinical Observations; only disease-specific phenotypes, tissue-localized responses, and clinical observations will be emphasized in that section.

Mitigating Oxidative Stress to Preserve Treg Viability

Oxidative stress constitutes a major driver of Treg depletion, since excess ROS generation can trigger Treg apoptosis and repress their immunosuppressive capacity (Figure 7). Figure 7 illustrates the mechanistic framework whereby H2 counteracts oxidative stress to sustain Treg viability. One key insight derived from this schematic is that H2 does not simply “expand Treg populations”; instead, it specifically rescues ROS-driven Treg loss. This mechanistic feature differentiates H2-mediated activity from non-specific IL-2-driven Treg expansion. As a selective antioxidant, H2 directly scavenges cytotoxic intracellular ROS (eg, ·OH) and alleviates oxidative injury.90 One investigation utilizing a chronic-pancreatitis mouse model demonstrated that H2 markedly lowered intracellular ROS abundance within Tregs, suppressed apoptotic cell death, and prevented Treg depletion in the spleen and peripheral circulation of model animals.91 Complete Treg depletion abolished the pancreatic-protective effects of H2. Conversely, adoptive Treg transplantation alleviated oxidative stress and diminished TNF-α concentrations, accompanied by elevated IL-10 secretion. Furthermore, Treg ablation via CD25-neutralizing antibody reversed H2-triggered mitigation of oxidative-stress responses as well as TNF-α down-regulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that Tregs act as essential effector cells that mediate H2-conferred pancreatic tissue protection. In H2O2-challenged in-vitro cellular assays, HRS diminished the Treg apoptotic rate in a dose-dependent fashion and augmented the secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators including IL-10 and TGF-β, suggesting that H2 exerts bio-effects by preserving Treg viability and potentiating their anti-inflammatory functionality. The antioxidant attributes of H2 may allow it to exert biological effects within AGE-RAGE-driven autoimmune disease settings.92 For example, AGEs accumulate in patients with type 1 diabetes and participate in the pathogenesis of cutaneous autoimmune disorders including vitiligo and bullous pemphigoid via triggering oxidative-stress responses and neoantigen generation. Available evidence demonstrates that antioxidant interventions such as HRW are capable of neutralizing ROS and attenuating AGE accumulation, consequently lowering protein immunogenicity. In a mouse model of dermatomyositis-related interstitial lung disease induced by anti-desmoglein 1 antibodies, oral HRW administration markedly mitigated oxidative stress within lung tissues, ameliorated interstitial inflammation and fibrosis, and down-regulated the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-13) and fibrotic markers (α-SMA, TGF-β, collagen).93 While none of the above-cited investigations have directly quantified Treg populations, the mechanism whereby H2 remodels the local immune microenvironment through oxidative-stress mitigation is highly consistent with its established capacity to preserve Treg viability and potentiate their immunosuppressive functionality. This observation implies that Tregs may serve as critical intermediate mediators in these pathological contexts. Future work should further explore whether H2 indirectly modulates Treg function by suppressing the AGE-RAGE signaling cascade, and whether combined application with AGE-targeted inhibitors can produce synergistic immunomodulatory effects, thus broadening its therapeutic scope across the heterogeneous spectrum of autoimmune disorders. Furthermore, the antioxidant potency of H2 has also been validated within the CNS. In experimental models of sepsis-associated encephalopathy, HRS substantially restored mitochondrial membrane potential, curbed ROS liberation, rescued ATP synthesis, and repressed neuronal apoptotic cell death.94 Even though Tregs were not directly assessed in this particular investigation, mitochondrial impairment represents a major driver of Treg oxidative-stress injury and functional compromise. This suggests that H2 may indirectly sustain Treg survival and stability within inflamed tissue microenvironments by safeguarding mitochondrial structural integrity.

Emerging evidence has demonstrated that H2 helps establish a tissue microenvironment permissive to Treg survival and function by mitigating local oxidative stress. For example, a double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial revealed that topical administration of high-concentration H2 gel (40 ppm) substantially alleviated oxidative stress in gingival tissues, elevated the anti-inflammatory mediator IL-10, and simultaneously down-regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α.95 Similarly, in a ultraviolet-induced skin photoaging model, an electrochemical microneedle patch capable of in-situ H2 generation synergistically suppressed the NOX/ROS axis, modulated TGF-β/Smad signaling cascades, and facilitated M2 macrophage polarization via sustained release of H2, microcurrent, and magnesium ions, ultimately ameliorating ultraviolet-triggered cutaneous inflammation and fibrotic remodeling.96 Although Treg populations were not directly quantified in either investigation, it is well established that IL-10 and TGF-β are indispensable cytokines for sustaining Treg stability and immunosuppressive competence, whereas persistent oxidative stress profoundly compromises Treg survival and functionality. Accordingly, H2 may foster a Treg-permissive microenvironment through oxidative stress attenuation and anti-inflammatory cytokine network remodeling, thereby indirectly reinforcing Treg-dependent immune tolerance.

Targeting Inflammatory-Signaling Cascades to Promote Treg Functional Activation

Excessive activation of the NF-κB signaling cascade constitutes a major driver for the establishment of a chronic inflammatory microenvironment, and persistent inflammatory stimulation can markedly compromise Treg stability and immunosuppressive competence (Figure 8). Figure 8 summarizes how H2 dampens pathological NF-κB signaling to preserve Treg function. Importantly, this schematic illustrates that H2 preferentially counteracts pathological inflammatory responses instead of interfering with physiological Treg activation, which underpins its favorable safety characteristics. Notably, NF-κB exerts dual-faceted roles within Treg biology: over-activation of the canonical NF-κB (p65/p50) complex triggered by pro-inflammatory stimuli (TNF-α, IL-1β, LPS) mediates pathological inflammatory cascades that destabilize FOXP3 expression and drive Treg phenotypic plasticity toward pro-inflammatory profiles; by contrast, non-canonical NF-κB or low-intensity canonical NF-κB signaling is indispensable for basal Treg activation, survival, and homeostatic function. Molecular H2 realizes selective suppression of pro-inflammatory NF-κB signaling without disrupting physiological Treg activity via three distinct mechanisms: first, H2 only dampens pathological-stress-triggered hyper-phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of canonical p65 NF-κB, while sparing the low-grade physiological NF-κB activity required for basal Treg activation; second, H2 preferentially suppresses NF-κB cascades within pro-inflammatory immune-cell populations (effector T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells), rather than acting directly upon Tregs, thereby lowering the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators that would otherwise undermine Treg stability; third, H2 couples NF-κB suppression with Nrf2-driven antioxidant-pathway activation. This combined effect synergistically sustains FOXP3 stability and Treg immunosuppressive competence whilst abrogating pathological inflammatory signaling. It is important to recognize that inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and PGE2 exhibit context-dependent dual functions. IL-6 signaling through the classical pathway (membrane-bound IL-6R) mediates anti-inflammatory and regenerative effects, whereas trans-signaling (soluble IL-6R) drives pathological inflammation.97 Similarly, PGE2 exerts opposing effects through different EP receptors: EP2/EP4 signaling can be either protective or pathogenic depending on the cellular context and disease stage.98 H2’s inhibition of NF-κB may therefore have differential consequences depending on which IL-6 or PGE2 signaling mode predominates. This complexity underscores the need for context-specific evaluation of H2 therapy and highlights that “anti-inflammatory” should not be equated with uniformly beneficial effects across all disease settings.

In a dextran sulfate sodium-induced murine colitis model, H2 gas inhalation effectively remodels intestinal microbial composition: it markedly suppresses the abundance of potential pathogenic bacteria (eg, Enterobacteriaceae) while enriching beneficial bacterial taxa, including Bacteroides and Lactobacillaceae. Notably, the enrichment of these beneficial gut microbes is positively correlated with H2-triggered FOXP3⁺ Treg activation and elevated colonic Treg proportions, alongside increased expression of IL-10 and TGF-β, two pivotal cytokines governing Treg differentiation and immunosuppressive competence. Such microbial structural optimization not only dampens the TLR4/NF-κB pro-inflammatory cascade but also activates the Nrf2-dependent antioxidant defense system, thereby restoring impaired intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. This protective effect is manifested by the upregulation of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin, as well as enhanced intestinal mucus secretion. More importantly, H2 intervention efficiently rectifies the disrupted Treg (FOXP3⁺)/Th17 (IL-17⁺) immune balance in colitic mice, thereby restoring intestinal immune homeostasis.99 Fecal microbiota transplantation further verified that the therapeutic efficacy of H2 relies on intestinal microbial modulation, demonstrating that microbiome functional reconstruction constitutes one core mechanism whereby H2 alleviates intestinal inflammation and reinforces local immune tolerance. This evidence tightly links the immunomodulatory actions of H2 to the gut-microbiota-immune axis, offering a novel mechanistic interpretation for its therapeutic potential in chronic intestinal inflammatory disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease. In a murine silicosis model, combined treatment with H2 and tetraphenylene markedly suppresses NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome activation, reduces the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators (eg, TNF-α and IL-1β), and reverses epithelial-mesenchymal transition, ultimately attenuating pulmonary fibrotic progression.100 In oxalate-induced renal injury models, HRW substantially mitigates tubular damage, crystal deposition, and renal interstitial fibrosis, while lowering serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and oxidative stress biomarker levels.101 Mechanistically, HRW elicits antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic bioactivities via suppression of the PI3K/AKT, NF-κB/NLRP3, and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling cascades. Although the aforementioned studies did not directly assess Treg abundance or functional status, accumulated evidence confirms that NF-κB suppression strengthens Treg stability and immunosuppressive capacity, whereas aberrant hyperactivation of TGF-β signaling drives Treg conversion toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes.102,103 Accordingly, H2 and HRW may indirectly reinforce Treg-dominated immune tolerance by remodeling inflamed and fibrotic tissue microenvironments, providing solid mechanistic support for their clinical applicability in chronic inflammatory and fibrotic disorders of the lung and kidney.

H2-dependent modulation of Treg-mediated immune tolerance relies not only on the direct preservation of Treg viability and phenotypic stability but also on indirect immune reprogramming via systematic remodeling of inflammatory microenvironments and immune-metabolic networks. Although most current investigations have not directly assessed Treg abundance or functional status, accumulating evidence demonstrates that H2 establishes a pro-tolerogenic microenvironment conducive to Treg differentiation, tissue infiltration, and effector function execution. These beneficial outcomes are achieved by upregulating core anti-inflammatory mediators (eg, IL-10), reshaping immune cell polarization profiles, and optimizing gut microbiota-derived metabolic patterns. In nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug-induced enteropathy models, HRW not only lowers intestinal luminal ROS abundance but also markedly elevates the intestinal content of SCFAs, particularly butyrate.104 As pivotal gut microbial metabolites, SCFAs directly drive intestinal Treg differentiation and potentiate IL-10 secretory capacity by activating G protein-coupled receptors or suppressing histone deacetylase activity. Fecal microbiota transplantation further validated that HRW-elicited intestinal protection is microbiota-dependent, indicating that H2 reinforces Treg-dominated immune tolerance indirectly through the microbiota-metabolite-immune regulatory axis. In neuroinflammatory disorders represented by Parkinson’s disease, H2 inhalation substantially ameliorates L-DOPA-induced dyskinesia, mitigates microglial overactivation, reduces striatal pro-inflammatory mediator (IL-1β, TNF-α) levels, and enhances local IL-10 expression.105 To date, no studies have directly quantified the abundance, phenotypic characteristics, or functional alterations of CNS-resident Tregs following H2 intervention via flow cytometry, immunofluorescence staining, or single-cell RNA sequencing. Current mechanistic inferences regarding H2-mediated Treg regulation during neuroinflammation are limited to indirect evidence derived from altered inflammatory profiles and downstream effector molecule (eg, IL-10) expression, highlighting a critical knowledge gap in this research field. While direct evidence for H2-mediated regulation of brain-resident Treg remains lacking, emerging concepts of inter-organ communication suggest indirect pathways through which H2 may influence neuroimmune homeostasis. Myokines-cytokines and other signaling molecules released from skeletal muscle during contraction-have been shown to modulate blood-brain barrier permeability, neuroinflammation, and microglial activation.106 Given that H2 can modulate muscle metabolism and reduce exercise-induced oxidative stress, it is plausible that H2 indirectly influences CNS immune tolerance through myokine-mediated signaling. Furthermore, endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response, which are central to Parkinson’s disease pathogenesis,107 may be modulated by H2 through its antioxidant properties, potentially creating a permissive environment for Treg infiltration and function in the CNS. As a signature anti-inflammatory effector cytokine of functional Tregs, elevated central IL-10 levels further suggest that H2 may ameliorate local neuroinflammation to facilitate Treg infiltration and immune regulatory function, thereby restoring disrupted neuroimmune homeostasis. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease models, H2 treatment effectively inhibits alveolar macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (iNOS upregulation) and promotes phenotypic transition toward the anti-inflammatory M2 profile (Arg-1 upregulation). This phenotypic shift reduces pulmonary pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β1) and rescues anti-inflammatory IL-10 expression.108 M2 macrophages act as a major cellular source of IL-10 and further potentiate Treg differentiation and phenotypic stability via paracrine secretion of TGF-β and IL-10. Accordingly, H2-driven modulation of macrophage polarization constitutes an essential indirect mechanism sustaining Treg-mediated immune tolerance within the inflamed lung microenvironment.

In high-fat-diet-induced chronic kidney disease murine models, oral supplementation with magnesium-H2 nanoparticles enables sustained molecular H2 release, up-regulates renal PTEN expression, and thereby dampens aberrant over-activation of the TGF-β/Smad2/3 and AKT/mTOR signaling cascades, ultimately ameliorating renal interstitial fibrosis and preserving renal function.109 In minocycline-triggered pulmonary-hypertension rat models, inhalation of 4% H2 failed to produce marked improvements in core pulmonary-artery-pressure parameters; nevertheless, it lowered systemic blood pressure and diminished pulmonary TGF-β expression together with mast-cell infiltration.110 Although this investigation did not directly assess Treg abundance or functional competence, TGF-β is a well-documented modulator of Treg plasticity: suppression of this signaling cascade can substantially reinforce Treg stability and restrain their conversion toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes.111 Accordingly, H2 may indirectly establish a tissue microenvironment permissive to Treg-mediated immune tolerance by attenuating TGF-β-driven fibrotic responses and inflammatory cascades. This observation not only broadens the therapeutic prospect of H2 for kidney disorders but also further validates its potential as a multi-target immunomodulatory agent.

Regulating the Apoptotic Signaling Cascade to Sustain Treg Pool Stability

Dysregulated Fas-FasL-dependent apoptosis represents a core mechanism responsible for Treg depletion and disrupted immune tolerance, and this pro-apoptotic signaling is markedly augmented under multiple pathological conditions, including pulmonary fibrosis and autoimmune disorders.112 The binding of Fas to its cognate ligand FasL initiates sequential activation of caspase family proteases, ultimately executing cellular apoptotic programs. Notably, Tregs exhibit high membranous Fas expression, rendering them inherently vulnerable to apoptotic elimination within inflamed pathological microenvironments.113 In patients with IgG4-PF-ILD, peripheral Treg counts decline substantially during disease progression, accompanied by expanded proportions of Fas⁺ Th and Tc cell subsets. This evidence indicates that hyperactivation of the Fas-FasL axis serves as a dominant driver of Treg apoptotic depletion. Following H2 intervention, patients display prominent restoration of peripheral resting Treg abundance and a pronounced reduction in Fas⁺ Th and Fas⁺ Tc cell populations. These clinical observations directly validate that H2 suppresses pathological Fas-FasL cascade overactivation, attenuates Treg apoptotic loss, and thereby sustains numerical stability of the circulating Treg pool.114

H2-mediated protection against Treg apoptosis relies on multi-target synergistic modulation rather than single-pathway regulation (Figure 9). Figure 9 integrates the Fas-FasL axis, pro-apoptotic miRNA networks, and NF-κB signaling cascades to illustrate the coordinated mechanisms whereby H2 sustains Treg pool stability. This multi-modal regulatory model explains why H2 exerts consistent yet moderate protective effects across diverse pathological contexts, rather than triggering drastic single-pathway phenotypic alterations. Accumulating evidence has verified that miR-21 directly targets the 3′-untranslated region of Foxp3, the master transcription factor governing Treg identity, thereby repressing FOXP3 protein abundance, compromising Treg immunosuppressive competence, and facilitating apoptotic cell death.115 Similarly, miR-210 modulates the TGF-β/Smad signaling cascade to reduce TGF-β secretion.116,117 Since TGF-β is an indispensable cytokine for preserving Treg phenotypic stability and effector functionality, diminished TGF-β bioavailability substantially accelerates Treg apoptosis. In murine models of acute cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, HRS intervention significantly elevates Treg abundance in peripheral blood and brain tissues while efficiently downregulating pro-apoptotic miRNA expression, with prominent reductions in miR-21 and miR-210 levels.118 Furthermore, H2 markedly suppresses hyperactivation of the NF-κB cascade, a canonical pro-inflammatory transcriptional pathway. Sustained NF-κB signaling drives robust secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α and IL-6. These inflammatory cytokines amplify Fas-FasL-dependent apoptotic signaling via paracrine crosstalk and disrupt the Treg survival-permissive microenvironment. H2-mediated NF-κB suppression effectively interrupts this self-amplifying pro-apoptotic vicious cycle. Based on the aforementioned mechanistic evidence, a set of molecular biomarkers can be applied to predict patient responsiveness to H2 therapy, which closely align with the core molecular targets whereby H2 modulates Treg survival and apoptotic resistance. First, membranous Fas and FasL expression levels on peripheral Treg, Th, and Tc cell subsets serve as direct predictive indicators. Patients with elevated baseline proportions of Fas⁺ T cells and augmented Fas/FasL expression are more prone to attain suppressed Treg apoptosis and restored immune tolerance following H2 intervention. Moreover, the declining magnitude of Fas⁺ effector T cells post-treatment can serve as a sensitive early efficacy biomarker. Second, circulating and intracellular miR-21 and miR-210 levels represent reliable predictive signatures. High baseline expression of miR-21/miR-210 reflects severe Treg functional impairment and heightened apoptotic susceptibility, and such patients exhibit more prominent therapeutic responses to H2. Furthermore, the magnitude of post-treatment miR-21/miR-210 downregulation is positively correlated with the recovery of Treg abundance and immune regulatory function. In addition, FOXP3 protein abundance, the Treg-to-effector-T-cell ratio, and serum IL-10/TGF-β concentrations act as complementary biomarker indices. These indicators enable comprehensive assessment of individual patient responsiveness to H2-driven Treg protection and immune tolerance remodeling.

In summary, H2 builds a microenvironment permissive to Treg survival, phenotypic maintenance, and effector function via a multi-layered, multi-target regulatory network. It not only directly suppresses hyperactivation of the Fas-FasL axis to attenuate pro-apoptotic signaling toward Tregs, but also preserves the expression stability of core Treg-associated functional genes, including Foxp3 and TGF-β-related signaling molecules, by down-regulating pro-apoptotic miRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-210. In parallel, H2 dampens NF-κB signaling-cascaded inflammatory responses, lowers the abundance of pro-inflammatory mediators, and rectifies disrupted immune tolerance homeostasis. The combined actions of this suite of regulatory mechanisms stabilize Treg pool abundance and functional competence, amplify their central mediating role in immune tolerance, and furnish critical mechanistic foundations for the translational application of H2 in immune-associated disorders.

Modulating Energy Metabolic Reprogramming to Preserve Treg Stability and Immunosuppressive Function

Metabolic reprogramming serves as a fundamental determinant of Treg phenotypic stability and functional integrity.119 Unlike conventional effector T cells, which predominantly depend on glycolysis for energy acquisition, Tregs preferentially utilize OXPHOS and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism to sustain their immunosuppressive phenotype.120 A metabolic shift toward augmented glycolytic flux and defective mitochondrial respiration substantially undermines Treg immune regulatory capacity and exacerbates pro-inflammatory cascades.121

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that H2 elicits potent anti-inflammatory bioactivities via modulating cellular energy metabolic reprogramming.122 Under inflammatory stress, immune cells typically undergo a pathological metabolic transition from OXPHOS to aerobic glycolysis. H2 efficiently reverses this aberrant metabolic shift, rescues impaired mitochondrial respiratory function, and restores the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes. This metabolic remodeling sustains the physiological metabolic signature permissive to Treg phenotypic stability and immunosuppressive functionality.

By rectifying pathological energy metabolic perturbations, H2 sustains Treg phenotypic stability and their immunosuppressive competence. This finding illustrates that H2 modulates Treg biology not merely via oxidative-stress-related pathways, inflammatory signaling cascades, and apoptotic programs, but also through energy-metabolic reprogramming, thereby constructing a more integrated regulatory network.

Unified Summary of Core Mechanisms

Across autoimmune syndromes, chronic inflammatory conditions, tissue-repair processes, and physiological immune homeostasis, H2 shapes Treg-dependent immune tolerance via four conserved core mechanistic modules: (1) mitigating oxidative stress and eliminating excess ROS to preserve Treg viability and mitochondrial functionality; (2) suppressing pathological NF-κB hyperactivation and NLRP3 inflammasome cascades, thereby diminishing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines that destabilize Tregs; (3) fine-tuning the TGF-β/Smad signaling axis to sustain FOXP3 expression stability and restrain Treg phenotypic plasticity toward pro-inflammatory profiles; and (4) remodeling gut microbial composition and elevating short-chain-fatty-acid biosynthesis to indirectly foster Treg differentiation and reinforce their immunosuppressive competence. These mechanistic modules are conserved among distinct pathological settings and will not be reiterated exhaustively within the subsequent disease-oriented subsections. Only disease-specific manifestations, clinical observations, and tissue-restricted biological effects will be elaborated in the following sections.

Autoimmune Diseases: Exploratory Clinical Observations and Pre-Clinical Mechanistic Clues

In autoimmune syndromes including RA and SLE, H2 has been shown to modulate crosstalk between Tregs and Bregs within exploratory clinical scenarios. Nevertheless, it should be underscored that current clinical evidence remains confined to isolated case reports and small-scale open-label trials, with a complete absence of large-size randomized controlled trials. The subsequent paragraphs synthesize these preliminary clinical observations together with supporting pre-clinical mechanistic data.

Crosstalk between Tregs and Bregs forms a central regulatory axis that sustains immune tolerance, particularly within systemic autoimmune syndromes such as RA and SLE. H2 not only preserves the phenotypic stability and immunosuppressive competence of Tregs, but also indirectly shapes B-cell compartment responses and augments Breg-driven immune-inhibitory effects via Treg-dependent paracrine signaling and cell-to-cell contact-mediated mechanisms. First, H2-induced functional Tregs secrete abundant anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β. These mediators exert paracrine effects upon B lymphocytes, foster the differentiation of naive B cells into IL-10-competent Bregs, and restrain the maturation of pro-inflammatory B-cell subsets as well as autoantibody-producing plasma cells. Second, H2-licensed Tregs establish direct cell-to-cell interactions with B cells through surface-expressed molecules such as CTLA-4, CD25, and Fas/FasL. Such interactions dampen aberrant B-cell hyperactivation, curtail the generation of autoantibodies (eg, anti-dsDNA, anti-Ro), and re-establish homeostatic equilibrium between effector-type B cells and Bregs. Through this cascade, H2 remodels B-cell homeostasis indirectly by amplifying the Treg-Breg regulatory circuit, which represents one essential mechanistic basis for its protective activities in RA, SLE, and additional autoimmune disorders.

It should be underscored that current evidence supporting H2-driven Treg modulation under autoimmune settings rests predominantly on pre-clinical data obtained from animal models. Clinical observations remain confined to isolated case reports and small-scale open-label exploratory trials, and robust confirmatory clinical evidence is still absent.

In preliminary clinical observations of Sjögren’s syndrome complicated by pulmonary hypertension, adjuvant H2 intervention markedly elevated the frequencies of CD127⁺ Tregs and Tr1 cell subsets, diminished B-cell and plasma-cell counts, and lowered serum anti-Ro antibody titers, accompanied by no notable adverse events.123 Comparable immunophenotypic ameliorations have been documented among SLE-PAH patients: following H2 treatment, the proportion of Fas⁺ apoptosis-susceptible effector T cells was decreased.135 One isolated single-case report further illustrated that H2-based therapy reshaped the balance between immature-Tregs and Tr1 cells, as well as the distribution of Fas-expressing B-cell populations, in the setting of autoimmune pathology.124

Among RA patients suffering from methotrexate-triggered myelosuppression, preliminary clinical findings revealed that H2-based intervention restored the frequencies of memory and activated Tregs, alongside mucosal-lesion recovery and ameliorated organ-system function.125 One isolated case report concerning refractory RA complicated by atlantoaxial subluxation indicated that adjuvant H2 intervention alleviated fatigue symptoms and reshaped Treg-Breg population dynamics. Although these observations support the safety and potential adjuvant value of H2, such limited clinical data cannot draw definitive causal inferences.126

A complex clinical case presenting with concurrent aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, preexisting RA, and newly onset SLE demonstrated that oral H2 intervention elevated circulating abundances of Tregs, Bregs, and Tr1 cells, reduced T-cell surface TIM3 expression, and facilitated seronegative conversion of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies.127 This single case only provides preliminary hypothesis-generating clues rather than definitive clinical validation. Longitudinal clinical observations in SLE-PAH patients further uncovered a biphasic immunomodulatory pattern of H2 treatment on KLRG1⁺ exhausted T cells, CD39⁺Helios− Tregs, and B-cell populations. Collectively, these findings remain exploratory and lack sufficient robustness for conclusive mechanistic or therapeutic validation.128

Chronic Inflammatory Diseases: Pre-Clinical Evidence and Small-Sample Clinical Indications

Within chronic inflammatory pathologies represented by allergic rhinitis and bronchial asthma, accumulating pre-clinical investigations have consistently demonstrated that H2 elevates the frequencies of CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺ Tregs and augments the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines. One small-scale exploratory clinical trial enrolling patients with chronic rhinitis documented symptomatic amelioration; nevertheless, no obvious alterations in Treg- and Breg-related immune-cell profiles could be detected within that cohort. Accordingly, although pre-clinical findings provide encouraging supporting evidence, robust clinical confirmation for Treg-dependent mechanistic actions is still absent.

A CD-MOF-integrated microneedle patch achieves intranasal co-delivery of therapeutic agents coupled with in-situ H2 production. This composite intervention alleviates allergic rhinitis via reshaping the Treg/Th17 immune equilibrium and restoring mucosal-epithelial barrier integrity. Such delivery-based therapeutic tactics can lower required hormone doses and enhance overall biosafety. These observations align well with pre-clinical data demonstrating that H2 elevates the frequencies of CD4⁺CD25⁺FOXP3⁺ Tregs and augments anti-inflammatory-cytokine secretion within allergic-rhinitis experimental models.129,130,136

One small-scale exploratory clinical trial involving 120 patients with chronic rhinitis demonstrated that H2 nasal irrigation markedly decreased nasal-symptom scores and nasal ECP concentrations, with more pronounced effects within the allergic-rhinitis subgroup.137 Although no statistically-significant shifts in Treg- and Breg-cell abundances could be detected, the observed clinical ameliorations are broadly consistent with pre-clinically described Treg-associated mechanistic pathways. These findings only deliver preliminary suggestive signals and cannot constitute conclusive mechanistic evidence.

Within pre-clinical asthma experimental models, inhaled H2 suppressed airway hyperresponsiveness and Th2-skewed inflammatory responses, while elevating pulmonary expression levels of IL-10 and TGF-β1.138,139 Such experimental observations support the potential of inhaled-H2-based regimens as a pre-clinical therapeutic strategy for allergic airway disorders through the reinforcement of Treg-dependent immune-tolerance mechanisms.

Tissue Injury Repair: Pre-Clinical Mechanistic Insights and Early-Stage Clinical Observations

Among patients suffering from acute subdural hematoma, exploratory adjuvant H2 intervention elevated the circulating frequencies of immunoregulatory T-regulatory subsets (CD127−/low, CD39⁺Helios⁺, nTregs); these immunological alterations correlated with subdural-effusion resolution and neurological functional recovery.131 In elderly patients burdened with multiple comorbid conditions, H2-based intervention increased the abundances of naive Tregs and CD39−Helios⁺ Tregs, while diminishing Fas-bearing effector-T-cell and plasma-cell populations, accompanied by ameliorated renal-organ performance and alleviated fatigue manifestations.132 Taken together, these merely represent early-phase clinical observations and are incapable of constituting definitive confirmatory evidence.

Within a murine heterotopic tracheal-transplantation model, HRW augmented FOXP3 expression and lowered local IL-6 concentrations, thereby mitigating obliterative airway disease under immunosuppressant-free conditions.133 This pre-clinical finding lends support to H2-driven Treg activation for transplant-associated immunomodulation; nevertheless, corresponding clinical verification remains urgently required.

Within experimental tissue-injury models, H2 fosters Treg-dependent immune tolerance predominantly by amplifying IL-10-related signaling cascades and delicately modulating the TGF-β/Smad signaling axis (as outlined in Unified Summary of Core Mechanisms). These molecular pathways restrain exaggerated inflammatory responses, facilitate reparative macrophage polarization, and establish a permissive microenvironment that sustains Treg phenotypic stability and functionality across neural, myocardial, cutaneous, and osseous-injury pathological settings.140–144

Physiological Processes: Exploratory Evidence in Pregnancy and Exercise-Associated Stress

H2 facilitates maternal-fetal immune tolerance by preserving the Treg/Th17 immune equilibrium and restraining exaggerated inflammatory reactions. Preliminary clinical observational data have associated diminished endogenous H2 concentrations in gravid women with an increased risk of preterm delivery, which hints at the potential utility of H2 as a predictive biomarker.134

However, current evidence directly clarifying the association between exogenous H2 supplementation and reduced preterm birth risk remains limited to pre-clinical and exploratory clinical data. To date, no large-scale randomized controlled hypothesis-confirming clinical trials have confirmed that exogenous H2 intervention can directly lower the incidence of preterm birth. Nevertheless, supportive pre-clinical and preliminary hypothesis-generating clinical evidence exists: animal studies demonstrated that oral administration of HRW in pregnant mice improved the uterine immune microenvironment homeostasis, upregulated the frequencies of Tregs and their function, reduced the infiltration of pro-inflammatory effector T cells, and decreased the expression of inflammatory mediators closely related to preterm delivery (such as IL-26).134 In vitro experiments using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells further confirmed that H2 treatment enhanced the immunosuppressive function of Tregs and inhibited the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways that trigger preterm birth. These pre-clinical findings provide a theoretical and experimental basis for hypothesizing that exogenous H2 supplementation may reduce preterm birth risk by maintaining maternal-fetal immune tolerance via Treg-dependent regulation. Future large-sample-size, long-term hypothesis-confirming clinical trials are urgently needed to verify the direct preventive and therapeutic effects of exogenous H2 on preterm birth.

In elite athletes, HRW alleviates exercise-induced skeletal-muscle injury and upregulates IL-10 expression.145 Pre-clinical studies confirm that H2 reduces oxidative stress, inhibits NF-κB-mediated inflammation, and antagonizes apoptosis in injured muscle tissue,146 which supports its adaptive immunomodulatory role in physiological stress via Treg-related pathways.

Disease-Intervention Characteristics, Molecular Mechanisms, Research Limitations and Future Prospects of Treg Regulation Mediated by H2 Administration Routes

As a core cell subset maintaining immune tolerance, Tregs are one of the key targets for H2 to exert immunomodulatory effects. This chapter systematically summarizes the disease-intervention characteristics of Treg regulation through inhalation, oral administration, injection and other routes (eg, nasal administration), deeply analyzes their molecular regulatory pathways, dissects current research limitations and explores future directions combined with cutting-edge advances in this field, so as to provide theoretical support for mechanistic research and clinical application of H2 immunomodulatory therapy.

Comparison of Disease-Intervention Characteristics of Different Administration Routes in Regulating Tregs

Existing studies have shown that H2 may participate in immune-tolerance regulation by modulating Treg cell functions, and its efficacy is closely related to the administration route. H2 inhalation can rapidly enter the blood circulation through the respiratory tract, target systemic immune cells, and significantly promote Treg cell proliferation and enhance their immunosuppressive activity in diseases such as chronic pancreatitis and colitis; oral H2 (eg, HRW) has low bioavailability but is convenient to administer with high safety. In studies on pregnancy maintenance, it can indirectly affect Treg cell functions by regulating gut microbiota and improve local immune imbalance; H2 injection (eg, H2-rich normal saline) can accurately deliver H2 molecules to target organs or lesion sites, with direct action and rapid onset. In global cerebral ischemia/reperfusion-injury studies, it can directly regulate Treg cells and inhibit abnormal immune responses; other administration forms exhibit mild and durable onset with high local nasal bioavailability, mainly exerting effects by sustained H2 release to induce stable activation of local Tregs and inhibit inflammatory infiltration, and are suitable for experimental scenarios of allergic rhinitis. Differences exist in the regulatory effects of H2 on Tregs and applicable disease scenarios among different administration routes, with detailed comparisons shown in Table 2. Notably, the complementary advantages of different H2 administration routes suggest that combination strategies (eg, inhalation plus oral administration) may achieve synergistic immunomodulatory effects. Inhalation enables rapid systemic H2 delivery to activate circulating Treg pools, while oral H2 steadily modulates the gut-microbiota-immune axis to enhance local and systemic Treg function. Such a combined regimen is expected to more stably maintain Treg numbers, stability, and immunosuppressive activity, especially in severe immune-related diseases with complex and persistent immune disorders. Molecular Mechanisms of Different H2 Administration Routes in Regulating Tregs

Table 3 systematically presents the specific molecular mechanisms by which different H2 administration routes regulate diseases via Treg cells. All these mechanisms center on Treg cell proliferation, activation, and functional maintenance but differ in downstream regulatory pathways: H2 inhalation primarily activates signaling pathways driving Treg cell proliferation through rapid systemic delivery; oral H2 indirectly modulates Treg cell functions via the gut microbiota-immune axis; and H2 injection directly targets key regulatory molecules governing Treg biology. Furthermore, the combination of different H2 administration routes may exert synergistic modulation of Treg-mediated immune tolerance. Systemic H2 delivery via inhalation and gut microbiota modulation driven by oral H2 can act in concert to simultaneously enhance Treg proliferation, function, and phenotypic stability. This combinatorial strategy holds great potential to more effectively reverse severe immune imbalance and reinforce immune tolerance in refractory and severe immune-related diseases.

Research Limitations and Future Prospects

Despite the promising findings, several critical limitations must be addressed before clinical translation: First, substantial heterogeneity exists among published pre-clinical and clinical investigations. Variations in animal models, H2 delivery modalities, treatment duration, intervention timing, and outcome readouts prevent direct cross-study comparison, meta-analysis, and robust consensus-building. Furthermore, mechanistic investigations remain incomplete. It remains poorly defined how H2 modulates the gene expression and protein stability of FOXP3, the master transcription factor governing Treg development and function. In particular, the differential effects of H2 on thymus-derived nTregs versus peripherally induced iTregs, as well as the underlying epigenetic (eg, TSDR methylation) and metabolic (eg, OXPHOS/glycolysis switching) mechanisms, have not been systematically elucidated; meanwhile, direct cytological and molecular evidence demonstrating H2 modulation of brain-resident Tregs in CNS diseases is still lacking, and conclusions can only be inferred indirectly from alterations in inflammatory factors and the tissue microenvironment. Second, substantial translational gaps persist between pre-clinical findings and real-world clinical application. Almost all mechanistic evidence for H2-dependent Treg modulation comes from animal disease models. Human-derived data are confined to case reports and small-size open-label pilot studies; these cannot establish causal relationships and are prone to selection bias and confounding factors. High-quality, multicenter randomized controlled trials are still lacking. Currently, clinical support for H2-mediated Treg regulation relies entirely on pre-clinical animal models, single-case reports, and small exploratory open-label studies; no large-scale prospective or randomized controlled trials are available to validate causality, efficacy, or long-term safety. In addition, the pharmacokinetic properties of molecular H2 in humans remain poorly characterized, including its tissue distribution, metabolism, half-life, and elimination pathways following different administration routes. Third, long-term safety evaluation represents a major unmet challenge for translating H2-based immunomodulation. Most published animal and human studies focus on short-term or sub-acute H2 exposure. Little information is available regarding immune consequences after months-to-years of continuous or intermittent H2 administration. Theoretically, sustained modulation of Treg homeostasis may carry potential risks: excessive enhancement of Treg function could possibly weaken anti-tumor immune surveillance, whereas abnormal Treg instability might aggravate autoimmune susceptibility. These theoretical risks have not been systematically evaluated in human cohorts, which constitutes a critical barrier prior to widespread clinical adoption. Fourth, the optimal dosing, concentration, and treatment duration of H2 are still unknown. There is currently no globally-recognized standardization framework for H2 administration strategies targeting Treg regulation. The effective therapeutic window, target-tissue H2 concentration, intervention timing (prophylactic versus therapeutic), and optimal treatment cycles are yet to be defined. Varied equipment parameters further complicate result interpretation. No consensus has been reached regarding effective H2 concentrations, therapeutic windows, or treatment schedules, further hindering standardized clinical application. Fifth, there is a notable lack of standardized delivery systems for H2 application. Inhalation devices, HRW production, oral H2 tablets, and injectable H2 solutions vary widely in preparation, stability, and actual H2 delivery efficiency, leading to inconsistent bioavailability and irreproducible outcomes. Additionally, research on combined therapeutic strategies is seriously insufficient. Current studies only involve single-agent H2 intervention, and the specific schemes, synergistic mechanisms, efficacy advantages and safety profiles of H2 combined with Treg-related targeted drugs or cell-based therapy have not been explored, which greatly limits the clinical translation of H2 for complex immune-related diseases.

Several specific translational barriers must be addressed before H2 can be established as a reliable immunomodulatory therapy. First, the lack of standardized H2 delivery systems-inhalation devices, HRW, tablets, and injectable solutions vary widely in preparation, stability, and actual H2 delivery efficiency-leads to inconsistent bioavailability and irreproducible outcomes. Second, pharmacokinetic data in humans remain sparse; tissue distribution, half-life, and elimination pathways following different administration routes require systematic characterization. Third, validated biomarkers for patient stratification are absent; as highlighted in the muscle atrophy literature, multi-omics approaches may identify predictive biomarkers for treatment response.147,148 Fourth, the heterogeneity of preclinical models (different animal strains, disease induction methods, H2 protocols) hampers cross-study comparison and meta-analysis. Addressing these barriers will require: (1) development of standardized H2 delivery devices with real-time concentration monitoring; (2) comprehensive pharmacokinetic studies in humans; (3) biomarker discovery using multi-omics approaches; and (4) consensus guidelines for preclinical H2 research methodology. The cGAS-STING pathway, which plays dual roles in tumor immunity,149 exemplifies the need for precise pathway modulation rather than blanket inhibition-a principle equally applicable to H2 therapy, where context-dependent effects must be carefully characterized.

Drawing upon the therapeutic value of Tregs highlighted by the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, future research can focus on four aspects: First, further explore the molecular mechanisms by which H2 regulates FOXP3 ubiquitination modification and Treg plasticity, and clarify its interplay with core immune-tolerance pathways. Second, with full awareness of existing translational limitations, future studies should conduct rationally designed clinical investigations. Priority should be given to well-controlled pilot clinical studies to monitor Treg subset dynamics, immune-related laboratory markers, and adverse-event profiles; long-term immune safety surveillance ought to be incorporated into study endpoints to assess potential tumor-related and autoimmune risks associated with chronic H2 exposure. Multicenter clinical studies should be performed in autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and other relevant conditions to verify the Treg-regulatory effects and clinical efficacy of H2. Third, establish preliminary standardized specifications for H2 administration strategies for immunomodulatory purposes. Researchers should fully report device parameters, actual H2 concentrations, administration modes, intervention time-points and treatment cycles in future publications, which will facilitate cross-study comparisons and accelerate the optimization of experimental protocols. Fourth, develop and verify promising H2-based combination therapies. Typical representative regimens include H2 plus engineered Treg and H2 plus AGE inhibitor strategies: 1) H2 + engineered Treg combination therapy: Engineered Tregs (such as CAR-Tregs and FOXP3-stabilized genetically modified Tregs) represent a core direction of current Treg clinical translation. However, their in vivo survival rate and functional stability are easily impaired by oxidative stress and the inflammatory microenvironment. H2 can selectively eliminate reactive oxygen species, inhibit the NF-κB and Fas-FasL apoptotic pathways, and construct a stable microenvironment to support the survival and function of engineered Tregs in vivo. Theoretically, H2 intervention can improve the persistence of engineered Tregs within inflammatory lesions, enhance FOXP3 stability, and reduce the risk of Treg transformation into pro-inflammatory effector cells. Meanwhile, engineered Tregs exert precise immunosuppressive effects, and the two interventions can synergistically amplify immune tolerance in the contexts of organ transplantation, refractory autoimmune diseases, and the tumor local microenvironment. For instance, in type 1 diabetes or organ transplant rejection, H2 inhalation or oral administration combined with CAR-Treg adoptive transfusion can not only protect CAR-Tregs against oxidative stress injury, but also enhance their targeted immunosuppressive function, which is expected to address the bottlenecks of short survival time and unsatisfactory efficacy of engineered Tregs in vivo. 2) H2 + AGE inhibitor combination therapy: AGEs bind to RAGE to induce excessive ROS production, impair Treg survival and FOXP3 stability, and drive the progression of diabetes-related immune disorders, autoimmune skin diseases, and chronic inflammatory fibrosis. AGE inhibitors (such as alagebrium and pyridoxamine) can block AGE-RAGE signaling, yet monotherapy yields limited antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects. H2 directly scavenges ROS induced by AGE-RAGE activation, alleviates oxidative stress-mediated Treg damage, and synergizes with AGE inhibitors to block the AGE-RAGE-oxidative stress-Treg dysfunction axis. Theoretically, this combination strategy not only reduces the formation and accumulation of AGEs, but also directly preserves Treg survival and function, thereby significantly improving immune tolerance in AGE-associated diseases with immune imbalance, such as type 1 diabetes with autoimmune complications, scleroderma, and chronic kidney disease accompanied by immune disorders. Drug repurposing offers a pragmatic pathway to enhance H2 therapy. Celecoxib, a selective COX-2 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in attenuating skeletal muscle atrophy through suppression of inflammation, oxidative stress, and ER stress via STAT3 inhibition.150 Given that celecoxib targets overlapping pathways with H2 (NF-κB inhibition, oxidative stress reduction), combination therapy with H2 and celecoxib could theoretically produce synergistic effects in chronic inflammatory conditions where Treg dysfunction is prominent. Similarly, salidroside, a natural compound with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, has shown promise in denervation-induced muscle atrophy by suppressing oxidative stress and inflammation.151 The combination of H2 with such agents warrants systematic investigation, particularly in complex diseases like diabetic nephropathy where multi-target interventions are increasingly recognized as necessary.152 Fifth, explore the synergistic effects and optimal regimens of combined H2 administration strategies (eg, inhalation plus oral administration). Clarify whether such combinations can synergistically enhance Treg regulation, FOXP3 stability, and immunosuppressive function, especially in severe immune-related diseases, with the goal of establishing more efficient and individualized H2 immunotherapy protocols.

Conclusion

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded for the discovery of Tregs and the master transcription factor FOXP3, has not only crowned decades of fundamental immunology research but also powerfully underscored the translational potential of Treg-targeted therapeutic strategies. Against this backdrop, molecular H2 has emerged as a distinctive candidate for Treg-directed immunomodulation, distinguished by its excellent biosafety profile, multiple available administration routes, and capacity to simultaneously interfere with diverse pathological drivers of Treg dysfunction. Unlike conventional single-target biologics or small-molecule drugs, H2 does not drive Treg expansion via direct receptor agonism but instead creates a permissive microenvironment—by alleviating oxidative stress, dampening pathological inflammation, and stabilizing metabolic and apoptotic homeostasis—that enables Tregs to survive, sustain FOXP3 expression, and exert their immunosuppressive functions more effectively.

Accumulating evidence, as systematically reviewed here, indicates that H2 regulates Treg stability and function through at least four interconnected and synergistic core mechanisms. First, as a selective antioxidant, H2 directly scavenges excessive hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and reduces intracellular ROS levels, thereby protecting Tregs from oxidative-stress-induced apoptosis and preserving mitochondrial integrity—a prerequisite for Treg metabolic fitness and suppressive capacity. Second, H2 inhibits pathological over-activation of the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome axis and modulates TGF-β/Smad signaling, which collectively limit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) that destabilize FOXP3 expression and drive Treg plasticity toward pro-inflammatory effector phenotypes (ex-Treg-Th17 or ex-Treg-Th1). Third, H2 modulates the Fas-FasL apoptotic pathway and down-regulates pro-apoptotic microRNAs (miR-21, miR-210), thereby reducing Treg apoptotic loss and maintaining the numerical and functional integrity of the peripheral Treg pool. Fourth, H2 reverses pathological metabolic reprogramming—shifting aberrant glycolysis back toward OXPHOS—which supports the metabolic phenotype required for Treg stability and immunosuppressive function. Importantly, these four mechanisms are not isolated but operate in a coordinated, self-reinforcing network: oxidative stress fuels NF-κB activation, which in turn promotes Fas-FasL-mediated apoptosis and metabolic imbalance; H2 acts at multiple nodes of this vicious cycle, producing a cumulative protective effect on Tregs that exceeds the sum of its individual pathway actions.

The therapeutic relevance of these mechanistic insights is supported by a growing body of disease-specific evidence spanning autoimmune diseases (RA, SLE, Sjögren’s syndrome), chronic inflammatory conditions (colitis, allergic rhinitis, asthma, COPD), tissue injury repair (cerebral ischemia, organ transplantation, cutaneous and osseous injury), and physiological processes including pregnancy maintenance and exercise-induced muscle stress. In preclinical animal models, H2 administration—whether via inhalation, oral HRW, or H2-rich saline injection—has consistently been shown to elevate Treg proportions, restore Treg/Th17 homeostasis, upregulate IL-10 and TGF-β expression, and ameliorate disease severity. Exploratory clinical case reports and small open-label studies, while insufficient to draw definitive causal conclusions, have provided valuable immunophenotypic clues—including expanded circulating Tregs and Tr1 cells, reduced Fas⁺ apoptosis-prone effector T cells, and decreased autoantibody titers—that align with the proposed mechanistic framework and support the feasibility and safety of H2 as an adjunctive immunomodulatory strategy in humans.

Nevertheless, it must be emphasized that the current evidence base remains predominantly preclinical, and the translational gap between laboratory findings and clinical practice is substantial. Several critical bottlenecks must be resolved before H2 can be positioned as a reliable Treg-targeted immunomodulator. First, the mechanistic foundation is still incomplete: the differential responsiveness of thymus-derived nTregs versus peripherally induced iTregs to H2 intervention has not been systematically dissected, nor have the underlying epigenetic mechanisms—particularly Foxp3 TSDR methylation dynamics and subset-specific metabolic flux differences—been elucidated. Direct evidence demonstrating H2 modulation of tissue-resident Tregs in organs such as the brain, lung, and kidney remains scarce, with most conclusions inferred indirectly from alterations in inflammatory cytokines and microenvironmental markers. Second, high-level clinical validation is urgently needed: to date, no large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trial has been conducted to confirm the efficacy, optimal dosing, or long-term safety of H2 in Treg-related immune disorders. The absence of such data precludes evidence-based clinical recommendations and leaves critical questions regarding causality, dose-response relationships, and patient stratification unanswered. Third, standardization of H2 administration protocols—including optimal concentration, treatment duration, intervention timing, delivery device specifications, and bioavailability assurance—has not been achieved, leading to substantial heterogeneity across studies and hindering cross-study comparisons and meta-analysis. Fourth, the long-term immune safety of sustained H2 exposure remains unevaluated: while short-term administration appears well-tolerated, the theoretical risks of excessive Treg enhancement (potentially impairing anti-tumor immune surveillance) or paradoxical Treg instability (potentially exacerbating autoimmunity) have not been systematically assessed in human cohorts, representing a major barrier to widespread clinical adoption.

Looking forward, future research should prioritize a balanced, multi-pronged translational agenda. At the mechanistic level, advanced techniques—including single-cell RNA sequencing, ATAC-seq for epigenetic profiling, and metabolomics—should be employed to dissect the differential effects of H2 on nTregs versus iTregs subsets and to map the precise molecular circuitry through which H2 modulates the gene-level expression of Foxp3, post-translational modifications of FOXP3, and metabolic reprogramming. At the clinical level, well-designed pilot randomized controlled trials with integrated immune monitoring (Treg subset dynamics, Foxp3 methylation status, cytokine panels) and long-term safety endpoints (tumor incidence, autoimmune flare rates, infection susceptibility) are urgently needed to bridge the current evidence gap. At the translational level, standardized reporting frameworks for H2 delivery parameters should be developed and adopted by the research community to facilitate data harmonization and meta-analysis. At the therapeutic innovation level, combination strategies—such as H2 plus engineered CAR-Treg adoptive therapy, or H2 plus AGE inhibitors—warrant systematic exploration, as H2’s microenvironment-stabilizing effects may synergistically enhance the persistence and function of cellular therapies while mitigating off-target risks. Additionally, multi-route combination regimens (eg, inhalation plus oral HRW) that leverage the complementary pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic advantages of different administration routes deserve rigorous investigation, as they may achieve more sustained and robust Treg modulation in severe or refractory immune disorders.

In summary, molecular H2 presents a biologically plausible, mechanistically multifaceted, and practically accessible approach for regulating Treg-mediated immune tolerance. Its unique combination of selective antioxidant activity, anti-inflammatory signaling modulation, apoptotic protection, metabolic reprogramming, and favorable safety profile distinguishes it from conventional immunomodulators and positions it as a promising adjuvant candidate for treating immune-related diseases. However, the path from experimental promise to clinical reality relies on rigorous mechanistic dissection, high-quality clinical validation, standardized protocol development, and comprehensive long-term safety evaluation. With systematic and sustained research efforts, H2 may ultimately help overcome current immunotherapeutic limitations and improve clinical outcomes for patients with autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammatory disorders, and transplantation-related conditions. Until then, cautious interpretation of existing data and rigorous translational research remain essential. (The complete abbreviations are shown in Table 4).
