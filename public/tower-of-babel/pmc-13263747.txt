The role of forensic evidence in Indonesia's criminal justice
Authors: Handar Subhandi Bakhtiar, Amir Ilyas, Abdul Kholiq, Handina Sulastrina Bakhtiar, Ahmad Yusup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims, first, to analyse the position of forensic evidence in the criminal justice process in Indonesia; and second, to examine the extent to which forensic evidence is effectively employed as a basis of proof by judges and public prosecutors in the criminal justice process in Indonesia. This study was conducted in two stages, namely a doctrinal analysis of legal regulations in Indonesia and an empirical study through the collection of court decisions betwen January 2023 and December 2024 from four district courts representing three regions of Indonesia. The role of forensic evidence in Indonesian criminal procedure remains problematic owing to the absence of explicit regulation. At present, it is not formally recognised as a distinct evidentiary category under the Criminal Procedure Code, but rather subsumed under expert testimony, documentary evidence, or indications as stipulated in Article 184. This ambiguity diminishes the attention given by law enforcement officials, prosecutors, and judges to the probative value of forensic science. Data from four district courts between January 2023 and December 2024 show that there are five categories of cases with the highest percentages decided on the basis of forensic evidence, namely narcotics offences (96.26%), assault (94.61%), firearms and sharp weapons offences (92.08%), electronic information and transactions offences (72.63%), and corruption (59.38%). It is therefore recommended that forensic evidence be specifically classified as an independent category of evidence within Indonesian criminal procedural law, in order to strengthen objectivity, enhance fairness, and ensure a more reliable criminal justice system.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of science and technology has fundamentally altered numerous facets of human civilization, notably the legal domain [1,2]. Among the most affected areas is the evidentiary system in criminal proceedings. In recent decades, exclusive reliance on testimonial evidence has been increasingly supplemented or replaced by forensic science, which aims to establish material truth through objective, reliable, and verifiable means [3,4]. Forensic evidence, representing the application of scientific methods to legal processes, is now widely regarded as essential for addressing the complexities inherent in contemporary crime [5]. In jurisdictions with advanced legal systems, forensic evidence holds a central role within the structure of criminal procedure [6]. It may function as independent proof or enhance the probative value of other forms of evidence [7,8]. This evolution demonstrates a broader commitment to evidence-based justice, where judicial decisions are grounded in objective scientific proof rather than subjective perceptions or disputed testimonies [9]. Forensic evidence thereby contributes to both the accuracy of criminal adjudication and the legitimacy and credibility of the judiciary ([10]; Khyati [11]).

In contrast, the recognition of forensic evidence in Indonesia remains limited and largely implicit. Indonesian criminal procedure law follows the negatief wettelijk bewijs theorie, or negative legal proof system [12]. This approach, codified in Article 183 of the Indonesian Criminal Procedure Code (Kitab Undang-Undang Hukum Acara Pidana, hereinafter referred to as KUHAP), mandates at least two lawful pieces of evidence as defined by statute, in addition to the judge's conviction. Under this framework, a conviction requires both the statutory minimum of evidence and the judge's belief in the truth of the matter [13]. Conversely, a judge cannot convict based solely on personal conviction without at least two valid forms of evidence. Within this doctrinal structure, forensic evidence is not explicitly recognised as a distinct evidentiary category. Instead, it is incorporated under statutory forms such as expert testimony, documentary evidence, or indications (Indications mean all information obtained during the trial that is used by the judge in forming judicial conviction) [14]. Although this classification is legally justifiable, it obscures the unique characteristics of forensic evidence, particularly its scientific foundation and methodological rigor. Instruments such as forensic reports, DNA analyses, ballistic examinations, and digital forensic audits constitute stronger forms of evidence, provided that the techniques employed are scientifically valid and reliable, and may exceed the probative value of traditional testimonial evidence [15].

The author fully acknowledges that forensic evidence itself has been the subject of substantial and well-founded criticism within contemporary legal and scientific discourse. This awareness is essential in order to position forensic evidence realistically within the criminal justice process, not as an infallible truth-producing mechanism, but as an evidentiary tool whose reliability depends on its scientific foundations, methodological rigour, and manner of judicial interpretation. In this regard, the 2009 report published by the National Academies of Sciences, Strengthening Forensic Science in the United States: A Path Forward [16]. The report represents a critical turning point in forensic scholarship, as it explicitly concluded that with the notable exception of nuclear DNA analysis many long-established forensic techniques lack sufficient empirical validation. Disciplines such as bite mark analysis, hair comparison, toolmark examination, and even fingerprint identification were found to suffer from the absence of standardised methodologies, scientifically established error rates, and robust experimental testing. The author views this critique not as a rejection of forensic evidence per se, but as an important reminder that legal reliance on forensic methods must be proportionate to their demonstrated scientific reliability.

These concerns were further reinforced by the 2016 report of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST), which emphasised that several forensic methods commonly admitted in court fail to meet the standards of foundational validity and applied reliability [17]. The author concurs with the PCAST's warning that the uncritical admission of such evidence particularly when presented as objective fact rather than probabilistic expert opinion creates a tangible risk of miscarriages of justice. This risk is amplified by well-documented factors such as cognitive bias, limited peer review mechanisms, and the absence of mandatory accreditation and proficiency testing in certain forensic laboratories. In the United Kingdom, the findings of the Scottish Fingerprint Inquiry underscore similar structural weaknesses [18]. The inquiry concluded that fingerprint evidence should be reported as expert opinion rather than incontrovertible fact, highlighting systemic problems including confirmation bias, insufficient disclosure of uncertainty, and a judicial culture that tends to treat forensic evidence as inherently infallible. Therefore, these critiques inform the author's cautious and reflective approach, reinforcing the need for a more transparent and scientifically grounded.

Several previous studies have examined the role of forensic evidence in the criminal justice process [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Forensic evidence serves as a vital element in the investigation, prosecution, and adjudication of criminal cases. Pujari and Dolai [25] demonstrate that forensic medicine remains central in solving crimes and delivering justice, particularly in addressing complex toxicological evidence. These insights underscore the enduring importance of forensic science in courtrooms. Goudswaard [26] revealed gaps in the use of forensic medical information in non-fatal injury cases in the Netherlands, attributing these to delays in accessing forensic doctors and a lack of inter-institutional coordination. They recommended clearer procedural frameworks and increased cooperation between public prosecutors and forensic institutions to enhance evidentiary quality and legal outcomes. Kar et al. [27] document the role of forensic psychiatry in reforming criminal justice systems, moving from punitive to rehabilitative approaches. This reflects the growing awareness that forensic evidence extends beyond material traces to include mental state evaluations influencing sentencing and rehabilitation. Wijoyo and Setyawati [28] similarly emphasize challenges in Indonesia, where forensic law struggles to integrate medical expertise within legal frameworks. De Roo et al. [19] highlighted how institutional hierarchies within law enforcement may influence the evaluation of forensic findings, thus calling for procedural independence and professional training to preserve objectivity in evidentiary assessments. Complementing this view Gultom et al. [29] conducted a juridical analysis of expert witness roles in Indonesia, underscoring the need for reforms that ensure scientific testimony aligns with legal standards of admissibility. In a related context, Simon and Kudar [30] illuminated the growing relevance of forensic linguistics, particularly in digital crime investigations, where traditional forms of physical evidence may be absent. Their study demonstrated how linguistic profiling and authorship attribution can contribute substantially to criminal identification and motive analysis. The digital era has presented new challenges for forensic practices. Almutairi and Moulahi [31] integrate federated learning and blockchain into digital forensics for Internet of Things (IoT)-based crimes, emphasizing privacy preservation and secure evidence sharing. Across jurisdictions, the evolution of forensic techniques and the integration of emerging technologies particularly artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly reshaped evidentiary practices and courtroom dynamics. In the United States and several other countries, AI-assisted tools are increasingly used in DNA mixture interpretation, such as probabilistic genotyping software (e.g. STRmix and TrueAllele), which assist courts in evaluating complex genetic evidence, while also raising concerns regarding transparency and explainability ([17,32,33]; [34]). In the United Kingdom, digital forensics and algorithmic tools have been incorporated into fraud, cybercrime, and terrorism cases, prompting guidance from the Forensic Science Regulator on validation, disclosure, and bias management [35]. Meanwhile, China has advanced the use of AI in facial recognition and digital surveillance evidence, integrating such technologies into criminal proceedings, albeit amid concerns regarding due process and error rates [36].

The historical development and structure of the Indonesian legal system cannot be separated from its colonial legacy and post-independence constitutional transformation. Indonesia adheres predominantly to the civil law tradition [37], inherited from the Dutch colonial administration, particularly through the Wetboek van Strafrecht (WvS) and the Herziene Inlandsch Reglement (HIR) [38]. Following independence in 1945, Indonesia retained many codified legal structures while gradually adapting them to national values grounded in Pancasila and the 1945 Constitution of the Republic of Indonesia (Undang-Undang Dasar Negara Republik Indonesia Tahun 1945). The regulation of all criminal proceedings and evidentiary processes is governed by the Criminal Procedure Code (KUHAP) which has been in force since 1981. The Indonesian evidentiary system therefore combines objective legality with subjective judicial assessment. Although judges possess independent judicial discretion in delivering their decisions, they remain bound by the limitations prescribed under the Indonesian Code of Criminal Procedure (KUHAP), which requires the fulfilment of at least two lawful items of evidence.

Indonesia, in broad terms, applies a hierarchical judicial system in which criminal adjudication is conducted through clearly structured levels of courts. District courts (Pengadilan Negeri) function as courts of first instance and exercise primary jurisdiction over all criminal cases, ranging from minor offences to serious crimes. Decisions of district courts may be appealed to the High Court (Pengadilan Tinggi) and, ultimately, reviewed by the Supreme Court (Mahkamah Agung) as the highest judicial authority. According to data from the Mahkamah Agung in 2024, there are 382 district courts operating nationwide, distributed across 34 High Court jurisdictions [39]. This extensive network places district courts at the core of criminal justice administration and makes them the primary venues for the examination and evaluation of evidence, including forensic evidence.

The organisation of forensic science in Indonesia, however, is highly centralised and state-controlled. Forensic examinations in criminal cases are conducted exclusively by the Forensic Laboratory Centre (Pusat Laboratorium Forensik Polri/Puslabfor Polri), which operates under the Indonesian National Police [40]. There are no independent or private forensic laboratories authorised to perform forensic analyses for criminal proceedings. As a result, the police institution simultaneously acts as investigator and sole provider of forensic scientific expertise. With regard to crime patterns, national statistics from the National Criminal Information Centre (Pusat Informasi Kriminal Nasional Polri/Pusiknas Polri) indicate that between January and December 2025 there were 380,424 criminal cases, of which the most prevalent offences were theft (44,671 cases), narcotics-related offences (43,899 cases), and assault (41,231 cases) [41]. These categories of offences are particularly dependent on forensic evidence, including fingerprint identification, DNA analysis, toxicology, and digital forensics.

In Indonesia, a number of cases are profoundly intertwined with the use of forensic evidence in the evidentiary process, such as The Wayan Mirna Salihin case [42,43], often referred to as the “cyanide coffee case”, demonstrated how forensic toxicology became the cornerstone of proof. Laboratory analysis confirmed the presence of cyanide in the victim's drink, which provided the prosecution with scientific support to substantiate its theory, reflecting how forensic evidence can strengthen the judge's conviction under the negatief wettelijk bewijs theorie. In the Nofriansyah Yosua Hutabarat case [44,45] or the “Brigadir J case”, forensic pathology, ballistics, and digital forensics collectively dismantled fabricated narratives and revealed inconsistencies in witness testimonies. This case signified the power of forensic evidence to expose attempts of obstruction and to safeguard judicial integrity. Likewise, the Engeline case [46,47] in Bali emphasised the relevance of forensic medicine; autopsy findings disclosed evidence of abuse and sexual violence, correcting misleading assumptions of accidental death. Based on those cases, judges still prioritise witness testimony or the defendant's confession over forensic evidence as the primary means of proving a crime, owing to a lack of understanding or clarity regarding the status of forensic evidence and its probative value under procedural law [[48], [49], [50]]. This condition reflects the need for a paradigm shift and highlights the urgency of recognising the status and validity of forensic evidence within the criminal justice process.

This study aims, first, to analyse the position of forensic evidence in the criminal justice process in Indonesia; and second, to examine the extent to which forensic evidence is effectively employed as a basis of proof by judges and public prosecutors in the criminal justice process in Indonesia.

Methods

This study employs a two-stage research design combining doctrinal analysis and empirical study. The first stage consists of a doctrinal legal study focusing on the legal framework governing the criminal evidentiary process in Indonesia. Primary legal sources were collected and analysed contextually, namely Law No. 8 of 1981 concerning the Criminal Procedure Code (Kitab Undang-Undang Hukum Acara Pidana/KUHAP) and Law No. 11 of 2008 on Electronic Information and Transactions (UU ITE), as amended by Law No. 19 of 2016 and further amended by Law No. 1 of 2024. The contextual analysis undertaken at this stage comprised the identification of all statutory provisions relating to the regulation of types of evidence admissible in criminal proceedings. This was followed by interpretative analysis through grammatical interpretation namely, construing the wording of statutory provisions in accordance with grammatical rules and systematic interpretation which involves reading each provision in relation to other articles within the same statute or related legislation so that the regulatory framework is understood as a coherent and integrated whole. Through these interpretative methods, the study assesses the legal position of forensic evidence within the statutory classification of evidentiary categories under Indonesian law. The second stage comprised an empirical study reviewing the number of cases decided on the basis of forensic evidence in district courts between January 2023 and December 2024, on the basis that this period represents the most recent data available from the Supreme Court. Data collection combined documentary analysis in the form of excerpts from judicial decisions issued by district courts with statistical data on criminal cases decided across all offence categories. Statistical data were compiled from case statistics decided by district courts throughout Indonesia during the period from January 2023 to December 2024. To identify the number of cases decided on the basis of forensic evidence, four district courts were selected as samples, representing the courts with the highest caseloads in 2023 and 2024: the Central Jakarta District Court and the South Jakarta District Court as representatives of western Indonesia, the Makassar District Court as a representative of central Indonesia, and the Ternate District Court as a representative of eastern Indonesia. In calculating the number of cases decided on the basis of forensic evidence, every case in which forensic evidence was utilised whether in the form of physical evidence, testimonial evidence, or expert opinion was categorised as a case involving forensic evidence. From all criminal cases decided by each district court, five categories of criminal cases were subsequently identified and ranked according to the highest percentages of cases decided on the basis of forensic evidence. The integration of qualitative and quantitative methods strengthens the validity of the study by providing both descriptive and analytical insights into the position and effectiveness of forensic evidence within Indonesia's criminal justice process.

Results

The Legal Framework Governing Forensic Evidence in Criminal Justice Process.

Forensic evidence refers to all forms of evidence obtained, analysed, and interpreted through scientific methods for use in investigation and trial proceedings [5,6,51]. Within Indonesian criminal procedure, forensic evidence occupies a crucial position in achieving the purpose of criminal procedure law, namely the pursuit of material truth (materiële waarheid) as the basis for law enforcement and justice [52,53]. Conceptually, forensic evidence is understood as the outcome of the application of scientific methods to the examination of evidence, whether in the form of physical evidence such as DNA, fingerprints, and shoeprints, or digital evidence such as electronic data and recordings, which are subsequently utilised by law enforcement officials to uncover a crime [[54], [55], [56]].

Indonesian Criminal Procedure Code (KUHAP)

Normatively, the admissible forms of evidence in criminal proceedings are stipulated in Article 184(1) of the Indonesian Criminal Procedure Code (KUHAP), namely witness testimony, expert testimony, documents, indications, and the defendant's testimony. Although the term “forensic evidence” is not explicitly mentioned, substantively it falls within the categories of expert testimony (Article 186 KUHAP), documents (Article 187 KUHAP), and indications (Article 188 KUHAP).

First, forensic evidence is regarded as expert testimony. Article 1, point (28) KUHAP defines expert testimony as the statement of a person with special expertise regarding matters necessary to clarify a criminal case for the purposes of examination. This provision affirms that forensic evidence cannot be presented arbitrarily but must be provided by professionals with specialised competence, such as forensic pathologists, toxicologists, molecular biologists (DNA), ballistics experts, and digital forensic specialists [[57], [58], [59]]. In practice, forensic doctors may provide opinions based on autopsy findings regarding cause of death, time of death, or indications of violence; ballistics experts may explain firearm characteristics, direction, and distance of shots; while digital forensic experts may trace electronic communications or social media activity [57].

Furthermore, Article 133(1) KUHAP authorises investigators to request the testimony of forensic medical experts, doctors, or other specialists when dealing with victims of injury, poisoning, or suspected criminal death [6]. Article 133(2) KUHAP requires such requests to be made in writing, specifying clearly the purpose of the examination, whether it be for injuries, corpses, or autopsies [60,61]. Forensic evidence may therefore also be embodied in documents such as expert reports or opinions detailing the scientific examination of the victim's body, which later serve as valuable proof in court. Article 186 KUHAP stipulates that expert testimony consists of what an expert declares in court. Thus, beyond being set out in written reports, expert evidence must be accounted for orally in court to explain the examination conducted. The presence of experts in court has strategic significance, enabling judges to probe the scientific basis of findings, test the objectivity of the analysis, and assess the connection of forensic evidence with the criminal elements charged [15].

Second, forensic evidence is considered documentary evidence. Article 187 KUHAP provides that documents include official records, expert certificates, or other documents prepared under lawful authority. Such documents may consist of expert reports, including visum et repertum (forensic medical expert report), toxicology reports, ballistics reports, or digital forensic reports prepared by competent officials, in an official form, signed under oath of office, or otherwise verified under oath [[61], [62], [63]].

Third, forensic evidence may be categorised as indications under Article 188 KUHAP. Indications derive from witness testimony, documents, and the defendant's testimony, which, when connected, generate the judge's conviction of the occurrence of a crime. Theoretically, indications function to bridge gaps in other evidence that may be incomplete or indirect. In the doctrine of criminal procedure, this concept is known as the principle of the corroboration rule, whereby multiple pieces of evidence must interrelate, support, and reinforce one another. Through such interconnection and consistency, the evidence collectively establishes the facts of the case and forms the basis for judicial conviction. [64]. Consequently, indications cannot be separated from forensic science, since scientific methods are essential to processing, analysing, and confirming the relevance of evidence found at a crime scene [[65], [66], [67]]. Article 188(2) KUHAP emphasises that indications can only be derived from witness testimony, documents, and the defendant's testimony, meaning that they are not independent evidence but are logically constructed from other categories. Within this framework, forensic evidence operates as a catalyst linking disparate items of proof.

Law No. 11 of 2008 on electronic information and transactions (UU ITE), as amended by Law No. 19 of 2016 and further amended by Law No. 1 of 2024

Technological advancement has brought significant changes to society, including in the field of law [68]. Social, economic, and political interactions increasingly depend on digital technology, producing a wide array of electronic data [69]. This situation requires the national legal system to adapt in order to accommodate forms of evidence unknown to conventional criminal procedure [70]. In Indonesia, recognition has been established through Law No. 11 of 2008 on Electronic Information and Transactions (UU ITE), later amended by Law No. 19 of 2016, and most recently by Law No. 1 of 2024. These reforms provide a stronger juridical foundation for recognising and using electronic evidence in both criminal and civil proceedings. Article 5(1) of the UU ITE states that “Electronic Information and/or Electronic Documents and/or their printouts constitute valid legal evidence.” This provision explicitly places electronic evidence on the same footing as traditional categories such as witness testimony, expert testimony, documents, indications, and the defendant's testimony under Article 184 KUHAP. Article 5(2) further affirms that electronic information and documents constitute an extension of lawful evidence under prevailing procedural law. Thus, electronic evidence is not merely supplementary but forms an integral part of Indonesia's evidentiary system. This recognition has several implications. First, from a juridical perspective, it fills a legal vacuum, since KUHAP, enacted in 1981, did not anticipate the rise of digital technology and therefore lacked any provision on electronic information [71].

The ITE Law (UU ITE) now allows judges to accept digital data without being constrained by traditional categorisations. Second, from a practical perspective, electronic evidence is vital in uncovering modern crimes such as cybercrime, online fraud, digital terrorism, corruption involving electronic transactions, and money laundering through digital banking ([72]; [73]. Without electronic evidence, enforcement against technology-based crimes would be nearly impossible. Nevertheless, admissibility is not unconditional. The validity of electronic evidence must meet principles of integrity, authenticity, and reliability [71]. Integrity requires assurance that data remain unaltered from creation to presentation in court. Authenticity ensures that data genuinely originate from the relevant source rather than manipulation [74,75]. Reliability refers to the credibility of the systems and methods used to produce, store, and present the data. These requirements are typically demonstrated through digital forensic experts, who explain examination methods, security procedures, and analytical results ([76]; [73].

In practice, electronic evidence is often obtained through digital forensics a scientific process of identifying, collecting, analysing, and presenting digital data as evidence [57,77,78]. Digital forensics ensures that evidence presented in court is defensible both technically and legally [[79], [80], [81]]. For instance, in cases of online banking crimes, electronic transaction records, server logs, or email communications may be forensically examined to prove unlawful conduct [57,82]. This process usually produces a written report (document) and is also clarified by the expert (expert testimony) in court. Accordingly, electronic forensic evidence may simultaneously fulfil two statutory categories under Article 184 KUHAP, namely as a document and as expert testimony.

Fig. 1 shows the comparative data from January 2023 to December 2024 in four district courts, it can be illustrated that the Central Jakarta District Court recorded the highest number of cases, namely out of a total of 1610 cases, 1282 cases (80%) were decided on the basis of forensic evidence. Finally, the Ternate District Court, with a total of 237 cases, decided 160 cases (68%) on the basis of forensic evidence.

The high use of forensic evidence in the two Jakarta-based district courts, namely Central Jakarta and South Jakarta, is attributable to the presence of national forensic laboratory facilities concentrated in Jakarta. The existence of the Forensic Laboratory Centre (Puslabfor), together with experts in various fields ranging from narcotics, forensic auditing, and digital forensics to academic institutions and research bodies in Jakarta, has significantly enhanced the capacity of forensic experts to provide comprehensive support for law enforcement. Meanwhile, the district courts of Makassar and Ternate also recorded significant reliance on forensic evidence. In Makassar, this is largely due to its status as the largest city in Central Indonesia and the location of the regional forensic laboratory serving Central and Eastern Indonesia. By contrast, in Ternate, the limited forensic laboratory facilities in North Maluku mean that cases must still be referred to the forensic laboratory in Makassar for examination. The necessity of referring forensic examinations to the forensic laboratory in Makassar results in significant delays in case processing, as evidence must be transported over long distances before analysis can be conducted. Such delays may hinder the timely progression of investigations and prosecutions, potentially affecting procedural efficiency and legal certainty. In addition, the reliance on distant forensic facilities leads to increased operational and logistical costs, including transportation, storage, and coordination expenses borne by law enforcement agencies. More critically, extended transportation times raise concerns regarding the integrity and quality of forensic evidence, as physical evidence may be exposed to degradation, contamination, or loss if not handled under optimal conditions.

In addition, documentary evidence such as visum et repertum (forensic medical expert report), toxicology reports, and digital communication analyses within narcotics distribution networks becomes crucial in persuading judges. Second, in assault cases, the use of forensic evidence reached 94,61%. Here, forensic medical experts play a dominant role, particularly through the preparation of visum et repertum (forensic medical expert report), which forms the basis for proving the victim's injuries. Forensic psychological assessments of trauma further extend the evidentiary dimension, encompassing not only physical injuries but also the psychological impact on victims [86,87]. Biomechanics, ballistics, and sharp weapons experts strengthen the analysis of the cause of injuries, whether inflicted by blunt objects, sharp objects, or firearms [88]. Radiological and biomechanical forensic reports further reinforce the accuracy of findings, enabling judges to assess events objectively. Third, in firearms and sharp weapons offences, the percentage of forensic evidence use stood at 92,08%, underscoring the critical importance of scientific proof in cases involving weapons. Forensic experts in ballistics, metallurgy, fingerprinting, DNA, and chemistry collaborate to reconstruct criminal events [89]. Ballistics test reports, gunshot residue analyses, and metallurgical reports assist in determining the origin and use of weapons as well as their connection with perpetrators. Biological evidence such as fingerprints or DNA found on weapons strengthens the identification of offenders. At the same time, victims' visum et repertum (forensic medical expert report) and forensic photographic documentation of the crime scene provide essential medical and visual corroboration of the facts. Fourth, in electronic information and transactions (ITE) cases, the percentage of forensic evidence use reached 72,63%. Although lower compared to physical crimes, the figure remains significant, reflecting the technological nature of such offences. Forensic evidence in these cases primarily derives from digital forensics, cybersecurity, telecommunications networks, cryptography, and multimedia forensics [90]. Digital analysis reports, server log reports, and social media examination results constitute the primary forms of documentary evidence. This illustrates that proof in ITE cases requires accurate digital reconstruction, given the electronic and easily manipulated nature of exhibits. The reliability of such evidence is decisive for successful prosecution, particularly in cases of online fraud, hate speech, and the digital sexual exploitation of children. Fifth, in corruption cases, the percentage of forensic evidence use stood at 59.38%. Although lower than in other categories, corruption cases involve a broader range of expertise, including forensic accounting, state finance, criminology, digital forensics, and administrative law. Documentary evidence such as investigative audit reports from the Audit Board of Indonesia (BPK) or the Financial and Development Supervisory Agency (BPKP), reports from the Financial Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (PPATK), and reports from the Financial Services Authority (OJK) on financial flows are of particular significance [91,92]. This reflects the nature of corruption offences, which typically involve complex financial transactions, administrative documentation, and bureaucratic procedural violations [93]. Accordingly, forensic reports in finance and administration carry substantial evidentiary weight in establishing state financial losses and the involvement of public officials.

Discussion

The significance of forensic evidence in case decisions

From January 2023 to December 2024, a comparative analysis of four Indonesian district courts: Central Jakarta, South Jakarta, Makassar, and Ternate reveals a consistent and analytically significant pattern: the greater the complexity of the offence, the stronger the reliance on forensic science to ensure judicial accuracy. The comparative data provide critical insight into the evolving evidentiary landscape of Indonesian criminal adjudication, demonstrating a clear shift towards the centrality of forensic evidence in the judicial determination of guilt. The figures indicate that forensic science has become indispensable in adjudicating complex offences such as narcotics-related crimes, corruption, assault, firearms and sharp weapons offences, and electronic information and transactions offences. These categories of crime are inherently technical in nature, involving factual determinations that extend beyond ordinary human perception and legal reasoning based solely on narrative accounts. Consequently, they cannot be resolved adequately through traditional forms of proof such as witness testimony or confessions alone. While such forms of evidence remain legally recognised and procedurally relevant, their epistemic limitations become particularly pronounced in cases requiring scientific, financial, or technological verification.

In narcotics cases, for example, judicial findings depend fundamentally on laboratory confirmation of both the type and quantity of seized substances. Without forensic chemical analysis, courts would lack a reliable basis to determine whether the material qualifies as a narcotic or psychotropic substance under the law, as well as to assess the legal consequences attached to the offence. Forensic examination serves not merely as corroborative proof but as a decisive element in establishing the legal status of the evidence [94]. Similarly, in corruption cases, forensic evidence plays a crucial role in reconstructing complex financial realities that are otherwise invisible to the court. Forensic financial audits are essential to trace illicit financial flows, quantify state losses, and substantiate the causal link between the exercise of public authority and unlawful personal gain. Corruption often involve sophisticated schemes designed to conceal wrongdoing, rendering conventional evidentiary methods insufficient. In this context, forensic accounting transforms abstract allegations into demonstrable facts, enabling judges to assess responsibility on a rational and verifiable basis. [95]. Therefore, these patterns support the broader analytical claim that forensic evidence has shifted from a supplementary evidentiary role to a structural necessity within modern criminal adjudication. Moreover, the increasing reliance on forensic science signifies that technical evidence exerts a substantial influence on the transformation of judicial paradigms in assessing the nature of crime.

The high levels of reliance on forensic evidence across Central Jakarta, South Jakarta, Makassar, and Ternate demonstrate that without forensic science, the judiciary would be ill-equipped to discharge its fundamental duty of establishing materiële waarheid or material truth. Second, the data underscores forensic evidence as a central determinant of judicial accuracy and fairness. By grounding decisions in scientific findings, judges reduce the risks of error, arbitrariness, and subjective bias [96]. Testimonial evidence, though important, may be influenced by unreliable memory, personal bias, or even coercion, while confessions remain vulnerable to questions of voluntariness [97]. Forensic evidence, by contrast, offers objective, reproducible, and scientifically verifiable results that anchor judicial reasoning. The consistency of high percentages across all four courts, ranging between 68% and 80% of cases relying on forensic proof, illustrates that forensic science is no longer peripheral but has moved to the centre of evidentiary assessment. This shift reflects the gradual transformation of Indonesian adjudication from one heavily reliant on traditional testimony towards one that embraces evidence-based justice. Third, the disparities between courts also highlight the significance of infrastructure in shaping evidentiary practice. The Central Jakarta and South Jakarta District Courts are institutionally advantaged by their geographical proximity to national forensic institutions, particularly the Forensic Laboratory Centre (Pusat Laboratorium Forensik/Puslabfor). This proximity significantly facilitates law enforcement efforts at every stage of the criminal process, from investigation through to evidentiary presentation at trial. Investigators are able to submit physical and digital evidence for examination more efficiently, while prosecutors can more readily integrate forensic findings into case construction. In addition, courts in Jakarta benefit from access to a broader pool of expert witnesses, including forensic specialists and experts from related disciplines such as medicine, chemistry, information technology, and financial analysis. This concentration of expertise enhances the quality of forensic analysis and expert reporting, thereby strengthening the evidentiary foundation upon which judicial decisions are made.

The Makassar District Court occupies a strategically important position within Indonesia's forensic infrastructure. Geographically, Makassar serves as a major regional hub and functions as a reference centre for forensic laboratory services for central and eastern Indonesia. As a result, law enforcement authorities in Makassar experience fewer logistical barriers in conducting forensic examinations and obtaining expert analyses compared to more remote jurisdictions. The availability of regional forensic facilities allows for relatively timely examination, preservation of evidentiary integrity, and smoother coordination between investigators, forensic practitioners, and prosecutors. Consequently, the use of forensic evidence in Makassar is more readily institutionalised within investigative and adjudicative practices.

By contrast, the Ternate District Court illustrates the structural challenges faced by courts located in geographically remote and archipelagic regions of eastern Indonesia. Ternate's limited access to forensic laboratory facilities means that forensic examinations must be conducted in Makassar. This dependency generates several systemic constraints. First, the need to transport evidence across long distances inevitably leads to delays in the investigative process, potentially slowing case progression and prolonging judicial resolution. Second, such referrals impose additional financial burdens, including transportation, storage, and coordination costs, on law enforcement agencies. Third, extended transportation and handling increase the risk of evidence degradation or contamination, which may compromise the probative value of forensic findings.

Variations in access to forensic infrastructure may compromise procedural fairness and equality before the law. As forensic evidence assumes a more prominent role in criminal adjudication, disparities in forensic resources can result in inconsistent capacities to establish material facts across jurisdictions. These findings highlight the necessity for policy interventions to expand regional forensic facilities, enhance technological and logistical support, and strengthen the capacity of judges, prosecutors, and defence lawyers.

Limited recognition of forensic evidence in Indonesian Criminal procedure law

The limited recognition of forensic evidence within Indonesian criminal procedure law presents both normative and practical challenges. Normatively, forensic evidence is not explicitly regulated in the Criminal Procedure Code (KUHAP). This absence results in uncertainty and ambiguity concerning its status, admissibility, and probative value. Although forensic science has become an indispensable part of modern criminal justice systems worldwide [98], in Indonesia it remains subsumed under broader categories such as expert testimony, documentary evidence, or indications. Such indirect recognition fails to capture the unique characteristics of forensic evidence as scientific proof, thereby diminishing its distinct role in bridging the gap between law and science.

From a practical perspective, this normative gap translates into inconsistent judicial interpretation. Judges are left without clear statutory guidance on how to evaluate forensic findings, leading to disparities in evidentiary assessment. In certain cases, judges accord decisive weight to forensic evidence, treating it as pivotal in establishing material truth. In other cases, however, judges relegate it to a mere supplementary role, continuing to prioritise witness testimony or the defendant's confession. This inconsistency undermines the predictability of judicial outcomes and, more significantly, jeopardises the fairness of criminal proceedings.

The Indonesian system of proof, rooted in the negatief wettelijk bewijs theorie as articulated in Article 183 KUHAP, requires both a minimum of two lawful pieces of evidence and the inner conviction of the judge. In theory, this system seeks to balance objectivity and subjectivity by preventing convictions based on unfounded beliefs while simultaneously ensuring judicial discretion. In practice, however, the absence of explicit legal recognition of forensic evidence as an autonomous category of proof has contributed to its subtle but consequential marginalisation within criminal proceedings. Although forensic findings are frequently presented in court, they are often subsumed under traditional evidentiary categories such as expert testimony or documentary evidence without adequate appreciation of their distinct epistemic character. This doctrinal ambiguity affects not only how forensic evidence is classified, but also how it is evaluated. When forensic reports are treated as secondary to witness testimony or confessions, their probative value may be understated, particularly in complex cases where scientific analysis is central to establishing material facts.

Concrete examples illustrate these challenges. In the Wayan Mirna Salihin case (the “cyanide coffee case”), forensic toxicology became central to the conviction, yet the controversy surrounding the judgment revealed judicial ambivalence regarding the weight of circumstantial forensic evidence. In the Brigadier J case, forensic pathology, ballistics, and digital forensics were instrumental in exposing fabricated narratives, but the public debate underscored the dependence on judicial interpretation in conferring [99] legitimacy upon scientific findings. Such cases reveal the dual reality of forensic evidence in Indonesia: indispensable in practice, yet insecure in law. Scholars have argued that the limited recognition of forensic evidence reflects a tension between traditional evidentiary paradigms and the demands of scientific modernity [15,100,101]. The classical categories of KUHAP were drafted in 1981, prior to the rise of DNA profiling, cybercrime, or digital forensics. At that time, the concept of forensic evidence as an autonomous evidentiary category had not yet crystallised. However, the exponential growth of forensic science in the past four decades necessitates reform. Without statutory reform, judges will continue to rely on discretionary interpretations, producing inconsistent outcomes.

The implications of this limited recognition are significant. First, it raises concerns about the accuracy of fact-finding. Forensic evidence, whether in the form of DNA profiling, toxicology reports, digital forensic audits, or ballistics analyses, provides objective and scientifically verifiable insights [102]. By treating such evidence as secondary, courts risk privileging subjective accounts, which may be distorted by bias, memory lapses, or coercion. Second, the inconsistency in judicial treatment erodes public confidence in the judiciary. Citizens may perceive outcomes as arbitrary, shaped more by the discretion of individual judges than by reliable scientific standards. Third, the absence of a clear and explicit doctrinal framework for forensic evidence hampers the development of criminal procedural law in keeping with rapid technological progress. In an era marked by digitalisation and the expansion of technologically mediated crime, legal doctrine that remains anchored solely in traditional evidentiary categories risks becoming increasingly detached from empirical reality. While other jurisdictions, such as South Korea, have progressively integrated forensic science as a central element of proof within criminal proceedings [99,103,104], Indonesia continues to rely on a procedural structure that does not formally recognise forensic evidence as an autonomous and primary category of proof. This gap exposes Indonesia's justice system to growing criticisms of doctrinal obsolescence.

The challenge becomes particularly pronounced in the context of cybercrime, where criminal conduct is manifested almost entirely through digital traces rather than physical acts observable by witnesses ([105]; [106]. Cyber offences such as online fraud, hacking, identity theft, ransomware attacks, and electronic financial manipulation generate evidence in the form of metadata, system logs, transaction records, and digital communications [107,108]. These forms of proof cannot be meaningfully assessed through witness testimony or confessions alone. If digital forensic evidence is treated merely as supplementary or auxiliary, rather than as a primary evidentiary foundation, courts risk misapprehending the factual structure of cyber offences and undermining the pursuit of material truth.

Developments in artificial intelligence (AI) further intensify this challenge. AI-driven forensic methodologies, including facial recognition technologies, machine learning-based pattern analysis, and advanced digital forensic tools, increasingly shape how evidence is generated and interpreted [109,110]. In jurisdictions such as South Korea [[111], [112], [113]], these technologies have been accompanied by legal and institutional adaptation, ensuring that forensic science occupies a central role in evidentiary assessment. In Indonesia, by contrast, the absence of doctrinal clarity may lead to inconsistent judicial treatment of AI-generated forensic evidence. Judges may either over-rely on technologically framed conclusions due to their perceived scientific authority or discount them due to uncertainty regarding their legal status and reliability. From a normative perspective, failing to position forensic evidence particularly digital forensics as a primary form of proof risks creating a structural mismatch between the nature of contemporary crime and the legal tools used to adjudicate it. As crimes become increasingly data-driven and algorithmically mediated, evidentiary systems that prioritise subjective accounts over scientifically verifiable proof may struggle to deliver accurate and fair outcomes. Moreover, without recognising digital forensic evidence as central, the legal system may lag behind investigative practice, resulting in a disjunction between how crimes are investigated and how they are proven in court.

The normative solution lies in revising the KUHAP through the draft bill currently under discussion. Explicit recognition of forensic evidence as a sui generis category would provide clarity on its admissibility and probative value. Such recognition should be accompanied by procedural safeguards, including standards for expert qualifications, accreditation of forensic laboratories, and protocols for chain of custody. These measures would ensure that forensic findings meet both scientific and legal reliability standards, thereby enhancing their legitimacy in court. On the practical level, capacity-building is essential. Judges, prosecutors, and defence lawyers must receive training in the basics of forensic science to enable informed evaluation of forensic reports. Without such knowledge, even statutory recognition would remain hollow, as legal actors would be unable to assess the validity of scientific findings. Similarly, investment in forensic infrastructure labs, equipment, and human resources is crucial to ensure timely and credible forensic analyses. The disparities observed between courts in Jakarta, Makassar, and Ternate demonstrate how uneven access to forensic facilities significantly shapes the role and effectiveness of forensic evidence in criminal adjudication. While the availability of forensic infrastructure directly influences evidentiary practice, these disparities must also be understood within the broader context of differing crime patterns and caseloads across jurisdictions. Variations in crime rates, offence types, and levels of urbanisation inevitably affect both the demand for forensic examination and the institutional capacity to respond to that demand. In Jakarta, where crime rates are higher and offences tend to be more complex and technologically mediated, courts benefit from close proximity to national forensic institutions and a dense concentration of expert resources. This facilitates frequent and routine use of forensic evidence in investigations and trials. Makassar, serving as a regional hub for central and eastern Indonesia, occupies an intermediate position, with relatively better access to forensic laboratories than more remote regions, enabling a moderate yet consistent reliance on forensic proof. In contrast, Ternate, characterised by its geographical remoteness and lower population density, may experience different crime dynamics, including lower overall case volumes and a distinct distribution of offence types. Nevertheless, limited access to forensic facilities in Ternate constrains the use of forensic evidence even in cases where scientific examination would be crucial. These disparities illustrate that forensic reliance is not solely determined by legal norms, but also by structural and contextual factors, including geography, infrastructure, and crime prevalence.

Conclusion

The role of forensic evidence within Indonesian criminal procedure remains problematic due to the absence of explicit regulation. At present, forensic evidence is not formally recognised as a distinct evidentiary category within the Criminal Procedure Code. Instead, it is only interpreted indirectly through its classification under existing categories of evidence as stipulated in Article 184 KUHAP namely expert testimony, documentary, or indications. This lack of clarity has significant implications, as it results in law enforcement officials, prosecutors, and even judges failing to accord forensic science the special attention it deserves in criminal justice process. Data on the number of cases decided on the basis of forensic evidence in four district courts illustrates that forensic evidence exerts a significant influence on judicial outcomes, particularly in offences that are heavily dependent upon such evidence, including narcotics, assault, firearms and sharp weapons offences, electronic information and transactions, and corruption. As a proposed solution, the author recommends that the Indonesian legal framework, particularly the Criminal Procedure Code, should be revised to include forensic evidence as an independent category. Such reform would ensure that law enforcement officials, in conducting evidentiary processes, actively pursue the fulfilment of forensic evidence, thereby realising a more objective and equitable criminal justice process.
