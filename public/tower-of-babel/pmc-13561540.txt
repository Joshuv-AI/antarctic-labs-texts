Driving retirement in older adults: a qualitative exploration into their plans, feelings, and support needs
Authors: Jade Carpenter, Sjaan Koppel, Oscar Modesto, Thomas B McGuckian, Benjamin Weir, Joanne M Bennett, Jennifer Baumbusch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesDriving retirement is a significant life transition for older adults (aged 65 and older), which has been associated with negative psychosocial and health outcomes.Research Design and MethodsThis qualitative study explores eight older adults’ plans, feelings, and support needs when stopping driving. After focus groups were conducted, participants were categorized according to Adler and Rottunda’s (2006. Older adults’ perspectives on driving cessation. Journal of Aging Studies, 20, 227–235) groupings—Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters—to examine their nuanced perspectives. Using thematic analysis, key themes were established across groups.ResultsThree primary factors prompted participants across all groups to begin thinking about retiring driving, including: Medical Reasons, Safety Reasons, and Licensing Barriers. Feelings around driving retirement were predominantly negative across groups, with the exception of Acceptance; however, differences between groups were observed where Proactives tended to discuss their feelings in the past tense, whereas Resisters discussed feelings in the present tense. Using the Social Ecological Model, participants across groups highlighted the strong need to enhance support systems at interpersonal, environmental, and policy levels, with a notable absence of discussions regarding individual-level supports.Discussion and ImplicationsFindings of this study suggest that driving retirement in older adulthood may be better conceptualized as a phase-based transition, rather than categories. This phase-based approach could better inform clinicians to assist older drivers in navigating their driving retirement transition by integrating early readiness assessments, phase-specific interventions, and post-retirement emotional support. Future research should aim to further explore evidence-based interventions at the individual level that support older adults pre- and post-driving retirement to facilitate a smoother transition whilst promoting well-being.Keywords: Driving retirement, Older adults, Social Ecological Model, Thematic analysis, Transportation

Introduction

For older adults (aged 65 years and older), preserving independence and mobility is crucial for healthy aging (Webber et al., 2010). Specifically, older adults aged 65–69 are relative to age groups with the highest proportion of vehicle drivers, suggesting older adults remain mobile by driving (Morgan, 2020). However, age-related declines in physical, visual, and cognitive health are associated with poorer driving performance (Bond et al., 2017) and are consistently demonstrated to increase the risk of older adults being involved/seriously injured/killed in motor vehicle crashes (Cox & Cicchino, 2021). With the increasing aging population, crash statistics involving older adults have risen, with a 19.3% increase in motor vehicle deaths between 2010 and 2020, and are anticipated to increase further (Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics, 2022). This increased safety risk persuades many older adults to transition into driving retirement (i.e., driving cessation) (Friedland & Rudman, 2009).

For some older adults, the decision to stop driving is uncomplicated, while for others, it is challenging (Buys et al., 2012). Adler and Rottunda (2006) conducted qualitative focus groups with older recently retired drivers to identify three groups based on their decision-making processes when stopping driving. These groups included: Proactives; individuals who independently decided to stop driving and actively sought support; Reluctant accepters; individuals who recognized the need to retire from driving, but stopped reluctantly; and Resisters, individuals who opposed the need to retire from driving until obligated.

Understanding Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) distinct groups provides valuable insight into the psychological dimensions of driving retirement. Research has demonstrated that older adults who retire from driving reluctantly or resist the transition are at greater risk of experiencing negative psychosocial outcomes, including increased mental health problems (Ang et al., 2019). In contrast, those who proactively plan for driving retirement tend to adapt more easily, mitigating these negative psychological impacts (Musselwhite & Shergold, 2013). These findings highlight the importance of targeted, group-specific interventions to support older adults through their transition into driving retirement.

In an attempt to understand the broader factors that accompany planning for retirement, a systematic review by Schofield et al. (2023) provided a complementary perspective by applying the Social Ecological Model (SEM) to 12 included studies. The SEM examines how individual, interpersonal, environmental, and policy-level factors interact to shape health-related behaviors (McLeroy et al., 1988). Schofield and colleagues employed a thematic synthesis approach to analyze qualitative data from the reviewed studies, then utilized an inductive approach to identify and code themes and sub-themes, which were categorized and refined using the four levels of the SEM. As a result, Schofield et al. established 11 sub-themes across four SEM levels that represent facilitators and barriers to planning for driving retirement. For a detailed outline of these findings, refer to Schofield et al. (2023).

At the individual level, Schofield et al. (2023) highlighted beliefs and feelings as key factors. Two sub-themes emerged: Who is in control? and Sense of burden. Maintaining control over the decision to stop driving aids planning and reduces depressive symptoms post-retirement (Oxley & Charlton, 2009). However, the fear of becoming a burden can hinder planning, as older drivers may be reluctant to seek support (Mullen et al., 2017).

At the interpersonal level, Schofield et al. (2023) found that Feedback Preferences, Family, Clinicians, Peer Support, and Health Communication all influence planning for driving retirement. Older drivers tend to prefer gradual, non-confrontational feedback from family, while feedback from healthcare professionals is often perceived as more authoritative (Friedland & Rudman, 2009). However, family members and clinicians may hesitate to initiate these discussions due to fears of negative reactions or damaging relationships (Söllner & Florack, 2019). Early conversations can reduce distress and improve planning (Lafrance et al., 2022), but delays can hinder effective planning (Liddle et al., 2008). Peer support and health communication, such as media campaigns, also encourage planning by normalizing the experience (Betz et al., 2015).

At the environmental level, Schofield et al. (2023) identified Infrastructure and Public Transport and Location as important sub-themes. Urban drivers or those living near family members tend to have more transportation options, which facilitates planning for driving retirement (Stinchcombe et al., 2024). Conversely, older adults in rural areas face limited transportation alternatives, making relocation or other life changes necessary to plan for retirement (Hansen et al., 2020).

At the policy level, Schofield et al. (2023) found that legislation plays a role in older drivers’ planning for driving retirement. They identified On-road Driving Tests and Mandatory Medical Examinations as key sub-themes. These assessments can provide early insight into impairments, facilitating planning (Hassan et al., 2015). However, they are often viewed as barriers due to the stress and potential threats to independence they may cause (Stinchcombe et al., 2024).

Altogether, Schofield et al. (2023) emphasize the importance of the SEM in understanding the multifaceted factors influencing driving retirement. The SEM provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating how factors at the individual, interpersonal, environmental, and policy levels interconnect to shape the transition into driving retirement. Although much research has identified facilitators and barriers, a gap remains in understanding the supports older adults may need during the pre-retirement phase. Supports, in this context, refer to ongoing resources and services—distinct from facilitators and barriers—that help older drivers plan for and navigate their eventual transition into driving retirement. By applying the SEM, this study aims to identify specific supports needed at each level of the model to ensure older drivers feel equipped as they approach driving retirement. This approach can inform the development of evidence-based programs to better prepare older adults for their transition into driving retirement, which could help to promote quality of life and autonomy during this transition.

In this present study, participants were initially categorized according to Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings—Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters—in aims of providing a framework to explore the differences between participants’ experiences around driving retirement. Although these groupings provide a foundational understanding of older adults’ attitudes around driving retirement, little is known about the nuanced planning processes, feelings, and support needs unique to each group. By combining the insights offered by Adler and Rottunda’s groupings with Schofield et al.’s (2023) adapted SEM, this study aims to provide a nuanced exploration of older adults’ experiences and needs when entering driving retirement. This approach can help further understand how to better support older drivers across multiple dimensions of driving retirement. This study ultimately seeks to inform the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions that can enable clinicians to provide tailored support to foster well-being and healthy aging in older adults transitioning from driving. Method

Transparency and openness

The current research study was not preregistered, and no formal analysis plan was preregistered. The interview questions used in the study are included in the manuscript under ‘Data Collection’. Due to ethical considerations and participant confidentiality, the dataset is not publicly available; however, anonymized datasets (i.e., focus group transcripts) may be shared upon reasonable request. As NVivo 14 (Lumivero, 2023) was utilized to aid coding for thematic analysis, the project file may also be made available upon request to support transparency and replicability.

Positionality statement

The primary researcher JC, acknowledges their positionality as a young person, an outsider to the experience of planning for driving retirement in older adulthood. JC has prior experience conducting a systematic review on the relationship between driving cessation and depressive symptomology in older adults. Analyses were conducted in conjunction with JMB, who has over 10 years of experience in road safety research with older adults, and OM, who is an expert in qualitative research methodology. This work was supported by SK, who also has over 20 years of experience in road safety research with older adults, TM, an emerging researcher in road safety research with older adults, and BW, a clinical psychologist.

Research design

This qualitative study uses thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) to examine data to address the following research questions: (1) Why are older drivers thinking about driving retirement?; (2) What are their feelings around driving retirement?; and (3) What supports do they think are needed to maintain their well-being as they transition into driving retirement? This approach was used to explore the subjective experiences of participants considering retiring from driving, and to compare these experiences according to their group categorization. Group categorization was conducted initially during data analysis, which was deductively analyzed from Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings, who identified three groups of older retired drivers: Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters. These groups were compared to further understand nuances of each group’s planning processes, feelings, and support needs around driving retirement. The first two research questions were analyzed using inductive approaches. The third research question was deductively analyzed, referring to the SEM adapted by Schofield et al. (2023). Schofield et al. identified factors that influence older adults when planning for driving retirement according to SEM levels (i.e., individual, interpersonal, environment, and policy levels). This theoretical framework was applied to explore what support systems/resources older adults may need at each level when planning for driving retirement.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were Australian older adults (aged 65 years and older) who had not stopped driving but were considering driving retirement. This study was advertised via paid Facebook advertisements, through advertising via professional and community networks, and word-of-mouth. Recruitment occurred from July 27th to August 21st 2024. Individuals first registered their interest in participating via a Qualtrics survey, which included the Participant Information Letter, and options to provide contact details. All interested participants were then contacted by JC to organize their focus group.

Data collection

Ethical approval was received from the ACU University Human Research Ethics Committee (#2024-3640SAP). Data collection was conducted in two phases: an online questionnaire via Qualtrics, and an online focus group via Microsoft Teams. All participants completed an information and consent form prior to participation in the study via Qualtrics, with a record of all consent forms retained.

Phase one

Participants completed an online questionnaire via Qualtrics, which included questions related to participant characteristics, including age, gender, residential state/territory, marital status, living situation, driving frequency, and whether they access alternative modes of transportation.

Data analysis

Participant categorization was initially conducted by JC and JMB, using focus group transcriptions and video recordings. Participants were categorized in Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings—Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters—using a deductive approach. These groupings were originally defined as follows: Proactives are individuals who independently decide to stop driving and actively seek support; Reluctant Accepters are individuals who acknowledge the need to retire from driving but do so reluctantly; and Resisters are individuals who oppose the need to stop driving and typically only do so when required.

To apply these groupings to this study’s sample of participants, two researchers (JC and JMB) independently reviewed the focus group transcripts and video recordings to identify verbal cues and narratives that aligned with the defining characteristics of each group. Specific indicators included participants’ language around autonomy, planning, resistance, and their emotional tone toward driving cessation. Discrepancies in initial categorization were discussed until consensus.

Following this, Braun and Clarke’s (2006, 2019) six-stage reflexive thematic analysis was utilized when analyzing focus group data, enabling the identification and interpretation of participants’ experiences around driving retirement through a constructivist lens. This inductive method ensured an authentic representation of participants’ experiences rather than imposing a pre-determined framework. Using Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis, themes are understood as interpretive and actively generated through the researcher’s engagement with the data, rather than as preexisting patterns to be discovered. Consequently, data saturation was not considered an appropriate criterion for sample adequacy. Instead, the focus was on obtaining a sufficiently rich dataset to address the research question and support meaningful thematic interpretation (Braun and Clarke, 2021).

Braun and Clarke’s (2006) reflexive thematic analysis was employed collaboratively by JC and JMB, which involved the following six steps: (1) familiarization, involving the process of reviewing Microsoft Teams transcripts and video recordings twice to ensure accuracy and enhance data familiarization; (2) generating initial codes, using NVivo 14 (Lumivero, 2023) to aid the coding process. A line-by-line open coding approach was applied, with researchers assigning descriptive labels to text segments that reflected participants’ experiences, attitudes, and emotions. Transcripts were imported into NVivo, where codes were systematically created and organized using the software’s node structure. JC conducted the initial coding, which was then reviewed by JMB to ensure consistency. NVivo facilitated efficient comparison across participants, supported visual mapping of themes, and helped track code frequency and co-occurrence across categories; (3) generating themes and sub-themes, which involved both inductive and deductive approaches. Inductively, codes were grouped based on shared meaning and emerging patterns, with no pre-determined framework applied. For research questions four to seven, deductive approaches were taken by mapping codes to the SEM levels. NVivo 14 was used to cluster related codes, visualize connections, and assist in organizing themes. JC led the initial theme development within NVivo, which was then reviewed and refined in collaboration with JMB and OM through iterative discussions; (4) reviewing themes, which was conducted collaboratively by JC, JMB and OM to ensure consistency, coherency and relevance to all research questions. This process involved iterative comparison of themes against the coded data, discussion of discrepancies, and evaluation of whether each theme was sufficiently distinct, internally coherent, and accurately representative of participants’ perspectives; (5) naming and finalizing themes and sub-themes, which was initially undertaken by JC, who developed descriptive names to reflect the content and scope of each theme. These names were then reviewed and refined by JMB and OM to ensure clarity, consistency and alignment with the coded data; and (6) producing the report, with finalized themes as presented in the results section. In addition, JC conducted a final review of all coded transcripts in NVivo 14 to cross-check that each theme and sub-theme was clearly grounded in the data and addressed at least one of the seven research questions. This process involved revisiting source excerpts linked to each theme and confirming their relevance to the study’s aims.

In addition to examining thematic content, the researchers observed patterns in participants’ emotional narratives and language use across focus groups. Group classifications (i.e., Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters) were applied post-hoc using Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings, which were reexamined during data analysis for potential fluidity between these groups.

Reflexivity and integrity

This study established reflexivity and integrity in qualitative research by following the American Psychological Association’s recommendations (e.g., utilizing research goals and considering underlying phenomena of the research area to guide methodology; Levitt et al., 2017). Perspective management was established by utilizing reflective research journalling to aid research processes and enable self-reflection on personal experiences, which may lower bias/assumptions when analyzing data. Weekly meetings were held between JC and JMB to discuss research procedures and data analysis, where disagreements were discussed until consensus. Findings were conferred with SK, an expert in road safety research in older adults; and OM, an expert in qualitative methodology. Both SK and OM provided critical feedback on the thematic structure and interpretation of results. OM offered suggestions to strengthen the clarity and coherence of themes based on qualitative best practices, while SK contributed insights to ensure the themes accurately reflected issues relevant to older drivers and road safety. These inputs led to minor refinements in theme labeling and clarification of theme boundaries.

Results

Participants categorized in accordance with Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings

Proactives

Three out of eight participants mentioned experiences related to proactive characteristics, which included: using alternative modes of transport (e.g., trains), involving family in planning, and relocating to more accessible living environments. For example, participant A (Proactive) mentioned: “I’m catching the train quite regularly now … I’m also about to get a mobility scooter.” These participants also described relying on family members for transportation planning or support, or choosing to move into retirement villages or residential areas with better public transport access and walkability. For example, participant B stated, “The retirement village I am moving into has a bus and they go to different shopping centres once a week.”

Reluctant accepters

Two out of eight participants mentioned experiences related to reluctant accepter characteristics, which included: trying not to think about driving retirement and feeling reluctance/resentment toward utilizing alternative forms of transportation. As participant D (Reluctant Accepter) mentioned:

“I try not to think about it, but I know I got to … it depends on whatever is available … If only we could look into a crystal ball. I know that I can’t plan it … so it’s best to sort of not think that far ahead, but I just know whatever is ahead you got to accept.”

Resisters

Three out of eight participants mentioned experiences related to resister characteristics, which included: not wanting to stop driving and being in denial about driving retirement. As participant F (Resister) mentioned: “I haven’t thought that much about the transition because I’m still in this denial phase … because of the mindset as a young man, that I am indestructible.”

Research question 1: Why are older drivers thinking about driving retirement?

Participants outlined their experiences around why they were thinking about driving retirement. Three primary themes were established: Medical Reasons, Safety Reasons, and Licensing Barriers.

Medical reasons

Four out of eight participants reported medical reasons elicited thoughts about driving retirement. Medical reasons included chronic illnesses (e.g., arthritis), previous health scares (e.g., heart attacks), and medication that negatively impaired driving abilities. As participant E (Reluctant Accepter) mentioned: “I’ve got enormous arthritis problems. Plus a disease called peripheral neuropathy. It is not only excruciatingly painful, it is a disease that will eventually stop me from driving.” For other participants, the medical reason was more immediate impact, as participant A (Proactive) mentioned: “The medication I have for pain actually makes it impossible for me to drive … I realised that I mentally wasn’t quite as focused as I thought I was….”

Safety reasons

Four out of eight participants reported safety reasons elicited thoughts about driving retirement. Safety reasons included previous crashes/near crashes and reduced confidence in driving abilities. As participant B (Proactive) explained:

“I’ve had a few instances on the road that have led me to believe that ‘hey, you’re not as sharp as you once were’ … a guy coming towards me flashed his lights and I hesitated for about two seconds before I pulled out and it just shows me that, you’re not as sharp as you once were and it’s time to look at what the alternatives are.”

For other participants like participant G (Resister), the “lost confidence on the road because of tailgating” making them choose whether they would “rather be safe than sorry.”

Licensing barriers

Three out of eight participants reported licensing requirements, particularly those tied to health and age, elicited thoughts about driving retirement. In some jurisdictions where the participants live, to maintain their driver’s license they need to complete mandatory medical assessments after a certain age. Participants reported that these medical assessments and perceived inconsistencies in how licensing decisions were made by different doctors were a barrier to maintaining their driver’s license.

For participant C (Proactive), an unexpected change in medical oversight led to a stricter interpretation of licensing conditions: “My usual doctor was away and I had another person, and she was very strict.” She basically said “at your age you should be having a occupational therapist assessment every year as well as the driving test.” Although this interaction occurred in a clinical context, the participant interpreted it as a barrier to renewing her driver’s license.

Similarly, participant H (Resister) expressed frustration about health-related licensing requirements:

“Next year I’ll probably have to take a driver’s test … because I have diabetes, I have to take a medical report every year. Sometimes that’s difficult. Because I have to not just see my doctor, but I have to see the specialist and sometimes you have to wait months to get in.”

This quote reflects how systemic delays in accessing medical documentation, as required for license renewal, can create stress and uncertainty, acting as perceived licensing barriers even when rooted in broader health system issues.

Discussion

This study aimed to understand older drivers’ planning processes, feelings and support needs related to driving retirement. The findings related to each of the research questions are discussed below with implications for clinical practice and future research examined.

Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters

Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings—Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters—may represent phases of psychological adjustment rather than distinct categories of older drivers. Commonalities and distinct differences were noted across the Proactives, Reluctant Accepters, and Resisters groups. Ascending average age was noted across groups, with Resisters being the youngest, followed by Reluctant Accepters, and Proactives being the oldest. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size, it may reflect differences in life stage. Younger older adults may still perceive themselves as healthy, capable drivers and therefore be more resistant to contemplating driving retirement. In contrast, older participants may have experienced greater changes in health, cognition, or driving confidence over time, contributing to increased acceptance of driving retirement and more proactive planning. Taken together, these findings support the possibility that older drivers may transition through these groups over time, rather than being fixed within one category, similar to the stages of grief (Marsa et al., 2024).

Further supporting this interpretation, differences were observed across groups in how participants described their feelings around driving retirement. Proactive participants tended to discuss their feelings in the past tense, whereas Resisters predominantly discussed their feelings in the present tense. This suggests that Resisters are still processing their feelings, whereas Proactives may have already adjusted. These findings are preliminary due to the limited sample size, with replication of these age and speech tense patterns required in future research, specifically larger, longitudinal studies.

While Adler and Rottunda’s framework provided a useful lens for understanding the participants’ perspectives and experiences, our findings suggest that these groups may be better conceptualized as fluid phases rather than fixed categories. Although participants were categorized according to the characteristics and discussions observed in the focus groups, their narratives often reflected evolving emotional and cognitive responses to driving retirement. For instance, those initially classified as Resisters demonstrated thoughts indicative of early-stage resistance, while others showed signs of progression toward reluctant acceptance or proactive planning. This interpretation is consistent with the observed age differences across groups and the shift from the present—to past-tense descriptions of emotions, suggesting that individuals may move between phases as they adjust over time. Understanding the groupings in this way offers a more dynamic framework, which can inform how clinicians tailor support and intervention development that aligns with individuals’ changing needs over time.

Reasons for thinking about driving retirement

When exploring why older drivers think about driving retirement, no distinct rationale was identified based on group categorization. Across all groups, Medical Reasons were the most discussed reasons, which highlights the important role that clinicians play. Clinicians can facilitate early and regular conversations about driving retirement, especially when there is a change in health status. These conversations could act to support people through the phases of planning and acceptance (Lafrance et al., 2022). Clinicians also play a key role in connecting older adults with relevant support resources, as many older adults report being uninformed on where to seek support when transitioning away from driving (Schofield et al., 2023). These resources can also help older adults to identify potential safety issues in their driving and help them understand the licensing process.

Feelings around driving retirement

A pattern of negative sentiments about driving retirement was observed—particularly encompassing the fear of reduced independence. This pattern is consistent with findings that suggest older drivers often view driving retirement negatively, and experience adverse mental health outcomes post-retirement (Liddle et al., 2024). Negative feelings were evident across all three groups; however, as previously noted, Proactives generally discussed feelings in the past tense, suggesting acceptance. Both Proactives and Reluctant Accepters mentioned acceptance as a positive, which highlights the importance of people coming to a place of acceptance to move through the phases of preparing for driving retirement. Resisters only talked in the present tense about their negative emotions and did not name any positive emotions, which suggests they may be at the beginning of the process and may benefit from targeted support from clinicians to help navigate and manage their feelings about driving retirement.

Support needed to aid driving retirement

Suggestions for supports to help older adults manage the transition into driving retirement were discussed across the Social Ecological Model (SEM). Although support needs varied somewhat across participant groupings, there was a clear emphasis on environmental and policy-level supports, whereas no participants identified supports at the individual level despite being provided with a definition and examples. This finding is noteworthy, as health promotion research often finds that individuals more readily identify programmatic or individual interventions than broader policy initiatives (Kelly et al., 2025).

The environmental SEM level was discussed the most, particularly by Resister participants who felt change was needed on a practical level to support their driving retirement. They cited the need for additional and more connected transportation options to help preserve their independence and mobility. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which highlight the need to diversify alternative transports to facilitate planning for driving retirement (Schofield et al., 2024). The lack of accessibility and connectivity in transportation services may compel older drivers to continue driving longer (Veerhuis et al., 2024). These findings highlight the need to implement more age-friendly transportation solutions that not only provide more options but enhance connectivity and accessibility of transportation infrastructure.

At the policy level, the findings of this study reflect previous research that highlights the structural and systemic barriers older adults face in maintaining mobility and independence. For example, financial constraints have consistently been cited as a key obstacle to accessing alternative transportation (Adler & Rottunda, 2006; Betz et al., 2019; Liddle et al., 2008; Schofield et al., 2024). Similarly, research by Curl et al. (2014) emphasized the importance of flexible, person-centered transport policies that accommodate varying needs and preferences. Inconsistent licensing rules and fragmented services have also been associated with reduced engagement in planning for driving retirement (Dickerson et al., 2024). The current findings support the need for cohesive, integrated policy systems that involve older adults in their design, ensuring supports are informed by lived experience rather than top-down assumptions.

Consistent with Vivoda et al. (2017), outer levels of the SEM may exert the greatest influence because they shape opportunities and behaviors across all lower levels of the system. These systemic changes may reduce emotional distress, promote autonomy, and improve the overall well-being of older adults during the transition away from driving. Our findings therefore suggest that improvements to transport infrastructure, policy consistency, and service accessibility may have broad downstream benefits for older adults navigating driving retirement. Future research should consider co-design approaches with older adults to identify setting-specific environmental and policy initiatives that best support this transition.

For the interpersonal level, consistent with previous research, the findings suggest that clinicians play a vital role in facilitating driving retirement discussions; however, variability in professional advice can create uncertainty and make planning more difficult for older adults (Betz et al., 2013). Similarly, Betz et al. (2019) highlighted that both clinicians and family members often experience uncertainty when initiating conversations about driving retirement, despite recognizing their importance. Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that older adults not only value these conversations, but specifically emphasize the need for clear, consistent, and timely guidance to support planning and decision-making. These findings highlight the need for clearer clinical guidelines and professional development to support healthcare professionals in initiating driving retirement discussions confidently and consistently.

Opinions regarding support from family/friends were mixed across all participant groups. While some welcomed assistance from family/friends, others strongly opposed it due to feelings of burdensomeness. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that perceived burdensomeness is a common consequence of driving retirement (Stinchcombe et al., 2024). Our findings further suggest that the role of family extends beyond providing practical assistance, with participants emphasizing the importance of respectful, collaborative conversations that preserve autonomy while facilitating planning. These findings reinforce recommendations for earlier, family-inclusive discussions that normalize support-seeking and reduce stigma surrounding driving retirement.

The absence of suggested individual-level supports may indicate that participants viewed solutions to the challenges of driving retirement as largely beyond their personal control rather than reflecting a lack of understanding of the individual level. This may also highlight limited awareness of strategies to manage the emotional challenges associated with driving retirement, which could increase vulnerability to poorer mental health outcomes (Chihuri et al., 2016). Evidence from broader aging research suggests that approaches such as mindfulness and self-compassion may promote psychological wellbeing in older adults and warrant investigation within driving retirement interventions (Othman et al., 2025). This finding reinforces the need to develop and evaluate evidence-based psychological interventions that support older adults in managing the emotional aspects of driving retirement.

Clinical implications

One of the goals of this paper was to bring together two different frameworks that have been developed/applied to older adults retiring from driving: Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groups and Schofield et al.’s (2023) SEM model. Models like this are often applied separately with limited integration to determine how they work together to aid understanding. Taking these two frameworks and applying them together in this study allowed for a more nuanced understanding of how different “groups” of older adults might consider the supports they need at different levels of the SEM. The combination of these frameworks is useful as it helps guide clinicians in determining the right support options for different ‘groups’ (or during different phases) at each of the broader SEM levels. This allows for the provision of more person-centered support rather than treating all older adults who are retiring from driving with a single approach.

The findings have several important clinical implications. If Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings are conceptualized as phases of adjustment rather than fixed categories, clinicians may be better able to tailor support to an individual’s stage of transition. Earlier phases may require greater emphasis on acknowledging emotional responses, validating concerns, and facilitating conversations about future driving retirement, whereas later phases may benefit from practical planning, connection with community supports, and strategies to maintain independence following driving cessation. While not originally intended as a theoretical model, patterns in participant response suggest that Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings may reflect fluid phases of readiness for driving retirement rather than static categories. These groupings were created from observed emotional language and participant reflections. While a limited number of participants spoke directly about life after driving retirement, references to post-retirement adjustment emerged (e.g., loss of independence and adjustment difficulties), suggesting a potential fourth phase requiring further investigation. This phase, though less developed in the current study, points to ongoing emotional and practical adaptation that may continue after driving cessation has occurred. This interpretation should be viewed as exploratory, and future studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to support these proposed phases.

Emphasizing emotional adjustment, not just practical planning, could help reduce distress and promote well-being in older adulthood.

By integrating early readiness assessments, phase-specific interventions and post-retirement emotional support, clinicians can play a vital role in assisting older drivers to navigate their transition into driving retirement with more assurance. Future research should aim to continue exploration into evidence-based interventions at the individual level that support older adults through the complex and nuanced aspects of the driving retirement transition.

Limitations

Whilst this study was able to obtain informative insights into older drivers’ experiences around planning for driving retirement, it is not without limitations. The focus group format facilitated important conversations and created a supportive environment for participants to share their experiences. However, the echoing of others’ ideas was common and may have inhibited the variability of the responses, potentially introducing group bias. Future research should consider using individual interviews alongside focus groups to mitigate this effect.

Although participants were recruited from multiple Australian states, the sample was not fully representative. The demographic makeup of the sample may not reflect the diversity of older drivers across Australia or internationally. In particular, Reluctant Accepters were somewhat underrepresented, which may have limited the depth and breadth of insight into this group’s specific experiences and support needs. Future studies should prioritize recruiting more diverse and representative samples to better capture the full range of perspectives within Alder and Rottunda’s groupings, especially those of Reluctant Accepters.

Additionally, while participants occasionally referenced rural or suburban environments during group discussions, geographic context (i.e., urban, suburban, rural or remote) was not systematically collected during recruitment. We did not ask for suburb or postcode data, and as such, we could not confidently categorize participants into residential environments/contexts. Future research should include more detailed geographic information to enhance interpretation of location-related factors in planning for driving retirement.

Lastly, our original intention was to recruit already retired drivers in addition to older drivers who are thinking about driving retirement and conduct a comparative analysis. No retired drivers responded to the study advertisement. Future research should aim to recruit older adults pre- and post- driving retirement to allow for comparative analysis of planning processes, feelings and support needs, and identify potential nuances on how support systems/resources may be tailored for those pre/post driving retirement.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that Adler and Rottunda’s (2006) groupings may be better conceptualized as fluid phases, rather than fixed categories. While participants were initially grouped based on characteristics and discussions observed in the focus groups, their narratives often reflected evolving emotional and cognitive responses to driving retirement. For instance, those initially classified as Resisters demonstrated thoughts indicative of early-stage resistance, while others showed signs of progression toward reluctant acceptance or proactive planning. This temporal variability supports the idea that older adults may move between phases depending on their health, social supports, or emotional readiness. Understanding the groupings in this way offers a more dynamic framework, which can inform how clinicians tailor support and intervention development that aligns with individuals’ changing needs over time.
