Building a precision therapeutics program at a tertiary care children's hospital
Authors: Luke Hamilton, Heidi Trinkman, Diana Carrasco, Kaitlin Smith, Kelly Vallance, Alejandra Dominguez, Danielle Miller, Megan Gibbs, Anish Ray
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION AND THE GENOTYPE‐FIRST ERA

Pediatric medicine is at a crossroads, mirroring the accelerating evolution of molecular science, informatics, and personalized healthcare strategies worldwide. Traditional, population‐averaged approaches to pediatric diagnosis and treatment have given way to precision models, placing each patient's unique genetic, molecular, and environmental context at the heart of medical management. Guided by these high‐resolution insights, clinicians are increasingly able to select therapies that maximize benefit, predict and avoid toxicity, and streamline the patient's care journey from diagnosis to survivorship. Nowhere is this paradigm shift more evident than in tertiary pediatric hospitals, where rare and refractory diseases demand solutions that extend beyond a “one size fits all” model. Our hospital recognized that building a robust precision therapeutics program meant moving beyond siloed pilot projects into a comprehensive, cross‐disciplinary system. We aimed to establish an operational hub capable of translating high‐complexity diagnostics into actionable, accessible, and equitable approaches.

In just over a decade, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized clinical pediatric genetics, transforming both the speed and precision with which clinicians identify molecular causes of disease. What was once limited to research settings has now become a standard diagnostic approach capable of delivering actionable results within clinically relevant timeframes. In fact, one early accomplishment in our program was a contractual agreement with a commercial lab conducting NGS on tumor tissue to primarily bill insurance and not to charge the patient's family for the uninsured and those declined by insurance. Long‐read sequencing technologies, optical genome mapping, and computational algorithms capable of detecting structural variants at base‐pair resolution are expanding the limits of discovery, particularly in malignancies such as pediatric solid tumors, where complex genomic rearrangements often drive disease biology. These innovations, coupled with rapidly falling sequencing costs and improved analytical pipelines, have inaugurated a true genotype‐first era in pediatric care—one in which the underlying molecular architecture of disease can be discerned before symptoms emerge, transforming diagnosis, prognostication, and therapeutic design.

Importantly, this transformation has redefined not only what clinicians can know but how medicine is practiced. Precision medicine is inherently collaborative, requiring the seamless integration of clinical genetics, pharmacogenomics, molecular pathology, oncology, bioinformatics, and laboratory medicine. It thrives at the interface of disciplines, where data from sequencing, imaging, and clinical observation converge to generate patient‐specific insights. The field has evolved from describing genotype–phenotype correlations toward constructing dynamic models that integrate genomic data with environmental, social, and developmental factors. This multidimensional perspective enables precision medicine to move from discovery to application, converting complex datasets into individualized therapeutic strategies that yield tangible clinical benefits. The ability to share and interpret genomic information across institutions has also accelerated this progress. Sophisticated systems for data sharing, variant curation, and evidence mapping now allow clinicians to contextualize findings in real time, connecting molecular discoveries with both population‐level datasets and emerging therapeutic trials. Through such infrastructure, every sequenced genome contributes not only to an individual's care but to a continuously learning healthcare ecosystem, ensuring that the next child with a similar mutation benefits from accumulated knowledge. Perhaps the most visible expression of precision medicine's promise is the advent of targeted molecular therapy. By directly addressing the genetic, epigenetic, or proteomic drivers of disease, targeted therapies have fundamentally reshaped the treatment landscape for selected pediatric populations. They now underpin clinical advances across a diverse range of disorders, from cystic fibrosis to inherited arrhythmias, metabolic diseases, and pediatric cancers. In oncology, the ability to match treatment to oncogenic drivers such as BRAF, ALK, or NTRK fusions represents one of the most profound therapeutic revolutions of modern pediatrics, offering options to children with otherwise refractory disease. At the same time, these therapies underscore the necessity of integrating precision insights into standardized care frameworks to ensure equitable access and rational utilization. Here, we provide an overview of our model's origins, evolution, and impacts—focusing on (1) oncology‐targeted therapeutics, (2) pharmacogenomics, (3) clinical genetics and genetic oncology, (4) research, and (5) informatics. We also discuss the lessons learned and the essential ingredients for creating a scalable precision medicine ecosystem in pediatrics.

PROGRAM DESIGN AND MULTIDISCIPLINARY FRAMEWORK

At our institution, we recognized early that successful deployment of Precision therapeutics would require moving beyond isolated projects into a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach; with this in mind, we established an operational hub to enable translation of highly complex diagnostics into actionable clinical solutions. Leaders for our program include a core team of physicians, pharmacists, clinical geneticists, molecular pathologists, and informatics specialists who focus on breaking down operational silos between different specialties.

PRECISION IN ONCOLOGY: MOLECULAR TUMOR BOARD

Over the past several decades, survival outcomes for children diagnosed with cancer have improved dramatically, representing one of the most significant achievements in modern medicine. Despite these advances, the prognosis remains dismal for children with certain high‐risk solid tumors—particularly those presenting with metastatic disease or suffering relapse after initial therapy. At our tertiary care children's hospital, this disparity inspired a focused initiative beginning in 2019 aimed at expanding tumor genomic sequencing and establishing a multidisciplinary molecular tumor board (MTB). The MTB integrates expertise from oncology, pathology, genetics, and pharmacology to interpret molecular data and translate findings into individualized treatment recommendations. Our clinical pharmacists help facilitate access to non‐commercial or non‐covered drugs. This occurs either through enrolling patients in clinical trials or pursuing the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) expanded access. Pharmacists coordinate with drug companies for permission, then navigate FDA review (risk–benefit assessment) and institutional review board approval. Once approved, they help secure and administer the drug—often at no cost—enabling treatment options that would otherwise be unavailable. 1

PRECISION IMPACT—CASE EXAMPLES

Genomic profiling led to actionable mutations in over 90% of reviewed hematologic malignancy cases, expanding access to novel therapies for leukemia and lymphoma patients. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, including those used for metastatic melanoma and Hodgkin lymphoma, have resulted in either tumor regression or prolonged disease stabilization. 2 All recommendations are tailored, with consideration given to germline and somatic data, pharmacogenetics, and the evolving landscape of pediatric clinical trials.

For a multitude of reasons, such as lack of targeted medication applicable for tumor type and or intended population, the adoption of recommended therapies in solid tumors has been slow, although not different from what others have reported. In one particular disease, namely Langerhans cell histiocytosis, the identification of BRAF or mitogen‐activated protein kinase activation led to the use of drugs originally approved for melanoma with tremendous success. We have since launched an institutional trial for newly diagnosed and relapsed patients with Langerhans cell histiocytosis to be treated with trametinib, approved by the FDA for melanoma. We have reported overwhelming success with this endeavor 3 and intend to treat patients for a minimum of 2 years, but the optimal duration of targeted therapy remains unknown.

THE RIGHT DOSE: PHARMACOGENOMICS IN ACTION

Initially focused on oncology, where chemotherapy‐related adverse effects are often life‐threatening, the service quickly expanded to immunology, nephrology, neurology, and pain management. To make this data useful for the busy clinicians, the team integrated it directly into the electronic health record. Instead of issuing a complex multipiece report, doctors received discrete, actionable alerts when writing their prescription. For example, if the child's genetics indicate “poor metabolizer” for a specific drug, the system triggers a pop‐up notification to adjust the dose or choose a different medication. This integration led to over 125 consultations in the first year alone, significantly improving patients’ safety. All consults were able to be seen, and results were assessed through this process. A minimum of one and a maximum of 13 actionable findings were found per patient.

GENETIC ONCOLOGY: A PROACTIVE APPROACH FOR FAMILIES

Genetic Oncology (GO) services extend precision medicine's promise by identifying and surveilling children (and families) at risk for hereditary cancer. Genetic syndromes predispose children to cancer at rates higher than previously appreciated. These syndromes frequently present first with non‐malignant features—including overgrowth, developmental delay, or multiple congenital anomalies—before malignancy emerges. Such pediatric‐onset syndromes include Perlman syndrome, Rasopathies (e.g., neurofibromatosis type I), and mTORopathies (e.g., tuberous sclerosis and PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome). As NGS becomes standard in genetics clinics, there is a concurrent rise in incidental and secondary findings relevant to cancer risk. 4 In a cohort of over 16 700 children, 1464 received an American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics secondary finding, and nearly 12% carried a pathogenic variant in a cancer predisposition gene. 5 Across studies, up to 10% of childhood cancers have a hereditary basis. 6 The GO clinic employs comprehensive protocols for diagnosis, risk stratification, and genetic counseling. Since the GO clinic initiation, 54 pediatric patients have been diagnosed with cancer predisposition syndromes. Cascade testing in families has identified 13 affected siblings, 11 parents, and 4 children, allowing for targeted intervention and multidisciplinary support.

A PARADIGM SHIFT IN PEDIATRIC CARE

The adoption of precision medicine in pediatrics, as illustrated by CCHCS's journey, is representative of a sector‐wide embrace of individualized patient care. The challenge lies not only in deploying high‐complexity diagnostics but in weaving them seamlessly into clinical workflows, patient journeys, and educational pipelines. This process requires a systemic approach to harness multi‐omic data, advance interdepartmental collaboration, transform longstanding treatment paradigms, and empower all stakeholders. The shift from population‐averaged treatment plans to highly personalized therapies addresses both the heterogeneous landscape of pediatric disease and the lifelong consequences of early therapeutic decisions. However, the real test is integration—bridging gaps between molecular diagnostics, informatics, clinical expertise, and health system infrastructure. At Cook Children's, operationalizing this vision meant eliminating silos and building workflows where clinical genetics, pharmacogenomics, oncology, and laboratory medicine converge in patient‐centric pathways.

CONCLUSION: DEFINING SUCCESS AND NEXT STEPS

The experience at CCHCS offers a blueprint for other pediatric institutions aiming to build precision medicine at scale. Success stems from a cross‐disciplinary foundation, collaborative leadership, robust data infrastructure, and patient‐centric engagement. One major hurdle identified frequently at MTB meetings surrounds the lack of novel drugs tied to driver mutations, yet lacking sufficient pediatric data for regulatory approval to support unrestricted use. Insurance approval remains a major hurdle in this regard as well. An additional challenge involves a clinical situation where an actionable target and its relevant target have been identified, yet a curative measure requires definitive therapy. One example is acute myeloid leukemia with FMS‐like tyrosine kinase 3 internal tandem duplication mutation, which is targetable by several approved drugs; however, patients often require bone marrow transplant based on overall risk profile. Other remaining challenges—access, interpretation, cost, and equity—demand ongoing innovation and advocacy. Achieving a truly scalable ecosystem will require sustained attention to these domains and continual integration of research, education, and operational best practices.
