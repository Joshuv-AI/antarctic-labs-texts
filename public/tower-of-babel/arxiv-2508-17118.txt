Abstract
. In this paper we investigate the functional equation φ ⁡ ( x + y 2 ) ​ ( ψ 1 ​ ( x ) − ψ 2 ​ ( y ) ) = 0 ( for all ​ x ∈ I 1 ​ and ​ y ∈ I 2 ) \varphi\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\left(\psi_{1}(x)-\psi_{2}(y)\right)=0\hskip 56.9055pt\left(\mbox{ for all }x\in I_{1}\mbox{ and }y\in I_{2}\right) where I 1 , I 2 I_{1}\,,I_{2} are open intervals of ℝ \mathbb{R} , J = 1 2 ​ ( I 1 + I 2 ) J=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}\right) moreover ψ 1 : I 1 → ℝ \psi_{1}:I_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} , ψ 2 : I 2 → ℝ \psi_{2}:I_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} and φ : J → ℝ \varphi:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R} are unknown functions. 1.
Introduction

During the whole paper let

I_{1}\,,I_{2}
be
nonempty open intervals of the real line, and let

J:=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}\right)
.
Then
J
is again a nonempty, open interval.
We will consider the functional equation

(1)

\varphi\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\bigl(\psi_{1}(x)-\psi_{2}(y)\bigr)=0\hskip 56.9055pt\left(\mbox{ for all }x\in I_{1}\mbox{ and }y\in I_{2}\right).

If there exist functions

\psi_{1}:I_{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\psi_{2}:I_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\varphi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that the equation (
1
)
is fulfilled for all

x\in I_{1}
and for all
y\in I_{2}\,
, then
we say that the triplet of functions

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution of (
1
).
This functional equation appears during the investigation
of the invariance problem of
generalized weighted quasi-arithmetic means.
Since this class of means was introduced by
J. Matkowski in 2010
[
22
]
, the
generalized weighted quasi-arithmetic means are often referred to
as Matkowski means.
Special cases of the general invariance problem
for Matkowski means have been investigated by many
authors, such as Matkowski
[
21
]
,
Jarczyk
[
14
,
12
]
,
Daróczy, Páles
[
6
,
7
]
,
Maksa
[
5
]
and
Burai
[
3
,
4
]
.
For a summary about invariance problems
of means the interested reader
should study the survey paper of
Jarczyk and Jarczyk
[
13
]
.

The general equivalence problem of Matkowski means
can be formulated as follows.
If
\emptyset\neq J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an open interval,
then we are looking for those Matkowski means

M,N,K:J\times J\longrightarrow J

which fulfill the invariance equation

(2)

M\left(N(u,v),K(u,v)\right)=M(u,v)\hskip 56.9055pt(u,v\in J).

A recent achievement in the investigation of
the general invariance problem of Matkowski means is
the work of T. Kiss
[
15
]
. In that paper
– based on certain ideas of Z. Páles –
a particular functional equation
is established which is equivalent to
(
2
).
This equivalent equation is

(3)

F\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(y)=G\left(g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(y)\right)

where
x,y
are from an open interval.

Our considered equation (
1
) can be
derived from (
3
), such that

\varphi,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}
are
obtained as compositions of functions
which either appear in (
3
)
or they are derivatives of the functions in (
3
).
However, functions themselves in (
3
)
are put together from the generator functions of
the Matkowski means in the invariance problem.
Thus it is clear that the initial regularity assumption for
the generators determines the level of regularity
we can assume when working on our main equation
(
1
).

The solutions of equation (
1
)
were characterized by Kiss
[
16
]
under the
assumption that the set of zeros of

\varphi
is closed.
The primary information about the solutions is that
these must be constant on some open subintervals of
their domain. This implies some consequences
which are crucial when one wishes to determine
the solutions of the equation (
3
)
and thus the invariance equation (
2
).
On the other hand, when

\varphi
is obtained from (
3
)
with the previously mentioned process,
then
\varphi
is a derivative,
so
\varphi^{-1}(0)
is not necessarily closed.
This is the reason why
during the Problems and Remarks session of the
59th International Symposium on Functional Equations
T. Kiss proposed
the following question (see
[
1
]
).
Does the characterization
[
16
, Theorem 6]

of the solutions of (
1
) remain valid when the
Darboux property is assumed for
\varphi
,
instead of the closedness of
\varphi^{-1}(0)
?

This would mean that (
3
) could
be solved imposing only differentiabilty on the
functions in the equation,
without requiring the continuity of the derivatives.
To our best knowledge,
continuous differentiability is
the weakest regularity assumption under which
the solutions of (
3
) are discussed.
However, it seems reasonable to investigate
(
3
) and thus the invariance problem of
Matkowski means under weaker, more natural
regularity assumptions.
The main result of our paper will make it possible
to consider (
3
)
assuming that the functions in the equation are
differentiable (but not necessarily continuously
differentiable).

Let us mention that a similar process of
weakening the regularity assumptions occurred during
the investigation of the Matkowksi–Sutô problem.
In that case, firstly
the analytic solutions have been described by
Sutô
[
27
,
28
]
,
then the assumption was weakened to
twice continuous differentiability
by Matkowski
[
21
]
.
Daróczy and Páles solved the Matkowski–Sutô
equation under continuous differentiability

[
6
]
, and finally they were able to
solve it assuming only that the functions
are continuous, strictly monotone (see
[
7
]
).

In our paper we are going to give a negative answer
to the proposed problem of Kiss.
We give a counterexample, namely a solution of
(
1
) consisting of Darboux functions
which are not constant on any open subinterval.
This information seems disappointing for one who
wishes to solve (
3
) under
simple differentiability condition.
However, in our main theorems,
we are going to show that if we directly assume

\varphi
to be a derivative, then the
solutions are exactly the same as in the setting
discussed by Kiss in
[
16
]
.
As hinted above, this was a desired achievement,
because it means
that the same techniques which were used
in
[
15
]
during the
investigations of equation (
3
)
containing continuously differentiable functions
can be applied in the situation when only
differentiability is assumed.

The mentioned main result of this paper is the
characterization of the solutions of (
1
)
when
\varphi
is assumed to be measurable.
Measurability is a common weak regularity assumption
in the theory of functional equations.
For many notable functional equations
(e.g. Cauchy, Jensen, Pexider type equations) the
measurability of the solution automatically
implies very strong regularity, such as
higher order differentiability, as it is
thoroughly discussed in
[
19
]
.
We will see that for the alternative
equation (
1
) the situation is different,
as the solutions can be rather irregular.
Yet the set of problematic irregularities of
\varphi

is just a zero measure set, which is still tolerable
for the applications.
Numerous authors have addressed the question of
measurability of the solutions of particular
functional equations in different settings,
such as Járai
[
9
,
11
]
,
Losonczi
[
20
]
, Kochanek and Lewicki
[
18
]
,
Ger
[
8
]
.
The interested reader should also get acquainted
with the monograph
[
10
]
of Járai
concerning the regularity of
solutions of functional equations.

2.
A solution consisting of irregular Darboux functions

In this section
we answer the open problem
proposed by Kiss
[
1
]
during the
59
th ISFE.
Namely, we prove the existence of a
nontrivial solution of (
1
) consisting of
such Darboux functions that are not constant on
any nonempty open interval.

Throughout the whole paper we consider
the real line
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the
standard norm topology
induced by the absolute value.
The cardinal number
continuum will be denoted by
\mathfrak{c}
.
We will use the notation

\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}

for the set of extended real numbers.
If
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval,
we say that a function

f:I\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
has the
Darboux property (or, simply, is Darboux) if
the following assertion holds:
for all
x,y\in I
such that

x<y
and
f(x)\neq f(y)
,
and for every

\lambda\in\left]\,\min\{f(x),f(y)\}\,,\,\max\{f(x),f(y)\}\,\right[\,
,
there exists

z\in\left]x,y\right[
for which

f(z)=\lambda
holds.
We note that this is equivalent to
the property that
f(J)
is an
interval whenever
J\subseteq I
is an interval
(see
[
24
, Theorem 9.2]
).

For any function

f:I\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
the support of
f

will be denoted by
\mbox{\rm supp}f
:

\mbox{\rm supp}f:=\{\,x\in I\ :\ f(x)\neq 0\,\}.

Sometimes we call a triplet a functions

(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2})
a
trivial solution of (
1
) if

\varphi(u)=0
for all
u\in J
or

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)
for all

(x,y)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}\,
.

As mentioned earlier, Kiss proposed the question whether
the solutions of (
1
) can be characterized in the
same way as in
[
16
, Theorem 6]
, when we assume that

\varphi
is Darboux (or even all three functions

\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}
are Darboux).
We proceed with an example that gives a negative answer
for this question.

Theorem 1
.

The functional equation (
1
) has a solution

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)
such that

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
are Darboux functions,
yet none of these functions are continuous
at any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hamel basis for
\mathbb{R}
,
that is,
\mathcal{H}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}

if we consider it as a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
.
It is well-known
(see
[
19
, Theorem 4.2.1.]
) that such basis

\mathcal{H}
exists, moreover we may assume that

1\in\mathcal{H}
.
As the cardinality of any Hamel basis
is continuum, we may define a bijection

\beta:\left]0,2\right[\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}

such that
\beta(1)=1
.
We split
\mathcal{H}
into two parts
with the help of this bijection:

\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\beta\left(\,\left]0,1\right]\,\right)\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 28.45274pt\mathcal{H}_{2}:=\beta\left(\,\left]1,2\right[\,\right).

Clearly,
1\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and

\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathfrak{c}
.
Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be the linear hulls of

\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
That is,

G_{i}:=\{\,q_{1}h_{1}+\dots+q_{n}h_{n}\ :\ n\in\mathbb{N}\mbox{ and }q_{1}\,,\dots\,,q_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}\,,h_{1}\,,\dots h_{n}\,,\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\,\}

for
i=1,2
.
Due to the definition,
G_{i}
is in fact a
vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
with the basis
\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
In particular,
\left(G_{i}\,,+\right)
is an
additive subgroup of
\left(\mathbb{R},+\right)

for
i=1,2
.

Claim 1
.

Let us define the relation

\sim_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}

in the following way:
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

x\sim_{1}y\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x-y\in G_{1}\,.

Then
\sim_{1}
is an equivalence relation.
Moreover, if
b,h\in\mathcal{H}_{2}

are arbitrary then

b\sim_{1}h
implies
b=h
.

Proof of Claim 1
.

One can easily check that
\sim_{1}
is indeed
reflexive, symmetric and transitive,
because
\left(G_{1}\,,+\right)
is a group.
Moreover, if
b\sim_{1}h
then
their difference is in
G_{1}\,
,
hence there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

q_{1}\,,\dots\,,q_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
,

h_{1}\,,\dots\,,h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
such that

b-h=q_{1}h_{1}+\dots+q_{n}h_{n}

which means

0=h-b+q_{1}h_{1}+\dots+q_{n}h_{n}\,
.
Now
h,b\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
so if

b\neq h
was true then this would mean that the set

\{h,b,h_{1}\,,\dots\,,h_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{H}

is linearly dependent which is impossible, since

\mathcal{H}
is a basis.

\boxtimes

Since an equivalence relation always induces a partition on the
original set, we will consider the equivalence classes
of
\sim_{1}\,
.
For any number
x\in\mathbb{R}
its equivalence
class will be denoted by
[x]_{1}\,
.
We may note that
[x]_{1}\,
is
nothing else but the coset

x+G_{1}
of the subgroup

\left(G_{1}\,,+\right)

in the Abelian group
\left(\mathbb{R}\,,+\right)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of all equivalence classes:

\mathcal{A}:=\{\,[x]_{1}\,:\,x\in\mathbb{R}\,\}.

As assured in Claim
1
,
all the elements of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are
in different equivalence classes, so

\mathfrak{c}=\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{H}_{2}\leq\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{A}\leq\mbox{\rm card}\mathbb{R}=\mathfrak{c}
, hence

\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{A}=\mathfrak{c}
. So there exists a bijection

\gamma:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Claim 2
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

f(x):=\gamma\left(\,[x]_{1}\,\right)\hskip 56.9055pt(x\in\mathbb{R})

maps every nonempty open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}

onto
\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
f
has the
Darboux property, but it
f
is not continuous
at any point.

Proof of Claim 2
.

First of all, every class in
\mathcal{A}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
.
This follows from the fact that

1\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
implies
\mathbb{Q}\subset G_{1}\,
,
thus, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the inclusion

[x]_{1}=x+G_{1}\supset x+\mathbb{Q}
holds while

\mathbb{Q}
is dense.

Now let
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be
any open interval and let

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.
As
\gamma:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bijection,
there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\gamma\left([w]_{1}\right)=\lambda
.
As we have established earlier in the proof,

[w]_{1}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
,
so there exists
z\in[w]_{1}

such that
z\in I
.
But then

f(z)=\gamma\left([z]_{1}\right)=\gamma\left([w]_{1}\right)=\lambda.

Since
\lambda
was arbitrary,
we have obtained
f(I)=\mathbb{R}
.
The fact that
f
is nowhere continuous but the
has the Darboux property is an immediate consequence.

\boxtimes

We may note that the function

x\mapsto f(-x)
is again a nowhere continuous
Darboux function.

Claim 3
.

There exists a nowhere continuous Darboux function

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi=G_{1}\,
.

Proof of Claim 3
.

Let us consider a relation

\sim_{2}
on
G_{1}
defined by

x\sim_{2}y\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x-y\in\mathbb{Q}\hskip 42.67912pt\left(x,y\in G_{1}\right).

It is trivial that

\sim_{2}
is an equivalence relation, since

\left(\mathbb{Q},+\right)
is a subgroup of

\left(G_{1}\,,+\right)
. Consequently, the
equivalence classes yield a partition for
G_{1}\,
.
We may denote
the equivalence class of an element

x\in G_{1}
by
[x]_{2}\,
,
and this class is simply the coset
x+\mathbb{Q}

in the Abelian group
\left(G_{1}\,,+\right)
.
Therefore every equivalence class is dense in
\mathbb{R}
.

We may also claim that no equivalence class can contain
two different elements from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Indeed,

h,b\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,
,
h\neq b
and
h\sim_{2}b

would mean that there exists

q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

h-b=q
, so
0=b-h+q
.
In other words,

\{1,b,h\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset\mathcal{H}
is a
linearly dependent system,
which contradicts that
\mathcal{H}
is a Hamel basis.

\mathcal{B}
will denote the set of
equivalence classes, i.e.

\mathcal{B}:=\{\,[x]_{2}\,:\,x\in G_{1}\,\}
.
Since all elements from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
are
in different classes, we have

\mathfrak{c}=\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{H}_{1}\leq\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{B}\leq\mathfrak{c}

which ensures
\mbox{\rm card}\mathcal{B}=\mathfrak{c}
.
Hence there exists a bijection

\delta:\mathcal{B}\longrightarrow\left]0,1\right[\,
.

Let us define an auxiliary function

\psi:G_{1}\longrightarrow\left]0,1\right[

with the formula

\psi(x):=\delta\left(\,[x]_{2}\,\right)\hskip 42.67912pt\left(x\in G_{1}\right).

Given this
\psi
we may construct

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\varphi(x):=\begin{cases}\psi(x)\qquad&\mbox{ if }x\in G_{1}\\
0\qquad&\mbox{ if }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus G_{1}\,.\end{cases}

Clearly,
\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi=G_{1}\,
,
as the range of
\delta
is
\left]0,1\right[\,
,
so
\varphi(x)=\psi(x)\neq 0

for all
x\in G_{1}\,
.
Now let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be any nonempty open interval
and
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[\,
be also
arbitrary.
Now there exists
w\in G_{1}\,
such that

\delta\left([w]_{2}\right)=\lambda
.
But
[w]_{2}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
, so there exists

z\in I\cap G_{1}
such that

z\in[w]_{2}
which means

\varphi(z)=\psi(z)=\delta\left([z]_{2}\right)=\delta\left([w]_{2}\right)=\lambda.

This shows that

\left]0,1\right[\subseteq\varphi(I)
.
Together with the fact that
the range of
\varphi
is
\left[0,1\right[\,
,
this implies the Darboux property of
\varphi
.
Moreover,
\varphi
cannot be continuous at any point

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
because in that case it would map an open neighborhood
of
x_{0}
into an interval with length less than
1
.

\boxtimes

Now let us define

\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

as

\psi_{1}(x):=f(x)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\psi_{2}(x):=f(-x)\hskip 28.45274pt(x\in\mathbb{R})

where
f
is the function
appearing in Claim
1
.
We proceed to show that

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution of (
1
).
We distinguish two cases.
Firstly, suppose that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

are such that

\frac{x+y}{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus G_{1}\,
.
Then the equation (
1
)
is obviously fulfilled, since

\varphi\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=0
.
The second possibility is when

\frac{x+y}{2}\in G_{1}\,
. That is,

x+y=2g
for some
g\in G_{1}

which is equivalent to

x-(-y)=2g\in G_{1}\,
.
This means
x\sim_{1}-y
whence

[x]_{1}=[-y]_{1}
. Consequently,

\psi_{1}(x)=f(x)=\gamma\left([x]_{1}\right)=\gamma\left([-y]_{1}\right)=f(-y)=\psi_{2}(y)\,,

which implies

\psi_{1}(x)-\psi_{2}(y)=0
, so (
1
)
is again fulfilled.
Summarizing the two cases, we may conclude that,
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}

and for every
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
the functional equation (
1
) holds.
Finally, let us recall that none of the three functions
are continuous at any point,
as we have established in
Claims
2
and
3
.
∎

3.
Solutions under measurability condition

As we will see in the sequel, the irregular behavior
of the previous solution was due to the fact that

\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
was either a set
with Lebesgue measure
0
,
or it is possible that
\varphi
was not even measurable
(depending on the measurability properties of
the Hamel basis
\mathcal{H}
).

In this section we characterize the solutions of
(
1
) under the assumption that

\varphi
is Lebesgue measurable.
When we claim that a triplet

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution, we mean it in such a way that
there are appropriate nonempty open intervals

I_{1}\,,I_{2}
and
J=\frac{1}{2}(I_{1}+I_{2})

so that

\varphi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\psi_{1}:I_{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\psi_{2}:I_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. In most cases
we do not emphasize these domains explicitly.
From now on, by measurability we
mean Lebesgue measurability,
and the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}

will be denoted by
\mu
.

During our investigations
we will often consider the reflection of a subset

S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with respect to
another subset
\emptyset\neq M\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
For this purpose let us introduce a notation:

\left(S\,|\,M\right):=\{2m-x\,|\,m\in M\mbox{ and }x\in S\}.

Using the pointwise multiplication and the
Minkowski sum of sets we have
\left(S\,|\,M\right)=2M-S
.
In the particular case when

M=\{m\}
is a singleton
we will write

\left(S\,|\,m\right)
instead of
\left(S\,|\,\{m\}\right)
.
Similarly, for

s,m\in\mathbb{R}
we will use the notation

\left(s\,|\,m\right)=2m-s
.

We mention that a slightly more general version of
this reflection operator is introduced in

[
16
]
as well,
where plenty of its properties are discussed.

At this point we formulate an elementary statement
concerning sets of
\mathbb{R}
with positive Lebesgue measure,
which will be used frequently later on.

Proposition 1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a Lebesgue-measurable set
with
\mu(A)>0
.
Then there exists
s\in A
such that
for all
\delta>0
it holds that

\mu\left(\,A\cap\left[s,s+\delta\right]\,\right)>0.

Proof.

In fact much more is true, since due to the
Lebesgue Density Theorem
(see
[
24
, Theorem 21.29]
), the Lebesgue density is

1
almost everywhere on
A
. That is,

\lim_{\delta\to 0+}\frac{\mu\left(\,A\cap\left[s-\delta,s+\delta\right]\,\right)}{2\delta}=1

holds for almost every
s\in A
.
For any such number
s
the inequality

\mu\left(\,A\cap\left[s,s+\delta\right]\,\right)>0
must
be fulfilled for all
\delta>0
,
otherwise the Lebesgue density in that point could
be at most
\frac{1}{2}\,
.
∎

We will use this statement in the particular case when

A=\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
.

Corollary 1
.

Let

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

be a solution of (
1
) such that
the function
\varphi
is measurable.
Then

\mu\left(\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)>0

implies that there exists

s\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
such that

\mu\left(\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap\left[s,s+\delta\right]\,\right)>0\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ for all }\delta>0.

The next lemma will play a crucial role later on.
It turns out that the measurability provides us a tool
which helps us to replace the condition for
the set of zeros of
\varphi
utilized in
[
16
]
.
This tool is a classical
theorem of Steinhaus
[
26
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

be a solution of (
1
).
Suppose that
\varphi
is measurable,
and
s\in J
is such a point that

\mu\left(\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap\left[s,s+\delta\right]\,\right)>0

for every
\delta>0
.
Then, if
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq I_{1}

is an interval fulfilling

\left(K\,|\,s\right)\subseteq I_{2}\,
, then
there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for which

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda\qquad\mbox{ holds for all }x\in K\mbox{ and }y\in\left(K\,|\,s\right).

Proof.

Let

x,y\in K
be arbitrary numbers,
fulfilling
x<y
.
Now

\left(\,[x,y]\,\,|\,s\right)=\left[2s-y,2s-x\right]\subset\left(K\,|\,s\right)\subseteq I_{2}\,,

using the definition of the reflection operator,
and the assumption for
K
.
Considering that
I_{2}
is open,
there exists
d>0
such that

2s-x+2d\in I_{2}\,
, so

\left(\,[x,y]\,\,|\,\,[s,s+d]\,\right)=\left[2s-y,2s+2d-x\right]\subseteq I_{2}

holds.
On the other hand, we have supposed

\mu\left(\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap\left[s,s+d\right]\,\right)>0
.
Therefore, applying the
theorem of Steinhaus, the difference set

\Delta:=\{\,t_{1}-t_{2}\ :\ t_{1}\,,t_{2}\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap[s,s+d]\,\}

contains an open neighborhood of
0
,
i.e. there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that

\left]-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\right[\subseteq\Delta
.
Now let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

h:=\frac{y-x}{2m}\,\in\,\left]\,0,\varepsilon\,\right[\,\mbox{ is fulfilled}.

Then there exist

s_{1}\,,s_{2}\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap[s,s+d]

such that

s_{2}=s_{1}+h
.
Let us introduce new notations
for some particular points:

x_{j}:=x+j\cdot 2h\,,\qquad\widehat{x}_{j}:=\left(x_{j}\,|\,s_{1}\right)\ \mbox{ and }\ \widehat{\widehat{x}}_{j}:=\left(\widehat{x}_{j}\,|\,s_{2}\right)\qquad(j=0,1,\dots,m).

Obviously,

x_{0}=x
,
x_{m}=y
and
x_{j}\in[x,y]
.
Therefore

\widehat{x}_{j}\in\left(\,[x,y]\,\,|\,\,[s,s+d]\,\right)\subseteq I_{2}

for
j=0,1,\dots,m
while

\widehat{\widehat{x}}_{j}=2s_{2}-\widehat{x}_{j}=2s_{2}-2s_{1}+x_{j}=x_{j}+2h=x_{j+1}\in I_{1}\qquad\mbox{for }j=0,1,\dots,m-1.

At this point let us utilize the fact that

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution of (
1
).
In particular,

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\frac{x_{j}+\widehat{x}_{j}}{2}\right)\bigl(\psi_{1}(x_{j})-\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle=0\qquad(j=0,1,\dots m)\ \mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\frac{\widehat{\widehat{x}}_{j}+\widehat{x}_{j}}{2}\right)\bigl(\psi_{1}(\widehat{\widehat{x}}_{j})-\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle=0\qquad(j=0,1,\dots m-1),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\varphi\left(s_{1}\right)\bigl(\psi_{1}(x_{j})-\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle=0\qquad(j=0,1,\dots m)\ \mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\varphi\left(s_{2}\right)\bigl(\psi_{1}(x_{j+1})-\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle=0\qquad(j=0,1,\dots m-1).

As
s_{1}\,,s_{2}\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
,
these equations imply

\psi_{1}(x_{j})=\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{j})=\psi_{1}(x_{j+1})\qquad\mbox{ for every }j=0,1,\dots,m-1.

Therefore

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{1}(x_{0})=\psi_{1}(x_{1})=\dots=\psi_{1}(x_{m})=\psi_{1}(y)

holds as well.
Since
x,y\in K
were arbitrary, it follows
that there exists

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\psi_{1}(x)=\lambda
for all
x\in K
.

Finally, we have to verify that

\psi_{2}
is also constant on
\left(K\,|\,s\right)

with value
\lambda
.
For this purpose let observe that

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution of (
1
) if,
and only if,

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{2}\,,\psi_{1}\right)

is a solution.
Thus let us apply the same proof we have just carried out,
but now for the solution

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{2}\,,\psi_{1}\right)
,
and with the starting interval

\left(K\,|\,s\right)\subseteq I_{2}\,
.
Since

\left(\left(K\,|\,s\right)\,|\,s\right)=K\subseteq I_{1}\,
,
the assumptions of the lemma are fulfilled
for the solution

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{2}\,,\psi_{1}\right)
.
Hence, according to the first part of our proof,
there exists
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\psi_{2}(y)=\nu
for all

y\in\left(K\,|\,s\right)
.
But, for the
points

x_{0}=x\in K
and

\widehat{x}_{0}\in\left(K\,|\,s\right)

introduced before, we had

\lambda=\psi_{1}(x_{0})=\psi_{2}(\widehat{x}_{0})=\nu\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

The next proposition claims that nontrivial solutions
are locally constant on some ends of their domains.

Proposition 2
.

Let

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

be a solution of (
1
) such that

\varphi
is measurable.
Suppose that there exist

\gamma_{1}\in I_{1}\,
,

\gamma_{2}\in I_{2}
such that for

s=\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}\in J

it holds that

\mu\left(\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap\left[s,s+\delta\right]\right)>0\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ for all }\delta>0.

Then exactly one of the following three cases holds.

Case 1.

There exist
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
nonempty open intervals

P_{i}\subsetneqq I_{i}
such that

\gamma_{i}\in P_{i}
and
\inf P_{i}=\inf I_{i}

(
i=1,2
)
moreover

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda\qquad\mbox{ holds for all }x\in P_{1}\mbox{ and }y\in P_{2}\,.

Case 2.

There exist
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
nonempty open intervals

Q_{i}\subsetneqq I_{i}

such that

\gamma_{i}\in Q_{i}
and
\sup Q_{i}=\sup I_{i}

(
i=1,2
) moreover

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda\qquad\mbox{ holds for all }x\in Q_{1}\mbox{ and }y\in Q_{2}\,.

Case 3.

\psi_{1}
is constant on
I_{1}
or

\psi_{2}
is constant on
I_{2}
.

Proof.

Let us define the sets

C_{1}:=\{\,x\in I_{1}\,:\,\left(x\,|\,s\right)\in I_{2}\,\}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 14.22636ptC_{2}:=\{\,y\in I_{2}\,:\,\left(y\,|\,s\right)\in I_{1}\,\}.

Since
I_{1}\,
,
I_{2}
are open intervals,
it is easy to see that

C_{1}\,
,
C_{2}
are also open intervals and,
clearly,

\gamma_{1}\in C_{1}\,
,
\gamma_{2}\in C_{2}\,
.
We denote the endpoints of the intervals as

\alpha_{i}=\inf C_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\ \beta_{i}=\sup C_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}\qquad(i=1,2).

From the definition of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
it is
straightforward that
\left(C_{1}\,|\,s\right)=C_{2}
and

\left(C_{2}\,|\,s\right)=C_{1}\,
.
In particular,

\left(\alpha_{1}\,|\,s\right)=\beta_{2}
and

\left(\beta_{1}\,|\,s\right)=\alpha_{2}\,
, using the
convention that

\left(+\infty\,|\,s\right)=-\infty
and

\left(-\infty\,|\,s\right)=+\infty
.

We point out that that

\inf I_{1}<\alpha_{1}
and
\sup I_{2}>\beta_{2}

cannot occur simultaneously.
Assume, on the contrary, that this is not the case.
Firstly, this would imply

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now, since
I_{1}\,
,
I_{2}
are open and

\left(\alpha_{1}\,|\,s\right)=\beta_{2}\,
,
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

x_{1}:=\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon\in I_{1}\ \mbox{ and }\ \left(x_{1}\,|\,s\right)=2s-\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon=\beta_{2}+\varepsilon\in I_{2}\,,

so

x_{1}\in C_{1}
while

x_{1}<\alpha_{1}=\inf C_{1}

which is a contradiction.
For similar reasons,

\inf I_{2}<\alpha_{2}
and
\sup I_{1}>\beta_{1}

cannot occur simultaneously, either.

Finally, recalling

\left(C_{1}\,|\,s\right)=C_{2}
and using
Lemma
1
,
there exists a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

such that

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda\qquad\mbox{ holds for all }x\in C_{1}\mbox{ and }y\in C_{2}\,.

Now there are three possibilities.

(i)

C_{i}=I_{i}
for at least one

i\in\{1,2\}
,
which yields Case 3. of the statement.

(ii)

\alpha_{1}=\inf I_{1}
but

\beta_{1}<\sup I_{1}\,
.
We have seen that the latter inequality implies

\alpha_{2}=\inf I_{2}\,
.
Now
\beta_{2}=\sup I_{2}
would
result in the previous case with

C_{2}=I_{2}\,
, so we consider

\beta_{2}<\sup I_{2}\,
.
Using Lemma
1

we get Case 1. of our statement,
since
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}

are constant functions with the same value

\lambda
on

P_{1}:=C_{1}=\left]\,\alpha_{1}\,,\beta_{1}\,\right[\subsetneqq I_{1}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 8.53581ptP_{2}:=C_{2}=\left]\,\alpha_{2}\,,\beta_{2}\,\right[\subsetneqq I_{2}\,,\mbox{ respectively.}

(iii)

\inf I_{1}<\alpha_{1}\,
, which implies

\sup I_{2}=\beta_{2}\,
.
Again, to avoid
C_{2}=I_{2}\,
,
we restrict ourselves to

\inf I_{2}<\alpha_{2}
and thus

\sup I_{1}=\beta_{1}\,
.
This way we get Case 2. of the proposition,
because Lemma
1

ensures that

\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}

are constant functions with the same value

\lambda
on the intervals

Q_{1}:=C_{1}=\left]\,\alpha_{1}\,,\beta_{1}\,\right[\subsetneqq I_{1}\hskip 8.53581pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 8.53581ptQ_{2}:=C_{2}=\left]\,\alpha_{2}\,,\beta_{2}\,\right[\subsetneqq I_{2}\,,\mbox{ respectively.}

∎

Now we have all the auxiliary tools to describe the
structure of the possible solutions of (
1
)
under the assumption that
\varphi
is measurable.
In the next theorems some open intervals of the form

\left]\,\alpha,\beta\,\right[\subseteq\mathbb{R}

will appear where

\alpha,\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
\alpha\leq\beta
.
We will use the convention that if

\alpha=\beta
then automatically

\left]\,\alpha,\beta\,\right[=\emptyset\,

even if
\alpha,\beta
are extended real numbers.

Theorem 2
.

Let

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

be a solution of (
1
)
and suppose that

\varphi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is measurable.
Then exactly one of the following statements is true.

(1)

\mu\left(\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=0

or there exists

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda

for all

x\in I_{1}
and
y\in I_{2}\,
.

(2)

There exist constants

\lambda,\nu\in\mathbb{R}

and, for
i=1,2
, there exist

a_{i}\,,b_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}

such that

\inf I_{i}\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}\leq\sup I_{i}

and for the open intervals

U_{i}:=\left]\,\inf I_{i}\,,a_{i}\,\right[\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad V_{i}:=\left]\,b_{i}\,,\sup I_{i}\,\right[

the following assertions are fulfilled:

•

U_{i}\neq I_{i}
and
V_{i}\neq I_{i}
for
i=1,2
;

•

U_{1}\,,U_{2}
are both nonempty or

V_{1}\,,V_{2}
are both nonempty;

•

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda
for all

(x,y)\in U_{1}\times U_{2}
and

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\nu
for all

(x,y)\in V_{1}\times V_{2}
;

•

\mu\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cup\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=0

where

K_{i}:=I_{i}\setminus(U_{i}\cup V_{i})

is a nonempty subinterval
which is closed in
I_{i}
(
i=1,2
).

(3)

There exist a constant

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and an index

j\in\{1,2\}
such that

\psi_{j}(z)=\lambda
for all
z\in I_{j}\,
,
while there exists a finite or countable collection of
disjoint, nonempty open intervals

U_{n}\subset I_{i}

(
i\neq j
and
n=1,\dots,N

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
or
N=\infty
)
with the following properties:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\neq I_{i}\ \mbox{ and }\ \psi_{i}(z)=\lambda\ \mbox{ for all }\ z\in\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\,,\mbox{ moreover }

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=0\ \mbox{ for all }\ t\in\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\right)+I_{j}\right).

Proof.

It is easy to see that the first case can indeed occur,
for instance when the solution is trivial.
However, from now on, assume that
(1)

does not hold.
In particular,

\mu(\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi)>0
,
so we may use Corollary
1

to see that the assumptions of
Proposition
2
are fulfilled.

We start our investigations in the situation when
Case 1. or Case 2. holds in
Proposition
2
.
That is, neither
\psi_{1}
nor
\psi_{2}
is constant.
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
let us define the sets

\displaystyle A_{i}\left(\lambda\right)

\displaystyle:=\{\,t\in I_{i}\ :\ \psi_{i}(z)=\lambda\mbox{ for all }z\in\,\left]\,\inf I_{i}\,,t\,\right[\,\}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle B_{i}\left(\lambda\right)

\displaystyle:=\{\,t\in I_{i}\ :\ \psi_{i}(z)=\lambda\mbox{ for all }z\in\,\left]\,t,\sup I_{i}\,\right[\,\}\hskip 28.45274pt(i=1,2).

Obviously, such a set can be empty
but when it is not empty
then it is an interval.
Furthermore, if

A_{i}(\lambda)\neq\emptyset

(or, respectively,
B_{i}(\lambda)\neq\emptyset
)
for some

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
then

A_{i}(\kappa)=\emptyset

(respectively,
B_{i}(\kappa)=\emptyset
),
for every
\kappa\neq\lambda
.
Since
\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}
are not constant functions,

A_{i}(\lambda)\neq I_{i}
and

B_{i}(\lambda)\neq I_{i}
for
i=1,2
and
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now we will determine the sets

U_{i}\,,V_{i}
appearing in
case
(2)
of our theorem.
Let

U_{i}:=\begin{cases}\left]\,\inf I_{i}\,,\sup A_{i}(\lambda)\,\right[\ &\mbox{ if there exists }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ such that }A_{i}(\lambda)\neq\emptyset\\
\emptyset\ &\mbox{ if }A_{i}(\lambda)=\emptyset\ \mbox{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\end{cases}\hskip 28.45274pt(i=1,2).

Similarly, let

V_{i}:=\begin{cases}\left]\,\inf B_{i}(\nu)\,,\sup I_{i}\,\right[\ &\mbox{ if there exists }\nu\in\mathbb{R}\ \mbox{ such that }B_{i}(\nu)\neq\emptyset\\
\emptyset\ &\mbox{ if }B_{i}(\nu)=\emptyset\ \mbox{ for all }\nu\in\mathbb{R}\end{cases}\hskip 28.45274pt(i=1,2).

According to the previous observation
on the uniqueness of the constants

\lambda,\nu
, these sets are well-defined.
Due to Proposition
2
,

U_{1}\neq\emptyset
and
U_{2}\neq\emptyset
, or

V_{1}\neq\emptyset
and
V_{2}\neq\emptyset

surely holds (possibly at the same time).
The definition of
U_{i}\,,V_{i}
guarantees that

\psi_{i}
is constant
\lambda
on
U_{i}
and

\psi_{i}
is constant
\nu
on
V_{i}
for

i=1,2
.

To conclude case
(2)

we have to show that

\mu\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cup\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=0

Assume that the contrary is true, thus either

\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)>0\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ or }\hskip 14.22636pt\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)>0

holds. We only discuss the situation when

\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)>0
.
From the other one we could derive a contradiction analogously.
In this case, using
Proposition
1
, there exists

s_{0}\in\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi

such that

\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\cap\left[\,s_{0}\,,s_{0}+\delta\,\right]\right)>0\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ for all }\delta>0.

That is, there exist

c_{1}\in K_{1}
and
c_{2}\in I_{2}
such that

s_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{1}+c_{2}\right)
.
Hence the assumptions of Proposition
2

are fulfilled for the solution, but
now with the points

c_{1}\in K_{1}\,
,
c_{2}\in I_{2}
and
s_{0}\in J
.
As we have assumed that neither of

\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
is constant,
necessarily Case 1. or Case 2. is relevant
in Proposition
2
.
Either way,
\psi_{1}
is constant on
an interval that contains
c_{1}
and
extends until one of the endpoints of
I_{1}\,
.
Consequently,

c_{1}\in U_{1}
or
c_{1}\in V_{1}
is fulfilled
which contradicts the fact that

c_{1}\in K_{1}=I_{1}\setminus\left(U_{1}\cup V_{1}\right)
.

Finally, from the definitions of
U_{i}
and
V_{i}\,
,
together with the condition that neither

\psi_{1}
nor
\psi_{2}
is constant,
we get that
K_{i}\neq\emptyset
.
Moreover, as it is obtained after the
removal of open intervals

U_{i}
,
V_{i}
from the two ends of

I_{i}
, it follows that

K_{i}
is closed in
I_{i}
(
i=1,2
).
Thus we have verified that

U_{1}\,,U_{2}\,,V_{1}\,,V_{2}\,,K_{1}\,,K_{2}

indeed possess the properties
assured in case
(2)
of our theorem.

The only remaining situation is when there exist

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and an index

j\in\{1,2\}
such that

\psi_{j}(z)=\lambda
for all

z\in I_{j}\,
, while
at the same time
\psi_{i}

is not constant on
I_{i}

(
i\neq j
).
We proceed with analyzing the structure of

\left(I_{j}\,|\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)\cap I_{i}\,
.
First of all,

I_{j}
is open, so the reflection

\left(I_{j}\,|\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=2\cdot\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi-I_{j}

is also open in
\mathbb{R}
, thus

\left(I_{j}\,|\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)\cap I_{i}

is open as well.
Hence, according to the
Representation theorem for open sets of
\mathbb{R}
,
it is the union of a countable collection of
nonempty, disjoint open intervals
(see
[
2
, Theorem 3.11]
).
That is, there exist open intervals

U_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

(
n=1,\dots,N
where

N\in\mathbb{N}
or
N=\infty
)
such that

U_{k}\cap U_{l}=\emptyset
for all indices

k\neq l
, and

\left(I_{j}\,\,|\,\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)\cap I_{i}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\,.

Of course this implies
U_{n}\subseteq I_{i}\,
.
Now let

m\in\{1,\dots,N\}
be
an arbitrary index, and
z\in U_{m}\,
.
Since

z\in\left(I_{j}\,|\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)\cap I_{i}\,
,
there exist

s\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
and
w\in I_{j}
such that

z=2s-w
.
Putting
z
and
w
into (
1
)
and using that
\varphi(s)\neq 0
,
we get

\psi_{i}(z)=\psi_{j}(w)=\lambda
.
So
\psi_{i}
is indeed constant on

\bigcup U_{n}
with value
\lambda
.

For the last step let us choose an arbitrary

t\in\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup U_{n}\right)+I_{j}\bigr)
. Then there exist

z\in\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup U_{n}\right)

and

w\in I_{j}
such that

t=\frac{1}{2}(z+w)
, which means

z=2t-w
. But if

t\in\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi
then we have

z\in\left(I_{j}\,|\,\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)\cap I_{i}=\bigcup U_{n}\,
,
opposed to

z\in\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup U_{n}\right)
.
Therefore
\varphi(t)=0
must hold for every

t\in\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup U_{n}\right)+I_{j}\bigr)
.
Thus we have concluded case
(3)
of the theorem.
∎

4.
Solutions when
\varphi
is a derivative

We wish to emphasize that
Theorem
2
is
not an "if and only if" type statement.
This happens because for the reverse implication
we could only guarantee that

\varphi
is
0
almost everywhere on some intervals,
which is insufficient to automatically produce a solution.
Therefore we are still a step away from a characterization
theorem analogous to
[
16
, Theorem 6.]
by Kiss.
However, we have already mentioned
in the Introduction, that for the applications
the meaningful condition for
\varphi

would be that it is the derivative
of a differentiable function.
We know that a derivative is measurable,
so Theorem
2

gives the three possible forms of a solution.
What is more,
under the assumption that
\varphi
is a derivative,
we can prove the converse of our theorem.
The reason for that is the following statement.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\emptyset\neq J\subseteq\mathbb{R}

be an open interval, and let

\Phi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a differentiable function such that

\varphi=\Phi^{\prime}
is zero almost everywhere.
Then
\varphi(x)=0
for all
x\in J
.

Proof.

It is well-known (see
[
25
, Theorem 7.21]
)
that if a real function is differentiable
(everywhere) and the derivative is Lebesgue integrable
on compact intervals then
the Newton–Leibniz formula holds for the derivative.
Hence, for all
x,y\in J
,
x<y
, we have

\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)=\int_{x}^{y}\Phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt=\int_{x}^{y}\varphi(t)\,dt=0.

Of course, the integral above is the Lebesgue integral,
and we used that
\varphi
is zero almost everywhere.
Hence
\Phi
is constant, so

\varphi=\Phi^{\prime}
is identically zero.
∎

The main result of this section is the complete
characterization of the solutions of (
1
)
when
\varphi
is a derivative.

Theorem 3
.

Let

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

be a solution of (
1
)
and suppose that
there exists a differentiable function

\Phi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\varphi(u)=\Phi^{\prime}(u)

for every
u\in J
.
Then exactly one of the following statements is true.

(1)

\varphi(u)=0
for all
u\in J
or
there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda

for all
x\in I_{1}
and
y\in I_{2}\,
.

(2)

There exist constants

\lambda,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and, for

i=1,2
, there exist

a_{i}\,,b_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\inf I_{i}\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}\leq\sup I_{i}
and
for the open intervals

U_{i}:=\left]\,\inf I_{i}\,,a_{i}\,\right[\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad V_{i}:=\left]\,b_{i}\,,\sup I_{i}\,\right[

the following assertions are fulfilled:

•

U_{i}\neq I_{i}
and
V_{i}\neq I_{i}

for
i=1,2\,
;

•

U_{1}\,,U_{2}
are both nonempty or

V_{1}\,,V_{2}
are both nonempty;

•

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\lambda
for all

(x,y)\in U_{1}\times U_{2}
and

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{2}(y)=\nu
for all

(x,y)\in V_{1}\times V_{2}\,
;

•

\varphi(u)=0
for all

u\in\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cup\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right)

where

K_{i}:=I_{i}\setminus\left(U_{i}\cup V_{i}\right)

is a nonempty subinterval
which is closed in
I_{i}
(
i=1,2
).

(3)

There exist a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

and an index

j\in\{1,2\}
such that

\psi_{j}(z)=\lambda
for all
z\in I_{j}\,
,
while there exists a finite or countable collection of
disjoint, nonempty open intervals

U_{n}\subset I_{i}

(
i\neq j
and
n=1,\dots,N

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
or
N=\infty
)
with the following properties:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\neq I_{i}\ \mbox{ and }\ \psi_{i}(z)=\lambda\ \mbox{ for all }\ z\in\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\,,\mbox{ moreover }

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=0\ \mbox{ for all }\ t\in\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(I_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}U_{n}\right)+I_{j}\right).

Conversely, if there exist nonempty open intervals

I_{1}\,,I_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

J=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}\right)

such that for the functions

\psi_{1}:I_{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\psi_{2}:I_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\varphi:J\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
one
of the three cases above holds,
then the triplet

\left(\varphi\,,\psi_{1}\,,\psi_{2}\right)

is a solution of (
1
).

Proof.

Firstly let us observe that the direct implication
is a special case of Theorem
2
,
because
\varphi
is a derivative, so it is measurable.
However, in case
(1)
and
(2)
we can
improve the assertions

\mu\left(\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=0\ \mbox{ and }\ \mu\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\,\cup\,\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right)\right)\cap\mbox{\rm supp}\varphi\right)=0

to

\varphi(u)=0\,\mbox{ for all }u\in J\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\varphi(u)=0\,\mbox{ for all }u\in\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1}+I_{2}\right)\cup\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{1}+K_{2}\right),

respectively. This fact is the consequence
of Lemma
2
, which
ensures that if

\varphi
is zero almost everywhere on an open interval,
the
\varphi
is identically zero on that interval.
The converse implication was verified by T. Kiss during
the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 6.]
.
∎
