The 1-year obesity treatment intervention can improve fasting plasma glucose in children aged 6–12 years: randomized controlled trial
Authors: A. Martikainen, AM Eloranta, U. Schwab, T. Örmälä
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLifestyle interventions can reduce the risk of becoming an overweight adult and reduce the risk of health problems. The aim was to investigate the effects of obesity treatment on BMI-for-age, metabolic markers, and quality of life in children with overweight or obesity.MethodsA randomized 1-year lifestyle intervention, including appointments every 3 months, a total of 5 sessions. A multidisciplinary team consisting of a pediatrician, a nurse, and a clinical nutritionist conducted the study. The participants were 6–12-year-old children with overweight or obesity in the Hyvinkää area, Finland. The inclusion criteria were weight for height at least + 40% or ≥ 30% and ascending growth curve. Participants were randomized to the intervention group (n = 21) or the control group (n = 19). The intervention group received lifestyle counseling with an emphasis on nutritional guidance. The control group continued in the primary care and did not have any intervention during the study period. Laboratory values and anthropometric measurements were measured at baseline and at 12 months. In addition, quality of life was evaluated by a questionnaire.ResultsIn the intervention group fasting plasma glucose decreased statistically significantly (p = 0.017) and the difference in the change between the groups approached statistical significance (p = 0.080). No other significant changes were found between the intervention and the control groups at the end of the intervention. The increase of BMI-for-age stopped in both groups.ConclusionEncouragement to healthy lifestyle can improve glucose metabolism in children with obesity and to stop excessive weight gain.Trial registration18.12.2018 (§142/2018, HUS/2666/2018).ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06126679.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-026-09768-3.

Background

Clinical interventions to manage childhood obesity have the potential to reduce the prevalence of obesity in adults, improve long-term quality of life, and reduce health care costs [1–3]. Lifestyle interventions in children with overweight or obesity, including multicomponent guidance with healthy diet, increasing physical activity, and decreasing sedentary time with behavioral change, can be effective by decreasing body weight, body mass index (BMI), and BMI z score. They can also decrease the risk of health problems associated with obesity in adulthood [3, 4, 4–9]. For example, it has been shown that a decrease in adiposity between childhood and adulthood is associated with marked reductions in the risks of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [10]. Some studies have reported that lifestyle interventions resulted in improvements in plasma lipid concentrations, insulin sensitivity, and blood pressure in children with excess body weight, even in the absence of weight loss or change in body composition [8]. The risks of health problems among children with overweight or obesity who become nonobese by adulthood is like in those persons who were never obese [10]. Lifestyle interventions are safe in children. Studies have found no evidence of adverse effects on growth, eating disorder pathology, or mental health [5, 6, 11].

Studies show that weight loss is greater when the duration of treatment is longer than 6 months [3, 5, 8]. Motivation for treatment and adherence to the protocol contribute to a favorable outcome [12]. Pediatric obesity treatment is most effective when it starts at a young age. Starting around the age of 6 years has been shown to be beneficial [4, 5, 12, 13]. Including a dietary component in the intervention can be a critical part of the treatment of childhood obesity [8]. Most lifestyle-based weight loss interventions have provided dietary counseling and some information about principles of behavior change, and many have also provided information related to physical activity or sedentary behavior and involved parents [3, 5, 14]. These interventions have resulted in improved nutritional knowledge and eating habits of school children, even if there have been no changes in the anthropometry [15]. Moreover, a greater number of contacts is associated with the success of interventions [3, 5, 14]. However, intensive contacts increase the costs of interventions and make them difficult to implement in clinical settings. It has been estimated that interventions in children with excess body weight could be cost saving [16]. The lifetime cost is almost three times greater per child with obesity than per child with overweight [16]. In adulthood, the costs of overweight and obesity are greater the longer the person has been living with obesity, especially from childhood or adolescence [16].

This study investigates the effects of a long-term but less intense lifestyle intervention initiated at an optimal age before puberty and with parental support among overweight children. The aims were to investigate the effects of an obesity treatment intervention on BMI-for-age, laboratory markers of plasma lipid profile, glucose metabolism and liver health, and quality of life in 6–12-year-old children with overweight or obesity.

Methods

This was a randomized 1-year lifestyle intervention study called the Hyvinkää Childhood Obesity Study (HCOS). The main aim was to investigate the effects of the lifestyle intervention on BMI-for-age and metabolic markers. The study was conducted in coordination with Helsinki University Hospital (HUS), Hyvinkää Hospital and it was carried out in cooperation with the municipalities of the Hyvinkää Hospital area in Southern Finland. The study was launched in December 2018, and it was completed in December 2023.

Study participants

Study participants were recruited from health check visit in the primary care, as previously reported [17]. The inclusion criteria were overweight (weight for height at least + 40% or ≥ 30% and on the rise) and age 6–12 years. The exclusion criteria were a diagnosis of endocrine diseases and mental illnesses. Participants had to be well-motivated and participated in the study with one or both parents. The total study sample included 47 overweight or obese children. Eligible participants were randomized to either the intervention group or the control group using opaque sealed envelopes. Seven of the participants dropped out during the study. Their data have not been processed.

The final study sample included 40 children. In the intervention group the participants were 11 ± 1.4 (mean ± SD) years old at the baseline, and 13 were girls and 8 were boys. In the control group the participants were 10 ± 1.7 years old, and 9 were girls and 11 were boys. Most of the participants had experienced only a few difficulties in physical, psychological, or social wellbeing in the last 3 months.

Calculation of the sample size

Calculations of sample size have been previously presented [17]. The estimated sample size was 40 children per group with the assumption that 10% of participants will drop out in both groups. Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, we did not achieve the estimated sample size.

Study design

The study design is presented in Fig. 1. The intervention included family-based lifestyle counseling, with an emphasis on nutritional counseling and to support the parents in managing their children’s behavior. We used motivational interviewing. The aim of counseling was to increase awareness of healthy dietary and physical activity habits; to achieve a suitable energy balance, to create a positive attitude to physical activity, to promote optimal sleep duration and to improve the children’s body image and body control. Meetings were every 3 months. The multidisciplinary team consisted of one pediatrician, one specialist nurse and one clinical nutritionist. Children with their parents met the pediatrician two times, the specialist nurse five times and the clinical nutritionist three times during the 1-year intervention (Table 1). Dietary and behavioral counseling was delivered by the clinical nutritionist. Dietary guidance was based on the Nordic and Finnish nutrition recommendations [18, 19], emphasizing appropriate energy intake, regular meal frequency, quality of dietary fat and carbohydrates, including lower sugar and higher fiber intakes. This included increasing the consumption of vegetables, berries, fruit, sources of unsaturated fats, and wholegrain products, replacing high-fat milk and meat products with fat-free and low-fat alternatives and reducing the consumption of high-fat and high-sugar snack foods and sugar-sweetened beverages. Joint family meals were encouraged.

The specialist nurse measured the participant’s anthropometric measurements and blood pressure at the beginning of the study, at 6 months and at 12 months. The nurse advised participants to sleep 8–10 h every night and reduce sedentary time, and improve diet as discussed with the clinical nutritionist. The pediatrician performed a clinical inspection and evaluated the laboratory analyses. The participants met the pediatrician at the baseline and at 12 months.

The participants in the intervention group were advised and motivated to increase their regular daily physical activity and to reduce sedentary habits. The designed group exercise guidance with a physical education instructor and a voluntary lecture for parents about increasing physical activity and reducing sedentary time had to be canceled after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. The entire research team encouraged and motivated the participants and their parents to carry out the instructed lifestyle changes. Families were instructed to use Finnish lifestyle internet manuals for kids and families provided by the Finnish Heart Association to help implement the lifestyle changes at home. Focus was on educational and behavioral counseling to keep up healthy growth and development with a positive attitude. The changes implemented by the family were discussed during the following study visits.

The control group continued with the routine primary care and did not receive any special intervention during the study period. Routine primary care included the annual check-up by a nurse at school. All participants were asked to fill out study questionnaires at baseline and at 12 months.

The specialist nurse measured body height and weight, waist circumference, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. BMI-for-age was calculated [17].

Laboratory analyses

As previously described [17] laboratory analyses included fasting plasma concentrations of total, HDL and LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose, alanine transaminase (ALAT) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and serum concentrations of insulin and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Homeostasis model of insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR) was calculated (fasting insulin µU/ml × fasting glucose mmol/l/22.5). Laboratory samples were taken after 12 h of fasting at baseline and at 12 months, and they were performed in the laboratory of Helsinki University Hospital.

Measurement of quality of life

Quality of life was assessed by a questionnaire concerning the child’s psychological, physical, and social difficulties during the previous 3 months [20]. The rates were summed as the overall physical (range 12–60), psychological (range 19–95), and social (range 6–30) well-being scores were separated. A higher score indicated more difficulties and lower well-being. The study questionnaires were sent to all participants by mail and filled out by the parents with their child at home.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical analysis software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, version 29.0.0.0 (241)). The normal distribution of the variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk’s test and observing histograms. Logarithmic transformations were made to variables with skewed distribution: plasma triglycerides, hs-CRP, and ALAT and serum insulin. For these, logarithmic transformations were used in the analyses. At the baseline, group differences were tested using independent-samples T test. Changes within the intervention and control groups during the 1-year study period were tested using paired samples T test, and the effects of the intervention were investigated using general linear model for repeated measures. Analyses included those who participated in the entire intervention, and we used per protocol analysis. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical issues

Our study has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of HUS and has been carried out in compliance with appropriate ethical standards as previously described [15].

Results

During the 1-year study period plasma fasting glucose concentration decreased significantly (p = 0.017) in the intervention group and differences between the groups approached statistical significance (p = 0.080); also, the excessive weight gain stopped in both groups (Table 2). The children’s quality of life remained principally good and there were no significant changes during the intervention. At baseline BMI-for-age was significantly higher in the control group than in the intervention group (p = 0.007). Plasma LDL cholesterol concentration was elevated in both groups and fasting serum insulin concentration in the intervention group, but there were no significant differences between the groups (Table 2). Discussion

In this randomized, controlled study, we found favorable changes in some metabolic risk factors. The excessive weight gain stopped in both groups during the 1-year intervention, and fasting plasma glucose concentration decreased significantly in the intervention group. Our results suggest that a family-based intervention can have small but favorable effects on glucose metabolism. However, to detect differences between the intervention and the control groups in BMI-for-age, a longer period or more contact hours with participants may be needed.

According to previous studies, childhood overweight and obesity are associated with impaired glucose and cardiovascular metabolism in children [21, 22]. Especially, the risk of hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, and prediabetes are increased. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is also strongly associated with obesity in children, and this includes elevated plasma liver enzyme concentrations [23]. Persistent low-grade inflammation can increase the risk of obesity-related diseases [24]. Our results suggest that lifestyle guidance with an emphasis on nutritional counseling can reduce these risk factors, and our results are supported by previous research evidence [3–8]. We found that concentrations of plasma glucose decreased significantly in the intervention group. This may suggest that participants have made favorable lifestyle changes, which may be associated with a decrease in BMI-for-age and stopping the excessive weight gain. ALAT concentration decreased in the intervention group and HbA1c in the control group, but changes were not significant. At the baseline, the LDL cholesterol concentration was elevated in both groups and serum insulin in the intervention group; otherwise, laboratory values were within reference values. Based on this, rapid excessive weight gain in children should be appropriately addressed, and these children should be addressed to enhanced lifestyle guidance and support. We can suggest that the measurement of laboratory values in clinical work is not necessary in the early stage of overweight in prepubertal children, and the treatment should focus on supporting healthy eating habits and increasing exercise. Puberty seems to be a critical period for the development of hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, and other features of the metabolic syndrome [24]. Our study participants were prepubertal, so this was an optimal age to affect these risk factors. Participation in this study indicated a good motivation to make changes. It has been noticed that obesity treatment is most effective when it starts at a young age [4, 5, 12, 13], and good motivation for treatment contributes to favorable outcomes [12].

During the study, BMI-for-age turned slightly downward both in the intervention and the control groups, which was a non-significant change. We had meetings every 3 months during the 1-year intervention, and we had a total of 10 contact hours. In previous weight management interventions, over 26 contact hours have been generally effective in reducing excess weight in children and adolescents after 6 to 12 months intervention, compared with little or no reduction in control groups [5, 14], and the more contacts, the more significant effect. Considering the number of face-to-face counseling and contact hours, we succeeded with a smaller number of contacts to stabilize weight gain. Moreover, there were also favorable changes in laboratory values. Without the challenges caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, and with the targeted number of study participants and by keeping physical activity guidance involved the whole study period, the results could have been more effective and significant. In previous studies, it has been shown across variable levels of contacts that some children in both intervention and control groups may achieve large reductions in excess weight, some have no or modest changes, and some continue to gain weight [14]. It may be possible that even talking about overweight treatment together with good motivation has a positive effect on stopping excessive weight gain in children. In the control group, families may have been interested in making lifestyle changes affecting the results in the control group. Furthermore, results of the baseline measurement, study questionnaires, and food diary might have increased the awareness among both the children and the families. In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced participant’s lifestyle and further BMI-for-age. In addition, height growth varies in children aged 6–12 years and may have contributed to the change in BMI-for-age. There is still a lot of height growth left, and the development of puberty is just coming. So, stopping extra weight gain together with height growth in the coming years will be very important for the long-term health of the participants and prevention of obesity.

More resources and more guidance from a clinical nutritionist should be allocated to health care to support the treatment of overweight at an early stage in children. Previous studies support the importance of a multiprofessional team and inclusion of nutrition as a critical part of the treatment [8]. It has been shown that by improving nutritional knowledge and eating habits of school-aged children, the consumption of fast food, sweets, and salty snacks can reduce significantly, which is a favorable change although there were no changes in the anthropometrics during a 12-month intervention [15]. Moreover, children’s living environment should encourage healthy lifestyles, for example by enhancing access to healthy food, outdoor activities and improving sports facilities.

Strengths and weaknesses

This is a randomized trial and professionals with long-term clinical experience have been responsible for conducting the study and the visits of the intervention group. Nutrition counseling was supervised by a clinical nutritionist. Study questionnaires have been used in a previous study (the Finnish Physical Activity and Nutrition in Children Study) as previously described [17]. The COVID-19 pandemic was an unpredictable challenge for conducting our research. Due to this, we had to interrupt the exercise group guidance. Moreover, the number of participants was smaller than estimated and thus the statistical power was weakened. The response rate in the questionnaire related to quality of life was low despite several reminders by phone calls or text messages. It is possible that those who completed the questionnaire were more motivated and health-oriented and had less difficulties in well-being. This may have affected the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, even a less intensive intervention, together with long-term duration, motivation, and parental involvement, can have beneficial effects on glucose metabolism. Clinicians or public-health practitioners should intervene in excessive weight gain in children by providing guidance supporting a healthy lifestyle at an early stage. However, further studies are needed with a larger number of participants and longer-term effects.
