The dysbiosis of intestinal microbiota associated with isolated tricuspid valve regurgitation
Authors: Tongkai Ge, Dou Fang, Lishan Zhong, Shanwen Pang, Lin bin Hua, Jimei Chen, Junfei Zhao, Huanlei Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is an increasingly recognized yet frequently underappreciated valvular disorder that adversely affects both survival and quality of life, with progressively worse outcomes as severity increases. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have considerably expanded our understanding of the human microbiome, uncovering its close associations with a range of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, heart failure, and hypertension. However, the potential role of intestinal dysfunction and the gut–heart axis in TR pathophysiology remains largely unexplored.MethodsA total of 20 patients diagnosed with TR and 10 healthy age- and gender-matched patients were enrolled in this study. Fecal samples were collected before the operation and subjected to 16 S rDNA gene sequencing.ResultsThe gut microbiomes of TR and healthy patients were significantly different in terms of alpha-diversity and beta-diversity analysis. Indicator analysis further showed 9 indicative bacterial genera and 2 bacterial species. Genera known to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including Faecalibacterium, Blautia, and Ruminococcus were depleted, whereas Lactobacillus and Veillonella were enriched. Their correlations with clinical features using Pearson analysis showed that Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus, and Veillonella were potentially involved in the pathophysiology of TR. Functional predictions indicated enrichment of pathways related to carbohydrate, lipid, and nucleotide metabolism, as well as immune and cardiovascular diseases, whereas pathways linked to amino acid and vitamin metabolism were reduced. Notably, pathways involving D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism and Staphylococcus_ aureus infection were upregulated.ConclusionsPatients with tricuspid regurgitation (TR) exhibit gut dysbiosis, characterized by significant alterations in taxonomic composition. These findings suggest a dysregulation of the gut-host interaction in TR.

Introduction

Isolated tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is an increasingly recognized yet frequently underappreciated valvular disorder that adversely affects both survival and quality of life, with progressively worse outcomes as severity increases [1]. Despite its clinical importance, TR remains challenging to diagnose and manage because symptoms often appear late, imaging assessment can underestimate its severity, and optimal timing for surgical or transcatheter intervention is still debated [2–4]. Current guidelines for valvular heart disease provide only limited recommendations regarding the diagnosis and management of TR, and its underlying pathophysiology remains insufficiently elucidated [5].

The gut microbiota, often described as “the hidden organ,” plays essential roles in food fermentation, defense against pathogens, and the maintenance of mucosal immunity and intestinal homeostasis [6]. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have considerably expanded our understanding of the human microbiome, uncovering its close associations with a range of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, heart failure, and hypertension [7, 8]. Notably, the potential role of intestinal dysfunction and the gut–heart axis in TR pathophysiology remains largely unexplored.

From a hemodynamic perspective, chronic venous congestion caused by TR may lead to intestinal wall edema, increased permeability, local ischemia, and impaired barrier integrity [9]. These alterations in the intestinal milieu can disrupt the healthy microbial community, resulting in dysbiosis [10]. In turn, dysbiotic gut microbiota may decrease beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, thereby directly or indirectly affecting myocardial function and systemic inflammation [11]. Moreover, emerging evidence highlights the role of systemic inflammation and gut microbiota in the progression of other cardiovascular conditions, such as abdominal aortic aneurysm and hypertension [12, 13].Therefore, intestinal dysfunction is not merely a consequence of TR-induced congestion but may actively contribute to disease progression through the gut–heart axis.

In the present study, we performed a cross-sectional analysis to characterize changes in gut microbiota composition in patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation prior to surgical intervention. We hypothesized that underlying cardiac abnormalities may influence both the composition and function of the gut microbiota, potentially affecting disease progression and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Experimental design and cohort study

The hospital’s Research Ethics Committee approved this study, conducted at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No. KY-Z-2022-256-01). Twenty patients requiring surgical intervention for isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) were enrolled in the study and comprised the TR group. The control group consisted of ten healthy, age- and gender-matched volunteers. To minimize confounding factors, fecal samples were collected within 24 h of hospital admission (morning collection) and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Comprehensive medical histories and physical examinations were obtained for screening. Participants with the following conditions were excluded: kidney dialysis, acute infection, inflammatory bowel disease, cancer, autoimmune disorders, a cold within the past 14 days, experiencing diarrhea in the week prior to enrollment, experiencing constipation in the week prior to enrollment, jaundice in the last 30 days, or antibiotic/probiotic use within the past month.

Fecal sample collection and 16 S rDNA gene sequencing

Fecal samples from all participants were collected and immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. DNA extraction was performed using the Hitpure Stool DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China, D3141). The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, annealing at 60 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Primers 341 F (5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3’) were used for amplification. The PCR products were quantified with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, California, USA). Only samples with a DNA concentration ≥ 5 ng/µL were used for subsequent library construction. Library quality was assessed using the ABI Step OnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA). Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), producing 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatics analysis

Following the QIIME quality control pipeline with a default quality threshold of ≤ 3, we utilized QIIME2’s DADA2 plugin to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Venn analysis was performed using the R package Venn Diagram (version 1.6.16). Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, ACE, and Shannon, were calculated in QIIME (version 1.9.1). Beta diversity was visualized via non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Biomarker taxa across groups were identified using LEfSe (version 1.0) and the labdsv R package (version 2.0–1). Microbial functional profiling was predicted using PICRUSt2 and Tax4Fun.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as medians (interquartile range) or means ± SD, and descriptive data are reported as frequencies. The distributions of continuous data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For quantitative variables of normal distribution presented as mean (standard deviation). Statistical differences among groups of continuous variables following a normal distribution were assessed using Student’s t test while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for data with a skewed distribution. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, which are reported as percentages. Correlations between variables were analyzed by Pearson correlation. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was employed to identify differentially abundant taxa across taxonomic levels. Following the identification of biologically relevant gut microbiota, log-transformed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores were calculated to quantify their effect sizes. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed by R version 4.1.1.

Result

Demographic characteristics of all participants

A total of 30 patients were enrolled in the study, including 20 patients with severe TR and 10 healthy controls. Baseline characteristics were summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in most baseline variables, including age, gender, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), albumin, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), platelet count, serum ALT, and triglycerides. Compared with the control group, patients with TR exhibited significantly higher values of right ventricular free wall thickness (RVFWT), right atrial dimension, right ventricular dimension, E/A ratio, white blood cell count, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), serum creatinine, serum total bilirubin (TBIL), and cholesterol levels. Conversely, right ventricular fractional area change (RVFAC) and lymphocyte count were significantly reduced in the TR group.

Bacterial community composition profile in different groups

A total of 30 fecal samples were analyzed from volunteers and patients with cardiac TR. The samples were divided into two groups: Healthy group (N) and TR group (T). Venn diagrams were used to illustrate the shared and unique bacterial genera between the groups. A total of 134 genera were shared, while 21 and 24 genera were unique to the healthy and TR groups, respectively (Fig. 1A).

The top 10 most abundant taxa were selected to generate a stacked histogram of relative abundance. At the phylum level (Fig. 1B), Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota together accounted for more than 80% of the gut microbiota. In the healthy group, Firmicutes (53.12%), Bacteroidota (24.12%), and Proteobacteria (16.39%) were predominant, whereas in the TR group, the same three phyla remained dominant, but their relative abundances shifted to 40.56%, 31.15%, and 21.65%, respectively. At the family level (Fig. 1C), Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Bacteroidaceae were among the most abundant families. Notably, Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae were enriched in the healthy controls, while Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroidaceae were more prevalent in the TR group. At the genus level (Fig. 1D), Bacteroides, Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Phascolarctobacterium, and Prevotella were relatively more abundant in the TR group, whereas Faecalibacterium, Blautia, Ruminococcus, and Agathobacter were enriched in healthy controls. At the species level (Fig. 1E), Bacteroides fragilis, Phascolarctobacterium_faecium, and Bacteroides_uniformis were significantly enriched in the TR group. In contrast, Bacteroides_vulgatus, Ruminococcus_sp.N15.MGS57, Eubacterium_rectaleATCC33656, and Roseburia_inulinivorans were more abundant in the healthy group.

Correlation between gut microbiota and clinical parameters

Indicator analysis further showed 9 indicative bacterial genera (Fig. 5A) and 2 bacterial species (Fig. 5B) (median relative abundance > 0.1% and P < 0.05) identified as biomarkers that may distinguish between the control and the TR groups. To explore their clinical relevance, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between these taxa and clinical parameters, including height, weight, body mass index (BMI), albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), creatine kinase-MB (CKMB), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), creatinine (CREA), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C, LVEF%, FAC%, right ventricular wall thickness, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), right atrial vertical diameter, right ventricular vertical diameter, and tricuspid regurgitation area. Among the findings, Lactobacillus showed significant positive correlations with SII (r = 0.364, p = 0.048; Fig. 6A) and CKMB (r = 0.449, p = 0.013; Fig. 6B), while Veillonella was also positively correlated with SII (r = 0.459, p = 0.011; Fig. 6C). Additionally, Megasphaera_micronuciformis demonstrated a positive association with ALT (r = 0.387, p = 0.035; Fig. 6D). Conversely, Ruminococcus was negatively correlated with both the tricuspid regurgitation area (r = − 0.379, p = 0.039; Fig. 6E) and the right atrial vertical diameter (r = − 0.378, p = 0.039; Fig. 6F), respectively. These findings suggest that several species are potentially involved in the pathophysiology of TR.

Gut microbial functional disruption in patients with TR

We performed STAMP analysis to explore differences in predicted functional pathways of the intestinal microbiota between the two groups based on preoperative stool samples. At level 2, functional predictions generated by PICRUSt2 revealed distinct differences between the TR group and healthy controls. Compared to the control group, TR patients exhibited an increased abundance of pathways associated with carbohydrate metabolism, membrane transport, nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, immune responses, immune-related diseases, and cardiovascular conditions. In contrast, pathways involved in the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, energy production, amino acid metabolism, and environmental adaptation were relatively reduced (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The pathophysiology of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is driven by increased right atrial and ventricular pressures, volume overload, and reduced cardiac output. This leads to decreased arterial-venous pressure gradients in the intestinal capillary network, compromising gut perfusion [14]. The intestinal villi, optimized for nutrient absorption, are particularly vulnerable to ischemia and hypoxia due to oxygen shunting at their base [15]. Visceral congestion in TR creates anaerobic conditions, leading to intracellular acidosis and a lower gut pH, which may disrupt the balance of SCFA-producing bacteria. This hypoxic microenvironment likely contributes to gut microbiota alterations observed in our study. The current study provided evidence that the gut microbiota in TR patients before the operation differs from that in normal controls in terms of taxonomic composition, alpha diversity, and functional profile. These findings suggested that the interplay between the host and the gut microbiota may be dysregulated in patients with TR, and indeed TR may influence the composition and function of the gut microbiota.

The gut microbiota is a dynamic ecosystem that maintains continuous communication and symbiosis with the host, influencing susceptibility to various diseases [16–18]. Consistent with previous reports [19, 20], in this study, the gut microbial composition was mainly dominated by Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota at the phylum level, accounting for over 80% of the total community. Alpha diversity was notably reduced in the TR group, indicating a loss of microbial richness and evenness. The TR patients had decreased relative abundance of several beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera, including Faecalibacterium, Blautia, Ruminococcus, Roseburia, Dorea, and Butyricicoccus, all of which have previously been linked to intestinal barrier function, anti-inflammatory effects, and cardiovascular health [21–24]. Specifically, Faecalibacterium_prausnitzii is regarded as one of the most important anti-inflammatory commensals due to its ability to induce IL-10 production and inhibit NF-κB signaling pathways [25, 26]. Roseburia, Ruminococcus, and Butyricicoccus are also key butyrogenic bacteria capable of fermenting dietary fibers, thereby supporting colonic pH balance and epithelial repair [27]. In addition, Blautia and Dorea have been consistently associated with healthy gut states, and their reduction is frequently observed in conditions involving impaired barrier function and chronic inflammation [22]. In this study, Ruminococcus was negatively correlated with right atrial vertical diameter and tricuspid regurgitation area, implying a potential protective role in maintaining right heart structure and function.

Traditionally regarded as a classic probiotic, Lactobacillus is well known for its roles in regulating microbial homeostasis and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [28, 29]. Several studies have also reported an increased abundance of Lactobacillus in patients with chronic heart failure [30–32]. Interestingly, the TR group exhibited an enrichment of Lactobacillus. Correlation analysis further demonstrated significant positive associations between Lactobacillus and both CKMB and SII, suggesting a potential link with myocardial injury and systemic inflammation [33, 34]. One possible explanation is that in patients with TR, intestinal hypoperfusion and venous congestion may impair barrier function, thereby creating a distinct gut microenvironment. Such dysbiosis could promote the expansion of specific Lactobacillus strains with context-dependent functional properties that differ from their conventional probiotic effects. At the same time, barrier dysfunction may facilitate the translocation of endotoxins such as LPS into the circulation, subsequently activating inflammatory pathways, inducing cardiomyocyte injury, and elevating CKMB levels. Nevertheless, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus remained low, and its functional role in the setting of TR is still uncertain. Further studies are needed to clarify its mechanistic significance.

Alongside changes in gut microbial composition, we observed functional dysbiosis in bacterial metabolic pathways. TR patients exhibited an increased abundance of pathways associated with carbohydrate metabolism, membrane transport, nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, immune responses, immune-related diseases, and cardiovascular conditions. In contrast, pathways involved in the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, energy production, amino acid metabolism, and environmental adaptation were decreased. At the level 3, the results revealed a significant enrichment of pathways associated with D-Arginine and D-Ornithine metabolism, along with an elevated risk of Staphylococcus. Because arginine metabolism is closely linked to nitric oxide (NO) synthesis [35], perturbations in microbial arginine/ornithine pathways may plausibly affect NO bioavailability, endothelial function, and host antimicrobial responses.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center observational study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Larger, multi-center cohorts are needed to validate these results across more diverse populations. Second, the functional predictions generated by PICRUSt2 are indirect and experimental validation will be essential to determine their biological relevance. Finally, given the observational design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Future studies incorporating longitudinal follow-up and preclinical experimental models are warranted to clarify causal mechanisms and to translate the present associations into mechanistic understanding. Despite these limitations, our findings provide novel insights into the potential involvement of gut microbial metabolism in TR and highlight testable hypotheses for future research.

Conclusion

Patients with tricuspid regurgitation (TR) exhibit gut dysbiosis, characterized by significant alterations in taxonomic composition. These findings suggest a dysregulation of the gut-host interaction in TR. However, further studies investigating the causality of these changes are needed to move from observational data to preclinical validation.
