Greenhouse gas emissions from freshwater wetlands of the Doon Valley, Northwest Himalaya, India
Authors: Ayushi Baiswar, Sameer K. Tiwari, Sehajnoor Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The subtropical wetlands of the Doon Valley function as significant net sources of greenhouse gases (GHGs), with methane (CH₄) dominating the radiative forcing (∼62% of CO₂-equivalent emissions) despite lower molar fluxes than carbon dioxide (CO₂). High-resolution field measurements reveal that CH₄ emissions are primarily controlled by anaerobic conditions, sustained soil moisture, elevated temperatures, and low dissolved oxygen, whereas CO₂ fluxes exhibit greater temporal variability and respond strongly to thermal regimes and ionic strength. A key biogeoclimatic insight is the seasonal decoupling of soil moisture and atmospheric water vapor (H2O), where summer drying coincides with peak humidity driven by energy-limited evapotranspiration. Pronounced spatial heterogeneity in gas fluxes further suggests that land-use context and modified hydrological pathways may interact with climatic drivers to influence wetland carbon dynamics. The wetland complex (221.39 ha) emits approximately 0.0195 Mt CO₂-eq annually, underscoring its disproportionate role in regional GHG budgets. These findings reveal strong coupling among hydrological saturation, thermal regimes, redox conditions, and atmospheric moisture in regulating GHG emissions. The study underscores the high climate sensitivity of monsoon-dependent wetlands and highlights the need for targeted hydrological restoration and continuous monitoring to mitigate future amplification of emissions under warming scenarios.

Introduction

Anthropogenic intensification of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions continues to accelerate global warming, exerting unprecedented pressure on earth system processes. Long-term atmospheric monitoring indicates that global mean concentrations of CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O have reached 417.9 ± 0.2 ppm, 1923 ± 2 ppb, and 335.8 ± 0.1 ppb, respectively, in 2022, representing increases of 150%, 264%, and 124% above pre-industrial levels1,2. The rise in CH4 and N2O concentrations poses a greater impact on global warming compared to CO2, as their 100-year global warming potentials are 34 and 298 times higher than that of CO2, respectively3.

Wetlands are among the most climate-sensitive ecosystems and play a disproportionately significant role in global carbon storage and GHG emissions. Although they occupy only 5–8% of terrestrial land area, they store 20–30% of global soil carbon due to persistent anoxic conditions that inhibit aerobic decomposition and promote organic matter accumulation4,5. Natural wetlands sequester approximately 1280 Tg-C yr⁻¹ as CO₂ while emitting ~ 448 Tg-C yr⁻¹ as CH₄, resulting in a net sink of nearly 830 Tg-C yr⁻¹6.This dual role positions wetlands as both stabilizing components of the carbon cycle and as the largest natural source of atmospheric CH₄7–9. Their sensitivity to hydrology, plant functional types, nutrient availability, and redox conditions underscores the need for process-based assessments of carbon cycling and GHG exchange10–12.

Global wetland extent has declined dramatically, with over half of the wetland area degraded or lost since the industrial revolution, primarily due to agricultural expansion, drainage, river abstraction, and urban development13–15. Such anthropogenic alterations lower water tables, enhance soil aeration, and accelerate the oxidation of organic carbon, resulting in elevated CO₂ emissions and disturbing the natural exchange of GHGs16,17. Field studies demonstrate that drained wetlands often undergo a functional shift from CO₂ sinks to net CO₂ sources12,18. These transitions are further modulated by temperature and soil moisture, which regulate carbon mineralization, methanogenesis, and diffusive transport19,20. Despite significant global progress, major data gaps persist. India possesses extensive wetland systems, yet empirical GHG assessments remain sparse21–23. Data scarcity is particularly acute in the Himalayan foothills, a region identified by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)IPCC24 as a “white spot” due to the absence of systematic measurements. The limited available studies25 suggest that Himalayan wetlands exhibit strong sensitivity to climatic warming, monsoonal shifts, and hydrological changes, all of which may substantially alter GHG exchange.

Emerging research indicates that GHG fluxes in Indian wetlands are highly responsive to climate-induced variability in hydrology and trophic conditions26. Monsoon-driven fluctuations in water level, temperature, and substrate supply may intensify or suppress CH₄ and CO₂ emissions, thereby modifying wetland radiative forcing27. Global syntheses have increasingly emphasized the need for enhanced wetland monitoring, standardized methodologies, and improved integration of wetlands into national carbon accounting frameworks. Moomaw, et al.28 highlight that climate-driven hydrologic changes may rapidly shift wetlands between net carbon sinks and net GHG sources. In line with these recommendations, recent international guidance stresses the urgent need for comprehensive wetland inventories and robust GHG assessment protocols in climate-sensitive regions29. Parallel evaluations highlight that managing CH₄ emissions from wetlands is crucial for mitigating positive climate feedbacks under global warming scenarios30.

Given these substantial knowledge gaps, the subtropical Himalayan wetlands of the Doon Valley represent an ecologically critical yet understudied system whose monsoon-driven hydrology and rapid land-use change may significantly influence regional CH₄ and CO₂ budgets. This study presents the first high-resolution, multi-site assessment of GHG dynamics across seven representative wetlands in the Himalayan foothills, providing a comprehensive quantification of spatiotemporal variability in ambient CH₄ and CO₂ concentrations and fluxes. By analyzing the controlling influences of soil moisture, temperature, and key physicochemical properties, this research addresses a significant knowledge gap for mountain-foothill wetland ecosystems that are highly climate-sensitive yet remain among the least studied components of the global GHG budget. Specifically, the study aims to (i) quantify the spatial and temporal patterns of CH₄ and CO₂ emissions and (ii) evaluate the relative radiative forcing contributions of these gases using updated IPCC global warming potential (GWP) metrics. This approach facilitates a deeper understanding of wetland climate sensitivity under scenarios of projected warming and intensified monsoonal activity. Ultimately, the study seeks to generate robust empirical data to enhance regional GHG inventories and inform evidence-based wetland management strategies in the Himalayan foothills and other ecologically similar regions worldwide.

Results

Ambient concentrations of greenhouse gases

Methane (CH4)

Ambient CH₄ concentrations showed clear spatial and seasonal variability across the Doon Valley wetlands. In winter, CH₄ levels exceeded the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) global background value (1908 ppb) at most sites, ranging from 1606.34 ± 293.25 ppb at Asan Left (AL) to 2115.62 ± 47.68 ppb at Asan Ramsar Site (ARS) (Fig. 1a; Table 1). During summer, concentrations increased across all wetlands, ranging from 1896.06 ± 283.62 ppb at AL to 2658.04 ± 94.38 ppb at ARS, with all sites except AL surpassing the global background threshold. This consistent summer enhancement indicates strong seasonal intensification, driven by higher temperatures, sustained saturation, and reduced oxygen availability that promote methanogenesis, whereas cooler and more oxygenated winter conditions suppress CH₄ production.

Carbon dioxide (CO2)

Atmospheric CO₂ levels varied noticeably across different sites and between seasons. In winter, most wetlands exceeded the NOAA global background value (418 ppm), with concentrations ranging from 348.59 ± 69.34 ppm at AL to 481.71 ± 112.92 ppm at Asan Right (AR) (Fig. 1b; Table 1). During summer, CO₂ levels increased at all sites, ranging from 434.98 ± 13.33 ppm at Dakpathar (DP) to 590.91 ± 66.14 ppm at Nakronda (NK), with all sites surpassing the global background threshold. The consistently high summer CO₂ concentrations indicate elevated microbial respiration driven by higher temperatures, increased organic loading, and reduced freshwater availability, whereas lower winter concentrations reflect reduced contamination and suppressed microbial activity under cooler conditions.

Water vapor (H2O)

Ambient H₂O concentrations displayed strong seasonal contrasts across the wetlands. During winter, H₂O levels ranged from 12,164.82 ± 1,829.17 ppm at AL to 19,450.21 ± 2,628.05 ppm at DP (Fig. 1c; Table 1). Summer measurements showed substantial increases at all sites, with a maximum concentration of 36957.08 ± 3855.54 ppm recorded at NK. These patterns are consistent with higher atmospheric temperatures and enhanced evapotranspiration during the summer season.

Methane fluxes (FCH4) and their correlations

Methane fluxes (FCH₄) showed strong spatial and seasonal variability across the seven wetlands, with winter values ranging from 0.03 to 185.83 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, the minimum recorded at DP (0.03 ± 0.01 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and the maximum at AL (185.83 ± 162.92 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹). In summer, fluxes increased substantially, ranging from 0.82 to 508.29 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹, with the lowest again at DP (0.82 ± 0.81 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and the highest at Asan Central (AC) (508.29 ± 63.58 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Seasonal summation of winter and summer fluxes further indicated that AC (605.78 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and AL (517.45 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) were the dominant CH₄-emitting sites, while DP remained the least emissive (0.84 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 1). Across all wetlands, higher FCH₄ values consistently coincided with elevated soil moisture, such as at AL (0.81 ± 0.36 m³/m³ in winter), NK (0.87 ± 0.09 m³/m³ in winter), and AC (0.56 ± 0.42 m³/m³ in summer), compared to low-moisture sites like ARS (0.06 ± 0.15 m³/m³ in summer), indicating a strong positive association between saturation and CH₄ production. Similarly, wetlands with higher summer soil temperatures, such as AL (41.61 ± 0.46 °C), AC (36.76 ± 0.85 °C), and AR (35.42 ± 1.94 °C), exhibited markedly elevated CH₄ emissions relative to cooler sites, reflecting the temperature sensitivity of methanogenesis. Electrical conductivity (EC) also covaried with CH₄ flux, with high-summer flux wetlands showing elevated EC, including NK (816 µS/cm), AL (325 µS/cm), and AC (173.5 µS/cm). In contrast, Dissolved oxygen (DO) exhibited an inverse relationship with methane emissions: wetlands with persistently low DO, such as AL (1.5 mg/L in both seasons) and NK (4.8–4.40 mg/L ), showed substantially higher CH₄ fluxes than oxygen-rich sites such as DP (11.1 mg/L in winter) and Manu Swamp (MS) (11.50 mg/L in winter), confirming that anoxic conditions favor methanogenic pathways. Ambient CH₄ concentrations also aligned with measured fluxes, with higher FCH₄ observed at sites exhibiting elevated atmospheric CH₄ levels, such as ARS (2115.62 ± 47.68 to 2658.04 ± 94.38 ppb) and AC (1910.31 ± 221.47 to 2256.41 ± 22.84 ppb), compared to sites with lower ambient levels. H₂O vapor exhibited a similar positive correlation, with markedly higher values in summer (up to 36957.08 ± 3855.54 ppm at NK), matching the seasonal increase in methane flux. pH exhibited no consistent monotonic pattern, with high fluxes occurring across both alkaline (pH 8.5 at DP and AC in winter) and moderately acidic (pH 6.8 at AL in summer) waters, suggesting that pH effects were secondary to moisture and temperature (Fig. 2c). Basin-scale integration identified AC as the dominant CH₄ source (605.78 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹), contributing markedly higher emissions than the other wetlands, including AL (517.45 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹), AR (316.38 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹), MS (320.73 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹), and NK (110.79 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 2). Across the seven wetlands covering 221.29 ha, the area-weighted cumulative CH4 flux corresponds to an annual emission of ~ 0.000405 Mt CH₄ yr⁻¹ for the full wetland complex. When expressed as CO₂-equivalents using a 100-year GWP of 3031, these methane emissions amount to 0.0121 Mt CO₂-eq yr⁻¹, demonstrating that CH₄ contributes the majority of the total greenhouse gas burden despite its lower mass emission relative to CO₂. Overall, CH₄ fluxes were positively associated with soil moisture, soil temperature, EC, water vapor, and ambient CH₄ concentrations, while DO showed a clear negative relationship, indicating that hydrological saturation, thermal conditions, and redox status are the dominant controls on methane emissions across the Doon Valley wetland systems. The coefficient of variation (CV) for CH₄ flux exhibited relatively low variability across all wetlands, with values ranging from 1.15% to 7.78% (Table 1). Winter CVs spanned a broader range, from 1.15% at MS to 7.78% at AC, whereas summer CVs remained comparatively constrained, ranging between 1.80% and 4.07%. Site-specific CVs during winter were 7.38% (DP), 7.78% (AC), 1.48% (AL), 1.70% (AR), 1.93% (ARS), 2.06% (NK), and 1.15% (MS), while summer CVs were 3.06% (DP), 1.94% (AC), 2.64% (AL), 3.43% (AR), 2.26% (ARS), 1.80% (NK), and 4.07% (MS).

Seasonal variation in CO₂ flux (FCO₂) and their correlations

CO₂ fluxes displayed distinct spatial and seasonal variability across all wetlands, with winter fluxes ranging from 0.04 to 0.51 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, the minimum observed at DP (0.04 ± 0.11 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and the maximum at NK (0.51 ± 0.48 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), while summer fluxes ranged much higher, between 0.41 and 3.43 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, with the lowest at MS (0.41 ± 0.38 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and the highest at AC (3.43 ± 2.20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 1). For the entire study period, the highest cumulative CO₂ emission occurred at AC (3.90 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and the lowest at ARS (0.68 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 2). Seasonal enhancement of FCO₂ corresponded with increases in soil temperature and decreases in soil moisture at several wetlands, where summer temperatures ranged from 29.94 ± 0.29 °C at NK to 41.61 ± 0.46 °C at AL, while winter temperatures remained substantially lower (19.64 ± 1.54 °C at NK to 26.45 ± 3.91 °C at ARS). Higher FCO₂ values were typically associated with elevated EC (Fig. 3), particularly at sites such as NK (673–816 µS/cm) and AL (279–325 µS/cm), and with lower DO concentrations, where wetlands showing reduced DO such as AL (1.5 mg/L in both seasons) and NK (4.8–4.4 mg/L) exhibited correspondingly elevated CO₂ flux in summer. FCO₂ also showed positive correlation with ambient CO₂ concentrations, which ranged from 348.59 ± 69.34 ppm at AL in winter to 590.91 ± 66.14 ppm at NK in summer, suggesting parallel increases in surface exchange during warmer months. When examined alongside FCH₄, CO₂ flux displayed similar spatial and seasonal patterns, with wetlands exhibiting higher CH₄ emissions, such as AC (508.29 ± 63.58 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and AL (331.62 ± 211.01 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) in summer, also showing elevated CO₂ fluxes. FCO₂ further showed positive correlation with water vapor concentrations, which increased substantially in summer (reaching 36957.08 ± 3855.54 ppm at NK), whereas correlations with pH were weak (Fig. 3c), with high CO₂ fluxes occurring across both alkaline (pH 8.5 at DP and AC in winter) and moderately acidic (pH 6.8 at AL in summer) conditions. Basin-scale integration identified AC as the dominant CO₂ source (3.90 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 2), emitting substantially more than the other wetlands, including NK (3.74 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), MS (0.73 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), AL (0.89 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), and AR (0.78 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Based on area‐weighting across the seven wetlands (total area 221.29 ha), the cumulative CO₂ flux corresponds to an annual emission of ~ 0.00744 Mt CO₂ yr⁻¹ ( 0.00744 Mt CO₂-eq yr⁻¹ because GWP₁₀₀ of CO₂ = 1) 31 for the entire wetland complex. Overall, CO₂ fluxes were most closely aligned with variations in soil temperature, EC, DO, H2O vapor, ambient CO₂ concentrations, and CH4 flux, highlighting consistent seasonal co-variation among these biophysical parameters across all wetlands. The CV for CO₂ flux exhibited pronounced heterogeneity across wetlands and seasons, ranging from 1.51% (NK, winter) to 657.67% (AC, summer), indicating substantial differences in temporal dispersion among sites (Table 1). Winter CVs remained comparatively moderate, with values of 19.15% (DP), 4.60% (AC), 8.99% (AL), 5.88% (AR), 16.08% (ARS), 1.51% (NK), and 5.91% (MS). Summer CVs displayed a far broader numerical spread, with DP (50.80%), AC (657.67%), AL (3.09%), AR (12.37%), ARS (9.90%), NK (2.44%), and MS (12.65%) (Table 1).

Discussion

The Doon Valley represents a distinctive geomorphological setting as an elongated intermontane basin situated between the Lesser Himalaya and the Siwalik ranges within the Western Himalayan region. This unique physiographic configuration supports a mosaic of ecologically significant systems, including extensive Sal (Shorea robusta) forests, freshwater wetlands, and diverse wildlife habitats, which collectively regulate regional biodiversity patterns and drive critical biogeochemical processes. The region is increasingly subjected to both natural variability and anthropogenic pressures, enhancing its ecological vulnerability. In this context, field-based measurements of greenhouse gas emissions from freshwater wetlands are essential for capturing site-specific carbon dynamics and understanding their sensitivity to environmental controls. Such observations not only improve process-level understanding but also provide a crucial basis for upscaling greenhouse gas flux estimates and assessing their implications across broader Himalayan ecosystems.

CH₄ concentrations and fluxes across the Doon Valley wetlands exhibited marked spatial and seasonal heterogeneity, reflecting the interplay of hydrological conditions, thermal regimes, and redox-sensitive biogeochemical processes. CH₄ concentrations ranged from 1606.34 ± 293.25 ppb at AL in winter to 2658.04 ± 94.38 ppb at ARS in summer. At the same time, CH₄ fluxes showed an even stronger seasonal amplification, increasing from winter values as low as 0.03 ± 0.01 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at DP to summer maxima of 508.29 ± 63.58 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at AC. This seasonal amplification highlights the sensitivity of methanogenic processes to favorable environmental conditions, consistent with patterns reported in tropical and subtropical wetlands7,32,33.

The coefficient of variation (CV) further supports this pattern of stability in CH₄ dynamics, with CV values remaining consistently low across all wetlands (< 8%) (Table 1). These low CV values indicate that, despite strong differences in fluxes between sites and seasons, methane production remained relatively stable over time. Wetlands such as NK, AR, and MS showed the lowest CV values, demonstrating minimal temporal fluctuation in CH₄ emissions and reflecting the persistent saturated conditions that maintain steady methanogenesis34,35. Sites with slightly higher CV values, such as AC and AL in winter, still exhibited modest variability compared to CO₂, underscoring the greater temporal stability of methanogenic processes relative to other respiratory pathways. Sites with comparatively higher winter variability, namely AC (7.78%) and DP (7.38%), summer CVs declined sharply (AC: 1.94%; DP: 3.06%), underscoring the predominance of seasonally stable hydrological and redox conditions during the warm, monsoon-driven period.

Hydrological control emerged as a dominant driver of CH₄ fluxes, with the highest summer emissions recorded at wetlands exhibiting elevated soil moisture, such as AL (0.81 m³/m³ in winter) and AC (0.56 m³/m³ in summer). These conditions facilitate the development of anaerobic environments by restricting oxygen diffusion34,36. DO further modulated CH₄ dynamics, with high-flux wetlands consistently exhibiting lower DO concentrations (Table 1; Fig. 2b). This inverse relationship underscores the importance of anoxic conditions in promoting methane production while limiting oxidation, thereby enhancing net CH₄ emissions12,35.

EC displayed a positive correlation with CH₄ fluxes, particularly at sites such as NK and AL (Fig. 2d). Elevated ionic strength is typically associated with increased organic substrate availability or anthropogenic nutrient enrichment, both of which may stimulate methanogenic pathways37. These results align with multi-wetland assessments showing that EC can serve as an integrative indicator of wetland trophic status, influencing carbon turnover38.

Atmospheric CH₄ concentrations showed strong spatial coherence with flux patterns. High ambient CH₄ at ARS and AC matched their elevated CH₄ fluxes (Table 1), indicating strong local source atmosphere coupling. H₂O vapor, which increased sharply in summer (up to 36,957 ± 3,855 ppm at NK), also showed positive correlation with fluxes, reflecting enhanced evapotranspiration and boundary-layer humidity under warm, saturated summer conditions, known to enhance CH₄ transport through plant aerenchyma36.

Basin-scale integration further confirmed the spatial dominance of specific wetlands in regional CH₄ emissions. AC contributed the largest CH₄ flux, followed by AL and MS, consistent with their higher moisture, lower DO, and elevated summer temperatures. The cumulative CH₄ emission for the 221.29 ha wetland complex was ~ 0.0121 Mt CO₂-eq yr⁻¹ using a 100-yr GWP of 3031. Although methane was emitted in much smaller quantities than CO₂, it contributed over 60% of the total CO₂-equivalent emissions, highlighting its disproportionately large climatic influence within these wetland systems. This observation is consistent with global wetland assessments, which show that modest increases in CH₄ flux can disproportionately affect radiative forcing due to methane’s high warming potential31,39.

Overall, the spatiotemporal controls on CH₄ concentrations and fluxes in the Doon Valley wetlands were jointly governed by soil temperature, hydrological saturation, DO, EC, and near-surface atmospheric properties. The strong co-variation among these biophysical factors highlights their collective role in shaping methane production, oxidation, and transport, consistent with mechanistic models of wetland methane cycling40. These results underscore the vulnerability of wetland CH₄ emissions to climate-driven shifts in hydrology and temperature, indicating potential amplification under future warming scenarios.

Methane dynamics across the Doon Valley wetlands were strongly governed by redox-sensitive processes, with distinct site-level variations reflecting differences in electron-acceptor availability and organic-matter decomposition pathways. Wetlands such as AC and AL exhibited characteristics of strongly reducing environments, supported by low DO and elevated EC, indicating enhanced anaerobic metabolism. The low DO recorded at AL and the elevated EC at NK (816 µS/cm) provide evidence for active anaerobic mineral dissolution and accelerated reduction processes that create niches favourable for methanogenic archaea.

The distribution of soil moisture across the wetlands further amplified these redox transitions. High moisture values (0.81 m³/m³ at AL and 0.87 m³/m³ at NK) reflect limited gas diffusion and strong physical isolation from atmospheric oxygen, conditions that intensify reducing soil chemistry. Under these conditions, methanogenesis becomes a dominant terminal pathway of carbon mineralization34,35. By contrast, wetlands with intermittent aeration, such as ARS, where moisture remained as low as 0.06 m³/m³ during summer, likely favored mixed redox regimes where methane production and oxidation co-occur, resulting in comparatively smaller net fluxes. Similar relationships have been reported in seasonally flooded tropical and monsoon-influenced wetlands, where shifts in water-table position alter oxygen penetration into sediments, driving changes in redox potential and consequently regulating CH₄ production and oxidation36.

Temperature also modulated redox-driven CH₄ formation by accelerating both organic matter breakdown and hydrogen availability for methanogenic archaea. Elevated temperatures (up to 41.61 °C at AL) catalyze syntrophic interactions between fermenters and methanogens, reducing the energetic barrier for CH₄ formation under highly reduced conditions33. This is supported by the disproportionately higher flux from warm, reduced sites such as AC, where both substrate supply and redox state appear optimized for methanogenesis.

Ambient CH₄ accumulation also reflected strong redox control, with the highest concentrations at ARS (2658.04 ± 94.38 ppb). Rather than being driven solely by atmospheric mixing, these elevated levels indicate suppressed methane oxidation under anoxic conditions. Reduced soils inhibit methane monooxygenase, the enzyme responsible for aerobic CH₄ consumption, allowing more methane to escape unoxidized41. Such low-redox environments promote methanogenesis while constraining methanotrophy35, resulting in net CH₄ buildup and reinforcing its disproportionately large contribution to greenhouse forcing in the studied wetlands7,39. The data indicate that methane production in these wetlands is governed by a tightly coupled suite of redox-sensitive processes involving soil saturation, thermal enhancement of anaerobic metabolism, and suppression of methane oxidation. These interactions create biogeochemical feedbacks that are highly sensitive to hydrological and climatic variability4.

Spatial variation in ambient CO₂ concentrations across the Doon Valley wetlands reflected strong controls exerted by hydrological and biogeochemical gradients. Winter concentrations ranged from 348.59 ± 69.34 ppm at AL to 481.71 ± 112.92 ppm at AR, while summer concentrations rose substantially, peaking at 590.91 ± 66.14 ppm at NK. The elevated summer concentrations at NK corresponded with higher soil temperatures (29.94 ± 0.29 °C) (Fig. 3e) and moderate soil moisture (0.34 ± 0.25 m³/m³) (Fig. 3f), conditions known to enhance both autotrophic (plant respiration) and heterotrophic (microbial decomposition) processes contributing to CO₂ production42.

DO further modulated CO₂ dynamics, with low DO values aligning with higher CO₂ at sites such as AL and NK (Fig. 3b). Oxygen scarcity is known to suppress aerobic decomposition pathways while stimulating anaerobic mineralization, thereby increasing CO₂ release from wetland soils43,44. Similar patterns have been reported across freshwater wetlands where oxygen limitation promotes fermentation, metal reduction, and other anaerobic pathways that elevate CO₂ efflux45. Elevated EC also corresponded with higher CO₂ concentrations (Fig. 3d), particularly at NK (673–816 µS/cm) and AL (279–325 µS/cm), suggesting a role for ionic strength in enhancing substrate availability and solute reactivity, which collectively support more rapid carbon mineralization46–48.

Seasonal variations in ambient CO₂ closely tracked the flux measurements, with FCO₂ rising from winter minima (0.04 ± 0.11 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at DP) to summer maxima (3.43 ± 2.20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at AC). These transitions are consistent with the temperature dependence of wetland respiration under warm, oxygen-limited summer conditions8. The CV provided additional evidence of strong temporal variability in CO₂ emissions across the basin. CO₂ exhibited a far broader CV range than CH₄, spanning from low variability at NK in winter to very high variability at AC in summer (Table 1). These differences indicate that CO₂ production and efflux were highly sensitive to short-term environmental fluctuations, particularly thermal and hydrological changes. Wetlands with moderate CV values (AL, AR, MS) maintained relatively stable respiration rates, whereas high-CV sites such as AC experienced episodic summer respiration pulses. This variability underscores the responsiveness of CO₂ dynamics to transient shifts in temperature, DO, and water-table position.

Basin-scale integration (Table 2) identified AC as the dominant CO₂ source, followed by NK, while ARS and MS contributed comparatively less. Based on an area-weighted integration of flux measurements across all sampled sites, the total annual CO₂ emission from the 221.29-hectare wetland complex was around 0.00744 Mt CO₂ yr⁻¹. These spatial differences emphasize the role of oxygen availability, hydrological connectivity, and carbon substrate supply in shaping ecosystem respiration, consistent with established wetland carbon cycling frameworks4,7. The observed spatial and seasonal variability in CO₂ concentrations highlights the integrated influence of DO limitation, thermal conditions, ionic composition, and atmospheric moisture on carbon dynamics in monsoon-driven wetlands. These findings underscore the need for high-resolution monitoring of hydrological and biogeochemical conditions when assessing CO₂ exchange in rapidly changing wetland landscapes.

The Doon Valley wetlands act as a measurable regional source of GHGs, emitting a total of 0.01954 Mt CO₂e yr⁻¹ from a combined area of 221.29 ha. This total comprises 0.01210 Mt CO₂e yr⁻¹ from CH₄ and 0.00744 Mt CO₂e yr⁻¹ from CO₂. While this constitutes only ~ 0.00047% of India’s national GHG emissions 4,133 Mt CO₂e;49, the emissions arise from less than 0.01% of the country’s geographical extent, underscoring the disproportionately strong radiative influence of small wetland complexes. On a per-area basis, the emission intensity (~ 88.29 t CO₂e ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) is considerably lower than fluxes reported from tropical peatlands, which can exceed 15,000–50,000 t CO₂e ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹50, placing Doon Valley wetlands within the low-to-moderate emission category for subtropical systems.

CH₄ contributes ~ 62%, despite representing a smaller mass flux than CO₂. CO₂ accounts for the remaining 38% of the total GHG burden. This highlights the disproportionate radiative impact of CH₄ relative to CO₂ within this subtropical wetland system. This imbalance reflects the strong role of hydrological saturation, reduced oxygen availability, and warm-season soil temperatures in supporting methanogenesis across sites such as AC, AL, and MS. The highest CH₄ fluxes in the basin occurred at AC (508.29 ± 63.58 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and AL (331.62 ± 211.01 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹) during summer (Table 1), comparable to fluxes reported from similar subtropical Himalayan wetlands51(Table 3). The strong temperature sensitivity of methanogenic pathways, projected warming, and intensified monsoon recharge are likely to elevate future CH₄ emissions32,52.

CO₂ fluxes displayed greater spatial and seasonal heterogeneity, ranging from low winter efflux (0.04 ± 0.11 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at DP) to high summer emissions (3.43 ± 2.20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at AC). NK exhibited the highest cumulative CO₂ release (3.74 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), consistent with its elevated ion concentrations, higher ambient CO₂, and warmer surface temperatures (Table 2). These patterns align with established relationships between hydrological modification, nutrient enrichment, and enhanced microbial respiration in human-impacted wetlands6,53. Winter CO₂ fluxes were consistently lower than summer values across all wetlands due to reduced biological and environmental activity during colder months. Lower winter soil temperatures suppress microbial respiration and enzymatic decomposition, leading to diminished heterotrophic CO₂ production54. Reduced solar radiation and shorter photoperiods also limit plant metabolic rates, further lowering autotrophic respiration55. Comparison with regional studies (Table 3) indicates that the Doon Valley wetlands exhibit lower CO₂ emissions but comparable CH₄ fluxes relative to other subtropical Himalayan wetlands25,51. This combination suggests that portions of the wetland complex maintain partial carbon-sink potential but that this sink strength is spatially constrained by hydrological fragmentation, seasonal shifts in redox conditions, and localized anthropogenic disturbance. These observations are consistent with patterns reported in similar wetland systems25,51, where CH₄ emissions are enhanced under warm and saturated conditions, while CO₂ fluxes remain comparatively lower in seasonally dynamic environments. In contrast, wetlands with more persistent inundation or higher productivity typically exhibit greater emission intensities. Overall, these comparisons indicate that although the controlling mechanisms are similar across wetland systems, the magnitude of emissions in the Doon Valley is moderated by its subtropical climate, seasonal hydrological variability, and heterogeneous landscape characteristics25.

Beyond natural hydrological and biogeochemical controls, anthropogenic pressures in the Doon Valley may partially modulate greenhouse gas emissions from these wetlands. The catchment has undergone rapid urban expansion and agricultural intensification, along with rivers increasingly influenced by wastewater discharge, all of which can elevate nutrient and labile carbon inputs to wetland ecosystems. Such inputs can stimulate microbial respiration and methanogenesis, thereby enhancing both CO₂ and CH₄ emissions without necessarily altering seasonal patterns6,53. Although anthropogenic inputs were not directly measured, the strong spatial differences in gas fluxes indicate that local land use and hydrological alterations likely interact with climate-driven processes to influence the carbon balance of the Doon Valley wetlands.

Water vapor exhibited pronounced spatial and seasonal variability across the Doon Valley wetlands, reflecting regulation by surface temperature, soil moisture, and boundary-layer humidity. Summer H₂O concentrations were substantially higher than winter values (Table 1), coinciding with peak atmospheric temperatures and enhanced evapotranspiration in humid subtropical wetlands56. Although soil moisture decreased across most sites during summer, atmospheric H₂O concentrations increased, indicating an inverse relationship between soil water availability and atmospheric humidity. This suggests that elevated temperatures drive increased evaporation and plant transpiration, enriching near-surface atmospheric moisture despite declining soil water content57,58. H₂O vapor also displayed strong co-variation with GHG fluxes across sites, with wetlands such as NK and AC exhibiting both higher H₂O concentrations and elevated CH₄ and CO₂ fluxes (Table 1). This relationship reflects the influence of atmospheric moisture on biological activity, where warmer and more humid conditions enhance microbial processes and greenhouse gas production12,59.

H₂O vapor dynamics act as an integrative indicator of hydrological state, thermal regime, vegetation structure, and GHG-producing processes within the Doon Valley wetlands. Spatial variability further highlights heterogeneity in moisture retention, canopy characteristics, and hydrological connectivity25,35, while the observed associations with soil temperature and moisture emphasize the importance of microclimatic controls in regulating near-surface humidity7. These findings underscore the need to incorporate atmospheric moisture dynamics into wetland GHG assessments, particularly in monsoon-driven systems undergoing rapid environmental change.

Despite the comprehensive assessment, certain uncertainties related to spatial scaling and temporal variability should be considered when interpreting the results. The study is based on selected wetlands within the Doon Valley, and although these sites capture a range of hydrological and biogeochemical conditions, they may not fully represent the heterogeneity of the entire wetland network across the region. Spatial differences in land use, hydrological connectivity, and local environmental conditions could influence greenhouse gas dynamics beyond the sampled locations. Additionally, measurements were conducted during specific seasonal periods, and short-term observations may not capture interannual variability. These temporal limitations may influence the magnitude and variability of observed fluxes. Therefore, future studies incorporating broader spatial coverage and long-term monitoring are necessary to improve the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Materials and methods

Study area and geological setting

Doon Valley (30°01′–30°31′ N, 77°38′–78°19′ E), located in the Dehradun district of Uttarakhand, is one of the most prominent valleys in the sub-Himalayan belt. The valley stretches approximately 80 km in length and 25 km in width, covering nearly 1950 km²60 (Fig. 4). It is enclosed by distinct geomorphic and hydrological boundaries: the Lesser Himalaya to the north, the Siwalik Range to the south, and the major Himalayan rivers, the Ganga and Yamuna, flanking the eastern and western margins. The geological framework comprises a wide range of lithologies, including carbonates, limestone, phyllites, sandstones, and coarse clastic deposits, representing rock units from the Precambrian to the Quaternary61. Several mountain-fed river systems shape hydrological connectivity; among these, the Song and Asan rivers serve as the dominant drainage pathways feeding into the Ganga and Yamuna basins62. The region experiences a subtropical climate, characterised by a monsoon peak that delivers roughly 85% of the annual rainfall between June and September, with the remainder attributed to winter western disturbances63.

The valley’s landform assemblage includes terraces, floodplains, dip-slope units, dissected hills, and a sequence of alluvial fans. These fan surfaces, categorised as isolated hills of fan material (IHF), proximal fan (PF), and distal fan (DF), record multiple phases of deposition and incision from ~ 43 ka to ~ 4 ka64. Their spatial arrangement and sedimentary architecture strongly influence permeability contrasts, groundwater flow paths, and the distribution of wetland habitats across the valley. Table 1 summarises the geographical attributes of the seven studied wetlands. DP is situated in a perennial zone downstream of the Tons–Yamuna confluence. The Asan Conservation Reserve comprises four sampling sites: AC, AL, AR, and the ARS, representing a hydrologically active wetland complex with extensive submerged plains and high biodiversity65. NK Wetland is positioned adjacent to the Moist Siwalik Sal Reserve Forest and serves as a critical water source for irrigation and domestic use, with surrounding vegetation dominated by Sal and mixed deciduous forest types. MS, occupying ~ 30 ha, comprises around 20 ha of swamp vegetation, featuring hydrophytic shrubs, algal mats, and scattered tree cover; however, parts are increasingly threatened by settlement encroachment66.

The geomorphic and hydrogeological configuration of the valley plays a crucial role in shaping the function of the wetland. Variations in sediment texture, aquifer geometry, and fan terrace stratigraphy influence groundwater residence times, water-table fluctuations, and wetland hydroperiods64. The chemical composition of groundwater, derived from carbonate silicate bedrock, governs nutrient supply and buffering capacity in wetland soils, which in turn affects redox gradients and microbial pathways responsible for CH4 and CO2 production67.

Trace gas measurements and flux calculations

This study employs an advanced analytical framework to quantify ambient concentrations and soil fluxes of CO₂, CH₄, and H₂O across wetlands of the Doon Valley, Uttarakhand. Measurements were performed using the LI-COR laser-based Trace Gas Analyzer (TGA-7810) coupled with the LI-COR Soil Flux Smart Chamber (Fig. 5a–b), enabling high-resolution assessments of GHG dynamics across contrasting wetland environments. Detailed methodology and instrument specifications are provided in recent studies68,69. The TGA-7810 operates on Optical Feedback Cavity-Enhanced Absorption Spectroscopy (OF-CEAS)70, a highly sensitive technique that directs a tunable laser into a high-reflectivity optical cavity, where photons undergo multiple interactions within the resonance mode. Rapid scanning of the laser frequency (~ 0.25 s) and the application of a spectral-fitting algorithm enable the precise quantification of trace-level atmospheric greenhouse gases (Fig. 5a). This configuration provides superior sensitivity and stability compared to conventional absorption-based methods. Numerous techniques exist for measuring soil GHG fluxes, including eddy covariance, static and dynamic chambers, micrometeorological approaches, and soil respirometry71,72. While widely used, these methods vary in temporal resolution and accuracy. Recent advancements, particularly the LI-COR Smart Chamber system, have substantially improved fine-scale flux measurements of CH₄ and CO₂ by integrating controlled chamber operation with real-time gas analysis. In this study, the Smart Chamber interfaced with the TGA-7810 to derive instantaneous fluxes during chamber closure, enabling simultaneous quantification of all target gases.

Field sampling and physicochemical measurements

For soil atmosphere flux measurements, PVC collars were installed 70–80% of their height below the soil surface before sampling to minimise disturbance. In waterlogged wetlands, collars were additionally stabilised using wooden supports to maintain vertical alignment (Fig. 5b). The LI-COR Smart Chamber was connected to the trace gas analyzer via Ethernet, and soil parameters were recorded using a Stevens Hydra Probe. Operational settings included the selection of soil type, a collar offset of 2.0–2.5 cm, a soil surface area of 318 cm², a 45-second post-purge period, and a 21-second dead-band interval. The Smart Chamber cycled automatically through programmed open–close sequences, storing measurements internally. Flux measurements were conducted during daytime hours (approximately 09:00–15:00 local time) in both the winter (February) and summer (June) seasons of 2022, with measurements taken on a daily basis. To account for spatial heterogeneity in soil and hydrological conditions, multiple sampling locations were selected within each wetland. At each location, three replicate flux measurements were conducted and averaged to obtain a site-specific flux estimate. The mean of all site-specific flux values within a wetland was subsequently calculated to derive a representative CH₄ and CO₂ flux for that wetland. Surface-water samples were collected at all sites to determine the physicochemical characteristics of the water and the associated soils. Parameters measured included pH, DO, and EC (Figs. 5c–d). These parameters were selected based on their established role in regulating wetland biogeochemical processes and greenhouse gas dynamics, particularly through their influence on redox conditions, microbial activity, and carbon transformation pathways. Field measurements were acquired using Hach® multiparameter probes with analytical precision of ± 0.05 for pH, ± 0.1 mg/L for DO, and ± 0.01 µS/cm for EC. Before each measurement, the probes were rinsed with Milli-Q water, and pH and EC were calibrated using National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) certified standard solutions to ensure measurement accuracy and reliability. The pH measurements were carried out using a calibrated digital pH meter. Prior to use, the instrument was calibrated using standard buffer solutions of pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0 at room temperature, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The electrode was rinsed with distilled water and gently dried between successive measurements to prevent cross-contamination.

Conclusion and future perspective

The wetlands of the Doon Valley act as significant and spatially heterogeneous sources of greenhouse gases (GHGs), with CH₄ contributing the largest share of radiative forcing despite lower molar emissions compared to CO₂. This study highlights the key biophysical and biogeochemical controls governing gas dynamics in this subtropical Himalayan system, with implications for wetland carbon cycling under changing climatic conditions.

CH₄ emissions were strongly associated with anaerobic conditions, with the highest fluxes observed at sites characterized by sustained soil moisture, elevated temperature, and low dissolved oxygen (DO), which together promote methanogenesis. The strong relationship between CH₄ flux, soil moisture, and DO confirms the dominant role of hydrological saturation and redox conditions in regulating methane emissions. In contrast, CO₂ fluxes exhibited greater temporal variability and were primarily influenced by seasonal temperature changes and substrate availability, reflecting the combined effects of autotrophic and heterotrophic respiration. Spatial differences in CO₂ emissions further indicate the importance of site-specific physicochemical conditions in controlling carbon release.

A notable observation was the seasonal decoupling between soil moisture and atmospheric H₂O vapor, where declining soil moisture during summer coincided with increased atmospheric humidity driven by enhanced evapotranspiration. At the ecosystem scale, the wetlands emit approximately 0.01954 Mt CO₂-eq yr⁻¹, with CH₄ contributing ~ 62% of total radiative forcing. Despite their limited spatial extent, these wetlands exert a disproportionate influence on regional GHG budgets and remain sensitive to projected warming and monsoon intensification.

While this study provides important insights, certain uncertainties related to spatial representativeness and temporal variability should be acknowledged. The analysis is based on selected sites and seasonal observations, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity and interannual dynamics of the entire wetland system. Future studies incorporating broader spatial coverage and long-term monitoring would help refine these estimates and improve regional-scale assessments.

The findings emphasize that wetland GHG dynamics are regulated by interacting thermal, hydrological, and redox processes. Anticipated climatic changes, particularly rising temperatures and altered monsoon patterns, are likely to enhance these processes and increase overall emissions. In addition to climatic influences, local land-use changes and hydrological modifications may further alter carbon dynamics.

From a management perspective, maintaining balanced hydrological regimes, preventing prolonged anoxic conditions, and reducing external nutrient and organic matter inputs are critical for limiting CH₄ emissions. Restoration of native vegetation and improved water management practices can help regulate carbon fluxes and enhance ecosystem resilience. Integrating long-term monitoring with process-based modelling will be essential for developing effective mitigation strategies. Overall, this study underscores the importance of subtropical Himalayan wetlands in regional GHG budgets and highlights the need for targeted, science-based conservation and management approaches.
