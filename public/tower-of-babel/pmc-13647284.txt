iProDNet: integrated probabilistic differential network inference under heterogeneous biological conditions
Authors: Heewon Park, Seiya Imoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding phenotype-specific network rewiring is essential for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying complex diseases. Although numerous differential network analysis methods have been developed, most focus on only a limited aspect of network information. Consequently, these approaches often fail to jointly characterize regulatory activity and network architecture, provide limited statistical evidence at the network level, and offer little capability for prioritizing key genes that drive phenotype-specific network rewiring. To address these limitations, we developed a novel computational framework, integrated probabilistic differential network analysis (iProDNet), for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules under heterogeneous biological conditions. A key feature of iProDNet is the integration of regulatory effects and network topological characteristics into a unified network-aware gene activity score. The framework further transforms gene-level differential activity into network-level statistical evidence using a nonparametric log-likelihood ratio approach and naturally prioritizes genes that contribute to phenotype-specific network rewiring. We evaluated iProDNet through Monte Carlo simulations and compared its performance with existing methods. Across diverse simulation settings, iProDNet consistently achieved superior discriminative performance while maintaining a balanced tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity. We further applied iProDNet to whole-blood RNA-sequencing data generated by the Japan Corona virus disease 2019 (COVID19) Task Force to identify molecular interactions associated with severe COVID19. The proposed framework identified distinct rewired subnetworks related to immune regulation, ribosome-mediated translation, and interferon-driven antiviral responses. Furthermore, iProDNet successfully prioritized biologically relevant markers, including B2M, ISG15, and multiple ribosomal protein genes, all of which have previously been implicated in COVID19 severity and antiviral immunity. These results demonstrate that iProDNet provides a statistically robust and biologically interpretable framework for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules and key regulatory drivers. As such, it offers new opportunities for investigating molecular rewiring mechanisms and discovering disease-associated therapeutic targets.Keywords: differential gene network analysis, network rewiring, log-likelihood ratio, nonparametric inference, gene prioritization

Introduction

Identifying network rewiring events that underlie phenotypic differences has become an important research topic in systems biology. While conventional transcriptomic analyses primarily focus on differential expression of individual genes, accumulating evidence suggests that disease progression is often accompanied by substantial alterations in network-level regulatory programs. Recent advances in high-throughput transcriptomic technologies have enabled the construction of large-scale gene regulatory networks, enabling the development of computational approaches for identifying phenotype-specific network rewiring events and their key regulatory drivers. A major objective of differential network analysis is to identify gene networks whose architectures differ substantially between phenotypes.

Numerous computational approaches have been proposed for this purpose. DiffGraph identifies network rewiring through differential graphical models by estimating changes in conditional dependency structures between phenotypes [1]. WGCNA constructs weighted co-expression networks and identifies biologically meaningful modules based on pairwise gene correlations [2]. DGCA detects phenotype-specific changes in gene–gene correlation patterns through differential correlation analysis [3]. Recently, nethet was developed to characterize heterogeneous biological network structures in high-dimensional settings [4]. Nonlinear approaches have also been developed for modeling complex biological systems. These include RNDEtree, which combines an ordinary differential equation model with a flexible neural tree [5], and a two-step gene regulatory network inference method integrating time-delayed correlation with a complex-valued S-system [6]. Related computational frameworks have also been applied to complex-valued system identification and post-translational modification site prediction [7, 8]. Recent computational advances have further expanded network-based analyses to single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data. These approaches include graph-based inference of gene regulatory networks [9], integration and interpolation of heterogeneous spatial transcriptomic data [10], and incorporation of intercellular communication into the prediction of intracellular regulatory relationships [11]. Collectively, these developments highlight the importance of integrating heterogeneous regulatory information to characterize phenotype-specific network alterations. Although these methods have demonstrated utility in detecting network differences, existing approaches typically characterize differential networks using only a single aspect of network information, such as topological properties, correlation patterns, or edge-level regulatory changes. Consequently, they often overlook the joint effects of regulatory activity and network architecture, limiting their ability to comprehensively characterize phenotype-specific network rewiring. Furthermore, existing methods generally provide limited statistical evidence at the network level and offer little capability for prioritizing key genes that drive phenotype-specific network rewiring. In addition, many approaches rely on restrictive parametric assumptions that may not adequately capture the complex characteristics of biological network data [12].

To address these challenges, we developed a novel computational framework, integrated probabilistic differential network analysis (iProDNet), for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules under heterogeneous biological conditions. A key innovation of iProDNet is the integration of regulatory activity and network topology into a unified network-aware gene activity score. Specifically, regulatory effects are combined with multiple topological characteristics, including hubness, betweenness centrality [13], and PageRank [14], enabling characterization of both functional activity and structural importance within gene regulatory networks. Furthermore, we extend gene-level differential regulatory activity into network-level statistical evidence through a probabilistic framework that aggregates gene-specific signals into a network-level LLR score [12]. Another important feature of iProDNet is its nonparametric likelihood framework. Unlike existing approaches that rely on restrictive distributional assumptions, iProDNet estimates underlying probability densities directly from observed data using kernel density estimation, enabling flexible characterization of complex biological network structures. Furthermore, the proposed framework naturally prioritizes genes according to their contributions to phenotype-specific network rewiring, thereby facilitating the identification of candidate biomarkers and key regulatory drivers. Consequently, iProDNet provides a comprehensive framework for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules by prioritizing key regulatory genes and characterizing molecular rewiring associated with complex biological phenotypes. We evaluated the performance of iProDNet through extensive Monte Carlo simulations using cancer-related gene regulatory networks constructed from the cancer cell line encyclopedia (CCLE) dataset. Additionally, we applied iProDNet to whole-blood RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data generated by the Japan COVID19 Task Force to identify severe COVID19-specific molecular interplays. We found distinct rewired subnetworks associated with immune regulation, ribosome-mediated translational processes, and interferon-driven antiviral responses. Furthermore, iProDNet successfully prioritized biologically relevant markers, including B2M, ISG15, and several ribosomal protein genes, which have previously been implicated in COVID19 severity and antiviral immunity.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: The Materials and methods section introduces the proposed iProDNet framework. The Results section presents Monte Carlo simulation studies and the application of iProDNet to severe COVID19 transcriptomic data. Finally, the Discussion section summarizes the main findings and discusses their implications.

Results

Network-level differential regulation detection

We evaluated the performance of the proposed iProDNet through extensive Monte Carlo simulations. Gene expression data were obtained from the CCLE dataset available through the DepMap portal (https://depmap.org/portal/), comprising mRNA expression profiles of 19 221 genes across 1406 cancer cell lines. We consider cancer and normal cell lines as two phenotypes and infer phenotype-specific gene networks corresponding to cancer and normal conditions. Network nodes comprise genes involved in cancer-related pathways obtained from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Expression profiles of p genes were used to infer the colorectal cancer gene network from 85 colorectal cancer cell lines. In the simulation study, the total network size was defined as p to evaluate performance across different network dimensions. Accordingly, the colorectal cancer network was constructed using all 87 colorectal cancer pathway genes together with p-87 genes randomly selected from the remaining expression dataset. Although the analysis could be restricted to the 87 pathway genes, the inclusion of additional genes enabled evaluation under larger and more diverse network-size scenarios. For comparison, a normal gene network was inferred using expression profiles from an identical gene set measured in 103 noncancerous cell lines. Then, we applied the proposed iProDNet to identify differentially regulated gene networks. If the colorectal cancer pathway-related network was identified as a colorectal cancer-specific gene network, the result was considered a true positive for differentially regulated gene network identification. To evaluate the spurious detection rate of differentially regulated gene network identification, we constructed two gene networks using expression profiles of randomly selected p genes and two randomly selected sets of 85 cell lines. Since both networks were generated under the null setting without phenotype-specific regulatory signals, the identification of a differentially regulated gene network was considered a false positive. The simulation procedure was repeated 50 times. The same simulation framework was applied to gastric, nonsmall cell lung, small cell lung, and pancreatic cancers. For each simulation replicate, B=20 phenotype-label permutations were performed while preserving the original group sizes. The phenotype-specific directed networks were re-estimated for every permutation using the same -regularized regression procedure (i.e. lasso [15]). The observed absolute differential activity scores (i.e. ) were used to estimate , whereas all permuted scores (i.e. ) were pooled to estimate . Both densities were estimated using a Gaussian kernel, with the bandwidth selected separately for each distribution by unbiased cross-validation [20]. The prior probability in (15) was estimated as the proportion of genes whose absolute differential activity scores met or exceeded the pooled permutation mean. The network-level score was calculated by summing the gene-level LLR scores, and a network was classified as differentially regulated when its cumulative LLR score was greater than zero. No additional edge-weight or subnetwork-size filtering was applied in the simulation study. We evaluate our strategy against existing differential network methods, including DiffGraph [1], WGCNA [2], DGCA [3], nethet [4], and chNet [21], together with three high-dimensional covariance tests: Li–Chen [22], PE-Cov [23], and Schott [24].

For iProDNet, the network-level LLR scores were used as decision statistics, and different thresholds on these scores were varied to obtain the ROC curve. For DiffGraph, WGCNA, DGCA, nethet, Li–Chen, PE-Cov, Schott, and chNet, the decision statistics were defined as the negative logarithm of their corresponding P-values. As shown in Fig. 2, iProDNet demonstrated strong and competitive performance across cancer types and network dimensions. Li–Chen and Schott also showed consistently high discrimination, whereas chNet performed well in most settings. PE-Cov exhibited strong performance in several cancer types but showed reduced discrimination in NSCL. The performance of WGCNA, DGCA, and nethet varied across scenarios, with pronounced reductions in the larger gastric cancer networks. DiffGraph generally showed weaker discrimination, with ROC curves frequently approaching the diagonal reference line. Overall, these results support the competitive performance of iProDNet across the evaluated settings, although no single method uniformly outperformed all others. The union of genes contained in these pathways was used to define a nonredundant candidate node set for network analysis, with genes shared by multiple pathways included only once.

For gene network construction, patients classified as asymptomatic (Level 1) or mild (Level 2) were grouped as nonsevere COVID19 cases, whereas patients classified as critical (Level 4) were grouped as severe COVID19 cases. Directed gene regulatory networks were separately estimated for the severe and nonsevere COVID19 groups using the -regularization method (i.e. lasso [15]). For each target gene, its expression was regressed on the expression levels of all other genes, with a nonzero regression coefficient defining a directed edge from the predictor gene to the target gene. The regularization parameter was selected using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [26].

When all inferred interactions were retained, the resulting dense network was dominated by a single large component, providing few distinct molecular modules for downstream analysis. Thus, we retained only the top 10% of edges by absolute weight to limit weak regulatory associations and identify interpretable network communities. Using the Louvain algorithm, the severe COVID19 network was partitioned into communities, of which seven contained >20 genes and were retained as candidate subnetworks for subsequent iProDNet analysis. For each candidate subnetwork, the background distribution was constructed using 20 phenotype-label permutations. The absolute normalized differential activity scores obtained from the 20 permutations were pooled to estimate , whereas the observed absolute differential activity scores were used to estimate . Both distributions were estimated using Gaussian kernel density estimation, with the bandwidth selected separately for each distribution using unbiased cross-validation (UCV) [20]. The prior probability was estimated as the proportion of genes whose observed absolute differential activity scores met or exceeded the pooled mean of absolute permutation scores.

Discussion

We developed a novel computational framework, iProDNet, for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules under heterogeneous biological conditions. Unlike existing differential network approaches that primarily focus on changes in differential correlations or edge-level rewiring, iProDNet integrates various regulatory activity and network topologies into a unified network-aware gene activity score. Specifically, regulatory effects were combined with multiple topological characteristics allowing the proposed framework to capture both functional activity and structural importance of genes within gene regulatory networks. This network-aware representation enables a more comprehensive characterization of molecular rewiring associated with disease phenotypes.

Extensive Monte Carlo simulations demonstrated the practical utility of the proposed framework. Across diverse cancer-related pathways and network dimensions, iProDNet consistently outperformed existing approaches. Application of iProDNet to severe COVID19 transcriptomic data identified distinct rewired subnetworks enriched for translational regulation and interferon-mediated antiviral responses. The proposed framework additionally prioritized key genes, including B2M, ISG15, OASL, IRF1, and multiple ribosomal proteins, which have previously been implicated in COVID19 severity and antiviral immunity. These findings demonstrate the capacity of iProDNet t identify disease-associated regulatory modules and biologically meaningful candidate markers from complex gene regulatory networks. Overall, iProDNet provides a statistically robust and biologically interpretable framework for identifying phenotype-specific regulatory modules and prioritizing key regulatory genes.

We anticipate that iProDNet will be a valuable tool for uncovering disease-associated regulatory mechanisms and guiding the discovery of therapeutic targets across various complex diseases.

The thresholds used to retain the top 10% of edges and communities containing >20 genes may affect the resulting network structure and the number and composition of the subnetworks identified. Accordingly, the findings reported in this study should be interpreted in the context of these prespecified criteria. Establishing objective and data-driven criteria for network sparsification and subnetwork selection remains an important direction for future research.
