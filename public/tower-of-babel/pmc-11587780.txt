Transcriptomic analysis reveals differential transcriptional regulation underlying Citrus Bacterial Canker (CBC) tolerance in Citrus sinensis
Authors: Zhigang Ouyang, Xinyou Wang, Xi Peng, Leijian Zhong, Wei Zeng, Tongqi Huang, Ruimin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The sustainable development of the citrus industry is greatly affected by citrus canker, an important bacterial disease. To explore the transcriptional regulatory mechanism of citrus resistance to canker disease, this study used the susceptible Citrus sinensis cv. ‘Newhall’ and its citrus canker-resistant bud mutation variety ‘Longhuitian’ (LHT) as materials. Through analysing the variances in leaf phenotypes between Newhall and LHT, as well as the variations in their transcriptional expression under Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri (Xcc) inoculation, our study concluded that LHT displays markedly greater resistance to Xcc compared to Newhall. Additionally, the spongy parenchyma of LHT leaves is significantly thicker than that of Newhall, and the stomatal number is significantly higher in LHT leaves, while the length and width of individual stomata in LHT leaves are significantly smaller than those in Newhall. RNA-seq analysis indicates that the differentially expressed genes between LHT and Newhall are involved in biotic stress-related biological processes, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, as well as phytohormone signalling pathways. Furthermore, significant differences were observed in reactive oxygen metabolism and phenylalanine metabolism pathways. The findings of our study provide data support for a deeper understanding of the citrus-Xcc interactions and offer valuable clues for unravelling citrus resistance to citrus canker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11070-y.

Introduction

Citrus Bacterial Canker (CBC) is a severe citrus disease found globally, causing substantial agricultural losses [1–3]. It manifests as raised corky lesions on citrus tree fruits, leaves, and stems, primarily attributed to Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri (Xcc) bacterial infection eliciting a hypersensitive response leading to necrosis and canker development [4, 5]. Symptoms in Citrus sinensis include corky cankers on fruit and leaves, potentially resulting in defoliation, dieback, and fruit loss, impacting crop yield and market value [6]. Control strategies for CBC involve cultural, chemical, and biological methods, including pruning infected parts [7], copper-based sprays [8], and biocontrol agents like Bacillus [9] and Pseudomonas spp [10]. Research is ongoing to develop genetically resistant C. sinensis varieties against CBC through genetic engineering, aiming to disable pathogen-exploited genes using clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9 technology [11].

Xcc contains the pathogenicity effector PthA4, a transcription activator-like effector that is delivered through the type III secretion system. PthA4 triggers the activation of the susceptibility gene CsLOB1(Cs_ont_7g005220) by attaching to particular cis acting elements in its promoter region [12]. This interaction ultimately leads to the up-regulation of downstream genes, resulting in the characteristic canker symptoms such as hypertrophy and hyperplasia [1]. Utilizing CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome editing, modifications to the promoter or coding region of CsLOB1 have demonstrated the ability to confer resistance to Xcc in citrus plants [12, 13]. The study of RNA transcripts under specific conditions, known as transcriptomics, provides valuable insights into plant gene expression patterns when faced with disease challenges [14]. Transcriptome analysis revealed that CsLOB1 exerts a positive regulatory influence on several biological processes including cell wall degradation, cell wall modification, cytokinin metabolism, cell organization, cell division, and the cell cycle [13, 15]. Moreover, through chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing experiments, 565 genes were found to be directly regulated by CsLOB1 [13]. Integration of RNA-seq and chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) data has shown that CsLOB1 directly activates genes involved in cell wall remodelling, cytokinin, and brassinosteroid hormone metabolic pathways [13]. Furthermore, CsLOB1 has been demonstrated to directly impact the promoter region of a zinc finger C3HC4-type RING finger gene, ultimately causing the onset of canker symptoms [2].

In order to ensure food security, it is imperative to implement robust disease resistance strategies due to the significant threat that plant diseases pose to agriculture worldwide [16]. Developing disease resistance strategies relies heavily on understanding plant immunity mechanisms and defense responses against pathogens [17, 18]. Transcriptional regulation, governed by transcription factors (TFs) like WRKY, MYB, and NAC, plays a vital role in activating defense-related genes during pathogen attacks [19]. The merging of transcriptomics and investigations into transcriptional regulation provides a complete perspective on the regulatory networks that govern plant disease resistance mechanisms [20, 21]. This can aid in the identification of the crucial genes and pathways involved in defense responses. For example, through RNA-seq analysis and subsequent weighted gene co-expression network analysis, a number of key regulatory transcription factors, such as ethylene-responsive transcription factor 9, were discovered to be involved in citrus-Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus interaction [22].

Crop productivity faces considerable challenges from plant viruses, fungi, bacteria, and nematodes, underscoring the necessity of disease resistance in breeding programs [23]. The development of disease-resistant, high-yielding crop varieties through plant breeding is essential in addressing the various pathogens that endanger global food production [24, 25]. The tolerance germplasm, which highlights natural resistance traits, is a valuable asset for improving plant resilience [26, 27]. In a sweet orange orchard located in Nankang district, Ganzhou city, China, we and our collaborators discovered a stable bud mutation of C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’, characterized by altered leaf phenotypes. Furthermore, both the leaves and fruits exhibited enhanced resistance to CBC in the field. This bud mutation, named ‘Longhuitian’ (abbreviated as LHT hereafter) showed stable biological traits after multiple grafting events. However, Continuous field observations over multiple years have shown that there is no significant difference between Newhall and LHT in terms of plant growth, fruit characteristics, quality, and production (Figure S1).

In this study, we conducted an indoor evaluation to determine the resistance of Newhall and LHT to Xcc. Furthermore, we conducted measurements on the length-to-width ratio and stomatal density of their leaves. Utilizing RNA-seq, we analysed the local expression variations between Newhall and LHT, and identifying genes that showed differential expression at 24 and 48 h following Xcc inoculation. Through gene enrichment analysis, a comparison was made between the metabolic pathways and biological processes related to the differentially expressed genes in Newhall and LHT. In addition, we investigated the changes in hormone signal transduction and secondary metabolism processes between Newhall and LHT. Moreover, we explored the differences in gene expression related to reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism and phenylalanine (PAL) metabolism pathways upon Xcc infection in Newhall and LHT. Additionally, we confirmed the RNA-seq data through RT-qPCR. The findings of this study contribute valuable information towards improving our understanding of citrus resistance to CBC and shed light on the transcriptional response of citrus to Xcc infection.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Two-year old grafted C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ and ‘LHT’ were cultivated in the plant artificial climate cultivation room at Gannan Normal University, with cultivation conditions of 28℃ ± 2℃, air humidity of 55% ± 5%, and a light cycle of 14 h of light and 10 h of darkness. C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ is a bud mutation of the well-known ‘Washington’ navel oranges, and ‘LHT’ is a bud mutation of ‘Newhall’. The abbreviation ‘LHT’ stands for ‘Longhuitian’, a location in Ganzhou city, China.

Xcc inoculation and phenotypic observation

To determine the resistance of Newhall and LHT to Xcc inoculation, 12 fully expanded tender leaves for each cultivar were carefully selected, and a wound was created on the abaxial surface of the leaves using a sterile 1 ml syringe needle. The Xcc strain, as previously mentioned, was employed for inoculation [28]. The Xcc cultivation was suspended in ddH2O at a concentration of 5 × 104 colony-forming unit (cfu)/ml. Then, 200 µl of this suspension was injected into the abaxial side of the leaves. Following Xcc injection, the plants were placed in a growth chamber that maintained a temperature of 28 °C, 80% humidity, and a 12-hour light cycle. Disease symptoms on the plant leaves were documented using a digital camera. The procedure for quantifying Xcc proliferation in the leaves was as follows: A leaf with a diameter of 6 mm was combined with 0.2 ml of ddH2O and homogenized. The mixture was then briefly centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. This supernatant was diluted ten-fold before determining the Xcc count using a bacterial counting chamber under a microscope. The Xcc inoculation experiments were conducted with three biological replicates.

For paraffin section, leaves were cut into slices and fixed with formalin-aceto-alcohol, followed by washing, dehydration, embedding, sectioning, staining with Safranin O/Fast Green, and finally observed under a Leica DM300 Microscope (Leica, NY, United States) [29]. The thickness of the upper epidermis, palisade tissue, spongy parenchyma, and lower epidermis in the leaves of Newhall and LHT were measured using ImageJ v1 (https://imagej.net/ij/). For SEM observation, leaves were cut into 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 pieces, then immediately placed in 2% glutaraldehyde for fixation. After another round of rinsing with PBS buffer, the leaves underwent dehydration using a series of ethanol concentrations, followed by immersion in isoamyl acetate. The samples were dried using critical point CO2, coated with gold, and observed using a scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) [30]. The stomatal density of Newhall and LHT leaves was manually counted, while the stomatal dimensions were measured using ImageJ v1.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

The RNA-seq samples utilized either Xcc-free or Xcc-infected leaf samples from C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ and ‘LHT’. Total RNA was isolated with the Omega Plant RNA kit (Omega Bio-Tek, China) and the quality of the total RNA was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, MA, United States), and Agilent 2100 (Agilent, CA, United States). Next, mRNA was isolated with Oligo(dT) biotin magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation and priming of the mRNA. Subsequently, the first and second strand cDNA were synthesized, with end repair and sequencing adapter ligation. The purified ligation products were then PCR amplified to generate RNA-seq libraries. The generated RNA-seq libraries were then sequenced using MGISEQ-200 (BGI, China).

RNA-seq analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, raw reads were first subjected to quality control using fastp to obtain clean reads by filtering out low-quality data [31]. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 software, with the Hisat2 parameters set to “-rna-strandness RF” and default settings [32]. The reference genome of C. sinensis was obtained from the CPBD database (Version 3.0) [33]. Based on the HISAT2 alignment results, transcript reconstruction was performed using StringTie, and gene expression levels in each sample were calculated using RSEM [34]. The raw count value of all genes in C. sinensis samples was employed for further investigation. DESeq2 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples, with significant differentially expressed genes selected based on a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.05 and |log2(Fold change)| greater than 1 [35]. In this research, three biological replicates were employed for every sample. The DEGs following Xcc infection at 24–48 h were compared with control samples, as well as between 48 h and 24 h, separately for Newhall and LHT. Moreover, DEGs between Newhall and LHT at the identical infection time point were determined. For heatmap visualization, the sequencing data for each sample was normalized using Counts Per Million (CPM). The expression of CsLOB1 was examined in samples and displayed using Pheatmap (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html). Moreover, the |log2(Fold change)| and FDR for CsLOB1 expression between control and each Xcc infection stage in Newhall and LHT were presented.

Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analysis

The ‘gogseasenior’ package in OmicShare database (www.omicshare.com) was utilized to perform GO enrichment analysis of DEGs, with default parameters. The enriched GO terms that were found to be significant were then visualized using REVIGO and Cytoscape [36]. GO enrichment was also conducted using the ‘simplifyEnrichment’ package in R software for specific DEGs [37]. Furthermore, the KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs was carried out using the ‘pathwaygseasenior’ package within the OmicShare database (www.omicshare.com), with the default parameters.

MapMan analysis

In order to gain a deeper insight into the biological functions of the DEGs, MapMan (v3.6.0) was utilized to categorize and display the DEGs onto specific metabolic pathways or processes [38]. Firstly, all proteins encoded by the C. sinensis were annotated using Mercator4 online [39]. The annotation file was used as the mapping file for MapMan analysis. We selected DEGs related to biotic stress for presentation because these genes are the focus of our research. In a previous study, ROS related genes in C. sinensis were identified [40], while genes related to PAL metabolism were identified in the annotated file by Mercator4 with the BINCODE name ‘9.2’.

RT-qPCR analysis

In order to validate the reliability of RNA-seq analysis, we performed RT-qPCR validation. Total RNA was extracted using the same method as in the RNA-seq analysis. Subsequently, the total RNA was reverse transcribed to generate the first cDNA using EasyScript® First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Transgen, China). Six randomly selected DEGs were chosen for RT-qPCR, with specific primers listed in Table S1. The StepOnePlus™ fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (ABI, CA, United States) was used to carry out the qPCR experiments, with the citrus glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase gene (Cs_ont_2g025950) employed as the internal control. The relative expression profiles of selected genes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method [41]. In this study, the relative expression of candidate genes was compared to the control (leaves that were not infected with Xcc) for Newhall and LHT, respectively. Additionally, in order to facilitate visualization and comparison with the RNA-Seq data, we convert the relative expression values to the base 2 logarithm.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v25 software is used for conducting significance analysis on experimental data. A t-test with a P value less than 0.05 is considered significant, whereas a t-test with a P value less than 0.01 is considered highly significant.

Results

The bud mutation of C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ LHT, showed a high tolerance to CBC

We and our collaborators discovered a bud mutation branch on the C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ plant in the orchard, which showed stable biological traits after multiple grafting events and named as LHT. The leaves of LHT showed significant changes in characteristics, and LHT exhibited resistance to citrus canker in the field. When inoculated with Xcc in the lab, LHT showed a significantly reduced diseased area compared to Newhall (Fig. 1A). The number of bacteria in Xcc inoculated leaves of LHT were significantly less than in Newhall leaves (Fig. 1B). When comparing the leaf anatomical structures of LHT to Newhall, it was found that the thickness of the upper and lower epidermis, as well as the palisade tissues, showed no significant changes. However, the spongy parenchyma of LHT was observed to be thicker than that of Newhall (Fig. 1C and D). Additionally, SEM observation of stomata of mature leaves revealed a significant increase in the number of stomata per unit area in LHT than Newhall (Fig. 1E and F). At the same time, the stomatal length and width of LHT leaves were significantly lower than those of Newhall leaves (Fig. 1G).

DEGs analysis revealed significant transcription variations

In order to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the difference in Xcc resistance between Newhall and LHT, we identified DEGs in Newhall and LHT under conditions absence of Xcc inoculation, as well as 24 h and 48 h after Xcc inoculation, using RNA-seq. Initially, the variations in gene expression of Newhall and LHT post Xcc inoculation in comparison to control were assessed, respectively (Fig. 2A). In Newhall, after 24 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 1,794 up-regulated DEGs and 1,579 down-regulated DEGs compared to the control without inoculation. After 48 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 2,986 up-regulated DEGs and 2,095 down-regulated DEGs compared to the control. When comparing 48 h to 24 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 1,055 up-regulated DEGs and 363 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 2A, Table S2). There were 475 common DEGs between the differential DEGs sets of Newhall (Fig. 2B). In LHT, after 24 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 2,247 up-regulated DEGs and 2,276 down-regulated DEGs compared to the control without inoculation. After 48 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 2,090 up-regulated DEGs and 2,284 down-regulated DEGs compared to the control. When comparing 48 h to 24 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 41 up-regulated DEGs and 26 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 2A). There were 17 common DEGs between the differential DEGs sets of Newhall (Fig. 2C). The DEGs analysis results indicate that the gene expression levels in LHT leaves did not show significant changes between 48 h and 24 h of Xcc infection. Subsequently, we compared the number of DEGs in Newhall and LHT leaves inoculated with Xcc at different time points. Initially, in healthy leaves absence of Xcc inoculation, there were 3,173 up-regulated DEGs and 3,225 down-regulated DEGs in LHT compared to Newhall (Fig. 2D). After 24 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 2,451 up-regulated DEGs and 3,035 down-regulated DEGs in LHT compared to Newhall (Fig. 2D, Table S2). After 48 h of Xcc inoculation, there were 1,855 up-regulated DEGs and 2,893 down-regulated DEGs in LHT compared to Newhall (Fig. 2D). A total of 2,485 common DEGs between the differential DEGs sets of LHT compared with Newhall (Fig. 2E). The noticeable contrast in the quantity of DEGs between Newhall and LHT during different Xcc inoculation stages suggests a significant difference in the transcription patterns of their genes.

GO enrichment analysis revealed DEGs involved in defense response

As we focused on the variation in Xcc resistance between Newhall and LHT, we initiated a GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs absence of Xcc inoculation (Fig. 3). Prior to Xcc inoculation, DEGs in LHT compared to Newhall shows enrichment in multiple stress-related GO terms, such as GO:0050896 (response to stimulus), GO:0009611 (response to wounding), GO:0009605 (response to external stimulus), GO:0006950 (response to stress), GO:0009607 (response to biotic stimulus), GO:0010200 (response to chitin), GO:0006979 (response to oxidative stress), GO:0006952 (defense response), GO:0009719 (response to endogenous stimulus) (Fig. 3A). The enrichment GO terms with a P value less than 0.05 were subsequently visualized using REVIGO (Fig. 3B). The analysis revealed two distinct GO term networks, centred around ‘response to hormone’ and ‘methylglyoxal catabolic process to lactate’ (Fig. 3B). This indicates that phytohormone signalling pathways and lactate metabolic processes may play a role in Xcc resistance.

In order to investigate the candidate genes involved in Xcc resistance in LHT, we analysed the up-regulated gene set in LHT compared to Newhall. Among the up-regulated DEGs, we found a significant number of genes annotated as NB-LRR genes, serine/threonine kinase genes, and genes encoded disease resistance proteins and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinases (MAPKKK) (Figure S2).

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed DEGs involved in secondary metabolites and plant-pathogen interaction

Furthermore, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between Newhall and LHT absence of Xcc inoculation (Figure S3). The KEGG enrichment results revealed that in the absence of Xcc infection, LHT had up to 464 DEGs involved in ‘Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’, 116 DEGs involved in ‘plant-pathogen interaction’, and 755 DEGs involved in ‘metabolic pathways’ (Figure S3). Additionally, it is worth noting that 69 DEGs were involved in ‘Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’, 14 DEGs in ‘Brassinosteroid biosynthesis’, 50 DEGs in the ‘MAPK signalling pathway’, and 85 DEGs in ‘plant hormone signal transduction’ (Figure S3).

Xcc infection induced different biological processes in Newhall and LHT

Additionally, we conducted GO enrichment analysis of DEGs between Newhall and LHT under Xcc inoculation (Figure S4). At the stage of Xcc inoculation for 24 h, the GO enrichment analysis shows that the DEGs are involved in biological processes of GO:0009611 (response to wounding), GO:0006950 (response to stress), GO:0009605 (response to external stimulus), GO:0050896 (response to stimulus), GO:0006952 (defense response), GO:0009607 (response to biotic stimulus), GO:0016491 (oxidoreductase activity), GO:0055114 (oxidation-reduction process), GO:0009617 (response to bacterium), and GO:0042445 (hormone metabolic process) (Figure S4A). Moreover, At the stage of Xcc inoculation for 48 h, the GO enrichment analysis shows that the DEGs are involved in biological processes of GO:0009605 (response to external stimulus), GO:0019748 (secondary metabolic process), GO:0043207 (response to external biotic stimulus), GO:0009607 (response to biotic stimulus), GO:0006952 (defense response), GO:0006950 (response to stress), and GO:0042445 (hormone metabolic process) (Figure S4B).

To uncover more potential Xcc resistance-related genes, we conducted a thorough analysis of 2,485 common DEGs found in the differential DEGs sets of LHT when compared with Newhall (Figs. 2E and 4). Evidently, the 2,485 DEGs are split into two distinct classes, with one class displaying up-regulated expression in LHT and the other class demonstrating up-regulated expression in Newhall (Fig. 4). Interestingly, the most common keywords that appeared in the GO terms of genes up-regulated in LHT or Newhall were ‘process’, ‘response’, ‘development’, ‘regulation’, ‘cell’, ‘homeostasis’, ‘organization’, ‘transport’, and ‘cycle’ (Fig. 4). However, 928 DEGs out of the 2,485 DEGs up-regulated expression in LHT while 1,557 DEGs up-regulated expression in Newhall (Fig. 4). At present, we are unable to completely analyse the function of each DEG, but this finding provides us with some fresh perspectives. For example, with more DEGs up-regulated in Newhall, it prompts us to consider if there are any disease-susceptible genes within this group.

Xcc infection altered different biotic stress responses between Newhall and LHT

In addition, we utilized MapMan to visually display the 2,485 common DEGs found in the differential DEGs sets of LHT when compared with Newhall involved in the biological stress response between Newhall and LHT (Fig. 5). Among the DEGs, 36 were related to hormone signalling, including 7 involved in gibberellic acid signalling, 6 in Auxins signalling, 2 in brassinolide signalling, 5 in abscisic acid signalling, 1 in ethylene signalling, 1 in salicylic acid signalling, 5 in jasmonic acid signalling, and 9 in cytokinin signalling, with varying expression trends (Fig. 5). Furthermore, 31 DEGs were linked to cell wall metabolism, with 13 up-regulated and 18 down-regulated (Fig. 5). Additionally, 31 DEGs were involved in proteolysis, with 7 up-regulated and 24 down-regulated (Fig. 5). There were 11 DEGs encoded heat shock proteins, with 2 up-regulated and 9 down-regulated (Fig. 5). 23 DEGs were associated with redox state, with 9 up-regulated and 14 down-regulated (Fig. 5). Notably, 68 DEGs were identified as transcription factors, including 2 ERF, 3 bZIP, 1 WRKY, and 2 MYB, all of which were down-regulated (Fig. 5). Lastly, 33 DEGs were linked to secondary metabolites, with 10 up-regulated and 23 down-regulated (Fig. 5). To conclude, the biotic stress response related genes of LHT did not exhibit a distinct response to Xcc infection when compared to Newhall.

DEGs involved in ROS and PAL metabolism response to Xcc infection

MapMan analysis indicated various DEGs involved in ROS metabolism and secondary metabolites (Fig. 5). Therefore, we further analysed the expression patterns of ROS metabolism associated genes and PAL metabolism pathway genes in Newhall and LHT during Xcc infection. For 91 ROS metabolism associated genes in the genome of C. sinensis [40]. 36 showed significant differential expression between Newhall and LHT (Fig. 6). Specifically, 9 DEGs were up-regulated in Newhall, while 27 were up-regulated in LHT (Fig. 6). This suggests that the expression levels of ROS metabolism-associated genes are generally higher in LHT than in Newhall, which is a positive indicator for plants to combat external biological stress.

RT-qPCR validation

In order to validate the accuracy of the RNA-seq analysis, we selected 12 DEGs including ROS related and PAL metabolism pathway genes for verification using RT-qPCR method (Fig. 8). The RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of the 12 selected DEGs was generally consistent with the RNA-seq results, but there were some differences (Fig. 8). For example, in the RNA-seq analysis, CsTRX7 had the highest expression in the control samples of Newhall, while in the RT-qPCR results, it had the highest expression in the LHT sample inoculated with Xcc after 24 h (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, Cs4CL15 showed low expression in LHT in both control and Xcc-infected samples during RNA-seq analysis. However, RT-qPCR results revealed that Cs4CL15 was highly expressed in the LHT sample inoculated with Xcc after 24 h (Fig. 8G).

CsLOB1 differentially expressed between Newhall and LHT

After Xcc infection, CsLOB1 exhibited continuous up-regulation in both Newhall and LHT (Figure S5). Notably, CsLOB1 displayed a higher expression level in LHT compared to Newhall, with a log2 fold change value greater than 0 (Figure S5). However, only samples absence of Xcc infection showed significant differential expression based on the set threshold (Figure S5).

Discussion

The presence of citrus canker poses a major challenge to the advancement of the citrus industry [15]. A key focus for promoting the sustainable growth of the citrus industry is the selection of resistant germplasm materials and the breeding of disease-resistant or tolerant varieties. The C. sinensis cv ‘Newhall’ bud mutation, LHT, was identified as having strong resistance to citrus canker, whereas the Newhall variety itself is susceptible to the disease [43]. This finding is fascinating for gaining insight into citrus-Xcc interactions. It is especially noteworthy considering the significant changes observed in the leaf traits and structures of LHT compared to the parent plant, Newhall. Stomata are a vital element of the plant innate immune system, and they are crucial in preventing bacterial invasion [44]. Nevertheless, the relationship between stomatal sizes and the defense against pathogens in most plants remains unclear at present [45]. In this study, it was observed that the stomatal sizes on the disease-resistant LHT leaves were smaller in comparison to Newhall. Nevertheless, there is insufficient evidence to support the claim that smaller stomatal sizes contribute to LHT’s resistance to Xcc. While this phenotype has sparked new hypotheses, additional analysis of stomatal sizes across a wider range of citrus germplasm materials is necessary.

Furthermore, RNA-seq was utilized to reveal the mechanism by which LHT resists Xcc. One important finding from our study was the absence of notable alterations in gene expression levels in LHT leaves following 24 h of Xcc infection. Pathogen invasion often leads to the reprogramming of the transcriptional expression patterns of plants [46, 47]. This implies that LHT might have a resistance mechanism that relies on quick alterations in gene expression, as opposed to susceptible citrus varieties like Newhall. The transcription patterns seen in Newhall and LHT indicate a significant contrast in their reaction to Xcc inoculation. Although Newhall showed a diverse set of DEGs after Xcc inoculation, LHT consistently maintained its transcription profile. This difference implies that LHT may have a more effective or unique array of defense mechanisms against Xcc compared to Newhall. Additionally, a variance in the basal levels of gene expression can be observed between Newhall and LHT. The GO term networks revealed these DEGs centred around ‘response to hormone’ and ‘methylglyoxal catabolic process to lactate’, suggest potential areas of interest for further investigation. Specifically, the term ‘response to hormone’ indicates the phytohormone signalling processes that play a role in citrus-Xcc interactions. Phytohormone signalling transduction is crucial in plant-pathogen interactions [48]. For example, salicylic acid has the ability to trigger the activation of systemic acquired resistance genes, thereby boosting plant defences against pathogens [49]. Additionally, suppressing jasmonic acid pathway has been shown to enhance Arabidopsis resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato strain DC3000 [50]. In our study, it was discovered that Newhall and LHT exhibit numerous DEGs in phytohormone signalling pathways, specifically in gibberellic acid, abscisic acid, jasmonic acid, and cytokinin signalling pathways. The primary role of gibberellic acid and cytokinin is their collaborative regulation of plant growth [51]. Nevertheless, our results suggest that there is a complex interplay of hormones contributing to citrus resistance against Xcc.

In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis shed light on the pathways that might be crucial for the significant Xcc resistance observed in LHT had significantly more DEGs involved in ‘Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’ and ‘Plant-pathogen interaction’. Secondary metabolites are widely recognized for their function in protecting plants against various biotic stresses, such as pathogen invasion [52, 53]. The upregulation of multiple genes associated with secondary metabolite biosynthesis in LHT implies a heightened biochemical defense mechanism against Xcc. Upon analysing the biological processes triggered by Xcc infection in Newhall and LHT, we observed significant disparities in the enriched GO terms. For instance, at 24 h post-inoculation, the DEGs in Newhall and LHT were involved in biological processes like ‘response to wounding’, ‘response to stress’ and ‘defense response’. However, by 48 hours, the DEGs in LHT were more involved in secondary metabolic processes. These discrepancies suggest that defense mechanisms in LHT may be temporally regulated, with a greater reliance on secondary metabolism for Xcc resistance as time progresses. Moreover, the presence of a considerable amount of DEGs linked to plant-pathogen interaction suggests the presence of a unique interaction pattern between LHT and Xcc, which could be a crucial element in the resistance observed [54].

Subsequently, our analysis broadened to cover DEGs involved in ROS metabolism and the PAL pathway. We identified 36 ROS metabolism associated genes showing significant differential expression between Newhall and LHT, with the majority of these genes being up-regulated in LHT. Given that the increased expression of genes related to ROS metabolism is frequently linked to improved defense against biological stress in plants, this discovery is significant [55, 56]. With regards to the PAL metabolism pathway, we found that 19 out of 40 identified genes in C. sinensis showed variations in expression between Newhall and LHT during Xcc infection. Notably, the expression of key enzymes in the PAL pathway, like PAL and CHS, varied not only between the resistant and susceptible cultivars but also within each cultivar itself depending on the gene concerned. PAL is engaged in the response of plants to pathogen invasion [57]. CHS, a crucial enzyme in the production of plant flavonoids, has been found to be essential in plant defense against pathogens [58]. Our research sheds light on the role of ROS and PAL metabolism in the citrus-Xcc interaction.

Furthermore, results indicated that CsLOB1 exhibited noticeable up-regulation in both Newhall and LHT under the influence of Xcc infection. The differential expression of CsLOB1 in response to Xcc infection provides fascinating insights into the operational mechanisms of this gene. Since a higher expression level was detected in LHT compared to Newhall, we speculate that there may be other mechanisms in LHT to resist Xcc infection, considering CsLOB1 is a susceptibility gene [1]. This information is paramount in the ongoing quest to understand the pathogenic mechanisms of Xcc and to innovate countermeasures. Future studies should concentrate on investigating how LHT exhibited resistance to Xcc through a signalling pathway that is independent of CsLOB1.

Conclusion

To sum up, our analysis of the RNA-seq data from the Xcc-susceptible variety Newhall and its resistant bud mutation variety LHT revealed a plethora of DEGs that play roles in multiple biological processes and KEGG pathways. These DEGs also impact ROS metabolism and PAL metabolism, as well as phytohormone signalling pathways, particularly gibberellic acid, abscisic acid, jasmonic acid, and cytokinin signalling pathways. Our study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms of citrus resistance to Xcc, providing essential data for the development of citrus varieties resistant to Citrus canker.
