Chylous pericardial tamponade following postoperative chylothorax after right upper lobectomy: a case report of a rare entity
Authors: Susanne Boutellier, Adrian Zehnder, Hans Gelpke, Arash Najafi, Philipp Karl Buehler, Benedikt Florian Scherr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative chylothorax is a known but uncommon complication of lung cancer surgery. Progression to chylous pericardial effusion and tamponade is exceedingly rare but can be rapidly fatal if not recognized and treated promptly.Case presentationA 72-year-old man underwent right upper lobectomy with bronchus-sleeve resection and systematic mediastinal lymphadenectomy for squamous cell carcinoma. In the early postoperative course, a high-output chylothorax was diagnosed. On postoperative day (POD) 4, he developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with hemodynamic instability, requiring brief ICU admission for cardioversion. On POD 6, he suddenly deteriorated with obstructive shock with hypotension, tachycardia, pronounced mottled skin extending to the abdomen, and decreased level of consciousness. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Emergency pericardiocentesis drained 800 mL of milky fluid with high triglycerides, consistent with chylopericardium, and resulted in immediate hemodynamic stabilization. Interventional radiology attempted bilateral intranodal lymphangiography and thoracic duct embolization via lymphatic, venous and percutaneous routes; despite partial opacification of lymphatic channels, catheterization and embolization was unsuccessful. On POD 7, surgical re-thoracotomy with thoracic duct ligation above the diaphragm and lymphatic fistula closure was performed. The pericardial drain was removed on POD 8, echocardiography confirmed no recurrence, and the patient recovered uneventfully.DiscussionChylous pericardial tamponade is extremely rare but life-threatening. Previous reports describe a spectrum from successful conservative therapy to surgical interventions, with at least one fatal outcome despite drainage (Fukumoto et al. Surg Case Rep. 11:87; 2025). Our case highlights three points: (1) transthoracic echocardiography is indispensable for rapid diagnosis of tamponade in unstable postoperative patients; (2) interventional radiology, although attempted, was unsuccessful and should not delay definitive treatment; (3) surgical thoracic duct ligation and pericardial drainage remain the most reliable interventions.ConclusionChylous pericardial tamponade should be considered in patients with postoperative chylothorax who deteriorate hemodynamically. Rapid echocardiography, emergency pericardial drainage, and timely surgical management are key to survival.Keywords: Chylopericardium, Pericardial tamponade, Chylothorax, Lobectomy, Thoracic duct ligation

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man with a history of squamous cell carcinoma of the right upper lobe (cT2a cN0, cM0, stage IB) underwent elective right upper lobectomy with bronchus-sleeve resection and systematic lymphadenectomy (stations 2R, 4R, 7, 8, 10, 11) after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy.

In the immediate postoperative course, his right pleural drain produced increasing amounts of milky fluid, and biochemical analysis confirmed a chylothorax with triglycerides as high as 6.1 mmol/l (540 mg/dl). Conservative measures (nil per os, parenteral nutrition, octreotide) were initiated. Despite this, drain output remained high (> 2,000 mL/day).

On POD 4, he developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response resulting in hemodynamic instability. He was transferred to the ICU, underwent successful cardioversion, and was subsequently hemodynamically stable.

It was possible to detect the underlying lymphatic-pleural communication with an MRI on POD 5 (Fig. 1).

Proof of the lymphatic-pleural connection (see yellow arrows) in the coronal MRI section just above the level of the diaphragm

On POD 6, the patient`s condition deteriorated abruptly. On ICU admission, he presented with obstructive shock presenting hypotensive with systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg, tachycardia, cool peripheries, and pronounced mottling of the skin extending up to the abdomen. His vigilance was reduced, indicating cerebral hypoperfusion. Heart sounds were markedly muffled.

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography immediately revealed a large circumferential pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of the right atrium and ventricle, confirming tamponade physiology (Fig. 3).

The patient after admission to the intensive care unit and following insertion of a pericardial drain on POD 6, which evacuated approximately 800 ml of chylous fluid, verified by high triglycerides and cholesterin

Urgent pericardiocentesis was performed via a subxiphoid approach. Approximately 800 mL of pressurized milky fluid could be drained (Fig. 2), laboratory analysis confirmed the fluid was chylous with triglyceride levels of 2.7 mmol/l (239 mg/dl). Hemodynamic status stabilized rapidly: blood pressure normalized, lactate levels cleared, and norepinephrine support was discontinued.

Small pericardial effusion (yellow arrows) without hemodynamic compromise on coronal CT scan obtained on postoperative day 1

The same day, interventional radiology was consulted. Bilateral intranodal lymphangiography with iodized oil (Lipiodol) was performed. Partial opacification of lymphatic channels could be performed, but due to the small lymph node size, Lipiodol reached the lymphatics only on the right side and in minimal amounts. Also, attempts to catheterize and embolize the thoracic duct via a transvenous approach at the junction of the internal jugular and subclavian veins were unsuccessful because of technical difficulties. A percutaneous puncture attempt of the thoracic duct also failed due to extravasation. The procedure was aborted after unsuccessful attempts at catheterization.

Due to persistent leakage and high risk of recurrence, surgical re-thoracotomy was undertaken on POD 7. During exploration of the lymphadenectomy sites at stations 4R (right lower paratracheal lymph nodes) and 7 (subcarinal lymph nodes with closest proximity to the pericardium as possible site of entrance), a marked chylous effusion was observed. Due to the continuous and significant outflow of chyle, the decision was made to ligate the thoracic duct immediately at the superior border of the diaphragm. Following dissection along the azygos vein above the diaphragm, the dissection was extended to the aorta, allowing identification, encirclement, and ligation of the thoracic duct, which resulted in a marked reduction of fluid leakage at stations 4R and 7, leaking tributaries were clipped. A pericardial window was created to prevent future tamponade.

Postoperatively, drainage decreased and fluid cleared from chylous to serous. On POD 8, the pericardial drain was removed. Serial echocardiography confirmed no recurrent effusion. The patient remained stable, was transferred to the ward, and continued to recover without further complications.

Discussion

Chylous pericardial effusion progressing to tamponade is an exceptionally rare but highly threatening complication after lung surgery. A literature review of 95 articles published between 1946 and May 2021 identified 98 cases of chylopericardium, with 40% classified as secondary, and cardiac tamponade secondary to chylopericardial effusion occurring in 38% of cases [1]. Chylopericardium arises in most cases from disruption or obstruction of the thoracic duct, leading to abnormal communication with pericardial lymphatics or impaired chyle drainage. Etiologies include iatrogenic injury following surgery or radiotherapy, mediastinal neoplasms (e.g., lymphoma, signet ring carcinoma), central venous thrombosis, traumatic lesions (blunt, penetrating), congenital lymphatic abnormalities (e.g. lymphangiomatosis), inflammatory (e.g., acute pancreatitis, Behçet syndrome) or infectious diseases (e.g., tuberculosis) or idiopathic. The clinical presentation can be dramatic, as in our patient who developed acute obstructive shock with hypotension, mottling, and reduced vigilance. The pathophysiology in this case was hypothesized to be a high-flow lymphatic leak from injured tributaries of the thoracic duct during mediastinal lymphadenectomy, with subsequent drainage into the pericardium through small defects or inflammatory adhesions. Once sufficient fluid accumulates, pericardial pressure rises, right-sided chambers collapse and cardiac filling is impaired, leading to obstructive shock. This highlights the indispensable role of bedside transthoracic echocardiography, which in our patient immediately identified tamponade physiology and guided life-saving drainage.

When reviewing the literature, the spectrum of presentations and outcomes becomes clear. Mizushima and colleagues in 1996 described one of the earliest cases after a right upper lobectomy. Their patient developed a chylous pericardial effusion that resolved gradually with dietary modification alone [2]. This report shows that in selected cases with lower leak volume and without hemodynamic compromise, conservative management can succeed. Similarly, Wait and coworkers in 2013 reported a case after lung transplantation where pericardial drainage and a strict fat-free diet were sufficient to control the effusion [3]. Both of these cases underline that if the patient remains stable, conservative measures may be attempted, although they require close monitoring.

In contrast, several later reports illustrate that surgical treatment is often necessary. Yang et al. in 2017 described a patient who developed a massive chylous pericardial effusion after lobectomy. Despite initial pericardial drainage, the leak persisted and shifted between pleural and pericardial spaces. Definitive resolution was achieved only after thoracic duct ligation and creation of a pericardial fenestration [4]. Jeon and Kim in 2020 reported a similar presentation after a single-port VATS lobectomy. Their patient presented with acute tamponade about one month postoperatively, required emergency pericardiocentesis, and was cured after thoracic duct ligation the following day [5]. Both of these reports highlight that while pericardiocentesis is vital to stabilize the patient, it is rarely sufficient, and surgical duct ligation is frequently required to definitively halt the leak.

More recently, Zhang and colleagues published a case of chylopericardial tamponade after lobectomy with systematic lymphadenectomy in 2023. Their patient deteriorated on POD 6, with transthoracic echocardiography confirming tamponade. As in our case, surgical revision with thoracic duct ligation and pericardial fenestration was curative. The authors emphasized that transthoracic echocardiography was superior to chest radiography, which had not revealed the effusion despite fulminant tamponade [6]. This aligns closely with our own experience, underlining the diagnostic value of ultrasound at the bedside.

A sobering perspective is provided by Fukumoto et al. in 2025, who reported the first fatal outcome from this complication. Their patient, who had a persistent chylothorax after lobectomy, was awaiting surgery when he suddenly developed cardiac arrest due to tamponade. Despite prolonged resuscitation and emergency pericardiocentesis, the patient succumbed to multi-organ failure [7]. This report underscores the lethality of delayed recognition and the narrow therapeutic window.

In summary, Chylopericardial tamponade is a rare but life-threatening complication of chylopericardium, resulting from rapid or large-volume accumulation of chyle in the pericardial space, leading to increased intrapericardial pressure and impaired cardiac filling. Etiologies include thoracic duct injury, malignancy, central venous thrombosis, or trauma. Clinically, patients present with signs of tamponade such as dyspnea, hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure, tachycardia, and pulsus paradoxus. Diagnosis is made via echocardiography and pericardiocentesis, revealing milky fluid with high triglyceride levels and lymphocytic predominance. Emergent pericardial drainage is essential for hemodynamic stabilization, followed by targeted therapy addressing the underlying cause and reduction of chyle flow through dietary modification or total parenteral nutrition. Adjunctive therapy with octreotide may be considered. In cases of persistent effusion or recurrent tamponade, surgical thoracic duct ligation and creation of a pericardial window are indicated.

Placed in this context, our case highlights two aspects. First, the role of interventional radiology, which is rarely discussed in previous reports. In our patient, lymphangiography provided useful anatomical information but attempts at thoracic duct embolization failed. This highlights both the potential value and the limitations of minimally invasive approaches. They may be valuable in stable patients but are unreliable in the acute tamponade setting and should not delay surgery. Second, the importance of a structured management pathway becomes evident. Echocardiography must be performed early in any unstable postoperative thoracic patient, pericardiocentesis should be undertaken without delay to stabilize hemodynamics, and surgical ligation of the thoracic duct remains the most reliable definitive therapy.

In summary, the literature shows that chylous pericardial effusion after lung surgery can occasionally resolve with conservative measures, but more often requires surgical management, and in rare cases may be fatal. Our case adds to the body of evidence by showing both the limits of interventional radiology and the value of early echocardiography. For thoracic surgeons and intensivists, awareness of this rare entity and readiness to act decisively are essential to prevent catastrophic outcomes.

Conclusion

Chylous pericardial tamponade is a rare but catastrophic complication after lung resection. Clinicians must remain vigilant in patients with postoperative chylothorax who develop hemodynamic instability. Point-of-care echocardiography is invaluable for rapid diagnosis. Emergency pericardiocentesis provides immediate stabilization, but surgical thoracic duct ligation and pericardial fenestration are often necessary for definitive cure. Interventional radiology may complement diagnosis but has limited therapeutic success in this acute scenario.
