Brain structural MRI changes before and after 6 months of preventive treatment with erenumab and onabotulinumtoxinA in migraine
Authors: Joana Rosell-Mirmi, Edoardo Caronna, Víctor J Gallardo, Deborah Pareto, Alicia Alpuente, Marta Torres-Ferrús, Eulalia Giné-Ciprés, Àlex Rovira, Patricia Pozo-Rosich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStructural neuroimaging findings in migraine remain heterogeneous, and the impact of preventive treatments such as erenumab and onabotulinumtoxinA (OnabotA) on brain plasticity is poorly understood. We aimed to comprehensively characterize brain morphology, including less explored regions such as the cerebellum and corpus callosum (CC), compare people with migraine with healthy controls (HC), identify baseline predictors of treatment response, and evaluate longitudinal structural trajectories following preventive therapy.MethodsThis prospective study included participants with migraine with ≥ 8 headache days/month eligible for erenumab or OnabotA, together with HC. T1-weighted MRI was acquired on 3T MRI scanners at baseline (M0) in all participants and repeated at 6 months (M6) in eligible participants with migraine. Regions of interest (ROIs) were segmented, and cortical thickness (CTh), cortical surface area (SA) and brain volume values were extracted using FastSurfer. Data were analysed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM). Response was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly headache days at M6.ResultsWe included 189 participants with migraine and 63 HC; 87.9% were female (222/252), and age was similar between groups (adjusted-p = 0.627). Compared with HC, individuals with migraine showed larger volumes in the mid-posterior (adjusted-p = 0.001) and central CC segments (adjusted-p = 0.030). 2) At baseline, non-responders (NR) showed higher CTh in the left parahippocampal gyrus than responders (R) (adjusted-p = 0.008). In addition, a significant response-by-treatment interaction was observed in the bilateral caudate volume: post-hoc contrast showed higher caudate volumes in OnabotA responders than in OnabotA NR (adjusted-p = 0.041 for both hemispheres), whereas no such difference was obsvered with erenumab. Longitudinally, several time-by-treatment interactions indicated different structural trajectories between treatments. In most of these regions, post-hoc contrasts showed lower M6 values in the erenumab group, including SA in the left fusiform, left pericalcarine, left rostral anterior cingulate, and right lingual regions, and volume in the bilateral cerebellar cortex, right hippocampus, and left amygdala. The left choroid plexus showed an opposite pattern, with increased volume in the erenumab group. Comparable longitudinal changes were not observed in the OnabotA group.ConclusionOur results identify CC alterations and baseline parahippocampal and caudate morphometry as potential markers of migraine and treatment response. Introduction

Structural brain morphology detected by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be measured in terms of volume, cortical thickness (CTh), or cortical surface area (SA) within predefined regions of interest (ROIs) [1]. Several studies have reported structural differences in people with migraine, but no reliable neuroimaging biomarkers for migraine diagnosis have yet been identified [2]. The magnitude of these differences compared with healthy controls (HC) is generally small [3–6], and methodological differences across studies limit reproducibility, interpretation, and comparability [7, 8].

Another relevant field of neuroimaging research concerns the impact of migraine preventive therapies on brain structure. The preventive treatment arsenal for migraine has expanded from non-specific oral medications to onabotulinumtoxinA (OnabotA) and migraine-specific therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway [9]. Clinical data on erenumab [10, 11] and OnabotA [12] suggest that earlier initiation of preventive therapy may improve patient outcomes, with higher response rates observed in individuals with fewer monthly headache days at baseline. Identifying structural brain features associated with migraine, disease progression, and treatment response may help to better understand treatment mechanisms and support the rationale for earlier intervention [13].

Outlining the structural characteristics of the migraine brain is essential, as functional alterations, which are more prominent in migraine, are likely to be underpinned at least partly by structural variability [14]. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that people with migraine show specific structural brain differences compared with HC, that baseline structural features may distinguish responders from non-responders to established preventive treatments, and that some structural features may change over time following preventive therapy.

Thus, the objectives of this study were to: (1) characterize brain morphology in people with migraine compared with HC; (2) compare baseline morphology between responders and non-responders after six months of preventive treatment with erenumab or OnabotA; and (3) examine pre–post treatment brain changes and evaluate whether longitudinal trajectories of CTh, SA, and subcortical volumes differed between erenumab- and OnabotA-treated participants.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is an analysis of prospectively collected morphological neuroimaging data from participants included in the Migraine Adaptive Brain (MAB) Registry. The MAB Registry is an ongoing multimodal registry that includes clinical, molecular, neuroimaging, and neurophysiological data from people with migraine and HC consecutively recruited since 2018 at Vall d´Hebron University Hospital. Data are collected prospectively using standardized procedures. Some participants included in the MAB Registry were also enrolled in phase IV clinical trials conducted consecutively in our center since 2020. These trials are embedded in the MAB Registry and follow the standardized assessments and data-collection procedures of the registry. HC with a history of migraine in a first-degree relative were excluded from the registry.

From the MAB Registry, we selected right-handed adult participants with at least one baseline brain MRI available. This cohort included people with migraine and HC, balanced for age and sex, recruited between March 2020 and January 2024. Participants with migraine were included in phase IV trials with preplanned MRI assessments and an interventional phase with preventive treatments: erenumab (EudraCT 2019-002224-32 and REEC ID 2020-003650-77) or OnabotA (REEC ID 2020-002639-31). Participants with migraine had a diagnosis of chronic migraine (CM) or high-frequency episodic migraine (HFEM; ≥8 headache days/month) according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders criteria, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [15], had failed at least two migraine preventive treatments, and were not taking preventive medication at inclusion. We excluded participants who: (1) had relevant neurological or psychiatric comorbidities or other chronic pain syndromes; (2) were taking drugs with central nervous system effects (antidepressants, neuroleptics, benzodiazepines, antiepileptic, etc.) or had a history of substance or drug abuse; (3) had incidental relevant structural abnormalities on brain MRI; or (4) were breastfeeding, pregnant, or planning to become pregnant during the study period.

Study procedures

As part of the standardization of the MAB Registry and the embedded phase IV trials, both participants with migraine and HC were assessed at screening by a neurologist specialized in headache. To further confirm the diagnosis, all participants completed a daily electronic headache diary (eDiary) for at least one month before MRI acquisition. Participants with migraine also completed eDiaries throughout the study period to monitor headache frequency, including monthly headache days (MHD), monthly migraine days (MMD), and monthly acute medication days (MAMD) (Fig. 1). Patient-reported outcomes measures (PROMs) were also assessed, as detailed in the Supplementary Material (Additional file 1).

Study workflow. Participants completed a daily electronic headache diary (eDiary) for at least 1 month before MRI acquisition. M0 = baseline; M6 = 6-month follow-up

A baseline brain MRI was performed in all participants before treatment initiation. Participants with migraine received either subcutaneous erenumab 140 mg monthly or OnabotA 195U quarterly according to the PREEMPT protocol [16]. Because individuals with migraine were consecutively recruited from our clinic, treatment assignment corresponded to the phase IV trial that was actively enrolling participants at the time of inclusion in the MAB Registry. The first treatment dose was administered after the baseline MRI (month 0, M0). Follow-up visits were performed at 3 months (M3) and 6 months (M6), when headache outcomes and side effects were assessed. No changes in preventive medications were allowed throughout the study. In participants with migraine, a second brain MRI was performed at M6 (mean follow-up time = 179.7 ± 27.3 days), except for participants belonging to one of the two erenumab trials (REEC ID 2020-003650-77), in which a 6-month follow-up MRI was not included in the study design.

MRI acquisition and image processing

MRI acquisitions were performed on two 3.0-T scanners: MAGNETOM Tim Trio and MAGNETOM Prisma (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The Tim Trio scanner was equipped with a 32-channel phased-array head coil, whereas the Prisma used a 64-channel head–neck coil. The 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence used for morphometric processing was acquired with identical parameters on both scanners: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, 192 slices, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. A 3D T2-weighted FLAIR sequence was also acquired, although acquisition parameters differed between scanners. On the Tim Trio scanner, 3D FLAIR parameters were TR = 5000 ms, TE = 3.94 ms, TI = 1800 ms, flip angle = 120°, FOV = 240 × 256 mm, 176 slices, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. On the Prisma scanner, 3D FLAIR parameters were TR = 7000 ms, TE = 4.1 ms, TI = 2050 ms, flip angle = 120°, FOV = 240 × 256 mm, 176 slices, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm.

Image processing was performed using the FastSurfer (v1.0) pipeline [17], a deep learning-based neuroimaging pipeline developed to emulate FreeSurfer’s recon-all outputs with high computational efficiency. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Material (Additional file 1 - MRI acquisition and image processing Section). The pipeline provides automated processing and ROI-based segmentation based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas [18]. For the 31 bilateral cortical regions, we extracted CTh and SA. Volumetric measures were obtained for subcortical structures, including the thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, choroid plexus, and the cerebellar cortex. Although the corpus callosum (CC) is a white matter structure, it was included because of its central role in interhemispheric connectivity and its reported sensitivity to migraine-related alterations [19]. Five CC segments were analysed: posterior, mid-posterior, central, mid-anterior, and anterior. All segmentation and co-registration outputs were visually inspected by a trained technician to ensure anatomical accuracy. Only images free of movement artifacts were included. A detailed list of the ROIs included is provided in Supplementary Table 1 (Additional file 1).

Outcomes

Outcomes measures were CTh, SA, and subcortical volumes. These measures were compared: (1) at baseline between participants with migraine and HC, including additional comparisons between HC and migraine subgroups (HFEM, CM, and medication overuse [MO]); (2) at baseline between responders and non-responders, with treatment response defined as ≥ 50% reduction in MHD at M6; and (3) between baseline (M0) and 6 months (M6) in participants with migraine included in the longitudinal assessment, including the evaluation of differential longitudinal trajectories between erenumab and OnabotA.

Statistical analysis

Certain variables were imputed to preserve the data integrity using predictive mean matching (PMM) from the mice package (v.3.16.0). To ensure that findings were not influenced by headache state during MRI, a sensitivity analysis was performed, as described in the Supplementary Material (Additional file 1). Group differences in demographic and clinical variables were assessed using standard parametric or non-parametric tests, as appropriate. Full statistical procedures are provided in the Additional file 1 - Statistical Analysis Section.

To identify baseline differences between HC and participants with migraine (Analysis 1), generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were fitted for CTh, SA, and volumes. A subgroup sub-analysis was also performed by creating a categorical variable with four mutually exclusive groups: HC, HFEM without MO, CM without MO, and MO. The MO group included participants with HFEM or CM who presented medication overuse. Models were adjusted for education and the presence of depressive levels. Anxiety symptoms (BAI) and depressive symptoms (BDI-II) scores were highly correlated (r = 0.7, p < 0.001), and therefore only depressive symptoms was included to avoid collinearity. Two exploratory analyses were additionally performed to assess the impact MO and the specific differences between migraine subgroups. First, comparisons were performed between HC and participants with migraine categorized by MO status. Second, a direct sub-analysis comparing HFEM and CM was performed to explore the impact of attack frequency on brain morphometry. Both models were GLMMs and were adjusted by the same clinical covariates.

For the comparison between responders and non-responders (Analysis 2), GLMMs were used to model treatment response, treatment, and the response-by-treatment interaction using baseline data. Age, depressive symptoms, and MO status were included as covariates. Erenumab and non-response were used as reference levels for treatment and response, respectively. Therefore, the interaction term tested whether the responder versus non-responder difference differed between treatments. When a significant interaction was identified, post-hoc simple-effect contrasts based on estimated marginal means (EMMs) were used to directly compare responders and non-responders within each treatment group. These EMM contrasts were used for the within-treatment p-values shown in the figures.

For the longitudinal analysis (Analysis 3), GLMM were fitted for each CTh, SA, and volume ROI using age, depressive symptoms, MO status, time, treatment, and the time-by-treatment interaction as fixed effects. Participant was included as a random effect to account for repeated within-subject measures. Treatment and the time-by-treatment interaction were included to evaluate differential longitudinal trajectories between erenumab and OnabotA. Because erenumab was used as the reference treatment, the TIME coefficient represented the M0-to-M6 change within the erenumab group, whereas the TIME×treatment interaction tested whether the M0-to-M6 change in the OnabotA group differed from that observed in the erenumab group. Treatment-specific M0-to-M6 changes, including those within the OnabotA group, were obtained using post-hoc EMM simple-effect contrasts. Therefore, a significant TIME×treatment interaction was interpreted as evidence of different longitudinal trajectories between treatments, but not necessarily as evidence of a significant within-treatment change in the OnabotA group.

For all models, MRI scanner was included as a random effect. For SA and volume models, estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) was included as a covariate to account for individual differences in head size. A square root transformation was applied to the SA values and a logarithmic transformation for volumes values to address convergence issues during model fitting.

Considering the simultaneous analysis of multiple statistical tests, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, with adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. No a priori statistical power analysis was conducted; sample size was determined by the number of available participants, consistent with the observational and registry-based nature of the study. This should be considered when interpreting subgroup and interaction analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.2). EMMs, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals were obtained using the emmeans package (v2.0.1). Figures were produced using ggplot2 (v 3.4.1), and brain maps were generated using geom_brain within the ggseg package (v2.0.0).

Results

A total of 252 participants were included: 189 individuals with migraine and 63 HC (Fig. 2). Most participants were female (87.9%, 222/252), and age was similar between groups (HC: 39.5 ± 9.7, migraine: 40.3 ± 10.1 years; adjusted-p = 0.627). Anxiety and depressive symptom scores were statistically significantly higher in the migraine group (BAI: HC 3.6 ± 4.2 vs. migraine 16.0 ± 11.6, adjusted-p < 0.001; BDI-II: HC 2.7 ± 3.3 vs. migraine 10.4 ± 8.6, adjusted-p < 0.001). Education level was significantly higher in HC than in participants with migraine (HC: 15.4 ± 3.5, migraine: 14.0 ± 3.2 years; adjusted-p = 0.002). In the migraine group, mean MHD was 16.2 ± 5.7 and mean MMD was 9.9 ± 5.0, 69.8% (132/189) of participants with migraine had CM. Baseline migraine characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Analysis 1. Differences between migraine and HC

HC vs. Migraine

Initial baseline comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between HC and participants with migraine in CTh or SA after correction for multiple comparisons. However, volumetric analysis showed significantly larger CC volumes in the migraine group than in HC, specifically in the mid-posterior (adjusted-p < 0.001) and central (adjusted-p = 0.018) CC segments. Figure 3 shows the EMMs and 95% confidence interval for these ROIs.

HC vs. HFEM, CM, and MO

In the comparison between HC and migraine subgroups (see Supplementary Table 2 in Additional file 1 for clinical data), no CTh ROI differed significantly after correction for multiple comparisons. For SA, the right posterior cingulate gyrus showed significantly lower values in individuals with HFEM compared with HC (adjusted-p = 0.046). For volumetric ROIs, the mid-posterior CC was statistically significantly larger in HFEM compared with HC (adjusted-p = 0.003).

In the comparison between HC and participants with MO, the mid-posterior CC (adjusted-p = 0.001) and central CC (adjusted-p = 0.030) were statistically significantly larger in participants with MO than in HC. Analysis 2. Differences between responders and non-responders at baseline

Of the 189 individuals with migraine, 174 started a preventive treatment: 93 started erenumab and 81 started OnabotA. After six months, 94.8% (165/174) completed the follow-up clinical visit, allowing treatment response to be assessed. Among these 165 individuals, mean MHD decreased from 16.6 ± 5.3 to 10.8 ± 6.9 (adjusted-p < 0.001) and mean MMD decreased from 10.7 ± 5.1 to 6.4 ± 4.8 (adjusted-p < 0.001). Overall, 39.1% (64/165) of participants achieved ≥ 50% reduction in MHD and were classified as responders.

The response rate was slightly higher for erenumab (41.8%, 38/91) than for OnabotA (35.1%, 26/74), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.499) (Table 5). Baseline clinical characteristics did not differ significantly between the erenumab and OnabotA cohorts (see Supplementary Table 3 in Additional file 1).

Discussion

This study investigated structural brain differences in migraine compared with HC, as well as longitudinal brain changes over 6 months of preventive treatment with either erenumab, a migraine-specific therapy targeting the CGRP pathway, or OnabotA. The novelty of this study lies in the use of structural MRI data to comprehensively assess a well-phenotyped migraine cohort and to explore potential structural markers for disease characterization, treatment response, and longitudinal treatment-associated change.

Although these treatments differ in their approved indications (OnabotA for CM and erenumab for both episodic migraine (EM) and CM), the two treatment cohorts were reasonably comparable in our study. First, the EM group largely represented HFEM, a phenotype that lies close to CM and for which the distinction from CM may be somewhat arbitrary in biological terms [20]. Second, as part of a phase IV trial, erenumab could be prescribed without prior failure of OnabotA, unlike current reimbursement criteria in Spain [21]. Together, these factors likely reduced baseline differences between the cohorts.

Overall, our findings suggest that structural brain differences in migraine are subtle at baseline but become more evident when examined in relation to migraine subtype, treatment response, and longitudinal change after preventive therapy.

Differences between migraine and HC

We observed subtle but statistically significant structural differences between migraine and HC. Specifically, participants with migraine showed increased volume of the mid-posterior and central CC segments, with the mid-posterior segment also emerging in subgroup analyses. CC alterations have previously been reported in MRI studies of migraine [19, 22], and this structure is thought to support interhemispheric integration of sensory and associative information [23]. This finding may be consistent with altered multisensory integration in migraine, although its biological significance should be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity of structural MRI findings across studies [2, 14]. Indeed, previous work has reported alterations in different cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar regions depending on migraine phenotype, sample characteristics, and analytic methods [5, 24, 25]. For example, a recent large-sample study found reduced cortical surface area in the left insula [3], a result that we did not replicate, possibly reflecting differences in cohort composition, disease severity, medication exposure, or methodology [8, 14].

Furthermore, we observed larger SA in several cortical regions in CM compared with HFEM. One possible explanation is that these differences reflect structural remodelling associated with the greater cumulative burden of migraine attacks in chronic migraine [2, 6, 14]. Repeated nociceptive activation may contribute to plastic changes in regions involved in pain processing, salience, affective regulation, and cognitive control [2, 14, 24, 25]. Alternatively, these findings may represent pre-existing structural characteristics associated with vulnerability to migraine chronification. Because our baseline analysis is observational, both interpretations remain plausible.

Our findings are partly in line with previous studies suggesting morphometric differences between episodic and chronic migraine, although the direction of these effects has not been consistent. Planchuelo-Gómez et al. reported lower values in CM than in EM for some measures [24], whereas in our sample several SA values were higher in CM than in HFEM. Similar inconsistencies have been highlighted in recent reviews [2, 8, 14]. Therefore, rather than supporting a simple unidirectional model of cortical loss or gain with chronification, the available evidence suggests a more complex and region-specific pattern of brain remodelling influenced by factors such as attack frequency, disease duration, medication use, comorbid symptoms, and migraine state at the time of scanning [2, 7, 8, 14].

Differences between responders and non-responders at baseline

We found greater baseline CTh in the left parahippocampal gyrus in non-responders. This region has been implicated in stress-related processing, pain-related attention, and memory [26, 27], and greater thickness may reflect a more pronounced alteration in limbic-sensory network organization associated with poorer treatment response. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously, as the functional meaning of regional morphometric differences remains uncertain and structural MRI findings in migraine treatment-response phenotypes are heterogeneous.

When considering treatments separately, responders treated with OnabotA showed larger bilateral caudate volumes at baseline than non-responders. This finding was based on post-hoc within-treatment EMM contrasts following a significant response-by-treatment interaction, rather than on the isolated interaction coefficient itself. The caudate nucleus is involved in motor, cognitive, and affective regulation, and has been linked to pain modulation and action-selection processes, making it plausible that baseline differences in this region may relate to treatment-specific response mechanisms. One possible interpretation is that caudate morphology captures interindividual differences in the integrity of networks relevant to the mechanism of action of OnabotA; however, this hypothesis remains speculative and warrants replication in independent cohorts.

Only a few studies have examined baseline neuroimaging markers of treatment response in migraine. For OnabotA, one study described greater CTh in responders in the right primary somatosensory cortex, anterior insula, left superior temporal gyrus and pars opercularis [28], findings that we did not replicate. Likewise, a prospective study of galcanezumab reported no significant baseline structural differences between responders and non-responders. Taken together, these observations suggest that baseline structural predictors of treatment response may exist, but are likely to be modest, treatment-specific, and sensitive to cohort composition, imaging methods, and response definitions. This view is also consistent with recent work emphasizing the need for robust biomarker development and multimodal prediction frameworks in migraine [2, 14].

Differences before and after treatment

The most prominent structural changes in our cohort were observed longitudinally, when pre- and post-treatment MRI scans were compared. These changes involved multiple morphometric parameters across regions related to cognitive control and semantic or valuation-related processes, including the right pars opercularis, pars orbitalis, and pars triangularis; visual and memory processing, including the left fusiform, right lingual gyrus, and hippocampus; and pain modulation or affective regulation, including the left rostral anterior cingulate, left amygdala, and bilateral cerebellar cortex. The left choroid plexus showed a different pattern, with increased volume at M6 in the erenumab group.

Specifically, the right parsopercularis and parsorbitalis, both part of the inferior frontal gyrus, have been implicated in inhibition, emotional processing, and pain regulation; therefore, structural changes in these regions may be relevant to both nociceptive hypersensitivity and the cognitive-affective dimensions of migraine [29, 30]. The cerebellum, which contributes to sensory integration and descending pain modulation [31, 32], has also been increasingly recognized as a relevant structure in migraine pathophysiology, with both structural and functional alterations reported in previous studies [33, 34]. Although still relatively understudied, our findings support its involvement in treatment-related brain changes.

When examining treatment-by-time interactions, erenumab and OnabotA showed different longitudinal trajectories in several SA and volume ROIs. In most of these regions, post-hoc EMM contrasts showed lower values at M6 in the erenumab group, whereas comparable decreases were not observed in the OnabotA group. These findings should be interpreted as treatment-associated differences in longitudinal trajectories, rather than as direct evidence that the interaction terms represent absolute within-group reductions. Moreover, morphometric reductions cannot automatically be interpreted as recovery or normalization and may instead reflect more complex forms of structural remodelling.

The available literature on longitudinal structural MRI changes after preventive treatment remains limited. One structural MRI study at three months of galcanezumab reported CTh reductions in responders in regions including the somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate, medial and superior frontal gyri, and supramarginal gyrus [35]. Although the specific regions do not fully overlap with ours, both studies support the idea that anti-CGRP therapies are associated with measurable structural brain changes over time. In parallel, functional MRI studies with erenumab have shown decreased activation in the operculum, insula, thalamus, and cerebellum [36], some of which are anatomically or functionally related to the regions showing alterations in our cohort.

Despite variability across studies, the observation that brain changes occur during treatment is consistent with the notion that preventive therapies acting primarily in the periphery may nevertheless induce central modulatory effects, likely through reduced nociceptive input and secondary changes in pain-processing networks [36, 37].

Overall, our findings support the concept of a clinical-structural continuum in migraine pathophysiology. Baseline differences observed between HFEM, CM, MO, and HC, particularly in right-hemisphere cortical SA regions and CC volume, suggest a pattern of structural remodelling associated with disease burden and chronic pain. In addition, baseline brain structure may have prognostic relevance, as regions such as the left parahippocampal gyrus and caudate nucleus distinguished future responders from non-responders.

Importantly, this structural profile does not appear to be static. Our longitudinal results show that preventive treatment, particularly erenumab, was associated with changes in SA and volume in regions implicated in pain processing, affective regulation, and memory, including the hippocampus, amygdala, anterior cingulate, and cerebellum. These findings raise the possibility that clinical improvement may be accompanied by modulation of morphometric features, consistent with treatment-related neuroplasticity. However, these interpretations should remain cautious, as morphometric changes cannot be assumed to reflect recovery per se and may instead represent complex forms of structural adaptation.

Although MRI is not currently sufficient as a standalone biomarker, continued advances in neuroimaging methods and their integration with clinical and biological data may help move the field toward precision medicine in migraine. Such approaches could improve our understanding of migraine pathophysiology, support treatment stratification, and help define the optimal timing for preventive intervention, not only to reduce disability but also potentially to limit long-term disease-related brain changes.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of an untreated longitudinal control group limits interpretation of whether observed changes reflect treatment effects, natural disease progression, or other time-related factors. However, withholding preventive therapy from participants with HFEM or CM for this duration would be ethically problematic. Second, follow-up MRI timing showed some variability. Although the target interval was 6 months, the standard deviation was 27.3 days, primarily due to scheduling restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic. This variability has increased the variance in our longitudinal estimates and potentially blurred some treatment effects. However, because this variability was present in both treatment groups, it is unlikely to have introduced a systematic bias in the observed interactions.

The use of two different MRI scanners reflects real-world technological updates in clinical research settings, but scanner was included as a random effect in the statistical models. Another methodological limitation concerns the use of FastSurfer for longitudinal analysis. Unlike the traditional FreeSurfer pipelines, FastSurfer does not currently implement a specific longitudinal workflow that creates a subject-specific template to reduce within-subject variability. Therefore, M0 and M6 images were performed independently, which may have reduced sensitivity to very small structural changes over time. However, FastSurfer has demonstrated strong test-retest reliability compared with cross-sectional FreeSurfer [17], which partially mitigates this concern. Finally, we studied only erenumab among anti-CGRP therapies to avoid heterogeneity across drugs, which limits generalizability to the entire drug class [37]. Due to the reduced sample size at six months, comparisons between treatments should be interpreted cautiously, although our study provides preliminary evidence suggesting potential treatment-specific effects that warrant further longitudinal investigation.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that patients with migraine exhibit subtle but distinct structural brain differences compared with healthy controls, as well as baseline morphometric features associated with subsequent clinical response to preventive treatments, particularly in regions involved in pain, sensory, and limbic processing. Longitudinally, erenumab and OnabotA showed different structural trajectories over time, with several cortical surface area and subcortical volume changes being more evident in the erenumab group. These findings should be interpreted as treatment-associated differences in longitudinal brain trajectories rather than as definitive evidence of direct treatment-specific structural recovery or loss. Overall, our results support the idea that brain structure in migraine is not static and may be modulated over time during preventive therapy. Future advances in MRI acquisition and analytical methods may enable the detection of even more subtle alterations, providing deeper insight into migraine pathophysiology, treatment-related brain changes, and the development of clinically useful biomarkers.
