Multifluid tensorial equations for the flow of semi-dilute monodisperse suspensions
Dalton J. E. Harvie 1

Abstract
Using the volume averaging technique of Jackson (1997) , we derive a set of two-fluid equations that describe the dynamics of a mono-disperse non-Brownian colloidal suspension in the semi-dilute regime. The equations are tensorial and can be applied in arbitrary geometries. Closure models are developed that represent the stress surrounding each particle as a sum of stresses due to fluid movement through a fixed bed of particles, and those due to interactions between particles. Emphasising pragmatism, the developed closure models are consistent with current knowledge of particle interactions in these systems but employ parameters that can be tuned to represent the microstructure of specific particle suspensions. Within the interaction model, a model for the particle distribution around each particle is used that depends on the strain rate field, allowing anisotropy of the microstructure (and hence normal suspension stresses) to develop within the suspension in response to arbitrary strain fields. Force moments acting on particles during particle interactions are calculating by summing hydrodynamic contributions between particle pairs, but adjusted to recognise that multi-particle interactions can increase the effective stress generated during each interaction. Finally, an order of magnitude analysis is performed on the derived momentum equations to determine what terms are significant, leaving only terms in the final equations that are required to predict behaviour during laminar flow within the targeted semi-dilute regime. In a companion paper (referred to as paper II) ( Noori et al., 2024 ) we chose sets of microstructure parameters that predict experimentally measured bulk suspension behaviour, creating a link between particle properties (i.e. roughness), suspension microstructure and shear-induced particle migration in arbitrary flow fields.

1
Introduction

Solid-liquid suspension flows occur in wide variety of industrial, biological and environmental applications. As discussed in two recent and comprehensive reviews
(
Guazzelli & Pouliquen, 2018
;
Morris, 2020
)
, despite the large amount of work has been done to understand these complex systems, we do not have the tools available to describe the flow of non-dilute suspensions moving through complex geometries. This hampers our ability to (e.g.) design flowing particulate technology to suit specific applications.
Hence, the aim of this paper is to use a methodical averaging approach, combined with physics-informed closure models, to derive a frame-invariant tensorial set of constitutive equations that can predict the flow of mono-disperse non-Brownian colloidal suspensions of spheres within an incompressible and Newtonian fluid, above the dilute concentration limit, and through complex geometries.

Previous works that are relevant to this study come from a variety of research domains. Considering dilute suspensions where particles interact only with the fluid and not each other, various averaging techniques have been used to derive frame-invariant constitutive equation sets. Using the same volume averaging technique as employed in the present study,
Anderson & Jackson (1967)
;
Jackson (1997)
;
Jackson (1998)
obtained a set of ‘two-fluid’ equations applicable to a dilute suspension of mono-disperse spheres, with particle stresses modelled as those on an isolated sphere moving in a quadratic velocity field. By definition ‘multi-fluid’ equations consist of mass and momentum conservation equations for each phase present, with ‘two-fluid’ referring to situations where just a single fluid and single solid phase are present.
Zhang & Prosperetti (1997)
derived a very similar set of equations using an ensemble averaging technique. Both equation sets contain a faxen interphase force, in addition to drag, inertial, viscous and buoyancy terms, and predict a Newtonian suspension with a viscosity that varies linearly with solids volume fraction (
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
) as predicted by Einstein (
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}(1+\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\phi_{\mathrm{s}})
). These equations are only suitable for predicting suspension behaviour when the solid volume fraction is less than a few percent.

In the more industrially relevant semi-dilute regime, where particle to particle interactions are important, the governing equations are less well understood. In a influential paper
Batchelor & Green
used ensemble averaging of the bulk suspension to calculate an effective viscosity for the suspension of
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}(1+\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}\phi_{\mathrm{s}}+c\phi_{\mathrm{s}}^{2})
with
c\approx 7.6
. The analysis employed particle mobility functions based on the interactions of two isolated force and torque free particles, and used a ‘re-normalisation’ technique to find the average stesslet on each sphere using the approximation that this stresslet is determined by a particle’s interaction with its closest neighbour.
Batchelor & Green
also calculated the distribution of particles surrounding a reference particle in a pure straining flow, giving important insight into the microstructure of a suspension.
Batchelor & Green
’s techniques have been extended to include effects of microstructure anisotropy linked to surface roughness and non-hydrodynamic interaction forces by (amongst others)
Zinchenko (1984)
,
Brady & Morris (1997)
,
Wilson & Davis (2000)
,
Wilson & Davis (2002)
,
Wilson (2005)
and
Brady et al. (2006)
. While these studies provide important links between suspension microstructure and the bulk stresses existing within a suspension for specific flow fields, they have stopped short of providing frame-invariant constitutive equations that can be applied in arbitrary flow fields. Also, as the averaging is performed over the entire suspension, these studies do not distinguish between phase velocities and hence are not able to predict particle migration. Further, the suspension stresses predicted via these studies generally underpredict measurements, even in the semi-dilute regime, suggesting that models based on strict interpretations of force-free binary particle hydrodynamic interactions may not capture all of the relevant physics.

From a different perspective, particle migration within a range of simple flow fields and across a range of particle concentrations has been predicted by the suspension balance (SB) model, which uses phemological rheological equations to calculate averaged particle stresses, rather than stress closure models based on direct microhydrodynamic averaging
(
Nott & Brady, 1994
;
Morris & Brady, 1998
;
Morris & Boulay, 1999
)
. These models use a low-inertia suspension balance equation, consistent with
Batchelor & Green
’s suspension stress equation, combined with a force balance equation for the particle phase, allowing particle migration to be predicted. Normal viscosity coefficients fitted to experimental data have produced good agreement between experimentally measured and simulated particle migration results, at least for specific unidirectional and curvilinear flow fields. Initial implementations of the method used the same particle stress in the particle phase equation as used in the fluid equation, however a volume averaging analysis by
Nott et al. (2011)
(with some similarity to
Jackson
’s analysis and dilute closure) has shown that these stresses are distinct. The SB equations have been adapted to arbitrary two-dimensional flow fields using a technique that calculates local stress tensors within local coordinates that are aligned with the principal axes of the local flow
(
Miller et al., 2009
)
. This is a worthwhile approach but does involve the complexity of an eigenvector analysis at each point in space and time that stresses are required. The SB and related diffusive flux (DF)
(
Phillips et al., 1992
)
models are discussed in greater detail in paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
, and compared against results generated from the present study.

In the concentrated regime, characterised by solid volume fractions higher than approximately 40%, particles interact directly via contact and friction forces as well as hydrodynamically. As highlighted in reviews on this research field
(
Morris, 2020
;
Lemaire et al., 2023
)
, this leads to even more complex behaviour such as shear-thinning and jamming. In terms of frame-invariant constitutive tensorial equations, some works are based on the concept of tracking suspension structure, based on the displacement between closely interacting particle pairs
(
Phan‐Thien, 1995
;
Phan-Thien et al., 1999
;
Gillissen & Wilson, 2018
;
Gillissen & Wilson, 2019b
)
. Concentrated suspensions are not the focus of this work.

Reviewing this literature shows that while our understanding of suspension stresses within the semi-dilute regime has advanced considerably, a) there is uncertainty regarding the exact form of the individual phase equations that are required to predict (e.g.) particle migration, and b) there is no analytical link between particle properties and bulk suspension behaviour that is valid outside of simple flow geometries. This limits our ability to design technologies or optimise processes that utilise these industrially relevant semi-dilute suspensions. This paper addresses this knowledge gap: Specifically, we derive constitutive equations that can predict the velocities of the two interpenetrating phases in arbitrary flow fields by averaging closure models of microstructure processes.

In section
2
we briefly describe the volume averaging technique of
Jackson (1997)
and derive the multifluid fluid, solid and mixture momentum equations applicable to mono-disperse colloidal suspensions in the semi-dilute regime. Frame-invariant closure models are then detailed that represent the stress surrounding each particle as a sum of stresses due to fluid movement through a fixed bed of particles (section
3
), and those due to interactions between particles (section
4
). Finally, an order of magnitude analysis is performed on the derived momentum equations to determine what terms are significant (section
5
). In a companion paper (referred to as paper II)
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
we analyse the performance of the derived constitutive equations by calculating the resulting shear and normal stresses as a function of suspension microstructure/particle properties, and evaluate their ability to predict shear induced migration (SIM) in benchmark pressure driven flows.

2
Averaged Equations of Motion

In this section we derive averaged equations of motion for each of the two phases from the local equations of motion. The analysis applies to a suspension of solid spherical particles within an incompressible Newtonian fluid. The particles have a radius of
a
and are much smaller than the containing vessel. We utilise averaging methods first detailed in
Anderson & Jackson (1967)
but that were more rigorously applied in
Jackson (1997)
to a dilute (order
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
) suspension of hard spheres. The main novelties in this section are the definition of the particle stress terms that appear in the averaged momentum equations, and the quantification of the averaging error inherent to each momentum equation. These, along with details the averaging techniques, support subsequent analysis.

The
Anderson & Jackson (1967)
technique defines various averages using a local weighting function,
g(r)
. This function is defined to be a monotonic decreasing function of
r
. No specific form for
g(r)
is required, however its amplitude and radius obey

4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}g(r)r^{2}dr=1\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{l}g(r)r^{2}dr=\int_{l}^{\infty}g(r)r^{2}dr

(1)

Hence
l
is a measure of the radius of the weighting function.

Several types of averages are required to derive the averaged equations. The
fluid phase average
is a volume average performed over the fluid phase within a suspension region, relating a point property
f
at
\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}
to its average at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}
via

\phi_{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})=\int_{V_{\mathrm{f}}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}|)dV_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

(2)

Here
\phi_{\mathrm{f}}
is the average volume fraction of the fluid phase, which can be evaluated by setting
f=1
in this equation. The fluid phase volume within the suspension is represented by
V_{\mathrm{f}}
. A
solid phase (volume) average
,
\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}
, analogous to equation (
2
), is also defined with the integral taken over the solid phase volume
V_{\mathrm{s}}
. The corresponding solid phase volume fraction is
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
, resulting in
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}=1-\phi_{\mathrm{f}}
exactly. Finally a
mixture volume average

\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{m}}=\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}
is defined using an integral taken over the entire suspension volume
V
.
Particle phase averages
are defined separately as ensemble averages relating a property
f^{p}
associated with particle
p
to the average property at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}
via

n(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}})=\sum_{p}f^{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)

(3)

Here
n
is the average particle number density (defined by setting
\left\langle{f}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}=f^{p}=1
in equation (
3
)),
\nu=4\pi a^{3}/3
is the particle volume and
V^{p}
represents the volume associated with particle
p
. The sum is taken over all particles
p
in the domain, each with centroid
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
. Equation (
3
) does not place any constraints on how the particle property
f^{p}
is related to spatially varying local particle properties, however for velocity we require that
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
represents the velocity of the centroid of particle
p
, that is
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
. Note that in terms of notation a superscript
p
refers to a specific particle (e.g.,
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
and
V^{p}
), while the subscripts of p, s, f and m, represent particle, solid, fluid and mixture phase or averages, respectively (e.g.,
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}
,
\phi_{\mathrm{f}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{m}}
or
V_{\mathrm{s}}
).

There are several assumptions employed in all the averaging analyses. Firstly, a separation of three lengthscales: Namely
a\ll l\ll L
, where
a
is the particle radius,
l
is the previously defined radius of the averaging weighting function, and
L
is a lengthscale over which averaged variables vary (ie, a lengthscale of the macroscopic flow). Further, any analysis based on any of the averaging definitions is only valid in regions that are located far (
\gg l
) from any boundaries, such that average variable values do not depend on conditions that are outside of the suspension region. This assumption is utilised not only in the averaging definitions, but also in derivations for identities of the averages of temporal and spatial derivatives, as given in
Anderson & Jackson (1967)
. Various other averaging identities that are used in the following are derived in Appendix
A.1
.

Turning to the averaged equations of motion:
Jackson (1997)
has already derived averaged continuity equations for each phase from the local continuity equation. The derivation involves applying the fluid and solid averaged temporal and spatial derivative identities (equations (4) and (5) in
Jackson (1997)
) to a species invariant property (ie,
f=1
) while noting the local incompressibility constraint
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}=0
. This produces for the fluid and solid phases,

\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{f}}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{s}}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}=0

(4)

respectively, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}
is the fluid volume averaged velocity and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}
is the solid volume averaged velocity. This derivation is exact provided the region is far from boundaries. Summing these equations leads to the mixture based incompressibility statement,

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{m}}=0

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{m}}=\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the mixture averaged velocity. Note that a particle-number-based continuity equation is also derived in
Jackson (1997)
(their equation (15)), however not directly used in this study.

The averaged momentum equations are similarly derived by averaging local momentum conservations over their respective phases. Specifically, performing a fluid phase average of the local Cauchy momentum equation produces

\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\left[\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\right]=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{f}}p_{\mathrm{f}}+\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{m}}\\
+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,f}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,f}}+\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{f}}

(6)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
are the density and viscosity of the fluid phase, respectively,
p_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle{p}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}
is the fluid volume averaged pressure,
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{m}}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{m}}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{m}})^{T}
is the symmetric, trace-less (due to equation (
5
)) strain rate based on mixture velocities, and
\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}
is the acceleration due to gravity. An error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{f}}
of
\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}l\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}/L^{2}\right)
in this equation has resulted solely from averaging the inertial advection term, as derived in Appendix
A.1
and summarised by equation (
134
). Also, the stress within a Newtonian fluid
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{}}=-p\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}+\mu_{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}})^{T})
has been applied within the fluid phase to produce the averaged pressure and viscous stress terms
(
Jackson, 1997
)
. As detailed in
Joseph & Lundgren (1990)
, the form of these terms is consistent with those derived using alternative ensemble averaging approaches (see also
Drew & Mandyam (1998)
).

Two forces are defined in equation (
6
). The first of these,

\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,f}}=-\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}

(7)

is a ‘Reynolds’ type stress term that results from decomposing and averaging the inertial advection term. Here
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
is the difference between the local and average fluid velocities, defined only within the fluid.
The second force represents the effect of particle surface stresses on the fluid phase, and is given by

\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,f}}=-\sum_{p}\int_{S^{p}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}|)dS_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})=\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})
is the total surface traction acting at
\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}
on the particle’s surface,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
is an unit normal pointing outwards from the particle, and
S^{p}
is the surface area, all associated with particle
p
. While we focus on particle surface stresses that originate from hydrodynamic interactions in this study, we note that equation (
8
) applies regardless of the origin of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{}}
.

Forming a solid phase momentum equation is slightly more involved. Adapting the method of
Anderson & Jackson (1967)
, particle phase averaging of Newton’s second law applied to a particle
p
gives

\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\nu\left[\frac{\partial n\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot n\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}\right]=\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,s}}+\rho_{\mathrm{s}}n\nu\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}

(9)

where the average force exerted by the fluid on the solid phase is given by

\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,s}}=\sum_{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)\int_{S^{p}}\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})dS_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

(10)

While equation (
9
) is exact, we wish to express the momentum balance in terms of solid phase, rather than particle phase averaged quantities, and doing these conversions introduces averaging errors. Specifically, using equivalences derived in Appendix
A.1
for
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\nu\approx\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}\approx\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}\approx\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}
, given by equations (
130
), (
132
) and (
138
), respectively, yields

\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right]=\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,s}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,s}}+\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{s}}

(11)

where another ‘Reynolds’ type stress force

\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,s}}=-\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}

(12)

is defined in terms of the solid perturbation velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
is the local velocity of the relevant particle
p
, and the error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\rho_{\mathrm{s}}a\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}/L^{2},\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\rho_{\mathrm{s}}a^{2}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}/L^{2}\right)
has resulted from averaging the inertial and weight terms (noting
\mathcal{O}\left(a,b,...\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(a\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(b\right)+...
).

In addition to the fluid and solid phase momentum equations, equations (
6
) and (
11
) can be added to yield a mixture momentum equation,

\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{m}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rho}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{m}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rho}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rho}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{\mathrm{f}}p_{\mathrm{f}}+\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{m}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\rho,\text{m}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,f}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{rst,s}}\\
+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,f}}+\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{tot,s}}+\rho_{\mathrm{m}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{f}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{s}}

(13)

where

\boldsymbol{f}_{\rho,\text{m}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{\mathrm{s}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}})(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}})

(14)

is an inertial correction term generated by summing the phase averaged momentum advection terms
(
Jackson, 1997
)

and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\rho}=(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\rho_{\mathrm{s}}\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}})/\rho_{\mathrm{m}}
is the mass averaged suspension velocity.

To proceed further we make two key assumptions regarding the stress experienced by each particle. Working directly in particle surface tractions, we firstly assume that the total stress acting on the surface of the reference particle can be expressed as a function of the averaged fluid and solid fields,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
and
p_{\mathrm{f}}
. Secondly, as illustrated in the schematic Figure
1
, we then partition this traction into two terms,

\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}},p_{\mathrm{f}})=\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}),p_{\mathrm{f}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}})+\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}},p_{\mathrm{f}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}})

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
represents the traction exerted on the reference particle due to (confined) fluid movement, and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}
represents the traction exerted on the reference particle due to relative particle movement. More rigorously, the ‘fixed’ traction
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
is defined as that on the surface of particle
p
(centred at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
) when the particle is travelling at velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
in the presence of all other particles, but with the other particles all travelling at the reference particle velocity of
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
, and all within a fluid having the average velocity and pressure fields of
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
p_{\mathrm{f}}
, respectively. In contrast, the ‘interaction’ traction
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}
is defined as the traction experienced by a particle
p
when all other particles (i.e.
q
at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{q}
) are moving on average at their local
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{q})
velocity, minus the traction experienced by particle
p
in the same situation but where all other particles are moving at the velocity of the reference particle,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
. In both situations all particles again exist within a fluid having the average velocity and pressure fields of
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
p_{\mathrm{f}}
, respectively.

3
Fixed particle forces

3.1
Fixed particle stress model closure assumptions

As mentioned above, integrals of the traction around the reference particle when surrounded by a fixed array of particles, required by equation (
21
) (with
j=
fix), are calculated based on the motion of a force-free sphere in a quadratic velocity field, but modified to account for the presence of the surrounding particles.
Nadim & Stone (1991)
provides a solution for the traction surrounding a single force-free sphere moving within a quadratic velocity field.
Jackson (1997)
has already added to this linear velocity field and weight effects
(
Leal, 1992
)
, and incorporated it into the suspension equations to model dilute (that is, non-interacting) particle stresses. However there are some difficulties with this approach that resulted in a correction to the original paper
(
Jackson, 1998
)
, and inconsistencies that remain when the suspension is not dilute that we wish to address.

Importantly we make three modifications (closure assumptions) to
Jackson
’s analysis to account for the presence of the surrounding particles on the traction integrals.

The discussed traction solution is based on the Stokes equations surrounding a single sphere. Our first modification recognises that the far-field pressure field local to the reference particle, which determines the form drag acting on the particle, is not consistent with the pressure field existing within the suspension at the particle’s location. There are two reasons why the dilute local and suspension pressure fields are inconsistent (as employed in
Jackson (1997)
, for example). Firstly by basing the local traction calculation on the Stokes equations, pressure gradients due to acceleration (changes in inertia) are not captured at a local level, in contrast to the suspension equations which do account for inertia and hence generate pressure gradients due to acceleration. Indeed, accounting for acceleration at the local level was the purpose of the
Jackson (1998)
correction to the original dilute suspension derivation. Secondly, in the presence of other particles the local pressure gradient is further modified from the pressure gradient acting on the reference particle in isolation, due to (e.g.) drag forces from the other particles acting on the fluid flow. This effect is not included in either of the
Jackson
derivations. Below we account for both these inertial and surrounding particles’ effects on the local pressure gradient by introducing a body force
\boldsymbol{b}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
into the locally solved Stokes equations, with the force chosen so that the local pressure gradient used in the quadratic field traction calculation matches the fluid phase suspension pressure gradient, evaluated at the location of the reference particle
p
. This modification ensures that whatever forces and (potentially) additional physics are introduced to the suspension scale momentum equations, the resulting pressure gradient will be faithfully reproduced on the local particle scale.

The second modification to the dilute traction solution recognises the effect that the surrounding fixed particles have on the skin drag experienced by the reference particle. Specifically, for a given fluid phase velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
that surrounds the reference particle, the effect of a fixed array of surrounding particles is to decrease the volume available through which the surrounding fluid can flow, which in turn increases the fluid strain rates existing on the surface of the reference particle. Viscous stresses experienced by the reference particle are the product of fluid viscosity
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
and local strain rates. Hence, to capture the increase in strain rates on the reference particle due to the fixed array of particles in our closure model, we multiply the fluid viscosity in the traction expression by a factor
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
which increases with the local particle concentration. That is, within the dilute traction expression we use the local viscosity

\mu(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})=\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))\mu_{\mathrm{f}}

(22)

rather than the fluid viscosity
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
. In paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
we show how
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
can be related to measured suspension properties such as the hindered settling function or mixture shear viscosity. Note that this closure model uses a modified viscosity to capture the increase in particle traction due to increased local strain rates, rather than representing the traction on a sphere in a variable viscosity fluid.

The final modification to the local traction expression is concerned with how the fluid phase velocity field,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
, is used to represent the local fluid velocity field surrounding the reference particle, given that the fluid phase velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
is not divergence free. The
Nadim & Stone (1991)
and
Leal (1992)
analyses on which the traction expression is based utilise divergence-free properties of the local field velocity characterisation tensors, so the
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
field cannot be used directly with the traction expression. As a closure assumption we propose physically reasonable adjustments to the suspension scale
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
field to create local velocity field vectors and tensors that are consistent with a divergence free velocity field, but can be used in the reference particle traction expression.

Evaluating the fixed particle traction

4.1
Interaction particle stress model closure assumptions

Closure assumptions are required to model the interaction traction
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}
that acts on the surface of particle
p
. Although this traction can be formerly defined from equation (
15
), we model it using a pairwise sum over all surrounding particles (
q

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}},p_{\mathrm{f}})-\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}),p_{\mathrm{f}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{q\neq p}\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}),\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp}),\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp}\right)

(52)

where the mid-point between the pair of particles is
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}+\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{q})/2
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{q}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
is the displacement between them. In the above the pairwise interaction traction between two general particles has been defined as

\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin}}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})=\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin*}}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})-\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{sin}}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}})

(53)

where
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin*}}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})
is the (total binary) traction acting on the surface at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}+a\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}
of a particle at any general location
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}
due to a binary interaction with a second particle existing at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}}
and where the two particles travel in an undisturbed strain rate field of
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}
, and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{sin}}(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}})
is the equivalent (single) traction acting on the particle at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}
and surface at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}+a\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}
and within the same undisturbed strain field of
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}
, but when in isolation. How
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}
is defined is detailed below.

A number of closure assumptions are employed in the approximation of equation (
52
). Firstly, the total traction difference represented by
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
is approximated by a pairwise sum of traction differences between particle
p
and each of its neighbours (
q

\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}
is modelled as
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin*}}
in each term in this sum, representing the interaction between two particles in an infinite medium. Similarly, the contribution to
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
in each term in the sum is modelled as
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{sin}}
, being the traction on an isolated particle in the same flow field and in an infinite medium which can be interpreted as the dilute limit of
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
. Note that as the distance between particles becomes large both
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin}}
approach a consistent zero limit.

The second closure assumption employed in equation (
52

\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}
on the real fluid field variables
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
p_{\mathrm{f}}
, but instead define an undisturbed and divergence-free ‘interaction’ velocity field in the neighbourhood of each particle interaction that is based on
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp})
and
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{qp})
. Neglect of
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
p_{\mathrm{f}}
on
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{int}}^{p}
is justified given that the influence of these fluid fields is already captured in the total traction via
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{fix}}^{p}

The interaction velocity field is defined by the strain rate tensor
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
, which is calculated for each binary interaction to ensure that the relative isolated velocity between the two particles is equal to
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{q})-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})

to account for multi-particle and even friction effects (in a phenomenological sense) acting during the interaction. The calculated interaction velocity field is constrained to be divergence-free so that existing correlations for the force and force moment acting on the reference particle within an incompressible fluid during the interaction can be used. More details of how
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}

4.2
Defining the interaction particle density function
p(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})

How the non-dimensional PDF
p
is modelled is critical in determining the form of the suspension scale interaction stresses. In particular, anisotropy of the PDF results in non-Newtonian suspension stresses, which cause shear induced migration of particles
(
Batchelor & Green, 1972
;
Lemaire et al., 2023
)
. Much research has been done on understanding the form of this PDF in solid-liquid suspension flows, with experimental measurements
(
Rampall et al., 1997
;
Gao et al., 2010
;
Blanc et al., 2011
;
Blanc, 2012
;
Blanc et al., 2012
)
largely corroborating theoretical analysis
(
Arp & Mason, 1977
;
Zinchenko, 1984
;
Da Cunha & Hinch, 1996
;
Brady & Morris, 1997
;
Wilson & Davis, 2000
;
Wilson & Davis, 2002
;
Wilson, 2005
)
and Stokesian simulations
(
Wilson & Davis, 2002
;
Morris & Katyal, 2002
;
Drazer et al., 2004
;
Gao et al., 2010
;
Blanc et al., 2012
)
. Based on this knowledge, in the following we assume a general form for the PDF that is able to capture its main observable traits, while in paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
we adopt a specific form for the PDF that is consistent with rough-particle interactions in strong flows, with coefficients chosen to reproduce experimentally observed particle migration at the suspension scale.

The general form adopted for
p
is

p(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})=\left[f_{2}^{*}(r)-f_{1}^{*}(r)\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\right]q(r)

(58)

where
q(r)
is the isotropic PDF derived by
Batchelor & Green (1972)
for dilute particle interactions,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the non-dimensional divergence-free strain rate that characterises the interaction (see equation (
96
)),
\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}=\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}}/r
is the unit binary interaction vector,
r=|\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}}|
and
f_{2}^{*}(r)
and
f_{1}^{*}(r)
are functions of particle separation
r
that describe the magnitudes of the isotropic and anisotropic regions within the PDF, respectively. These
f^{*}
functions are specified explicitly in paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
, although we provide constraints on their values in the following.

The form of
p
as represented by equation (
58
) has been chosen to capture four observed traits of the suspension PDFs:

4.2.1
Isotropic PDF based on dilute particle interactions

Considering only hydrodynamic pairwise interactions,
Batchelor & Green
found that for any point that lies on a streamline originating in the far field,
p(\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})=q(r)
, with
q(r)
defined by

q(r)=\frac{1}{1-A}\exp{\int^{\infty}_{r}\frac{3(B-A)}{r(1-A)}dr}

(59)

Here
A(r)
and
B(r)
are the same particle mobility functions used in Section
4.4
. The proposed PDF uses
p=q
in any isotropic regions where
f_{2}^{*}=1
and
f_{1}^{*}=0
.

4.2.2
Screening effects at large distances

As highlighted by previous researchers
(
Batchelor & Green, 1972
;
Drew, 1976
, e.g.)
, integrals of force moments such as contained in equation (
54
) (with e.g.
m=1
) that are based on hydrodynamic pairwise interactions are mathematically divergent when evaluated over all space (i.e., all surrounding particles).
Batchelor & Green (1972)
used this as motivation to develop a renormalisation technique that uses an analogy between the local and average strain rates generated by the interacting particle to model the total force dipole on the reference sphere as that due to the closest interacting particle. Later studies recognised that the renormalisation terms included in the dipole integrals do not (by design) affect their evaluation, provided that the integral is performed in polar coordinates, with the radial integration carried out last
(
Zinchenko, 1984
;
Wilson & Davis, 2000
)
.

Our problem is more complex than these previous works in that we need integrals of both the pairwise force moments
and
forces (multiplied by
\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}}
) evaluated over the surrounding particles, as represented by equation (
54
) with
m=1
and
m=0
, respectively. Rather than trying to adapt
Batchelor & Green (1972)
’s renormalisation technique, we build upon the observations of
Jackson (1997)
and take a different approach. Namely, we contend that there is no reason why the average forces or force moments exerted on a particle need be equal to the sum from pairwise interactions from all surrounding particles, given that the forces and moments from individual particles at larger distances from the reference sphere may be screened by those that are closer. Hence, our pragmatic approach to evaluating the stress integral in equation (
54
) is to use
p=0
(i.e.,
f_{1}^{*}=f_{2}^{*}=0
) for
r>{r_{\infty}}
. We refer to
{r_{\infty}}
as a ‘screening length’.

Note that other researchers have used a similar maximum PDF radius: For example
Gillissen & Wilson (2018)
and
Gillissen & Wilson (2019a)
who consider intermediate particle volume fractions and smooth particles, use an equivalent
r_{\infty}
that is just larger than
2a
to limit interactions to particles that are in close contact with the reference particle, lying in an ‘interaction shell’.

In similarity with the renormalisation technique, we do recognise that in a semi-dilute suspension forces and force moments on the reference particle will be dominated by those generated by the closest interacting particles, and hence set
{r_{\infty}}
to be a function of the distance to the nearest neighbouring particle(s). This produces integrated stresses that are convergent for
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\rightarrow 0
, despite
{r_{\infty}}\rightarrow\infty
in the same dilute limit. Details of how
{r_{\infty}}
are evaluated are provided in paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
.

4.2.3
Exclusion of particles from the inner closed streamline region

Under pure straining flow a region of closed streamlines surrounds the reference sphere, extending in both directions outwards from the centre of the reference sphere over parts of the velocity and vorticity plane
(
Batchelor & Green, 1972
;
Arp & Mason, 1977
)
. The probability of finding a second sphere within this closed streamline region (forming a doublet) is not represented by
q(r)
, but rather depends on an interplay between surface roughness, multi-body interactions and flow history. A second sphere trapped within these closed streamlines, and rotating around the reference sphere close to the shear plane (ie, the plane formed by the velocity and velocity gradient vectors), must pass within a distance of
4.2\times 10^{-5}a
or smaller from the reference sphere
(
Arp & Mason, 1977
)
. However, as this dimension is smaller than the roughness of most practical particles, contact forces push the second particle across the hydrodynamic streamlines, causing secondary spheres to be excluded from this part of the closed streamline region. This mechanism has been confirmed via trajectory analysis
(
Da Cunha & Hinch, 1996
)
and via examining PDFs measured from both experiments
(
Rampall et al., 1997
;
Blanc et al., 2012
;
Blanc et al., 2011
)
and Stokesian dynamics simulations
(
Drazer et al., 2004
;
Morris & Katyal, 2002
;
Blanc et al., 2012
)
. In particular, the dilute (
\phi=5\%
) PDF measurements of
Blanc et al. (2011)
, measured near the shear plane, show that the shape of the excluded particle region is consistent with theory, being fore-aft symmetric around the velocity gradient and vorticity plane and extending as a narrowing slit outwards from the reference particle in both directions along the velocity axis.

As the particle concentration increases, multi-body interactions become more important, and are able to move secondary particles both into and out of the closed streamline region. The experimental PDF measurements of
Blanc (2012)
show that as the volume fraction increases from
\phi=5\%
, the regions of particle deficit (that is,
p(\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})<1
) along the velocity axis and within the shear plane reduce in size, until at
\phi\approx 25\%
, they are no longer visible at the resolution of the data. This is consistent with the experimental results of
Rampall et al. (1997)
that show particle probability densities increasing within these excluded regions as the volume fraction is increased from
\phi=5\%
to
15\%
, and with the Stokesian dynamics results of
Drazer et al. (2004)
that show that spherical averages of the PDF function across a range of radii approach the isotropic PDF
q(r)
as the particle volume fraction increases from
\phi=1\%
to
25\%
. Similar results at higher volume fractions also show that the isotropic spherical average of
p(\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{}})
is well represented by
q(r)
, suggesting that the closed streamline particle deficit regions are absent at these volume fractions
(
Morris & Katyal, 2002
)
.

Note that closed streamlines surrounding the reference particle that are located away from the shear plane (that is, some distance along the vorticity axis away from the centre of the reference particle) have a larger minimum approach distance between spheres compared to those occurring on the shear plane
(
Arp & Mason, 1977
)
. This means that secondary particles impacting away from the shear plane can be caught in doublets and not excluded by practically relevant roughness. Although there are few PDF measurements available in the literature which look beyond the shear plane, the Stokesian dynamics results of
Gao et al. (2010)
at
\phi=31.9\%
and shown over the velocity and vorticity plane support this analysis. Under dilute conditions it is hence difficult to predict the probability of finding particles within these outlying parts of the closed streamline region. However for our purposes this is of minor significance because; a) the results of
Arp & Mason (1977)
show that the secondary particles must impact the reference particle quite close to the velocity and vorticity plane for the minimum separating gap size to be larger than practical particle roughness, and b) the effect of any inaccuracies from this part of the PDF on the resulting stress will be slight as the number of secondary particle impacts within this region under linear shear conditions is low, given the proximity to the same velocity and vorticity plane.

It should be noted that considerably less research has been conducted on the PDF formed during linear extensional strain, as opposed to linear shear strain.
Batchelor & Green (1972)
predict that no closed streamline regions are present during extensional strain, however it is expected that particle roughness will still cause secondary particle deficits in regions of extension, given that under extension strain colliding particles that are subject to hydrodynamic forces still pass within small distances of the reference sphere
(
Arp & Mason, 1977
)
.

To capture the absence of particles occurring within the closed streamline inner region under our targeted semi-dilute conditions, we use an approximate model and assume that
f_{1}^{*}=f_{2}^{*}=0
for
r<r_{0}
, where
r_{0}>2a
is a critical inner region dimension defined in paper II
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
.

4.2.4
Anisotropic PDF caused by particle roughness

As well as excluding particles from the closed streamline region, particle roughness also alters the trajectories of secondary spheres that approach the reference sphere from the far field. Under dilute conditions in a linear shear flow, this has the effect of moving the departing particle boundary of the thin excluded particle region further into the expansion quadrant of the flow
(
Da Cunha & Hinch, 1996
;
Rampall et al., 1997
)
, breaking the fore-aft symmetry of the particle distribution (around the velocity gradient and vorticity plane)
(
Drazer et al., 2004
)
. As mentioned above, it is this fore-aft asymmetry that causes normal stresses to develop in the suspension. As the particle volume fraction increases and the size of the excluded particle regions decreases, the particle distribution becomes more fore-aft asymmetric, with the location of maximum particle deficit near
r=2a
moving around the reference sphere, towards the centre of the expansion quadrant
(
Blanc et al., 2012
)
. At high volume fractions (
\phi\gtrsim 45\%
) complex structures develop that are highly anisotropic, with clustering particularly at increasing radial multiples of the particle diameter
(
Gao et al., 2010
;
Blanc et al., 2012
)
. These structures presumably result from the interplay between particle roughness, multi-body interactions and possibly contact friction.

Following the above physical description of how roughness induces anisotropy in the PDF, we postulate that
p
is also a function of the non-dimensional relative velocity between the two particles as represented by
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}
. Indeed, in regions where this parameter is negative, secondary particles move from the far field towards the reference particle, and the local PDF may be increased or decreased (depending on
r
) due to roughness stopping the relative normal motion of the particles. Conversely, in regions where
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}>0
, particles move away from the reference particle, and realising that these particles may have been displaced from their approaching hydrodynamic streamlines by the particle roughness, the local PDF may be decreased relative to the isotropic
q(r)
value. Hence, on physical grounds, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}
is included in equation (
58
) as a means of modelling the expanding and contracting regions of the flow surrounding the reference particle.

The inclusion of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}
in the PDF form can also be justified based on previous experimental analysis. Specifically,
Morris & Katyal (2002)
performed a spherical harmonic decomposition of the PDF obtained via Stokesian dynamics for the linear shear of a
\phi=55\%
suspension, including in their simulations a small amount of Brownian diffusion (
\mbox{Pe}_{\mathrm{}}=1000
). Of the first nine terms of the decomposition, the first (
g_{00}
in their notation) dominates at all radial locations shown. This term represents an isotropic contribution to the PDF and is captured by
q(r)
in our equation (
58
). The next two largest terms are represented by
g_{22}
and
g_{2,-2}
. The first of these (
g_{22}
) contributes an even function (around the velocity gradient and vorticity plane) to the PDF that hence does not contribute to the normal stresses
(
Batchelor & Green, 1972
)
. The second of these terms however (
g_{2,-2}
) contributes an odd function (around the velocity and vorticity plane) to the PDF, and thus is the largest term in the decomposition that contributes to the normal stresses in the suspension. The harmonic basis function for this term can be shown to be equal to the anisotropic multiplier used in our PDF under linear shear conditions (that is,
\boldsymbol{\hat{\dot{\gamma}}}_{\mathrm{s}}:\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}
), implying that our
q(r)f_{\mathrm{1}}^{*}(r)
in equation (
58
) is conceptually equivalent to the
g_{2,-2}
coefficient. Hence, our proposed PDF can represent the isotropic and leading normal stress terms to this measured PDF when represented in spherical harmonics.

In principle we could represent the PDF using a greater number of spherical harmonic terms than used in equation (
58
), however to represent these in a frame invariant manner would require a significantly more mathematically complex PDF. Also, as discussed by
Morris & Katyal (2002)
, it may take many terms for the series to converge. This is certainly an avenue for future work.

4.3
Defining the binary interaction strain rate tensor,
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}

We next detail how the local binary interaction far-field fluid strain rate
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
is calculated from
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
. This tensor represents the strain rate of the incompressible fluid that locally surrounds each interacting pair of particles, and is chosen so that the particles, when moving force and torque free and in isolation, and at infinite dilution, move at the local velocity defined by
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
. As a sphere in linear Stokes flow travels at the velocity of the undisturbed fluid at its centroid location, this implies that the local fluid velocity of the reconstructed ‘dilute’ velocity field is also equal to
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
at the centroid of both interacting particles, and that the velocity of the reference sphere relative to this reconstructed dilute velocity field is zero.

There are additional modelling constraints imposed on the calculation of
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
. As the subsequent force analysis only requires the symmetrical and spherical parts of this strain-tensor, only these parts are included in
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
, with the anti-symmetric part of this flow field left undefined. Further, in the absence of any deviatoric but presence of dilational strain in the
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
field, the dilute interaction velocity field is so chosen to be symmetrical around the axis between particles (
\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}
), capturing the correct relative particle velocity as described by
\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,s}}
. Note that unlike the particle scale velocity field used in the analysis of the fixed particle forces, for the interaction forces the representation of the suspension scale divergence at the local particle scale is not confined to the physical dimensions of the problem. This modelling choice makes the following mathematical analysis tractable. Finally, as detailed below, under non-dilute conditions the calculated ‘dilute’ strain rate is increased to capture the effects of multi-particle interactions.

With these constraints the following strain rate tensor results:

\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{int}}\left[\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{s}}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,s}}\left(3\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}\right]\right]

(60)

The first term in the square braces is the symmetrical divergence-free component of the solid velocity field,
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{s}}
, or deviatoric strain rate of the solid particles. Note from equation (
95
) with
i=\text{s}
that
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{s}}
is already divergence-free. The second term of
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
captures the expansion and contraction (dilation) of the solid particle field, represented via
\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,s}}
as defined via equation (
94
) with
i=\text{s}
. And finally, like
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
for the fixed stresses, the ‘dilute’ strain rate calculated by matching the local
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}
field is multiplied by a factor
\lambda_{\mathrm{int}}
to increase the applied stresses occurring during an interaction, accounting for multi-particle interactions using an effective medium concept. This factor can equivalently be viewed as an increase in viscosity acting during the interaction. It is included in the calculation for the interaction strain rate
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
so that it is evaluated at the mid-point between particles (like
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
), ensuring reciprocity of forces acting on each particle during each interaction.

4.4
Defining the binary interaction force
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}
and force moment
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}^{\prime}

With
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{\mathrm{bin}}
defined, we now outline a physical model that describes how the two particles hydrodynamically interact so that expressions for
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}^{\prime}
can be determined.

Focusing first on the force the fluid exerts on each particle during the interaction,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}
(defined by equation (
54
with
m=0
), equation (
55
) gives
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}-\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{sin}}
, where
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}
is the total force exerted during the pairwise interaction, and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{sin}}
is the force exerted when the particle travels in isolation. Calculating
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{sin}}
is straightforward, using known results for isolated spheres in a linear strain rate field. Calculating
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}
is more complex however, requiring a model for the trajectory of the particles, or equivalently the net forces acting on them, during the interaction. We discuss three models that describe the interaction — the ‘constrained’, ‘free’ and ‘semi-free’ models — before choosing the last to implement mathematically to determine
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}
.

The central assumption under the constrained model is that the particles move exactly at the local reconstructed fluid velocity (i.e., based on
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
) during an interaction, or equivalently, that the slip velocity of each particle is at all times zero.
Kim & Karrila (1991, p178)
provides a resistance matrix that expresses the forces, torques and stresslets that the fluid exerts on two interacting particles within a Stokesian linear strain field as a function of the each of the particle’s slip velocities and slip rotations, as well as the undisturbed fluid deviatoric strain tensor. As we shall see in more detail below, we are primarily interested in finding the forces and stresslets that the fluid exerts on the particles in order to calculate
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin}}^{\prime}
, respectively. Hence, in principal the constrained method involves setting the slip velocity of each particle to zero (and potentially the slip rotation too), and then using the binary strain rate tensor
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
to calculate the force, torque and stresslet exerted by the fluid on the particles from the available resistance matrix.

The first complication with this approach is that not all particle trajectories are physically feasible under the constrained approach. Specifically, if the surrounding interacting particle trajectory comes within a distance of
2a
from the reference particle (i.e.,
r<2a
) the particles will overlap, and the resistance matrix coefficients become undefined. As we shall find, particles that come within small distances of each other are particularly important to calculating the suspension stress tensors, so this deficiency of the constrained method is significant. A second issue with the constrained method is that for these particle dynamics to be realised in a multi-particle system, the net force from the remaining particles would have to be sufficient to completely resist the force from the binary interaction. This is a poor assumption, particularly for interacting particles that are closest to the reference particle and hence dominate the generated particle stresses.

By contrast, the central assumption under the free method is that during an interaction each particle is force and torque free. This resolves the overlapping trajectory problem of the constrained method as under the free method particle trajectories do not exactly follow the undisturbed fluid streamlines, but rather deviate from these streamlines according to the stokes flow solution, moving around each other as they pass by (in the case of deviatoric strain). However, due to the (net) force free assumption under the free model, each particle within a binary interaction feels no resistance to movement from the remaining particles. Noting that each pairwise interaction actually occurs within a field of other particles, this is a poor assumption, particularly as the particle concentration increases. Note that under the free model,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}=0
, leading (after subsequent analysis) to
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{int,s}}=0
and no interaction induced particle migration.

As a compromise, in this study we use a ‘semi-free’ model of interaction that calculates the resistance offered by the remaining particles during a binary interaction as intermediate between the constrained and free models. It also avoids the
r<2a
issue of the constrained model. Under this model the trajectories of the interaction particles are calculated using the free model, but then the force that the interacting particle exerts on the reference particle is modelled as that would be required on the reference particle, when in isolation, that causes it to move at the relative velocity predicted from the two particle problem. A similar assumption is used for the torque that the fluid exerts on each particle during the interaction. To find the trajectories of the interacting particles, we use the two particle mobility matrix provided by
Kim & Karrila (1991, p179)
that expresses the particle slip velocities, particle slip rotations, and particle stresslets in terms of the net forces and torques acting on each particle, as well as the undisturbed deviatoric strain tensor. So by setting the force and torque acting on each particle to zero, the slip velocity and slip rotation of both particles can be calculated.

To calculate the value of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{sin}}
under the semi-free model, we recognise that the local undisturbed velocity field defined by
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{bin}}
was constructed so that when in isolation the reference particle moves at the local solid velocity value, implying no slip velocity and no net force on the single isolated particle. Hence
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{sin}}=\boldsymbol{0}_{\mathrm{}}
. To calculate the value of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{bin*}}

(
Kim & Karrila, 1991
, e.g. see)

Finally, the Reynolds stress terms require magnitudes for

6
Conclusions and future work

Figures
2
and
3
summarise the findings of this work. Three simplified momentum equations are supplemented by equations for the interphase forces
\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{drag,s}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{\mathrm{faxen,s}}
, viscous stress definitions for the mixture (
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{dil,m}}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{int,m}}
) and solid (
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{int,s}}
) phases, definitions of strain rates that recognise the physical dimensions of the problem and potentially divergent nature of the phase velocities, and suspension viscosities that quantitatively link the micro-structure of the suspension to its bulk stresses. In paper II we link this microstructure to particle roughness
(
Noori et al., 2024
)
and examine the subsequent performance of the equations for predicting particle migration in dilute and semi-dilute pressure driven suspension flows.

Linking macroscopic suspension properties such as pressure and strain-rates to microscopic analyses involves choices via the design of the closure models. The stress closure model that has split the total stress on each particle as being from a fixed bed and from particle interactions has resulted in the non-Newtonian suspension scale interaction stresses being different for each phase, and the generalised Newtonian suspension scale fixed stresses acting only on the fluid phase. This is consistent with the updates to the SB model
(
Nott et al., 2011
)
, also based on a
Jackson (1997)
type analysis. The pressure gradient appears as acting proportionally on both phases in our equations, consistent with
Jackson (1998)
’s corrected analysis and
Zhang & Acrivos (1994)
’s analysis, but in difference to
Nott et al. (2011)
’s results. This proportionality is a direct result of accounting for pressure in the closure model for the local stress surrounding each particle in the former studies.
Nott et al. (2011)
’s dilute stress closure model only considers the viscous stresses acting on each particle, which appears to be an omission.

A key assumption in the
Batchelor & Green (1972)
type analyses is that interacting particles are modelled as being force free during their binary interactions. If applied through our analysis this assumption would not affect the mixture momentum equations (and subsequent suspension viscosity), but would result in no interaction stress being applied to the solid phase, with the potential effect of causing reverse shear-induced particle migration. Our ‘semi-free’ interaction model recognises that the modelled binary interactions between particles in a suspension can result in non-zero forces on particles, as each particle is interacting with more than one particle at any one time, and it is the sum of these forces that results in the suspension level stress.

The interpretation of
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
as being a stress multiplier around the surface of all of the particles due to particle crowding (i.e., multi-particle effects), has resulted in a faxen force that involves gradients of this parameter. The form of this force needs validation. Via this closure parameter, the averaging process has also linked the suspension’s hindered settling function to its dilute shear viscosity, being two well studied functions. The validity of this result is examined in paper II.
\lambda_{\mathrm{int}}
conceptually models the same increase in stress as
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
, but during the microhydrodynamic particle interactions. On this basis then
\lambda_{\mathrm{int}}
could justifiably be set equal to
\lambda_{\mathrm{fix}}
.
\lambda_{\mathrm{int}}
is more important at higher volume fractions.

There are many aspects of this work that could be improved. Contact forces are neglected, except for the effect they have on the suspension microstructure. This is justified given the scope of the work (semi-dilute), but note that the normal contact forces would be well modelled by the normal hydrodynamic forces employed in this model regardless, as these are determined by the drag on each sphere when moving as a pair. Tangential or friction forces may not be well modelled by the hydrodynamic interaction however, but could be incorporated into the model via alternative mobility functions. Attempts have been made to model the stresses due to bed dilation or compaction (‘bulk’ viscosities), however this has been viewed as secondary to modelling deviatoric strain: Specifically, because the structure of the microstructure PDF is only a function of the deviatoric field, and not the rate of dilation, the resulting model is best viewed as applicable to flow fields that involve small amounts of dilational strain relative to the deviatoric strain. This, however, is probably a reasonable assumption for most application systems, but again could form an avenue for future work. Indeed, a strength of the present analysis is that it links suspension microstructure to bulk behaviour using a defined set of closure assumptions, allowing future models for the microstructure (described by the
f_{i}^{*}
parameters) to be easily converted to continuum-based suspension-scale constitutive equations.

Starting with the fluid based average of two generic variables

131

\nu\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
,
\mathcal{O}\left(a^{2}\nu\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(\nu a^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\nu a^{3}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)
, respectively. Here
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the solid phase averaged angular velocity of the particles. There are two possible assumptions for evaluating the magnitude of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}
. As shown by
Jackson (1997)
and discussed in section
5
, under dilute conditions this angular velocity is continuously driven towards the angular velocity of the fluid, leading to
\mathcal{O}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}/L\right)
. However under concentrated conditions solid particles can ‘jam’ or alternatively slip past each other along fault lines, suggesting instead a higher estimate of
\mathcal{O}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}/a\right)
. For the semi-dilute conditions targeted in this study the lower estimate for
\mathcal{O}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)
is physically more appropriate, however even using the high concentration estimate we find

\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}=n\nu\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)

(132)

showing that solid and particle averaged velocities can be interchanged, given the separation of lengthscales.

A.1.3
Reynolds stress averages

In this section we decompose the inertial advection terms, thus defining the Reynolds stress terms and evaluating the error associated with this averaging process.

Commencing with the fluid phase, the average of the fluid velocity dyadic can be written as

\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}

where as per the main text, the perturbation velocity of the fluid phase is defined within the fluid as
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}
. Now, utilising the repeated average equations (
126
) and (
128
) we find

\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{l}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right)

(133)

however

\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}-\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{l}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{l}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\right)

Therefore the two averaged perturbation terms can be removed from equation (
133
) with no increase in error, giving finally the following relationship for the fluid phase inertial decomposition,

\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}=\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}+\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{f}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{f}}\frac{l}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}\right)

(134)

The solid phase analysis is slightly more complex as we wish to express the Reynolds stress term using solid phase averaged variables, rather than particle phase ones. Recognising that the repeated averaging identities used in the above fluid phase analysis apply also to the particle phase, the solid phase inertial advection term can be written

n\nu\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}=n\nu\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}+n\nu\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{l}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

(135)

where the particle based perturbation velocity is defined as
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime p}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
. Now, using equations (
130
) and (
132
) the first term on the right can be written

\displaystyle n\nu\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\left[\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right]\left[\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

(136)

For the second term we first define the solid based perturbation velocity within the volume of each particle
p
as
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})
, and then expand the following inertial term using Taylor series expansions of both
g(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}|)
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}})
around
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
to give

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\int_{V^{p}}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}|)(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))dV_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)\int_{V^{p}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))dV_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}}{}\cdot\sum_{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)\int_{V^{p}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}))dV_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)\int_{V^{p}}\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})+\mathcal{O}\left((\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right]\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})+\mathcal{O}\left((\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right]dV_{\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n\nu\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

Evaluating the all the terms within the integral that involve the truncation error
\mathcal{O}\left((\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\right)
, we find that overall they contribute the same error to the above expression as the final error that is already included in the expression. So recognising that
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\nu\sum_{p}g(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}|)\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})\right]\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{p})\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(n\nu\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=n\nu\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

(137)

Finally substituting results from equations (
136
) and (
137
) into equation (
135
) gives the final identity

n\nu\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{p}}=\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}+\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{a}{L}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)

(138)

Note that if we had instead defined the solid perturbation velocity using the local solid velocity at
\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{}}
rather than the particle velocity
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}
the error in equation (
138
) using our high concentration assumption for
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}
would have been
\mathcal{O}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)
, and hence non-negligible. This subtle difference highlights the care required in evaluating the solid phase Reynolds stress term to ensure non-negligible averaging errors.

A.2
Dimensional operator notation

The dimensional operator notation
[...]_{\text{D}}
means to evaluate all of the contents of the brackets, recursively if necessarily, in only the physical dimensions of the problem, and to leave all other elements zero. To represent this mathematically in a two dimensional problem using tensor index notation, used for example in the microstructure algebra, a dimensional delta function
d_{i}
is included in every dimensional sum, with this delta defined as

d_{i}=1-\delta_{iD}

(139)

where
D
is the number of the dimension that is not physical. Note that if using implied Einstein summation convention the
d_{i}
subscript does not imply summation. In three dimensional problems
d_{i}=1
always and hence the
[...]_{\text{D}}
operator can be ignored.

To illustrate, the physical dimension unit tensor is

\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{D}}=\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}\right]_{\text{D}}=\sum_{i,j}=\delta_{ij}d_{i}d_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}

(140)

while some strain rate related variables are,

\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph}}=\frac{2}{N}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}\right]_{\text{D}}=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}d_{i}

(141)

\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}}-(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{}})^{\text{T}}-\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph}}\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}\right]_{\text{D}}=\sum_{i,j}\left[\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph}}\delta_{ij}\right]d_{i}d_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}

(142)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{\mathrm{}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{f}}-\frac{N}{2(2+N)}\sum_{i,j,k}\left(\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{k}}+\delta_{ik}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{j}}+\delta_{jk}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}\right]_{\text{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\text{f},k}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}-\frac{N}{2(2+N)}\left(\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{k}}+\delta_{ik}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{j}}+\delta_{jk}\frac{\partial\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{sph,f}}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\right]d_{i}d_{j}d_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}

(143)

where
N
is the number of physical dimensions for the problem.

A.3
Spherical integral identities

The following integrals are used in the derivation of the collision stress tensor. They are evaluated around a sphere surface
S_{r}
of radius
r
, assuming that
\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}
is symmetric and traceless, and that
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}
is an outward pointing normal to the sphere surface:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\frac{8\pi r^{2}}{15}\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}

(144)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\int_{S_{r}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}dS

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\int_{S_{r}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}dS=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{3}\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}

(145)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\frac{16\pi r^{2}}{105}\left[2\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}+4\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}\right]

(146)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\int_{S_{r}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\int_{S_{r}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi r^{2}}{15}\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}

(147)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{5}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}dS=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{3}\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}

(148)

\displaystyle I_{6}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}dS=4\pi r^{2}

(149)

\displaystyle I_{7}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}dS=0

(150)

\displaystyle I_{8}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\frac{16\pi r^{2}}{15}\dot{\gamma}^{2}

(151)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{9}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{I}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{}}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{3}\epsilon_{lik}\Omega_{l}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}

(152)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{10}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{}}\times\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{}}\right)dS=\boldsymbol{0}_{\mathrm{}}

(153)

where
\dot{\gamma}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}:\boldsymbol{\dot{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{}}}
. In deriving these relationships we have used the following identities:

\displaystyle\int_{S_{r}}n_{i}n_{j}dS

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{3}\delta_{ij}

(154)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{r}}n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}dS

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{15}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{lj}+\delta_{il}\delta_{kj}\right)

(155)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{r}}n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}n_{m}n_{n}dS

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{105}\left[\delta_{ij}\left(\delta_{kl}\delta_{mn}+\delta_{km}\delta_{ln}+\delta_{kn}\delta_{lm}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\delta_{ik}\left(\delta_{jl}\delta_{mn}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{ln}+\delta_{jn}\delta_{lm}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\delta_{il}\left(\delta_{jk}\delta_{mn}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{kn}+\delta_{jn}\delta_{km}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\delta_{im}\left(\delta_{jk}\delta_{ln}+\delta_{jl}\delta_{kn}+\delta_{jn}\delta_{kl}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\delta_{in}\left(\delta_{jk}\delta_{lm}+\delta_{jl}\delta_{km}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}\right)\right]

(156)

The first two of these identities are quoted in
Jackson (1997)
. The third was calculated using computational symbolic algebra applied to all the possible combinations of the six normal coefficients.
