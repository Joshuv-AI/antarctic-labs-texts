Atherogenic index of plasma as a predictor of cardiovascular outcomes in patients with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries
Authors: Side Gao, Sizhuang Huang, Xinming Liu, Mengyue Yu, Lin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherogenic index of plasma (AIP) has emerged as a novel marker of atherosclerosis and a predictor of outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease. However, the prognostic significance of AIP in a distinct population with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) remains unclear. This prospective cohort study included 1179 patients with MINOCA who were stratified by the tertile levels of AIP defined as base 10 logarithm of the ratio of fasting triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) including all-cause death, reinfarction, stroke, revascularization and hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure. Over the median follow-up of 41.7 months, patients with higher AIP tertiles had more cardiometabolic risk factors and a significantly higher incidence of MACE (9.9%, 14.3%, 18.6%; p = 0.002). Elevated AIP was associated with an increased risk of MACE even after multivariable adjustment [tertile 1 of AIP as reference; tertile 2: hazard ratio (HR) 1.50, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.03–2.32, p = 0.036; tertile 3: HR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.21–2.63, p = 0.003]. AIP as a continuous marker remained a robust risk factor in all patients and across subgroup analyses. A near-linear relationship was observed between AIP levels and the risk of MACE following MINOCA. Moreover, AIP showed a moderate ability to predict MACE with an area under the curve of 0.67. Higher AIP levels were independently associated with poorer outcomes after MINOCA. These data indicate the role of atherogenic dyslipidemia in MINOCA pathogenesis and support the utility of AIP for risk stratification in this population.Keywords: Myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), Atherogenic index of plasma, Cardiovascular outcomesSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The global prevalence of dyslipidemia is rising at an alarming rate and closely linked to the growing burden of cardiovascular comorbidities worldwide. Atherogenic dyslipidemia, a distinct lipid phenotype characterized by elevated triglycerides (TG), low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and elevated lipoprotein (a), is a well-established, modifiable risk factor for the development and progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD)1. Notably, even in individuals with optimally controlled LDL-C—the primary target of current lipid-lowering guidelines—concurrent elevated TG and reduced HDL-C level confer a significantly increased risk of incident CVD, highlighting the limitations of isolated LDL-C assessment in capturing full atherogenic risk2. To address this gap, the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the base 10 logarithm of the TG/HDL-C ratio, has emerged as a novel, integrated marker of plasma atherogenicity. Derived from the standard lipid panels at no additional cost, AIP reflects the balance between pro-atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoproteins. Its calculation normalizes the skewed distribution of the raw TG/HDL-C ratio to enhance its clinical utility3. Emerging data has validated AIP as a robust correlate of key cardiometabolic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, arterial stiffness, coronary artery calcification, and both subclinical and symptomatic coronary artery disease (CAD)4–10. Recent studies further reported that elevated AIP independently predicted outcomes in the general population and in those with established obstructive CAD11–17, solidifying its role as a prognostic lipid marker in traditional CVD cohorts.

Myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), defined as ischemia-driven acute myocardial infarction (AMI) with < 50% stenosis in all major coronary arteries on invasive angiography18, accounts for 6–15% of all AMI cases and represents a heterogeneous entity with multifactorial pathogenesis—including atherosclerotic plaque disruption, coronary spasm, spontaneous dissection, embolic events, and microvascular dysfunction19,20. Accumulating data reveals that patients with MINOCA still face a substantially elevated risk of recurrent events, comparable to that of those with obstructive AMI in some cohorts, despite the receipt of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT)21–25. This residual risk indicates a critical unmet need for robust, accessible risk stratification tools in MINOCA, as current strategies often rely on traditional clinical factors (e.g., LDL-C alone) and may be insufficient to capture the full spectrum of atherogenic risk in this population. Also, dyslipidemia is highly prevalent in MINOCA patients, yet the utility of integrated lipid markers in risk prediction remains unexplored. While AIP has been validated in obstructive CAD, its prognostic implications in the distinct, understudied MINOCA population have not been previously elucidated. Given its low cost, ease of calculation from routine lipid testing, and proven prognostic value in other CVD cohorts, the novel index AIP may be a promising marker to address the unmet risk stratification needs in MINOCA.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the association between baseline AIP levels and long-term cardiovascular outcomes in a prospective cohort of MINOCA. We assessed whether AIP independently predicted outcomes in this population after adjustment for conventional risk factors and lipid markers, and evaluated its utility as a complementary risk stratification tool for clinical practice. Our findings may fill a gap in MINOCA management and provide clinical evidence for the potential value of integrated lipid profiling in this high-risk, heterogeneous patient group.

Methods

Study population

The present study established a long-term single-center prospective observational cohort of MINOCA patients who were consecutively recruited at Fuwai Hospital from Jan. 2015 to Dec. 2019, as previously reported25. The diagnosis of MINOCA was made in accordance with the Fourth Universal Definition of AMI20, combined with angiographic evidence of < 50% stenosis in coronary arteries. We primarily enrolled MINOCA patients with coronary-related etiologies, and atherosclerotic mechanisms account for the majority of cases. Patients with MI-like symptoms but non-coronary myocardial injury and no ischemic features (e.g., heart failure, myocarditis) were excluded. Other coronary-related causes (e.g., coronary spasm) may coexist with the atherosclerotic subtype, though they constituted only a small proportion of the cohort. Ultimately, 1179 eligible patients with or without ST-segment elevation (STEMI or NSTEMI) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Patients were stratified into three groups based on the tertile levels of AIP as follows: Tertile 1 (T1): AIP < 0.04, Tertile 2 (T2): 0.04 ≤ AIP < 0.27, Tertile 3 (T3): AIP ≥ 0.27. Patients received GDMT as part of routine clinical care. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital and was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Data collection and study endpoints

Baseline data were collected from electronic medical records and cross-checked for accuracy by two investigators. Fasting lipid profiles including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer. LDL-C concentrations were calculated using the Friedewald formula. Fasting blood glucose, peak troponin I (TnI), and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) at admission were recorded. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed via echocardiography using the biplane Simpson method. As previously described3, AIP was calculated as the base 10 logarithmic value of the ratio between fasting TG and HDL-C [log10 (TG/HDL-C), both in mmol/L]. This index integrates elevated TG and reduced HDL-C levels by logarithmic transformation to enhance its linear association with plasma atherogenicity and acts as a robust risk predictor independent of LDL-C3. Comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were diagnosed in line with the latest clinical practice guidelines, based on medical history, laboratory test results, and ongoing medication use.

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of first major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE), a composite endpoint including all-cause death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, unplanned ischemia-driven coronary revascularization, and hospitalization for unstable angina (UA) or heart failure (HF). A secondary “hard” composite endpoint was also defined, comprising death, nonfatal MI, stroke, and revascularization. All adverse events were adjudicated by a blinded committee of expert cardiologists and biostatisticians who were unaware of AIP group allocation to ensure data reliability. Standardized diagnostic criteria were applied for each endpoint. Briefly, reinfarction was diagnosed per the 4th Universal Definition of AMI26; stroke was defined by the presence of new-onset neurological impairment and corresponding cerebrovascular lesions on neuroimaging27; revascularization was performed at the discretion of the physician based on evidence of myocardial ischemia and worsening coronary stenosis; hospitalization for UA or HF was regarded as unplanned inpatient admission due to clinical deterioration of these conditions after the index MI. Patients were followed up through telephone interviews or face-to-face clinic visits at 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter until the end of the observation period. The follow-up procedures were conducted using a pre-designed structured questionnaire by trained, independent research staff to ensure consistency of data collection.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range; categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Group differences were compared using one-way analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis H test, and Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to estimate the cumulative incidence of MACE across AIP tertile groups, with comparisons assessed using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to explore the association between AIP levels and cardiovascular outcomes, with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated to quantify the risk magnitude. Covariates in the multivariable Cox model included age, sex, MI type (STEMI or NSTEMI), body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia considering the clinical relevance of these variables and their uneven distribution across AIP tertile groups. These variables are well-recognized confounders with prognostic implication in both general AMI and MINOCA populations, and some of these covariates were unevenly distributed across AIP tertiles, indicating they were potential confounders of the AIP-MACE association. Other covariates such as LVEF, peak TnI, and NT-proBNP were comparable among groups and were not included. Still, some potential confounders including lifestyles and the intensity and adherence to lipid-lowering therapy were unmeasured.

The AIP was analyzed as both a continuous variable and a categorical variable (per tertiles) to comprehensively evaluate its prognostic value. For categorical analysis, the tertile stratification was used to explore the dose-response relationship between AIP levels and MACE risk, with T1 (AIP < 0.04) set as the reference group. Additionally, to identify the optimal AIP cut-off value for MACE prediction in MINOCA, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed, and the Youden’s index was calculated to determine the cut-off value (AIP = 0.18) that maximized the balance between sensitivity and specificity for MACE prediction. Patients were dichotomized into low-AIP (< 0.18) and high-AIP (≥ 0.18) groups for further subgroup analysis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to visualize the dose-response relationship between AIP and MACE risk, using the median AIP level as the reference value (HR = 1). Four knots were set at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the AIP distribution based on standard statistical practice for RCS analysis. The subgroup Cox regression analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the AIP-MACE association across subgroups defined by age, sex, BMI, MI type, and the presence of hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia. The predictive accuracy of AIP for MACE was assessed by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), with AUC values categorized as small (0.56–0.63), moderate (0.64–0.70), and strong (≥ 0.71)28. The DeLong’s test was used to compare the AUC values of AIP with those of isolated TG and HDL-C. All analyses were 2-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and R software (version 4.2.1; https://www.r-project.org/). The survival, rms, ggplot2, and plotRCS packages in R were used for RCS analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline data across AIP tertile groups were compared (tertile 1: AIP < 0.04, tertile 2: 0.04 ≤ AIP < 0.27, tertile 3: AIP ≥ 0.27) (Table 1). Patients with higher tertile of AIP were younger and more often male. As expected, this group had higher levels of LDL-C and TG, alongside lower HDL-C and elevated fasting glucose concentrations. They also had a higher body mass index (BMI), a greater proportion of STEMI cases, and increased rates of diabetes and dyslipidemia. Individuals with higher AIP tertiles were more likely to be prescribed angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB) and β-blockers. The cardiac function, peak TnI levels, and other medications did not differ significantly among the groups.

Association between AIP and outcomes

A total of 168 patients experienced a MACE over a median follow-up of 3.5 years. Of these, 18 died, 41 had reinfarction, 12 had a stroke, 46 underwent revascularization, and 71 was hospitalized for UA and 48 for HF. Individuals with higher tertiles of AIP had a significantly higher incidence of MACE (9.9%, 14.3%, 18.6%; p = 0.002) (Table 2). The rate of the composite “hard” endpoint of death, MI, revascularization or stroke also increased in patients with rising AIP tertiles (5.8%, 8.9%, 11.2%; p = 0.027). The Kaplan-Meier curves further showed a higher cumulative incidence of adverse events in higher AIP tertile groups (log rank p = 0.012) (Fig. 2).

At multivariate Cox analysis, elevated AIP levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE (for per 1SD increase in AIP, HR 2.72, 95% CI: 1.60–4.61, p < 0.001). Patients with rising tertiles of AIP also had an increased risk of MACE even after multivariate adjustment (tertile 1 as reference; tertile 2: HR 1.50, 95% CI: 1.03–2.32, p = 0.036; tertile 3: HR 1.78, 95% CI: 1.21–2.63, p = 0.003) (Table 3). The RCS analysis exhibited a near-linear relationship between AIP levels and the risk of MACE (Fig. 3). Further, we used ROC analysis to identify the optimal cutoff value of AIP for MACE prediction, which was identified as 0.18. The rates of MACE in patients with AIP below and above the cutoff were 10.1% and 18.9%, respectively (HR 2.07, 95% CI: 1.48–2.90, p < 0.001). At subgroup analyses (Fig. 4), an AIP level above the cutoff remained a risk factor of MACE across different subgroups, indicating that the impact of AIP was independent of clinically relevant covariates and conventional risk factors.

Discussion

In the present study, we confirmed the prognostic value of AIP in a unique cohort of MINOCA. As an integrated lipid index that quantifies the combined imbalance of elevated TG and reduced HDL-C, AIP was independently associated with long-term cardiovascular outcomes after MINOCA and enabled more precise risk stratification for MACE compared with isolated measurements of TG or HDL-C alone. These data align with previous findings regarding the impact of elevated AIP on poorer outcomes in patients with obstructive CAD and further extend the clinical significance of AIP to the distinct MINOCA population. More notably, our findings underscore the utility of AIP as a reliable and valid marker of atherogenic potential, supporting its role in risk stratification in the contemporary real-world management of MINOCA patients.

MINOCA represents a clinically heterogeneous entity with a complex spectrum of underlying mechanisms, which necessitates comprehensive diagnostic assessments to identify the root cause of ischemia, as such characterization not only enables accurate risk stratification but also facilitates the implementation of etiology-driven, tailored therapeutic strategies18,19. In line with previous studies, atherosclerotic mechanisms constitute the majority of cases in our cohort. However, due to the limited availability of multi-modal imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), the precise causes of MINOCA for each patients were not fully captured. This limitation may underestimate subtle atherosclerosis and prevent further investigation into the relation between AIP and MINOCA phenotypes. Recently, the outcomes of MINOCA have raised more concerns given a considerably high event risk in this population21–25. Consistently, our study observed a mortality rate of 1.5% and a MACE rate of 14.2% during follow-up, highlighting the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors and optimize daily care for MINOCA patients.

Dyslipidemia is present in a wide range of cardiometabolic disorders and markedly contributes to the development of CVD1. With the widespread use of statin therapy for primary or secondary settings targeting LDL-C, its unequivocal benefits have been witnessed in the reduction of CVD risk29. However, there is still residual risk among statin-treated individuals, even with low LDL-C levels30,31. In this context, the role of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) and HDL-C in atherogenesis should be noted. Previous studies have shown that TRLs, a family of lipoproteins defined by their high TG content, can stimulate inflammation, enhance oxidative stress, promote foam cell formation, impair endothelial function, and promote plaque initiation and growth31,32. By contrast, HDL-C exerts its anti-atherogenic effects by clearing excess cholesterol. Atherogenesis rarely occurs due to isolated TRL elevation or HDL-C reduction—it is typically driven by an imbalance between these two lipoproteins33,34. Conditions like metabolic syndrome cause both elevated TRLs and reduced HDL-C, which creates a “pro-atherogenic milieu”: TRLs drive plaque initiation and inflammation, while HDL-C’s ability to counter these processes is weakened. Hence, it is not surprising that both TG and HDL-C correlate with cardiovascular risk, independent of LDL-C and other known risk factors. Yet, isolated measurements of TG or HDL-C may not fully confer the residual risk—this gap has driven the development and validation of AIP.

As reported, AIP defined as log10 (TG/HDL-C) is a validated, cost-effective marker of atherogenic dyslipidemia that directly reflects the balance between pro-atherogenic TRLs and anti-atherogenic HDL-C. Increasing evidence has showed that AIP closely correlates with a broad spectrum of cardiometabolic and atherosclerotic diseases4–10. More recently, AIP has been confirmed as an independent predictor of adverse events in general population and in patients with obstructive CAD—including stable CAD, acute coronary syndromes, and those undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention11–17. In these populations, elevated AIP not only predicts incident MACE and CAD progression but also improves risk stratification in high-risk subgroups (e.g., patients with diabetes, metabolic syndrome, or chronic kidney disease) where traditional lipid markers often underestimate risk35,36. Importantly, AIP was not developed to replace conventional lipid markers such as LDL-C; instead, it complements these metrics to build a more comprehensive risk profile by capturing non-LDL-C lipid dysregulation.

Despite these evidence, however, data on the prognostic value of AIP in MINOCA population are lacking. Our findings fill this gap by demonstrating that elevated AIP is closely associated with an increased risk of MACE after MINOCA, an association that remained significant after rigorous adjustment for traditional risk factors and lipid markers. Furthermore, an AIP level beyond the optimal cut-off of 0.18 was a robust predictor of MACE in entire cohort and across subgroups, including those on statin therapy and patients with LDL-C levels below the target of 1.8 mmol/L. This latter finding is of particular clinical importance: statins effectively lower LDL-C but often fail to fully correct the TG/HDL-C imbalance, leaving a reservoir of residual risk that AIP uniquely detects. Our results thus highlight the critical role of non-LDL-C lipid dysregulation and reveal that TG/HDL-C imbalance contributes to adverse outcomes after MINOCA beyond the atherogenic burden mediated by LDL-C alone.

Further, AIP exhibited a moderate predictive value for MACE in MINOCA patients, with an AUC of 0.67. This moderate AUC is consistent with the performance of most lipid-based prognostic markers in CVD, and it is necessary to interpret this value in a balanced context. First, a moderate AUC does not reflect a limitation of AIP itself, but rather a complex interplay of lipid, inflammatory, hemodynamic, genetic, and lifestyle factors, and no single biomarker can capture all of these contributors to risk. Second, AIP should be regarded as a complementary rather than a standalone tool to augment existing risk stratification strategies. That said, when integrated with other established risk factors, AIP offers information regarding lipid-mediated atherogenic potential and thereby enhances the accuracy of risk prediction in MINOCA.

Mechanistically, the prognostic value of AIP in MINOCA may be largely explained by the link between AIP and atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability. Emerging evidence has validated AIP as an independent predictor of high-risk vulnerable plaque features, including thin cap, large necrotic core, plaque inflammation, microcalcification, and plaque disruption, all of which are tightly associated with acute thrombotic events and adverse clinical outcomes37,38. Atherosclerotic plaque disruption constitutes the most prevalent underlying mechanism in MINOCA. In this context, elevated AIP reflects a pro-atherogenic lipid milieu characterized by TRLs accumulation and reduced HDL-C, which can destabilize coronary plaques and trigger acute ischemic events. Therefore, AIP not only serves as a statistical predictor but also biologically mirrors the plaque vulnerability burden that drives recurrent MACE in MINOCA patients.

Our study has several clinical implications. First, AIP can facilitate more precise prognostic stratification in patients with MINOCA. In routine practice, an elevated AIP may indicate a higher atherogenic risk and a greater likelihood of poor outcomes. Second, AIP assessment may guide personalized lipid-lowering therapy in MINOCA beyond the LDL-C-centric approach. Undoubtedly, LDL-C remains the primary target while AIP can serve as a secondary target for patients with well-controlled LDL-C yet high AIP, a common scenario in those with metabolic syndrome or insulin resistance. Such patients may benefit from adjunctive lipid-lowering agents to correct TG/HDL-C imbalance and mitigate lipid-related residual risk. Randomized controlled trials are warranted to explore the benefits of AIP-targeted therapy in MINOCA patients. Third, AIP is highly responsive to lifestyle modifications (e.g., dietary changes, increased physical activity) and medications, making it an ideal marker for monitoring treatment response and adherence. A failure of AIP to decrease with therapy may indicate poor adherence to lifestyle changes or suboptimal pharmacologic dosing, and a persistently elevated AIP acts as a red flag to prompt re-evaluation and more aggressive strategies. Collectively, these implications position AIP as a valuable tool to address the unmet needs of MINOCA management.

Limitation

Certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. To begin with, the results presented herein are derived from a single-center and may not be representative. This inherently restricts both the overall sample size and the total count of observed events and may introduce selection bias. Thus, large-scale, nationwide registry-based studies of MINOCA are warranted. Second, as with all observational research, the current study is unable to establish a causal relationship between AIP levels and the prognosis after MINOCA. Even though we accounted for a range of confounding factors via multivariate adjustment and subgroup analyses, the potential impact of residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. Third, we did not capture the exact mechanism for every patient due to the low usage of multi-modal imaging such as OCT and CMR. This diagnostic limitation is inherent to our study design which relied on conventional diagnostic tools rather than comprehensive multi-modal approaches, and may lead to under-diagnosis of subtle atherosclerosis and other mechanisms. In this regard, the prognostic value of AIP across different MINOCA subtypes requires further validation. Fourth, the measurements of AIP were only obtained at baseline. In clinical practice, the dynamic changes in AIP levels during treatment and the specific patterns of such fluctuations may hold greater clinical relevance for guiding patient management.

Conclusion

Elevated AIP was independently associated with an increased risk of MACE after MINOCA, even after adjustment for conventional risk factors and lipid markers. AIP exhibited moderate predictive accuracy and may act as a complementary biomarker of TG/HDL-C imbalance for risk stratification in this population. If confirmed in larger cohorts, AIP might be incorporated into routine clinical care to improve residual risk stratification and optimize dyslipidemia management for patients with MINOCA.
