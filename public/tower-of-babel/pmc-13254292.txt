Insects manipulate host nuclear trafficking and epigenetic regulation to facilitate herbivory
Authors: Ze-Long Zhang, Tang-Bin Hu, Xin-Ye Xu, Hai-Bin Lu, Jia-Bao Lu, Ji-Chong Zhuo, Yi-Zhe Wang, Zong-Tao Sun, Jian-Ping Chen, Jun-Min Li, Chuan-Xi Zhang, Hai-Jian Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effectors secreted by pathogens or insects manipulate host plant cellular processes depending on their target destination. However, our current knowledge regarding nucleus-localized effectors from herbivorous insects remains limited. Here, we demonstrate that Nilaparvata lugens evolve a nuclear localization signal (NLS)-containing salivary effector NlAMSP that is specialized for targeting host plants. NlAMSP resides in the cytoplasm of insect salivary glands, but, upon secretion, migrates into the nucleus of rice cells. This nuclear translocation is enabled by the cleavage of its signal peptide, allowing the NLS-dependent import via the host importin-α/β pathway. SUMOylation at sites within the NLS is essential for the NlAMSP function, enhancing its nuclear localization and protein stability by preventing autophagy-associated degradation. In plants, NlAMSP interacts with the histone deacetylase OsHDA706 and redirects it from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby disrupting its interaction with the JA biosynthesis regulator OsLOX14 in the cytoplasm. This interference reduces OsLOX14 accumulation and suppresses the JA-associated defense responses. Furthermore, nucleus-localized OsHDA706 diminishes histone H4K5ac and H4K8ac, thereby suppressing the expression of NLR and WRKY genes essential for rice resistance to N. lugens. Our findings uncover a mechanism by which an insect effector manipulates host nuclear trafficking and epigenetic regulation to facilitate herbivory.

Introduction

Plants, including the cultivated crops, are constantly exposed to biotic stresses from herbivorous insects, bacteria, and fungi. To survive or fend off attacks, plants have evolved specific receptors to perceive the “non-self” molecules or the wounded signals, and activate the pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), including the changes in hormonal signaling, a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the activation of defense genes1–3. In response, attackers secrete a mixture of effectors into the plant cells to interfere with PTI4,5. Although some effectors can be recognized by the host resistance (R) genes, attackers have further evolved strategies to evade the effector-triggered immunity (ETI)6. Understanding the functions of effectors can not only reveal the regulatory mechanisms of plant immunity but also promote crop protection.

Transcriptional reprogramming is a significant outcome of plant PTI and ETI signaling7. Histone acetylation, a key epigenetic modification, exerts a critical role in reshaping chromatin structure and controlling gene transcription8. Histone acetylation is a reversible process where specific lysine residues are acetylated or deacetylated by the action of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs)7. Typically, histone acetylation promotes gene expression, while deacetylation suppresses it. For example, in Arabidopsis, HATs from the CBP/p300-family form a complex with the nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related genes 1 (NPR1) to boost the transcription of pathogenesis-related genes (PRs)9; while in rice plants, histone deacetylase 701 (OsHDA701) negatively regulates plant immunity by decreasing the histone H4 acetylation of defense-related genes10. In addition to histones, HATs and HDACs also acetylate and deacetylate specific lysine sites of other proteins. This non-histone acetylation is widely involved in plant biological processes such as growth, hormone signal transduction, and stress responses11–13. For example, in Arabidopsis, the jasmonate (JA) signaling pathway is controlled by the reversible acetylation of TOPLESS14, while in rice, OsHDA714 controls the lysine acetylation of ribosomal proteins15. Despite considerable research on plant HATs and HDACs, it remains largely unknown how plants coordinate histone and non-histone acetylation to regulate immunity.

Considering the importance of acetylation in plant immunity, pathogens have developed various strategies to cope with this regulatory network during the long-term coevolution. Fungi, nematodes, and bacteria have been extensively reported to employ secreted metabolites or effectors to regulate the enzymatic activities and subcellular location of plant HAT/HDAC11,16,17. In the interaction between geminivirus and tobacco, the viral protein V2 interacts with NbHDA6 and suppresses the methylation-mediated transcriptional gene silencing by competing with the interaction of NbHDA6 with NbMET118. In the Ustilaginoidea virens and rice system, the fungal effector UvSec117 interferes with the activation of host defense genes by recruiting OsHDA701 to the nucleus and enhancing the OsHDA701-modulated deacetylation19. Recently, a fierce arms race focusing on non-histone acetylation has been reported in plants and viruses. The rice OsHDA706 interacts with and deacetylates the JA biosynthetic enzyme OsLOX14 to promote its stability, leading to improved antiviral immunity. In response, the virus encodes an effector protein P2 to disrupt this physical association, thus reducing the OsLOX14 stabilization20. Compared with extensive studies on the acetylation-mediated pathogen-plant interaction, few studies have assessed the role of histone/non-histone acetylation in the plant response to insect herbivory21,22. Particularly, to the best of our knowledge, no insect-derived component has been reported to regulate the plant acetylation network.

Insect effectors modulate diverse host cellular processes in a target-dependent manner. These proteins are broadly categorized as extracellular or intracellular, with the latter being further classified by their subcellular localization, such as the cytoplasm, nucleus, or chloroplast23. For example, in Myzus persicae, effectors like MpPIntO1 and MpC002 accumulate in salivary sheaths around the feeding sites, while Mp10 targets the cytoplasm and chloroplasts of mesophyll cells24. Although a few nucleus-localized effectors have been identified, such as Nilaparvata lugens SR45-interacting salivary protein (NlSISP), Apolygus lucorum ASP1, and Sitobion miscanthi SmCSP4, their nuclear import mechanisms and functions remain poorly understood25–27. To date, only one chewing insect effector, Helicoverpa armigera HARP1, has been well-characterized in this context. Remarkably, there is no detectable nuclear localization signal (NLS) motif for nuclear importation in HARP128. It exploits the protein-mediated endocytosis to access the plant nucleus, where it disrupts JA signaling via direct interactions with JAZ repressors28,29. NLS-dependent nuclear targeting has been reported in bacterial and fungal pathogens23, but it remains largely unknown whether comparable mechanisms exist in insect species, and whether NLS functions when salivary effectors reside in the insect salivary glands and are secreted into host plants.

The brown planthopper, Nilaparvata lugens, is a destructive pest that causes severe yield reductions and economic losses in rice crops globally. Like other herbivorous insects, the successful feeding and extensive damage of N. lugens are closely associated with its secreted saliva30,31. Our previous studies have comprehensively analyzed N. lugens salivary components, which reveal the presence of numerous planthopper-specific proteins with unknown function32. Although these salivary proteins are restricted to limited taxa, some play critical roles in the establishment of compatible insect-plant interactions33. For example, the salivary sheath protein LsSP1 assists in planthopper feeding by inhibiting cysteine protease-mediated defense activation34. The salivary protein BISP targets rice receptor-like kinases to suppress basal defenses, but can be recognized by R genes in resistant plants35. Species-specific genes are believed to be particularly significant for taxon-specific adaptations and environmental interactions36. Nevertheless, our knowledge of the functions of these proteins in the planthopper-rice interaction remains scarce. In the present study, a nucleus-destinated salivary effector was employed as a molecular probe to unveil the mechanism of planthoppers in remodeling plant defense regulated by the histone/non-histone acetylation network, thereby providing mechanistic insights into the insect-plant interactions.

Results

NlAMSP is a salivary effector that migrates into the rice nucleus after secretion

While the presence of NLS motifs in the secreted effectors has been documented in a few pathogens23, it remains largely unknown whether similar features are prevalent in insects. Our previous work identified 41 signal peptide-containing proteins in the saliva of N. lugens37. Among these, three proteins were predicted to contain putative NLS motifs, including NlSISP (accession: OQ470537.1), NlSalivarp7 (accession: KU365966.1), and one previously uncharacterized protein (accession: MF278711.1) (Table S1). NlSISP was recently shown to interact with the rice splicing factor OsSR45 in the plant nucleus27, whereas NlSalivarp7 mediates tricin metabolism38. In contrast, the uncharacterized protein MF278711.1 has not been functionally investigated to date and was tentatively designated N. lugens acetylation-manipulating salivary protein (NlAMSP) based on its subsequently revealed function. To examine their subcellular localization in planta, truncated versions of these proteins lacking signal peptides were fused to GFP tags and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana. NlSISP and NlAMSP were predominantly localized to the nucleus, whereas NlSalivarp7 did not show nucleus-specificity (Fig. S1). Transcriptomic analyses based on transcript-per-million (TPM) values revealed that NlAMSP exhibited a transcript abundance more than five-fold higher than that of NlSISP (Table S1). Given its previously uncharacterized function and higher transcript abundance, we selected NlAMSP for further analysis. NlAMSP contained a 981-nt open reading frame, encoding a protein of 326 amino acids (Fig. S2). No conserved domain was detected in NlAMSP, and no homologous AMSP gene was detected outside the Delphacidae (Table S2).

Based on spatial-temporal expression analysis, NlAMSP was highly expressed at the nymph and adult stages, but lowly expressed at the egg stage when N. lugens did not feed (Fig. 1a). NlAMSP was a salivary gland-specific protein, with nearly no transcript or protein detected in other tissues (Fig. 1b, c). Additionally, from LC-MS/MS analysis of watery saliva collected from N. lugens feeding on artificial diets, two unique peptides derived from NlAMSP were identified (Fig. S3). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining with α-NlAMSP antibody showed that NlAMSP was mainly distributed in a pair of follicles in primary salivary glands (Fig. 1d). Notably, NlAMSP is restricted in the cytoplasm of salivary glands, but not the nucleus (Fig. 1d). NlAMSP was a salivary protein that was secreted into rice plants during N. lugens feeding, with a band approximately 35 kDa detected in the infested plants but not the non-infested ones (Fig. 1e). The distribution of secreted NlAMSP in rice plants was further investigated by IHC staining. The results indicated that NlAMSP was not only detected in salivary sheath, but also in the rice vascular bundle near the salivary sheath (Fig. 1f). Interestingly, the majority of NlAMSP fluorescence signals were overlapped with rice cell nucleus (Fig. 1f). Immunoelectron microscopy observations of N. lugens-infested plants further validated the presence of NlAMSP in rice cell nucleus, as indicated by the 15-nm immunogold mainly distributed in the nucleus but not in the other organelles (Fig. 1g). NlAMSP promotes N. lugens feeding

To assess the functional relevance of NlAMSP in insect feeding, NlAMSP was silenced by RNA interference. Treating N. lugens with dsNlAMSP efficiently suppressed the target gene at both transcriptional and protein levels (Fig. S4a, b). No significant difference in survivorship was observed between dsGFP and dsNlAMSP treatments (Fig. S4c). However, the fecundity of dsNlAMSP-treated N. lugens was significantly reduced (Fig. 2a). Honeydew is an indicator of feeding status in planthopper species39. The amount of honeydew excreted by dsNlAMSP-treated N. lugens was significantly reduced compared with the control (Fig. 2b). Therefore, the electrical penetration graph (EPG) was used to monitor the insect feeding behavior (Fig. S5). Salivary proteins are generally thought to manipulate host plant physiological processes, thereby influencing insect performance. To assess whether NlAMSP modulates defense responses in rice, we compared the feeding preferences of N. lugens nymphs on plants previously infested with dsGFP- or dsNlAMSP-treated insects according to the method previously described34. The results showed that rice plants exposed to dsNlAMSP -treated N. lugens were significantly less preferred by nymphs than plants pre-infested with dsGFP-treated controls (Fig. 2d), suggesting that dsNlAMSP-treated N. lugens might elicit plant defenses.

To investigate the potential role of NlAMSP in host plants, transgenic rice plants overexpressing NlAMSP-sp were constructed. NlAMSP-sp was constitutively expressed under the control of a CaMV 35S promoter, and two independent homozygous lines (oxSP#1 and oxSP#2) were selected (Fig. S6a). The empty vector (EV) transgenic plant was used as a negative control. From bioassay results, N. lugens excreted more honeydew (non-significant for oxSP#1; significant for oxSP#2) on oxSP plants than on EV plants (Fig. 2e), and oxSP plants were more attractive than EV plants (Fig. 2f; Fig. S6b). EPG analysis showed that insects feeding on oxSP plants spent a significantly longer duration in the xylem ingestion phase (N5), whereas the duration of the phloem ingestion phase (N4) did not differ significantly from that of the control (Fig. S6c). It is noteworthy that this feeding pattern differs from that observed in NlAMSP-silenced insects, in which phloem ingestion (N4) was markedly reduced. This inconsistency likely reflects the fundamentally different experimental contexts, as overexpression of NlAMSP in plants may not fully recapitulate the effects of NlAMSP naturally secreted by insects during feeding. Collectively, these results suggest that NlAMSP plays a role in N. lugens feeding on rice plants by affecting plant physiology.

NlAMSP interacts with rice OsHDA706 and redirects it from the cytoplasm to the nucleus

To understand the potential roles of NlAMSP in the insect-plant interaction, NlAMSP-sp was used as a bait to screen a rice cDNA library using yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen assays. Ten proteins were identified as the potential interactors, including the OsHDA706 (Table S3). OsHDA706 (LOC_Os06g37420) contained a 1059-nt open reading frame, encoding a protein of 352 amino acids. No nuclear localization signal was detected inside OsHDA706 (Fig. 3a). The relative transcript levels of OsHDA706 in response to N. lugens infestation were first investigated. The expression of OsHDA706 was significantly induced at 12 h post-infestation (Fig. 3b). Point-to-point Y2H assays demonstrated that OsHDA706 specifically interacted with NlAMSP(22–71), the region prior to NLS sequences (Fig. 3c, d). The interaction between NlAMSP-sp and OsHDA706 was further confirmed by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BIFC) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP assays). In the BIFC assay, strong yellow fluorescence signals were detected in the cell nucleus when nYFP-OsHDA706 was co-expressed with cYFP-NlAMSP-sp in N. benthamiana or rice protoplast (Fig. 3e; Fig. S7), indicating that OsHDA706 interacted with NlAMSP in the nucleus, in accordance with the finding that NlAMSP migrated into rice nucleus after secretion. The NlAMSP-sp was mainly distributed in the cell nucleus, while OsHDA706 was mainly located in the cell cytoplasm when it was transiently expressed alone (Fig. S8). Thereafter, OsHDA706-mCherry was co-expressed with NlAMSP-sp-GFP and GUS-GFP, respectively. According to our findings, OsHDA706-mCherry was almost totally transported to the cell nucleus in the presence of NlAMSP-sp-GFP (Figs. 3g; S9). In contrast, GUS-GFP control did not affect the location of OsHDA706-mCherry (Figs. 3g; S9). Western-blotting analysis came to similar results, with a more intense band of OsHDA706-flag in the nuclear sample when NlAMSP-sp-myc was co-expressed (Fig. 3h). These findings indicate that NlAMSP affects OsHDA706 by redirecting it from the cytoplasm to the nucleus.

NlAMSP abolishes OsHDA706-OsLOX14 association and reduces OsLOX14 accumulation

To investigate the influence of OsHDA706 on N. lugens performance, transgenic rice plants with overexpression and knockout of OsHDA706 were constructed (Fig. 4a, b). Two independent homozygous lines were selected and subjected to functional analysis, respectively. Bioassay results showed that both knockout lines (koHDA#1 and koHDA#2) were beneficial for N. lugens feeding, as evidenced by increased honeydew excretion (non-significant for koHDA#1; significant for koHDA#2) and longer phloem sap ingestion (significant for both lines) (Fig. 4c, d). For OsHDA706-overexpressing transgenic rice plants (oxHDA#1 and oxHDA#2), a trend of reduced honeydew excretion (non-significant for oxHDA#1; significant for oxHDA#2) and phloem sap ingestion (non-significant for both lines) by N. lugens was observed compared to controls (Fig. 4c, d). A previous study indicates that OsHDA706 primarily functions in the cytoplasm, where it deacetylates OsLOX14, thereby enhancing its stability20. The involvement of OsLOX14 in rice resistance against N. lugens was firstly investigated. Compared with insects feeding on the EV control, those feeding on LOX14-knockout plants (koLOX#1) excreted a slightly greater (but not significantly) amount of honeydew, while those feeding on OsLOX14-overexpressing plants (oxLOX#1 and oxLOX#2) excreted significantly less honeydew (Fig. 4e). Feeding preference experiments yielded similar results: insects preferred to feed on koLOX plants but avoided oxLOX plants (Fig. 4f–i). These results suggest that OsLOX14 confers plant resistance against N. lugens. We next examined whether NlAMSP influenced OsLOX14 acetylation. OsLOX14-flag and OsHDA706-flag were co-expressed with either NlAMSP-myc or a GUS-myc control, followed by immunoprecipitation with anti-flag beads and detection of acetylation levels. Notably, co-expression with NlAMSP-myc significantly increased OsLOX14 acetylation compared to the GUS-Myc control (Fig. S10). Moreover, the overall accumulation of OsLOX14 was markedly reduced in the presence of NlAMSP (Fig. S10), indicating that NlAMSP increased OsLOX14 acetylation, leading to its decreased accumulation.

The interaction between OsHDA706 and OsLOX14 was primarily localized to the cytoplasm, as indicated by a strong fluorescence signal in the cytoplasm when nYFP-OsHDA706 and cYFP-OsLOX14 were co-expressed in N. benthamiana or rice protoplast (Figs. 4j; S11). When NlAMSP-mCherry was co-expressed with this pair, the cytoplasmic fluorescence signal was markedly attenuated (Figs. 4j; S11). This observation was corroborated by Western-blotting analyses of cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions, which showed a reduced accumulation of both OsHDA706 and OsLOX14 in the cytoplasm in the presence of NlAMSP (Fig. 4k). To assess whether NlAMSP influences the interaction between HDA706 and OsLOX14, we performed Co-IP assays in the presence of the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132, which was added to prevent potential degradation of OsLOX1420. The results showed that HDA706-GFP was successfully immunoprecipitated by OsLOX14-flag in the absence or at the low concentration of NlAMSP-myc (Fig. 4l). However, when the NlAMSP-myc concentration increased, HDA706-GFP failed to interact with OsLOX14-flag (Fig. 4l). Collectively, these results indicate that NlAMSP disrupts the OsHDA706–OsLOX14 interaction and diminishes the cytoplasmic levels of these proteins.

Previous work has demonstrated that overexpression of OsHDA706 or OsLOX14 increases JA levels, whereas suppression of these genes leads to reduced JA accumulation20. Given that NlAMSP reduced OsLOX14 abundance, we next examined JA levels in transgenic plants overexpressing NlAMSP. The results showed that endogenous JA levels in both oxSP plants were significantly lower than those in EV plants (Fig. S12a). Consistently, the expression of several JA-associated genes, including OsLOX1, OsMyc2, OsPR1b, and OsPR10, was significantly lower in oxSP plants than in EV plants (Fig. S12b). Collectively, these results indicate that NlAMSP attenuates JA accumulation and JA-associated transcriptional responses in rice.

Furthermore, transgenic rice plants overexpressing OsHDA706-NLS were generated (Fig. S16a). From bioassays, two independent homozygous lines (oxHDA-NLS#1 and oxHDA-NLS#2) were more attractive to N. lugens, and the tested insects excreted more honeydew on oxHDA-NLS plants (Figs. 6b, c; S16b). Collectively, these findings indicate that the nucleus-localized OsHDA706 benefits N. lugens, consistent with the results observed from the whitefly-tobacco system (Fig. S15). Additionally, both oxHDA-NLS lines exhibited reduced levels of histone acetylation at H4K5ac and H4K8ac (Fig. 6d), similar to those observed in the oxSP plants, suggesting that OsHDA706-NLS effectively mimics the function of NlAMSP in the nucleus.

To investigate the underlying mechanism by which NlAMSP and OsHDA706-NLS enhanced insect performance, transcriptomic analysis on EV, oxSP#2, and oxHDA-NLS#2 plants was performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first principal component (PC1), accounting for 59% of the total variance, primarily reflected overall transcriptomic variation that was not associated with the transgenic treatments (Fig. 6e). In contrast, the second principal component (PC2), which explained 27% of the variance, clearly separated EV plants from both oxSP and oxHDA-NLS lines (Fig. 6e). Upon differential expression analysis, 2,133 and 1,198 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in oxSP vs. EV and oxHDA-NLS vs. EV comparisons, respectively (Table S4; Fig. 6f). Given that oxSP and oxHDA-NLS similarly affected plant histone acetylation, we focused on DEGs that displayed consistent expression trends between the two transgenic lines.

Totally 295 co-upregulated and 228 co-downregulated genes were identified (Fig. 6f). Notably, 228 out of the 309 (73.7%) downregulated DEGs in the oxHDA-NLS vs. EV comparison were also downregulated in the oxSP vs. EV comparison (Fig. 6f), suggesting that OsHDA706-NLS may converge on a similar host gene suppression pattern to NlAMSP. Among the top 10 co-downregulated genes, seven were annotated to be associated with disease resistance, including four NBS-LRR-type disease resistance proteins (NLRs), two receptor-like protein kinases (RLKs), and one disease resistance−responsive dirigent−like protein (Fig. S17). Based on enrichment analysis, the co-downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to MAPK signaling, plant hormone signal transduction, and transcription factors (Fig. 6g). In contrast, most co-upregulated genes were associated with metabolic processes, with significant enrichment being observed in pathways involving glycosyltransferases, nitrogen metabolism, and amino sugar/nucleotide sugar metabolism (Fig. 6h). There were 14 and 8 co-regulated genes annotated as NLRs and RLKs (Fig. 6i, j). Interestingly, the majority of these genes were downregulated in oxHDA-NLS and oxSP plants. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation confirmed the expression patterns of these DEGs, which were consistent with the transcriptomic data (Fig. S18), supporting the reliability of the transcriptomic analysis. Collectively, these findings suggest that both NlAMSP and OsHDA706-NLS may suppress host defense gene expression within the nucleus.

To test the roles of DEGs in rice resistance against N. lugens, we selected five genes that showed significant downregulation in oxSP and oxHDA-NLS plants. These included three NLRs (Os11g11770, Os09g10054, and Os11g11810), one RLK (Os07g35310), and one WRKY transcription factor (Os08g38990). The knockout mutants of these DEGs were generated and validated, respectively (Fig. S19). Based on host choice experiments, knockout mutants of koOs11g11770, koOs09g10054, koOs08g38990, and koOs11g11810 were attractive to N. lugens, with more insects settling on these transgenic plants than on the wild-type plants. In contrast, no significant attraction was observed in comparison of koOs07g35310 transgenic plants with those of the controls (Figs. 6k–m; S20). Honeydew excretion analysis showed that N. lugens feeding on koOs11g11770 and koOs08g38990 plants excreted significantly more honeydew than on wild-type controls. In contrast, although insects feeding on koOs09g10054 and koOs07g35310 plants exhibited a slight increase in honeydew excretion, this difference was not statistically significant. These results suggest that the downregulation of Os11g11770 and Os08g38990 partially explains the improved performance of N. lugens on the oxSP and oxHDA-NLS plants.

The SP, NLS, and SUMOylation sites are critical for the subcellular location of NlAMSP

Another interest of this study is the nuclear localization of NlAMSP in rice plants. The presence of NlAMSP in the plant nucleus but not in the insect nucleus prompts us to investigate the underlying mechanism (Fig. 1). Given that the SP directs proteins into the secretory pathway and is typically cleaved off during translocation across the membrane41, NlAMSP protein may be different between insect cells and plant cells. At first, we expressed the mCherry protein fused with the complete open reading frame of NlAMSP (NlAMSP-mCherry) or NlAMSP without SP (NlAMSP-sp-mCherry) in 293 T cells, respectively. The results showed that the NlAMSP-mCherry signal was detected in the cell cytoplasm but not in the nucleus (Fig. 7a, upper). In contrast, the NlAMSP-sp-mCherry was exclusively expressed in the cell nucleus, with the densest signal being detected in the nucleolus (Fig. 7a, lower). Then, we expressed NlAMSP-GFP and NlAMSP-sp-GFP in N. benthamiana or rice protoplasts. The results revealed that NlAMSP-GFP was mainly detected in the cell cytoplasm but not in the nucleus (Figs. 7b; S21). Plasmolysis analysis confirmed that NlAMSP-GFP could be secreted into the apoplast (Fig. 7b). For NlAMSP-sp-GFP, the fluorescence signal was mainly detected in the nucleus (Figs. 7b; S21). Western-blotting analysis further revealed that NlAMSP-GFP produced two bands in plant tissues, with the smaller band matching the size of NlAMSP-sp-GFP (Fig. S22), consistent with cleavage of the SP from a precursor form. The importance of NLS in NlAMSP subcellular location was also investigated. To this end, truncated NlAMSP without NLS and SP (NlAMSP-sp/-nls) were constructed and fused to the C-terminal GFP. Compared with NlAMSP-sp-GFP, NlAMSP-sp/-nls-GFP signal can be detected in the cytoplasm of N. benthamiana or rice protoplast (Figs. 7c; S21). Proteins containing NLSs are usually imported into the nucleus by the importin-α/β heterodimer42. A few pathogens take advantage of this system to enter the host nucleus43. The interaction between NlAMSP-sp and importins was tested. Point-to-point Y2H assays demonstrated that NlAMSP-sp not only interacted with Oryza sativa importins but also with those of N. lugens (Fig. 7d, e). In contrast, the empty vector control failed to interact with importins or NlAMSP-sp. The interaction between NlAMSP-sp and importins was further confirmed by Co-IP assays, with Osimportin-α1, Osimportin-α1a, Osimportin-α1b, Nlimportin-α1, Nlimportin-α2, and Nlimportin-α3 being successfully immunoprecipitated by NlAMSP-sp (Fig. 7f). These results suggest that NLS-containing NlAMSP-sp may migrate into the rice nucleus through the importin α/β nuclear transport pathway.

In Y2H screen assays, OsSIZ1 was identified as a potential interactor (Table S3). OsSIZ1 is a SUMO E3 ligase gene that conjugates SUMO proteins to lysine of target proteins, thus dramatically impacting the target, including altering its location, function, and/or activity44. Previous studies have shown that SUMOylation is critical for nuclear translocation of target proteins, and SIZ1s promote the transportation of interactors from the cytoplasm to the nucleus through the SUMOylation activity45,46. We further verified this interaction by BIFC assay, with yellow fluorescence signals being detected in both nucleus and cytoplasm when nYFP-OsSIZ1 and cYFP-NlAMSP-sp were co-expressed (Fig. S23). Additionally, the interaction between NlAMSP-sp and other SUMOylation-associated components was analyzed, which showed that NlAMSP-sp also interacted with OsSUMO1. In contrast, NlAMSP-sp failed to interact with the other components such as OsSIZ2, OsSCE1, OsSAE1, OsSAE2, and OsSUMO2 (Fig. S23). On the basis of these findings, the interaction between NlAMSP-sp and OsSIZ1 potentially results in the attachment of OsSUMO1 proteins to the SUMOylation sites of NlAMSP-sp.

Bioinformatic analysis predicted seven potential SUMOylation sites on NlAMSP, including two upstream of the NLS sequence (K52 and K63), two within the NLS sequence (K107 and K116), and three downstream of the NLS sequence (K123, K228 and K237) (Fig. S24; Table S5). To investigate the potential influence of SUMOylation on NlAMSP, we employed ML792, a selective inhibitor of the SUMO-activating enzyme (SAE) that effectively blocks SAE activity and global SUMOylation47. The NlAMSP-sp-GFP protein was transiently expressed in N. benthamiana and subsequently treated with either DMSO or ML792. Compared with the DMSO-treated control, ML792 treatment resulted in a markedly altered subcellular localization of NlAMSP-sp-GFP, with fluorescence predominantly observed in the cytoplasm (Fig. S25a). Notably, the overall fluorescence intensity of NlAMSP-sp-GFP was remarkably reduced after ML792 treatment (Fig. S25a). This reduction in protein level was further confirmed by Western-blotting analysis, and NlAMSP-sp-GFP bands significantly decreased upon ML792 treatment (Fig. S25b). These results suggest that SUMOylation affects both the accumulation and subcellular location of NlAMSP.

Site-directed mutation was performed to substitute lysine (K) with alanine (A) at the SUMOylation sites, resulting in the generation of five GFP-tagged NlAMSP mutants (Fig. S24). In the M2 (NlAMSPK107A/K116A/-sp) and M5 (NlAMSPK52A/K63A/K107A/K116A/K123A/K228A/K237A-sp), the GFP signal was almost exclusively localized in the cytoplasm, and fluorescence intensity was substantially decreased (Fig. S26a). By contrast, the M1 (NlAMSPK52A/K63A/-sp), M3 (NlAMSPK123A/K228A/K237A/-sp), and M4 (NlAMSPK52A/K63A/K123A/K228A/K237A-sp) mutants exhibited no differences in protein accumulation or subcellular localization compared with the wild-type control (Fig. S26a). The reduced protein levels in the M2 and M5 were further validated by Western-blotting (Fig. S26b), suggesting that SUMOylation within the NLS region was critical for NlAMSP accumulation and nuclear localization. To investigate the mechanism underlying the reduced accumulation of the M2, the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132, and the autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BAF) were used. Although M2-GFP was still localized in the cytoplasm, its fluorescence intensity was significantly increased following BAF treatment (Fig. S27a). On the contrary, MG132 did not affect the fluorescence signal. Western-blotting analysis further validated the increased accumulation of the M2-GFP protein in the presence of BAF (Fig. S27b). These results suggest that SUMOylation at sites within the NLS region potentially facilitates the nuclear translocation of NlAMSP and protects it from the autophagy-mediated degradation in the cytoplasm.

Discussion

Herbivorous insects deliver effectors with diverse subcellular locations depending on their target destination, and an advanced understanding of their underlying mechanisms will provide fundamental knowledge for developing efficient pest management strategies. In the present study, we reported that N. lugens employed NLS sequence motifs for specifically targeting the plant nucleus and remodeling the host histone/non-histone acetylation network to facilitate feeding. N. lugens infestation triggered transcriptional reprogramming of host plants, including the upregulation of JA and other defense pathways. Meanwhile, N. lugens delivered a salivary effector NlAMSP during feeding to redirect the rice histone deacetylase OsHDA706 from cytoplasm into the nucleus, which, on one hand, reduced the stability of OsLOX14 by disrupting the association of OsHDA706 with OsLOX14 in cytoplasm; on the other hand, the OsHDA706 in the nucleus prevented defense gene expression by increasing the H4K5ac/H4K8ac deacetylation. Our results demonstrate that insects independently evolve salivary effectors to master plant histone and non-histone acetylation.

Numerous effectors containing NLS sequences have been identified in bacterial and fungal pathogens. These NLS-containing proteins are imported into the nucleus through interactions with karyopherin transporters23. For example, the NLS in the Magnaporthe oryzae effector MoHTR1 mediates nuclear translocation in a host importin α-dependent manner, and empowers cytoplasmic effectors to relocate into rice nuclei46. Similarly, the NLS in a ribonuclease effector from Verticillium dahliae is vital for its nuclear localization and cell-death-inducing activity48. Our study shows the first evidence of an insect salivary effector employing a nuclear translocation strategy similar to that of microbial pathogens, suggesting the convergent evolution in nuclear transport mechanisms across different kingdoms. Although NlAMSP interacts with both insect and plant importin α, nucleus-localization is only observed after its secretion into rice plants, not within the salivary glands (Fig. 7). In eukaryotes, conventional protein secretion follows the endoplasmic reticulum-to-Golgi pathway, with subsequent vesicle-mediated transport to the plasma membrane49. The presence of an N-terminal signal peptide in NlAMSP likely prevents its physical interaction with importin α in insects, rendering the non-function of NLS. Therefore, the NLS in NlAMSP appears to have evolved specifically for plant nuclear targeting, a potential adaptation strategy shaped by the insect–plant interactions.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of effector proteins in planta serve as the molecular pivots in plant-pathogen conflicts50. Key modifications such as SUMOylation, myristoylation, phosphorylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination have been illustrated to regulate effector activity within the host50–53. For example, in Pseudomonas syringae, at least 16 effectors undergo SUMOylation mediated by the host’s enzymatic cascade, while in Xanthomonas campestris, the effector AvrXv4 becomes virulent only after SUMOylation post-delivery54. SUMOylation also plays a critical role in nuclear translocation and protein stability, as evidenced by the impaired nuclear localization and reduced stability of MoHTR1 upon mutation of its SUMOylation site46. Although numerous salivary effectors from herbivorous insects have been identified, no PTMs have been reported in these secreted proteins. Here, we demonstrate that SUMOylation of NlAMSP facilitates its nuclear translocation and protects it from autophagy-mediated degradation in the cytoplasm (Figs. S23–S27). Our findings reveal that, beyond NLS, PTMs make a vital impact on the nucleus-targeting of insect salivary effectors.

Hormone signaling and transcriptional reprogramming are key strategies for plant defense against herbivorous insects. Unlike most studies focusing on single effector-pathway interactions, this study reveals that NlAMSP mediates dual immunosuppressive mechanisms: (1) Non-histone acetylation: By sequestering OsHDA706 in the cytoplasm, NlAMSP destabilizes OsLOX14, thereby suppressing JA biosynthesis—a well-established pathway in rice biotic stress resistance (Fig. 4); and (2) Histone acetylation: Nuclear-localized OsHDA706 deacetylates H4K5ac/H4K8ac, leading to the downregulation of NLRs and RLKs essential for ETI/PTI responses (Figs. 5; 6). This dual targeting strategy represents a sophisticated evolutionary adaptation in herbivorous insects to simultaneously disrupt multiple layers of plant immunity. The suppression of the JA pathway mirrors the function of salivary protein HARP1 that stabilizes JAZ repressors28,29, while the observed histone modification changes mirror a few pathogen effectors regulating gene expression regulated by specific histone acetylation patterns11,16,17. Based on our findings, insect effectors may have evolved to exploit the pleiotropic functions of plant HDACs, which serve as the hubs connecting hormone signaling and chromatin remodeling. This dual targeting strategy likely provides N. lugens with a more robust suppression of plant defenses compared with effectors acting on single pathways, potentially explaining its status as a major rice pest.

In line with our findings, a model for the NlAMSP-mediated plant defense inhibition is proposed (Fig. 8). Under the non-infestation conditions, OsHDA706 interacts with and deacetylates the JA biosynthetic enzyme OsLOX14, preventing its ubiquitin-dependent degradation. Additionally, the absence of OsHDA706 in the plant nucleus maintains histone acetylation, sustaining the expression of defense-related genes, including a few NLRs, RLKs, and WRKY transcription factors. Upon N. lugens infestation, OsHDA706 expression is significantly upregulated. To counteract plant defenses, N. lugens employs a Delphacidae-specific effector protein NlAMSP. Synthesized in the insect salivary glands, NlAMSP contains a signal peptide that facilitates its secretion into the host while preventing its migration into the insect nucleus. After secretion, NlAMSP undergoes SUMOylation, which protects it from autophagy-mediated degradation in the cytoplasm. The SUMOylated NlAMSP then binds to OsHDA706 and abolishes the OsHDA706-OsLOX14 interaction, triggering OsLOX14 degradation and suppressing the JA-mediated defense responses. Moreover, NlAMSP possesses a nuclear localization sequence, enabling its translocation into the plant nucleus along with the OsHDA706. Methods

Insects and plants

The N. lugens strain was initially obtained from a rice field at Huajiachi Campus, Zhejiang University (Hangzhou, China). Both the insects and rice hosts were reared in a controlled chamber at 25 ± 1 °C, 70–80% relative humidity, under a 16/8 h light–dark cycle. N. benthamiana plants were grown separately at 23 ± 1 °C with the same photoperiod

Bioinformatic analysis of NlAMSP

To identify potential homologs of NlAMSP, its sequence was used as a query for BLAST searches against the gene sets of 30 insect species, applying an E-value cutoff of 10−5. NLSs were predicted using NovoPro (https://www.novopro.cn/tools/nls-signal-prediction.html, posterior threshold>0.95). Signal peptides and cleavage sites were identified with SignalP 6.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/SignalP/, Probability>0.5), and transmembrane domains were analyzed using TMHMM v2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/). Conserved motifs were annotated with InterProScan (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/), while SUMOylation sites and SUMO-interacting motifs were predicted using GPS-SUMO (https://sumo.biocuckoo.cn/).

qRT-PCR analysis

Fifth-instar N. lugens nymphs were dissected in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.68 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.47 mM KH₂PO₄, pH 7.4) using fine forceps (Ideal-Tek, Switzerland) to obtain carcasses (10), fat bodies (20), guts (30), and salivary glands (40). Testes (20) and ovaries (10) were collected from adult males and females, respectively. Samples from different developmental stages were also prepared, including eggs (100), nymphs (10), and adults (10 males and 10 females). The number of individuals used per sample is shown in parentheses. For rice samples, treated sheaths were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Total RNA Isolation Kit (#9109, Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (#R212-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler® 480 system with the SYBR Green Supermix Kit (#11202ES08, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Primers were designed with Primer Premier v6.0 (Table S6). N. lugens actin or O. sativa UBQ5 were used as reference genes. Relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method55. The control groups or the groups with the lowest transcript level were set at “1”. Three independent biological replicates were performed, each with two technical repeats.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Salivary glands of N. lugens were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (#E672002, Sango Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. For plant tissues, rice sheaths (~3 cm) from N. lugens-infested plants were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and vacuum-infiltrated at 4 °C. Samples were embedded in Jung Tissue Freezing Medium (#020108926, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at −40 °C, sectioned into 20-µm cross-sections using a Cryostar NX50 cryostat (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for an additional 30 min. Anti-NlAMSP serum (HuaAn Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), generated by immunizing rabbits with purified NlAMSP-His proteins, was conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ 488 NHS Ester (#A20000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were incubated with the fluorophore-conjugated antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; #ab104139, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal laser-scanning microscope.

Immunoelectron microscopy observations

Rice sheaths infested by N. lugens were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 3% paraformaldehyde and 0.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). Samples were dehydrated through a graded methanol series (30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%, v/v) and embedded in LR Gold Resin (#14371, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) containing 0.1% benzoin methyl ether (BENZIL, #11290, Electron Microscopy Sciences). Polymerization was performed under UV light at −20 °C for 72–96 h. Ultra-thin sections (~90 nm) were cut and mounted on nickel grids. Sections were blocked for 30 min, incubated with anti-NlAMSP serum (1:200 in blocking buffer) for 90 min, washed, and re-blocked. Grids were then incubated with a gold-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100; #ab27236, Abcam) for 60 min, washed, and air-dried. Finally, sections were counterstained with 3% uranyl acetate (w/v in distilled water) and examined using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Expression of recombinant proteins in 293 T cells

The coding sequences of NlAMSP and NlAMSP-sp were cloned into the mCherry-PCDNA vector (Sigma-Aldrich). HEK-293T cells (ATCC® CRL-3216™) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; #11995065, Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #10099141, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (#15140122, Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Transient transfection was carried out with jetPRIME reagent (#101000046, Polyplus, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence signals were visualized 36 h post-transfection using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Agrobacterium-mediated plant transformation in N. benthamiana

Target sequences were cloned into the indicated vectors and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 by heat-shock transformation. Transformed bacteria were grown on LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin and 10 μg/mL rifampicin at 28 °C for ~60 h. Colonies carrying the target vectors were further cultured in LB medium and collected by centrifugation at 2400 × g for 2 min. Bacterial pellets were resuspended in induction buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6, 200 μM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 1.0 and incubated for 2 h before infiltration into ~6-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. For plasmolysis experiments, infiltrated leaves were treated with 85 mM NaCl for 10 min. For inhibitor assays, leaves were infiltrated one-day post-Agrobacterium infiltration with 50 μM MG132, 10 nM Bafilomycin A1 (BAF), or 10 μM ML792. All inhibitors were initially dissolved in DMSO and then diluted with water; corresponding DMSO concentrations were used as controls. After an additional 24 h incubation, protein levels were analyzed by Western-blotting.

Rice protoplast preparation and transformation

The stems of 2-week-old rice seedlings were cut into 1–2 mm sections using a surgical blade. Subsequently, rice tissue segments were incubated in enzyme solution containing 4% cellulase RS (Yakult Pharmaceutical Industry, Tokyo, Japan), 2% macerozyme R-10 (Yakult Pharmaceutical Industry), 0.6 M mannitol, 20 mM MES (pH 5.7), 20 mM KCl, and 10 mM CaCl₂, with gentle agitation on a rotary shaker for 4 h. The digested mixture was filtered through a 40 μm nylon mesh to remove undigested tissue clumps. Protoplasts were washed twice with 10 mL W5 buffer (0.125 M CaCl₂, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MES, pH 5.7) by centrifugation at 100 × g for 2 min. The protoplasts were resuspended in 10 mL W5 buffer and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, then collected by centrifugation at 100 × g for 2 min. The pellet was resuspended in MMg solution (0.4 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl₂, 4 mM MES, pH 5.7) to a final density of 4 × 10⁵ cells/mL.

Protoplast transformation was performed in 1.5 mL tube based on a previously described protocol56. Briefly, 1.6 × 10⁴ protoplasts in 40 μL MMg buffer were transformed with expression vectors dissolved in 10 μL distilled water. Then, 60 μL PEG solution (40% w/v PEG 4000 [Sigma-Fluka, MO], 0.2 M mannitol, 0.1 M CaCl₂) was added to each tube, and the tube was vortexed for 15 seconds using a digital vortex mixer (GENIE2, Scientific Industries). Transformed protoplasts were washed with 500 μL W5 buffer per tube. The tube was centrifuged at 100 × g for 1 min at room temperature, and 200 μL of the supernatant was discarded; this washing step was repeated four times. The transformed protoplasts were resuspended in 100 μL W5 buffer. The tube was briefly vortexed for 5 seconds and incubated in darkness at 28 °C overnight. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) assays

For Y2H screening, NlAMSP-sp was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, while the O. sativa cDNA library was in pGADT7 (Clontech). Recombinant vectors were co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae Y2H Gold, and positive clones were selected on quadruple dropout (QDO; SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp) (#630428, Takara) solid medium for ~3 days at 30 °C. Colonies from QDO liquid medium were harvested, and plasmids were extracted using the TIANprep Yeast Plasmid DNA Kit (#DP112-02, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Plasmids were then introduced into E. coli DH5α (#TSC-C14, Tsingke), and potential interacting genes were identified by Sanger sequencing (YouKang Biotech, Hangzhou, China).

For Y2H point-to-point verification, target sequences were cloned into pGBKT7 or pGADT7 vectors. Primers used are listed in Table S6. Recombinant and corresponding empty vectors were co-transformed into Y2H Gold, plated on double dropout (DDO; SD/−Leu/−Trp) (#630417, Takara) medium at 30 °C for 3 days, and monoclonal colonies were then spotted onto QDO medium. Growth was recorded after 3 days at 30 °C.

In Co-IP assays, target sequences were cloned into LIC-myc or LIC-flag vectors to generate C-terminally tagged proteins. Five-week-old N. benthamiana leaves were infiltrated with A. tumefaciens carrying these constructs. Two days post-infiltration, leaves were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total proteins were extracted using IP lysis buffer (#87788, Thermo Scientific) with 1 g of tissue in 1 mL of buffer, supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (#56079200, Roche, Switzerland). After incubation at 4 °C for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was incubated with 20 μL of anti-Flag beads (#L00432-1, GenScript, Nanjing, China) for 4 h at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitants were washed four times with 1× PBS, resuspended in 80 μL of 2× SDS-PAGE sample buffer (500 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8; 50% glycerol; 10% SDS; 1% bromophenol blue; 2% β-mercaptoethanol), boiled at 95 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by Western-blotting.

For BiFC assays, target sequences were cloned into pCV-cYFP or pCV-nYFP vectors. Recombinant vectors were introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101 as described above. Co-infiltrations of recombinant and empty vectors were performed in N. benthamiana leaves or rice protoplasts. Plants were maintained in a climate chamber for 36–48 h, and YFP fluorescence was captured using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

RNA interference

The NlAMSP DNA sequence was amplified using primers listed in Table S6 and cloned into the pClone007 Vector (#TSV-007, Tsingke). PCR-generated DNA templates containing the T7 promoter sequence were used to synthesize double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) with the T7 High Yield RNA Transcription Kit (#TR101-01, Vazyme). RNA interference experiments were conducted as previously described57. Briefly, insects were anesthetized with carbon dioxide for 5–10 s, and dsRNA was injected into the mesothorax using a FemtoJet microinjector (Eppendorf-Netheler-Hinz, Hamburg, Germany). After injection, insects were maintained on 4–5-leaf stage rice seedlings for 24 h, and living individuals were selected for further analysis. Silencing efficiency was assessed on the fourth day post-injection using the qRT-PCR method described above.

Insect bioassays

For survivorship analysis, groups of 30–40 third-instar N. lugens nymphs were treated with dsRNA and maintained on 4–5-leaf stage rice seedlings in a climate chamber. Mortality rates were recorded daily for ten consecutive days. Three independent replicates were performed for each treatment. For honeydew analysis, parafilm sachets (Bemis NA, Neenah, WI, USA) were attached to the host plant stems, and fifth-instar nymphs were confined within the sachets. After 24 h of feeding, the accumulation of honeydew was measured by weighing the parafilm sachets before and after feeding using an electronic balance (accuracy 0.001 g; Sartorius, Beijing, China). At least ten independent replicates were performed for each treatment. For fecundity analysis, newly emerged adults were treated with dsRNA. One day later, insects were paired and allowed to oviposit for 10 days. Subsequently, the number of hatched offspring was counted. At least ten independent replicates were conducted for each treatment.

For host choice experiments in the rice–planthopper system, indicated 4–5-leaf stage rice seedlings were placed in a plastic cup (diameter 6 cm; height 10 cm) containing a release chamber. Groups of 40 N. lugens nymphs were released into the chamber, and the number of insects settling on each plant was recorded at 1, 3, 12, and 24 h. Similarly, in the tobacco–whitefly system, N. tabacum leaves at 48 h post-infiltration were placed in a Petri dish. Subsequently, 60 newly emerged female whiteflies were released at the center of the dish, and the number of insects settling on each leaf was recorded. At least three independent replicates were conducted for each experiment.

EPG recording analysis

EPG recordings were performed using the GiGA-8d amplifier (Wageningen Agricultural University, Wageningen, The Netherlands) with a 10 TΩ input resistance and an input bias current of less than 1 pA. Briefly, dsRNA-treated N. lugens nymphs were reared on filter paper with only water for 12 h. After anesthetization with CO₂ for 10 s, a gold wire (diameter 20 μm; length 5 cm; Wageningen Agricultural University) was attached to the insect abdomen and connected to the EPG amplifier using a water-soluble silver conductive glue (Wageningen Agricultural University). The plant electrode was prepared by inserting a copper wire (diameter 2 mm; length 10 cm) into soil containing a single rice plant. EPG recordings were conducted for 8 h inside a Faraday cage (120 × 75 × 67 cm; Dianjiang, Shanghai, China). The amplifier gain was set to 50×, and the output voltage was adjusted between −5 V and +5 V.

Recorded data were analyzed using PROBE 3.4 software (Wageningen Agricultural University), and feeding behaviors were classified as non-penetration (np), pathway phase (N1 + N2 + N3), phloem sap ingestion (N4), and xylem sap ingestion (N5)34. At least ten independent replicates were performed for each treatment.

Separation of the cell nucleus and cytoplasm

The nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separated according to the method previously described15. Briefly, the leaf sample was harvested, ground in liquid nitrogen, and extracted with lysis buffer (0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl₂, 0.1 mM PMSF, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The homogenate was filtered through double-layered Miracloth and centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet (nuclear fraction) was washed with buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM PMSF, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, protease inhibitors). The supernatant was centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min to remove debris and collected as the cytoplasmic fraction.

Generation of transgenic plants

The koLOX and oxLOX plants were kindly provided by Zong-Tao Sun as previously described20, while the koOs11g11770, koOs09g10054, koOs08g38990, koOs11g11810, and koOs07g35310 plants were obtained from the company Biogle Genetech, Hangzhou, China. The koHDA plants were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system via the custom service of BioRun Biosciences, Wuhan, China. All mutations were determined by PCR with genomic DNA as the template using sequence-specific primers (Table S6), followed by Sanger sequencing (YouKang Biotech). Two independent T2 homozygous knockout lines were used for subsequent experiments.

For generating the oxHDA and oxSP plants, the complete coding sequence of OsHDA706 and NlAMSP-sp was cloned into LIC-flag and LIC-myc vectors, respectively. For generating the oxHDA-NLS plants, the commonly used NLS tag (PKKKRKV) from Simian vacuolating virus 40 was fused to the N-terminal end of recombinant proteins. The recombinant vector was introduced into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 by the heat transfer method. Transgenic rice plants were generated through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The transgenic plants were grown in the greenhouse, and were confirmed by RT-PCR or Western-blotting assays. Two independent T2 homozygous lines were used.

JA quantification

Rice tissues were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. 0.5 g sample was accurately weighed and added into a tube, followed by the addition of 10 volumes of chromatographic‑grade acetonitrile and an appropriate volume of internal standard solution. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction at 4 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 13,523 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The remaining pellet was extracted again with 5 volumes of pre‑chilled chromatographic‑grade acetonitrile using the same procedure as described above. The supernatants from the two extractions were combined, followed by the addition of appropriate amounts of C18 and GCB. After vortex mixing for 30 s, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,523 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen gas, and the residue was reconstituted with methanol and ultrapure water. The reconstituted solution was centrifuged at 13,523 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.11 μm organic‑phase filter. The filtrate was stored at −20 °C and sent to WebiolotechTesting Technology Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China) for LC‑MS/MS analyses.

Histone extraction

Histones were extracted from two-week-old rice seedlings using the EpiQuik™ Total Histone Extraction Kit (#OP-0006-100, Epigentek, New York, USA), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The extracted histone fractions were used for immunoblot analysis with commercial antibodies purchased from PTMBio, Hangzhou, China. The antibodies used are listed below: Anti-H3 (#PTM-1002RM), Anti-H4 (#PTM-1009), Anti-H3K9ac (#PTM-112), Anti-H3K27ac (#PTM-116), Anti-H3K36ac (#PTM-117), Anti-H3K56ac (#PTM-162), Anti-H4K5ac (#PTM-119), Anti-H4K8ac (#PTM-120), Anti-H4K12ac (#PTM-121), and Anti-H4K16ac (#PTM-122).

Western-blotting assay

Western-blotting was performed as previously described34. Briefly, protein samples were separated on 10%, 12.5%, or 15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with the indicated primary serum diluted 1:5,000, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:10,000; #31460, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein signals were detected using an AI 680 image analyzer (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Buckinghamshire, UK). The band intensities in immunoblot analyses were quantified using ImageJ software v1.53e (https://imagej.nih.gov/). To ensure equal protein loading, membranes were further stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) or Ponceau S.

Transcriptomic analysis

To assess the effects of NlAMSP-sp and OsHDA706-NLS overexpression on rice, sheaths from EV, oxSP, and oxHDA-NLS plants were collected and homogenized using the TRIzol Total RNA Isolation Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted and sent to Novogene Institute (Novogene, Beijing, China) for transcriptome sequencing. All generated sequencing data have been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center under accession number PRJCA042886.

The output raw reads were filtered using the internal software, and the clean reads from each cDNA library were aligned to the reference sequences in the Rice Genome Annotation Project Database using HISAT v2.1.058. The low-quality alignments were filtered by SAMtools v1.759. Transcripts per million (TPM) expression values were calculated using Cufflinks v2.2.160. The DESeq2 v2.2.161 was adopted for analyzing the DEGs, and genes with log2-ratio > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 were identified. To reveal overall differences in gene expression patterns among different transcriptomes, the R function plotPCA (github.com/franco-ye/TestRepository/blob/main/PCA_by_deseq2.R) was used to perform PCA analysis. KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using TBtools software v1.069762. Statistical analysis

The log-rank test (SPSS Statistics 19, Chicago, IL, USA) was applied to determine the statistical significance of survival distributions. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test (comparisons between two groups) or one-way ANOVA test followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (comparisons among three groups) was used to analyze the results of qRT-PCR, EPG, honeydew measurement, and offspring measurement. Chi-square test was used to analyze the results of feeding preference results. The EPG data were first checked for normality and homogeneity of variance, and data not fitting a normal distribution were subjected to log10 transformation.
