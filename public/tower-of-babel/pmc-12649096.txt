Maternal mortality ratios in Southeast Asia (1990–2021): trends, disparities, and projections through 2050 based on the global burden of disease 2021, with reference to SDG 3.1
Authors: Jiale Feng, Jingya Huang, Qingguo Wu, Kai Wei, Qin Bin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Southeast Asia accounts for ~ 20% of global maternal deaths, with substantial disparities between and within countries. This study analyzed trends (1990–2021) and projected (to 2050) MMR in Southeast Asia using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database, addressing gaps in regional granularity.​​Methods​Data GBD 2021, covering MMR and age-standardized MMR (ASMR) for 1990–2021, stratified by country, age group, and cause.​Analysis: Trend assessment was conducted using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), while 30-year projections were generated via an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model.ResultsRegional Trends: The ASMR in Southeast Asia fell by 59%, dropping from 255.88 in 1990 to 104.56 per 100,000 live births by 2021. Introduction

A maternal death is defined as the demise of a woman during pregnancy or within 42 days of pregnancy termination, irrespective of the pregnancy’s duration or location. Such deaths stem from causes linked to or aggravated by pregnancy or its clinical management, excluding accidental or incidental causes [1]. Direct obstetric causes include haemorrhage and infection, while indirect causes encompass conditions like pregnancy-associated heart disease [2]. Global maternal mortality ratios (MMR) vary significantly due to factors such as individual behavioural traits (e.g., maternal age, parity, pre-pregnancy comorbidities), obstetric service accessibility/quality, socioeconomic/cultural contexts, and external environments (e.g., disasters, infectious diseases) [3–20].

Target 3.1 of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3.1) mandates reducing the global MMR to below 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030. However, the 2020 global average MMR was ~ 223 per 100,000 live births, with developing countries far from meeting the target [21]. Southeast Asia—housing nearly a quarter of the global population—had an MMR of 134 per 100,000 live births in 2020 (approaching the 140 upper limit for SDG compliance), but internal disparities remain stark. Countries like Indonesia, Myanmar, and Laos have persistently high regional MMR; Indonesia, for example, recorded an MMR of 249 per 100,000 live births in 2020 [22–24]. In 2023, ~ 260,000 maternal deaths occurred globally, with Southeast Asia accounting for 20% (~ 53,000) [25]. From 2016 to 2020, MMR stagnated or worsened in most regions, except Australia/New Zealand (34.6% reduction) and Central/South Asia (15.7% reduction) [26]. A further 30% MMR cut in Southeast Asia could reduce global maternal deaths by ~ 16,000, making the region pivotal to timely SDG 3.1 achievement [26].

The literature has used established maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) systems to examine the distribution of maternal deaths across subnational regions [23]. Global studies leveraging the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database lack granularity and targeted analysis for Southeast Asia [27–29]. The GBD database, however, has unique strengths: its MMR adheres to SDG 3.1 definitions with a standardized algorithm (eliminating cross-country definition discrepancies) [30], is incorporated into WHO/UNICEF/World Bank reports [31], and provides full-chain evidence (time-space-cause of death-risk factors-economic scenarios) [32]. This study analyzes Southeast Asia’s MMR epidemiological characteristics using the GBD 2021 database. It aims to enrich regional maternal mortality research, provide evidence for local policy-making, and offer references for other global regions.

Methodology

Data provenance

Information on MMR was sourced from the GBD 2021. This database offers a rigorous, empirically grounded analysis of the impact of diseases, injuries, and risk factors across varied age groups and sexes worldwide [33, 34]. It incorporates MMR data covering the period 1990 through 2021, in addition to age-standardized maternal mortality ratios (ASMR). These metrics, along with their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), are categorized by age group and for the 13 Southeast Asian countries/territories included in this study (Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Malaysia, Maldives, Mauritius, Myanmar, Philippines, Seychelles, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Timor-Leste and Vietnam). The authors affirm that all procedures adhere to the ethical standards outlined by the journal. Given that this study draws on publicly accessible data, no ethical approval was considered requisite.

Definition

The MMR is defined as the number of maternal deaths occurring within a specified time period per 100,000 live births in the same period [1]. To ensure comparability across populations with varying sizes and age distributions, age-standardized rates (ASRs) were computed. These rates, expressed per 100,000 population, are derived from the updated population age structure provided by GBD 2021 [35].

Statistical analyses

To evaluate the implications of maternal mortality, we employed ASMR and compiled statistics on maternal mortality rates across distinct age cohorts. Data were sourced from the GBD 2021, with findings presented as estimates incorporating 95% confidence intervals. For a comprehensive trend analysis, we computed the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) spanning 1990 to 2021. EAPC was calculated using the standard GBD 2021 algorithm: a weighted log-linear regression of ln (rateₜ) (natural logarithm of the rate in year t) on calendar year (t), with 95% uncertainty intervals derived from the bootstrap distribution of 1000 draws. The method relies on two core assumptions: (i) a constant linear trend of log-transformed rates [ln (rateₜ)] over the fitted period; and (ii) independence of annual rate estimates from their respective standard errors (SEₜ). Time series analysis was applied to investigate dynamic patterns in maternal mortality, and short-term projections were generated via the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model. Following preprocessing of time series data extracted from the GBD database, the ARIMA model was formulated and its parameters refined. The dataset was split into a training subset (for model development) and a validation subset (for model assessment) at a 7:3 ratio. To assess the model’s predictive performance on the validation subset, we employed root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), with a MAPE < 10% defined as a high-precision prediction. Leveraging the optimized model, we projected indicator values for the subsequent five time points and calculated 95% confidence intervals. Prior to ARIMA model fitting, we assessed the stationarity of each age-specific MMR time series using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. Non-stationary series were differenced as necessary until stationarity was achieved (ADF test p < 0.05). Model parameters were selected based on the minimization of both the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Following model estimation, we conducted residual diagnostics to verify compliance with ARIMA assumptions. The Ljung-Box test was applied to test for the presence of serial correlation in the residuals, with the null hypothesis being no autocorrelation. In all cases, residuals exhibited no significant autocorrelation (Ljung-Box p > 0.05), and visual inspection of the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plots confirmed that residuals approximated white noise. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1) with the Global Burden R package, and two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All visualisations and comparative analyses were generated de novo by the authors using GBD 2021 estimates; no figures or tables were reproduced from published GBD reports.

Epidemiological trends of maternal mortality ratio in Southeast Asia

Between 1990 and 2021 in Southeast Asia, the ASMR was predominantly driven by maternal haemorrhage and maternal hypertensive disorders, with these two conditions accounting for over 60% of all maternal deaths (Fig. 1). In 1990, the primary causes of ASMR, ranked in order, were haemorrhage during pregnancy, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, and pregnancy loss due to abortion or miscarriage. (Fig. 2A). By 2021, maternal haemorrhage remained the top cause, and maternal hypertensive disorders continued to be a significant contributor, while other direct maternal disorders had risen by two positions, meriting heightened attention (Fig. 2B). In Southeast Asia, the ASMR fell from 255.88 per 100,000 live births in 1990 to 104.56 per 100,000 live births by 2021, corresponding to a 59% decline (Table 1; Fig. 3A). The rate of decline slowed during the 1997 Asian financial crisis but accelerated notably in the 21st century (Fig. 3A). The largest contribution to this reduction came from maternal haemorrhage, which dropped from 120.56 to 32.94 per 100,000 live births, followed by hypertensive disorders in pregnancy and pregnancy loss due to abortion or miscarriage. (Table 1; Fig. 3A). Notably, maternal deaths aggravated by HIV/AIDS increased from 0.01 to 0.13 per 100,000 live births, a 13-fold rise (Table 1). Between 1990 and 2021, maternal haemorrhage remained the leading cause of ASMR in most Southeast Asian countries (Fig. 1). However, Malaysia was distinctive, with other direct disorders as the primary cause, while maternal hypertensive disorders were the main driver in Seychelles (Fig. 1, S1F). Sri Lanka, by contrast, demonstrated a notably larger share of deaths attributed to ectopic pregnancy relative to other countries in the region (Fig. 1, S1H). At the time of the 1997 Asian financial crisis, ASMR in Vietnam and the Lao People’s Democratic Republic remained largely unaffected, showing a steady decline from 1990 to 2015, though the downward trend slowed after 2015 (Fig. 3B, C). Thailand was the most severely impacted, with a marked rise in maternal mortality between 1997 and 2002 (Fig. 3D). Over the 1990–2021 period, Mauritius, Seychelles, and Thailand collectively exhibited a slight upward trend with fluctuations (Fig. 3E, F). Despite all three countries having already fallen below the threshold set by the SDG 3.1, attention is warranted on the sustained increases in ASMR attributed to other direct maternal disorders in Mauritius and Thailand (p < 0.001, Table S1). In 1990, obstructed labor and uterine rupture ranked as the leading causes of ASMR in Mauritius, trailed by haemorrhage during pregnancy and hypertensive disorders in pregnancy (Fig. 2A). By 2021, other direct maternal disorders had surged to the top, with mortality rising from 2.57 (2.01–3.19) to 16.37 (13.23–20.00) per 100,000 live births (EAPC = 4.46 [3.19–5.75], p < 0.001, Table S1, Fig. 2B). Indirect maternal deaths ranked second, increasing from 2.25 (1.78–2.78) to 11.07 (8.93–13.69) per 100,000 live births (EAPC = 5.13 [4.44–5.82], p < 0.001, Table S1). In Thailand, haemorrhage during pregnancy ranked as the primary cause of ASMR in 1990, with other direct obstetric disorders following thereafter. (Fig. 2A). By 2021, other direct maternal disorders had climbed to the top, with mortality increasing from 8.93 (4.94–14.45) to 21.18 (12.72–33.85) per 100,000 live births (EAPC = 2.65 [95% CI 1.80–3.49], p < 0.001, Table S1, Fig. 2B). Of particular concern, indirect maternal deaths have emerged as one of the major causes, surging from 0.77 (0.43–1.25) to 9.81 (6.01–15.88) per 100,000 live births (EAPC = 10.32 [95% CI 9.08–11.57], p < 0.001, Table S1).

Between 1990 and 2021, Southeast Asian nations with initially elevated ASMR—specifically Cambodia, Indonesia, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, the Maldives, Myanmar, and Timor-Leste—saw substantial declines. Cambodia’s ASMR dropped from 361.60 (95% UI 252.67–495.77) to 138.51 (85.58–222.19) per 100,000 live births; Indonesia’s from 472.47 (399.38–547.52) to 144.99 (107.82–204.36); Laos’ from 661.49 (447.83–923.13) to 153.26 (76.22–278.56); the Maldives’ from 292.79 (208.98–387.16) to 62.63 (40.58–91.32); Myanmar’s from 204.62 (149.36–278.15) to 150.02 (99.69–210.48); and Timor-Leste’s from 631.39 (401.02–876.71) to 189.88 (117.22–297.15) (Table S1,Figure S1A). Nevertheless, the rate of decline has generally decelerated across these six countries since 2015 (Fig. 3C, S1A–E). ASMR fluctuates in the Philippines, and other direct maternal disorders continue to grow (Figure S1G).

Currently, only the Maldives has fallen below the SDG3.1; the remaining five countries still have significantly higher ASMR, with Timor-Leste being the highest (Table S1). In 2021, indirect maternal deaths and maternal haemorrhage continued to contribute substantially to ASMR in Timor-Leste, collectively accounting for over 60% of cases, making them key priorities for subsequent interventions (Table S1, Figure S1A). The reductions in MMR across these six countries were primarily driven by declines in maternal haemorrhage and maternal abortion & miscarriage (Table S1). Notably, however, ASMR attributed to maternal deaths aggravated by HIV/AIDS and ectopic pregnancy are on the rise, which warrants vigilance (Table S1). Additionally, compared with 1990, in 2021, the impact of other direct maternal disorders, indirect maternal deaths, and late maternal deaths on ASMR —alongside long-recognized causes like haemorrhage in pregnancy, hypertensive disorders during gestation, and pregnancy loss from abortion or miscarriage—can no longer be overlooked. (Table S1).

Projections of maternal mortality ratios in Southeast Asia to 2050

Over the next three decades, Southeast Asia’s overall MMR will show complex age- and etiology-specific trends. Maternal disorders will rise slightly in 45–49 and 50–54 years groups but decline gradually in others, with the steepest drop in the 10–14 age group (Fig. 6).​ Ectopic pregnancy-related MMR will increase substantially in 10–14 years groups, rise modestly in 45–49 years groups, and show a sharp initial decline followed by rapid increase in 50–54 s; minor fluctuations will occur in remaining age groups (Figure S3A). MMR due to indirect maternal deaths and other direct maternal disorders will rise moderately in parallel among women aged 45–49 years and 50–54 years, with minimal changes in other age groups (Figure S3B, J). MMR due to late maternal deaths will decline slowly only in the 10–14 years group, while other age cohorts will see slight fluctuations (Figure S3C). In the 45–49 years group, MMR due to abortion and miscarriage will rise significantly, accompanied by modest increases in both the overall rate and the age-standardized rate (Figure S3D). The MMR will remain stable in the 50–59 years group and decline gradually in all other age groups (Figure S3D). MMR due to deaths aggravated by HIV/AIDS will decrease significantly in the 10–14 years group, fall slightly in the 15–19 years group, and rise initially followed by a decrease in the 20–29 years group—returning essentially to 2021 levels by 2050 (Figure S3E). Sustained increases will occur in the 40–54 years group, with a particularly notable rise in the 45–54 years cohort (Figure S3E). Overall and age-standardized rates will show a slight increase, with little fluctuation in the remaining age groups (Figure S3E). Maternal haemorrhage, the leading cause of MMR in Southeast Asia, will rise sharply in the 50–54 years group, increase slightly in the 45–49 years group, and decline in all other age groups (Figure S3F). MMR due to maternal hypertensive disorders will rise slowly in the 45–49 years and 50–54 years groups, while declining in other age cohorts—with particularly marked decreases in the 10–14 years and 35–39 years groups (Figure S3G). MMR due to obstructed labor and uterine rupture will increase slightly in the 45–49 years group, and show an initial decline followed by a rise in the 50–59 years group, with minor decreases in all other age groups (Figure S3H). MMR due to sepsis and other infections will remain largely stable in the 45–49 years and 50–59 years groups, while declining gradually in other age cohorts—with a substantial decline in the 10–14 years group (Figure S3I).

Discussion

Drawing on the GBD 2021 database, this study offers a systematic analysis of the epidemiological features of MMR across Southeast Asia and its individual countries from 1990 to 2021, along with 30-year projections. The results reveal a substantial reduction in regional MMR over the three decades, yet persistent intra-regional disparities—echoing the global unevenness in maternal health advancements. Notably, the region’s disease spectrum and age-specific mortality patterns have undergone intricate transformations, with multiple challenges projected to persist. These findings not only provide crucial evidence for comprehending Southeast Asia’s maternal health landscape and formulating targeted interventions but also contribute a regional viewpoint to global endeavors aimed at achieving SDG 3.1.

Our analysis confirms a significant decline in MMR across Southeast Asia between 1990 and 2021, though with striking cross-national variations that mirror the global divide between resource-abundant and resource-scarce settings. Historically high-burden countries, such as Cambodia, Indonesia, and the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, have achieved notable reductions but remain far from the SDG 3.1 of fewer than 70 per 100,000 live births, a situation paralleled in low-income countries worldwide. The slowdown in progress since 2015 suggests these nations may have reached a saturation point in utilizing basic healthcare resources, with deep-rooted socioeconomic barriers—including limited female education and inadequate transportation infrastructure—now hindering further gains [36–38]. In contrast, Thailand, Mauritius, and Seychelles have surpassed the SDG 3.1 threshold, joining high-income nations that have met global targets. However, they face emerging challenges, including fluctuating MMR and shifting disease profiles. For example, Thailand has witnessed a rising proportion of deaths attributed to other direct maternal disorders and indirect causes, while Mauritius recorded an EAPC of 5.13 for indirect maternal deaths (p < 0.001). These trends, observed globally in middle- and high-income contexts, are likely linked to demographic aging and the growing prevalence of pregnancies complicated by non-communicable diseases (e.g., cardiovascular conditions, diabetes), highlighting the need for adapted strategies to manage pregnancy comorbidities [39–41]. Maternal haemorrhage and hypertensive disorders remain leading causes of maternal death in Southeast Asia—consistent with their global dominance—though their proportional contributions have decreased, reflecting progress in addressing these traditional killers. This stands in contrast to the growing impact of other direct maternal disorders and indirect deaths, a trend increasingly evident worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-burden settings. This epidemiological shift signifies advancements in basic obstetric care that have reduced mortality from preventable causes on a global scale, while simultaneously amplifying challenges in managing complex cases involving rare obstetric complications and chronic disease comorbidities [42–46].

A key finding of our study is the identification of two high-risk age cohorts in Southeast Asia: 10–14 years and 50–54 years, with mortality risks significantly exceeding those of other groups, whereas the 20–29 years group exhibits the lowest risk. This phenomenon is likely linked to the physiological characteristics and social factors specific to these two high-risk age groups. Females aged 10–14 years, whose bodies are not fully mature, face elevated pregnancy risks and may encounter sociocultural barriers—such as limited access to preconception health knowledge and healthcare services—further exacerbating their vulnerability [47–50]. For women aged 50–54 years, declining physiological function and a higher prevalence of chronic diseases may contribute to increased risks of pregnancy complications [51, 52]. Notable variations exist in the distribution of specific maternal conditions across countries and age groups. Ectopic pregnancy, which is predominantly concentrated in the 40–54 years age group regionally (with Cambodia, Indonesia, and Mauritius exceeding the regional average), contrasts with Myanmar and Sri Lanka, where cases are concentrated in the 10–14 years group. These disparities may be associated with differences in national fertility patterns, accessibility of reproductive health services, and capacity for managing high-risk pregnancies [53–55]. Indirect maternal deaths are more prevalent in Timor-Leste and the Lao People’s Democratic Republic compared to the regional average, with elevated mortality rates observed in the 20–39 years age group. Conversely, Thailand, Mauritius, and Vietnam exhibit distinct trends, which may reflect variations in disease spectra, healthcare resource allocation, and capacity for managing chronic diseases [56–59]. Additionally, maternal deaths due to abortion and miscarriage are generally higher across all age groups in countries with traditionally high MMR compared to the regional average. Notably, Vietnam, despite having a MMR far below the regional level, may attribute this achievement to its investments in reproductive health services and preconception care [56, 58]. Maternal deaths aggravated by HIV/AIDS have a substantial impact in Cambodia and Thailand, primarily affecting women aged 45–54 years. This pattern is likely related to the HIV epidemic dynamics and accessibility of antiretroviral therapy in these two countries [60, 61].

Projections indicate that the overall MMR in Southeast Asia will continue to decline, though certain etiologies and age groups are showing increasing risks. Specifically, risks of ectopic pregnancy, indirect maternal deaths, and maternal hemorrhage are rising in the 45–54 age group, while maternal deaths aggravated by HIV/AIDS persistently increase among individuals aged 40 years and older. These trends are associated with population aging, rising prevalence of chronic diseases, and shifts in the focus of HIV prevention and control efforts. The MMR in the 10–14 age group is projected to experience the most significant reduction, a process that could be accelerated by sustained progress in girls’ education and the elimination of early marriage practices [12, 62]. Countries in the region need to strengthen the management of high-risk pregnancies, elevate both the quality and reach of reproductive health services, enhance prevention and control measures for chronic and infectious diseases, and further optimize the allocation of health resources.

Beyond the traditional obstetric model: three under-reported transitions in maternal mortality. The prevailing view that haemorrhage and hypertensive disorders still dominate maternal deaths is broadly correct, yet a finer-grained reading of cause-specific trajectories exposes three quiet but decisive shifts reshaping the region’s epidemiological landscape. First, the post-haemorrhage bottleneck. In Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, the Maldives, and Timor-Leste, deaths from obstetric haemorrhage have fallen two- to three-fold faster each year than the overall maternal mortality ratio (EAPC − 5.1% to-7.9% versus − 4.2% to-5.2%). This divergence implies that acute intrapartum bleeding is no longer the principal brake on progress; rather, conditions with far slower declines—obstructed labour and uterine rupture in Lao People’s Democratic Republic (EAPC-3.1%) or the nebulous category of “other direct maternal disorders” in Malaysia (EAPC1.2%)-now set the pace. These residual causes are typically rare catastrophes such as amniotic-fluid embolism or acute fatty liver of pregnancy, demanding tertiary-level expertise rather than basic emergency obstetric drills. Second, the late and indirect rebound. Five of the 11 countries studied show rising mortality beyond the traditional 42-day cut-off. Thailand’s increase is the steepest: from 2.24 to 6.58 late maternal deaths per 100 000 live births (EAPC3.4%). Simultaneously, indirect deaths—fuelled mainly by pre-existing cardiac disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease—have more than doubled in Thailand (EAPC10.3%) and risen sharply in Mauritius (EAPC5.1%). Better intrapartum survival has simply unveiled a lingering cardiometabolic burden that now stretches well past the conventional surveillance horizon. If routine maternal death surveillance audits continue to stop at 42 days, they will credit facility-based obstetric care with gains it only partly deserves, while missing the growing need for sustained postpartum follow-up. Third, the age-specific paradox. National averages mask diverging risk curves. Ectopic pregnancy deaths are shifting towards women aged 40 years and older in Cambodia, Indonesia, and Mauritius, whereas HIV-exacerbated maternal deaths cluster in the 45–54-year bracket in Cambodia and Thailand. Forecasts to 2050 suggest that haemorrhage itself will climb again among women aged 45 years and older, propelled by rising multiple gestations and caesarean-scar morbidity. These patterns call for age-stratified clinical pathways that acknowledge the distinct physiology of perimenopausal pregnancy, instead of a monolithic risk-assessment model. Taken together, the data signal a strategic inflection point: the next phase of maternal mortality reduction in southeast Asia must pivot from preventing isolated, acute events to managing complex, multisystem conditions that unfold across an extended perinatal continuum. Recent advances in the Internet of Things (IoT) have introduced transformative potential for maternal health surveillance. Wearable biosensors and wireless monitoring systems now enable real-time, continuous tracking of maternal vital signs and fetal parameters, even in low-resource settings [63–65]. While such technologies are not yet widely integrated into Southeast Asian maternal health programs, their compatibility with community-based care models and postpartum remote monitoring warrants further exploration [66, 67]. Achieving this will require non-obstetric specialties to be woven into maternal health programmes, surveillance to be extended to 365 days postpartum, and age-specific care bundles to be developed for the emerging high-risk cohorts.

Despite utilizing the GBD 2021 database, which encompasses extensive data across a wide range of countries and regions, it should be noted that GBD 2023 estimates have since been released; thus, any temporal changes after 2021 are not captured herein. This study has several additional limitations. First, GBD data rely on national reporting systems, which may suffer from incomplete or inaccurate data collection—particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources. Second, the study did not fully account for non-medical factors influencing maternal mortality, such as socioeconomic conditions and cultural practices, which may vary significantly across countries and regions. Furthermore, projections for the next 30 years are based on existing trends and models and may not fully reflect real-world outcomes, as future socioeconomic environments, advances in medical technology, and policy changes could all impact MMR. Long-horizon projections (≥ 20 years) rely on the assumption that the statistical properties of the past three decades persist. Although out-of-sample tests support 5-year accuracy, distant intervals are inherently speculative and should be iteratively revised with incoming data.

Conclusion

Southeast Asia has achieved notable advancements in lowering MMR, yet internal inequalities and shifts in disease spectra pose new challenges. To achieve the SDG 3.1, targeted interventions, strengthened health systems, and the elimination of social barriers will be essential. Such efforts can also serve as a practical model for fulfilling the global commitment of “leaving no one behind.” Continuous surveillance, cross-country collaboration, and evidence-based policy adjustments will be pivotal to advancing maternal health equity in the region.
