Deciphering the Immune Landscape of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm: A Machine Learning and Cross-Species Validated Multi-Omics Approach
Authors: Minyong Peng, Menglin Nie, Yida Cao, Qiulei Liu, Chenxu Xing, Xiaorui Li, Yongjun Li, Zhiyuan Wu, Wayne W Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is characterized by chronic vascular inflammation and immune dysregulation. We aimed to prioritize tissue-associated diagnostic markers and candidate immune regulators using integrated transcriptomics, machine learning, genetic analyses, single-cell data, and cross-species protein evaluation.Patients and MethodsBulk transcriptomic cohorts, WGCNA, and a multi-algorithm machine-learning workflow were used to derive a tissue-expression signature. Bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR), colocalization, and SMR provided genetic prioritization. Single-cell RNA sequencing and scTenifoldKnk virtual perturbation were used for cell localization and hypothesis generation. Genetic prioritization integrating bidirectional MR, Bayesian colocalization, and SMR-transcriptomic concordance converged on three genes: PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1. Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a chronic, progressive vascular disorder characterized by permanent dilation of the abdominal aorta and a high risk of fatal rupture.1 Although AAA has long been considered a degenerative vascular disorder characterized by connective tissue remodeling in the aortic wall, particularly involving the degradation of elastic fibers and fibrillar collagens that maintain aortic mechanical strength, growing evidence suggests that these structural alterations are largely secondary to sustained immune activation and chronic inflammatory processes within the vessel wall.2,3 Histopathological analyses have demonstrated extensive infiltration of immune cells, including macrophages, T lymphocytes, B cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, accompanied by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and proteolytic enzymes. These immune-mediated processes promote extracellular matrix degradation, vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis, and progressive weakening of the aortic wall, ultimately contributing to aneurysm formation and rupture.4

Growing evidence suggests that dysregulated innate and adaptive immune responses are central to AAA pathogenesis.5 Activated macrophages secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade elastin and collagen fibers, while T helper cell subsets modulate inflammatory cascades through cytokine production.4 Furthermore, immune-related signaling pathways, such as NF-κB, TNF, and IL-17 signaling, have been implicated in amplifying vascular inflammation.6 Despite substantial advances in understanding immune involvement, the specific immune-related molecular signatures and key regulatory genes driving AAA progression remain incompletely defined.7 Identifying reliable immune-related diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets is therefore of critical clinical importance.8

Recent advances in high-throughput transcriptome sequencing, machine learning algorithms, single-cell sequencing, and Mendelian randomization have provided new research paradigms for deciphering complex immune regulatory networks, identifying causal genes, and discovering potential therapeutic targets.9–12 These technologies enable systematic assessment of immune cell composition in tissues, inference of causal relationships between gene expression and disease risk, and elucidation of cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communications at single-cell resolution.

In this study, multiple AAA transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database to systematically identify immune-related diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets. Differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) were performed to identify key gene modules significantly associated with AAA, followed by functional enrichment analysis to characterize immune-related biological processes and signaling pathways. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed, and multiple machine learning algorithms were employed to screen hub genes with high diagnostic value. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was further applied to quantify feature contributions and improve model interpretability and robustness. Immune cell infiltration in AAA tissues was quantified using the CIBERSORT algorithm to identify key immune cell populations strongly correlated with hub genes. Integration of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis with Mendelian randomization (MR) enabled causal inference between candidate genes and AAA at the transcriptomic level. To further determine the cellular origin and intercellular interactions of these biomarkers, single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed to characterize gene expression patterns across distinct cell populations and to construct cell–cell communication networks. Additionally, virtual gene knockout analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of key genes on relevant signaling pathways, thereby providing functional insights into disease progression. Finally, the expression and biological relevance of the identified hub genes were validated in both AAA animal models and human aneurysmal tissues.

Through a multi-layered and multidimensional integrative strategy, this study systematically identified immune-related diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets in AAA, providing novel molecular evidence for early diagnosis and targeted therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Study Design

The study combined bulk transcriptomic screening, causal-inference analyses, single-cell profiling, and experimental validation. GEO expression datasets were first used to identify AAA-associated genes through differential-expression testing and weighted co-expression analysis. Candidate genes were then prioritized with protein-interaction mapping and machine-learning models, and model behavior was interpreted with SHAP. Immune-cell composition was estimated from bulk data, while eQTL-linked MR analyses were used to examine whether selected genes were plausibly related to AAA risk. Single-cell and virtual-knockout analyses were used to localize the relevant signals and explore their functional context, after which key findings were examined in murine AAA models and human tissue specimens. Data Collection and Analysis

Six GEO datasets were included in the bulk-expression workflow: GSE57691, GSE232911, GSE7084, GSE183464, GSE269845, and GSE202267. After excluding nine aortic occlusive disease samples, GSE57691 (Illumina HumanHT-12 v4.0, GPL10558) contributed 49 full-thickness AAA and 10 organ-donor control aortic-wall samples. GSE232911 (Affymetrix Human Transcriptome Array 2.0, GPL17586) contributed 220 media and adventitia profiles from thrombus-covered and thrombus-free regions of 76 patients with AAA and 26 corresponding profiles from 13 organ-donor controls. The integrated training dataset therefore comprised 305 expression profiles from 125 patients with AAA and 23 controls, with participant numbers distinguished from regional profile numbers.13,14 Microarray probes were mapped to gene symbols, and expression values were log2-transformed when required and normalized using limma (version 3.58.1). GSE57691 and GSE232911 were restricted to common genes, merged, and batch-adjusted using ComBat in sva (version 3.50.0). The GPL570 subset of GSE7084, comprising two pooled AAA and two pooled control abdominal-aorta samples, was used as the external microarray test cohort.15 The RNA-seq evaluation cohorts included seven AAA and seven control abdominal aortic-wall samples from GSE183464 (Illumina HiSeq 4000, GPL20301), three AAA aortic-wall samples and five non-AAA infrarenal aortic-wall controls from GSE269845, and four AAA and four normal aortic samples from GSE202267 (both Illumina NovaSeq 6000, GPL24676). Thrombus samples in GSE269845 and thoracic aortic aneurysm samples in GSE202267 were excluded. The processed FPKM matrices from GSE183464 and GSE269845 were normalized using limma, whereas GSE202267 raw counts underwent variance-stabilizing transformation using DESeq2 (version 1.46.0).16–18 Pre- and post-adjustment distributions and principal-component plots are presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis and Functional Enrichment of Candidate Genes

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed with WGCNA (version 1.73) to identify modules associated with AAA.19 Candidate powers from 1 to 20 were evaluated using the scale-free topology model fit and mean connectivity, and a soft-thresholding power of β = 8 was selected to construct the adjacency matrix. The adjacency matrix was converted to a topological-overlap matrix, hierarchical clustering was performed using topological-overlap dissimilarity, and modules were defined by dynamic tree cutting. Modules associated with AAA status were retained, and genes shared by these modules and the DEG set were taken forward. Functional interpretation used GO and KEGG analyses in clusterProfiler and DOSE (version 3.28.2).20,21

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction and Machine Learning–Based Hub Gene Identification

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database based on genes overlapping between the DEGs and the significant modules identified by WGCNA.22 The resulting interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).23 To identify potential hub genes within the PPI network, the cytoHubba plugin was applied, and genes were ranked using five topological algorithms. The 20 highest-ranking genes were retained for subsequent feature-selection and prediction analyses.

The 20 network-prioritized genes were evaluated in a staged machine-learning workflow using the integrated training dataset (GSE57691 and GSE232911). Initial feature reduction was performed with LASSO, Random Forest, Stepglm, and glmBoost; gene subsets from this stage were then used to train classifiers with eight additional learners (Ridge, ElasticNet, plsRglm, GBM, XGBoost, support vector machine, LDA, and Naïve Bayes), yielding 113 sequential or individual pipelines in total. Pipelines retaining five or fewer variables were excluded from comparison. Performance was compared by mean ROC AUC across the training set and three additional cohorts (GSE7084, GSE183464, GSE269845), and the model with the highest mean AUC was retained as the final workflow; genes selected by this model were designated hub genes.24

To obtain a participant-level estimate of classifier performance, the prespecified 12-gene panel and LDA classifier were subsequently evaluated using stratified 5×5 nested cross-validation without repeated feature selection. Expression profiles from the same participant in GSE232911 were averaged beforehand to prevent leakage from repeated sampling. Pooled outer-fold out-of-fold (OOF) predictions were used to estimate ROC AUC (95% CI), calibration, precision-recall AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, F1 score, and decision-curve analysis. A single Youden-index threshold derived from the OOF predictions was then applied unchanged to GSE7084, GSE183464, GSE269845, and GSE202267 for cross-cohort evaluation. All analyses were performed using the MASS (version 7.3–60.2) and pROC (version 1.19.0.1) packages. SHAP analysis was used to describe model feature contributions rather than causal or biological importance.25

Immune Infiltration Profiling and Hub Gene Association Using CIBERSORT

Bulk-expression profiles were deconvolved with CIBERSORT to estimate the relative abundance of 22 immune-cell populations. Expression matrices were uploaded to the CIBERSORTx platform (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), and the resulting cell-fraction estimates were used to compare immune composition between AAA and control tissue. We also tested correlations between hub-gene expression and inferred immune-cell abundance to identify gene-cell relationships associated with disease status.

Transcription Factor Regulatory Analysis and Drug–Gene Interaction Prediction

Potential transcriptional regulators of the hub genes were queried in TRRUST v2 (www.grnpedia.org/trrust).26 The 10 highest-confidence transcription factors were retained, and their expression relationships with the corresponding hub genes were evaluated by correlation analysis.

Drug-gene relationships were then explored with DSigDB. This analysis linked the selected hub genes to reported drugs and bioactive compounds, allowing identification of candidate agents with potential relevance to the AAA-associated molecular network.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

We used bidirectional two-sample MR to examine whether genetically predicted expression of the hub genes was associated with AAA risk. Reporting of the MR component followed the STROBE-MR guideline, and the completed checklist is included with the supplementary materials. Exposure instruments were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS resource (https://opengwas.io/datasets/), while AAA summary statistics were taken from FinnGen Release 12 (https://r12.finngen.fi/).27

Exposure-associated SNPs were selected at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and clumped at r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window. Instrument strength was checked with the F statistic, and variants with F < 10 were removed. The primary causal estimate was obtained with inverse-variance weighting, using fixed- or random-effects models according to Cochran’s Q test heterogeneity results. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed with the MR-Egger intercept and the MR-PRESSO global test (1,000 permutations; significance threshold = 0.05). If MR-PRESSO identified outliers, the analysis was repeated after excluding them. Additional diagnostics included funnel plots, Cochran’s Q test statistics, and leave-one-out analyses to evaluate whether any single variant materially altered the pooled estimate.

MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO packages, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value below 0.05.28,29

Bayesian Colocalization Analysis

To distinguish shared genetic signals from associations caused by linkage, Bayesian colocalization was performed with the COLOC R package. The analysis tested whether the exposure and outcome could be explained by the same causal variant within a locus, thereby providing an additional check on the MR findings.

The posterior probabilities of five mutually exclusive models were estimated: no association with either trait (H0), association with the exposure only (H1), association with the outcome only (H2), two distinct causal variants (H3), and one shared causal variant (H4). A posterior probability for H4 above 0.80 was interpreted as strong evidence that the traits shared a causal signal.30

Summary Data–Based Mendelian Randomization Analysis and Phenome-Wide Association Study

SMR analysis was conducted on a Linux platform to evaluate whether cis-eQTL signals and AAA GWAS signals supported shared gene-trait associations.31 Cis-eQTL data served as the exposure source and FinnGen AAA summary statistics as the outcome source. The HEIDI test was applied to differentiate likely pleiotropy from linkage, with default SMR settings retained. Genes were considered significant when they passed FDR correction (FDR < 0.05) and showed no evidence of heterogeneity by HEIDI (P_HEIDI > 0.05). Significant SMR genes were intersected with the hub-gene list, and single-variant effect estimates were visualized for the overlapping genes. To examine wider phenotypic associations, a gene-level PheWAS was performed through the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal, covering 17,361 binary traits and 1,419 quantitative traits; genome-wide significance was set at P < 5 × 10−8.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Analysis and Computer Simulation of Hub Gene Knockout

Single-cell analyses used GSE226492, which contains three AAA samples and three non-diseased aortic-wall controls33. Processing was performed in Seurat (version 5.4.0). Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 4,000 detected genes, or mitochondrial content above 10% were removed. After normalization and selection of highly variable genes, the data were reduced by PCA, clustered, and annotated by cell type. Sample-level batch structure was corrected with Harmony (version 1.2.4), and UMAP was used for visualization. Quality-control metrics, doublet assessment, and dimension-reduction diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Figure 2.

Within each annotated population, AAA and control cells were compared to identify cell-type-specific transcriptional differences. Hub-gene expression was profiled across the major cell compartments to determine both cellular enrichment and disease-associated shifts. GO and KEGG analyses were then used to interpret the biological programs represented by the cell-type-specific DEG sets.

Cell-cell signaling was reconstructed with CellChat (version 1.6.1). Ligand-receptor probabilities were used to quantify communication strength between cell populations, and pathway-level as well as centrality analyses were used to identify dominant sender, receiver, and mediator populations within the AAA microenvironment.

Virtual perturbation analysis was performed with scTenifoldKnk (version 1.0.3)34. Gene-regulatory networks were inferred from the scRNA-seq data, after which selected nodes were computationally removed to simulate loss-of-function states. Genes with significant network responses were ranked by combined Z scores and P values. This in silico analysis was prespecified as hypothesis-generating and does not constitute biological knockout or direct functional-validation evidence.

Murine Models of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

Experimental evaluation in mice used two complementary AAA models. Procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese Institutes for Medical Research (CIMR-IACUC-25-018), were reported in accordance with ARRIVE 2.0, guidelines, and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 2011). AAA was induced by continuous angiotensin II (AngII) delivery or local porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE) exposure.

For the Ang II model, an ALZET model 2004 micro-osmotic pump (Durect, Cupertino, CA, USA) was implanted subcutaneously over the dorsal surface to infuse Ang II (1,000 ng/kg/min; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 28 days; control animals received saline under the same conditions while maintained on a 40% high-fat diet.

The PPE model was established in C57BL/6J mice under inhaled isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were first induced in a chamber with 3–5% isoflurane in oxygen (1.0–1.5 L/min) and then maintained on 1.5–2.5% isoflurane through a face mask during the operation. After midline laparotomy, the infrarenal aorta was exposed and separated from adjacent structures. A segment of the vessel was contacted for 10 minutes with gauze soaked in PPE (≥ 3 units/mg protein; YuanYe Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), then rinsed with sterile PBS before layered abdominal closure. Sham mice underwent the same surgical exposure but received PBS in place of PPE, and all animals recovered on a warming pad.

Aortic enlargement was followed by high-frequency ultrasonography with the Vevo® 2100 system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). At day 28, animals were euthanized with 5% inhaled isoflurane until breathing ceased. Once deep anesthesia was confirmed by loss of reflexes, the chest was opened, the right atrium was incised, and ice-cold PBS was infused through the left ventricle to clear the circulation before tissue collection. The abdominal aorta was excised, freed of surrounding adventitial tissue under a dissecting microscope, photographed beside a calibration ruler, and measured with a digital caliper. Samples intended for protein assays were rinsed in saline, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored frozen until analysis. AAA was defined as a maximal diameter at least 1.5 times that of the normal aorta.

Human Sample of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

Human aneurysmal aortic tissue was collected from patients undergoing operative AAA repair, and non-aneurysmal abdominal aortic tissue was obtained from available control donors. The Beijing Hospital Ethics Committee approved the study (2025BJYYEC-KY342-01), all participants provided written informed consent before sampling, and the human-tissue study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Specimens were washed in chilled saline, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and preserved for protein measurements.

Western Blot

Whole-protein extracts were prepared from frozen aortic specimens in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing 1% PMSF. After centrifugation at 12,000 x g for 20 minutes at 4 degrees C, protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay. Ten micrograms of protein per sample were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with antibodies recognizing human/murine PTPN22/Ptpn22 (1:1,000; A1406, ABclonal), human HLA-DRB1 or its murine functional homolog H2-Eb1 (1:1,000; A7685, ABclonal), human HLA-DPB1 or its murine functional homolog H2-Ab1 (1:3,000; A25794, ABclonal), and β-actin (1:20,000; 66,009-1-Ig, Proteintech). Signals were acquired by chemiluminescence and quantified in ImageJ.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were examined for distributional assumptions. Two-group comparisons used unpaired t tests when normality criteria were met and nonparametric tests otherwise. All statistical and bioinformatic analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0. Pearson correlation was used for gene-expression relationships and Spearman correlation for associations between inferred immune-cell fractions and gene expression. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Result

Identification of Key Gene Modules Associated with AAA Through Integrated DEG Analysis and WGCNA

A total of 478 DEGs met log2 fold-change > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 between AAA and control profiles (Figure 2A and B).

A WGCNA was constructed to identify modules associated with AAA (Figure 2C). Module-trait relationship analysis revealed that MEred had the most significant positive correlation with AAA (R = 0.51, p = 3e-21), while MEturquoise showed a significant negative correlation (R = −0.37, p = 2e-11) (Figure 2D). Consequently, MEred was selected as the key module of interest. Further intra-module analysis confirmed a significant positive correlation between module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) within MEred. The 190 genes contained in this module were thus identified as critical AAA-related genes for subsequent downstream analyses.

Intersecting the DEG set with the AAA-associated WGCNA module yielded 99 shared genes (Figure 2E). Enrichment analysis of this overlap set pointed primarily to immune biology (Figure 2F–H). The strongest GO terms involved MHC class II antigen presentation, T-cell activation, and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, with related cellular-component and molecular-function terms centered on the MHC class II complex and peptide-antigen binding. KEGG analysis likewise highlighted immune-related pathways, including intestinal immune network for IgA production, allograft rejection, and cell-adhesion molecule interactions. A PPI network was then generated from these 99 genes to support downstream hub-gene prioritization (Figure 2I).

Development of a 12-Gene Diagnostic Signature for AAA Using Machine Learning and SHAP Analysis

The top 20 genes from the PPI network were ranked with the Degree algorithm in cytoHubba and then entered into the machine-learning workflow (Figure 3A). Across 113 trained classifiers, the Stepglm[both]+LDA combination achieved the highest average AUC across the training and test datasets (Figure 3B). This model retained 12 diagnostic genes: PTPN22, CTSS, GZMA, CD74, LCP2, HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1, and TNFSF13B.

Each of the 12 retained genes showed an AUC above 0.70 in the training cohort (Figure 3C). Their performance and expression patterns were then examined in GSE7084, GSE269845, GSE183464, and the external validation dataset GSE202267 (Figure 3D and E). The genes remained consistently upregulated in AAA samples across the validation datasets (P < 0.05), and pairwise-correlation analysis showed coordinated expression among several members of the panel (Figure 3F).

In the supplementary participant-level assessment, the prespecified 12-gene LDA classifier achieved an out-of-fold ROC AUC of 0.938 (95% CI, 0.890–0.987) and an average precision of 0.988. At the fixed Youden-derived threshold, the classifier showed a sensitivity of 0.960, a specificity of 0.783, and an accuracy of 0.932. Calibration results, additional classification metrics, decision-curve analysis, and cohort-specific ROC AUCs with 95% confidence intervals are presented in Supplementary Figure 3. GSE7084, GSE183464, and GSE269845 were evaluated as cross-cohort assessments, whereas GSE202267 served as the only untouched external evaluation cohort.

SHAP analysis was used to inspect the contribution of individual features to the final model. HLA-DRA, CTSS, and HLA-DPA1 ranked highest by global feature importance (Figure 3G). Dependence plots showed that higher HLA-DRA and CTSS expression generally increased the predicted probability of AAA, whereas TNFSF13B and HLA-DQB1 showed inverse effects in some ranges (Figure 3H). The feature-ranking pattern was confirmed by mean absolute SHAP values (Figure 3I), and sample-level waterfall and force plots illustrated how individual genes jointly influenced the predicted probability in representative cases (Figure 3J and K).

Next, we examined the associations between the prioritized genes and the inferred immune-cell landscape. As shown in Figure 4D, PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 were significantly correlated with multiple immune-cell subsets, particularly macrophages (M0 and M2) and T-cell populations, suggesting close associations with the inflammatory microenvironment. To explore potential upstream regulatory mechanisms, a transcription factor-target gene regulatory network was constructed (Figure 4E), highlighting CIITA and members of the RFX family as major regulators of HLA class II genes. Drug-gene enrichment analysis based on DSigDB identified several associated compounds, including (-)-isoprenaline, palladium-related entries, and azathioprine (Figure 4F and G). Genetic Prioritization of the 12-Gene Signature by MR, SMR, and Colocalization

To evaluate genetic support for the machine-learning-derived genes, bidirectional two-sample MR, Bayesian colocalization, SMR, and transcriptomic concordance analyses were integrated. These methods provide evidence of different strengths and were not treated as interchangeable proof of causality.

Forward MR associated genetically predicted PTPN22 expression with higher AAA risk (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.39, P = 0.01; 4 SNPs). HLA-DRB1 (5 SNPs) and HLA-DPB1 (8 SNPs) were not significant in forward MR (Figure 5A). Reverse MR did not support an effect of AAA liability on candidate-gene expression. MR-Egger intercepts did not indicate directional pleiotropy, but heterogeneity and the limited instrument counts reduce confidence in locus-specific inference. Full plots are shown inSupplementary Figures 5 and 6.

Colocalization analysis revealed that HLA-DRB1 (PP.H4 = 0.97) exhibited the highest posterior probability for a shared causal variant between eQTL and AAA GWAS signals (Figures 5B–D). Regional association plots for the remaining hub genes with limited evidence of colocalization are presented in Supplementary Figure 7.

SMR analysis detected genome-wide significant signals at several loci, including LDAH, PSMA6, and multiple HLA-region genes. Additional signals were observed at KLK13, APLNR, ALDH1A1, NOC3L, ARIDB38, CADM4, and PLAUR. Among genes with positive transcriptomic fold changes, 3 overlapped with positive SMR signals, whereas 9 were unique to transcriptomics and 518 were unique to SMR (Figure 5E). No overlap was observed among downregulated genes (Figure 5F).

At the gene level, PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 each showed concordant eQTL-GWAS effect patterns (Figure 5G, J, M). Regional analyses supported shared local signals at 1p13 for PTPN22, 6p21 for HLA-DRB1, and 6p21.3 for HLA-DPB1 (Figure 5H, K, N), and PheWAS results demonstrated broader immune-related associations for all three genes (Figure 5I, L, O).

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Landscape, Hub Gene Expression Dynamics, and Intercellular Communication in AAA

Analysis of GSE226492 retained 50,169 high-quality cells after quality control, including 18,425 AAA cells and 31,744 control cells. UMAP resolved ten major populations: fibroblasts, B cells, T cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, NK cells, plasma cells, and neutrophils (Figure 6A). Hub-gene expression differed by cell type and disease state (Figure 6C). HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DPB1, and CD74 were most prominent in macrophages and B cells and were more frequently expressed in AAA than in control tissue. Heatmap analysis showed coordinated increases in MHC class II-related genes together with TNFSF13B and CD74, whereas PTPN22 and CTSS displayed more restricted cell-type distributions (Figure 6D and E; Supplementary Figure 8). Candidates for experimental validation were selected by integrating complementary evidence rather than SHAP ranking alone. Following machine-learning identification of signature genes, bidirectional MR, Bayesian colocalization, SMR, and transcriptomic analyses were used for genetic prioritization. PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 were ultimately selected because they were the only three upregulated genes supported by concordant transcriptomic and SMR causal-effect directions, together with complementary MR and colocalization evidence. Within the MHC class II gene cluster, HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DPB1 showed more consistent genetic support than their corresponding α-chain genes, HLA-DRA and HLA-DPA1. This pattern is consistent with established HLA immunogenetics, in which β-chain genes are generally more polymorphic than their paired α-chain genes, particularly HLA-DRA.

Functional enrichment differed across cell populations (Figure 6F–H). Endothelial-cell and smooth-muscle-cell DEGs were enriched for focal adhesion and apoptotic signaling terms, whereas B-cell and T-cell DEGs were enriched for lymphocyte activation and endoplasmic-reticulum protein processing. In macrophages, DEGs were enriched for NF-κB signaling pathway, leukocyte activation, and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion (Figure 6I and J).

CellChat analysis identified macrophages and fibroblasts as major signaling nodes in AAA tissue (Figure 6K and L). Among the strongest macrophage-to-T-cell interactions were several MHC class II-CD4 ligand-receptor pairs involving HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DRA (Figure 6M). The CD74+TNFSF13B-TNFRSF13C axis connected macrophage-derived signals with B-cell survival, and CCL2-CCR2 together with TNF-related interactions contributed to broader immune-endothelial communication patterns (Figure 6N and O).

Collectively, these single-cell analyses provide a high-resolution cellular atlas of AAA, demonstrating pervasive upregulation of antigen presentation and immune activation hub genes across diverse cell populations, and delineating a complex intercellular communication network centered on MHC class II signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine-receptor interactions. Furthermore, they provide a robust scientific rationale for the subsequent in silico knockout analyses, as well as the empirical validation of PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 in murine and human tissue models.

Hypothesis-Generating in silico Perturbation Analysis Suggests Convergent Alarmin- and IL-17-Related Responses in AAA Macrophages

Hypothesis-generating in silico perturbation analysis was performed in AAA-derived macrophages for PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 using scTenifoldKnk. Each simulated perturbation affected only a small fraction of the modeled transcriptome, with approximately 0.3% of genes meeting the significance threshold (Figure 7A–C, E–G, and I–K), suggesting a focused rather than diffuse computational network response.

Perturbation of PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 produced convergent predicted transcriptional changes. S100A9, S100A8, FCN1, VCAN, and S100A12 were repeatedly ranked among the most strongly predicted upregulated genes. In particular, the recurrent increase in the alarmins S100A8 and S100A9 raises the hypothesis that these three prioritized genes are linked to regulatory programs that restrain alarmin-associated innate immune amplification. Because PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 were themselves upregulated in AAA tissues, the perturbation pattern is consistent with a working model in which their increased expression may represent a compensatory immune-regulatory response rather than a purely pathogenic signal. Under this model, these genes may function as molecular “brakes” that limit excessive inflammatory activation; however, the computational analysis cannot determine whether this response is protective, maladaptive, or secondary to established inflammation.

Functional enrichment analyses consistently identified innate immune defense, extracellular matrix organization, cell-adhesion molecules, and the IL-17 signaling pathway across all three perturbation models (Figure 7D, H, and L). The recurrent enrichment of IL-17-related programs after in silico perturbation of these AAA-upregulated genes raises the hypothesis that PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 may jointly act within a compensatory negative-feedback axis in AAA macrophages. Because IL-17 signaling is linked to neutrophil recruitment and vascular-wall inflammation, increased expression of these genes in aneurysmal tissue may help restrain excessive alarmin- and IL-17-driven inflammatory amplification and thereby support local immune homeostasis. Nevertheless, this proposed mechanistic model is hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as biological knockout evidence.

Protein-Level Evaluation in Murine AAA Models and Human AAA Tissues

Protein expression of PTPN22 and the murine homologs H2-Eb1 and H2-Ab1 was then examined in two mouse AAA models and in human tissue samples (Figure 8A). Ultrasound imaging and gross anatomical inspection confirmed aneurysm formation in the AngII and PPE models (Figure 8B and C).

Western blotting showed higher Ptpn22, H2-Eb1, and H2-Ab1 abundance in aneurysmal tissue from AngII-treated mice (Figure 8D and E), and the same pattern was observed in the PPE model (Figure 8F and G). Human AAA specimens likewise showed increased PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 protein levels relative to control aortic tissue (Figure 8H and I).

Discussion

This study combined transcriptomic screening, machine learning, causal-inference analyses, single-cell profiling, and experimental validation to identify immune-related markers of AAA. The analytic workflow narrowed a 12-gene diagnostic panel to three genes, PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1, that were supported by genetic, single-cell, and protein-level evidence. Across these layers, the results converged on antigen-presentation biology and inflammatory signaling as recurring features of AAA tissue.

The prevailing view of AAA as a purely degenerative vascular disease driven by mechanical and atherosclerotic factors has been progressively supplanted by evidence implicating chronic immune dysregulation as a central pathogenic force.1,2 Recent reviews have comprehensively characterized the immune landscape of AAA: Zhu et al documented the pivotal roles of immune cell infiltration and maladaptive adaptive immune responses in driving aortic wall degeneration.7 Márquez-Sánchez and Koltsova established that dynamic shifts in the composition and activation of infiltrating immune cells collectively drive vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) apoptosis and extracellular matrix destruction throughout disease progression.6 Within this context, the consistent upregulation of MHC class II molecules and their co-chaperone CD74 observed in the present study is mechanistically significant. MHC class II complexes present processed extracellular antigens to CD4⁺ T cells, a prerequisite for sustaining adaptive immune responses, and Lu et al provided direct evidence that AAA harbors features of an autoimmune antigen-driven process, including clonal T cell expansions and disease-associated HLA class II allele enrichment within lesional tissue.5 Notably, CD74 functions not only as an MHC class II chaperone but also as the receptor for macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), a pleiotropic pro-inflammatory cytokine, suggesting that its upregulation in AAA may simultaneously amplify antigen presentation and MIF-driven macrophage activation. The cell-cell communication analysis further identified a CD74-TNFSF13B–TNFRSF13C signaling axis linking macrophage-derived signals to B cell survival and differentiation, consistent with a model of sustained humoral immune activation within the aneurysmal microenvironment. The current findings establish dysregulated MHC class II-mediated antigen presentation as a central immunological hub in AAA pathogenesis. This conclusion is further supported by Gao et al, who identified the downstream consequences of aberrant antigen presentation, including MMP secretion, VSMC apoptosis, and cytokine-mediated inflammation, as core molecular mechanisms driving aortic aneurysm progression.32

PTPN22, which encodes protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 22, is a potent genetic risk locus shared across multiple autoimmune diseases. Tizaoui et al previously outlined its role as a multi-lineage immune regulator that modulates signaling in T cells, B cells, and innate immune cells.33 Furthermore, James et al revealed that PTPN22 enzymatic activity is subject to redox regulation via oxidation, which subsequently modulates the severity of T cell-dependent autoimmune inflammation.34 In the context of vascular pathology, Ruan et al recently identified PTPN22 as a critical genetic biomarker for AAA, demonstrating its consistent upregulation in aortic tissues and infiltrating immune cells.35 Building upon these clinical observations, the current study reveals that PTPN22 is significantly upregulated in AAA tissues, with preferential expression in T cells and macrophages at the single-cell level. Additionally, Mendelian randomization analysis provides genetic evidence supporting a causal role for PTPN22 in AAA susceptibility. By contrast, scTenifoldKnk predicted that acute computational loss of PTPN22 function activated S100A8/S100A9 alarmin signaling and the IL-17 pathway. These findings are not inherently contradictory because MR estimates the lifelong effect of genetically determined gene expression, tissue expression reflects established disease together with changes in cellular composition, whereas virtual perturbation models simulate an acute loss-of-function state. Therefore, the increased PTPN22 expression observed in AAA tissues may represent a compensatory response or reflect increased immune-cell infiltration, although maladaptive immune activation cannot be excluded. The present study cannot distinguish among these possibilities, and further mechanistic investigations are required to clarify the context-dependent role of PTPN22 in AAA pathogenesis.

HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DPB1 reside within the MHC region on chromosome 6p21.3, where extensive linkage disequilibrium complicates locus-specific causal inference. Although HLA-DRB1 showed strong support from colocalization analysis, forward MR did not demonstrate significant causal associations for either HLA-DRB1 or HLA-DPB1. Nevertheless, previous studies have implicated MHC class II-mediated antigen presentation as a key driver of adaptive immune activation in AAA.36 Gong et al reported that MHC class II-dependent activation of CD4⁺ T cells by infiltrating macrophages and dendritic cells promotes Th1/Th17 inflammatory responses during AAA progression.37 Consistent with these findings, the current single-cell analyses localized HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DPB1 predominantly to antigen-presenting cells and identified HLA-DRB1–CD4 and HLA-DPB1–CD4 interactions as major macrophage-to-T-cell communication pathways. Furthermore, virtual knockout of HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DPB1 produced transcriptional responses broadly similar to those observed following PTPN22 perturbation, including increased expression of S100A8, S100A9, FCN1, VCAN, and S100A12, together with activation of the IL-17 pathway. These findings suggest that these genes participate in a shared immune regulatory network associated with antigen presentation and inflammatory amplification. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, the corresponding murine proteins (H2-Eb1 and H2-Ab1) were upregulated together with Ptpn22 in both AngII- and PPE-induced AAA models, and increased expression of the human proteins was confirmed in AAA specimens. This cross-species pattern supports their involvement in a common tissue-associated immune response but does not establish a conserved causal function. It is noteworthy that the AngII- and PPE-induced murine AAA models differ substantially in their initiating mechanisms. Therefore, the concordant upregulation of Ptpn22, H2-Eb1, and H2-Ab1 across both models is more likely to reflect a convergent downstream response to vascular injury, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune-cell recruitment than a shared initiating mechanism. Furthermore, because temporal dynamics and spatial localization were not assessed in the present study, it cannot be determined whether the expression of these proteins differs across disease stages or aortic-wall compartments.

Pathway enrichment analysis reveals profound NF-κB signaling and leukocyte activation within the AAA macrophage compartment. This supports the paradigm that macrophages actively orchestrate adaptive immune hyperactivation, where M1 polarization drives ECM degradation and VSMC apoptosis via pro-inflammatory cytokines and MMPs.38 Concurrently, the uniform activation of the IL-17 pathway across all virtual knockout conditions establishes the Th17/IL-17 axis as a central pathogenic driver. IL-17 signaling, exacerbated by myeloid-derived suppressor cells and endothelial dysfunction, critically promotes neutrophil recruitment and further MMP-mediated structural damage.39,40 The current findings suggest that PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 collectively maintain immune tolerance by restricting Th17 expansion and dampening IL-17 signaling. Consequently, their dysregulation in AAA removes a crucial brake on vascular wall degeneration.3,4 Furthermore, the convergent upregulation of S100A8 and S100A9, the primary transcriptional responders to hub gene silencing, highlights the alarmin pathway as a key downstream effector. Upon inflammatory stimulation, these endogenous DAMPs activate TLR4 and RAGE cascades, amplifying NF-κB-driven cytokine production and macrophage activation.10,41 Clinical evidence corroborates this, linking elevated S100A8/A9 plasma concentrations to aneurysmal disease severity.42 These data indicate that PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 normally function upstream to restrain alarmin-mediated amplification. Their pathological overexpression in AAA likely reflects a failed compensatory response to a dysregulated inflammatory milieu, ultimately perpetuating a vicious cycle of sterile inflammation and aortic degeneration.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the diagnostic models were derived from diseased aortic tissue rather than blood-based screening samples and therefore do not establish noninvasive clinical utility. Second, the training data combined platforms, tissue compartments, and control sources; GSE232911 includes multiple regional/tunica profiles per participant, creating potential within-participant non-independence, and residual batch or source confounding may remain. Third, the supplementary nested cross-validation evaluated only the prespecified 12-gene LDA classifier and did not fully nest the original upstream feature- and model-selection workflow. Model generalizability was further limited by the use of several cohorts during model ranking, the small untouched external evaluation cohort, and the absence of complete participant-level covariates for multivariable adjustment. Fourth, the external cohorts and the human and murine protein-validation cohorts were small and heterogeneous. Fifth, the MR analyses relied on limited instrument sets, and the complex linkage-disequilibrium structure and potential local horizontal pleiotropy within the HLA region cannot be excluded; more permissive MHC-region instrument selection combined with LD-aware modeling was not performed. Sixth, scTenifoldKnk and drug-gene analyses are hypothesis-generating, and no direct cell-type-specific perturbation experiments were performed. Finally, the cross-sectional datasets did not permit temporal or spatially resolved assessment of these immune-expression changes during AAA progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this integrative multi-omics study prioritized PTPN22, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DPB1 as tissue-associated biomarkers and candidate immune regulators in AAA. Integrated transcriptomic, genetic, single-cell, and protein analyses consistently implicated antigen-presentation and inflammatory programs in AAA and supported increased expression of these genes in both human aneurysmal tissue and two independent mouse models. The genetic evidence provided varying levels of support across the three genes and should be interpreted as prioritization rather than definitive causal inference. Furthermore, the in silico perturbation and drug–gene analyses generated mechanistic and therapeutic hypotheses but did not establish biological function or clinical efficacy. Together, these findings provide a rationale for prospective clinical validation and direct mechanistic studies to determine the diagnostic and therapeutic relevance of these candidate genes in AAA.
