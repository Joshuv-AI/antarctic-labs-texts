Prevalence, aetiology and predisposing factors for tinea capitis amongst pupils in rural communities in the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana
Authors: Paa Gyasi Hagan, Nana Ayegua Hagan Seneadza, Kwabena Oppong Amoako, Nyonuku Akosua Baddoo, Afua Owusua Darkwah Abrahams, Worlanyo Kudzo Seneadza, Christian Owoo, Margaret Lartey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTinea capitis remains a common superficial fungal infection among school-aged children in sub-Saharan Africa. However, up-to-date data on its burden and aetiological agents in many regions of Ghana are limited. This study determined the prevalence, aetiology, and predisposing factors for tinea capitis among primary school children in rural communities in the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2019 among 708 pupils selected through multistage sampling from six rural basic schools in Sunyani, Techiman, and Kintampo North municipalities of the Brong Ahafo Region. Data were collected using structured questionnaires and clinical scalp examinations. Scalp scrapings and hair samples from clinically suspected cases were examined using potassium hydroxide microscopy and cultured on Sabouraud’s agar for fungal identification. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with tinea capitis.ResultsThe overall prevalence of tinea capitis was 15.0% (95% CI: 12.4–17.8), with the highest prevalence observed in Kintampo North Municipality (19.5%). The grey patch variant was the most common clinical presentation (76.8%). Of the 106 samples cultured, 30.2% yielded dermatophytes, predominantly Trichophyton tonsurans (68.8%), followed by Trichophyton rubrum (15.6%). Aspergillus niger was the most common non-dermatophyte isolate. Significant predictors of infection included residence in Kintampo North (aOR = 2.62; 95% CI: 1.66–4.15) and presence of scalp complaints (aOR = 9.10; 95% CI: 5.88–14.29). Not having family members with scalp lesions was protective (aOR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.52–0.93).ConclusionTinea capitis is moderately prevalent among pupils in rural communities of the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana. The predominance of the anthropophilic dermatophyte T. tonsurans indicates that person-to-person transmission is the principal route of spread, emphasizing the need for school- and household-based control measures. Geographic location, scalp complaints, and a family history of infection were significant predictors of disease. Strengthened surveillance, early case detection, and improved access to systemic antifungal therapy are essential to reduce transmission and the burden of tinea capitis among Ghanaian school children.

Background

Tinea capitis, also known as scalp ringworm, is a fungal infection of the scalp hair follicles and the surrounding skin caused by dermatophytes that predominantly affects prepubertal children and remains one of the most common superficial infections worldwide [1–3]. Although the disease occurs globally, it is most prevalent in low-and middle-income countries, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Asia, and Southern and Eastern Europe, where recent studies continue to report high prevalence rates among school-aged children [1, 4–6].

The epidemiology of tinea capitis varies across geographical settings and over time, influenced by socioeconomic conditions, hygiene practices, population density, and access to healthcare [7, 8]. The distribution of causative dermatophyte species is also evolving, with both anthropophilic and zoophilic organisms contributing to transmission [7, 9]. Clinically, tinea capitis presents with a wide spectrum of manifestations, ranging from non-inflammatory scaly patches and alopecia to severe inflammatory forms such as kerion [3, 10]. Delayed diagnosis and treatment may result in bacterial superinfection and psychosocial consequences. Children are particularly susceptible because of immature sebaceous gland activity, close interpersonal contact, and the sharing of hair grooming items. Reported risk factors include overcrowding, poor scalp hygiene, low socioeconomic status, and a family history of infection [1, 4].

In Ghana, however, comprehensive and up-to-date data on the burden and aetiological patterns of tinea capitis remain limited, with few published studies and little evidence from many parts of the country, including the Brong Ahafo Region [11]. The available Ghanaian literature is geographically sparse, with much of the primary epidemiological evidence originating from studies conducted in the Greater Accra Region more than a decade ago, with the prevalence rates of 8.4% and 8.7% [12, 13]. Recent studies continue to identify tinea capitis as the predominant superficial fungal infection in Ghana but also highlight the paucity of updated, region-specific epidemiological data needed to inform prevention and control efforts [11, 14]. Moreover, regional differences in climate, environmental conditions, population characteristics, and socioeconomic factors may influence both disease prevalence and the distribution of dermatophyte species. Generating region-specific data is therefore essential to guide local prevention and control strategies.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence, aetiological agents, and predisposing factors for tinea capitis among primary school children in rural communities in the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana.

Methods

Study design

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2019 among primary school children in rural communities of the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana.

Study area

The study was carried out in three municipalities within the former Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana, which was administratively reorganized in 2019 into the Bono, Bono East, and Ahafo Regions [15]. The selected municipalities were Sunyani, Techiman, and Kintampo North municipalities, representing the southern, middle, and northern belts of the region, respectively. The study area lies within the middle belt of Ghana and is characterized by a predominantly agrarian economy, with farming and livestock rearing as major economic activities. In many rural communities, households live in proximity to domestic animals, a factor that may contribute to dermatophyte transmission. The combined population of the study area is approximately 2.98 million [16].

Study population

The study population comprised pupils enrolled in kindergarten (KG) 1 through Primary 6 in selected basic schools in rural communities within the three municipalities. All pupils aged below 18 years who were present in school on the day of data collection were eligible to participate.

Sampling and participant selection

A multi-stage sampling approach was employed. A list of rural basic schools in the three municipalities was obtained from the Regional Education Directorate. Two schools were randomly selected (via balloting) from each municipality, resulting in six participating schools. Based on previously reported regional prevalence rates, ranging from 8.4% to 23% [13, 17, 18], the sample size was calculated using an assumed prevalence of 30% (to account for potential geographic, socioeconomic, and environmental variations), a 95% confidence level, a 10% non-response rate, and a design effect of 2 to account for the multi-stage sampling approach. This yielded an estimated minimum sample size of 708 pupils. Within each selected school, an average of 15 pupils were selected from each class (KG 1 to Primary 6) using systematic random sampling (with an interval of 3), with the class registers serving as the sampling frames. In all, 708 primary school children were recruited for the study.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted on school premises during break times, free periods, or after school hours to minimize disruption of school activities. A structured questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic information, including age, gender, ethnicity, parents’ occupations, and parents’ levels of education. Information on potential risk factors such as the presence (and kind) of animals at home, hair-barbing practices (place of haircut, use and source of hair clipper), sharing of hair comb, and bed sharing. For younger pupils, selected responses were verified with teachers or older siblings (where necessary). Each participant underwent a clinical examination of the scalp and exposed skin areas (whilst in their uniforms) in a well-lit classroom. Cervical and occipital lymphadenopathy were also assessed. A wood’s lamp examination was performed in a darkened room for pupils with clinically suspected lesions. A pupil was suspected to have tinea capitis if he/she had any clinical signs on the scalp such as scaly patches with or without alopecia, brittle hair strands, annular plaques, pustules, boggy masses (kerion) and thickened crusts (favus). For pupils with suspected tinea capitis, scalp scrapings and hair plucks were obtained using the blunt edge of a sterile scalpel blade and sterile forceps, respectively, under aseptic conditions. Before specimen collection, the affected area was cleaned with 70% ethyl alcohol to clear out the bacterial flora. The specimens were placed on a clean plain sheet of paper, folded into labelled envelopes and transported at room temperature to the Microbiology Laboratory of the University of Development Studies, Tamale, Ghana, for mycological analysis. Sample transportation and processing were completed within 24 h of collection. Direct microscopic examination using 20% potassium hydroxide was performed to detect fungal hyphae. Samples were cultured on Sabouraud’s agar supplemented with 0.5% chloramphenicol and 0.5% cycloheximide and incubated at 27 degrees Celsius for up to four weeks, with weekly observation for growth. Fungal identification was based on macroscopic colony morphology and microscopic characteristics using lactophenol cotton blue staining.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Stata version 16.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data, which were then presented in appropriate graphs and tables. The proportions of pupils with tinea capitis in the various municipalities were determined and compared across municipalities. The proportions of the different aetiological agents cultured from the specimens taken from the children with tinea capitis were calculated and compared. Logistic regression analyses were used to determine the significant predisposing factors associated with tinea capitis. The independent variables included in the models were municipality, age group, mother’s highest level of education, mother’s occupation, having scalp lesions, living with family members who had scalp lesions, presence of animals at home, sharing beds with others and carrying items on the head. Statistical significance was evaluated at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05).

Results

The study included 708 primary school children across three municipalities (236 each) in the Brong Ahafo Region. The mean age was 7.9 ± 3.2 years, with more than half aged between 8 and 12 years (53.7%). The sex distribution was relatively balanced, with slightly more males (52.3%) than females. Christianity was the predominant religion (75.4%), followed by Islam (20.6%). Regarding mothers’ education, 22.2% had no formal education, and 24.4% had only primary education, while nearly half (47.7%) were unsure of their mother’s highest level of education. Mothers’ occupations were predominantly farming (44.4%) and trading (27.7%). Notably, farming was most common in Kintampo North municipality (70.8%), whereas trading was more common in Sunyani and Techiman municipalities. Most children lived in households with more than six persons (64.4%), and 60.9% of households had 1–3 rooms. Other socio-demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1 below.

Generally, the prevalence of tinea capitis was 15.0% (95% CI = 12.4–17.8) and was highest among participants in the Kintampo municipality (19.5%; 95% CI = 14.6–25.1), followed by Sunyani municipality (15.7%; 95% CI = 11.3–20.9), then Techiman municipality (9.7%; 95% CI = 6.3–14.3). The grey patch type was the most common type of tinea capitis cases identified (76.8%). Seborrheic dermatitis-like tinea capitis and black dot type accounted for 12.5% and 5.3% of the cases, respectively (Figs. 1 and 2).

Among the 106 cases of tinea capitis observed, 30.2% were dermatophyte infections. Of the 32 isolated dermatophytes, more than two-thirds (68.8%) were Trichophyton tonsurans, and 15.6% were Trichophyton rubrum. The majority of the T. tonsurans infections were recorded in the Sunyani municipality, while Kintampo North municipality had the least number of this organism isolated. Among the non-dermatophyte fungal cultures isolated, 32.4% were Aspergillus niger, whilst there was no growth observed in 36.4% of cases (Table 3).

Approximately 98% of the cases of tinea capitis identified had the non-inflammatory types (Grey Patch Tinea Capitis [GPTC], Black Dot Tinea Capitis [BDTC] and Superficial Dermatophytic Tinea Capitis [SDTC]), while the inflammatory types (annular, pustular, favus, kerion) were seen in about 5% of the cases observed. Among the children who had animals at home, 98.9% had a non-inflammatory type of tinea capitis, whilst 3.3% had inflammatory tinea capitis. From the logistic regression analyses, factors significantly associated with tinea capitis were identified. Staying in the Kintampo North municipality significantly increased the odds of tinea capitis by almost 3-fold (aOR = 2.62; 95% CI = 1.66–4.15) compared with those staying in the Sunyani municipality. Participants who had scalp complaints were 9 times (aOR = 9.10; 95% CI = 5.88–14.29) more likely to have tinea capitis than those who did not. Participants without family members showing scalp lesions were 31% less likely to have tinea capitis compared with those with a family history (aOR = 0.69; 95% CI = 0.52–0.93). Not sharing a bed with others increased the odds of tinea capitis by 40% (aOR = 1.40; 95% CI = 1.01–1.92) compared with those who shared a bed with others (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides evidence on the prevalence, aetiology, and predisposing factors of tinea capitis among primary school children in rural communities in the Brong Ahafo region of Ghana. The overall prevalence of 15% confirms that tinea capitis remains an important public health concern in rural Ghanaian settings. Trichophyton tonsurans was the most prevalent dermatophyte cultured, whilst A. niger was the most prevalent non-dermatophyte cultured. The most frequently occurring clinical variant was grey patch tinea capitis. Geographical location, age, having scalp complaints, family history of scalp lesions, and bed-sharing factors were significant predisposing factors of tinea capitis among school children.

The observed prevalence falls within the range of 10–30% reported across sub-Saharan Africa, reflecting sustained endemic transmission among school-aged children [2, 6]. Although an earlier Ghanaian study conducted in the Greater Accra region reported a lower overall prevalence rate of 8.4%, higher rates (12.2% and 14.6%) were found in the rural schools within that study [13], comparable to our findings. The higher prevalence observed in Kintampo North suggests that environmental and socioeconomic factors may influence transmission dynamics. Factors such as overcrowding, large household sizes, rural agrarian living conditions, poor hygiene, and low socio-economic status have consistently been identified as drivers of dermatophyte transmission in endemic settings [5, 8, 19]. Close physical contact within households and classrooms likely facilitates person-to-person spread in these communities. Consistent with these observations, nearly one-third of the children in this study lived in households comprising more than six members, with a similar proportion residing in overcrowded households. These living conditions may have increased opportunities for close interpersonal contact, thereby promoting the transmission of dermatophyte infections.

Age-specific patterns were also evident. The highest prevalence occurred among children aged 8–12 years. This observation was similar to findings from both local [13] and regional [2, 4, 20] studies. This age group is typically the most physically active, frequently engaging in close-contact play and sports that may enhance transmission. In contrast, lower prevalence among adolescents aged 13–17 years aligns with previous reports [13, 21], and may reflect improved hygiene awareness, behavioural maturity and physiological changes associated with puberty, including alterations in sebum composition that inhibit dermatophyte growth.

Our study revealed a male preponderance among the children diagnosed with tinea capitis, consistent with findings from several previous studies [21–23]. The higher prevalence among males could be attributed to increased exposure to infectious agents through close body contact during play activities, which tend to be more common among males. In addition, behavioural factors such as comparatively lower attention to personal hygiene practices may contribute to increased susceptibility. The common practice of keeping short hair among boys may also facilitate easier access of fungal spores to the scalp, thereby increasing the risk of infection, as suggested in earlier reports. However, sex distribution in tinea capitis is not uniform across all settings. Some regional studies have reported a higher prevalence among prepubertal girls [24]. In such contexts, hormonal influences have been proposed as potential explanatory factors. For instance, variations in sebum composition and hormonal levels, including progesterone, may influence susceptibility to dermatophyte colonization. Differences in hair care practices and sociocultural grooming behaviours may also partly explain these contrasting findings. Together, these observations suggest that sex-related differences in tinea capitis prevalence are likely multifactorial and context-specific, reflecting an interplay of biological, behavioural, and environmental determinants.

The predominance of non-inflammatory clinical types, especially the grey patch variant, is consistent with infections caused by anthropophilic dermatophytes. Trichophyton tonsurans accounted for nearly 70% of isolated dermatophytes in this study. This aligns with global and African reports indicating that T. tonsurans has emerged as a dominant cause of tinea capitis in many regions, gradually replacing Microsporum species in several settings [7, 9]. Anthropophilic species are primarily transmitted through direct human contact or shared fomites, reinforcing the importance of school- and household-based transmission dynamics [10].

Residence in Kintampo North significantly increased the odds of infection nearly threefold. Geographic variability in prevalence has been documented across regions within the same country, often reflecting differences in socioeconomic conditions, hygiene practices, and access to healthcare [5, 6]. The strong association between scalp complaints and confirmed infection underscores the utility of symptom-based screening within school health programs. Family history of scalp lesions was independently associated with infection, supporting the well-established role of intrahousehold transmission [8, 10]. While a high proportion of pupils reported sharing combs and living in homes with animals, these factors were not independently associated after adjustment, suggesting that anthropophilic transmission may play a more dominant role in this setting than zoonotic spread. Contrary to expectations, children who did not share a bed with others had higher odds of tinea capitis. This finding is difficult to explain biologically and may reflect residual confounding, reverse causality, or unmeasured behavioural and environmental factors. For example, infected children may have been asked to sleep separately after developing the infection, or the observed association may be influenced by other exposures, such as contact with infected peers or the sharing of personal grooming items. Our cross-sectional study however captured the sleeping arrangement at the time of data collection.

The relatively low proportion of clinically suspected cases confirmed by culture highlights the limitations of clinical diagnosis alone, as several scalp conditions may mimic tinea capitis. Although all specimens were transported and processed within 24 h of collection, culture yield may have been affected by factors such as low fungal burden, prior antifungal exposure, sampling variability, or intermittent shedding of viable organisms. Similar challenges in mycological confirmation have been reported in field-based African studies [2]. These findings underscore the importance of mycological confirmation for accurate diagnosis and surveillance. An important observation in this study was the recovery of non-dermatophyte fungi, particularly A. niger, which accounted for approximately half of the non-dermatophyte isolates and was more frequently observed in Kintampo North Municipality. While non-dermatophyte fungi are often regarded as contaminants in superficial fungal infections, the exclusion of mixed dermatophyte and non-dermatophyte growth, together with the detection of most A. niger isolates on direct potassium hydroxide microscopy, suggests that some may represent true pathogens rather than contaminants. Similar reports of increasing isolation of non-dermatophyte moulds from suspected tinea capitis cases have been documented [25, 26]. Given the ubiquity of A. niger in soil and the frequent environmental exposure of children in these communities, including contact with soil-contaminated household items, further studies incorporating molecular identification methods are warranted to better define the role of non-dermatophyte fungi in tinea capitis.

A considerable proportion of collected samples in our study did not yield fungal growth on culture, a finding comparable to reports from similar studies in Nigeria [25]. Several factors may account for this observation. First, inadequate sample quantity or suboptimal sampling technique may reduce the likelihood of isolating viable dermatophytes. Second, prior or partial treatment with topical or systemic antifungal agents among some pupils could have suppressed fungal growth, leading to false-negative culture results despite clinical features suggestive of infection. Additionally, the fastidious nature of certain dermatophyte species may also have contributed to culture negativity [27, 28]. This finding highlights the importance of laboratory confirmation, as overreliance on clinical diagnosis may over or underestimate the true burden of infection. Although molecular methods such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) would have improved species identification and potentially enhanced detection of culture-negative infections, these methods were not performed because of financial and resource constraints.

Although tinea capitis is not life-threatening, its visible manifestations, particularly scalp scaling and alopecia, may contribute to stigma, psychological distress, and social difficulties among affected children, potentially affecting school attendance and quality of life [29]. These consequences highlight the importance of early identification and treatment, not only to reduce transmission but also to minimize the social and emotional impact of the disease among school-aged children.

The observed prevalence of tinea capitis in rural Ghana underscores the need for strengthened school health interventions. Integration of routine scalp examinations into Ghana’s School Health Education Programme could facilitate early detection and treatment. Evidence suggests that coordinated screening and treatment in school settings can significantly reduce transmission cycles [10]. Health education programs targeting pupils, parents, and teachers should emphasize avoidance of sharing grooming tools and early health-seeking behaviour. Given the significant role of intrahousehold transmission, interventions should extend beyond the index child to include affected household contacts. The predominance of anthropophilic species, particularly T. tonsurans, has therapeutic implications. Griseofulvin and terbinafine remain first-line systemic therapies, but species identification can inform optimal drug selection and treatment duration [3, 10]. Strengthening laboratory capacity for dermatophyte identification in regional facilities would support evidence-based management.

Our study provides important documentary evidence on the prevalence, aetiology, clinical variants, and predisposing factors of tinea capitis in the middle belt of Ghana. By employing a school-based design with multistage sampling and mycological confirmation in a relatively large sample, this study strengthens the existing body of literature and complements earlier work conducted in the southern zone of Ghana, particularly in the Greater Accra Region. The findings, therefore, contribute region-specific data that enhance understanding of the epidemiological variation of tinea capitis across ecological and socioeconomic zones within the country.

This study has several limitations. First, obtaining accurate sociodemographic and behavioural information from younger children was challenging; therefore, teachers and, where available, older siblings were consulted to verify responses. Although this improved data completeness, recall bias and misclassification cannot be excluded. Second, mycological investigations were performed only for pupils with clinically suspected tinea capitis. Consequently, asymptomatic carriers, particularly of anthropophilic species such as T. tonsurans, may have been missed, leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Third, fungal identification relied on conventional microscopy and culture. Although PCR would have improved species identification, they were not performed due to resource constraints. Fourth, as the study was conducted in selected rural communities, the findings may not be generalizable to the entire Brong Ahafo Region or other settings in Ghana. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal and temporal relationships between the identified risk factors and tinea capitis. In particular, sleeping arrangements were recorded at the time of the survey and may have changed following the onset of infection. Consequently, the observed association between sleeping alone and tinea capitis may reflect reverse causality rather than a true causal relationship.

Conclusion

Tinea capitis is moderately prevalent among primary school children in rural communities of the Brong Ahafo Region of Ghana. Anthropophilic dermatophytes, particularly T. tonsurans, predominate, suggesting that human-to-human transmission is the principal driver of infection. Geographic location, scalp complaints, and family history of infection were significant predictors. These findings highlight the need for school-based screening, household-level interventions, improved access to systemic antifungal therapy, and strengthened regional surveillance systems. Targeted and context-specific public health strategies are essential to reduce the burden and prevent the long-term clinical and psychosocial consequences of tinea capitis among Ghanaian school children.
