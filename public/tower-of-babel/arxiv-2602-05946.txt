f f -GRPO and Beyond: Divergence-Based Reinforcement Learning Algorithms for General LLM Alignment
Rajdeep Haldar, Lantao Mei, Guang Lin, Yue Xing, Qifan Song

Abstract
Recent research shows that Preference Alignment (PA) objectives act as divergence estimators between aligned (chosen) and unaligned (rejected) response distributions. In this work, we extend this divergence-based perspective to general alignment settings, such as reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where only environmental rewards are available. Within this unified framework, we propose f f -Group Relative Policy Optimization ( f f -GRPO), a class of on-policy reinforcement learning, and f f -Hybrid Alignment Loss ( f f -HAL), a hybrid on/off policy objectives, for general LLM alignment based on variational representation of f f -divergences. We provide theoretical guarantees that these classes of objectives improve the average reward after alignment. Empirically, we validate our framework on both RLVR (Math Reasoning) and PA tasks (Safety Alignment), demonstrating superior performance and flexibility compared to current methods.

2.1
Notation

Our notation follows
(
11
)
. Let
x
denote the prompt and
y
represent a response. We use
y_{w}
and
y_{l}
to indicate aligned and unaligned responses, with flexible contexts depending on the regime (high reward, preferred) or (low reward, unpreferred). The triplet
(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim{\cal D}
characterized the joint distribution of all possible prompt, aligned and unaligned response combinations. Marginalizing this joint gives us
(x,y_{w})\sim{\cal D}^{+}
(aligned distribution),
(x,y_{l})\sim{\cal D}^{-}
(unaligned distribution) and
x\sim{\cal P}
(prompt distribution). Moreover, for any distribution
{\cal G}
, let
{\cal G}_{x}
and
p_{{\cal G}}(y|x)
denote its distribution and density/mass w.r.t.
x
. Let
\pi_{\theta}
be the model/policy under optimization, and
\pi_{\rm ref}
be the reference policy before alignment, with
\theta
as the policy model and
\theta^{*}
as the parameter at convergence. Denote
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
as an older policy iteration strictly used for sampling, which does not go under optimization. The function
r(x,y)
is the explicit environmental reward, while
r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\ln\nicefrac{{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}}{{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}}
is the implicit policy reward with
\beta
as the KL regularization coefficient. Finally,
\sigma(\cdot),\vec{\sigma}(\cdot)
correspond to the sigmoid and SoftMax functions.

Given the above notations, the core objective of alignment is to optimize the policy
\pi_{\theta}
for maximum reward
r(x,y)
while still being not too far from
\pi_{\rm ref}
for a KL regularization penalty, i.e., solve the following:

\sup_{\theta}\E_{x}\left[\E\limits_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}r(x,y)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\;\|\;\pi_{\rm ref})\right],

(1)

and
(
24
)
show that the optimal policy for Eq. (
1
) is directly proportional to the reward function it is trained on:

\pi_{\theta^{*}}(y|x)\propto\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp{\left(r(x,y)\cdot\beta^{-1}\right)}.

(2)

2.2
Verifiable Reward Regime (RLVR)

This is the scenario when we use on-policy RL algorithms to optimize Eq. (
1
) using a non-parametric reward function
r(x,y)
that can be verified by the environment without direct access to supervised samples from
{\cal D}^{+}
(aligned) or
{\cal D}^{-}
(unaligned) distributions. We call this case
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards
(RLVR).

2.2.1
GRPO (On-Policy RL Aligorithm)

Popular On-policy algorithms like PPO and GRPO attain reward maximization of the policy
\sup_{\theta}\E_{x}\E\limits_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}r(x,y)
by solving an alternative objective iteratively:

\sup_{\theta}\E_{x}\E\limits_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y|x)}\cdot A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y).

(3)

Here,
A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y)
is an advantage estimate measuring improvement of
\pi_{\theta}
over
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
, it implictly depends on the reward
r(x,y)
.
In our work, we focus only on GRPO in this RLVR regime as it is currently the most dominant and successful on-policy method. GRPO uses
G
samples
\{y_{i}\}^{G}_{i=1}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
from the old policy to estimate the advantage by standardized scores of the sample rewards as

A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{i})=\frac{r(x,y_{i})-\hat{\mu}(r(x,\cdot))}{\hat{\sigma}(r(x,\cdot))},

(4)

where
\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma}
represent the sample mean and standard deviation.
Then the
\epsilon
-clipped GRPO objective to optimize Eq.
1
is as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}\E_{x}[\sum^{G}\limits_{y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}},i=1}\frac{1}{G}\min\left(R^{i}_{\theta}a_{i},\clip(R^{i}_{\theta},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)a_{i}\right)

\displaystyle-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\;\|\;\pi_{\rm ref})]

(5)

where
R^{i}_{\theta}=\nicefrac{{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x)}}{{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i}|x)}}
,
a_{i}=A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{i})
and
\epsilon
clipping is done to ensure stability at each policy iteration.

2.3
Preference Alignment Regime

In contrast to RLVR
§
2.2
, in the PA regime, we have direct access to preference data
(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim{\cal D}
or binary feedback data
(x,y_{w})\sim{\cal D}^{+},(x,y_{l})\sim{\cal D}^{-}
, supervised samples from the true aligned and unaligned distribution. However, since access to the non-parametric scalar reward
r(x,y)
is unavailable, we use supervised samples to conduct
reinforcement learning with human feedback
(RLHF), implicitly enforcing
r(x,y_{w})>r(x,y_{l})
.
Traditional RLHF is broken down into two steps:

(i) Reward Modelling
The reward is surrogated by a model
r_{\phi}(x,y)
parametrized by
\phi
. Trained on the preference data
(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim{\cal D}
to for a maximum likelihood estimate for the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
4
)
:

p(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=\frac{\exp r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})}{\exp r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})+\exp r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})}.

(6)

(ii) Reward Maximization
We solve the RL alignment objective Eq. (
1
) using
r_{\phi}
as the surrogate reward learned from preference, optimized by an on-policy algorithm like PPO, GRPO, etc.

2.3.1
Direct Aligners (off-policy) algorithms

Using Eq. (
2
), we can parametrize the reward in terms of the policy,
r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\ln\nicefrac{{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}}{{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}}
and substitute in Eq. (
6
) to get a direct alignment loss using only a supervision on the preference data, and obtain the DPO loss
(
24
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbbm{E}_{x,y_{w},y_{l}\sim{\cal D}}\log\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\big).

Other variants include BCO
(
14
)
, KTO
(
9
)
, etc., and, in general, these methods leverage the implicit reward
r_{\theta}
to maximize the likelihood over
{\cal D}^{+}
and reduce the likelihood over
{\cal D}^{-}
.

Divergence Framework

11
unifies preference alignment objectives under a single divergence-based framework by showing that popular alignment methods optimize variational representation of a divergence

\mathbb{D}\!({\cal D}^{+}\;\|\;{\cal D}^{-})

between an aligned distribution
{\cal D}^{+}
and an unaligned distribution
{\cal D}^{-}
.
Different alignment algorithms arise from different choices of the underlying divergence. For example, RLHF/DPO, BCO, and KTO correspond respectively to estimating the non-parametric DPO divergence, the Jensen–Shannon divergence, and the total variation distance.

More generally, for an arbitrary
f
-divergence (see Appendix, Def.
A
), they derive a direct, off-policy alignment objective termed the
f
-divergence Optimizer
(FDO) using the variational representation of
f
-divergences (Appendix Eq. (
22
)) with functional
T=g(r_{\theta})
:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\text{FDO}(f,g)}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\E_{{\cal D}^{+}}\!\left[g(r_{\theta})\right]+\E_{{\cal D}^{-}}\!\left[f^{*}\!\circ g(r_{\theta})\right],

(7)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\text{FDO}(f,g)}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=-\,\mathbb{D}_{f}({\cal D}^{+}\;\|\;{\cal D}^{-}).

(8)

In the above,
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to\effdom(f^{*})
is a monotonically increasing link function mapping the implicit reward
r_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
to the effective domain of the conjugate of
f
. At convergence, the optimal objective value recovers the negative
f
-divergence between
{\cal D}^{+}
and
{\cal D}^{-}
.
Besides convergence, their framework provides theoretical guarantees, such as
alignment consistency
, which ensures that optimization increases the policy’s probability mass on aligned samples while decreasing it on unaligned ones (Defn. 4.2,
11
).

3.1
Divergence Estimation via Importance Sampling

To extend the divergence framework to the RLVR regime, we need a divergence-based on-policy RL term that learns directly from verifiable rewards
r(x,y)
when we have no samples from the aligned distribution
{\cal D}^{+}
or the unaligned distribution
{\cal D}^{-}
.
To achieve this, we use FDO loss Eq. (
7
) as the starting point to derive an on-policy version of it. Let
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
be the action/response generator of an old iteration of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
. Then we can use importance sampling to rewrite the aligned
\E_{{\cal D}^{+}}(\cdot)
and unaligned
\E_{{\cal D}^{-}}(\cdot)
expectations in terms of
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
as the surrogate distribution as follows:

\displaystyle\E_{x}\E\limits_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\left(-w^{+}_{x,y}\,g(r_{\theta})+w^{-}_{x,y}\,f^{*}\!\circ g(r_{\theta})\right),

(9)

where
w^{\pm}_{x,y}=\nicefrac{{p^{\pm}(y|x)}}{{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y|x)}}
are the importance weights and
p^{\pm}
denotes the conditional aligned/unaligned density.

Unfortunately,
{\cal D}^{\pm}
are inaccessible under the RLVR setting; thus, we
artificially
define them using the observed reward
r(x,y)
and then design the loss based on them.

Assumption 3.1

(Reward–density correspondence)
.

For a fixed
x
, the verifiable reward induces aligned and unaligned densities as

p^{\pm}(y|x)\;\propto\;\exp(\pm r(x,y)).

These artificial distributions are practical in the sense that we expect our environmental rewards to reflect the likelihood of alignment. A higher-reward
y
is assigned more density in the aligned distribution, and vice versa. Note that this assumption is used solely to motivate our framework.

Given Assumption
3.1
, instead of directly using
w^{\pm}_{x,y}\propto\exp(\pm r)/\pi_{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}
, we propose to use
truncated importance weights

\tilde{w}^{\pm}_{x,y}
to ignore lower-weight terms, reducing variance in exchange for some bias:

\tilde{w}^{\pm}_{x,y}=\frac{p^{\pm}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y|x)}\cdot I^{\pm}(r(x,y)),

(10)

where
I^{\pm}(r(x,y))=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\pm[r(x,y)-\E_{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}r(x,y)]>0\right\}}
. It enforces choosing only higher/lower than average reward terms for sampling from aligned/unaligned distributions.

3.2
Estimating the Importance Sampling Term

The truncated weights
\tilde{w}^{\pm}_{x,y}
depend on densities
p^{\pm}(\cdot|x)
, whose values depend on the observed rewards. We will follow the GRPO sampling strategy
§
2.2.1
to get an empirical estimate of the truncated weights.

For a fixed prompt
x
, we sample G responses
\{y_{i}\}^{G}_{i=1}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
, fetch the rewards
\{r(x,y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G}
and compute the group advantages
\{A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G}
(Eq.
4
). For notational convenience we will use
r_{i}:=r(x,y_{i}),a_{i}:=A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{i})
interchangeably.

By Assumption
3.1
,
the aligned/unaligned densities satisfy

p^{\pm}(y|x)=\frac{e^{\pm r(x,y)}}{\E\limits_{y\sim\text{Uniform}(\cdot|x)}e^{\pm r(x,y)}}.

To approximate the denominator, we apply a sampled softmax
(Log-
Q
) correction
(
3
)
, yielding the plug-in (unnormalized) estimator

\hat{p}^{\pm}(y_{i}|x)=\frac{\exp(\pm r_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{G}\exp\!\big(\pm r_{j}-\ln\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{j}|x)\big)}.

(11)

The resulting truncated importance weight estimates are then given by:

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{i}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{p}^{\pm}(y_{i}|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i}|x)}\;\hat{I}^{\pm}_{i}

\displaystyle=\vec{\sigma}_{i}\!\big(\{\pm r_{j}-\ln\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{j}|x)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)\cdot\hat{I}^{\pm}_{i}

(12)

where
\hat{I}^{\pm}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\pm a_{i}>0\right\}}
a surrogate for
I^{\pm}(r(x,y))
.

To explain the operations, the importance weights
\hat{w}_{i}^{\pm}
are softmax-normalized scores that increase with the external reward while being regularized by the likelihood under the sampling policy
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
. Consequently, responses that are both highly rewarding (or highly unrewarding) and surprising under
\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
receive larger weight under the aligned (or unaligned) density. The restriction to positive- or negative-advantage samples implements truncated importance sampling, focusing the updates on the most informative, high-weight terms and yielding stable training.

3.3
f
-GRPO &
f
-HAL losses

Given an
f
-divergence and a monotonically increasing link function
g:{\mathbb{R}}\to\effdom(f^{*})
, we can estimate Eq. (
9
) using the weights Eq. (
12
) and simplifying to get the following:

\E_{x}\sum^{G}\limits_{y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}},i=1}-\frac{1}{G}\psi(r_{\theta,i},a_{i})\sign(a_{i}),

(13)

where
r_{\theta,i}=\beta\ln\nicefrac{{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x)}}{{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_{i}|x)}}
and
\psi(r_{\theta,i},a_{i}):=

\begin{cases}\vec{\sigma}_{i}\!\big(\{r_{j}-\ln\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{j}|x)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)\cdot g(r_{\theta,i}),&a_{i}>0\\
\vec{\sigma}_{i}\!\big(\{-r_{j}-\ln\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{j}|x)\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)\cdot f^{*}\!\circ g(r_{\theta,i}),&a_{i}\leq 0\end{cases}

(14)

Finally, we replace the sign term
\sign(a_{i})
in Eq. (
13
) with the scaled advantage
a_{i}(1+\beta^{-1})
, thereby accelerating updates using advantage magnitude and normalizing gradients to the same order as standard on-policy RL methods (Appendix
§
B.1
). We define the
f
-GRPO loss as follows:

{\cal L}^{(f,g)}_{\text{f-GRPO}}(\theta)=\E_{x}\sum^{G}\limits_{y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}},i=1}-a_{i}\frac{1+\beta^{-1}}{G}\psi(r_{\theta,i},a_{i}).

(15)

We combine
f
-GRPO and FDO to obtain a hybrid on-off policy-loss function that leverages rewards and supervised samples. The
f
divergence hybrid alignment loss (f-HAL) can be expressed as:

{\cal L}^{(f,g)}_{\text{f-HAL}}(\theta)=\lambda\underbrace{{\cal L}^{(f,g)}_{\text{FDO}}(\theta)}_{\textit{Direct Aligner}}+(1-\lambda)\underbrace{{\cal L}^{(f,g)}_{\text{f-GRPO}}(\theta)}_{\textit{On-policy RL}}.

(16)

Definition 4.1

(Reward-Aligned Distributions)
.

Given a reward
r(x,y)
and policy
\pi_{\theta}

{\cal D}_{(r,\theta)}^{+}

\displaystyle d{\cal D}_{(r,\theta)}^{\pm}\propto[\pm(r(x,y)-\mu^{\theta}_{r}(x))]_{+}\cdot e^{\pm r(x,y)}d{\cal P}(x)

The above definition defines the notions of aligned and unaligned distributions in terms of the average reward under a policy. The responses above (below) average reward are assigned a higher non-zero probability mass in the aligned (unaligned) distribution.

While Assumption

4.1
f
-GRPO vs GRPO

Similar to
Theorem

4.3
, the below characterizes the FP iterate for the unclipped GRPO objective.

Theorem 4.4
.

For the unclipped GRPO loss, as
G\to\infty
, almost surely:
\pi_{\theta^{(t+1)}_{\rm GRPO}}(y|x)\propto\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp\left(\beta^{-1}a_{t}(x,y)\right)
, where
a_{t}(x,y)=(r(x,y)-\mu_{r}^{\theta^{(t)}_{\rm GRPO}})/\sigma_{r}^{\theta^{(t)}_{\rm GRPO}}
. Furthermore,
\mu^{\theta^{(t)}_{\rm GRPO}}_{r}>\mu_{r}^{\theta_{\rm ref}}
for all
t>0
.

The policy post-alignment is reweighted from the reference distribution by an exponential of the standardized reward, in the same vein as
30
’s stationary policy result. Hence, GRPO increases the probability of above-average responses and decreases that of below-average ones, satisfying alignment consistency and leading to a reward improvement for any
t^{th}
iterate compared to the reference.

In contrast, for
f
-GRPO with the canonical link function (i.e.,
g^{-1}(f^{\prime}_{\infty})=\infty
), probability mass is assigned exclusively to above-average reward samples at each iterate
Equation

21
. GRPO, even after downweighting, still retains a nonzero mass on below-average responses. This results in a stronger result for
f
-GRPO with a canonical link that admits strict reward improvement until convergence to the maximal-reward policy, and suggests an empirical advantage over GRPO, which we validate in
§
5
.

Hybrid Alignment Mitigates Reward Hacking.

Tables

and
5
show that on-policy methods perform relatively poorly (red cells), with GRPO performing worst overall. This is consistent with

: when optimization relies on a learned reward model rather than a verifiable environment reward, on-policy updates can exploit vulnerabilities in the reward signal and converge to superficially high-reward yet suboptimal responses
(
26
)
.

Hybrid alignment mitigates reward hacking by combining on-policy exploration with off-policy direct supervision. It benefits from the exploration inherent to on-policy methods while preventing lazy reward-hacking suboptimalities by using preference data as guidance. For the larger Qwen-7B model, hybrid
f
-HAL generally outperforms pure direct aligners, indicating that the added exploration improves performance when sufficient model capacity is available. This behavior is theoretically grounded in
Equations

19
and
21
, where the mixture formulation of
f
-HAL assigns non-negligible probability mass to ideal responses, constraining degenerate solutions.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we present a unified divergence-based perspective for general LLM alignment, extending prior direct alignment frameworks to the RLVR regime. We introduce a principled recipe for constructing on-policy and hybrid alignment objectives, yielding
f
-GRPO and
f
-HAL as instances derived from general
f
-divergences, and provide theoretical guarantees characterizing their optimal policies and reward-improvement properties.
Empirically, we demonstrate the effectiveness and flexibility of this framework across both RLVR and preference alignment regimes.
Our work proposes divergence estimation as a unifying and practical foundation for general LLM alignment.

Lemma B.1

(Comonotone Covariance Inequality)
.

Let
X:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a random variable.
If
f,g:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are integrable and both non- decreasing (or both non-increasing), then

\E[f(X)g(X)]\ \geq\ \E[f(X)]\,\E[g(X)],

equivalently
\Cov(f(X),g(X))\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
X^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
X
. Then

2\,\Cov(f(X),g(X))=\E\!\Big[(f(X)-f(X^{\prime}))(g(X)-g(X^{\prime}))\Big].

(Indeed, expanding the RHS and using independence gives

\E[f(X)g(X)]+\E[f(X^{\prime})g(X^{\prime})]-\E[f(X)g(X^{\prime})]-\E[f(X^{\prime})g(X)]=2\E[f(X)g(X)]-2\E[f(X)]\E[g(X)]
.)

If
f
and
g
are both non-decreasing, then for every
(x,x^{\prime})
,

(x-x^{\prime})\geq 0\ \Rightarrow\ f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\geq 0\ \text{ and }\ g(x)-g(x^{\prime})\geq 0,

and similarly if
(x-x^{\prime})\leq 0
then both differences are
\leq 0
.
Hence
(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))(g(x)-g(x^{\prime}))\geq 0
pointwise, so the expectation is nonnegative:

\E\!\Big[(f(X)-f(X^{\prime}))(g(X)-g(X^{\prime}))\Big]\ \geq\ 0.

Therefore
\Cov(f(X),g(X))\geq 0
, which is equivalent to

\E[f(X)g(X)]\geq\E[f(X)]\E[g(X)]
.
The non-increasing case follows identically.
∎

Lemma B.2

(Monotone Reweighting Increases the Mean)
.

Fix
x
and let
q(\cdot\mid x)
be a distribution over
y
.
For any nonnegative weight function
w_{x}(y)
with
\E_{q}[w_{x}]>0
, define the tilted distribution

q_{w}(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{q(y\mid x)\,w_{x}(y)}{\E_{y\sim q}[w_{x}(y)]}.

Then for any measurable reward
r(x,y)
,

\E_{q_{w}}[r(x,y)]-\E_{q}[r(x,y)]=\frac{\Cov_{q(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big(r(x,y),\,w_{x}(y)\big)}{\E_{q}[w_{x}(y)]}.

In particular, if
w_{x}(y)
is (weakly) increasing in
r(x,y)
, then

\E_{q_{w}}[r(x,y)]\;\geq\;\E_{q}[r(x,y)],

with strict inequality unless
r(x,\cdot)
is
q
-a.s. constant or
w_{x}
is
q
-a.s. constant.

Proof.

By definition,

\E_{q_{w}}[r]=\int_{y}r(y)\,\frac{q(y)w(y)}{\E_{q}[w]}\cdot dy=\frac{\E_{q}[rw]}{\E_{q}[w]}.

Hence

\E_{q_{w}}[r]-\E_{q}[r]=\frac{\E_{q}[rw]-\E_{q}[r]\E_{q}[w]}{\E_{q}[w]}=\frac{\Cov_{q}(r,w)}{\E_{q}[w]}.

If
w
is non decreasing in
r
, then
r
and
w(r)
are comonotone, so

\Cov_{q}(r,w)\geq 0
(Lemma
B.1
), yielding

\E_{q_{w}}[r]\geq\E_{q}[r]
, with strict inequality unless
r
or
w
is
q
-a.s. constant.
∎

B.1
From Sign-Based Updates to Advantage-Weighted
f
-GRPO

In the main text, the preliminary form of the
f
-GRPO objective in Eq. (
13
) involves a sign-based weighting
\sign(a_{i})
, where
a_{i}
denotes a standardized reward-based advantage. This form arises naturally from the variational decomposition in Eq. (
9
), as the sign determines whether a sample contributes to the aligned or unaligned component of the divergence estimator. While this formulation is sufficient for identifying the correct update direction, it discards magnitude information contained in the advantage.

Connection to Standard Policy Gradient Methods.

Popular on-policy reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO and GRPO maximize expected reward by optimizing objectives of the form

\sup_{\theta}\;\E_{x}\E_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\Big[\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y|x)}\,A^{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(x,y)\Big],

(28)

where the advantage
A^{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(x,y)
scales the policy gradient and determines both its direction and magnitude. In particular, GRPO uses a batch-standardized advantage

A^{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(x,y_{i})=\frac{r(x,y_{i})-\hat{\mu}(r(x,\cdot))}{\hat{\sigma}(r(x,\cdot))},

(29)

so that gradient magnitudes are proportional to relative reward improvements.

Motivation for Advantage Weighting.

Using only
\sign(a_{i})
treats all above-average samples equally and all below-average samples equally, regardless of how strongly they deviate from the mean reward. Replacing
\sign(a_{i})
with
a_{i}
restores the standard policy-gradient behavior in which higher-advantage samples exert proportionally larger influence on the update. This modification preserves the alignment direction implied by the divergence estimator while improving optimization efficiency and variance properties.

Normalization and Scaling.

In
f
-GRPO, the policy score is parameterized as

r_{\theta,i}=\beta\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_{i}|x)},

so the gradient of the loss contains a factor of
\beta^{-1}
relative to the standard log-policy gradient. To ensure that gradient magnitudes remain comparable to those of conventional on-policy methods, we scale the advantage by
(1+\beta^{-1})
, yielding the final weighting
a_{i}(1+\beta^{-1})
. This normalization aligns the overall gradient scale with that of GRPO and PPO, facilitating stable optimization and fair empirical comparison.

Resulting Objective.

With this replacement, the resulting
f
-GRPO loss in Eq. (
15
) can be viewed as a divergence-based generalization of GRPO, where the policy gradient is weighted by a standardized advantage while the update direction is shaped by the chosen
f
-divergence through the function
\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
. This modification preserves the theoretical interpretation of
f
-GRPO as a divergence estimator while recovering the practical benefits of advantage-weighted on-policy optimization.
