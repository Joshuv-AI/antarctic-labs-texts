1.
Introduction

The topic of minimal surfaces in the complex hyperbolic plane

\mathbb{CH}^{2}\,=\,

\,\mathrm{SU}(2,1)/\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1))

lie in the intersection of Kähler geometry, representation theory, and harmonic map theory.
For a compact connected Riemann surface
\Sigma
, the nonabelian Hodge correspondence

[
6
,
4
,
3
,
14
]

associates to each reductive representation

\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma)\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)

a
\rho
–equivariant harmonic map
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}

and together they produce a polystable
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
Higgs bundle.
In this setting, conformal harmonic maps are precisely the minimal immersions; equivalently,
the Hopf differential is holomorphic and the harmonic map equations take a complex geometric form.
Thus, the geometry of minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
can be studied via Higgs bundle data.

A detailed algebro–geometric description in the compact case was developed by Loftin and McIntosh
in
[
7
]
.
In the mixed case (i.e.,
f
is neither holomorphic nor anti–holomorphic), the harmonic metric
splits the underlying holomorphic vector bundle
V
of rank
3
as

E\ \cong\ V\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma},

and the Higgs field is off–diagonal with respect to this decomposition

\Phi\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}0&\Phi_{1}\\
\Phi_{2}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{2})\ =\ 0,

with
\Phi_{1}\,\in\,H^{0}(\Sigma,\,V\otimes K_{\Sigma})
and
\Phi_{2}\,\in\,H^{0}(\Sigma,\,V^{*}\otimes K_{\Sigma})
.
The rank two vector bundle
V
fits into a short exact sequence

(1.1)

0\ \longrightarrow\ K^{-1}_{\Sigma}(D_{1})\ \xrightarrow{\,\ \Phi_{1}\ \,}\ V\ \xrightarrow{\,\ \Phi_{2}\ \,}\ K_{\Sigma}(-D_{2})\ \longrightarrow\ 0,

where
D_{i}
is the zero locus of
\Phi_{i}
for
i=1,2
, and
D_{1},\,D_{2}
are respectively the effective divisors of anti–complex and complex points of
f
.
The stability condition gives sharp slope inequalities
governing the existence of a minimal immersion and its associated representation.

The aim in this paper is to develop an analogous picture for punctured surfaces.
Let
\Sigma
be a compact Riemann surface and let
P\,\subset\,\Sigma
be a nonempty
finite subset of marked points such that
\chi(\Sigma_{0})\,<\,0
.
We consider reductive representations
\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
together with a

\rho
–equivariant conformal minimal immersion
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}

of finite energy (defined with respect to a fixed complete finite–area hyperbolic metric on
\Sigma_{0}
; see
Section
3
).
On a noncompact surface, the finite–energy requirement is a genuine restriction and interacts subtly with
the peripheral holonomy around punctures. In particular, Sagman’s criterion

[
11
]
implies that the existence of any finite–energy
\rho
–equivariant map forces every
peripheral element to be non-hyperbolic (see Corollary
4.2
). We are also able to rule out elliptic peripheral holonomy in this case.

In Corollary
4.9
, we show, under the assumption of parabolic holonomy
along peripheral loops, we show that the properness of the map
f
is equivalent to the completeness of the
induced metric
g_{f}
.

Properness and parabolic peripheral holonomy.
Our first main result is a precise characterization of when finite–energy minimal immersions have proper
ends: In Theorem
4.8
, we prove that if
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
is a finite–energy, conformal,
\rho
–equivariant minimal immersion, then

f
is proper around every puncture if and only if the conjugacy class
\rho(c)
is parabolic
for every peripheral loop
c
.

Parabolic Higgs bundles and an explicit parametrization.
When the peripheral holonomy is parabolic, the appropriate algebro–geometric objects are
tame parabolic Higgs bundles on
(\Sigma,\,P)
, with logarithmic singularities and strongly
parabolic residues. The tame nonabelian Hodge correspondence on punctured curves

[
13
,
2
,
9
,
1
]

relates such parabolic Higgs bundles to reductive representations with prescribed
parabolic conjugacy classes (see also
[
5
]
for background).
Starting from a finite–energy conformal minimal immersion with complete ends, we obtain a canonical associated
tame parabolic
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
–Higgs bundle
(E_{*},\,\Phi)

(Corollary

), whose parabolic weights encode the semisimple parts of the
peripheral conjugacy classes (Section

).

In the mixed case,
(E_{*},\,\Phi)
admits a holomorphic splitting and an off–diagonal Higgs field mirroring
the compact Loftin–McIntosh structure, now with logarithmic canonical bundle and divisors supported in
\Sigma_{0}
.
Our main algebro–geometric criterion (proved in Section
7
) is the following:
Theorem
7.1
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a reductive

with parabolic peripheral holonomy to admit a

\rho
–equivariant conformal minimal immersion

of finite energy with complete ends
in the mixed case.
Crucially, this condition is explicit: it is expressed in terms of the existence of tame parabolic Higgs data
together with concrete parabolic slope inequalities (see (
7.9
)–(
7.10
)).

Complete ends and examples.

A distinctive feature of the punctured setting is that the ends carry additional algebraic structure.
For complete ends, the Higgs field has nilpotent residues at
P
, and in the case of rank
3
there are only two possible
nilpotent Jordan types. In Section

of the associated residues and weights, yielding a concrete description of the asymptotic behavior at punctures.
At last, we construct explicit families of proper
\rho-
equivariant complete minimal immersions of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus P
with

n

\mathbb{CH}^{2}

n

Theorem

produces complete, finite–energy conformal harmonic maps with cuspidal ends
(unipotent monodromy) from explicit logarithmic Higgs data (

2.
Complex hyperbolic Space

2.1.
The projective model and Bergman metric

Let
{\mathbb{C}}^{2,1}
denote
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
equipped with the Hermitian form of signature
(2,\,1)

(2.1)

\big\langle(Z_{1},\,Z_{2},\,Z_{3})^{t},\,(W_{1},\,W_{2},\,W_{3})^{t}\big\rangle_{2,1}\,\;=\,\;Z_{1}\overline{W}_{1}+Z_{2}\overline{W}_{2}-Z_{3}\overline{W}_{3}.

Write
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})
for the complex projective space of lines in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2,1}
;
the point of
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})

for any nonzero
Z\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
will be denoted by
[Z]
.

The complex hyperbolic plane is the locus of negative lines for
\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\rangle_{2,1}
:

(2.2)

\mathbb{CH}^{2}\;:=\;\bigl\{[Z]\,\in\,\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})\;\big|\;\,\,\langle Z,\,Z\rangle_{2,1}\,<\,0\bigr\}.

This domain carries a natural
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
–invariant Kähler metric
h
, called the
Bergman metric. With this metric,
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
is identified with the rank-one Hermitian
symmetric space

\mathbb{CH}^{2}\,\;\cong\,\;\mathrm{SU}(2,1)/\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1)).

We normalize
h
so that its holomorphic sectional curvature is the constant
-4
.
In particular,
(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,h)
is complete and Kähler–Einstein.

Remark 2.1
.

With the above normalization (holomorphic sectional curvature
-4
), the sectional curvatures of the associated
Riemannian metric
g\,=\,\mathrm{Re}(h)
lie in the interval
[-4,\,-1]
.
In particular,
(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,g)
is a complete simply connected manifold of pinched negative
curvature. Note that this implies that
(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,g)
is
a
\mathrm{CAT}(-1)
complete space.

The Hermitian metric
h
decomposes as

h\,\;=\,\;g-\sqrt{-1}\,\omega,

where
g\,=\,\mathrm{Re}(h)
as before is the associated Riemannian metric and
\omega

is the associated Kähler form. Equivalently,

\omega(X,\,Y)\ =\ g(JX,\,Y),

where
J
is the (integrable) almost complex structure on
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
.

2.2.
Totally geodesic submanifolds and rank

Let
X\,=\,G/K
be a Riemannian symmetric space of noncompact type. A flat
in
X
is a complete, totally geodesic, isometrically embedded Euclidean submanifold

{\mathbb{R}}^{r}\subset X
. The rank
\mathrm{rk}(X)
is the maximum among the dimensions of flats in
X
.

For
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, the only flats are the geodesic lines; in other words,
\mathrm{rk}(\mathbb{CH}^{2})\,=\,1
.
More generally, the proper totally geodesic submanifolds of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
are precisely the following ones:

•

geodesics (real hyperbolic lines),

•

complex geodesics
\mathbb{CH}^{1}\subset\mathbb{CH}^{2}
(complex lines), and

•

totally real, Lagrangian planes
{{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}^{2}}\subset\mathbb{CH}^{2}
.

Among these, only the geodesics are flat.

2.3.
Isometries of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}

Define

\mathrm{U}(2,1)\ =\ \Bigl\{A\,\in\,\mathrm{GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})\ \Big|\ \,\langle A(Z),\,A(W)\rangle_{2,1}\,=\,\langle Z,\,W\rangle_{2,1}\ \ \forall\,\,Z,\,W\,\in\,{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\Bigr\},

and set
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)\,=\,\mathrm{U}(2,1)\cap\mathrm{SL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
. The projective unitary group is

\mathrm{PU}(2,1)\;=\;\mathrm{U}(2,1)/(\text{center})\;=\;\mathrm{SU}(2,1)/({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}).

The standard linear action of
\mathrm{U}(2,1)
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2,1}
descends to an action of

\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
on
\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})
; this action of
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
on
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})
preserves the domain
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
in (
2.2
). The resulting action of

\mathbb{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2,1})
on
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
is transitive and consists of holomorphic isometries of

(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,h)
. Conversely, every holomorphic isometry of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
is given by
the action of some element of
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
.
The full isometry group (allowing anti-holomorphic isometries) is generated by
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)

together with the complex conjugation (it is induced by the automorphism
A\,\longmapsto\,\overline{A}
of
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)
).

2.3.1.
Classification of elements of
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)

Take any
A\,\in\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
and choose a lift
\widetilde{A}\,\in\,\mathrm{U}(2,1)
.
The classification of the fixed-point locus
for the action of
A
on
\mathbb{CH}^{2}\cup\partial\mathbb{CH}^{2}
leads to
a trichotomy (analogous to the real hyperbolic geometry):

•

Elliptic:

A
fixes at least one point of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
.

•

Parabolic:

A
fixes exactly one point of the ideal boundary

\partial\mathbb{CH}^{2}
and fixes no point in
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
.

•

Loxodromic
(often called hyperbolic in the literature):

A
fixes exactly two points of the boundary
\partial\mathbb{CH}^{2}
and acts by translation
along the unique complex geodesic joining them (possibly with a rotational component).

See
[
10
]
for the details.

2.4.
Curvature and CAT
(0)
features

As mentioned in Remark
2.1
, the real sectional
curvatures satisfy

-4\;\leq\;K_{\mathrm{sec}}\;\leq\;-1.

In particular,
(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,g)
is complete, simply connected having negative sectional
curvature. Hence
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
is a Hadamard manifold; after rescaling, it is a CAT
(0)
space,
and in fact a CAT
(-1)
space (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
).

We will use the following facts:

(1)

For any fixed point
o\,\in\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, the function

p\,\longmapsto\,d(o,\,p)
is convex along geodesics.

(2)

Let
u
be a harmonic map from a Riemannian
manifold
Y
to
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
. Then the function
y\,\longmapsto\,d(o,\,u(y))
on
Y
is weakly
subharmonic.

3.
Proper minimal immersion with finite energy

Notation 3.1
.

Throughout,
\Sigma
is a compact connected Riemann surface of genus
g
.
Fix
n
distinct points

P\ :=\ \{z_{1},\,\cdots,\,z_{n}\}\ \subset\ \Sigma

of it such that
n\,\geq\,1

and

(3.1)

2g-2+n\,\;>\,\;0.

The condition
2g-2+n\,>\,0
implies that
\Sigma_{0}
is a hyperbolic Riemann surface; in particular,
it admits a complete finite–area hyperbolic metric. We fix such a metric on
\Sigma_{0}
and denote it by

g_{\Sigma}
; the area form on
\Sigma_{0}
for
g_{\Sigma}
is denoted by
dA_{g_{\Sigma}}
.

Fix a base point

x_{0}\ \in\ \Sigma_{0}.

Identify the universal cover of
\Sigma_{0}
(corresponding to the base point

x_{0}
) with the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}\,\subset\,{\mathbb{C}}
; the condition
in (
3.1
) rules out
\mathbb{C}
and
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Let

(3.2)

\varpi\ :\ \mathbb{H}\ \longrightarrow\ \Sigma_{0}

be the covering map. Its deck transformation group is the Galois group

\mathrm{Gal}(\varpi)\,=\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0}),

which will be identified with a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
via the action of

\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{H}
(see (
3.2
)).

We will consider pairs of the form
(\rho,\,f)
, where

(1)

(3.3)

\rho\ :\ \pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0})\ \longrightarrow\ \mathrm{PU}(2,1)

is a reductive homomorphism, and

(2)

f
is a
\rho
–equivariant conformal minimal immersion of finite energy

(3.4)

f\ :\ \mathbb{H}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb{CH}^{2}.

The
\rho
–equivariance condition means that

(3.5)

f(\gamma\cdot p)\;=\;\rho(\gamma)\cdot f(p)

for all
\gamma\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0})
and
p\,\in\,\mathbb{H}
.

The group
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0})
acts — via the homomorphism
\rho
(see (
3.3
)) — on
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
.
In view of (
3.5
), the map
f
descends to a map on
\Sigma_{0}
with values in the quotient
space
\mathbb{CH}^{2}/\rho(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},x_{0}))
.
Note that the action of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0})
on
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
preserves both
the complex structure and the metric
h
.
Hence the pulled back metric

(3.6)

g_{f}\ :=\ f^{*}h

on the universal cover
\mathbb{H}
of
\Sigma_{0}
is
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0},\,x_{0})
–invariant.
Consequently,
g_{f}
descends to a Riemannian metric on the quotient surface

\Sigma_{0}\,=\,\mathbb{H}/\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
; this descended metric on
\Sigma_{0}
is also
denoted by
g_{f}
.

By construction, the Riemannian metric
g_{f}
on
\Sigma_{0}
lies in the conformal class determined by the
complex structure on
\Sigma_{0}
. We say that
f
is complete on
\Sigma_{0}
if the descended metric
g_{f}
on

\Sigma_{0}
is complete.

Definition 3.2

(Properness around a puncture)
.

As in (
3.2
),
\varpi\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\Sigma_{0}
is the universal cover. Let

f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a
\rho
–equivariant map.
Take
p\,\in\,P
(see Notation
3.1
), and let
U^{*}\,\subset\,\Sigma_{0}
be a punctured disk
neighborhood of

. We say that

is proper around

if for some (hence any) base-point

o\,\in\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
and for every sequence
\{z_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}\,\subset\,\mathbb{H}
satisfying

\varpi(z_{k})\,\to\,p
in
\Sigma
, the following holds:

d_{\mathbb{CH}^{2}}\bigl(f(z_{k}),\,o\bigr)\ \longrightarrow\ +\infty.

Definition 3.3

(Completeness around a puncture)
.

Let
f\,:\,{\mathbb{H}}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a conformal immersion, and let
g_{f}
be the
induced metric on
\Sigma_{0}
(see (
3.6
) and the sentence following it). We say that

is complete around

(or that the end at

)
if the Riemannian surface
(U^{*},\,g_{f}\big|_{U^{*}})
is complete.

Properness around a puncture

is a condition on the map

, whereas completeness around

is a
condition on the induced (pullback) metric

g_{f}\,\;:=\,\;f^{*}h.

In general, completeness of

need not imply that

is proper on

, even when

is a pullback metric. What is always true (and which we shall use) is the following implication:
For pullback metrics, properness forces completeness provided the target is complete.
In Corollary

3.1.
Type decomposition of
df

Fix a local holomorphic coordinate
z
on
\Sigma_{0}
, and set

Z\,=\,\partial/\partial z
and
\overline{Z}\,=\,\partial/\partial\overline{z}
.
The complexified tangent bundle of
\Sigma_{0}
splits as

T_{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma_{0}=T^{1,0}\Sigma_{0}\oplus T^{0,1}\Sigma_{0}
,
and a similar decomposition holds for the complexified tangent bundle of the target. Using these

decomposes into type components:

df\;=\;f_{z}\,dz+f_{\overline{z}}\,d\overline{z}\;=\;(\partial f^{\prime}+\partial f^{\prime\prime})+(\overline{\partial}f^{\prime}+\overline{\partial}f^{\prime\prime}),

where the four components are the bundle maps

\partial f^{\prime}\ :\ T^{1,0}\Sigma_{0}\ \longrightarrow\ f^{\ast}T^{1,0}\mathbb{CH}^{2},\qquad\partial f^{\prime\prime}\ :\ T^{1,0}\Sigma_{0}\ \longrightarrow\ f^{\ast}T^{0,1}\mathbb{CH}^{2},

\overline{\partial}f^{\prime}\ :\ T^{0,1}\Sigma_{0}\ \longrightarrow\ f^{\ast}T^{1,0}\mathbb{CH}^{2},\qquad\overline{\partial}f^{\prime\prime}\ :\ T^{0,1}\Sigma_{0}\ \longrightarrow\ f^{\ast}T^{0,1}\mathbb{CH}^{2}.

Choose a local unitary frame of
f^{\ast}T\mathbb{CH}^{2}
with respect to the Kähler structure
h
.
Define the type–component magnitudes as follows:

u_{1}(z)\ :=\ \|\partial f^{\prime}(Z)\|,\qquad u_{2}(z)\ :=\ \|\partial f^{\prime\prime}(Z)\|.

In the above notation, the induced metric takes the form

(3.7)

g_{f}\ =\ \lambda(z)\,|dz|^{2},\qquad\lambda(z)\ :=\ u_{1}(z)^{2}+u_{2}(z)^{2}.

The total energy of

is

E(f)\ :=\ \int_{\Sigma_{0}}\lambda(z)\,dA_{g_{\Sigma}}.

In the sequel, we focus on
\rho
–equivariant (see (
3.3
))
minimal immersions that are proper and have finite energy.

4.
Isometry and properness

We begin with Sagman’s theorem; note that in the complex hyperbolic literature, “hyperbolic” is often called “loxodromic”.
(Here we follow
[
11
]
and use “hyperbolic” to mean an axial isometry with positive translation length.)

Proposition 4.1

(
[
11
, Proposition 3.8]
)
.

Let
M\,=\,\widetilde{M}/\Gamma
be a complete, finite–volume hyperbolic surface, and let

(X,\,g)
be a
\mathrm{CAT}(-1)
Hadamard manifold. For a representation

\rho_{1}\,:\,\Gamma\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{Iso}(X,g)
, there exists a finite–energy
\rho_{1}
–equivariant map

u\,:\,\widetilde{M}\,\longrightarrow\,X
if and only if, for every peripheral element

\gamma\,\in\,\Gamma
(equivalently, any element conjugate to a cusp subgroup),
the isometry
\rho_{1}(\gamma)
is not hyperbolic.

In our setup, the complement
\Sigma_{0}\,=\,\Sigma\setminus P
carries a fixed complete finite–area
hyperbolic metric, and hence
\Sigma_{0}
is a complete finite–volume hyperbolic surface. Moreover,

(\mathbb{CH}^{2},\,g)
with
g\,=\,\mathrm{Re}(h)
is a complete simply connected manifold of pinched
negative curvature; in particular it is a Hadamard
\mathrm{CAT}(-1)
space and a complete metric space (see
Remark
2.1
). Thus Proposition
4.1
applies with
M\,=\,\Sigma_{0}
and

X\,=\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, and thus yielding the following corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

If there exists a finite–energy
\rho
–equivariant (see (
3.3
)) map
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, then for every cusp loop
c\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
,
the conjugacy class of the element

is non-hyperbolic; equivalently it is either elliptic or parabolic.

We now show that the properness at a cusp excludes the elliptic case.

4.1.
Around a cusp

Fix a cusp loop

\Sigma_{0}

As mentioned above, it will be shown that the properness at a cusp rules out the elliptic case

.

To prove this by contradiction, assume that the conjugacy class of

\rho(c)
fixes some point

.
For a map

(see

If

Moreover, since

Lemma 4.3

For any

Proof.

If there is no such sequence

Y_{0}\,\geq\,Y
, such that
|{\bf g}(y)|\,\geq\,\varepsilon
for all
y\,\geq\,Y_{0}
.
Hence we have

\int_{Y}^{\infty}|g(y)|^{2}\,dy\ \geq\ \int_{Y_{0}}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{2}\,dy\ =\ \infty,

which contradicts the given condition that
g\,\in\,L^{2}([Y,\infty))
. In view of this contradiction,
we conclude that there exists a sequence of real numbers
y_{n}\,\to\,\infty
such that

{\bf g}(y_{n})\,\to\,0
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Take
U
defined in (
4.2
). Then, wherever
U
is differentiable, the inequalities

|U_{x}|\ \leq\ |f_{x}|_{h},\qquad|U_{y}|\ \leq\ |f_{y}|_{h}

hold.

Proof.

We prove the estimate for
U_{x}
; the proof for
U_{y}
is identical. For
h\,\neq\,0
,

\left|\frac{U(x+h,y)-U(x,y)}{k}\right|\ =\ \left|\frac{d_{h}(o,f(x+h,y))-d_{h}(o,f(x,y))}{k}\right|.

Since the distance function is
1
-Lipschitz, we have

|d_{h}(o,P)-d_{h}(o,Q)|\,\leq\,d_{h}(P,Q)
, and therefore

\left|\frac{U(x+h,y)-U(x,y)}{k}\right|\ \leq\ \frac{d_{h}\bigl(f(x+h,y),f(x,y)\bigr)}{|k|}.

Letting
k\,\to\,0
it follows that
|U_{x}|\,\leq\,|f_{x}|_{h}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
U\,\in\,C^{2}(S_{Y})
, where
S_{Y}
is defined in (
4.1
), be subharmonic
and
2\pi
-periodic in
x
. Assume that

(4.4)

\int_{Y}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(|U_{x}|^{2}+|U_{y}|^{2}\bigr)\,dx\,dy\ <\ \infty.

Denote

(4.5)

m(y)\ :=\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}U(x,y)\,dx,\qquad\mathcal{A}(y)\ :=\ \left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}U_{x}(x,y)^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}.

Then the following two hold:

(1)

the function
m
is bounded above on
[Y,\,\infty)
.

(2)

for each
y\,\geq\,Y
,

U(0,y)\ \leq\ \sup_{t\geq Y}m(t)+\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathcal{A}(y).

Proof.

First we shall show that
m
is convex. Since
U
is
2\pi
-periodic in
x
, it follows that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}U_{xx}(x,y)\,dx\,=\,0
. Using subharmonicity
\Delta U\,=\,U_{xx}+U_{yy}\,\geq\,0
, we compute

m^{\prime\prime}(y)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}U_{yy}(x,y)\,dx\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(U_{xx}+U_{yy})\,dx-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}U_{xx}\,dx\ \geq\ 0,

so
m
is convex on
[Y,\,\infty)
.

Next,

m^{\prime}(y)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}U_{y}(x,y)\,dx,

hence, by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|m^{\prime}(y)|^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|U_{y}(x,y)|^{2}\,dx.

Integrating over
y\,\in\,[Y,\,\infty)
and using (
4.4
) it follows
that
m^{\prime}\,\in\,L^{2}([Y,\,\infty))
. If
m^{\prime}(y_{0})\,>\,0
for some
y_{0}\,\geq\,Y
, then convexity
implies that
m^{\prime}(y)\,\geq\,m^{\prime}(y_{0})\,>\,0
for all
y\,\geq\,y_{0}
, which contradicts the above
observation that
m^{\prime}\,\in\,L^{2}([Y,\infty))
. Consequently, we have
m^{\prime}(y)\,\leq\,0
for all

y\,\geq\,Y
, so
m
is non-increasing and hence it is bounded above on
[Y,\,\infty)
. This proves (1).

To prove (2), fix
y\,\geq\,Y
and set
v(x)\,:=\,U(x,y)-m(y)
. Then

\int_{0}^{2\pi}v(x)\,dx\ =\ 0

and
v^{\prime}(x)\,=\,U_{x}(x,\,y)
. Since
v
is continuous with mean zero, there exists
x_{0}\,\in\,[0,\,2\pi]

such that
v(x_{0})\,=\,0
. For any
x
,

|v(x)|\,=\,\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}v^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right|\,\leq\,\int_{0}^{2\pi}|v^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\,\leq\,\sqrt{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|v^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\,=\,\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathcal{A}(y).

Thus we have

U(0,\,y)\ =\ m(y)+v(0)\ \leq\ \sup_{t\geq Y}m(t)+\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathcal{A}(y).

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Properness enforces parabolic peripheral holonomy

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a finite–energy, conformal,

\rho
–equivariant (see (
3.3
)) harmonic immersion, and let
c
be a cusp loop.
If
f
is proper around the corresponding puncture, then the conjugacy class of

\rho(c)
in
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
is parabolic.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.2
, the element
\rho(c)
is elliptic or parabolic.
To prove by contradiction, assume that
A\,:=\,\rho(c)
is elliptic. Choose a fixed point

o\,\in\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
for the action of
A
,
and work on the cusp strip
S_{Y}
defined in (
4.1
).

Since
E(f)\,<\,\infty
, we have, in particular,

(4.6)

\int_{Y}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lvert f_{x}\rvert_{h}^{2}\,dx\,dy\ <\ \infty.

Consider
U
defined in (
4.2
). After increasing
Y
— if necessary —- the properness
condition of
f
implies that

f(S_{Y})\cap\overline{B_{h}(o,1)}\,=\,\emptyset
, and hence
U
is smooth on
S_{Y}
. As noted above,
U
is
subharmonic on
S_{Y}
and
2\pi
–periodic in
x
(see (
4.3
) and the sentence
following it). Moreover, Lemma
4.4
gives that

|U_{x}|^{2}+|U_{y}|^{2}\ \leq\ |f_{x}|_{h}^{2}+|f_{y}|_{h}^{2},

and therefore

(4.7)

\int_{Y}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(|U_{x}|^{2}+|U_{y}|^{2}\bigr)\,dx\,dy\ <\ \infty.

Consider
\mathcal{A}(y)
defined in Lemma
4.5
. By Fubini’s theorem
and (
4.7
), we have
\mathcal{A}\,\in\,L^{2}([Y,\,\infty))
. Applying
Lemma
4.3
, choose
y_{n}\,\to\,\infty
such that

(4.8)

\mathcal{A}(y_{n})\ \to\ 0.

Since
f
is proper around the puncture, the sequence
(0,\,y_{n})
leaves every compact subset of

S_{Y}
(defined in (
4.1
)), hence
f(0,\,y_{n})

leaves every compact subset of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
. In particular, we have

(4.9)

U(0,y_{n})\ =\ d_{h}\bigl(o,f(0,y_{n})\bigr)\ \to\ +\infty.

On the other hand, Lemma
4.5
implies that

(4.10)

U(0,y_{n})\ \leq\ \sup_{t\geq Y}\,m(t)+\sqrt{2\pi}\,\mathcal{A}(y_{n}).

The function
m
is bounded above and
\mathcal{A}(y_{n})\,\to\,0
, so the right-hand side
of (
4.10
) is bounded
uniformly in
n
.
This contradicts (
4.9
). Therefore, we conclude that
\rho(c)
is not elliptic, and
consequently it is parabolic.
∎

We now prove the converse which says that parabolic peripheral holonomy enforces properness.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a finite–energy,
\rho
–equivariant
conformal harmonic map. If
\rho(c)
is parabolic for a cusp loop
c\,\in\,\Gamma
,
then
f
is proper around the corresponding puncture.

Proof.

To prove by contradiction, assume that
f
is not proper around the puncture corresponding to
the cusp loop
c
. As in (
3.2
),

\varpi\ :\ \mathbb{H}\ \longrightarrow\,\Sigma_{0}

is the universal covering map. The assumption that
f
is not proper means that there exists a compact subset

K\,\subset\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, and a sequence
p_{k}\,\in\,\mathbb{H}
, such that

\varpi(p_{k})\ \longrightarrow\ z_{i}\quad\text{ in }\,\Sigma,\ \ \,\text{ and }\ \ \,f(p_{k})\,\in\,K\,\ \ \forall\ \,k.

Passing to a subsequence, we may assume that

(4.11)

f(p_{k})\ \longrightarrow\ p_{\infty}\ \in\ \mathbb{CH}^{2}.

Choose a holomorphic coordinate function
\zeta
on
\Sigma
centered at
z_{i}
, so that
\zeta(z_{i})\,=\,0
.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon\,>\,0
, the punctured neighborhood

U^{*}\ :=\ \{\zeta\,\in\,\mathbb{C}\,\,\big|\,\ 0\,<\,|\zeta|\,<\,\varepsilon\}\ \subset\ \Sigma_{0}

is a punctured disk about
z_{i}
. Let
\Omega
be a connected component of
\varpi^{-1}(U^{*})
. Then

\varpi\,:\,\Omega\,\longrightarrow\,U^{*}
is a covering map whose deck transformation
group is generated by the cusp loop
c
.

Since
f
has finite energy on
\Sigma_{0}
, its restriction to
\Omega
has finite energy on
U^{*}
.
Consequently, the removable–singularity theorem for finite–energy harmonic maps into Hadamard targets

[
12
]
gives a continuous harmonic extension across the puncture

\overline{f}\ :\ \ U\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb{CH}^{2},

where
U\ =\ \{\zeta\,\in\,\mathbb{C}\,\,\big|\,\,\,|\zeta|<\varepsilon\}
, satisfying the condition
that
\overline{f}|_{U^{*}}
agrees with
f

(after identifying
U^{*}
with
\Omega/\langle c\rangle
). Moreover, the limit in (
4.11
)
satisfies the condition

(4.12)

\overline{f}(z_{i})\ =\ \overline{f}(0)\ =\ p_{\infty}.

Finally, using equivariance,

f(c\cdot q)\ =\ \rho(c)\cdot f(q)\qquad\text{for all }\,\,q\ \in\ \Omega.

Choose a sequence
q_{m}\,\in\,\Omega
with
\varpi(q_{m})\,\to\,z_{i}
. Then we also have

\varpi(c\cdot q_{m})\,\to\,z_{i}
, and by the continuity of
\overline{f}
at the puncture,

f(q_{m})\ \longrightarrow\ \overline{f}(z_{i})\ =\ p_{\infty},\qquad f(c\cdot q_{m})\ \longrightarrow\ \overline{f}(z_{i})\ =\ p_{\infty}.

Since
f(c\cdot q_{m})\,=\,\rho(c)\,f(q_{m})
, taking limits yields
\rho(c)\,p_{\infty}\,=\,p_{\infty}
. Thus
\rho(c)

fixes a point of
\mathbb{CH}^{2}
, and hence
\rho(c)
is elliptic. But this contradicts the hypothesis
that
\rho(c)
is parabolic. Therefore, it follows that
f
is proper around the puncture.
∎

The combination of Proposition
4.6
and Proposition
4.7

yields the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a finite–energy, conformal,

\rho
–equivariant minimal immersion. Then
f
is proper around every cusp if and only if
for every peripheral loop
c\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
, the holonomy
\rho(c)
is parabolic.

We end this section by mentioning a result on completeness and properness:

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
be a finite–energy, conformal,
\rho
–equivariant
minimal immersion. Assume that for every peripheral loop
c\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
, the
holonomy
\rho(c)
is parabolic. Then
f
is proper around every cusp if and only if
the induced metric
g_{f}
is complete on
\Sigma_{0}
.

Proof.

If
f
is proper around every cusp, then it follows directly that
(\Sigma_{0},\,g_{f})
is complete.

To prove the converse, first note that in view of Theorem
4.8
,
the given condition that the holonomy
\rho(c)
is parabolic implies that
f
is proper around every cusp
(this does not use completeness). Hence, within this parabolic holonomy
setting, the two conditions “proper ends” and “complete ends” are equivalent.
∎

5.
Parabolic
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
Higgs bundles

Recall the notation:
\Sigma
is a compact connected Riemann surface,

P\ =\ \{p_{1},\,\cdots,\,p_{m}\}\ \subset\ \Sigma

is a nonempty finite subset of distinct marked points, and
\Sigma_{0}\,:=\,\Sigma\setminus P
. Denote the log–canonical bundle

K_{\log}\ :=\ K_{\Sigma}(P)\ =\ K_{\Sigma}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(P).

In Sections
3
–
4
we considered pairs

(\rho,\,f)
consisting of a reductive representation

\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
and a
\rho
–equivariant conformal minimal immersion

f\,:\,\mathbb{H}\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{CH}^{2}
of finite energy. By Theorem
4.8
,
properness at each puncture is equivalent to the peripheral holonomy being parabolic. This is precisely the regime
of the tame/logarithmic nonabelian Hodge correspondence on punctured curves: Representations with prescribed
(parabolic) conjugacy classes around
P
correspond to polystable parabolic Higgs bundles with logarithmic
singularities along
P

[
14
,
2
,
1
]
.
The purpose of this section is to fix the parabolic
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
–Higgs data, including the role of
parabolic weights and nilpotent residues (encoding the unipotent part).

5.1.
Parabolic
\mathrm{U}(2,1)
Higgs data

A parabolic vector bundle
E_{*}
over
(\Sigma,\,P)
consists of a holomorphic vector bundle
E\,\longrightarrow\,\Sigma

together with — for each
p\,\in\,P
— a quasiparabolic filtration of the fiber

E_{p}\,=\,F^{1}_{p}\,\supsetneq\,F^{2}_{p}\,\supsetneq\,\cdots\,\supsetneq\,F^{\ell(p)}_{p}\,\supsetneq\,F^{\ell(p)+1}_{p}\,=\,\{0\},

and real weights

0\,\leq\,\alpha^{1}_{p}\,\leq\,\alpha^{2}_{p}\,\leq\,\cdots\,\leq\,\alpha^{\ell(p)}_{p}\,<\,1

assigned to the subspaces in the quasiparabolic filtration. In the
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
setting, the underlying
holomorphic vector bundle splits as

E\ =\,V\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma},

where
V
is some holomorphic vector bundle of rank two, and the Higgs field is off–diagonal with respect to
this splitting:

(5.1)

\Phi\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}0&\Phi_{1}\\
\Phi_{2}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\Phi_{1}\,:\,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\,\longrightarrow\,V\otimes K_{\log}
and

\Phi_{2}\,:\,V\,\longrightarrow\,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}\otimes K_{\log}
. These conditions on
\Phi_{1}
,
\Phi_{2}

imply that
\Phi
has at most logarithmic singularities along
P
, i.e.,
\Phi\,\in\,H^{0}(\Sigma,\,\mathrm{End}(E)\otimes K_{\log})
.

Definition 5.1

(Strongly parabolic Higgs field)
.

Let
E_{*}
be a parabolic vector bundle. A logarithmic Higgs field

\Phi\,\in\,H^{0}(\Sigma,\,\mathrm{End}(E)\otimes K_{\log})
is called strongly parabolic if for every

p\,\in\,P
, its residue
N_{p}\,:=\,\mathrm{Res}_{p}(\Phi)\,\in\,\mathrm{End}(E_{p})
satisfies the
condition

N_{p}(F^{j}_{p})\ \subset\ F^{j+1}_{p}

for all
j
.

Two parabolic
\mathrm{U}(2,1)
–Higgs bundles are considered equivalent if they differ by
tensoring with a parabolic line bundle. The corresponding equivalence classes are parabolic

\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
–Higgs bundles.

5.2.
Peripheral holonomy, weights, and nilpotent residues

Fix a point
p\,\in\,P
, and let
c_{p}\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
be a positively oriented small loop about
p
.
Assume that
\rho(c_{p})\,\in\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
is parabolic
(as mentioned before,
\rho(c_{p})
gives a conjugacy class in
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
;
the condition means that
the elements of this conjugacy class are parabolic). Write its Jordan decomposition as

(5.2)

\rho(c_{p})\ =\ (\rho(c_{p}))_{s}\,(\rho(c_{p}))_{u},

where
(\cdot)_{s}
is semisimple and
(\cdot)_{u}
is unipotent.

Weights and screw–parabolic semisimple parts.

Recall that
\rho(c_{p})
is parabolic (see Theorem
4.8

and Corollary
4.9
). From this it follows that all
the eigenvalues of the semisimple factor
(\rho(c_{p}))_{s}
in (
5.2
) have absolute value

1
and two of them are equal
[
10
, p. 16, Theorem 3.6(ii)]
.
Choosing a local lift to
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)
(always possible because
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
is
a free group; see Lemma
5.2
below), we may write the eigenvalues
of the semisimple part in the form

\mathrm{spec}\bigl((\rho(c_{p}))_{s}\bigr)\ =\ \left\{e^{2\pi\sqrt{-1}\alpha_{p,1}},\ e^{2\pi\sqrt{-1}\alpha_{p,2}},\ e^{2\pi\sqrt{-1}\alpha_{p,3}}\right\},

where
0\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,1}\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,2}\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,3}\,<\,1
. The
numbers
\alpha_{p,j}
are the parabolic weights at
p
. Note
\alpha_{p,1}+\alpha_{p,2}+\alpha_{p,3}=0\,\,\mathrm{mod}(1)
, and
\alpha_{p,1}=\alpha_{p,2}
or
\alpha_{p,2}=\alpha_{p,3}
.

Nilpotent residues and the unipotent part.

On the Higgs side, the unipotent factor
(\rho(c_{p}))_{u}
is reflected by the residue

N_{p}=\mathrm{Res}_{p}(\Phi)
of a strongly parabolic Higgs field (Definition
5.1
).
When the rank is
3
there are exactly two nilpotent Jordan types:

(5.3)

N_{p}\ \neq\ 0,\ \ N_{p}^{2}\ =\ 0\,\quad\text{ (Jordan type $(2,\,1)$) },\,\quad\text{ or}

N_{p}^{2}\ \neq\ 0,\ \ N_{p}^{3}\ =\ 0\qquad\text{(Jordan type $(3)$)}.

Each type determines a canonical two-step quasiparabolic filtration of the fiber:

(5.4)

0\,\subset\,F^{(1)}_{p}\,\subset\,F^{(2)}_{p}\,\subset\,E_{p}

defined by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{type $(2,1)$:}&F^{(1)}_{p}=\mathrm{im}(N_{p})\ \ (\dim=1),\qquad F^{(2)}_{p}=\ker(N_{p})\ \ (\dim=2),\\[3.99994pt]
\text{type $(3)$:}&F^{(1)}_{p}=\ker(N_{p})\ \ (\dim=1),\qquad F^{(2)}_{p}=\ker(N_{p}^{2})=\mathrm{im}(N_{p})\ \ (\dim=2).\end{array}

A full flag
0\,\subset\,L_{p}\,\subset\,V_{p}\,\subset\,E_{p}
refining (
5.4
)
together with weights
\alpha_{p,1}\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,2}\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,3}
gives the parabolic
structure. In particular,
N_{p}
is nilpotent with respect to such a flag, and hence the
Higgs field is strongly parabolic.

The parabolic weights encode the semisimple (screw) part of the peripheral conjugacy class, while the nilpotent residue
encodes the unipotent part. The two Jordan types in (
5.3
) reappear in Section
8

as the two end types discussed there.
Moreover, when proving stability in Section
7
, we keep the weights throughout and compute
parabolic degrees of the relevant
\Phi
-invariant subbundles.

5.3.
Parabolic degree, slope, and stability

Let
E_{*}
be a parabolic vector bundle on
(\Sigma,\,P)
of rank
r
.
Fix, at each parabolic point
p\,\in\,P
a weighted flag with weights
0\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,1}\,\leq\,\cdots\,\leq\,\alpha_{p,r}\,<\,1
(weights have multiplicities).
The
parabolic degree
and
parabolic slope
are defined by

\deg_{\mathrm{par}}(E_{*})\,:=\,\deg(E)+\sum_{p\in P}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(\dim(F^{j}_{p}/F^{j+1}_{p})\bigr)\,\alpha_{p,j},\ \ \,\mu_{\mathrm{par}}(E_{*})\,:=\,\frac{\deg_{\mathrm{par}}(E_{*})}{r}.

Any subbundle
F\,\subset\,E
inherits an induced parabolic structure
F_{*}
by intersecting flags; the
resulting parabolic vector bundle is denoted by
F_{*}
. The subbundle
F
is called
\Phi
–
invariant

if we have
\Phi(F)\,\subset\,F\otimes K_{\log}
.
A parabolic Higgs bundle
(E_{*},\,\Phi)
is
stable
(respectively,
semistable
) if for every proper
nonzero
\Phi
–invariant parabolic subbundle
F_{*}\,\subset\,E_{*}
one has

\mu_{\mathrm{par}}(F_{*})\,<\,\mu_{\mathrm{par}}(E_{*})
(respectively,

\mu_{\mathrm{par}}(F_{*})\,\leq\,\mu_{\mathrm{par}}(E_{*})
). When all the parabolic weights are zero,
parabolic (semi)stability coincides with the usual (semi)stability.

5.4.
Principal bundles and projective monodromy

Let
\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
be a reductive representation.
Since
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})
is a free group, the homomorphism
\rho
lifts to a homomorphism

\overline{\rho}\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

\mathrm{SU}(2,1)
–bundle on

has the following

(5.5)

P_{\overline{\rho}}\ :=\ (\mathbb{H}\times\mathrm{SU}(2,1))/\!\sim,\ \ \ (\gamma\cdot x,\,y)\ \sim\ (x,\,\overline{\rho}(\gamma)y),\ \ \ \gamma\,\in\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})

\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

\mathbb{H}\times\mathrm{SU}(2,1)\,\longrightarrow\,\mathbb{H}

The associated flat Hermitian vector bundle of signature

Quotienting by the center

If

Note that

is a free group because

is a noncompact Riemann surface; in fact,

Lemma 5.2
.

The representation
\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)

\overline{\rho}\ :\ \pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\ \longrightarrow\ \mathrm{SU}(2,1)

6.
From minimal immersion to parabolic data

As before,
\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,=\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma\setminus P)\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
is
a reductive representation, and

is a
\rho
–equivariant conformal minimal

Assume that
f
is proper around each puncture (see Definition

).
Then Theorem

implies that for every

and any peripheral loop

about

is parabolic.

Properness is a condition on the map

).

6.1.
Tame nonabelian Hodge correspondence (recall)

Let

(for example,

be a reduction of structure group of

to a maximal compact subgroup
K\,\subset\,G
.
Take a parabolic point

, and choose a local holomorphic coordinate function

, centered at

,
on a neighborhood of

compatible with the weighted parabolic filtration, we say that

determined by the parabolic weights

and the chosen nilpotent–orbit data; equivalently, there is a constant

such that

on a punctured neighborhood of

In this case, the Higgs field has at most logarithmic growth, i.e.

\displaystyle\Phi\ \in\

\displaystyle H^{0}\!\bigl(\Sigma,\,\mathrm{ad}(E)\otimes K_{\log}\bigr),

\displaystyle\Phi\ =\

\displaystyle\left(\frac{s_{p}}{z}+\text{(log--corrected nilpotent term)}+O(|z|^{\epsilon})\right)\,dz,

for some
\epsilon\,>\,0
. In particular,
\Phi
has a simple pole at
p
, and its residue is compatible with the
parabolic filtration: its semisimple part is prescribed by the weights, while the remaining part is nilpotent
and (strongly) parabolic with respect to the weighted flag.

We use the nonabelian Hodge theoretic correspondence for parabolic
G
–Higgs bundles and parabolic

G
–local systems. Under the usual topological constraints (vanishing of parabolic degrees for all characters),
one has the following:

•

Existence/uniqueness of an adapted solution.

A parabolic
G
–Higgs bundle
(E,\,\Phi)
admits a
c
–Hermitian Yang-Mills- Higgs metric

which is adapted at each
puncture if and only if
(E,\,\Phi)
is
c

is unique up to automorphisms of

(E,\,\Phi)
.

•

From Higgs data to flat connections and back.

Given an adapted solution

, the associated connection

D\,\;=\,\;A(h)\;+\;\Phi\;-\;\tau_{h}(\Phi)

is flat on
\Sigma_{0}
, with monodromy lying in the prescribed parabolic conjugacy class at each puncture.

Conversely, a polystable parabolic
G
–local system admits a compatible harmonic metric, giving a polystable
parabolic
G
–Higgs bundle with logarithmic singularities.

These statements for real reductive

are proved in
[

]
:

[

(existence/uniqueness of a quasi-isometric adapted Hermite–Yang-Mills-Higgs metric for

–Higgs bundles),
[

(description of the induced parabolic structure and the precise

[

(polystability of a parabolic

For complex groups and general tame harmonic bundles we refer to Mochizuki’s work

Remark 6.1

(On the constant
c
)
.

In
[
1
]
, the label “
c
” means that the harmonic metric
h
solves the
Hitchin equation with a
central
constant

F_{A(h)}\;-\;[\Phi,\tau_{h}(\Phi)]\;=\;-\,\sqrt{-1}\,c\,\omega\qquad\text{on }\Sigma_{0},

together with the prescribed asymptotics at the punctures. Here

c\in\sqrt{-1}\,\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{k})
lies in the center of the Lie algebra of a maximal compact subgroup

K\subset G
; it is determined by the topological type (equivalently, by the parabolic degrees of the bundles
associated to characters of
G
).

In our case, after passing to
G=\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
, this constraint is automatic:
\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
is
adjoint, so it has no nontrivial characters, and
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{k})\,=\,0
. Hence the relevant
equation is the standard one with
c\,=\,0
.

How the end hypothesis is used.

In our application, we do not use the implication of the form “complete end
\Rightarrow
tame”.
Instead, we first use the properness of ends to conclude that the peripheral holonomy is parabolic
(Theorem
4.8
). The resulting parabolic conjugacy class at each
p\,\in\,P

determines the corresponding parabolic weights (the semisimple “screw” part) together with the admissible
nilpotent residue data (the unipotent part); see Section
5
.
With these data fixed, the punctured Donaldson–Uhlenbeck–Yau correspondence then provides an adapted harmonic metric and
hence a logarithmic (parabolic) Higgs field. In particular, for purely unipotent monodromy, the residue is nilpotent,
whereas for screw–parabolic monodromy the residue has the prescribed semisimple part together with a nilpotent part
that is strongly parabolic with respect to the weighted filtration.

6.2.
From a harmonic map to a Higgs field

Let
\rho\,:\,\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,\mathrm{PU}(2,1)
be a reductive representation and

f\ :\ \mathbb{H}\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb{CH}^{2}

a
\rho
–equivariant minimal immersion.
By Lemma
5.2
, the representation
\rho
admits a lift

which is unique up to twisting by a central character. Fix one such lift.
Although

\rho

–equivariant:

\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

\mathrm{PU}(2,1)

Let

\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

\overline{\rho}
(see (

3
complex vector bundle, with flat connection

(see (

A
\rho
–equivariant map

to the maximal compact subgroup

of
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

encoded by a smooth Hermitian structure

(positive definite), i.e.,  a

is compatible with the
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)

In particular, starting from

on
E_{\mathrm{flat}}
(the Hermitian structure corresponding to the reduction defined by

Given any such Hermitian structure

on

\nabla_{H}
and a unique
H
–self–adjoint

Decomposing into types with respect to the complex structure on

\nabla_{H}\ =\ \nabla_{H}^{1,0}+\nabla_{H}^{0,1},\ \ \ \Psi_{H}\ =\ \Phi_{H}+\Phi_{H}^{\dagger},\ \ \ \Phi_{H}\ :=\ \Psi_{H}^{1,0}.

The operator

The Hermitian structure

Then

is harmonic if and only if the pair

satisfies the Hitchin equations,
and this is equivalent to the harmonicity of the corresponding

–equivariant map

Remark 6.2
.

Different choices of the
\mathrm{SU}(2,1)
–lift

differ by twisting by a central character

\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{0})\,\longrightarrow\,Z(\mathrm{SU}(2,1))\,\cong\,{\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}

–Higgs data.

We recall the fact about the tame harmonic bundle associated to a complete end

]

[

]

[

]
.

6.5.
Divisors and the logarithmic extension sequence

Assume that
f
is mixed. Then in (
6.4
) both off–diagonal components

\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are nonzero.
So
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
have at most logarithmic singularities along
P
. Let
D_{1}
(respectively,
D_{2}
)
be the effective divisor on
\Sigma
given by the zeros of
\Phi_{1}
(respectively,
\Phi_{2}
).
Geometrically, the support of
D_{1}
(respectively,
D_{2}
) is the set of anti–complex
(respectively, complex) points of the immersion
f
.

Using the canonical identification

V\otimes K_{\log}\ \cong\ \mathcal{H}om(K_{\log}^{-1},\,V),

the section
\Phi_{1}
determines an
{\mathcal{O}}_{\Sigma}
–linear morphism of coherent analytic sheaves

\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}\ :\ K_{\log}^{-1}\ \longrightarrow\ V.

It can be shown that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}
is injective as a
sheaf
map and its cokernel has torsion
precisely at the zeros of
\Phi_{1}
.

To prove this, note that at any point
x\,\in\,\Sigma
the stalk
(K_{\log}^{-1})_{x}
is a free

{\mathcal{O}}_{\Sigma,x}
–module of rank
1
, and
hence it is torsion–free, while
V_{x}
is torsion–free of rank
2
. Since
\Phi_{1}\,\not\equiv\,0
, the induced map on the generic point is nonzero, so the kernel of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}
is a torsion
subsheaf of
K_{\log}^{-1}
. But
K_{\log}^{-1}
is torsion–free, hence
\ker(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})\,=\,0
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}
is a sheaf injection.

Locally near a point
x\,\in\,\mathrm{supp}(D_{1})
, choose a
holomorphic coordinate function
z
and holomorphic frames so that

\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}
is represented by a column vector of holomorphic functions. If
\Phi_{1}
vanishes to
order
m\,=\,m_{x}
at
x
, then after changing the local frame of
V
we may write

\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}\ :\ {\mathcal{O}}_{\Sigma,x}\ \longrightarrow\ {\mathcal{O}}_{\Sigma,x}^{\oplus 2},\qquad f\ \longmapsto\ (z^{m}f,\,0).

From this normal form it is immediate that the cokernel has a torsion summand

{\mathcal{O}}_{\Sigma,x}/(z^{m})
; in particular, the torsion part of
\mathrm{coker}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})

is supported on
D_{1}
.

The image
\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})\,\subset\,V
is a rank–
1
subsheaf but it
need not be locally free at points of
D_{1}
.
We therefore take its saturation:

\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})^{\mathrm{sat}}\ :=\ \ker\!\Bigl(V\longrightarrow(V/\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}))/\mathrm{(torsion)}\Bigr).

On a Riemann surface, the saturation of a rank–
1
subsheaf of a vector bundle is a line subbundle
of the vector bundle. In the above local normal form,

\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})=z^{m}\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma,p}\cdot v_{1}
while

\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})^{\mathrm{sat}}\,=\,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma,p}\cdot v_{1}
; thus the discrepancy is measured by the
vanishing order of
\Phi_{1}
. Globally, one obtains a line subbundle

\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})^{\mathrm{sat}}\ \cong\ K_{\log}^{-1}(D_{1})\ \subset\ V,

and the quotient
\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})^{\mathrm{sat}}/\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})
is a
torsion sheaf supported on
D_{1}
.

Similarly, viewing
\Phi_{2}
as a sheaf morphism gives

\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}\ :\ V\ \longrightarrow\ K_{\log},

and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}
induces a surjection of sheaves

V\ \twoheadrightarrow\ K_{\log}(-D_{2}),

with cokernel a torsion sheaf supported on
D_{2}
.

Finally, in the mixed conformal case one has
\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi^{2})\,=\,0
, which forces

\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}\circ\widetilde{\Phi}_{1}\,=\,0
; equivalently,
\mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})\,\subset\,\ker(\widetilde{\Phi}_{2})
.
On the dense open subset
\Sigma\setminus(D_{1}\cup D_{2})
both maps have rank
1
, so the two line
subsheaves agree there. Taking saturations, yields

\ker(\widetilde{\Phi}_{2})\ \cong\ \mathrm{im}(\widetilde{\Phi}_{1})^{\mathrm{sat}}\ \cong\ K_{\log}^{-1}(D_{1}).

Altogether we obtain the logarithmic analogue of the Loftin–McIntosh
[
8
]
extension sequence:

(6.5)

0\ \longrightarrow\ K_{\log}^{-1}(D_{1})\ \xrightarrow{\ \widetilde{\Phi}_{1}\ }\ V\ \xrightarrow{\ \widetilde{\Phi}_{2}\ }\ K_{\log}(-D_{2})\ \longrightarrow\ 0.

Lemma 9.2

For
\Phi

\operatorname{tr}(\Phi^{2})\,\ \equiv\,\ 0.

Proof.

Write

and

Then
