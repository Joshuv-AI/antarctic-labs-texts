Abstract
We present the open-source Python package, BAGLE (Bayesian Analysis of Gravitational Lensing Events), which enables modeling and joint fitting of photometric and astrometric data sets. We describe the model parameterizations and present the equations for microlensing events containing either a point-source, point-lens or a finite-source, point-lens geometry both with and without microlensing parallax due to the motion of the Earth or a satellite around the Sun. Conversions between different coordinate reference frames are also derived. We compare our model light curves to those from other papers and microlens modeling software, finding good agreement, although with some differences in finite-source models at a ∼ \sim 1% level detectable with upcoming observations from space-based facilities. We also use BAGLE to demonstrate the impact of changing lens mass, lens distance, and blended source flux fraction on photometric lightcurves and astrometric trajectories in preparation for upcoming Gaia data releases and the launch of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and its Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey (GBTDS). In particular, we show that Roman GBTDS will detect significant microlensing parallax signals for events that are 2 × \times shorter in duration than from ground-based surveys. Additionally, long-duration events with durations of t E , ☉ > 100 t_{E,\sun}>100 days will yield microlensing parallax uncertainties of σ π E < 0.01 \sigma_{\pi_{E}}<0.01 with Roman, enabling confident identification of isolated stellar-mass black holes that can be modeled both astrometrically and photometrically with BAGLE for precise mass determinations. BAGLE is an open-source code and community development is encouraged.

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S,0},\sun}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{S},\sun}[t-t_{0,\sun}]+\pi_{S}\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\earth}(t)

Introducing lensing into the picture, the characteristic scale for a microlensing event is the Einstein radius, in angular units on the sky (i.e. milli-arcseconds), given by

\theta_{E}=\sqrt{\frac{4GM_{L}}{c^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{d_{L}}-\frac{1}{d_{S}}\right)}

(4)

where
M_{L}
is the mass of the lens and
d_{L}
and
d_{S}
are the distance
between the Sun and the lens and source, respectively. For simplicity, we will initially assume the lens is dark.

It is convenient to switch to a coordinate system normalized by the Einstein radius.
In any reference frame, we can redefine the relative positional offset as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{X_{S}}-\boldsymbol{X_{L}}}{\theta_{E}}.

(5)

Note that this is the true angular offset and not the lensed position seen in the sky. The magnitude of the separation vector,
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
, between the source and the lens determines the amount of magnification and shift in direction as a function of time during the microlensing event.

When a lens and source are separated (in projection) by
\lesssim\theta_{E}
, the source will be gravitationally lensed, producing two lensed images indicated as
-
and
+
for the closer and further images in projection.
The positions of the two lensed source images are

\boldsymbol{X}_{\pm,S}=\left(u\pm\sqrt{u^{2}+4}\right)\frac{\theta_{E}}{2}\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}

(6)

in angular units such as arcseconds.
The offsets of the two images from the true (unlensed) source position are described by

\boldsymbol{\delta_{\pm}}=\left(-u\pm\sqrt{u^{2}+4}\right)\frac{\theta_{E}}{2}\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}.

(7)

III.1
Microlens Event Characteristics

The motions of the source and lens produce a transient photometric event with a characteristic timescale defined as the Einstein crossing time,

\displaystyle t_{E,\sun}=\frac{\theta_{E}}{\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\sun}}

(13)

The transient photometric signal peaks at the “closest approach” distance, at time
t_{0,\sun}
, as

\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}=\frac{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S,0},\sun}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L,0},\sun}}{\theta_{E}}.

(14)

It is important to note that these equations are in the SSB reference frame and that the Einstein crossing time and closest approach time and distance change in different frames (see §
III.4.2
for geocentric definitions).

There is also a transient astrometric signal; however, the peak photometric magnification and the peak astrometric offset
do not
occur at the same time.
Given Eq.
10
, the maximum astrometric shift of the unresolved centroid occurs when the source and lens are separated by

u_{c,max,\odot}=\sqrt{2}

(15)

and at time,

t_{c,max,\odot}=t_{0,\sun}\pm t_{E,\sun}\sqrt{2-u_{0,\sun}^{2}}

(16)

in the SSB frame. The amplitude of the maximum astrometric shift is

\delta_{c,max,\odot}=\frac{u_{c,max,\odot}\theta_{E}}{u_{c,max,\odot}^{2}+2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}\theta_{E}.

(17)

When fitting photometry-only observations of microlens events, it is convenient to re-parameterize Equation
3
in dimensionless units of
\theta_{E}
.
Then, the amplitude of the relative proper motion in units of the Einstein radius is given by
1/t_{E,\sun}
per Eq.
13
.
To convert to an Earth-centric reference frame and to account for the Earth’s acceleration around the SSB, we express the relative parallax due to Earth’s motion in units of Einstein radii as

\pi_{E}=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\theta_{E}}.

(18)

The equation of relative motion as seen from Earth becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\earth}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}+\frac{\Delta t}{t_{E,\sun}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}-\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{P}(t)

(19)

\displaystyle=u_{0,\sun}\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}+\tau\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}-\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{P}(t)

(20)

where
\tau=(t-t_{0,\sun})/t_{E,\sun}=\Delta t/t_{E,\sun}
.
Thus, even without knowledge of the absolute source and lens positions, proper motions, and parallaxes, both the amplifications and centroid offsets in units of
\theta_{E}
can be determined from Equations
7
-
10
.
This is what is returned by all
bagle
photometry-only models (e.g.
PSPL_Phot_Par_Param1
).

The geometry of the lens–source–Sun (or SSB) system is set by
\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}
, which are orthogonal unit vectors. They are typically expressed in the Equatorial coordinate system that increases to the East (
\boldsymbol{\hat{E}}
) and North (
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
) and
\hat{z}=\boldsymbol{\hat{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
points away from the Solar System.
A common convention in the photometric microlensing literature is to define the source-lens geometry with a new vector

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{E,\sun}=\pi_{E}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{E,\sun}\;\;\textrm{where}\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{E,\sun}=\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}

(21)

and to allow
u_{0,\sun}
to have a sign such that

\displaystyle u_{0,\sun}>0

\displaystyle\;\;\textrm{if}\;\;(\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\cdot\hat{z}

\displaystyle>0

(22)

\displaystyle u_{0,\sun}<0

\displaystyle\;\;\textrm{if}\;\;(\boldsymbol{\hat{u}}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}\times\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\cdot\hat{z}

\displaystyle<0

(23)

as first developed in
30
. In other words, when the source passes East (West) of the lens,
u_{0,\sun}
is positive (negative). The unit-less closest approach distance,
u_{0,\sun}
, can also be expressed as an angular separation,
\beta=u_{0,\sun}\theta_{E}
.
These parameters and conventions are particularly important for modeling photometry-only data sets.

In the SSB lens-rest frame, the lensed source traces an ellipse during the lensing event with a major axis of

a=\frac{\theta_{E}}{2\sqrt{u_{0,\sun}^{2}+2}}

(24)

in the direction of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}
(or
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{E,\sun}
) and an ellipticity of

e=\left(\frac{2}{u_{0,\sun}^{2}+2}\right)^{1/2}

(25)

as shown in Figure
4
, bottom-right panel.
In the geocentric frame, this ellipse is modulated by the parallax signal (Figure
4
, left panel).

III.2
Parallax Vector Definition

Microlensing events with both photometric and astrometric signals provide much more information on the distances and parallaxes to the lens and source.
Thus, a careful treatment of parallax is essential.

\boldsymbol{P}(t,\alpha,\delta)
is the direction and magnitude of the parallax
vector at a given time,
t
and in the direction of the source-lens
system, (
\alpha
,
\delta
) given by the difference of the Earth and Sun’s position, normalized by 1 AU.

Precise ephemerides are available in the Astropy package
(
8
;
6
;
7
)
.
In BAGLE, we use the
get_body_barycentric_posvel()
function in the
astropy.coordinates
library to compute
\boldsymbol{P}
.
First we compute the 3D position vector for the Sun and the Earth relative to the Solar System Barycenter,
\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{sun},SSB}}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{earth},SSB}}(t)
.
These 3D vectors are projected onto the tangential sky plane at the microlensing target position using,

\displaystyle P_{E}=

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{sun},SSB}}(t)-\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{earth},SSB}}(t)]\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{E}}_{3D}

(26)

\displaystyle P_{N}=

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{sun},SSB}}(t)-\boldsymbol{Y_{\textrm{earth},SSB}}(t)]\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}_{3D}

(27)

where
\boldsymbol{\hat{E}}_{3D}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}_{3D}
point East and North.

In BAGLE, we use JPL ephemerides
(
50
)
through the Astropy interface, as they are more precise.
The JPL ephemerides require a 115 MB file download on first use only.
Calls to calculate the parallax vector are also cached to increase computational efficiency for model fitting. The cache folder is, by default, limited to 1 GB and can be changed in
parallax.py

Alternatively, a user can set

III.3
Blending

An idealized microlensing event with an isolated source and a dark lens would magnify the source flux and shift the source position over time as described in Equations
9
and
10
.
However, in most microlensing events, the lens is luminous and dilutes the microlensing signal.
Further, microlensing is most frequent in crowded fields, and the beam size or seeing of our telescopes typically includes multiple neighbor sources as well.
To capture this contamination, we define “blending” parameters such as the source flux fraction,

b_{sff}=\frac{F_{S}}{F_{S}+F_{L}+F_{N}}

(28)

where
F_{S}
is the unlensed flux of the source,
F_{L}
is the lens flux, and
F_{N}
is the flux from neighboring stars (including companions to the source or lens).
The photometric microlensing signal is then given as the total flux,

F_{cent}=A(t)F_{S}+\left(\frac{1-b_{sff}}{b_{sff}}\right)F_{S}.

(29)

The effect of blending is more complicated in the astrometric signal.
The blended astrometric signal requires knowledge of not only the flux ratios but also the relative position and motion vectors of the source, lens and neighbors.
The astrometric shift vector for a microlensing event with a luminous lens and no luminous neighbors is

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{c,LL}=\frac{1+g(u^{2}+3-u\sqrt{u^{2}+4})}{(1+g)(u^{2}+2+gu\sqrt{u^{2}+4})}\theta_{E}\boldsymbol{u},

(30)

where
g=F_{L}/F_{S}
is the lens-source flux ratio (see Appendix
E
).

The observed positions on the sky for a microlensing event including a luminous lens is

\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{cent},\earth}=b_{sff}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{S},\earth}+(1-b_{sff})\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\earth}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{c,LL}.

(31)

The observed photometry on the sky, including a luminous lens, is

mag_{cent,\earth}=-2.5\log\left(F_{S}\cdot A_{\earth}+F_{S}\frac{1-b_{sff}}{b_{sff}}\right)+ZP.

(32)

At this time, BAGLE’s astrometric models include blended light from a luminous companion in all model classes, and additional neighbors are not accounted for. However, all photometric models are able to handle blending from a luminous lens and/or other neighbors. Much higher spatial resolution is required for astrometric microlensing data sets than most photometric surveys provide, so blending from unrelated neighbors is reduced for the average astrometric data set in comparison to ground-based, non-adaptive optics (AO) photometric data. However, neighbor stars may still have an effect. Handling of luminous neighbor stars in astrometric models will be implemented in future BAGLE development as astrometric data sets improve and provide sufficient constraining power for the increased number of free parameters.

III.4
Parameterization

In order to fully model a microlensing event, we must choose a means of parameterizing
\boldsymbol{u}
.
We typically choose to fit for heliocentric parameters for astrometric fits as the position at time
t_{0,\sun}
and proper motion of the source are naturally expressed in heliocentric coordinates (§
III.4.1
). However, most of the exoplanet microlensing community adopts a pseudo-geocentric reference frame as they predominantly work with short-timescale events and photometry only. We present conversions to this pseudo-geocentric frame in §
III.4.2

III.4.2
(Not quite) Geocentric: geo–
t_{r}

While the above heliocentric frame is the most general, it has become common convention in the microlensing literature to switch to a stationary, rectilinear frame that is geocentric at some reference time,
t_{r}
.
In this frame, the parameters more closely mirror the properties of the observed photometric light-curve, such as the peak and width, at least for short-duration events with
t_{E,\earth_{r}}<50
days (Figure
5
).
Note that the convention in the microlensing literature is not a true geocentric reference frame and we will utilize
\earth_{r}
to indicate quantities in this new reference frame.
The reference time is commonly taken as the peak of the light curve or sometimes the
t_{0}
derived from an initial non-parallax fit.

To transform into this geo-
t_{r}
reference frame, a constant position and velocity offset are applied such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\earth_{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{\sun}(t)-\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{P}(t_{r})-(t-t_{r})\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})

(35)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}-\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r}),

(36)

where
\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})
is the instantaneous rate of change of the parallax vector at the reference time.
The observed source-lens separation in the geo-
t_{r}
frame can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\earth}(t)=

\displaystyle\;\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{0},\earth_{r}}+\left(\frac{t-t_{0,\earth_{r}}}{t_{E,\earth_{r}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}

\displaystyle-\pi_{E}\left[\boldsymbol{P}(t)-\boldsymbol{P}(t_{r})-(t-t_{r})\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})\right]

(37)

and is derived fully in Appendix §
C
.
Conversions between the heliocentric and geo-
t_{r}
frames are presented in Appendix §
B

including BAGLE convenience functions and parameterizations in the geo-
t_{r}
frame.
We note that BAGLE’s default
t_{0,\sun}
is in the heliocentric frame for most parameterizations and for internal storage, which is logical when incorporating astrometry into the models.

III.5
Finite Sources

The point-source, point-lens approximation is accurate for source stars with small angular radii of
\rho_{*}<<1
where
\rho_{*}=\theta_{S,*}/\theta_{E}
is the angular size of the source (
\theta_{S,*}=R_{S,*}/d_{S}
) in units of
\theta_{E}
and
R_{S,*}
is the physical radius of the star.
For larger stellar radii, finite-source effects may become important, calling for the use of a finite-source point-lens (FSPL) model. BAGLE currently models a finite source as a uniform disk shape. In this case, we can avoid costly inverse ray-shooting grid calculations and instead follow the contour integration approach presented by
17
. Note that when
u_{\odot}(t)\leq\rho_{*}
, either the source itself or the arbitrary points on its source radius approach the lens caustic. We found that this leads to skewed calculations of photometry and astrometry in regimes where
\boldsymbol{u}_{\sun}(t)\leq\rho_{*}
. To avoid erroneous results in such cases, we use an adaptive mesh grid in our contour integration calculations when
u_{\odot}\leq\rho_{*}
.

Comparison of the source’s centroid shift between a finite-source and a point-source event.

The true source position is represented by a circular outline of
n_{\rm outline}
points,
\boldsymbol{y}
, corresponding to the positions at each angle
\phi
around the star’s circumference, evenly spaced by
\Delta\phi
. Surface brightness is preserved in microlensing; thus, a uniformly-lit disk model will produce the same uniform surface brightness in the warped, lensed images.
Magnification can then be calculated as the total area of the lensed images divided by the area of the unlensed source.

The outlines of the major and minor image,
\boldsymbol{x_{\pm}}
, are computed by transforming each point in the true source outline contour to the source image outlines via the lens equation solutions given in Equation
7
. We refer to each point in the contour as
x_{I,i,j}
, where
I
indicates the image instance (
+
or
-
),
i
the point number (from 0 to
n_{\rm outline}
-1, inclusive), and
j
indicates the directional component (E=East or N=North).

Following the procedure outlined by
17
, we use Green’s theorem to convert the surface integral over the image to a line integral over the image boundary and compute the area of each image. To approximate this integral with a discrete set of image outline points via a trapezium approximation, we first follow their Equation (9), put in our terms as:

\Omega_{\pm}=\mp\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(x_{\pm,i+1,N}+x_{\pm,i,N})(x_{\pm,i+1,E}-x_{\pm,i,E}).

(38)

Following their Equation (10), we add a parabolic correction that takes into account local curvature:

d\Omega_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{24}\sum_{i}\left[(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}_{\pm})|_{\phi_{i}}+(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}_{\pm})|_{\phi_{i+1}}\right](\Delta\phi_{i})^{3},

(39)

where the primes indicate the derivative of
\boldsymbol{x}
in terms of
\phi
. This uses a wedge product, which for arbitrary 2-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
is,

\boldsymbol{v}\wedge\boldsymbol{w}=v_{0}w_{1}-v_{1}w_{0}.

(40)

From this, we can estimate the amplification of each image,

A_{\pm,\rm FS}=\frac{\Omega_{\pm}+d\Omega_{\pm}}{\Omega_{*}},

(41)

where
\Omega_{*}=\pi\theta_{*}^{2}
is the angular area of the star and
\theta_{*}
is the source radius in mas.

The total amplification is simply
A_{\rm FS}=A_{+,\rm FS}+A_{-,\rm FS}
. We present a comparison between the finite-source photometry and point-source photometry in
Figure 6
.

III.5.2
Finite Source Astrometry

We calculate the astrometric positions of each lensed image centroid via the
17
Equations (19-20), again using the Green’s theorem approximation via the discrete image outlines.
For each lensed image and dimension, we define

\displaystyle Y_{\pm,E}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i}(x_{\pm,i+1,E}+x_{\pm,i,E})^{2}(x_{\pm,i+1,N}-x_{\pm,i,N})

(42)

\displaystyle Y_{\pm,N}

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{1}{8}\sum_{i}(x_{\pm,i+1,N}+x_{\pm,i,N})^{2}(x_{\pm,i+1,E}-x_{\pm,i,E}).

(43)

Following their Equation (21) and (22), we add a parabolic correction to the above astrometric terms,

\displaystyle dY_{\pm,E}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{24}\sum_{i}\bigg[\bigg(x^{\prime 2}_{\pm,E}x^{\prime}_{\pm,N}+x_{\pm,E}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x^{\prime\prime}}_{\pm})\bigg)|_{\phi_{i}}

\displaystyle+\bigg(x^{\prime 2}_{\pm,E}x^{\prime}_{\pm,N}+x_{\pm,E}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x^{\prime\prime}}_{\pm})\bigg)|_{\phi_{i+1}}\bigg](\Delta\phi_{i})^{3}

(44)

\displaystyle dY_{\pm,N}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{24}\sum_{i}\bigg[\bigg(x^{\prime 2}_{\pm,N}x^{\prime}_{\pm,E}+x_{\pm,N}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x^{\prime\prime}}_{\pm})\bigg)|_{\phi_{i}}

\displaystyle+\bigg(x^{\prime 2}_{\pm,N}x^{\prime}_{\pm,E}+x_{\pm,N}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}_{\pm}\wedge\boldsymbol{x^{\prime\prime}}_{\pm})\bigg)|_{\phi_{i+1}}\bigg](\Delta\phi_{i})^{3}.

(45)

The centroid for each lensed image is then

\boldsymbol{X_{\pm,FS}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{Y_{\pm}}+\boldsymbol{dY_{\pm}}}{\Omega_{\pm}+d\Omega_{\pm}}

(46)

Overall, the flux-weighted image centroid (
\boldsymbol{X_{cent,FS,lensed}}
) for finite sources can be found using
\boldsymbol{X_{\pm,FS}}
,
A_{\pm,FS}
, the flux of the source (
F_{S}
), the flux of the lens(
F_{L}
) and the position of the lens
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\sun}
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X_{cent,FS,lensed}}=\frac{F_{L}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{L},\sun}\;+\displaystyle\sum_{I=\{+,-\}}F_{S}A_{I,FS}\boldsymbol{X_{I,FS}}}{F_{L}\;+\displaystyle\sum_{I=\{+,-\}}F_{S}A_{I,FS}}

(47)

We present a comparison between the centroid shift of a finite-source and point-source event in
Figure 7
.

V
BAGLE Model Fitting

BAGLE can simultaneously fit photometric and astrometric data using the PyMultiNest package
(
18
)
, which implements a nested sampling fitting technique.
Many parameters in microlensing introduce degeneracies and multi-modal solutions into the parameter space such as the degeneracy between
\pm u_{0}
.
We use nested sampling to fully explore degeneracies, find and separate multi-modal solutions, and fully sample the posterior probability distributions.
Nested sampling also has the advantage of returning the Bayesian evidence, which allows for hypothesis testing and comparisons across different model classes (e.g. with and without parallax).

In order to fit a microlensing event using BAGLE we instantiate a model class object to fit and one or more data set(s), where the data sets must be loaded into a data dictionary of type

The best-fit model parameters can be returned based on the maximum likelihood solution, the maximum aposteriori solution, or the mean or median of the marginalized 1D posterior probability distribution for each parameter.
In the mean case, parameter uncertainties are estimated as the standard deviation of the marginalized 1D posterior.
In the median case, 68.3%, 95.5% and 99.7% credible intervals are calculated from the marginalized 1D posterior.

The BAGLE model fitter includes three schemes for weighting different data sets. The default
MicrolensSolver
gives equal weight to each
point
in all the data sets. In some cases, e.g. when including astrometric data, which tends to have a significantly smaller data set, important data can be outweighed by the large data sets from ground-based microlensing surveys. In such a case, it may be optimal to instead use the
MicrolensSolverWeighted
, which has three options: ‘phot_ast_equal’, ‘all_equal’, or custom user-input weights. In the default case, ‘phot_ast_equal’, the total weighting of all photometric data sets is equal to the total weighting of all astrometric data sets. For ‘all_equal’, each data set is weighted equally, such that, e.g., one astrometric data set would be weighted equally to one photometric data set, regardless of the number of data points in each set or data sets per category. A user may alternatively input an array with a custom weight for each data set.

MicrolensSolver
and the alternatively weighted versions can be used to fit any BAGLE model.

Runtimes for the BAGLE model fitter are highly dependent on the data volume, data set number, and model complexity. For example, a PSPL model with parallax, one photometric data set, and reasonable but not extremely strict priors can run single-threaded in roughly a couple of minutes. Binary source, binary lens models with several photometric and astrometric data sets run multi-threaded can take days or even weeks to complete.
Example run-times in some simple cases are presented in §
VIII.5
.

Also note that the
MicrolensSolver
object has convenient likelihood and prior probability functions that can be based into alternate optimization engines such as
dynesty

(
54
)
and
emcee

(
25
)
, although we have found
pymultinest
to be the most robust and accurate.
Planned future work on BAGLE includes implementing a faster gradient-based fitter.

VIII.3
Results: Luminous Lenses

Luminous lenses can significantly complicate both the photometric and astrometric signal.
For photometry, blended light raises the background base flux level and can even prevent detection of the microlensing event except at the very peak.
This is especially relevant in ground-based, seeing-limited photometric surveys with spatial resolutions of
\sim
1” where there are typically multiple stars within a 1” patch at visible wavelengths towards the Galactic bulge.
Figure
18
(top panel) shows the impact of different amounts of blending,
b_{sff}
, on photometric lightcurves.

Astrometric trajectories are impacted by blending, even in the absence of gravitational lensing.
Figure
18
(middle, bottom panels) shows the impact of blending on the observed trajectory.
Even well before the microlensing event occurs, the observed position is the flux-weighted centroid of the light from both the source and lens (and possible luminous companions).
If such a trajectory were fit with a typical motion model with proper motion and parallax, the resulting proper motion and parallax would not reflect that of either the source or lens.
Thus an observer must take care to account for blending in deriving baseline quantities well before and after the lensing event.
As long as the source and lens remain unresolved, both must be modeled in baseline using the
b_{sff}
derived during the microlensing event.

Figure
19
shows how the observed trajectory on the sky becomes even more complex with the addition of lensing.
For especially long-duration events, where the annual parallax cycle is repeated multiple times during the event (i.e.
t_{E}>2
years), the parallax signal seen during the event is dramatically impacted and is not representative of either the lens or source parallax.

IX
Conclusion & Future Directions

We have presented the BAGLE microlensing event modeling and fitting package, which jointly models and fits photometry and astrometry. This paper presents the point-source, point-lens and uniform-source, point-lens model implementations.
The BAGLE implementations of Gaussian processes and binary source and lens models are beyond the scope of this paper and will be presented in

19
and
12
, respectively.

We presented the model parameters, equations, and fitting procedures and their relationship to alternative parameterizations in the literature.
Notably, BAGLE works in a coordinate system anchored to the Solar System Barycenter, which allows for robust estimation of astrometric trajectories on sky in addition to the photometric lightcurves that most other packages produce.
The lightcurves and trajectories output from BAGLE are in broad agreement with other packages, although BAGLE’s parallax model is more accurate thanks to reliance on JPL ephemerids for Earth’s position.
In parallax cases, the differences between lightcurves across the packages are large enough, in some cases, to be detectable in Roman GBTDS observations.

BAGLE’s model-fitting runtimes are comparable to VBMicrolensing/RTModel and MulensModel on a single event and faster than pyLIMA.
Simulation of large numbers of events at different sky coordinates is
5-400\times
faster with BAGLEfor all point-source, point-lens models.
In the future, we plan to implement automatic differentiation to increase BAGLE’s fitting efficiency and allow for the use of GPU-accelerated inference packages such as PyTorch or TensorFlow.

Thus far, the BAGLE joint photometric-astrometric fitting capabilities have been published for a relatively small set of ground-based photometric light curves with slow-cadence (a couple of points per year), ground-based adaptive optics or space-based astrometric follow-up.
The package is now used routinely to simulate microlensing lightcurves for Rubin and Roman
(
1
;
20
)
.
With the Roman Space Telescope’s launch approaching, BAGLE’s robust support for photometry and astrometry will be critical to leverage the influx of simultaneous, high-cadence photometric and astrometric microlensing data and study the properties and prevalence of isolated stellar mass black holes.

\displaystyle\theta_{E}

\displaystyle=t_{E,\sun}\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\sun}=t_{E,\earth_{r}}\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r}}

(B1)

\displaystyle t_{E,\earth_{r}}

\displaystyle=t_{E,\sun}\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\sun}}{|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}-\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})|}.

\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}}{\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}}{\pi_{E}t_{E,\earth_{r}}}

\displaystyle=t_{E,\sun}\frac{\frac{1}{\pi_{E}t_{E,\sun}}}{\left|\frac{\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}}{\pi_{E}t_{E,\sun}}-\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})\right|}

(B7)

\displaystyle t_{E,\earth_{r}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{E}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r}}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{E}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\sun}-\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})}{\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{E,\earth_{r}}}{t_{E,\sun}}\left(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{E,\sun}-\pi_{E}^{2}t_{E,\sun}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})\right)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{E,\earth_{r}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{E}\frac{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{E,\sun}-\pi_{E}^{2}t_{E,\sun}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})}{|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{E,\sun}-\pi_{E}^{2}t_{E,\sun}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})|}

(B9)

and the amplitude
\pi_{E}
is invariant.

Appendix D
Derivation for
t_{0,\earth_{r}}

We solve for the closest approach time in the geocentric(
t_{r}
) frame by finding when the time derivative of
|\boldsymbol{u}_{\earth_{r}}|
is zero,

\displaystyle\frac{d|\boldsymbol{u}_{\earth_{r}}|^{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle=2u_{\earth_{r},E}\frac{du_{\earth_{r},E}}{dt}+2u_{\earth_{r},N}\frac{du_{\earth_{r},N}}{dt}=0,

(D1)

which gives

\displaystyle u_{\earth_{r},E}\frac{du_{\earth_{r},E}}{dt}=-u_{\earth_{r},N}\frac{du_{\earth_{r},N}}{dt}

(D2)

\displaystyle\frac{u_{\earth_{r},E}\;\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},E}}{\theta_{E}}=-\frac{u_{\earth_{r},N}\;\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},N}}{\theta_{E}}

(D3)

We plug in and solve for
t_{0,\earth_{r}}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},E}\left[u_{0,\sun,E}+\left(\frac{t_{0,\earth_{r}}-t_{0,\sun}}{\theta_{E}}\right)\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},E}-\pi_{E}P_{E}(t_{r})-(t_{0,\sun}-t_{r})\pi_{E}\dot{P}_{E}(t_{r})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt=-\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},N}\left[u_{0,\sun,N}+\left(\frac{t_{0,\earth_{r}}-t_{0,\sun}}{\theta_{E}}\right)\mu_{\mathrm{rel},\earth_{r},N}-\pi_{E}P_{N}(t_{r})-(t_{0,\sun}-t_{r})\pi_{E}\dot{P}_{N}(t_{r})\right]

(D4)

and finally

\displaystyle t_{0,\earth_{r}}=t_{0,\sun}-t_{E,\earth_{r}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{rel}},\earth_{r}}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{0},\sun}-\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{P}(t_{r})-(t_{0,\sun}-t_{r})\pi_{E}\boldsymbol{\dot{P}}(t_{r})\right]

(D5)

Derivation for Astrometric Blending

Here, we calculate the astrometric shift due to microlensing for a luminous source, luminous lens, and blending from neighbors. Figure

The relevant parameters are defined in Table
7
. These parameters involve the solutions to the lens equation, which we can write as:

\boldsymbol{X}_{\pm,S}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\frac{\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}{u}\right)\boldsymbol{X_{S}}.

The magnification of each image is then:

A_{1,2}=A_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{2}+\frac{u^{2}+2}{2u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}.

The centroid of the unlensed system is

\boldsymbol{X_{cent,unlensed}}=\frac{F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}.

The centroid of the lensed system is shown below, in the case where the lens is at the origin (
\boldsymbol{X}_{L}=0
).

\boldsymbol{X_{cent,lensed}}=\frac{A_{1}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X}_{+,S}+A_{2}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X}_{-,S}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{A_{1}F_{S}+A_{2}F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}.

Thus, the observed centroid shift
\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,obs}}
is

\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,obs}}=\boldsymbol{X_{cent,unlensed}}-\boldsymbol{X_{cent,lensed}}=\frac{A_{1}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X}_{+,S}+A_{2}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X}_{-,S}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{A_{1}F_{S}+A_{2}F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}-\frac{F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}.

We can re-parameterize the source position in terms of
u
. For the numerator, the directional vectors matter:

A_{1}\boldsymbol{X}_{+,S}+A_{2}\boldsymbol{X}_{-,S}=\frac{u^{2}+3}{\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}\theta_{E}\hat{\boldsymbol{X_{S}}}=\frac{u^{2}+3}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}.

For the total flux, we only need the total magnification,

A_{1}+A_{2}=\frac{u^{2}+2}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}.

Thus we can rewrite the observed centroid shift
\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,obs}}
as

\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,obs}}=\frac{\frac{u^{2}+3}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{\frac{u^{2}+2}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}-\frac{F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}\boldsymbol{X_{N,i}}}{F_{S}+F_{L}+\sum_{i}F_{N,i}}.

(E1)

Next, we examine this result in some limiting cases.

E.1
Limit: Dark Lens, No Neighbors

For a dark lens and no luminous, blended neighbors
(
F_{L}=F_{N}=0
), Equation
E1
becomes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{u^{2}+3}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}}{\frac{u^{2}+2}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}}-\frac{F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}}{F_{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u^{2}+2}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}.

The resulting magnitude of the shift,

\delta_{c,S}=\frac{u}{u^{2}+2}\theta_{E},

is in agreement with Equation (15) from
22
.

E.2
Limit: Luminous Lens, No Neighbors

Next, we consider a luminous lens but no luminous neighbors (
F_{N}=0
). We will define
g=F_{L}/F_{S}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta_{c,LL}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{u^{2}+3}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}}{\frac{u^{2}+2}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}F_{S}+F_{L}}-\frac{F_{S}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}}{F_{S}+F_{L}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\frac{\frac{u^{2}+3}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}}{\frac{u^{2}+2}{u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}+g}-\frac{1}{1+g}\Bigg]\boldsymbol{X_{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+g(u^{2}+3-u\sqrt{u^{2}+4})}{(1+g)(u^{2}+2+gu\sqrt{u^{2}+4})}\boldsymbol{X_{S}}.

Then the magnitude of the astrometric shift with a luminous lens is

\delta_{c,LL}=\frac{u}{1+g}\frac{1+g(u^{2}-u\sqrt{u^{2}+4}+3)}{u^{2}+2+gu\sqrt{u^{2}+4}}\theta_{E},

which is in agreement with Equation (98) from
22
.
