Counting and building operators in theories with hidden symmetries and application to HEFT Preprint: IPPP/24/80
Rodrigo Alonso, and Shakeel Ur Rahaman

Abstract
Identifying a full basis of operators to a given order is key to the generality of Effective Field Theory (EFT) and is by now a problem of known solution in terms of the Hilbert series. The present work is concerned with hidden symmetry in general and Higgs EFT in particular and (i) connects the counting formula presented in Graf:2022rco in the CCWZ formulation with the linear frame and makes this connection explicit in HEFT (ii) outlines the differences in perturbation theory in each frame (iii) presents a new counting formula with measure in the full S ​ U ​ ( 3 ) × S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) × U ⁡ ( 1 ) SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1) group for HEFT and (iv) provides a Mathematica code that produces the number of operators at the user-specified order in HEFT.

2
Hilbert series in QFT

In this section we aim to provide an accessible and intuitive introduction to the use of Hilbert series in operator counting which will also serve to outline results that this work is based on. The exposition might differ from other works but not the content itself which can be found in e.g.
Henning:2017fpj
with more rigour and comprehensiveness.

At the centre of this method lies the character orthogonality theorem. Consider a group
G
, a group element
g
, and irreps
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
; one has that the group average over the product of characters
\chi
is orthogonal, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\chi^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\;,

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mbox{Tr}(g_{\mathcal{R}})\;,

(1)

where
V_{G}
is the volume of the group (number of elements if discreet, integral of the Haar measure if continuous) and the subindex
\mathcal{R}
in
g_{\mathcal{R}}
denotes the group element as a matrix in
\mathcal{R}
space. For any two representations one has that (a)
\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\Sigma_{\rm irreps}\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}
while if the two representations correspond to distinct fields, one has further (b)
\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
as one can check explicitly. Given (a) one can use the orthogonality theorem in eq. (
1
) to obtain the decomposition in irreducible representations of the combination
\mathcal{R}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{2}
while (b) gives an explicit form for the average to be evaluated.
As an example, consider
N
different fields each in a representation
\mathcal{R}_{i}
; the answer to the question, how many invariants can be built out of their product is
n_{I}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}

\displaystyle\otimes\mathcal{R}_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{R}_{N}=(n_{I}\times\mathbf{1})\oplus\dots\;,

(2)

\displaystyle n_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\chi_{\mathbf{1}}^{*}\prod_{i}\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}=\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\prod_{i}\chi_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}\;,

(3)

where we have used
(\chi_{\mathbf{1}})^{*}=1
with
\mathbf{1}
the singlet representation in eq. (
1
). A key assumption so far was distinguishable representations, let us see where the formula above fails when this is not the case. Take for example
G=SO(2)
and the square of a field
\phi
in the vector representation,

n_{I}=\frac{1}{V_{SO(2)}}\int d\mu_{SO(2)}\chi_{\mathbf{1}}^{*}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta(\chi_{\mathcal{R}})^{2}=\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})^{2}=2\,.

This counting returns two invariants built with
\phi^{2}
, yet we know there is one only. The offending term is
\epsilon^{ij}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
which trivially vanishes, so the solution is to take the symmetric product only.

In order to build the character of the symmetric product, it is useful to rotate to the Cartan basis where
g
is diagonal and
(g_{\mathcal{R}})_{ii}=x_{i}
with
x_{i}
parametrising the Cartan subalgebra which generates
[U(1)]^{\rm{rank}\,G}
; for the
SO(2)
example we would have
x_{1}=e^{i\theta}
,
x_{2}=e^{-i\theta}
. Here the action of the group element on the product

\displaystyle[g(\phi\otimes\phi^{\prime})]_{ij}=

\displaystyle(g_{\mathcal{R}})_{ii}(g_{\mathcal{R}})_{jj}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}^{\prime}=x_{i}x_{j}\phi_{\{i}\phi_{j\}}^{\prime}+x_{i}x_{j}\phi_{[i}\phi_{j]}^{\prime}

(4)

\displaystyle\chi_{\textrm{sym}\mathcal{R}^{2}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq j}x_{i}x_{j}

(5)

If we had instead three
\mathcal{R}
representations, the character is the ordered sum (
i\leq j\leq k
), and so forth for higher terms. All products can be in fact neatly packed into a single generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\chi_{\textrm{sym}([\mathcal{R}]^{n})}\phi^{n}=\prod_{i}(1+x_{i}\phi+(x_{i}\phi)^{2}+\dots)=\prod_{i}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}\phi}=\det[(1-g_{\mathcal{R}}\phi)^{-1}]\,,

(6)

where we have used
\phi
as a bookkeeping device, a practice common in the literature and throughout the rest of this work.
This expression can be cast as a Pleythistic exponential, defined as

\displaystyle\det(1-g_{\mathcal{R}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\textrm{Exp}\left(-\textrm{tr}[\log(1-g_{\mathcal{R}})]\right)=\textrm{Exp}\left(\sum\textrm{tr}(g_{\mathcal{R}}^{n})/n\right)\equiv\textrm{PE}[\chi_{\mathcal{R}}](x_{i})\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\textrm{PE}[f](x)

\displaystyle\equiv\textrm{Exp}\left(\sum_{n=1}f(x^{n})/n\right)\,.

(8)

One can then count singlet representations, i.e. invariants, by the formula

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\det(1-\phi g_{\mathcal{R}})^{-1}=\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\textrm{PE}[\chi\phi](x_{i},\phi)=\sum_{I}n_{I}\phi^{I}\;\;.

(9)

This expression captures the counting of invariants for any number of representations
\mathcal{R}
marked by the power of
\phi
; i.e. this polynomial in
\phi
has as coefficient of the
I
’th power the number of invariants with
I
representations. The name given to this function of
\phi
is the HS. It gives a name to this operator counting method since it is the central object containing all the information we are after. In order to evaluate the integral for arbitrary
\phi
, it is often useful to use the determinant form and the residue theorem. For our applications however, solving the integral in general is not possible and instead one expands on the field and finds the coefficient of interest.

The extension of the Hilbert series to one more field is straightforward, there will be one more Pleythistic in the integrand

\displaystyle H(\phi_{\mathcal{R}},\varphi_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}})=n_{k,l}\phi^{k}\varphi^{l}=\frac{1}{V_{G}}\sum_{g}\textrm{PE}[\phi\chi_{\mathcal{R}}](x_{i},\phi)\times\textrm{PE}[\varphi\chi_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}](x_{i},\varphi)\;,

(10)

where
n_{k,l}
is the number of invariants that can be built off of
k

\phi
-fields and
l

\varphi
-fields.

A couple of generalisations will be useful

•

Fermions
If the field
\psi
anti-commutes we should select instead fully antisymmetrised combinations, these are indeed finite and collected in
\det(1+g\psi)
, which when written in terms of an exponential, brings a sign change

\displaystyle\textrm{PE}[f]_{\psi}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\textrm{Exp}\left(-\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n}f(x^{n})/n\right)\;.

(11)

•

Flavour
The case in which
n_{f}
of our representations are distinct but in the same representation, one has the product of
n_{f}
pleythistic exponentials;

\displaystyle\left(\textrm{PE}[f](x)\right)^{n_{f}}

\displaystyle\equiv\textrm{Exp}\left(n_{f}\sum_{n=1}f(x^{n})/n\right)\;,

(12)

and one can give the counting as a function of
n_{f}
.

2.1
Free field case

The discussion so far did not involve the field nature of our representations, i.e. the spacetime dependence. This field nature is reflected in the fact that
\partial\phi
is an independent object to build invariants with and one with different transformation properties under the Lorentz group, which for the purposes of counting can be taken as the compactified version
SO(d)
for
d
dimensions.

Consider the series of derivatives arranged in an infinite-dimensional array,

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\phi\,,&\partial_{\mu}\phi\,,&\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\,,&\dots\end{array}\right)^{T}\;.

Let us denote by
\Box
the vector Lorentz representation (in
SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R}
notation the
(1/2,1/2)
), the characters of the first two terms in
\mathscr{R}
are
\chi_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\chi_{\mathcal{R}}\chi_{\Box}

\partial_{[\mu}\partial_{\nu]}=0

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}\equiv\sum_{n}(\partial)^{n}\chi_{\mathcal{R}\times\textrm{sym}(\Box)^{n}}=\chi_{\mathcal{R}}\sum_{n}(\partial)^{n}\chi_{\textrm{sym}\Box^{n}}=\chi_{\mathcal{R}}\det(1-\partial g_{\Box})^{-1}\;,

(14)

where
\partial
acts here as another bookkeeping device

1

In the literature one might find

\displaystyle(\det(1-\partial g_{\Box})^{-1}\phi)^{2}\mbox{Tr}(g_{\textrm{sym}\mathcal{R}^{2}})\;.

(15)

This formula, as can be easiest checked in

\displaystyle\prod_{i}(1+x_{i}\phi+(x_{i}\phi)^{2}+\dots)\prod_{j}(1+x^{\prime}_{j}\partial\phi+(x^{\prime}_{j}\partial\phi)^{2}+\dots)\prod_{k}(1+x^{\prime\prime}_{k}\partial^{2}\phi+(x^{\prime\prime}_{k}\partial^{2}\phi)^{2}+\dots)

\displaystyle\det(1-g_{\mathcal{R}}\phi)^{-1}\det(1-g_{\mathcal{R}\times\Box}\partial\phi)^{-1}\det(1-g_{\mathcal{R}\times\textrm{sym}\Box^{2}}\partial^{2}\phi)^{-1}\dots=\det(1-\hat{g}_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}\phi)^{-1}\;,

\displaystyle g_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}\mathscr{R}_{\phi}=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}g_{\mathcal{R}}&&&\\

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&&&\\

For the Hilbert series, one can use the determinant form or the PE using the trace-log formula to obtain a function of the character as

\displaystyle H(q,\phi,\partial)=\frac{1}{V_{G}V_{SO(d)}}\int d\mu_{G}\int d\mu_{SO(d)}\textrm{PE}[\phi\chi_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}\chi_{G}](\phi,\partial,x,y)\;,

2.2
IBP, EoM and CFT

Counting operators in field theory has led naturally to consider the infinite-dimensional representation
\mathscr{R}_{\phi}
assembled out of field derivatives and hence highly reducible under
SO(d)
. Given it is treated as a whole however the question arises: could an encompassing group have this representation as an irrep? On the other hand, redundancies in the QFT formulation imply that the same physics encoded in the whole set of operators that formula (
29
) counts is described by a subset of them. As math would have it, rather enthrallingly, these two points are connected through the conformal group.

Let us first pursue the group that would present
\mathscr{R}_{\phi}
as an irrep. The starting realisation is that the action of the derivative itself is encoded in a raising ladder operator connecting the different Lorentz irreps, take
P_{\mu}=-i\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle i\epsilon_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}\mathscr{R}_{\phi}=\epsilon_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\mathscr{R}_{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&\epsilon^{\mu}&&\\
&0&\eta^{\mu\nu}\epsilon^{\rho}&\\
&&0&\\
&&&\dots\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\phi\\
\partial_{\mu}\phi\\
\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\rho}\phi\\
\dots\end{array}\right)\;,

and the exponentiated form would shift
\mathscr{R}_{\phi(x)}\to\mathscr{R}_{\phi(x+\epsilon)}
.
In analogy with spin, we can assume that a ladder operator comes from the complex-coefficient combination of generators

\displaystyle P_{\mu}=

\displaystyle-i\partial_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{\mu}+i\tilde{L}_{\mu}\right)\;,

\displaystyle K_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{\mu}-i\tilde{L}_{\mu}\right)\;,

(38)

Now
K
in matrix form is a lowering ladder operator and as such has the inverse units of
\partial
, that is
[K]=[x]
. Equivalently through its action on spacetime
i\epsilon P
sends
x\to x+\epsilon
so
i\varepsilon_{\mu}K^{\mu}
will schematically send
x\to x+x^{2}\varepsilon
since
[\varepsilon]=[K^{-1}]=[x^{-1}]
. The explicit expression
K_{\mu}=c_{\mu\rho\sigma\kappa}x^{\rho}x^{\sigma}\partial^{\kappa}
follows; assuming the dimensionless tensor
c
is made out of the metric one can find its form by demanding
[K,K]
be closed to be

\displaystyle K_{\mu}=\frac{i}{2}\left(x^{2}\partial_{\mu}-2x_{\mu}x\partial\right)\;.

(39)

The commutation of
K,P
does return the action of the Lorentz group (
\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}\equiv ix_{[\nu}\partial_{\mu]}
) but also a new generator

\displaystyle[K_{\mu},P_{\nu}]

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{J}_{\mu\nu}-2i\eta_{\mu\nu}\textrm{D}\;,

D

\displaystyle\equiv ix^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}\;.

(40)

The Lie algebra so unearthed is now complete and looking at its entirety one finds themselves contemplating the conformal group
SO(d,2)
. This group has rank one unit higher than
SO(d)
, the extra non-compact element of the Cartan-subalgebra being dilations D with parameter
q
(in analogy with
x_{i}
), now crucially a real number rather than a phase.

The advantages of finding a group in this vast space are twofold, first one recovers character orthogonality for full representations; for this we can split the conformal group Haar measure when compactified (
SO(d+2)
) and in the Cartan basis as

\displaystyle\int d\mu_{SO(d)}\oint_{|q|=1}\frac{dq}{2\pi iq}\prod_{i<r}\left(1-x_{i}q\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{x_{i}q}\right)\left(1-\frac{x_{i}}{q}\right)\left(1-\frac{q}{x_{i}}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\mu_{SO(d)}\int\frac{dq}{2\pi i}\det(1-qg_{\Box})[\det(1-qg_{\Box})]^{*}\;,

(42)

while the trace in this space includes the parameter
q
as

\displaystyle\chi_{SO(d,2)}=q^{\Delta}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}\det(1-g_{\Box}q)^{-1}=q^{\Delta}\chi_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}(\partial\to q)\;,

(43)

replacing the somewhat awkward insertion of
\partial
in the previous section by a group-defined parameter
q
which also marks the dimension of our field,
\Delta
. Further, the integral over two characters reads

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{SO(d)}}\int d\mu_{SO(d)}\oint\frac{dq}{2\pi iq}\det(1-qg_{\Box})[\det(1-qg_{\Box})]^{*}\chi_{SO(d,2)}^{*}\chi_{SO(d,2)}^{\prime}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{SO(d)}}\int d\mu_{SO(d)}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\chi_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\oint\frac{dq}{2\pi iq}q^{\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}}=\delta_{\Delta\Delta^{\prime}}\int\frac{d\mu_{SO(d)}}{V_{SO(d)}}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\chi_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\;,

(45)

so we recover group orthogonality and a parameter that allows the selection of mass dimension in
q
.

It is useful nonetheless to keep track of derivatives, so let us from now on use:

\displaystyle\chi_{SO(d,2)}=q^{\Delta}\chi_{\mathcal{R}}\det(1-g_{\Box}\partial q)^{-1}=q^{\Delta}\chi_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}(\partial\to\partial q)\,.

(46)

The second advantage of the conformal group, this time a special property rather than a shared feature, is that the derivative operator will be of the ladder form on any representation. As such any combination of fields and derivatives that can be written in the form of a total derivative will feature in the lower components of a
SO(d+2)
representation but never on the first element, denoted primary operator. Primary operators are then by construction operators that cannot be given as a total derivative and using
SO(d+2)
as our group not only counts mass dimension but discards total derivatives implementing integration by parts (IBP) relations.

The orthogonality theorem then can be used to project on singlets of mass dimension
\Delta
with effective measure (where we substitute
q\to q\partial
as in eq. (
46
)

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\mu_{SO(d)}}{V_{SO(d)}}\oint\frac{dq}{2\pi iq}\det(1-q\partial g_{\Box})[\det(1-q\partial g_{\Box})]^{*}\chi_{SO(d,2)_{\Delta,\mathbf{1}}}^{*}\textrm{PE}[\chi_{SO(d,2)}]

(47)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\mu_{SO(d)}}{V_{SO(d)}}\oint\frac{dq}{2\pi iq}q^{-\Delta}\det(1-q\partial g_{\Box})\textrm{PE}[\chi_{SO(d,2)}]\;.

(48)

The
q
integral we will in fact not perform and instead
q
itself is turned into a bookkeeping device to signal mass dimension as

\displaystyle H(q,\phi,\partial)=\int[d\bar{\mu}(q\partial)]\textrm{PE}[\phi\chi_{SO(d,2)}\chi_{G}](\phi,q,\partial,\{x_{i}\})\;,

(49)

where we have defined the measure

\displaystyle[d\bar{\mu}(q\partial)]\equiv\frac{d\mu_{G}}{V_{G}}\frac{d\mu_{SO(d)}}{V_{SO(d)}}\det(1-q\partial g_{\Box})\;,

(50)

compared with eq. (
29
) it is the determinant factor addition to the measure that takes care of IBP.

Lastly EoM, or more in general field redefinitions, allow for the removal of certain terms. For a scalar, one can choose terms proportional to
\partial^{2}\phi
to be discarded. The conformal group has in fact something to say here too. For representations that saturate the unitarity bound components proportional to

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{R}}_{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\phi\\
\partial_{\mu}\phi\\
(\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}-\frac{1}{d}\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial^{2})\phi\\
\dots\end{array}\right)\;,

and one can derive

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}=\mbox{Tr}(g_{\tilde{\mathscr{R}}})=\chi_{\mathscr{R}_{\phi}}(1-(q\partial)^{2})\,,

(55)

since the subtraction of EoM can be thought of as removing
(q\partial)^{2}
acting on the representation
\mathscr{R}_{\phi}
.
The objects so obtained are true representations closed under composition so one can decompose a product in terms of
\tilde{\chi}
characters,
\tilde{\chi}_{\mathscr{R}}\tilde{\chi}_{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\rm irreps}\tilde{\chi}_{i}
. In this basis however we have lost orthogonality so the formula that counted invariants requires modification. The extra step can be understood with linear algebra and projection on a non-orthogonal basis, considering the equivalence

3.1
The CCWZ frame

The case of HEFT has that

The building of our set of operators is then straightforward but when it comes to quantizing, i.e. choosing the interpolating fields that excite one particle states, we will distinguish two possibilities. The two procedures are physically equivalent and yield the same

Summary

Characterising the scalar boson discovered at the LHC is the key to complete our understanding of electroweak symmetry breaking. Current and future experimental data requires for its interpretation a theory as general as possible so as to leave no possibility unexplored. EFT offers this generality when one counts all operators to a given order. This counting problem has a known solution in terms of the Hilbert Series, including the case of spontaneous symmetry breaking necessitated for application to EWSB. The theory framework to describe SSB, ‘phenomenological’ Lagrangians a.k.a. CCWZ, has long been known yet in the most general EFT to describe EWSB Lagrangians in a different frame, the linear one, has been traditionally used. In this sense the use of the CCWZ frame became evident when an unambiguous counting formula was first made explicit in
Graf:2022rco
.

The present work explored the counting and connection between the CCWZ and linear frames in general and for HEFT in particular. Either frame captures the same physics so the researcher can choose at their own discretion yet we found each has certain advantages. The CCWZ frame is the frame for unambiguously counting operators and presents fields which pick up a phase in the unbroken group under a broken rotation but do not mix with one another. The linear frame displays the limit of a renormalisable theory most clearly yet the perturbative expansion obscures the broken-gauge invariance of physical states. For the case of HEFT we found it possible to perform the counting in the linear frame, presented the new formula in eq. (
173
) and underlined that it is the special property of filled up linear representations (including the Maurer-Cartan form in the linear frame) that affords this. Lastly, this work presented a Mathematica code to count operators in HEFT for the three counting formulae of eqs. (
182
,
183
, and
184
) as well as SMEFT to any order provided enough computer time.
