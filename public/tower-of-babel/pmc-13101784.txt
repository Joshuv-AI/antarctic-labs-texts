Advancements in nanomaterial-based adjuvants for animal vaccines
Authors: Linyi Dai, Lei Yang, Vitalii Nedosekov, Jing Ma, Weihuan Fang, Huapeng Feng, Jianhong Shu, Yulong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing adjuvants with immunostimulatory effects is crucial for enhancing the efficacy of various vaccines, controlling the outbreak and prevalence of animal infectious diseases. The rapid advancement of nanomaterials in recent years has opened new avenues for developing veterinary vaccine adjuvants. Adjuvants prepared from nanomaterials offer multiple advantages difficult to achieve with traditional adjuvants. This review summarizes the immunological mechanisms of nanoadjuvants, focusing on recent advances in inorganic nanomaterials, organic and polymeric nanomaterials, as well as hybrid and composite nanomaterials as potential veterinary vaccine adjuvants. It categorizes various nanomaterial adjuvants based on target species, diseases, and administration routes, and provides design recommendations, where appropriate, for the selection of appropriate adjuvant platforms tailored to specific species, pathogens, vaccine types, and delivery methods.

Introduction

Veterinary vaccines underpin animal health and livestock productivity by reducing the risk and impact of infectious disease outbreaks across species [1]. Vaccines function by stimulating the innate immune system and inducing robust immune responses. To enhance efficacy, adjuvants are incorporated into vaccine formulations to amplify the animal's immune responses. Adjuvant is defined as a component in a vaccine that can enhance the intensity, breadth and duration of the immune response [2,3]. It can be classified into two types: immunostimulants and delivery systems [4]. While lacking antigenic components themselves, adjuvants aid vaccines in enhancing immune responses by improving antigen stability, amplifying and prolonging the immune system's response to antigens to boost vaccine efficacy, and reducing doses and administration frequency [5,6]. The most widely used adjuvant types in veterinary vaccines today remain aluminum and oil emulsion adjuvants [7,8]. However, aluminum adjuvants have limited physicochemical properties and potential biosafety concerns. Furthermore, they primarily activate humoral immunity. This limitation severely restricts their application in vaccines targeting viral infections and other conditions where T-cell immunity plays a dominant role [9,10]. Other adjuvants also face constraints, including significant adverse effects, high synthesis costs, unclear chemical structures, and the potential to induce excessive inflammatory responses [11,12]. Besides, traditional adjuvant types have proven increasingly inadequate in addressing the emergence of various infectious diseases. The immune evasion mechanisms of pathogens causing infectious diseases, such as foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), are complex and relying solely on humoral immunity is insufficient for effective prevention [13]. Therefore, demand for novel adjuvants capable of simultaneously coordinating humoral and cellular immunity has grown steadily. Developing novel adjuvants has become a primary objective for many researchers, particularly in the field of veterinary vaccines. The key challenge lies in balancing immunogenicity, safety and cost-effectiveness when designing new adjuvants.

The rapid advancement of nanotechnology has provided novel approaches to addressing these challenges. Nanomaterials typically consist of nanoparticles (NPs) with dimensions of around 100 nm. Compared to their bulk counterparts, nanoparticles exhibit smaller volumes and larger surface areas, endowing them with high tunability and unique physicochemical properties [14]. These unique properties enable them to play distinctive roles in veterinary vaccines. Nanomaterials can efficiently load antigens through electrostatic adsorption, hydrophobic interactions or covalent coupling to form vaccine complexes. This loading method ensures that antigens and adjuvants are taken up by the same immune cells, resolving the issue of inconsistent spatiotemporal distribution between the two components in traditional mixed vaccines, which would significantly enhance the vaccine's immunogenicity [15,16]. Second, the size of antigen-binding nanomaterials resembles that of most viral pathogens. This characteristic enables them to mimic natural infection processes, facilitating more efficient recognition and uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [17]. Certain nanomaterials can activate the immune system by promoting the maturation, activation and antigen presentation of APCs, while some inherently possess the ability to activate innate immune pathways, such as those governing expression of interferon genes, which could regulate the intensity and types of immune responses [18]. Significant progress has been made in the application of nanomaterials across various fields, including animal vaccinology, antimicrobial agents, animal reproductive therapy, disease diagnostics, etc. [19]. In the field of veterinary medicine, various diseases mainly concentrate in the respiratory system and the digestive system. The mucosal surfaces of these systems are the main sites for pathogen infection and are also ideal locations for vaccine administration [20]. Nanoadjuvants play a significant role in the development of mucosal vaccines by protecting antigens, enhancing antigen uptake, and targeting immune cells [21]. Nanomaterials not only enhance antigen immunogenicity but also enable controlled antigen release and offer manageable biosafety profiles [1,22]. These characteristics establish them as an ideal platform for next-generation adjuvants, thus providing solutions to several major challenges facing modern veterinary vaccines.

This paper systematically reviews advances on veterinary nanoadjuvants in recent years from materials, mechanisms, animal species tested, administration routes, antibody responses and protection efficacy in field trials. Design and evaluation strategies for inorganic, organic and hybrid platforms as well as issues related to residues, biosafety and manufacturability for food animal uses are also covered. We aim to provide insights for rationally selecting nanomaterial platforms tailored to specific application scenarios, thereby accelerating the translation of nanomaterial adjuvants from laboratory research to practical use.

Immunological mechanisms of nanoadjuvants

The ability of nanoadjuvants to significantly enhance vaccine immunogenicity is primarily attributed to their multidimensional, multilevel immunomodulatory properties. Compared to traditional adjuvants, nanomaterials not only optimize antigen delivery and presentation processes but also achieve more precise, efficient, and durable immune protection by regulating key nodes in innate and adaptive immune responses. Specifically, nanoadjuvants operate through the following core immunological mechanisms: Utilizing their carrier properties to form antigen reservoirs, enabling sustained antigen release and prolonged stimulation; acting as immunostimulants to directly activate innate immune signaling pathways such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and cGAS-STING, providing critical signals for adaptive immune initiation; regulating APC maturation and cytokine environments, guiding Th1/Th2 balance and cytotoxic T cell responses to modulate the type and intensity of adaptive immunity; and promoting cross-presentation of antigens crucial for inducing potent cellular immunity. These mechanisms could operate synergistically, forming the immunological basis of enhanced vaccine efficacy by rationally designed high-performance adjuvants.

Enhancing antigen delivery and targeting

After entering the body, the primary task of antigens is to be transported through lymphatic vessels into lymph nodes (LN). During this process, larger particles (>100 nm) are typically captured by migratory dendritic cells (DCs) within tissues, which then migrate to LN via afferent lymphatic vessels for antigen presentation. Smaller particles (10–100 nm) can directly drain into LN through interstitial spaces in capillary lymphatic endothelium. Both delivery mechanisms prevent antigen clearance by peripheral tissues (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, within the LN, they participate in a series of immune responses, prompting antibody-secreting plasma cells and effector T cells to exit the LN via efferent lymphatic vessels. These cells then enter the bloodstream or migrate to the infection site to achieve the desired immune effect [23,24]. This process is the core mechanism by which vaccines activate specific immune responses, directly influencing antibody production and T-cell killing efficacy. Designing carriers for optimal antigen delivery could enhance antigen utilization efficiency and immunogenicity. Certain nanoadjuvants promote the antigen uptake by APCs and achieve lymph node targeting by mimicking the size and shape of pathogens. Cui et al. [25] designed a virus-mimicking nanovaccine composed of chitosan using microfluidic technology. Modified with heparin sulfate (SH) to mimic the outer structure and composition of viruses, this vaccine—termed SH/CS-OVA—exhibited a threefold increase in mucus layer penetration capability and a 4.8-fold improvement in evading macrophage phagocytosis. Additionally, it significantly enhanced in vivo DC recruitment and uptake, promoted DC maturation, prolonged antigen retention in the lungs, and facilitated rapid drainage to LN. Furthermore, materials such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and oil-in-water emulsions like squalene emulsions commonly exhibit lymph node targeting capabilities [26,27]. Tannic acid (TA), a polyphenol capable of forming hydrogen bonds with electronegative atoms, exhibits high affinity for elastin, which is abundantly present in lymphatic ducts. Liu et al. [23] discovered that TA-modified nanoparticles diffuse through lymphatic ducts and deposit in lymph node, triggering potent innate immune responses. This demonstrates that vaccine targeting capabilities can be enhanced through modification with assistive targeting molecules. Silva et al. [28] designed nanoparticles containing saponins and the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA), which modulate lymph flow in a mast cell-dependent manner and promote antigen entry into draining LN.Fig. 1Immunological mechanisms of nanoadjuvants. Formation of antigen reservoirs and controlled release

Upon entering the body, antigens induce immune responses while being gradually cleared. Protein/peptide antigens often have relatively short half-lives in the body [29]. Consequently, multiple vaccinations are often required, imposing significant labor and economic burdens on the livestock industry. Therefore, developing vaccines that reduce the number of immunizations, persistently store antigens within the body, and provide sustained, stable stimulation to the immune system represents a challenging goal in the current veterinary vaccinology field. Certain nanoadjuvants prolong antigen exposure by forming antigen reservoirs within tissues, enabling sustained antigen release (Fig. 1B). Dendritic mesoporous silica nanoparticles, owing to their unique core-radial pore structure, can form protein caps around encapsulated antigens, achieving prolonged antigen release in vivo [30]. Nanoparticles composed of Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) exhibit characteristics for sustained release of payload molecules, playing a crucial role in developing vaccines with controlled release. By adjusting the ratio of lactic acid to glycolic acid, molecular weight, and functionalization, their physicochemical properties—such as release kinetics and cell interactions—can be modulated to achieve gradual antigen release [31]. Additionally, adjuvant types such as LNPs and chitosan also create reservoirs at the site of immune action, thereby prolonging antigen release [32,33]. In addition, vaccines are being developed for induction of mucosal immunity against pathogens infecting epithelial cells using alternative administration routes beyond conventional injections, such as oral or intranasal delivery. However, conventional vaccines and adjuvants often lack sufficient efficacy for mucosal immunity, and ensuring oral vaccines active upon reaching the absorption site remains a challenge. Chitosan-based materials have shown a potential in this field. Vaccines encapsulated in chitosan exhibit extended storage stability under acidic conditions, reducing inactivation in gastric fluid. Additionally, their adhesive properties prolong retention time within the intestinal tract [34,35].

Activation of innate immune responses

Vaccine antigens can activate the innate immune responses via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) (Fig. 1C). PRRs are widely distributed on the surface and within innate immune cells, recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to initiate immune defense [36]. PRRs typically influence innate immune responses through multiple pathways: Promoting the production of inflammatory and chemotactic cytokines, stimulating expression of type I interferons (IFN-I), promoting the migration of APCs, enhancing antigen uptake and processing. Adjuvants often modulate innate immune signaling. Some adjuvants act as PAMPs, DAMPs, or their analogues that interact with PRRs on APCs—such as TLRs, cGAS-STING, RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), and NOD-like receptors (NLRs)—to trigger innate immune responses and promote APCs activation and maturation [4]. Various TLR agonists play crucial roles in stimulating immune responses. They not only induce expression of IFNs and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) via IFN signaling, but also drive NF-κB and MAPK pathways to promote pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12) [37]. Certain metal ions are found to activate innate immune responses. For instance, Mn2+ loaded in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) activates the cGAS-STING pathway, while Zn2+, Cu2+, Fe3+, and Hf4+ can activate the STING pathway [38].

Regulation of adaptive immune responses

The adaptive immune response is a highly specific and memory-based defense mechanism. When the number of invading antigens is too large or their virulence too strong to be completely eliminated by the innate immune system, lymphocytes and adaptive immune mechanisms are activated, triggering the body's specific recognition and clearance of pathogens. The specificity of adaptive immunity ensures the immune system's potent and targeted responses to diverse antigens, while its memory function enables rapid, robust reactions upon future encounters with identical or similar antigens. This makes it crucial for host defense during late-stage infections and in preventing recurrent infections [39]. Specifically, antigens processed by APCs form MHC-II peptide complexes are presented to CD4+ T cells, inducing their differentiation into distinct Th cell subtypes. Th1 cells primarily activate macrophages and B cells, and enhance cellular immunity to eliminate intracellular pathogens; Th2 cells activate B cells and promote antibody production, driving humoral immunity; and Th17 cells play a crucial role in defending against extracellular bacteria, fungi, and mediating inflammatory responses (Fig. 1D) [40]. Finally, the body forms immune memory—a key step in vaccine efficacy—by generating durable, effective memory cells that provide protective effects against future pathogen infections. Multiple factors regulate immune responses at this stage, including adjuvant type and morphology. Tursi et al. [41] designed a DNA vaccine encapsulated in LNPs that was able to induce robust innate and adaptive immunity. This approach relies on upregulation of cGAS-STING pathway and activation of migratory dendritic cells, inducing superior antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses and memory responses sustained for over a year. Kumar et al. [42] stretched spherical polystyrene particles with diameters of 193 nm and 521 nm into rod-shaped particles measuring 376 nm and 1530 nm in length, respectively, for conjugation with ovalbumin (OVA), and found that both particle types successfully delivered OVA. The spherical particles tended to stimulate Th1 immune responses, while rod-shaped particles elicited Th2-biased responses. Smaller spherical particles induced stronger Th1 and Th2 immune responses, demonstrating that vaccine shape and size modulate immune responses.

Enhancing cross-presentation

Exogenous antigens are primarily presented via MHC class II molecules, activating CD4+ helper T cells to coordinate humoral and cellular immunity. Cross-presentation of antigens refers to the presentation of exogenous antigens on MHC class I molecules, activating CD8+ T cell-mediated cellular immune responses—a process primarily carried out by specific DC subsets (Fig. 1E) [43]. This mechanism enables DCs to initiate cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) immunity even without direct infection by cytoplasmic pathogens (e.g., certain viruses). In vaccine and adjuvant development, stimulating DC cross-presentation is crucial, as this pathway allows some subunit vaccines to elicit cytotoxic T cell-mediated immune responses. Antigen-loaded nanoparticles often mimic the immune process of endogenous antigens after being endocytosed into endosomes/lysosomes. LNPs are commonly used to encapsulate mRNA vaccines. Upon being endocytosed by DCs, the mRNA escapes from LNPs and endosomes, translating the target antigen through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus [44]. The translated protein undergoes further processing by cytoplasmic proteasomes, is loaded onto the endoplasmic reticulum, and presented to MHC-I molecules to enhance humoral and cell-mediated immune responses [45]. Following internalization of the nanoparticles coated with polyethyleneimine (PEI) into endosomes/lysosomes, the unsaturated amino groups in PEI cause the retention of chloride ions and water, inducing the swelling and rupture of the endosomes to facilitate the cross-presentation of antigens [46]. Zhang et al. [47] utilized polyethyleneimine-modified Chinese yam polysaccharide PLGA nanoparticles combined with squalene to fabricate a positively charged nanoparticle-stabilized Pickering emulsion adjuvant system (PEI-CYP-PPAS). This system was loaded with an H9N2 avian influenza vaccine. The chickens immunized with CYP-PPAS/H9N2 exhibited higher proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells alongside increased cellular activation rates, demonstrating the adjuvant's capacity to enhance antigen cross-presentation and induce CD8+ T cell responses. Additionally, various nanoparticles including chitosan-based nanoparticles and MOFs have also been shown to promote antigen cross-presentation [48,49].

Material categories of common nanoadjuvants

With ever-increasing introduction of nanotechnology in veterinary vaccines, diverse materials have been tested as platforms for developing next-generation adjuvants due to their unique physicochemical and biological properties. Current nanoadjuvant designs generally focus on achieving targeted antigen delivery and controlled release for efficient activation of innate immunity and optimization of the Th1/Th2 balance and cytotoxic T cell responses. Based on composition and structure, nanomaterial adjuvants for veterinary vaccines can be primarily categorized into three types: inorganic nanomaterials, organic nanomaterials, and hybrid/composite nanomaterials (Fig. 2). Inorganic materials such as gold, carbon, and silicon possess the ability to independently form unique nanostructures with excellent stability and tunable pore diameters/structure. However, they have poor biodegradability and potential toxicity risks. Organic nanomaterials such as LNPs, chitosan, and sodium alginate are favored due to their good biocompatibility, degradability, and ease of functionalization. Additionally, some organic materials, such as chitosan, exhibit immune-stimulating capabilities and excellent adjuvant properties, but may have insufficient in vivo stability and complex preparation processes. Hybrid/composite nanomaterials can integrate the advantages of different materials for synergistic effects. For example, combination of inorganic metal elements with immune-stimulating activity, such as aluminum and manganese, with organic materials can achieve functional complementation and performance optimization. However, the complexity of their synthesis process and long-term biological safety still requires systematic evaluation (Table 1). Inorganic nanomaterials

Silicon

In recent years, research on silica-based nanoparticles in the field of veterinary nanoadjuvants has proliferated. Silicon dioxide nanoparticles (SNPs) possess large specific surface areas and high physicochemical stability, and can be engineered into various shapes, sizes, and customized surface functionalities [103]. SNPs facilitate sustained antigen release, enhance phagocytic uptake, and stimulate robust immune responses. They can drive both humoral and cellular immunity while maintaining excellent biocompatibility and low production costs [104]. Compared to soluble vaccine formulations, antigen-loaded SNP accumulate in APCs within draining LN post-injection, significantly enhancing the induction of antigen-specific B and T cell responses [105]. Cheng et al. [106] immunized chickens with a gp85 vaccine loaded onto silica nanoparticles as an adjuvant against J-subgroup avian leukosis. Compared to the Freund's group, the silica nanoparticle adjuvant group exhibited longer-lasting antibody levels, faster recovery from viremia, and significantly enhanced protective efficacy of the gp85 vaccine against J-subgroup avian leukosis virus. Besides, the addition of SNPs to the water-in-oil Pickering emulsion can stabilize it as a carrier for protein vaccines [107].

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) possess a unique center-radial pore structure, featuring large pore sizes, high surface areas, and high loading capacities. They are suitable for oral delivery of antigens and also retain the fundamental advantages of SNPs (Fig. 3A). Virginio et al. [52] evaluated the adjuvant potential of MSNs conjugated with a recombinant surface peptide domain of Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (Mhp). They found that MSNs demonstrated excellent capacity to elicit humoral immune responses in mice, comparable to those induced with the traditional adjuvant alum. Concurrently, MSNs enhanced IFN-γ and IL-4 expression levels in splenocytes and exhibited additional IL-10-mediated anti-inflammatory effects. This indicates that Mhp vaccines conjugated to MSNs can induce superior humoral and cellular immune responses in mice. MSNs also exhibit advantages as an adjuvant for FMD virus-like particles (VLPs) vaccines, including excellent biocompatibility, high protein loading capacity, and comparable antibody response levels and protective efficacy to the traditional Montanide ISA 206 adjuvant [51]. This demonstrates MSNs' excellent adjuvant potential as a replacement for traditional adjuvants like aluminum adjuvants in veterinary vaccines. Another commonly used silica nanoparticle is dendritic fibrous nano-silica (DFNS). They possess a unique open three-dimensional dendritic superstructure, enable delayed antigen release in vivo, and induce sustained immune responses [30]. He et al. [53] combined Cistanche deserticola polysaccharide (CDP) with DFNS to design CDP-DFNS, which was loaded with H9N2 antigen for oral immunization (Fig. 3B). They found that CDP-DFNS not only enhances antigen uptake by inducing macrophage activation but also significantly activates DCs and lymphocytes. This leads to increased levels of H9N2-specific antibodies, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), and Th-related cytokines. These results suggest that DFNS holds potential as a component of oral vaccine adjuvant. Notably, while SNPs exhibit potent effects, extensive research indicates that they may cause concentration-dependent toxicity in the respiratory, circulatory, digestive, and nervous systems. Particularly when administered via the lungs, SNPs mediate ROS/PARP/TRPM2 signaling pathways that induce pneumonia in mice, leading to increased ROS production, inflammation, and fibrosis in lung tissue [111]. Gold

The inert nature of gold confers low toxicity when used as a vaccine delivery system [112]. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), with gold as their primary framework, possess excellent plasticity and biocompatibility alongside potent surface functionalization capabilities and optical properties. AuNPs can serve as carriers by anchoring various nucleic acids in specific ratios [113]. When exposed to biological media, gold nanoparticles become coated by proteins and other biomolecules, forming a so-called "biocrown" that enhances cellular uptake and targeting [114]. These properties make AuNPs valuable for exploration as delivery carriers and vaccine adjuvants. Research also indicates that AuNPs exhibit excellent thermal stability. Vaccines formulated with freeze-dried AuNP adjuvants demonstrate superior shelf-life at room temperature or even higher temperatures compared to conventional vaccines, aiding vaccination efforts in remote areas and reducing costs [115]. AuNPs primarily influence processes such as antigen presentation, cellular uptake, blood clearance, biodistribution, and immune responses by engineering specific sizes and shapes [116]. Tao et al. [59] addressed the poor immunogenicity of matrix protein 2 (M2e) by conjugating AuNPs with the extracellular domain of influenza A ion channel membrane M2e from a universal influenza A virus, using CpG as a soluble adjuvant to prepare AuNP-M2e + sCpG. Intranasal administration induced B-cell activation in mouse lungs and robust serum antibody responses. Challenge studies in immunized mice demonstrated excellent protective efficacy, laying the foundation for a potential universal influenza A vaccine. Teng et al. [108] selected gold nanocages (AuNCs) as an adjuvant for the FMD vaccine (Fig. 3C). Their research revealed that AuNCs exhibit minimal in vivo and in vitro cytotoxicity, enhance the uptake of VLPs in BHK-21 and RAW264.7 cell lines, activate DCs via TLR4, and promote secretion of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. In mice and guinea pigs, they also elicited robust immune responses against FMDV, significantly enhancing CD8+ T cell proliferation and secretion of immune-related cytokines IFN-γ and IL-12 p70. This demonstrates promising potential as an adjuvant. However, AuNPs as adjuvants lack clinical data support, and cost and manufacturing processes remain to be the challenges [117].

Carbon

Carbon-based nanomaterials exhibit exceptional plasticity, enabling their assembly into diverse structures. Their high surface area facilitates attachment of various therapeutic molecules both internally and externally. Vaccines encapsulated within carbon-based nanomaterials could be protected from enzymatic degradation, thereby enhancing vaccine biostability [118]. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are cylindrical nanostructures formed by carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice (Fig. 3D). Their needle-like structure efficiently penetrates biological membranes, promoting antigen uptake by DCs (Fig. 3E) and transporting antigens into the cytoplasm (Fig. 3F) [119]. Their surfaces, composed entirely of carbon atoms, enable effective binding of diverse molecules. While CNTs themselves are non-immunogenic, when conjugated with specific antigens as a delivery system, they can induce specific humoral and cellular immune responses. They enhance antigen specificity, stability, and immunogenicity, activate DCs, lymphocytes and the complement system, stimulate interleukin production, and remain relatively stable after functionalization. Being non-toxic and insoluble in water prolongs their influence on immune response activation [120]. Nanotubes adsorb antigens via intermolecular interactions, and their adjuvant effects were comparable to Freund's adjuvant using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the model antigen [121]. Pimentel et al. [61] designed a nanocomposite featuring noncovalent binding between synthetic peptides and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), enabling the adsorption of a vaccine peptide for bovine babesiosis—containing the MSP1a core motif—onto the nanoparticle surface. The composite exhibited high stability and good dispersibility, eliciting high antibody levels upon immunization in mice and promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Tomporowski et al. [122] employed MWCNTs as a delivery system in combination with a potent avian immunostimulant—synthetic unmethylated cytosine-phosphate-guanine oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG ODN)—to reduce the dose of the immunostimulant. Cell uptake studies demonstrated that MWCNT-conjugated CpG ODN penetrates cells more efficiently than free CpG ODN. In vivo studies further revealed that MWCNT-conjugated CpG ODN exhibits superior efficacy with reduced toxicity at lower injection doses sufficient to protect chickens from lethal bacterial challenge. This demonstrates that MWCNT can serve as an effective adjuvant. Current research on CNT-based vaccine adjuvants primarily focuses on fish diseases, leaving substantial unexplored territory for other animal diseases. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of carbon-based nanomaterials limits their dispersion stability, and their poor biodegradability remains a significant challenge requiring resolution.

Organic nanomaterials

Chitosan and alginates

Chitosan is a natural polymer extracted from chitin. In addition to its low toxicity and excellent adhesion properties, it also exhibits good biocompatibility and biodegradability. These advantages make chitosan perform exceptionally well in immune stimulation and drug delivery for development of various vaccine adjuvants (Fig. 4A) [125,126]. Antigens administered orally or intranasally are captured by gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) and nasopharyngeal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT), respectively, inducing local secretory IgA antibodies that subsequently trigger humoral immunity [127,128]. Chitosan's unique adhesive properties confer significant advantages for vaccine preparation via mucosal immunization (Fig. 4B and C). Chitosan can also be specially processed to form diverse derivatives with enhanced or unique properties [129]. Research indicates that certain chitosan derivatives possess the ability to enhance cellular immune responses by activating signaling pathways such as cGAS-STING [130]. Antigens loaded onto chitosan demonstrate enhanced uptake by DCs (Fig. 4D) and improved lysosomal escape (Fig. 4E). Its relatively lower cost compared to other materials and simpler preparation methods for CS-based adjuvants have also led to increasing attention in the field of vaccine adjuvants [131].Fig. 4Chitosan nanoparticles. Chitosan-based nanoparticles can be administered via multiple routes: intramuscular, intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, oral, or even nasogastric feeding. Bugybayeva et al. [66] utilized mannan chitosan nanoparticles (mChit-NPs) to encapsulate and adsorb inactivated H1N2 swine influenza A virus as an intranasal vaccine for piglets. They demonstrated that this intranasal vaccine exhibited high loading efficiency, no cytotoxicity, superior ability to induce sIgA over commercially available vaccines, and reduced intranasal viral replication. Additionally, the two distinct loading approaches exhibit complementary immunogenic profiles: the encapsulated vaccine induces high levels of specific neutralizing antibodies and robust cellular immune responses, while the adsorbed vaccine elicits high IgG levels and hemagglutinin-inhibiting antibodies. Li et al. [67] constructed mannosyl-modified chitosan-coated PLGA nanoparticles (MCS-PLGA-NPs) loaded with nucleic acid vaccines against FMDV. This intranasal vaccine achieves immune effects by targeting mannose receptors on APCs surfaces and also demonstrated high protection rates against pathogenic FMDV when administered via subcutaneous injection. In the field of oral vaccines, Jin et al. [68] developed an N-2-HACC-Al nanoadjuvant by crosslinking N-2-hydroxypropyl trimethylammonium chloride chitosan (N-2-HACC) with aluminum ions. PEDV inactivated vaccines loaded in this adjuvant via electrostatic adsorption exhibits excellent biosafety, significantly enhances humoral and cellular immunity, induces robust mucosal immunity, and demonstrates remarkable efficacy through both injection and oral administration. This addresses the lack of adjuvants with effective immune-activating capabilities for PEDV inactivated vaccines, providing new insights for developing adjuvants for oral mucosal vaccines. Additionally, Leroux et al. [69] encapsulated vaccines against Echinococcus granulosus within chitosan-coated nanoparticles, enabling oral immunization against cystic echinococcosis. Renu et al. [70] prepared orally administered chitosan nanoadjuvants effective against avian Salmonella infection. However, chitosan suffers from poor solubility in neutral and alkaline environments, and the process for preparing uniform, stable, and reproducible nanoadjuvants remains complicated [132]. To achieve broader immunity, superior cross-protection against multiple viral and bacterial diseases, and balanced Th1-Th2 immune responses, future research on chitosan nanoparticle adjuvants should focus on exploring suitable surface modifications and optimizing particle size, shape, and surface charge according to the route of administration [127].

Alginate, a natural polysaccharide extracted from brown algae, is composed of two monomeric units: β-D-mannuronic acid and α-L-glucuronic acid. These monomers are arranged in a repeating sequence along the polymer chain, forming a distinct linear structure. Alginate possesses the unique property of forming hydrogels, enabling its significant role in fields such as tissue engineering, pharmaceuticals, and life sciences. Its excellent biocompatibility allows efficient integration with biological systems while avoiding strong rejection reactions [133]. Although alginate itself exhibits limited adjuvant potency, it frequently serves as a component of vaccine delivery carriers when crosslinked with other materials to form gels. Alginate-based adjuvants demonstrate outstanding performance in delivering various drug components and across multiple administration routes, including oral, ocular, nasal, and injectable formulations [134]. Due to the crosslinking capability between alginate's carboxyl groups and chitosan's amino groups, electrostatic interactions form polyelectrolyte complexes (PECs). This property enables alginate to be frequently crosslinked with chitosan to create micron-sized microspheres or nanoparticles. Its biodegradability, non-toxicity, and ease of modification into diverse forms and sizes have led to widespread use in recent years for designing various pharmaceutical formulations [135]. Ahmadivand et al. [74] demonstrated that a DNA vaccine targeting infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV), delivered via chitosan/tripolyphosphate (CS-TPP) nanoparticles and alginate microspheres incorporated into fish feed, significantly upregulated key immune gene expression in fish. This immunization strategy also reduced mortality and viral load following challenge infection. Lee et al. [75] administered chitosan/sodium alginate nanoparticles encapsulating bee venom (CH/AL-BV) to piglets via the nasal route concurrently with PRRSV vaccination. The nanoparticles demonstrated excellent sustained-release properties, mucosal adhesion capacity, and the ability to induce specific antibodies as well as significantly enhanced Th1-related immune responses. In PRRSV challenge experiments, viral loads in serum and lung tissues were significantly reduced in the CH/AL-BV group, proving the adjuvant's effective immunostimulatory capacity.

Lipid

Lipids have been used as vaccine delivery systems for several decades. Commonly used lipid nanoparticles include liposomes or LNPs. Liposomes are among the most classical and mature nanocarriers for vaccine delivery. These spherical vesicles are surrounded by one or more layers of phospholipid bilayers and contain a water-based core, within which hydrophilic or hydrophobic antigens and immune stimulants can be loaded. They are structurally similar to biological membranes, thus having excellent biocompatibility for absorption by macrophages [136]. LNPs are a class of protective shells containing various lipid components, typically around 100 nm in size, primarily composed of four key constituents: ionizable lipids, polyethylene glycol (PEG) lipid conjugates, and auxiliary lipids (e.g., cholesterol and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine [DSPC]) [137]. These components self-assemble through effective synergistic interactions and encapsulate nucleic acid vaccines. LNPs play crucial roles in: encapsulating RNA to shield it from ribonuclease-mediated degradation, thereby maintaining stability; Cationic LNPs interact with anionic cell membranes to enhance antigen uptake; pH-sensitive ionizable lipids facilitate in vivo escape, enabling antigen payload release into the cytoplasm while preventing degradation of the delivered active components before reaching target cytoplasm [138,139]. Studies indicate that LNPs can stably deliver and release antigens as well as immunostimulants, promoting antigen uptake and maturation of DCs (Fig. 5A), generating persistent local immune responses at the injection site (Fig. 5B), and inducing production of various cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-α, and IFN-γ [141,142]. In recent years, LNPs have been extensively studied as adjuvants for veterinary vaccines. Their uniform particle size (Fig. 5C and D) and excellent lymph node targeting qualify them for strong adjuvant activity when used as carriers for DNA/mRNA vaccines in food animals such as pigs and chickens (Fig. 5E). Yin et al. [13] synthesized and used IMQP, a lipophilic immunostimulant derivative of imiquimod (IMQ) with a long carbon chain, as a nanoadjuvant to load inactivated FMDV for subcutaneous immunization. The LNPs efficiently delivered antigens to immune cells and significantly promoted cytokine and chemokine expression and secretion while activating both Th1 and Th2 immune responses, thereby contributing to immune balance (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, LNPs possess the capacity to bind other adjuvant molecules to enhance immunogenicity. For instance, LNPs loaded with mRNA vaccines conjugated to α-galactosylceramide—an NKT cell agonist—demonstrated markedly superior immunotherapeutic efficacy against Chlamydia psittaci. Parrots immunized via intramuscular injection exhibited significantly reduced visceral lesions and pathogen load following challenge infection [77]. Alternatively, co-administration with TLR7/8 agonist R848 demonstrated immunoprophylactic efficacy against H5N1 influenza virus [78]. However, LNP vaccines also face challenges, including unstable biosafety, short-lived preventive effects, difficulties in large-scale production, and stability defects in vivo and in vitro [144].

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) & polyethyleneimine (PEI)

PLGA is a copolymer composed of lactic acid and glycolic acid that is fully biodegradable in biological systems. It exhibits tunable degradation rates, controllable mechanical strength, and excellent biocompatibility [145,146]. PLGA has been extensively used as a carrier in cosmetics, food, drug delivery systems, and agriculture. It degrades in vivo into CO2 and H2O via the tricarboxylic acid cycle with negligible toxicity [147]. PLGA can directly load vaccines to enhance their immunogenicity. PLGA-coated inactivated Newcastle disease virus (NDV) vaccines enable slow and pulsatile antigen release, and induce high levels of HI and IgY antibodies that balance humoral and cellular immune responses. This enhances the protective efficacy of inactivated NDV vaccines, achieving 100% survival after challenge with highly pathogenic NDV [89].

PLGA can be modified and loaded with other materials to achieve multifaceted adjuvant advantages. Gao et al. [84] loaded the cytokine IL-2 onto the surface of PLGA nanoparticles and coated them with dendritic cell (DC) membranes carrying FMDV antigens. These nanoparticles exhibit easy penetration into LN, possess sustained-release properties, and significantly enhance antibody secretion as well as T cell activation and proliferation capabilities. Feng et al. [148] combined anti-CD205 monoclonal antibody (MAb) with PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating Eucommia polysaccharide (EUPS) to prepare a nanoadjuvant that targets DC maturation and proliferation. This adjuvant was demonstrated to efficiently induce humoral and cellular immune responses, enhancing the immunogenicity of a FMD vaccine in mice. Huo et al. [85] prepared PLGA nanoparticles (CMR-PLGA/p54 NPs) loaded with African swine fever virus (ASFV) antigen (p54) and encapsulated within red blood cell membranes modified with mannose and TLR9 agonist CpG. Following subcutaneous immunization, these nanoparticles were found to enable efficient antigen delivery to splenic DCs through the spleen-targeting capability of red blood cells and the mannose-mediated targeting of DCs. This approach also induced significant humoral and cellular immune responses. This unique spleen-targeting capability demonstrate the broad potential of this vaccine strategy. However, some PLGA nanoadjuvants also exhibit limitations such as complex preparation processes, vigorous reactions during synthesis, and weak immune-enhancing effects [149].

PEI is a cationic polymer composed of monomer units -(CH2CH2NH)n-that possesses a high charge density that enables its modification of numerous nanoparticles. PEI-modified particles can induce cells to produce a series of transcription factors associated with immune responses, thereby achieving an immunostimulatory effect [150]. Concurrently, PEI's "lysosomal escape" property enhances antigen cross-presentation of PEI-modified particles while mitigating potential toxicity [151]. The amino groups on the PEI surface can tightly bind to the carboxyl groups on polysaccharides. After PEI modification, polysaccharides often carry a positive charge, which interacts attractively with negatively charged cell membranes, promoting effective binding between the loaded vaccine and cells. Therefore, PEI is frequently used to modify various polysaccharides with biological activity or targeting effects to achieve stronger immune responses [152].

In recent years, PEI-modified polysaccharide-coated PLGA nanoparticles have been employed as research models to investigate the effects of various target polysaccharides on immune responses, with particularly notable achievements in vaccine adjuvants for H9N2 avian influenza. PLGA nanoparticles were coated with PEI-modified camel thorn honey polysaccharides, filled with squalene, and assembled into Pickering emulsions (PEI-PPAS) [86]. This material exhibits a unique strawberry-like structure and its rough surface could adsorb large amounts of antigen through simple mixing. With high antigen loading efficiency and stability, it possesses strong immunostimulatory properties, serving as an effective adjuvant for H9N2 antigens. Zhang et al. [47] designed a Pickering emulsion using Chinese yam polysaccharides (CYPs) as the polysaccharide component. The H9N2 avian influenza vaccine loaded onto this emulsion also induced potent humoral and cellular immunity, with efficacy surpassing that of traditional aluminum adjuvants. Gu et al. [153] developed an adjuvant by encapsulating Angelica sinensis polysaccharides (ASP) into PLGA nanoparticles coated with PEI. This approach also demonstrated superior immune-stimulating effects over commercially available aluminum adjuvants and the oil adjuvant ISA-201 when used with an inactivated H9N2 virus vaccine. Moreover, this nanoadjuvant model proved effective with other vaccines. Zhang et al. [91] designed a PEI-modified PLGA nanoparticle encapsulating Chinese yam polysaccharide (CYPP-PEI). When loaded with porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) antigen, this nanoadjuvant not only enhanced macrophage antigen uptake but also stimulated corresponding antibody and cytokine production, eliciting a robust and sustained immune response. Crucially, this nanoadjuvant demonstrated exceptional storage stability and high antigen loading efficiency. However, PEI's high cationic charge density confers significant cytotoxicity, poor cell-specific targeting, and poor biodegradability in vivo [154]. Consequently, chemical modification and functionalization of PEI nanoadjuvants remain key research priorities.

Hybrid/composite nanomaterials

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)

MOFs are a class of hybrid materials formed by combining metal clusters with organic linkers. They possess high surface areas while exhibiting highly tunable size and pore structure, making them crucial for drug delivery applications [155]. Additionally, MOFs feature excellent biocompatibility and environment-friendly preparation processes, rendering them suitable for antigen delivery to enhance immune responses (Fig. 6A) [158]. Studies indicate that MOFs can promote antigen escape from lysosomes (Fig. 6B). Li et al. [159] modified γ-cyclodextrin metal-organic frameworks (γ-CD-MOFs) made from cyclodextrin and potassium with Span-85. Experiments in mice demonstrated multifaceted stimulation of immune responses: while inducing high antigen-specific IgG titers and cytokine secretion, it significantly accelerated splenic cell proliferation and activated maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), confirming its potential as a adjuvant. Stillman et al. [157] utilized aluminum metal-organic frameworks (Al-MOFs) as novel aerosol adjuvants to enhance OVA immunogenicity (Fig. 6C). They discovered that Al-MOFs possess optimal aerodynamic diameters enabling efficient pulmonary deposition while eliciting robust mucosal humoral and cellular immune responses. Zeolite imidazolate framework (ZIF) is a material within MOFs that exhibits excellent biocompatibility and adjuvant activity. It possesses stable chemical and thermal properties along with the characteristic of degradation in acidic environments, making it an ideal template for loading functional nanomedicines for targeted therapy [160]. Zeolite imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), formed by zinc ions (Zn2+) and 2-methylimidazole (2-MeIm), induces robust humoral immune responses. ZIF-8 formed by self-assembly of Mn-MOF-74 and ZIF-8 on yeast surfaces promoted DC maturation and activation, as well as antigen uptake and presentation, while enhancing both humoral and cellular immune responses [48]. Ding et al. [95] designed Cap@ZIF-8-CpG as a novel subunit vaccine using ZIF loaded with PCV2 Cap protein and the immune adjuvant CpG. They found that this formulation effectively enhanced antigen resistance to denaturation, increased mouse IgG antibody titers and lymphocyte proliferation, and challenge tests demonstrated the vaccine conferred immunity against PCV2 in mice (Fig. 6D). Yin et al. [96] synthesized amino-functionalized ZIF-7/8 nanoparticles and prepared ZIF-7/8-ADA by coating with alginate di-aldehyde (ADA) to load inactivated pseudorabies virus (PRV). This MOF exhibited high stability, monodispersity, and high antigen loading capacity. This immunostimulation strategy enhanced Th1/Th2 immune responses, outperforming the commercially available aluminum adjuvant and oil adjuvant ISA-201. Challenge studies in mice also confirmed that ZIF-7/8-ADA provided superior preventive efficacy compared to alum adjuvant, achieving the same level of protection as the commercial adjuvant ISA-201. Currently, MOF-type adjuvants have been extensively studied in oncology and human vaccines, yet they have not gained application as veterinary adjuvants. However, their outstanding performance and versatility warrant broader exploration.

Quantum dots (QDs)

QDs are the smallest nanomaterials (2–10 nm) with unique chemical and physical properties. As semiconductor crystals, they play a vital role in bioimaging, fluorescent biosensors, and electrochemical analysis. Their distinctive functions in cell tracking and drug delivery suggest the potential of QDs as adjuvants [161,162]. Current QDs applications in adjuvants primarily focus on carbon-based quantum dots. Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) are photoluminescent nanocarbon particles composed of discrete, sub-10 nm quasi-spherical nanoparticles, primarily consisting of C, O, and N elements. They exhibit low toxicity, chemical inertness, and excellent biocompatibility. Cheng et al. [98] prepared functionalized CQDs conjugated with the recombinant gp85 protein from the avian leukosis virus J subtype (ALV-J) protein vaccine. They then conducted immunization and challenge experiments on chickens. Results demonstrated that CQD-based adjuvants enabled rapid antibody production with prolonged persistence in experimental chickens, exhibiting superior protective efficacy over the Freund's adjuvant group. These findings highlight CQD's potential as an excellent candidate nanocarrier for vaccine delivery. Huang et al. [99] designed a novel adjuvant for influenza vaccines by modifying graphene oxide quantum dots with carnosine, resveratrol, and zinc ions to form self-assembled nanoparticles (ZnGC-R). This adjuvant exhibits high antigen loading, enhances antigen utilization, promotes DC recruitment, and activates APCs. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the nanoparticles induced significantly stronger Th1/Th2 immune responses in mice compared to commercial adjuvants, and achieved 100% protective efficacy against H1N1 influenza virus infection. Current research on QDs predominantly focuses on fluorescence sensing and detection, their potential applications as adjuvants awaits further exploration. Issues such as cytotoxicity, environmental sensitivity, and stability of certain metal quantum dots warrant close attention when investigating their adjuvant effects [163].

Composite materials

Given the distinct advantages and disadvantages of different materials, researchers often combine multiple materials to create composite nanomaterials with multifaceted benefits. Particularly by integrating inorganic and organic materials, adjuvants can possess excellent physical properties of inorganic materials while also leveraging the superior immune-stimulating capabilities of organic materials. Addressing the limitations in current PCV2 vaccine adjuvants, such as adverse reactions, insufficient cell-mediated immune responses, and inadequate antibody production, Xu et al. [100] developed a novel adjuvant consisting of violet polyphenolic polysaccharides (VPP) as a modifier, AuNPs, and chitosan. These nanoparticles maintain structural stability and exhibit excellent dispersibility, effectively preserving the inherent properties of polysaccharides. When loaded with PCV2 vaccine and administered to mice, the nanoparticles demonstrated high bioavailability and significantly enhanced the immune response in mice. This demonstrates the potential for developing inorganic-organic composite nanomaterials as adjuvants with superior structural and immunogenic capabilities. Liu et al. [101] synthesized the composite nanoadjuvant N-2-HACC-Al using N-2-HACC and aluminum sulfate. This nanoparticle exhibited excellent thermal stability, biodegradability, and low cytotoxicity. After immunizing chicken with a bivalent vaccine containing Newcastle disease (ND) and H9N2 avian influenza, the chicken demonstrated significantly higher antibody and cytokine levels compared to commercially available vaccines, demonstrating that N-2-HACC-Al NPs represent a highly effective nanoadjuvant with significant application potential. In recent years, an increasing number of composite adjuvants have attracted attention. While leveraging their flexible and tunable properties, it is equally important to fully understand the molecular mechanisms and safety aspects associated with material composites.

Beyond chemically engineered nanoadjuvants, biologically self-assembled nanoplatforms have emerged as a complementary class of vaccine systems that combine precise antigen display with intrinsic adjuvant-like properties.

Biological self-assembled materials

Besides various chemically synthesized nanoparticles, the fields of structural biology and protein engineering have given rise to a unique class of nanocarriers: bio-derived self-assembled nanomaterials. These platforms form through spontaneous, highly ordered assembly of identical protein subunits into monodisperse, cage-like, or virus-like structures. Their synthesis relies on recombinant DNA technology and heterologous expression systems. Prominent examples of such materials include VLPs and ferritin nanoparticles. These protein-based nanoparticles offer unparalleled precision in size and geometry, as well as excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. Furthermore, they can be genetically or chemically modified to present antigens or immunostimulatory molecules in dense and repetitive arrays. As powerful dual-functional platforms, they serve both as antigen carriers and as immunostimulants, exhibiting natural adjuvant effects. Consequently, these biomaterials have been the subject of extensive research in recent years.

VLPs

VLPs are multi-protein structures formed by self-assembly of viral structural proteins. Their morphology closely resembles that of natural viruses. Due to absence of viral genetic materials, they are non-infectious and cannot replicate [164]. Their highly repetitive and ordered surface arrays act as PAMPs, which are easily recognized by B cells, thereby inducing a robust humoral immune response [165]. Furthermore, VLPs can be effectively internalized by dendritic cells and macrophages via various endocytic pathways, promoting antigen processing and cross-presentation to CD8+ T cells, thereby bridging humoral and cellular immunity [166]. In the field of veterinary vaccinology, VLPs have achieved significant commercial success. The PCV2 VLP vaccine, one of the most prominent examples, derived from self-assembly of the viral Cap protein into antigenic particles that retain the virus's characteristic features and immunogenicity, and its application in pigs has demonstrated high efficacy and safety [167].

VLP-based vaccine candidates have also been explored for prevention of other animal diseases. Meng et al. [168] employed CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology using a canarypox virus vector (ALVAC) to generate RABV-like VLPs (ALVAC-RABV-VLP). This vaccine induced sustained and effective humoral immune responses in mice following a single dose administration. Mice challenged with a lethal dose of RABV exhibited a 100% survival. Similarly, in dogs and cats, a single immunization with ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicited a stronger and longer-lasting antibody response than commercial inactivated rabies vaccines. Various studies have shown that VLP vaccines could achieve expected preventive effects against viral infections such as FMDV, porcine rotavirus (PoRV), porcine parvovirus (PPV), PEDV, and feline calicivirus (FCV) [[169], [170], [171], [172], [173]]. VLPs also possess high editability. Qi et al. [174] expressed the PCV2 Cap protein fused with an epitope of a molecular adjuvant C3d which was shown to significantly accelerate antibody production, one week faster than that of commercial PCV2 subunit vaccines and reduced PCV2 viral load in challenged piglets. These results provide innovative strategies for developing more efficient and cost-effective VLP vaccines. Despite their significant efficacy, production of VLP vaccines is sometimes hindered by factors such as complex processes, challenges in achieving high yields, and the need for careful optimization of assembly conditions [175]. Nevertheless, their inherent adjuvant properties and safety make them an ideal platform for next-generation veterinary vaccines.

Ferritin nanoparticles

Ferritin is a family of proteins found in most organisms. These proteins naturally self-assemble from 24 monomeric subunits, allowing for diverse modifications of both the ferritin surface and its inner cavity through chemical and genetic methods. Their stable hollow spherical structure and extremely high biocompatibility make them widely utilized in various applications. In addition to loading various drugs, genes, or even peptides expressed on its surface, the triple symmetry axis of the ferritin structure also enables it to present the loaded antigens in the correct conformation [176,177]. In the veterinary field, ferritin-based nanovaccines have demonstrated potential against a variety of important pathogens. Sun et al. [178] designed three self-assembled nanoparticle vaccines derived from thermophilic archaeal ferritin, each targeting epitopes of the PRRSV GP3/GP4/GP5 proteins. These vaccines effectively activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as effector memory T cells in mice. Vaccinated piglets exhibited milder clinical symptoms and lung tissue damage, as well as lower viral loads in serum and lung tissue upon viral challenge. Ferritin-based nanovaccines have demonstrated significant efficacy against common veterinary pathogens, including FMDV, RABV, PEDV, swine influenza viruses (SIVs), and ASFV. Animals vaccinated with ferritin vaccines often exhibit higher titers of specific IgG antibodies, stronger T-cell immune responses, and higher challenge protection rates compared to those vaccinated with conventional vaccines [[179], [180], [181], [182], [183]]. The advantages of ferritin vaccines, including safety, reliability, rapid production and low cost, have attract attention in the vaccine field in recent years [184], with several ferritin vaccine candidates entered Phase I clinical trials [180], demonstrating their promising prospects in the vaccine sector.

Design and application of nanoadjuvants

The exceptional performance of nanoadjuvants stems not only from their unique material properties and immunomodulatory mechanisms but also relies on rational design tailored to specific application scenarios. Compared to traditional adjuvants, the programmability of nano-platforms enables targeted optimization of immune response types, intensity, and persistence through precise control over size, shape, surface chemistry, and antigen loading strategies. When designing responses to specific pathogens, one should specifically consider the relationship between animal species and pathogens (Fig. 7). Design principles for nanoadjuvants

Size and shape

When designing nanoadjuvants for veterinary vaccines, the choice of materials and preparation methods must be determined based on such factors as vaccine type and route of administration. Maintaining a size and shape that preserve biological activity is critical for efficient delivery and proper functioning. Adjuvants of different sizes exhibit distinct cellular uptake and transport mechanisms, which can influence processes such as lymphatic drainage and DCs uptake. Particles that are too small may struggle to remain effectively at the injection site or be sufficiently taken up by APCs, while those that are too large may impede lymphatic drainage or trigger non-specific clearance. Adjuvants of different sizes and shapes, assembled from the same nanomaterials using different preparation methods, may mediate different types of immune responses [42]. Liu et al. [185] synthesized large-pore mesoporous silica nanoparticles (LMSN) of different sizes to load influenza split vaccine (SV). They found no significant difference in the vaccine-loading capacity. However, compared to the larger and smaller particle sizes, only the size of about 250 nm LMSN-M exhibited higher retention rates and cellular uptake in mesenteric LN, and significantly enhanced the mucosal immune response of the SV.

The immunogenicity of vaccine with nanoadjuvants is also influenced by their shape. Tazaki et al. [186] designed spherical and rod-shaped AuNPs for RNA vaccines and found that both exhibited immunogenicity following subcutaneous injection. However, the spherical particles did not show significant activity via intranasal immunization, indicating that the routes of administration may impose specific requirements on adjuvant shape. Zhao et al. [187] produced AuNPs of the same size but different shapes (cage-shaped, rod-shaped and star-shaped) for tumor antigens. The results showed that rod-shaped and star-shaped nanovaccines primarily flowed into the follicular regions of LN, while cage-shaped vaccines exhibited stronger pericortical retention. Star-shaped AuNPs captured and retained more antigenic epitopes and induced a stronger humoral immune response, whereas cage-shaped nanovaccines preferentially presented peptide-MHC I complexes to induce CD8+ T-cell immunity. This demonstrates that the geometric shape of nanoadjuvants not only influences their in vivo distribution kinetics, efficiency and mechanisms of endocytosis but also determines the type of immune pathways activated.

Charge and chemical modification

The surface charges of nanoadjuvants have a significant impact on their immunogenicity. Surface charges can promote interactions between the adjuvant and cell membranes and facilitate the formation of a “protein crown”. They also influence lymphatic drainage and the efficiency of adsorption or conjugation of antigens or immunostimulatory molecules (such as TLR agonists) onto the particle surface [188,189]. By modifying the surface charges of antigens, electrostatic adsorption and precise anchoring between the two components can be achieved, thereby increasing antigen utilization for enhanced humoral and cellular immunity, and reduced doses of antigens and/or adjuvants [190]. Furthermore, the charges of nanoadjuvants also influence their interaction with immune cells. Positively charged nanoparticles are generally more readily taken up by cells but may be associated with higher cytotoxicity or nonspecific inflammatory responses. Conversely, nanosystems with moderate negative charges may maintain good immunostimulatory capacity while improving biocompatibility [191,192].

Chemical modification of adjuvants using different materials could impart various properties. Suberi et al. [193] optimized biodegradable poly(amine-ester) (PACE) liposomes through terminal group modification and the use of polyethylene glycol which was used to load mRNA encoding the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) spike protein for inhalation administeration to mice. This LNP achieved high levels of mRNA transfection in epithelial cells and antigen-presenting cells throughout the lungs, induced robust cellular and humoral adaptive immunity and protected mice from a lethal viral challenge. Furthermore, co-delivery and synergistic stimulation can be achieved by directly conjugating immunostimulatory molecules, such as TLR agonists, to the surface or interior of the nanoparticles [194]. Surface modification of adjuvants using biodegradable and biocompatible materials such as PLGA and liposomes can improve adjuvant biocompatibility and reduce their potential toxicity [195,196].

Antigen loading

Loading antigens and immunostimulatory molecules onto carriers using different methods is also a key factor in altering adjuvant properties. Encapsulating antigens or immunostimulatory molecules within nanoparticles or adsorbing them onto surfaces via physical encapsulation or electrostatic adsorption represents a relatively straightforward approach. Examples include RNA vaccines encapsulated by LNPs, viral or VLP vaccines loaded in polymeric particles such as PLGA, and inorganic nanoparticles like mesoporous silica [51,89,136]. Such adjuvants primarily ensure targeted delivery and sustained release of active components. Chemical conjugation methods covalently link antigen or adjuvant molecules to functional groups on or within nanoparticle surfaces, enabling stable, efficient antigen loading and co-localized delivery [197]. Wang et al. [198] developed a compact nanovaccine enabling tight integration of antigen proteins with CpG within a nanoscale disulfide network. This vaccine consists almost entirely of antigen (up to 97 wt%), exhibiting exceptionally high antigen loading capacity. Another method for loading the active ingredients of the vaccine is to utilize the intermolecular interactions, by self-assembling the active components to form self-assembled nanoparticles (SANPs). SANP formation relies on multiple forces including electrostatic, hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals, and π-π interactions [199]. These forces enable autonomous assembly of SANP modules without external intervention. SANPs engineered through gene fusion or tag conjugation can attach exogenous genes or antigen proteins to specific sites, delivering self-assembled antigen-loaded nanoparticles [200]. This approach achieves high antigen loading density and excellent stability [183,201].

Applications of nanoparticle adjuvants in veterinary vaccine

Swine vaccines

The swine industry faces challenges of complex diseases as well as significant prevention and control pressures. Nanoparticle adjuvants have made remarkable progress in enhancing the efficacy of swine vaccines. Nanoparticle adjuvants primarily based on materials such as LNPs and chitosan have been extensively researched in various swine vaccines. Liu et al. [80] encapsulated an mRNA vaccine targeting classical swine fever virus (CSFV) within LNPs. Vaccination and challenge studies in pigs demonstrated stable adjuvant effect. LNP vaccines delivering DNA plasmid encoding hemagglutinin (HA) gene of the Influenza A Virus in Swine (IAV-S) also provided excellent protection against homologous IAV-S strain infection [83]. For infectious diseases such as African swine fever and porcine delta coronavirus (PDCoV) infection, LNP vaccines were found to elicit durable, broad and potent humoral and cellular immune responses [79,81]. Renu et al. [202] developed a nasally delivered chitosan nanoparticle vaccine (CS NPs-KAg) encapsulating inactivated killed IAV-S antigen (KAg), conjugated with mannose to create the mCS NPs-KAg vaccine for piglet immunization. The mCS NPs-KAg vaccine was found to induce higher levels of sIgA and IgG. Compared to commercial vaccines, this vaccine effectively reduces respiratory viral load, improves viral clearance rate, and decreases overall pulmonary lesion severity. Ferreira et al. [203] loaded the proteins obtained from the decomposed Mhp onto MSNs and conducted oral immunization in pigs. They observed antibody responses 7 days post-oral vaccination with MSNs, reduced pulmonary lesions after challenge, and decreased Mhp shedding, demonstrating mucosal protective effects in pigs. This provides favorable data for future development of vaccines against Mhp. Research also indicates that MSNs, when used as a vaccine for porcine transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV), enhance the activation of porcine DCs and the expression of various cytokines in immature DCs [50].

Avian vaccines

Poultry farming is characterized by intensive and large-scale operations, requiring vaccines that are cost-effective and suitable for mass immunization (e.g., via drinking water or aerosol). Administering nanovaccines to poultry offers advantages such as enhanced immune stimulation, antimicrobial activity (antiviral, antibacterial, antiparasitic or antifungal), and protection against mycotoxins [204]. Nanoadjuvants demonstrate flexibility in enhancing immune efficacy and adapting to farming needs. The antimicrobial activity exhibited by various nanoformulations gives nano-vaccines an advantage in overcoming antibiotic resistance barriers [205].

In recent years, chitosan and LNP have been the primary adjuvant components in poultry vaccine development. Guo et al. [206] delivered avian influenza DNA vaccines using cocoic acid (CA)-modified lipid nanoparticles (CLNPs), demonstrating through in vivo chicken experiments that they can elicit desirable humoral and cellular immune responses. Acevedo et al. [207] immunized chicks with an inactivated Salmonella vaccine delivered via chitosan nanoparticles (CNPs) and challenged them with Salmonella Enteritidis. They observed significantly higher levels of specific IgA and IgY in the bile of vaccinated chicks, along with increased T lymphocyte proliferation rates. Concurrently, intestinal bacterial loads were significantly reduced, demonstrating the feasibility of CNP adjuvants in avian vaccines. Kumar et al. [208] explored antigen-chitosan-PLGA nanoparticles as a delivery system for inactivated infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) antigen. These nanoparticles induced robust cell-mediated immunity. Immunized poultry exhibited stronger resistance to IBDV challenge infection than those vaccinated with commercial vaccines. Furthermore, the role of chitosan in adjuvant development for vaccines against various veterinary diseases—including Newcastle disease virus, Pasteurella multocida, and Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG)—has also been validated [[71], [72], [73]]. Xu et al. [209] demonstrated that LNP-loaded mRNA vaccines targeting duck tambousu virus (DTMUV) induced not only exceptionally high neutralizing antibody titers in immunized laying ducks but also effective maternal transfer into egg yolks, confirming their potential for maternal immune protection of offspring. Kumosani et al. [210] administered aerosolized LNP encapsulating H9N2 and MG antigens, supplemented with echinacea extract (EE) as an immunostimulant via intranasal vaccination to broiler chickens. This approach induced sufficient local immunity to protect chickens against H9N2 and MG infections.

Aquatic animal vaccines

The treatment of aquatic animal diseases faces serious challenges, including a lack of fundamental research on pharmacodynamics, pharmacogenetics, toxicology, and their impacts on aquaculture ecosystems, as well as the emergence of drug resistance in aquatic pathogens. Relying solely on chemical drugs and antibiotics is no longer sufficient to address current disease issues. In new ecological farming models, integrated disease prevention and control utilizing probiotics, immunostimulants and vaccines is increasingly advocated [211]. Given the unique aquaculture environment, injection-based immunization is impractical and immersion and oral immunizations offer ideal approaches [212].

Nanoparticle adjuvants, particularly inorganic nanomaterials, play an irreplaceable role in protecting antigens and penetrating fish physiological barriers. Chitosan, due to its outstanding mucosal immune capabilities, is also frequently used as a vaccine adjuvant for aquatic animals. Sukkarun et al. [213] developed a chitosan-based composite nanoparticle vaccine loaded with Aeromonas veronii. Through immersion immunization of red tilapia (Oreochromis sp.), this nanoparticle vaccine demonstrated strong gill-binding and penetration capabilities, and significantly improved survival rates post-challenge infection. Huo et al. [214] developed an oral vaccine using CS/SA nanoparticles loaded with grass carp reovirus (GCRV) DNA. Following vaccination, it significantly increased survival rates after GCRV infection and markedly suppressed GCRV replication in the hindgut, spleen, and cephalic kidney. Jia et al. [62] discovered that functionalized single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) significantly elevated serum antibody levels, enzyme activity, and immune-related gene expression in Micropterus salmoides when used as a delivery vehicle for the DNA vaccine. This approach also provided substantial protection against largemouth bass ulcer syndrome virus (LBUSV) infection. Additionally, vaccines delivered via SWCNT carriers have demonstrated efficacy in preventing various fish diseases, including spring viroviremia of carp virus (SVCV), Taiwan grouper iridovirus (TGIV), and Streptococcus [[63], [64], [65]].

Other animal vaccines

Beyond swine, poultry and aquatic animals, nanoadjuvants also demonstrate promising application potential in vaccines for food animals such as cattle and sheep, as well as companion animals. Recent studies indicate that various nanoparticles can effectively enhance the immunogenicity and safety of vaccines for these animal species. Lacasta et al. [215] used HBcAg-p67C (a chimeric hepatitis B core antigen displaying p67C in virus-like particles) and silica vesicle (SV)-p67C, both adjuvanted with ISA 206 VG, to vaccinate cattle. This induced robust antibody and T-cell responses against p67C. Pérez et al. [216] vaccinated sheep with prototypical vaccines against bluetongue virus (BTV) formulated using aluminum hydroxide, biomimetic apatite nanoparticles (ApNPs), and microcrystalline tyrosine (MCT). Compared to the aluminum adjuvant group, the ApNPs and MCT groups demonstrated faster neutralizing antibody responses and reduced local reactions (vs postmortem granulomas in the aluminum adjuvant group), demonstrating that ApNPs and MCT represent more biocompatible, safer, and viable alternative adjuvants suitable for sheep vaccines. Wan et al. [217] packaged mRNA sequences encoding rabies virus (RABV) glycoprotein (RABV-G) into lipopolyplex (LPP) nanoparticles. Immunization of dogs revealed that low doses of LPP-mRNA-G induced high levels of virus-neutralizing antibody titers in dog.

Conclusion and outlook

Nanomaterials demonstrate multifaceted advantages in the development and application of novel veterinary vaccine adjuvants. Due to their precisely controllable physicochemical properties (such as size, shape, surface charge, and functional group modifications) and diverse antigen loading and delivery capabilities, nanoadjuvants exhibit significant benefits in enhancing vaccine immunogenicity, improving immune response types, increasing cross-protection, and prolonging protection duration. Compared to traditional aluminum or oil-in-water emulsion adjuvants, nanoadjuvants not only facilitate targeted antigen delivery to lymphoid tissues and APCs but also coordinate immune responses by activating multiple innate immune pathways. They are particularly effective in antiviral vaccines requiring strong T-cell immunity. Materials such as AuNPs, mesoporous silica, LNPs, PLGA, and MOFs have demonstrated superior adjuvant effects in vaccines targeting various animal diseases including FMD, PRRS, avian influenza, and infectious splenorenal necrosis.

Nevertheless, numerous challenges remain in translating nanomaterial adjuvants from basic research to clinical and field applications. While most nanomaterials exhibit good biocompatibility, long-term toxicity, metabolic pathways in vivo, biodistribution, and potential effects on the immune system remain incompletely understood. Additionally, large-scale production and quality control of nanoadjuvants present significant bottlenecks. These issues have prevented the majority of nanoadjuvant research from advancing beyond the experimental stage and making a full replacement of traditional adjuvants in production applications. To overcome the bottlenecks in these transformation processes, close collaboration among materials scientists, veterinarians, regulatory agencies, and industry partners is necessary. Future research should not only focus on enhancing immunogenicity but also prioritize "developability" parameters at the early stage of adjuvant design, including formulation stability, cost-effectiveness, and safety.

Looking ahead, future research on nanoadjuvants can focus on the following directions: 1) utilizing multi-omics and single-cell technologies to reveal the mechanisms of interaction between nanomaterials and immune cells; 2) developing integrated delivery systems that synergistically deliver adjuvants, antigens, and targeting molecules; 3) establishing standardized target animal toxicity models to enhance safety evaluation and translational research; 4) expanding adjuvant design for mucosal immunity, cross-protection, and multivalent vaccines to address emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases; 5) incorporating artificial intelligence and computational modeling to assist nanomaterial design and efficacy prediction; and 6) prioritizing "developability" parameters at the early stage of adjuvant design, including formulation stability, cost-effectiveness, and safety. With continuing achievements in these directions through in-depth interdisciplinary integration and a full-chain R&D strategy that spans from basic research to industrial application, nanoadjuvants are expected to achieve safer and more controllable, broad-spectrum and highly efficient application goals against emerging and reemerging infectious diseases of animals.
