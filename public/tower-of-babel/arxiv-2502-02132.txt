How Memory in Optimization Algorithms Implicitly Modifies the Loss
Matias D. Cattaneo, Boris Shigida

Abstract
In modern optimization methods used in deep learning, each update depends on the history of previous iterations, often referred to as memory , and this dependence decays fast as the iterates go further into the past. For example, gradient descent with momentum has exponentially decaying memory through exponentially averaged past gradients. We introduce a general technique for identifying a memoryless algorithm that approximates an optimization algorithm with memory. It is obtained by replacing all past iterates in the update by the current one, and then adding a correction term arising from memory (also a function of the current iterate). This correction term can be interpreted as a perturbation of the loss, and the nature of this perturbation can inform how memory implicitly (anti-)regularizes the optimization dynamics. As an application of our theory, we find that Lion does not have the kind of implicit anti-regularization induced by memory that AdamW does, providing a theory-based explanation for Lion’s better generalization performance recently documented [ 13 ] . Empirical evaluations confirm our theoretical findings.

It is reasonable to expect that adding exponentially decaying memory to an algorithm in such a way as described above (for example, replacing the gradient with exponentially averaged past gradients) changes the optimization dynamics, thereby affecting the performance of the trained model. The technique we introduce identifies
how
the iteration evolution changes when memory is added. This technique starts with an iteration having memory, and replaces it by a memoryless iteration that approximates the original one, provided a correction term is added. Specifically, we start with algorithm (
1
), and then construct a corresponding new memoryless iteration:

{\color[rgb]{0.55,0,0}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}=}\underbracket{\color[rgb]{0.55,0,0}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})}_{\text{depends on the whole history $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$}}
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}=\underbracket{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big[\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\overbracket{\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})}^{\text{correction}}}\big]}_{\text{only depends on $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}$ (no memory)}},

(4)

where we slightly abuse notation and put

\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})\equiv\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\underbrace{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}_{\text{$n+1$ times}}),

and where the function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
captures a correction due to the presence of memory.
We then prove an explicit bound on the approximation error
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\|
, as a function of the learning rate
h

Our theory only relies on memory decaying sufficiently fast, not necessarily in the form of momentum variables, and thus covers all the examples listed above and many others, while also allowing for both full-batch and mini-batch training.

where in the last step we used
\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(s)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})-\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})=O((n-s){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})
, which follows from Taylor expansion and
Assumption

3.1
. Insert this into
Equation

8
and use
Assumption

3.1
again to continue:

\displaystyle F_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}).

We conclude that the original numerical iteration can be rewritten in the form (
4
),

where the linear in
h
correction function is defined as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}=(M_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,M_{d}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}
with

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\boxed{M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).}}

(9)

The derivation of the memoryless iteration is now complete. Although not a proof yet, it is the first step towards the approximation bound constituting our main theoretical result.

Approximation Bound

An argument similar to the derivation in

3.1
can be made to obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.2

(Memoryless approximation: 1-step error bound)
.

Under

3.1
, there exists a discrete memoryless iteration
\big\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfying (
4
) with initial condition
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
, correction function defined in

9
, and a constant
C_{1}
not depending on

, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}\big\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})\big\|_{\infty}\leqslant C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3}.

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}

(Global error bound on a finite “time” horizon)

In the setting of

3.2
, let

be the sequence of vectors generated by the iteration in

1

\max_{n\in -NoValue-[0\nolinebreak\mathinner{:}\nolinebreak\lfloor T/{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\rfloor -NoValue-]}\big\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\|_{\infty}\leqslant C_{\ref{globalerrorbound}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}

4
Application: the Effect of Memory on AdamW, Lion and Signum

We first study AdamW with memory by an application of
Theorems

3.2
and
3.3
.
Neglecting coefficients decaying to zero exponentially fast, we have

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}=(1-\lambda{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg\lparen\underbrace{\frac{\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})}{\sqrt{\big\lparen\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\big\rparen^{2}+\varepsilon}}}_{\approx\;\sign(\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}))}+\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\bigg\rparen,

where
\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is given by

{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg\lparen\frac{\beta_{1}(1-\beta_{1})^{-1}-\beta_{2}(1-\beta_{2})^{-1}}{\big\lparen\big|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|^{2}+\varepsilon\big\rparen^{1/2}}+\varepsilon\frac{\beta_{2}(1-\beta_{2})^{-1}}{\big\lparen\big|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|^{2}+\varepsilon\big\rparen^{3/2}}\bigg\rparen\big\lparen\nabla\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}+\lambda\nabla^{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\theta}\big\rparen.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{1,\varepsilon}
is the perturbed one-norm defined as
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{v_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon}
. Taking
\varepsilon
to zero, we can write this in the form of preconditioned gradient descent (with decoupled weight decay):

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}=(1-\lambda{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\frac{\nabla\tilde{{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})}{\big|\big\lparen\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\big\rparen\big|},

where

\tilde{{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\overbrace{\big[1+\lambda\big\lparen\tfrac{\beta_{2}}{1-\beta_{2}}-\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}\big\rparen h\big]{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}^{\text{rescaled loss}}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big\lparen\tfrac{\beta_{2}}{1-\beta_{2}}-\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}\big\rparen\overbrace{\big\lparen\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1}+\lambda\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\big\rparen}^{(*)}

is the modified loss. Assuming
\beta_{2}>\beta_{1}
, we see that
(*)
is implicitly anti-penalized. By Theorem 1.1 in

[
66
]
, full-batch AdamW converges to a KKT point of the constrained optimization
\min_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1/\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. If
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1/\lambda
, then the norm
\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1}
dominates the term
\lambda\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}
in absolute value, so the main effect of memory is anti-penalizing the one-norm of the gradient. Thus,
if weight decay is sufficiently small, memory anti-regularizes (large-batch) AdamW
. Incidentally, by Lemma 3.8 in that work,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a KKT point of this optimization problem if and only if the constraint is satisfied and
(*)=0
. This is a generalization of the observation that the correction term is zero if and only if the point is stationary, true of simpler full-batch algorithms (for Adam with
\lambda=0
it follows from the above; for full-batch GD with momentum it is clear from (
6
)).

Consider now Lion-
\mathcal{K}
(
Example

1.5
). Neglecting terms going to zero exponentially fast as
n\to\infty
, the memoryless iteration is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\lambda)\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big[-\nabla\mathcal{K}\big\lparen-\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\big\rparen+\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\big]

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\frac{\rho_{1}}{1-\rho_{2}}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{K}(-\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\nabla^{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big[\nabla\mathcal{K}(-\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))-\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}\big].

As mentioned above, ordinary Lion is recovered by setting
\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x})=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}
. This function is not differentiable, so let us replace it with the smooth convex approximation
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1,\varepsilon}
, where
\varepsilon
is a small positive constant. The results of
Section

3
can be applied, and the memoryless iteration is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg[\frac{\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})}{\big\lparen|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})|^{2}+\varepsilon\big\rparen^{1/2}}+\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\bigg]

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\frac{\rho_{1}}{1-\rho_{2}}\frac{\varepsilon}{\big\lparen|\nabla_{r}{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon\big\rparen^{3/2}}\nabla_{r}\big[\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}+\lambda(\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big].

This term is small as long as
\varepsilon
is small. Therefore, better generalization of Lion on a number of tasks
[
13
]
may be partially attributed to the fact that memory does
not
anti-regularize Lion. In addition, notice that the correction terms are exactly the same for Adam with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=:\beta
and Lion with
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\beta
.
Since Lion with
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
is Signum
[
8
]
, we provide a novel perspective on the similarity between Adam with
\beta_{1}\approx\beta_{2}
and Signum, a point discussed and verified empirically in
[
69
]
.

5
Further Implications

5.1
Modified Equations

We have taken a very general algorithm (
1
) and converted it (under
Assumption

3.1
) to a memoryless iteration (
4
) with
O(h^{2})
uniform error bound on a finite time horizon (
Corollary

3.3
). Since this iteration has no memory, standard methods can be used to derive an ordinary differential equation (ODE) in the form
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{G}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=:\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})

whose continuous solution approximates this iteration and hence the initial algorithm (with the same approximation guarantee). Similarly to
Section

2
, the derivation is heuristic (although the approximation bound is easily made rigorous) and proceeds as follows. Relating the iteration number
n
of a discrete iteration and the time point
t=n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}
on a continuous trajectory, we would like the continuous trajectory to satisfy the same one-step relation as the discrete iteration, up to
O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3})
:

\boldsymbol{\theta}((n+1){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}(n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big[\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}(n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}))+\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}))\big]+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3}).

In fact, we will ensure it is true for
n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}
replaced by any
t
:

\boldsymbol{\theta}(t+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big[\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))+\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))\big]+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3}).

(10)

But, using a Taylor expansion, and recalling that we are finding the trajectory satisfying
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)=\boldsymbol{G}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))
, hence
\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)=\nabla\boldsymbol{G}_{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}(t+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)+\frac{{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}}{2}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big\{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))\big\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}}{2}\big\{\nabla\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})\big\}+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}\bigg\{\boldsymbol{G}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))+\frac{\nabla\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))}{2}\bigg\}+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3}).

In order to match (
10
), we need

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\bigg\lparen\boldsymbol{M}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}+\frac{\nabla\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{2}\bigg\rparen.

So, apart from the correction term coming from memory, the ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
derived has another term

{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}\frac{\nabla\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{2}

arising from the fact that the algorithm is discrete.

For the example of full-batch heavy-ball momentum GD as in
Section

2
, where
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-(1-\beta)^{-1}\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(ignoring coefficients going to zero exponentially fast in
n
), this additional term is equal to
{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}(1-\beta)^{-2}\nabla\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}/4
, providing additional implicit regularization. We recover the ODE derived by
Kovachki and Stuart [37]
,
Ghosh et al. [26]
:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\frac{\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{1-\beta}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\frac{1+\beta}{(1-\beta)^{3}}\frac{\nabla\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}}{4}.

5.2
Mini-Batch Training

In specific cases, it is possible to identify the additional implicit regularization that is introduced to the algorithm by noise, if mini-batch training is used as opposed to full-batch. Assume that the form of
\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
is given by

\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{k}\boldsymbol{g}^{(\pi(n-k))}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}),

where the
\{\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}(\cdot)\}_{k=0}^{n}
functions are uniformly bounded along with two derivatives, and
\pi
is a random permutation of
\lparen 0,\ldots,n\rparen
(chosen among all such permutations with equal probability). The interpretation is that
n
is a large number of mini-batches in one epoch, and mini-batches are sampled randomly without replacement.

The correction term introduced by memory (
9
) is

M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\beta\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta^{k}\nabla g_{r}^{(\pi(n-1-k))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}\sum_{b=0}^{n-l}\beta^{b}\boldsymbol{g}^{(\pi(n-l-b))}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

We can take the average
\mathbb{E}
over all permutations
\pi
(re-orderings of mini-batches). Deferring some details to
Appendix

E
, the result is that for large
n

\mathbb{E}[M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]/{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\approx\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{3}}\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
+\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{2}(1+\beta)}\mathbb{E}[\lparen\nabla g_{r}^{(\pi(1))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lparen\boldsymbol{g}^{(\pi(1))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen].

The second term can be interpreted as implicit regularization by noise. For clarity,
\pi(1)
is a uniformly distributed random variable over
\{0,\ldots,n\}
, so this expectation is an average over mini-batch indices.

For example, take
\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
the
k
th minibatch loss. Then we obtained that “on average” mini-batch GD with momentum is given by the iteration like (
6
), except the modified loss has an additional regularization term:

\tilde{{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})={\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\beta}{2(1-\beta)^{2}}\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}+\underbracket{\frac{{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\beta}{2(1-\beta)(1+\beta)}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}^{(\pi(1))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}}_{\text{regularization by mini-batch noise}}.

6
Limitations and Future Directions

The approximation bounds in
Section

3.2
are in terms of the learning rate
h
, which means that
h
has to be sufficiently small for (our approximations and hence) the optimization trajectories to be close. This is a standard limitation in the literature. Fortunately, practically relevant learning rates are often small enough (especially mid-training, if a learning rate decay schedule is used). Additionally, there is a non-negligible effect of mini-batch noise on the picture we are describing in
Appendix

J
; in particular, Lion does not necessarily outperform Adam if batches are small
[
13
]
. We are able to characterize this effect using similar techniques, but this is out of scope of this article and is a work in progress.

Taking a broader view, one may question the effect of (explicit or implicit) regularization on training progress and outcomes in deep learning, which is an intricate question not easily amenable to theoretical analysis
[
67
,
1
,
34
]
.
Between 2023 and 2025, the mainstream paradigm in deep learning (especially large language models)
is best described as scaling-centric rather than generalization-centric
[
65
]
which decreased interest in implicit regularization.
The main purpose of this work is to introduce a general framework for identifying correction terms, whether they are treated as implicit regularizers or not. In the future, it is likely possible to build on it to study implications for the training dynamics, including characterizing the properties of the loss landscape around the optimizer’s trajectory.
Additionally, it is reasonable to anticipate that because of limited high-quality data, overfitting is about to become a widely important issue again, if not already
[
65
, Section 7]
,
[
62
,
35
]
.

Finally, we discuss some of the most popular optimizers in recent years, but other important algorithms like Shampoo
[
30
,
58
]
or its versions are also amenable to this analysis, and the approximation results in
Section

3.2
hold for them (assuming a typical choice of momentum schemes). However, interpreting the higher-order corrections is not trivial, and we leave that as additional future work.

Appendix A
Related Literature

Approximating a memoryful iteration with a memoryless one is closely connected with the method of modified equations (sometimes called
backward error analysis
), where a discrete algorithm like (
1
) is approximated by a continuous solution of an ordinary differential equation or stochastic differential equation. Typically, this method can only be applied to an algorithm with no memory, in a possibly enlarged phase space as opposed to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; for example, heavy-ball momentum GD has no memory when viewed as a discrete iteration
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{m}^{(n)})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, where
\boldsymbol{m}^{(n)}
is the “momentum variable”. The general technique introduced in this paper can be used to derive a memoryless discrete iteration which can then be approximated by a continuous trajectory. Background on the method of modified equations can be found in
Ernst Hairer and Wanner [23]
,
Li et al. [42]
.

Works deriving modified equations for (S)GD with or without momentum include
Barrett and Dherin [6]
,
Smith et al. [59]
,
Ghosh et al. [26]
,
Farazmand [24]
,
Kovachki and Stuart [37]
,
Miyagawa [50]
,
Rosca et al. [56]
,
Li et al. [42]
. In particular,
Ghosh et al. [26]
identified that momentum strongly regularizes the loss function in the case of GD, though their error bounds both have a different focus (continuous approximation rather than discrete one), and follow a different approach which appears hard to generalize to other algorithms.
Our approach works for a wide class of algorithms, and we recover their main results in
Section

5
.
Works approximating adaptive methods with continuous trajectories include
Ma et al. [47]
,
Malladi et al. [48]
,
Barakat and Bianchi [5]
,
Compagnoni et al. [17]
. More recently,
Cattaneo et al. [11]
studied the special case of Adam / RMSProp.
We built on this work to conduct empirical evaluations.
Their focus is not on memory but on continuous approximations; in particular, they do not have approximation bounds between two discrete iterations like we do. In addition, their arguments are highly specialized to Adam, and they do not incorporate weight decay. Although we also discuss Adam (with weight decay) extensively, it is only because of its importance in practice, and our results cover a much broader class of optimizers.

This paper is also connected to the strand of the literature studying the implicit bias of optimization algorithms. For example,
Xie and Li [66]
and
Chen et al. [12]
prove that weight decay causes AdamW and Lion to solve an
\ell_{\infty}
-norm constrained optimization problem. In that, they behave asymptotically like (normalized) steepest descent with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
-norm.
Bernstein and Newhouse [7]
also view Adam and Lion as smoothed-out versions of steepest descent.
This perspective is connected to the Moreau-Yosida approximation of the loss function
[
9
]
; the latter work provides a concrete way to write down popular optimizers (including SGD with momentum, RMSProp and Adam) as a sequence of optimization problems.
In addition, a large body of work is devoted to the bias of optimization algorithms towards the direction of the max-margin vector

[
60
,
51
,
52
,
55
,
63
,
29
,
32
,
33
]
. Similarly,
Damian et al. [19]
,
Li et al. [44]
,
Arora et al. [4]
,
Wen et al. [64]
,
Damian et al. [20]
explore the sharpness of regions SGD converges to.
Gunasekar et al. [28]
,
Arora et al. [3]
study implicit regularization in matrix factorization.
Li et al. [43]
prove in a certain setting that a larger learning rate leads to better generalization.

Broader Impacts

This paper presents a general framework for contrasting certain properties of optimization algorithms commonly used for training neural networks, and thus this work can lead to societal consequences as common of deep learning.

Appendix E
Mini-Batch Training: Details

Let us take the expectation of the correction term with respect to the random permutation of mini-batches, that is, take the average over all re-orderings
\pi
of
(\boldsymbol{g}^{(0)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}^{(n)})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta^{k}\nabla g_{r}^{(\pi(n-1-k))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}\sum_{b=0}^{n-l}\beta^{b}\boldsymbol{g}^{(\pi(n-l-b))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad:=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta^{k}\nabla g_{r}^{(\pi(n-1-k))}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}\sum_{b=0}^{n-l}\beta^{b}\boldsymbol{g}^{(\pi(n-l-b))}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Note that
\mathbb{E}\nabla g_{r}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
depends only on whether
i=j
or
i\neq j
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]/{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}=C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+C_{\ref{twoneq}}(\beta)\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})],

where
C_{3}(\beta)
and
C_{4}(\beta)
are given by

\displaystyle C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta):=\beta\sum_{b=0}^{n-1}\beta^{b}\sum_{l=1}^{b+1}\beta^{b+1-l}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{2}(1+\beta)},

\displaystyle C_{\ref{twoneq}}(\beta):=\beta\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta^{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}\sum_{b=0}^{n-l}\beta^{b}-C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{2\beta^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{3}(1+\beta)}.

We can simplify

\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\frac{1}{(n+1)n}\sum_{i\neq j}\nabla g_{r}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+o_{n}(1),

where
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the average of
\{\boldsymbol{g}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
,
o_{n}(1)
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
.

So, for large
n
we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]/{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\approx C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+C_{\ref{twoneq}}(\beta)\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad=\big\lparen C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)+C_{\ref{twoneq}}(\beta)\big\rparen\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)\mathbb{E}[\lparen\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lparen\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen]

\displaystyle\quad\approx\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{3}}\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+C_{\ref{twoeq}}(\beta)\mathbb{E}[\lparen\nabla g_{r}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla g_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lparen\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rparen].

Appendix F
Proof of
Theorem

3.2

Since, by the assumptions of the theorem,

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{r}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\theta}_{r}^{(n)}=-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\quad-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}\underline{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})},

(12)

we need to show that

\displaystyle F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}).

(13)

By Taylor expansion with the Lagrange remainder,

\displaystyle F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\rparen

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{n}\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k_{1})}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\rparen^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\frac{\partial^{2}F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k_{1})}\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k_{2})}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k_{2})}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\rparen

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(\char 97\relax)}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\rparen+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}),

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is some point on the segment between
\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}\big\rparen
and
\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\rparen
; in (a) we used
Assumption

3.1
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k_{1})}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}=O(k_{1}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k_{2})}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}=O(k_{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})
.
Since the underlined term in
Equation

12
is
O(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}=\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s+1)}\big\rparen

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\big\{\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)})+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})\big\}

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})+O(k^{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}),

where in the last step we used
\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(s)})-\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})=O((n-s){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})
, which follows from Taylor expansion,
Assumption

3.1
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}=O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}})
. Combine this with

14
to get

\displaystyle F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\bigg\{{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})+O(k^{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2})\bigg\}+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{F}^{(s)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)})+O({\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}),

which is (

Appendix G

\displaystyle=d_{1}\bigg\lparen 1+\frac{d_{3}}{d_{1}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg\rparen e^{d_{2}(n-1){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}\leqslant d_{1}\lparen 1+d_{2}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\rparen e^{d_{2}(n-1){\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}

by

,

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{1}e^{d_{2}n{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{2}

by the inequality

for all

.

Next, write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\quad=-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})-\big\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}\big\}

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big[\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\big]-\big\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})\big\}

Then

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big|F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\big|+\big|\tilde{\theta}_{r}^{(n+1)}-\tilde{\theta}_{r}^{(n)}+{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}F^{(n)}_{r}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})\big|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\big|F_{r}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)})-F_{r}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\big|+C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3}

by

3.2
,

\displaystyle\quad={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg|\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{r}^{(n)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\big\lparen\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n-k)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-k)}\big\rparen\bigg|+C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}^{3},

\boldsymbol{\zeta}

Lemma H.2
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{b_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be sequences of reals such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lparen|a_{k}|+|b_{k}|\rparen<\infty
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}b_{s}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Take such positive integer
n_{0}
that for any
n_{0}\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant n_{2}
we have
\sum_{s=n_{1}}^{n_{2}}\lparen|a_{k}|+|b_{k}|\rparen<\varepsilon
. Then for any

the following holds:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{k}|\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}|b_{s}|=\sum_{k=1}^{n-n_{0}}|a_{k}|\underbrace{\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}|b_{s}|}_{<\varepsilon}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=n-n_{0}+1}^{n}|a_{k}|}_{<\varepsilon}\sum_{s=n-k}^{n-1}|b_{s}|<\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lparen|a_{k}|+|b_{k}|\rparen.

Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lparen|a_{k}|+|b_{k}|\rparen
is a finite constant, the statement follows.
∎

J.1
A Note on the Edge of Stability and Comparisons with Lion on Vision Tasks

Cohen et al. [16]
notice that in a sense Adam trains at the edge of stability. They view Adam as momentum gradient descent with evolving preconditioner

\mathbf{P}_{t+1}=(1-\beta_{1}^{t+1})\bigg[\diag\bigg\lparen\sqrt{\frac{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t+1}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t+1}}}\bigg\rparen+\epsilon\mathbf{I}\bigg].

They define “preconditioned sharpness” to be the top eigenvalue of the preconditioned Hessian
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{P}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t})
, where
\mathbf{H}_{t}
is the Hessian of the loss, and observe that this quantity often oscillates around the stability threshold
\frac{2+2\beta_{1}}{(1-\beta_{1})\eta}
, where
\eta
is the learning rate. (This fraction comes from the fact that if the preconditioner were constant, Adam would become a form of preconditioned gradient descent with EMA-style momentum, and this is the ordinary stability threshold of EMA-style heavy-ball momentum on the quadratic Taylor approximation of the loss; we refer to
Cohen et al. [16]
for details.) They use large-batch training on CIFAR-10/100. We train a CNN on CIFAR-10 as well, and reproduce this result in
Figure

5
. We also plot ordinary sharpness
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{H}_{t})
(top hessian eigenvalue), which first increases and then decreases. Recall that this is an extremely unstable regime of training
[
47
]
.

oscillates around the stability threshold. Right: the plots of ordinary sharpness

Note that the parameter controlling the exponential forgetting of gradients
\beta_{1}
corresponds to the
\rho_{2}
parameter of Lion, so the default
\rho_{2}=0.99
in Lion would match
\beta_{1}=0.99
rather than
\beta_{1}=0.9
in Adam.
If we take
\beta_{1}=0.99
which is the “smooth” regime of training, preconditioned sharpness does not reach the stability threshold (
Figure

6
). Note also that ordinary sharpness
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{H}_{t})
(top hessian eigenvalue) is much lower for small
\beta_{2}
(especially noticeable for
\beta_{1}\leqslant 0.9
). This suggests that in large-batch training on vision tasks, taking
\beta_{2}<\beta_{1}=0.99
strongly regularizes training, moving the model parameters to flatter regions of the loss space. It is a promising direction to investigate the limits of such regularization: for example, taking
\beta_{2}
near the threshold of divergence may regularize training so much that the default Lion will not be able to match in terms of generalization error.

AdamW: memoryless update

The (full-batch) memoryless AdamW approximation is

\theta^{(n+1)}_{j}=\theta^{(n)}_{j}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}F^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}),\\

where

\displaystyle F^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sqrt{|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon}}+\lambda\theta_{j},

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-h\biggl(\frac{\beta_{2}}{1-\beta_{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\beta_{2}^{n+1}}{1-\beta_{2}^{n+1}}\biggr)\frac{|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}\big\lparen\nabla_{j}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}+\lambda[\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{j}\big\rparen}{(|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=}+h\biggl(\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}-\frac{(n+1)\beta_{1}^{n+1}}{1-\beta_{1}^{n+1}}\biggr)\frac{\big\lparen\nabla_{j}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}+\lambda[\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\theta}]_{j}\big\rparen}{\sqrt{|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon}}.

Lion-
\mathcal{K}
with bias correction

The Lion-
\mathcal{K}
algorithm with bias correction is defined as in Example 1.5 of the paper except bias correction is added:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{m}_{1}^{(n+1)}+\boldsymbol{m}_{2}^{(n+1)})+\boldsymbol{m}_{3}^{(n+1)},

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{m}_{1}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-\rho_{2}}{1-\rho_{2}^{n+1}}\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho_{2}^{n-k}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{2}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=-\bigg\lparen 1-\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}}\bigg\rparen\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{3}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}.

\varepsilon
): memoryless update

The memoryless iteration is given by

\theta^{(n+1)}_{j}=\theta^{(n)}_{j}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}F^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})-{\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}),\\

where

\displaystyle F^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sqrt{|\nabla_{j}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon}}+\lambda\theta_{j},

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle={\hyperref@@ii[def:lr]{h}}\bigg[\frac{\rho_{1}}{1-\rho_{2}}-\frac{(n+1)\rho_{2}^{n}\rho_{1}}{1-\rho_{2}^{n+1}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\varepsilon}{\big\lparen|\nabla_{j}{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon\big\rparen^{3/2}}\nabla_{j}\big[\|\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}+\lambda(\nabla{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\hyperref@@ii[def:loss]{\mathcal{L}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big].
