RFW captures species-level metagenomic functions by integrating genome annotation information
Authors: Kai Mi, Rui Xu, Xingyin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryFunctional profiling of whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing (WMS) enables our understanding of microbe-host interactions. We demonstrate microbial functional information loss by current annotation methods at both the taxon and community levels, particularly at lower read depths. To address information loss, we develop a framework, RFW (reference-based functional profile inference on WMS), that utilizes information from genome functional annotations and taxonomic profiles to infer microbial function abundances from WMS. Furthermore, we provide an algorithm for absolute abundance change quantification between groups as part of the RFW framework. By applying RFW to several datasets related to autism spectrum disorder and colorectal cancer, we show that RFW augments downstream analyses, such as differential microbial function identification and association analysis between microbial function and host phenotype. RFW is open source and freely available at https://github.com/Xingyinliu-Lab/RFW.

Introduction

Understanding the integral roles of microbial function in the maintenance of metabolism and microbiome homeostasis is key to deciphering the host-microbiome interaction.1,2 Increasing functional profiling tools for shotgun metagenomic data by quantification of the microbial genes and metabolic pathways have been reported in recent years.3,4,5,6,7,8

Whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing (WMS) has empowered species-level resolution of microbial functions. The tools for analyzing WMS functional profiles can be classified into two main categories, i.e., assembly based and sequence based. An early practice of assembly-based functional profiling was that Qin et al. annotated functions of predicted open reading frames (ORFs) from assembled contigs and featured the human gut microbial gene catalog.9 In the same period, the sequence-based functional profiling tool MG RAST server4 was proposed, assigning the potential function to each sequence by a BLAST10 search against the SEED11 database. Subsequently, MEGAN5 introduced KEGG12 metabolic maps for microbial bioactivity pathway analysis. Given the latter sequence-based strategy would be more friendly to functional abundance (FA) quantification, most follow-up methods, including ShotMAP,13 COGNIZER,14 FMAP,15 mi-faser,16 Fun4Me,17 HUMAnN6,7,8 etc., were constructed upon the strategy. These methods built their pipeline utilizing different alignment programs (BLAST, DIAMOND,18 FragGeneScan,19 RAPSearch2,20 etc.) and different reference databases (KEGG, Pfam,21 GO,22 SEED, eggNOG,23 manually curated database, etc.). Instead of sequence alignment, Carnelian et al. trained a Vowpal Wabbit one-against-all (OAA) classifier to predict the function of the k-merized sequence directly24; Hoarfrost et al. presented LookingGlass for the interpretation of short DNA reads by deep learning.25

Among these methods, HUMAnN is considered a state-of-the-art approach and has been applied in multiple recent studies.26,27,28,29,30 Compared with other methods, HUMAnN counted the reads alignment hits in a weighted manner rather than relying solely on best-hit assignments. HUMAnN also embraced a tiered-search manner in sequence alignment, which significantly accelerated the mapping computation and accuracy. Notably, HUMAnN was used as benchmark for genus-level microbial function prediction from 16S rRNA sequencing, including PICRUSt, Tax4Fun, and PICRUSt2.31,32

Currently, shotgun metagenomic functional profiling methods rely on the information extracted from the sequencing reads. However, Zhernakova et al. reported that the profiled microbial gene function richness had a significant association with sequencing depth, while the microbial composition was robust when confronted with sequencing depth.33 Furthermore, PICRUSt demonstrated better agreement with WMS HUMAnN when using a deeper sequencing depth.34 These findings raised our concern about the adequacy of WMS as a proxy for the true metagenome in functional profiling.

Here, we present RFW (reference-based functional profile inference on WMS), a framework for functional profile prediction from WMS taxonomic profiles using the genome function reference database. Our results show that using HUMAnN for WMS analysis resulted in functional information incompleteness due to sampling depth and that RFW can help to address the issue. To address potential bias arising from relative abundance, we further developed DFSCA-BC (differential functional sub-community abundance identification with bias correction). This method for differential absolute microbial FA analysis between groups was built upon our previously published taxonomic absolute abundance change quantification method QMD (quantification of microbial absolute abundance differences).35 Finally, we applied RFW and DFSCA-BC to several datasets to demonstrate the full profile of functional annotations from metagenomics data.

RFW and DFSCA-BC are open source and freely available at https://github.com/Xingyinliu-Lab/RFW. RFW supports multiple forms of microbial function description, including KEGG Orthology (KO) EC (enzyme commission) numbers36 and clusters of orthologous genes (COG).37 Because the EC numbers could be directly mapped into MetaCyc38,39 pathways, we selected 4th-level EC enzyme families (hereafter referred to enzymes) for our analysis and comparisons.

Results

Function profiling on WMS by HUMAnN exhibited functional information loss

Microbial functional profiles are a representation of the metabolic and physiological capabilities of a microbial community. To assess the adequacy of WMS for microbial function profiling, we constructed a microbial function reference database by annotating protein functions of high-quality representative genomes from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (release r207),40 covering 65,703 bacterial and archaeal species encompassing 317,542 genomes (see STAR Methods). The GTDB was developed utilizing genome information obtained from environmental and clinical samples, with NCBI Assembly database41 as its sole genome repository, which encompasses microbial genome information from a wide range of host habitats. This approach leverages the microbe genome as the most comprehensive source of information on its bioactivity function. We used eggNOG-mapper v.2 to predict the functional annotations of the non-representative genomes. Microbial functional annotations from KEGG provided an orthogonal method to compare the genome-annotated reference. Potential functions of three common bacteria, Escherichia coli UMN026 (Figure S1A), Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron VPI 5482 (Figure S1B), and Lactobacillus reuteri DSM 20016 (Figure S1C), were mapped against each other by Venn plot. The encoded enzymes from the genome annotation result were in agreement with that from KEGG at 90%. Representative genomes for each GTDB species cluster serve as proxies for providing species-level microbial functional information. To assess the intra-species diversity of microbial functions, we randomly selected 7–30 non-representative genomes (average completeness: 99.19%) for the ten bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Alistipes indistinctus, resulting in a total of 266 genomes. Our evaluation revealed a high degree of functional consistency between the non-representative genomes and their corresponding representative genomes. Using the representative genome to assess bacterial function resulted in a true positive rate (TPR) of 97.0% and a true negative rate (TNR) of 99.7% (Figures S1D and S1E). These values exhibited slight intra-species variations.

We used the reference database to investigate whether HUMAnN could identify all microbial functions in WMS from cultured bacterial isolates, in which the potential effects of complex microbiome composition on functional profiling were reduced. The WMS dataset came from Elie et al.,42,43 involving 15 bacteria isolated from C57BL/6J mice (see Table S1). When comparing the genome-annotated and HUMAnN-reported bacteria enzymes, a discernible lack of WMS functional profiling was observed, and approximately 30% of genome functions were mis-detected by HUMAnN (Figure 1A) in a species-dependent manner (Figures 1B–1E). For example, WMS on the Escherichia coli isolate covered 88.3% of enzymes (962/(962 + 128)) (Figure 1B), whereas Parabacteroides goldsteinii coverage was only 50.3% (366/(366 + 362)) (Figure 1E). 109 EC enzymes in total were missing in more than half of the 15 bacteria, provided in Table S2. Specifically, EC 3.1.3.100 (thiamine phosphate phosphatase) and EC 3.6.5.4 (signal-recognition-particle GTPase) were encoded by all 15 bacteria according to their genomes. Additionally, enzyme protein information collected from KEGG and UniProt supported the bacterial encoding capability (see Table S3). Next, we investigated HUMAnN’s performance on more complex microbiome samples by analyzing a total of 589 WMS samples from healthy people or patients with adenoma or colorectal cancer (CRC) in five established cohorts, including Feng et al.44 (ERP008729), Vogtmann et al.45 (PRJEB12449), Wirbel et al.46 (PRJEB27928), Yu et al.47 (PRJEB10878), and Zeller et al.48 (PRJEB6070). Ten common bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Alistipes indistinctus, were selected to evaluate the taxon-level functional profiling of HUMAnN. Relative abundance (calculated by Kraken249) multiplied by the sample reads count was used to represent the sequencing depth for each taxon. When the sequencing reads aligned to Escherichia coli were less than 106, the number of EC enzymes reported by HUMAnN declined sharply (Figure 1F). Synchronously, the mis-detected genome function increased with a shallow sequencing depth (Figure 1G). It was also noticed that HUMAnN-reported enzymes had a high degree of agreement with the genome annotation function (Figure 1H). With the average total sequencing reads in a WMS sample around 107 (Figure S1F), HUMAnN-reported functions would decrease significantly if the relative abundance of Escherichia coli were lower than 5%–10%. Similar conclusions were drawn for the remaining nine bacteria (Figures S2A–S2I). We obtained the same conclusion using another relative abundance calculation method, MetaPhlAn450,51 (Figures S2J–S2S).

At the community level, we calculated the potential repertoire of microbial functions by integrating the genome annotation reference database and taxonomic information identified obtained from either Kraken2 or MetaPhlAn4. We then compared the results to these generated by HUMAnN. Consistent with the observations at the individual taxon level, HUMAnN exhibited substantial functional information loss at the community level (Figure 1I). Inference from Kraken2 profiling yielded a greater number of enzymes than that from MetaPhlAn4, likely due to the higher number of taxa identified by Kraken2. According to the number of sequencing reads, we divided the enrolled WMS samples into four-quarters. A trend was observed in four datasets that HUMAnN reported more enzymes with increased sequencing depth at the community level (Figure 1J). This directly translates to a greater number of detected genome functions (Figure 1K).

Insufficient sequencing depth is a major cause of information loss in HUMAnN functional analysis

To further investigate the impact of sequencing depth on information loss, we assessed the performance of HUMAnN on an ultra-deeply sequenced human gut microbiome metagenomic dataset. The data were derived from Hao et al.52 (PRJNA763692), comprised of six metagenomes from three male and three female individuals, with sequencing depths ranging from 3.00 × 108 to 4.08 × 108 reads (after low-quality reads removal and decontamination), approximately 10-fold higher than typical shotgun metagenomic sequencing depths. To systematically evaluate the effect of sequencing depth, we resampled the data at seven levels, 100%, 75%, 50%, 25%, 10%, 5%, and 1%, generating a total of 42 sequence files spanning a wide range from 1/10th to 10 times the depth of conventional metagenomic sequencing. During the analysis, the 100% resampled samples served as the benchmark against which the information loss in microbial functional analysis was compared for the resampled datasets.

On the resampled ultra-deep sequencing WMS datasets, a linear increase in the number of community-level microbial functions reported by HUMAnN with increasing sequencing depth could be immediately observed (adjusted R-squared: 0.817, p: <2.2 × 10−16, Figure 2A). Correspondingly, the number of genome-annotated microbial functions identified by HUMAnN also increased (Figure 2B), while information loss was mitigated as the sequencing depth increased (Figure 2C). These findings were derived from the reference database in conjunction with Kraken2 taxonomic profiling to obtain the genome-annotated microbial functions of the samples. The increase in sequencing depth improved the detection of low-abundance microbes, resulting in a higher number of microbial species reported by Kraken2 (Figure 2D). However, the community-level microbial functions estimated based on Kraken2 taxonomic profiling approached stability once sequencing reads exceeded 1 × 107 (Figure 2E), and the number of mis-detected ECs remained below 50 (Figure 2F), significantly lower than the number of ECs missing in HUMAnN analysis (Figure 2G). This observation implies that estimating microbial functional profiles using taxonomic profiling in conjunction with a genome annotation reference database exhibits considerable robustness when confronted with insufficient sequencing depth. An analysis conducted based on taxonomic profiling outputs from MetaPhlAn4 yielded similar conclusions (Figures S3A and S3B). HUMAnN utilizes taxonomic profiling information generated by MetaPhlAn to expedite the sequence alignment process. For reads that do not align to identified species' genomes, a comprehensive protein database is searched. To enhance the generalizability of our findings, we compared the performance of eggNOG-mapper v.2.1.453,54 in the functional profiling of resampled ultra-deep sequencing data from SRR15910061. The method eggNOG-mapper employs MMseqs2 to perform searching and alignment of sequencing reads against the eggNOG 5.0 database. These designs ensure that the outputs of eggNOG-mapper are not influenced by taxonomic profiling.

Analysis results revealed that HUMAnN reported the fewest ECs (Figure S3G). The number of ECs reported by eggNOG-mapper increased significantly with increasing sequencing depth, gradually approaching the level of EC inference from Kraken2 profiling. The number of ECs inferred from MetaPhlAn4 was lower than that from eggNOG-mapper, possibly due to MetaPhlAn4’s conservative species identification, which ultimately affects the EC information inferred from its profiling (Figure S3G). The higher the number of ECs reported by each method, the higher the number of common ECs detected among the remaining bench data (Figure S3H) and the lower the number of ECs mis-detected (Figure S3I). In summary, the use of eggNOG-mapper demonstrates that information loss in functional profiling using WMS reads-to-function approaches is not dependent on the specific functional profiling method.

At the taxon level, exemplified by Escherichia coli, increasing the sequencing depth facilitated HUMAnN in reporting a greater number of bacterial functions (Figure 2H), reducing the occurrence of functional information loss concomitantly (Figure 2I). This observation was consistently replicated for nine other common bacteria, i.e., Akkermansia muciniphila, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (Figures S4A–S4I). Similar conclusions were drawn when employing MetaPhlAn4 to obtain bacterial abundance information (Figures S4J–S4S). Notably, MetaPhlAn4 reported the abundance of some bacterial taxa, while HUMAnN failed to assign any functional roles to these taxa. Examples include Streptococcus salivarius in the SRR17827823 resampled dataset (Figure S4L), Roseburia intestinalis in the SRR17827823 resampled dataset (Figure S4O), and Escherichia coli in the SRR17827823 resampled dataset (Figure S4S). For certain bacteria, such as Akkermansia muciniphila (with 552 genome-annotated EC4 enzymes) (Figures S4A and S4J) and Prevotella copri (with 569 genome-annotated EC4 enzymes) (Figures S4E and S4N), even when their abundance in the samples was relatively high, with corresponding reads exceeding 1 × 107, a substantial number (>100) of ECs remained consistently unidentified by HUMAnN. These observations align with our previous findings from the analysis of WMS on cultured bacterial isolates, suggesting that in addition to insufficient sequencing depth, the limitations imposed by functional sequence conservation and the inherent short-read nature of second-generation shotgun sequencing further hinder HUMAnN’s accuracy in both species classification and functional annotation, potentially contributing to the information loss.

Aromatic compound metabolic pathways were remarkably mis-detected by HUMAnN

A function reported by HUMAnN is considered as true positive if it is substantiated by genome annotation. The true positive rate (TPR) of HUMAnN detection on the genome functions by Kraken2 (Figure 3A) for around 1,100 bacterial enzymes approached 80%–100% in the 589 WMS samples. Similarly, a high TPR was observed with MetaPhlAn4 (Figure 3B). However, the TPRs of the around 2,000 remaining enzymes were less than 80%, with more than 1,000 enzymes having a TPR below 20%. For example, EC 1.1.1.108 (carnitine 3-dehydrogenase) were predicted to be positive in more than 580 samples at the community level based on the genome information, but HUMAnN only reported it in 3 samples, resulting in a TPR at 0.52% (3/580 = 0.52%). When comparing HUMAnN TPRs with the genome functions inferred from Kraken2 and MetaPhlAn4 taxonomic profiling, respectively, we found a high degree of concordance in the prevalence of inferred genome functions (Figure 3C). Next, we made a further survey on WMS function profiling information loss preference. We employed Fisher’s exact test and identified enzymes with HUMAnN detection rates that differed significantly from their potential genome function. The most involved 20 pathways containing these mis-detected enzymes are listed in Tables S4 and S5. These mis-detected enzymes were enriched in aromatic compound metabolic pathways, including PWY-6954 (superpathway of aromatic compound degradation via 2-hydroxypentadienoate), PWY66-5 (superpathway of cholesterol biosynthesis), ALL-CHORISMATE-PWY (superpathway of chorismate metabolism), and PWY-5655 (L-tryptophan degradation IX).

To illustrate this point, we examined the detailed enzyme detection for PWY-5655 (L-tryptophan degradation IX) in Table S6. Among the 13 enzymes involved in this pathway, EC 1.2.1.10 (acetaldehyde dehydrogenase), EC 3.5.1.9 (arylformamidase), and EC 4.1.3.39 (4-hydroxy-2-oxovalerate aldolase) had high TPRs exceeding 90%. The TPRs for other enzymes were relatively lower. In particular, we highlighted the encoding bacteria for EC 3.7.1.3 (L-kynurenine hydrolase) and EC 4.1.1.45 (aminocarboxymuconate-semialdehyde decarboxylase) at the phylogenetic tree by AnnoTree.55 AnnoTree stated that bacteria from Actinobacteriota (24.96%), Bacteroidota (18.39%), Firmicutes (7.00%), and Proteobacteria (38.00%) were capable of encoding EC 3.7.1.3 (Figure 3D) and bacteria from Actinobacteriota (27.93%), Bacteroidota (16.32%), Firmicutes (7.53%), and Proteobacteria (31.96%) were capable of encoding EC 4.1.1.45 (Figure 3E). In other words, these two enzymes had relatively common functions in bacteria. Our analysis on all community-level potential genome functions also suggested that the two enzymes should be positively detected in most samples. However, HUMAnN only reported 8 and 0 positive samples for EC 3.7.1.3 and EC 4.1.1.45, respectively, highlighting how HUMAnN can miss important microbial functional information. We also observed that there were fewer potential positive samples for EC 3.5.99.5 (2-aminomuconate deaminase) assessed from MetaPhlAn4 than by HUMAnN, again suggesting that the taxonomic profiling by MetaPhlAn4 was too conservative.

KEGG orthologs provide more gene functional information beyond enzyme annotations. Therefore, we further analyzed the information loss of HUMAnN in terms of KEGG orthologs. KEGG orthologs that were reported based on taxonomic profiling (both Kraken2 and MethPhlAn4) in at least 580 out of 589 WMS samples and missed by HUMAnN were piped into the analysis. It was observed that these KEGG orthologs were enriched in functions such as two-component system, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and benzoate degradation (Figure 3F). The above enriched pathways play crucial roles in bacterial physiology and biochemistry, involving cell structure, signal transduction, metabolic regulation, immune evasion, and other critical aspects. Together, the results of the above analysis indicated that the functional profiling in WMS mediated great information loss. Impressively, the annotated genome function reference database could be a good complement for functional profiling.

The RFW framework for functional profile inference on WMS

There is inherent information loss in inferring microbiome functions solely from functional predictions of sequencing reads. To address this, we propose a complementary approach that leverages the functional information contained within microbial genomes. Our strategy involves first determining the taxonomic composition and abundances of the microbiome, then querying the genomes of these species for their respective functional profiles. By multiplying the functional profiles of each species by its abundance, we can generate a more comprehensive estimate of the microbiome’s functional potential. In the RFW framework, we established two schemas for describing microbial functional profile. One is (FA), multiplying the relative abundance of community members by the annotated function counts of their respective reference genomes. The concept of FA is in line with traditional functional profiling methods and used as the benchmark of mock communities in simulation tests of HUMAnN8,56 and PICRUSt.34 We are of the opinion that FA is a middle concept between RNA expression levels and taxon abundance in metagenomics. The second is FCSA, the sum of relative abundances of sub-community members capable of encoding the specific function. This allows FSCA to be easily extended to account for the sub-community abundance involved in pathways. In the RFW program, pathway FSCA is calculated as summing the relative abundances of taxa participating in the pathway, akin to that employed by PICRUSt2.32 Additionally, FSCA offers a powerful advantage in identifying differences in microbial functions between groups, and we will expound this in the next section.

As expected, RFW-FA using taxonomic profiling from Kraken2 (Figure 4B) exhibited a strong positive Pearson correlation for common functions with HUMAnN, similar to MetaPhlAn4 (Figure S5A). Notably, the correlation between RFW-FA with taxonomic profiling from Kraken2 and that from MetaPhlAn4 approached 1, suggesting the robustness of RFW's inferred FA against different taxonomic profiling methods (Figure 4C).

RFW also facilitates taxon-level functional analysis. Shallow sequencing depth, however, reduced the correlations between the FA of Escherichia coli estimated by RFW and HUMAnN (Figure 4D). In other words, HUMAnN’s information loss not only included mis-detected microbial functions but also distorted FA. This conclusion is further supported by analysis on RFW taxon-level abundance for another nine bacteria with taxonomic profiling from Kraken2 (Figures S5B–S5J) and MetaPhlAn4 (Figures S5K–S5T).

The DFSCA-BC algorithm for absolute abundance change quantification of microbial function

Relative abundance is widely used to describe microbiome composition. In contrast, absolute abundance measures the microbial load per unit sample mass (e.g., volume or weight). Our previous work, QMD, has demonstrated that a deviation exists between taxon relative abundance changes and absolute abundance changes. Moreover, the deviation is precisely equal to the total microbial absolute abundance change between groups.35 RFW-FSCA measures the relative abundance of taxa encoding some certain microbial function f, and we found that the deviation still exists when comparing FSCA between groups.

Denote sub-community Lf for microbial function f, i.e., L={Taxon|ϕTaxonf=1}. The absolute abundance change Δf of sub-community Lf between control (C) and treat (T) could be depicted as(Equation 1)Δf=ΔΨ+E(log(FSCAfT))−E(log(FSCAfC))where ΔΨ is the log fold change of total microbial absolute abundance of all taxa between groups, E(log(FSCAfC)) and E(log(FSCAfT)) are the expectations of logged microbial relative abundance of sub-community Lf, i.e., FSCAf, of control and treat, correspondingly. The deviation ΔΨ can be obtained following the pipeline shown in our previous work, QMD.35 A detailed proof on Equation 1 is provided in the STAR Methods section.

Hence, a statistical hypothesis could be established for identifying absolute-abundance-changed microbial functions:(Equation 2)H0:Δf=ΔΨ+E(log(FSCAfT))−E(log(FSCAfC))=0

i.e.,(Equation 3)H0:ΔΨ+E(log(FSCAfT))=E(log(FSCAfC))

In this way, we built up a method for differential microbial function identification. We name the hypothesis test DFSCA-BC. Furthermore, DFSCA-BC can also be used to identify absolute abundance changes of sub-community involved in pathways. Specially, Equation 3 would degrade to H0:ΔΨ=0 for microbial functions that are expressed universally in all microbiota, and the absolute abundance of these microbial functions would be judged as statistically changed if ΔΨ≠0.

RFW enables insights into associations between microbial functions and host phenotypes

Association analysis is another crucial approach in deciphering the host-microbiome interaction. To demonstrate the benefit of full-view profiles derived from RFW in association analysis, we calculated the Pearson correlation between the microbial functions and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) symptom severity of 39 children with ASD from a study by Zhang et al.69 Evidence has been increasingly accumulating to support associations between ASD and gut microbiome dysbiosis.70,71 Our analysis identified 16 microbial functions that exhibited strong correlations with different ASD symptom rating criteria (Table S8).

By integrating biochemical reaction information from KEGG, MetaCyc, Brenda,72 and MetaNetX,73 we found that some of the 16 microbial functions were potentially connected by common metabolic pathways, forming metabolic networks. For instance, EC 6.3.2.2 and EC 4.2.1.22 were involved in a glutamic acid and homocysteine-related metabolic network (Figure 7A). Glutamic acid is a precursor of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Higher blood glutamic acid levels in patients with ASD were previously reported,74 along with a correlation with the severity of social symptoms.75 Mutations on the Homo sapiens isoenzyme of EC 4.2.1.22, cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), can lead to homocystinuria and a variety of CNS disturbances, including ASD.76 Microbial-derived EC 4.2.1.22 exhibited a negative correlation with the RBSR (Repetitive Behavior Scale, Revised) score (Figure 7B). HUMAnN did not detect EC 4.2.1.22 in most samples and thus missed this correlation (Figure 7C). Glutathione cysteine ligase (GCL), the isoenzyme of EC 6.3.2.2, was also found to be impaired in autism.77 RFW (Figure 7D) showed a stronger correlation between EC 6.3.2.2 and CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale) than HUMAnN (Figure 7E).Figure 7Microbial RFW-FSCA correlated with ASD symptoms(A) EC 6.3.2.2 and EC 4.2.1.22 involved metabolic network.(B) Correlation of EC 4.2.1.22 FSCA with ASD RBSR.(C) Correlation of EC 4.2.1.22 HUMAnN with ASD RBSR.(D) Correlation of EC 6.3.2.2 FSCA with ASD CARS.(E) Correlation of EC 6.3.2.2 HUMAnN with ASD CARS.RBSR, Repetitive Behavior Scale, Revised; CARS, Childhood Autism Rating Scale. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ns for non significant.

Other metabolic networks of the 16 microbial functions were related to oxaloacetic acid, acetyl-coenzyme A (CoA), inosine, S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine, pyrocatechol, and phenol (Figure S6). The first two networks (Figures S6A and S6B) were part of the Krebs cycle and associated with bioenergetic fluxes. Discussion

Existing methods for microbial functional profiling suffer from significant information loss, impacting both function detection and abundance calculation. To address this limitation, we developed a new framework, RFW, for microbial function inference on WMS with the aid of an annotated genome function reference database in this work. Previous reference-based functional profile inference methods, i.e., PICRUSt232,34 and Tax4Fun2,86,87 were designed for genus-level function inference based on 16S rRNA gene sequences. Our work was the first species-level function inference method for WMS sequencing data.

RFW’s performance was validated on the WMS datasets from five CRC-healthy cohorts and on the WMS dataset from ASD cases and healthy controls. Both ASD and CRC have been extensively investigated in the context of gut microbiome associations. It is demonstrated that the number of microbial functions reported by HUMAnN on WMS reduced sharply when the relative abundance of taxa was lower than 5%–10%. This implies that achieving complete microbial function information for 1% of abundance taxa would require deep sequencing with 10 times the current depth, 100 times for taxa with an abundance of 0.1%, and so on. This would undoubtedly bring about a high experimental cost. Even for WMS from bacterial isolates, HUMAnN still did not detect multiple microbial functions that had been registered in KEGG and UniProt. This loss might occur during library preparation, sequencing, or alignment search on the short DNA reads. Analysis on HUMAnN’s detection preferences revealed the ongoing lost microbial pathways, including those involved in cholesterol and tryptophan metabolism.

RFW is designed to predict microbial functional profiles from WMS taxonomic profiles using the genome function reference database. Certainly, the accuracy of RFW depends on accurate species identification and abundance estimation. Although this study cannot benchmark the accuracy of taxonomic profiling methods, we evaluated RFW abundance derived from both Kraken2 and MetaPhlAn4, which are commonly used in WMS taxonomic profiling. The results showed a high degree of consistency between RFW abundance estimates from the two methods for co-detected microbial functions. Meanwhile, for both taxonomic profiling methods, RFW revealed significantly more microbial functions than HUMAnN. Research efforts have been devoted to improving taxon abundance estimation accuracy,88 and we also expect to embrace more accurate taxonomic profiling methods in the future.

The completeness of the RFW profile significantly improved the performance of differential microbial function identification. We further proposed a new hypothesis test, DFSCA-BC, for absolute microbial FA changes quantification and differential microbial function identification between groups. RFW and DFSCA-BC highlights several altered microbial functions related to CRC patients. Additionally, RFW facilitated association construction between gut microbial functions and autism symptoms. An interesting point is that while multiple studies have investigated the role of corresponding Homo sapiens isoenzymes, there is a lack of research on the role of these microbiome-derived enzymes.

Additionally, RFW demonstrated its superiority in computational efficiency. In the CRC-healthy cohort datasets, HUMAnN3 requires approximately 72.52 CPU hours (4.53 h × 16 threads @ Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2696 v.4) to analyze a single sample. This translates to a total of approximately 42,714.28 CPU hours for the entire 589 samples. These findings align with the reported average analysis time of 52.5 CPU hours per sample for HUMAnN3 by Beghini et al.7 In contrast, RFW demonstrated significantly improved computational efficiency compared to HUMAnN. When utilizing Kraken2 taxonomic profiling, RFW completed the analysis of all 589 samples in approximately 37 CPU hours (3.70 h × 10 threads @ Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2696 v.4). When employing MetaPhlAn4 taxonomic profiling, all 589 samples were completed in approximately 11.7 CPU hours (1.17 h × 10 threads @ Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2696 v.4) in RFW analysis. This represents a substantial reduction in analysis time compared to HUMAnN3.

RFW provided support for functional profiling at the species and community levels. The genome function reference database was built from function annotation on representative microbe genomes from the GTDB. Due to a lack of strain-level genomes, RFW could not generate functional profiles or distinguish functional variation at the strain level, which is the limitation of our approach.

In summary, RFW provides more comprehensive species-level information from complex microbial systems. This functional information can be instrumental in diverse downstream modeling tasks, such as differential microbial function identification, association analysis between microbial function and metabolites, etc. We believe that RFW will be a nice WMS functional profiling tool for renewing our knowledge on host-microbe interactions.

Limitations of the study

RFW complements the exploration of functional profiles in microbial systems by leveraging genomic functional information. However, due to the scarcity of strain-level genomes, RFW is unable to generate functional profiles or differentiate functional variations at the strain level, which represents a limitation of our approach.

The accuracy of RFW is contingent upon the precision of microbial species identification and abundance estimation. Given the continuous emergence of novel and more accurate methods for microbial species identification and abundance estimation in recent years, we encourage users to employ these advanced methods to enhance the accuracy of RFW applications. The performance of RFW is also influenced by the genome coverage and accuracy of eggNOG annotations for species in the reference database. However, this study did not benchmark these factors.

Method details

Sequence datasets processing

To evaluate the information loss of existing microbial functional profiling methods and the performance of our proposed RFW method, we employed a diverse collection of sequencing datasets. These included datasets from 15 isolated bacterial cultures (PRJNA551571), cohorts of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients and healthy individuals (ERP008729, PRJEB12449, PRJEB27928, PRJEB10878, PRJEB6070), a cohort of autism spectrum disorder patients and healthy individuals (CRA001746), and ultra-deep sequencing datasets (PRJNA763692).

For all datasets, we performed taxonomic and functional profiling of the gut microbiome using the WMS method. The sequencing depth was accessed by FastQC v0.12.1.94 Adapter and low quality reads were removed by Trimmomatic v0.39.95 Contamination reads were removed by bowtie2 2.3.5.190 with H. sapiens GRCh38 + major SNVs. The Kraken2 based taxonomic profiling were performed by Kraken v2.1.249,96 and braken v2.849,97 with Kraken Standard plus protozoa & fungi 3/14/2023 Refseq indexes. The MetaPhlAn4 based taxonomic profiling were performed by MetaPhlAn v4.0.3 with mpa_vJan21 CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202103. Functional profiling of WMS was done by HUMAnN 3.6.151(database: full_mapping v201901b, full_chocophlan v201901_v31, uniref. 90_annotated v201901b_full, MetaPhlAn v4.0.3 mpa_vJan21 CHOCOPhlAnSGB 202103). Note that the decontamination and taxonomic profiling were excluded from the analysis of PRJNA551571.

Additionally, for the 15 isolated bacterial cultures (PRJNA551571), we conducted taxon identification. Contigs were assembled by megahit v1.2.9,89 indexed by bowtie2 v2.3.5.1,90 binned by Metabat2 v2.12.1.91 The generated MAGs was CheckM2 v1.0.192 and taxonomy assigned by GTDB-Tk v2.2.4.93

For the ultra-deep sequencing datasets, we performed resampling to assess the robustness of our results. The resampling was done by seqtk v1.4-r130.54 In addition to employing HUMAnN for functional analysis, we also utilized eggNOG-mapper v2.1.453,54 to perform functional profiling of resampled ultra-deep sequencing samples. The resampled ultra-deep sequencing reads were first converted into FASTA format using vsearch-2.17.1. The eggNOG-mapper homology searches were performed using the parameters ‘-m mmseqs --itype metagenome --genepred prodigal --no_annot --no_file_comments'. Orthology and functional annotation were carried out using the parameters ‘--annotate_hits_Table --no_file_comments'.

RFW reference database building

With eggNOG-mapper (V2.1.4),53,54 we annotated protein-coding gene functions of representative genomes from 62,291 bacteria and 3,412 archaea species in GTDB (Genome Taxonomy Database, r207, Release date: April 8, 2022).40 The annotation database was based on eggNOG 5.0.98

RFW algorithm overview

RFW offers two ways, RFW-FA and RFW-FSCA, for estimating the abundance of microbial functions within a community. Denote taxa set identified from taxonomic profiling as Taxa={Taxon1,Taxon2,…Taxonp}, where Taxoni is the ith species in the microbiome. And the corresponding relative abundance of each taxon is (xTaxon1,xTaxon2,…xTaxonp)′. Denote the genome functions of Taxoni as {ϕTaxonif|f∈F}, in which ϕTaxonif=1 if Taxoni is capable to encode function f, otherwise ϕTaxonif=0. F represents all potential genome functions of taxa. Denote function counts on f of Taxoni as {ϑTaxonif|f∈F}. The information on genome functions and their counts can be extracted from the reference database. To further illustrate the concept, consider the example of Escherichia coli. Gene functional annotation reveals that there are 33 ORFs (open reading frames) in the E. coli genome that can encode EC 2.7.13.3 (histidine kinase). In this scenario, ϕ = 1 and ϑ = 33. Conversely, E. coli cannot encode the EC 1.1.1.7 (propanediol-phosphate dehydrogenase) enzyme, resulting in ϕ = 0 and ϑ = 0.

For RFW-FA,FA={∑ixTaxoni∗ϕTaxonif∗ϑTaxonif|f∈F}

For RFW-FCSA,FSCA={∑ixTaxoni∗ϕTaxonif|f∈F}

We extended FSCA to encompass the abundance of sub-communities involved in pathways. The pathway-FSCA is defined as the total relative abundance of sub-community members contributing to the specific pathway according to MetaCyc’s pathway definition. In addition, pathway coverage was assessed to determine the ratio of pathway reactions involved by microbe-derived enzymes.

The RFW algorithm implementation

RFW was implemented as open source. The basic algorithm was coded in python and the genome-function reference database was managed by SQLite3.

RFW uses a taxonomic abundance table as its input. To map species identified in the taxonomic abundance table, RFW was designed to support both NCBI tax id and GTDB Taxonomy identifier. NCBI tax id mapping was used for Kraken2 based profiling. For MetaPhlAn4 derived taxonomic profiling, we used command sgb_to_gtdb_profile for transfering MetaPhlAn SGB to GTDB Taxonomy identifier.

RFW support KO, EC and COG for describing microbial function. Based on user-defined parameters, RFW outputs FA or FSCA to describe microbial functional abundance, as well as pathway-FSCA and pathway-coverage information. The mapped and no-mapped taxa information would also be provided in the output.

Proof on DFSCA-BC algorithm Equation 1

Without loss of generality, suppose the sub-community L for encoding funcition f were composed with two taxa, Ta,Tb, i.e., L={Taxon|ϕTaxonf=1}={Ta,Tb}. We have,(Equation 4)zL,jK=zTa,jK+zTb,jK=AL,jK∗sjK=aL,jK∗mjK∗sjKWhere AL,jK represents the total number taxa in sub-community L contained in Group K, sample j. aL,jK represents the absolute abundance of taxa in sub-community L in Group K, sample j. mjK represents the sampling mass of Group K, sample j. sjK is the sampling fraction of Group K, sample j. zL,jK, zTa,jK, and zTb,jK represents sequencing read counts of Taxai in Group K, sample j. K denotes the group index. For simplicity of exposition, K = T, C, namely the C (Control) group and the T (Treatment) Group.

Then the relative abundance of sub-community L, i.e., the FSCAf,jK for function f could be calculated as:(Equation 5)FSCAf,jK=oTa,jK+oTb,jK=zTa,jK+zTb,jK∑izi,jK=zL,jK∑izi,jK

Hence,(Equation 6)FSCAf,jK=zTa,jK+zTb,jK∑izi,jK=mjK∗(aTa,jK+aTa,jK)∗sjK∑iai,jK∗mjK∗sjK=aL,jK∗mjK∑iai,jK∗mjK=aL,jK∑iai,jK

i.e.,(Equation 7)aL,jK=FSCAf,jK∗∑iai,jKwhere ∑iai,jK is the total absolute abundance of all taxa. Denote ΨjK=∑iai,jK, we have(Equation 8)aL,jK=ΨjK∗FSCAf,jK

Thus, the absolute abundance change Δf of microbial function f isΔML=E(log(aLT))−E(log(aLC))=E(log(ΨjT∗FSCAf,jT))−E(log(ΨjC∗FSCAf,jC))=E(log(ΨjT))+E(log(FSCAf,jT))−(E(log(ΨjC))+E(log(FSCAf,jC)))=E(log(ΨjT))−E(log(ΨjC))+E(log(FSCAf,jT))−E(log(FSCAf,jC))=ΔΨ+E(log(FSCAf,jT))−E(log(FSCAf,jC))where ΔΨ=E(log(ΨjT))−E(log(ΨjC)) is the log fold change of total microbial absolute abundance of all taxa between groups. DFSCA-BC algorithm implementation

DFSCA-BC was implemented as open source. The basic algorithm was coded in python.

The algorithm takes RFW-FA or RFW-FSCA functional abundance table, and the sample group info as input.

DFSCA-BC would output estimated microbial function absolute abundance changes between groups.

KEGG module enrichment

R package MicrobiomeProfiler v1.4.099 was used to conduct the KEGG module enrichment analysis on KEGG orthologs which were reported based on taxonomic profiling (both Kraken2 and methphlan4) in at least 580 out of 589 WMS samples.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differentially abundant microbial function identification by DESeq257 were conducted by python package pydeseq2 v0.3.5. Functions with adjusted pvalue <0.05 were determined as differentially abundant.

R function t.test() with paired = FALSE, var.equal = FALSE was used to conduct two-sided Students’ t-test. R function cor.test()was used to conduct pearson correlation analysis.
