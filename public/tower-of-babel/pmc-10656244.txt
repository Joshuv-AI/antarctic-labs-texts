Precise regulation of the multicolor spectrum of carbon dots based on the bionic leaf vein ultrasonic microreactor
Authors: Longshi Rao, Bin Sun, Yang Liu, Qing Zhang, Guisheng Zhong, Mingfu Wen, Jiayang Zhang, Ting Fu, Xiaodong Niu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A bionic vein microchannel was designed with good fluidity and high energy transfer efficiency.•A high-efficiency and controllable ultrasonic microreactor system was built to synthesize the CDs in situ.•The application of the ultrasound can promote the redshift of the CDs' emission spectrum, improve the PLQY and reduce the FWHM.•The high-performance CDs' photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) was up to 83.1%, demonstrating the effectiveness of the ultrasonic microreactor.•The CDs' multicolor spectra from the blue to the red region can be precisely tuned by adjusting reaction temperature, flow rate, and precursor concentration.

Introduction

Carbon dots (CDs) are emerging as promising fluorescent carbon nanomaterials known for their high luminescence performance, nontoxic properties, and excellent stability [1], [2], [3]. They exhibit outstanding potential in diverse applications, including ultra-resolution displays, visible light communication, biomedicine, and sensing [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. One crucial prerequisite for the practical application of CDs is the ability to acquire multicolor spectra. However, the current focus of high-performance CDs is primarily on the short-wavelength region. Consequently, there needs to be more reports on long-wavelength emissive CDs with intense orange or red fluorescence. This scarcity would be attributed to the absence of an effective synthesis strategy and difficulties in tuning multicolor spectra [10], [11], [12]. As a result, the widespread use of CDs is severely limited, particularly in fields like deep-tissue imaging and full-color light-emitting diodes. Therefore, achieving accurate control of CDs' multicolor spectra becomes a pivotal challenge for large-scale applications of CDs.

Unlike traditional quantum dots, CDs' emission spectra do not change significantly, emitting mainly blue/green light [13], [14]. This is because most of the changes in CDs’ multicolor spectra are related to the surface functional groups rather than the particle sizes, and the formation of surface functional groups of CDs is closely linked to their modulability during the preparation procedure [15], [16], [17]. Currently, multicolor CDs preparation is primarily based on hydrothermal and solvothermal methods [10]. However, such methods face such problems as uneven concentration distributions of ionic solutions, variable temperature, poor amplification stability and repeatability, and low productivity, all of which make precise tuning of multicolor CDs spectra challenging. In addition, relevant applications require rapid, large-scale preparation and high-throughput screening of high-performance CDs. Unfortunately, intermittently preparing high-performance CDs in small milliliter volumes leads to uneven response, poor handling, reduced stability, repeatability issues during amplification, and low synthesis efficiency [5]. As a result, the high cost of high-performance CDs becomes a bottleneck for scaling up their applications. With this in mind, there is an urgent need for a large-scale, universal preparation method to achieve precise regulation of CDs’ multicolor spectra.

Compared with the traditional preparation methods of CDs, the microreaction method has the advantages of continuous control of the preparation process, uniform reaction, good repeatability, etc. [18], [19]. Shao et al. proposed a capillary microreactor for the preparation of full-color CDs, which shortened the reaction time to twenty minutes, greatly improving production efficiency [20]. Puyol et al. developed a fully integrated low-temperature co-fired ceramic microreactor for the preparation of CDs [21]. The preparation process was monitored in real-time through an optical window, and the reaction conditions were adjusted in time to obtain high-quantum yield CDs. As a vital component of the microreactor system, the functional design of the microchannel flow field directly affects the performance of CDs. To accurately tune the multicolor spectra of CDs, high efficiency and uniformity of heat transfer in microreactors are needed, which require different functions of microchannels and play an essential role in heat transfer management. To improve heat transfer performance, it is necessary to artificially design complex functional structures with multiple shapes within the microchannels. However, due to the limited research on the application of microreactors in CDs, the design theory of the microchannel functional structure and the quantitative characterization strategy of its heat transfer performance have yet to be developed. Therefore, design theory and thermal performance assessment of the complex functional structures of microreactors are the keys to breaking the bottleneck of precise regulation of CDs’ multicolor spectra.

Up to now, the microreactor has yet to be widely used in the preparation of CDs. The main reason is that some materials in the microchannel are easily blocked, which leads to a decrease in the mass transfer efficiency of the microreactor. Therefore, external forces must be introduced to improve the mass transfer efficiency. Ultrasound is the ideal solution because of its high penetration, low cost, and safety [22]. By adding ultrasound to the microreactor, the cavitation can not only enhance the mixed mass transfer of microchannels but also control the emission spectra by using the instantaneous high temperature and pressure generated by ultrasound to power the formation of nanocrystals, promote nucleation and growth rates, and control the morphology of nanocrystals [23], [24], [25]. Since ultrasonic cavitation is a complex, random, and heterogeneous dynamic process, effective uniform regulation is a prerequisite for mass transfer and enhanced reaction.

In this work, we developed an innovative method for co-regulating CDs' multicolor spectra using an ultrasonic microreactor. Based on bionics and fractal theory, we designed a bionic vein microchannel with good fluidity and high energy transfer efficiency. The influence of fractal angles, depth-to-width ratios, and inlet angles on the flow uniformity of the microchannel was investigated by numerical simulation. To improve the efficiency of ultrasonic energy transfer, we directly coupled the microreactor and ultrasonic transducer to construct the ultrasonic microreactor. Simulation technology was used to determine whether the ultrasonic microreactor resonated and whether the energy distribution was uniform. After that, a high-efficiency and controllable ultrasonic microreactor system was constructed to synthesize CDs in situ driven by ultrasonic. At the same time, the influence of ultrasonic field intensity on the preparation of CDs in a microreactor was investigated to verify the ultrasonic enhancement on the properties of CDs. We discovered that adjusting key reaction parameters such as reaction temperature, flow velocity, and precursor concentration enabled us to achieve CDs' multicolor spectra from the blue to the red region. We also observed that the excitation-dependent or excitation-independent behavior of the CDs produced by different conditions differed. The high-performance CDs' photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) was up to 83.1 %, demonstrating the effectiveness of our approach.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Aqueous ammonium citrate (A.R.) and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8 %) were procured from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, P. R. China). The scientific reagents utilized in this experiment were acquired without undergoing additional purification. Deionized water (H2O, 18.2 MΩ) was employed for all experimental procedures.

Construction of ultrasonic microreactor system

Fig. 1 displays the five main components of the ultrasonic microreactor system: (1) a raw material transportation unit with two high-precision syringe pumps and two check valves, (2) an ultrasonic control unit consisting of an ultrasonic generator, an ultrasonic power amplifier, and a piezoelectric transducer, (3) the microreactor unit, consisting of two layers, with the bottom layer containing an embedded microchannel with a complex structure and the top layer comprising a smooth cover plate, with two inlets on the top panel and a single outlet port on the bottom panel, (4) the temperature control unit, which includes a temperature controller, adjustable power supply, and heating element, that allows for temperature variation ranging from room temperature to 300 °C during the reaction, and (5) a collection unit, where a flask with an ice bath collects the resulting sample.

A schematic diagram of the ultrasonic microreactor system includes five primary units: (1) raw material transportation unit (high-precision syringe pump and check valve), (2) ultrasonic control unit (ultrasonic generator, ultrasonic power amplifier, and piezoelectric transducer), (3) microreactor unit (lower layer containing an embedded microchannel and the upper layer comprising a smooth cover plate), (4) temperature control unit (temperature controller, adjustable power supply, and heating element), and (5) collection unit (flask).

Synthesis of multicolor luminescent CDs

CDs can be prepared in series using the aforementioned ultrasonic microreactor system. Generally, anhydrous ammonium citrate was used as the precursor, DMF as the modifier, and water as the solvent. Anhydrous ammonium citrate powders were dissolved in H2O as precursor A; equal volume DMF was used as precursor B. Before the mixtures were injected into the microreactor, we adjusted the ultrasound frequency, turned on the temperature control unit, and after the temperature hit the set point, activated the high-precision syringe pump and ultrasonic control unit at the same time to clean the microchannel. After that, the supply voltage in the temperature control unit must be adjusted to ensure that the temperature of the microreactor can still be stabilized to the set value when fluid is continuously flowing through the microreactor. Next, two high-precision syringe pumps were loaded with precursor solutions A and B, respectively, and the flow rate of the syringe pumps was kept constant to continuously and stably inject the reaction solutions back into the microreactor system. The ultrasound control unit and temperature control unit were set. Because of the coupling effect of ultrasonic cavitation and high temperature and pressure, the precursor solution quickly underwent condensation and charring reactions to form CDs with high fluorescence yield and quality. The as-prepared product was collected in a vial and soaked in ice-cold water.

The structure design of microchannels with multiple branching channels

The performance of CDs is not only related to reaction parameters but is also closely linked to the functional structure within the microreactors. Our previous studies revealed that the optical properties and size distributions of CDs prepared by microreactors with different flow field structures were other, i.e., whether or not the flow rate and heat transfer in the microchannels was uniform would have a severe effect on the degree of condensation and charring of the CDs [26], [27]. Therefore, using different microchannel types for CDs preparation leads to significant differences in surface functional group types and numbers, affecting the multicolor spectral modulation of CDs.

In nature, numerous network structures, such as plant leaf veins (Fig. 2a), have evolved fractal systems with good properties due to natural selection over time. For example, there is less resistance to fluid during flow, energy consumption is reduced during discharge, and they are highly efficient in material and energy transport. Based on the characteristics of the branching structure and its processing feasibility, we propose a bionic leaf vein microchannel and its optimized design, as shown in Fig. 2b and 2c, the major structural parameters of the microchannel were displayed in Fig. 2d. The relationship between the various structural parameters in the microchannel is primarily determined based on Murray's law [28], i.e., the cube of the radius (diameter) of the main channel is expected to be equal to the sum of the cubes of the radius (diameter) of the branch channels, namely D03=D13+D23. If the fluid in the channel is laminar, then it exists D0/D1=23. Here, the main channel width (D0) is 1.0 mm, both branch channel width (D1) and aggregation channel width (D2) is 0.8 mm, and the main channel length (L) is 60 mm.

Characterization

The simulation software COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 (COMSOL, Sweden) was applied to the structural design of the bionic leaf vein microchannel. The crystalline phase of the product was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8-ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu-Kα radiation source (λ = 0.15418 nm) at 35 kV and a count rate of 2°/min in the range of scan angles from 5° to 60°. The observations were carried out using transmission electron (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. A Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Vertex 33, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) was employed for the acquisition of FTIR spectra, covering the spectral range from 4000 to 500 cm−1. Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted using a Thermo Scientific instrument (Thermo K-Alpha, T.F.S., Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al-Kα excitation source (1486.6 eV) as the X-ray source. The ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectrometer (Tu-1901, Purkinje China) was used to obtain the UV–Vis absorption spectra of the samples. The product PL spectrum was recorded using a xenon lamp-based fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000, Shimadzu, Japan). Absolute PLQY was recorded on an Edinburgh FS5 with an integrating sphere. 365 nm is used as the excitation wavelength for the PLQY test of all samples [29].

Results and discussion

Optimization of flow field structure of bionic vein microchannels

The uniformity of velocity distribution significantly impacts heat and mass transfer in microchannels [30], [31]. Therefore, we propose a microchannel flow field structural design method based on flow velocity distribution optimization. The fluid flow properties of the bionic leaf vein microchannel are simulated and analyzed using the COMSOL Multiphysics simulation software. This research investigates the impact of various factors, including the fractal angle (β), channel depth-to-width ratio (h/D1), and inlet angle (γ), on the uniformity of the microchannel flow. We can establish a foundation for CDs' subsequent preparation and spectral regulation by studying these effects.

To ensure accurate simulation results, it is necessary to conduct a grid-independence check on the number of grids used during the simulation. For this study, a model (Fig. S1) was created in COMSOL, and three different grids with low-density (L), medium-density (M), and high-density (H) were generated to evaluate the relative error of the microchannel's average flow velocity. The results showed that the 'M' grid had a relative error of 1.83 % compared to the 'L' grid, and the 'H' grid had a relative error of only 0.43 % compared to the 'M' grid. This indicates that doubling the grids results in a small relative error. Considering the complexity of calculations, the 'M' grid density division under the control of the physical field was selected for simulation research.

Effect of different fractal angles on flow characteristics of the bionic vein microchannels

We explored the effect of fractal angles on fluid flow uniformity in microchannels of bionic veins, that is, by observing the impact of different fractal angles on the microchannel flow velocity distribution. The XOY plane (Fig. S2a) and ZOX plane (Fig. S2b) of the microchannel were intercepted, respectively, and cloud images of the velocity distribution of these two planes were obtained, as illustrated in Fig. 3a-d.

Cloud plot of the flow velocity distribution of microchannels with different fractal angles in the XOY plane: (a) 30°, (b) 45°, and (c) 60°. (d) the effects of three fractal angles (30°, 45°, and 60°) on the velocity of fluid flow in the main channel (in the ZOX plane); (e) the effects of different fractal angles on the velocity distribution of the fluid flow in the branch channel; and (f) the effects of different fractal angles on the standard deviation of the fluid flow speed.

Firstly, we analyze the velocity distribution of the branching channels in the XOY plane using equations (1), (2). At the same injection velocity, the velocity distribution of the microchannels with three fractal angles (30°, 45°, and 60°) is similar; that is, the flow velocity of the inlet and outlet of the microchannel is higher, and the flow velocity of each branching channel is lower, as shown in Fig. 3a–c. The branching channels can be further divided into 12 groups, categorized based on their distance from the inlet. The branching channels closest to the inlet form the first group, while those nearest to the outlet constitute the 12th group (e.g., Fig. 3a), with symmetrical branching channels belonging to the same group. According to the detailed results presented in Fig. 3a–c, the flow velocity within symmetrically branching channels in the same group remains constant. Furthermore, the flow rate within the branching channels decreases from the first group to the 12th group. This reduction is attributed to an increasing diversion of liquid flow from the main channel as the branching channels progress, leading to a gradual decrease in both the liquid flow rate within the main channel and the liquid pressure, as illustrated in Fig. S3a–d.

In addition, we investigated the velocity distribution of the main channel in the ZOX plane, as represented in Fig. 3d. The results indicated that the velocity of the main channel of three fractal angles gradually decreases from the inlet to the outlet and then abruptly increases near the outlet. By differentiating the color of the velocity cloud plot, the flow velocity of the second half (part II) of the main channel of the 30° fractal angle microchannel is significantly lower than that of the other two types of fractal angle microchannels. Since the smaller fractal angle is more conducive to fluid diversion, fluid flows rapidly from the first half (part I) of the main channel into each of the branch channels; thus, the velocity of the flow in the second half (part II) of the main channel is significantly reduced. Generally, from the perspective of flow uniformity of the microreactor, microchannels with 60° and 45° fractal angles have more uniform flow diversion effects, and 30° fractal angles have a relatively poor performance.

We further investigated the flow velocity distribution in each branch channel and the flow uniformity of the entire microchannels with different fractal angles. To achieve this, we studied the variation of the average flow velocity of each branch channel and the standard deviation of the entire flow velocity (equations (1)-(3)). According to Fig. 3e, we observed that the average flow velocity of the branch channel initially decreased and then increased. The flow of fluid in the channel belongs to the state of first spreading and then aggregating, which results in the corresponding decrease and increase of flow velocity. Interestingly, the flow velocity in the first group of the branch channel corresponding to the 30° fractal angle is significantly higher than that in the branch channel corresponding to the other two fractal angles. This result is in agreement with the findings in Fig. 3d.

As shown in Fig. 3f, the impact of different fractal angles on the standard deviation of fluid flow velocity in the branch channel is quite notable. The deviation from the flow velocity for the branch channel with three fractal angles is less than 1 %. For instance, the most significant fractal angle of 30° has a deviation of about 0.7 %, while the smallest fractal angle of 45° has a variation of about 0.58 %. The difference between the largest and smallest is approximately 0.12 %, which may not be significant. It is worth noting that the more minor the standard deviation of velocity, the smaller the gap in flow velocity between branch channels, resulting in a higher uniformity of flow velocity of branch channels. Fortunately, in this case, the deviations in microchannel flow velocity from the three fractal angles are minimal, providing further evidence that the uniformity of the fluid flow velocity beneath the fractal network channel is relatively high.

Design and characterization of ultrasonic microreactor

To improve the efficiency of ultrasound energy transmission, we directly coupled the ultrasound transducer and the microreactor to construct an ultrasound microreactor. The structural matching allows the whole microreactor to maintain a resonant state in the longitudinal direction so that the microreactor is just at the antinode of the most significant vibration displacement and the sound intensity distribution is uniform. The resonant state of the ultrasonic microreactor is sensitive to the size, shape, and material of each component, so it is difficult to obtain all of the size and material parameters of each element by the traditional method of numerically calculating the resonant frequency. Therefore, based on prior research and other design knowledge and experience, we pre-determine some of the parameters of the ultrasonic microreactor, such as that shown in Fig. S4 and Fig. S5, and use the simulation method to judge if the ultrasonic microreactor is in a resonant state and if the distribution of energy is uniform.

Vibration displacement distribution of ultrasonic microreactor

The primary factor affecting the performance of ultrasonic microreactors is the uniform distribution of the sound field and the sound pressure field. Firstly, we analyzed the distribution of sound fields. To meet the needs of CDs preparation, we applied an ultrasonic transducer with a resonant frequency of 20 KHz to generate ultrasound power. To test whether the resonant state could be achieved after combining the ultrasonic transducer with the microreactor, we analyzed it via COMSOL. In the multi-physics domain of COMSOL, the ultrasonic frequency was set to 20 KHz to investigate the displacement due to the vibration of the ultrasonic coupled microreactor. The results of the simulation show that the resonant frequency of the ultrasound microreactor is 19.2 KHz, which differs somewhat from the nominal sandwich transducer value (20 KHz), primarily because the longitudinal length of the ultrasonic microreactor is longer than the ultrasonic transducer and the corresponding resonant frequency is lower after coupling to the transducer.

We further simulate the distribution of vibration displacements at the resonant frequency and find that it is in a longitudinal resonant state, as shown in Fig. 9a. The minimum displacement due to vibration occurs in the connection between the piezoelectric ceramic plate and the top port of the transducer. The maximum displacement due to vibration occurs in the microreactor. In contrast, the microreactor surface is uniformly distributed, as shown in Fig. 9b. In addition, we conducted tests to analyze the distribution of vibration displacement along the diameter of the microreactor surface, as depicted in Fig. 9c. It is evident that the displacement caused by vibration varies at different positions on the microreactor surface. However, the relative error between the maximum and minimum amplitudes is only 2.88 %. This finding further demonstrates the uniform distribution of ultrasound energy within the microreactor.

Sound pressure distribution of ultrasonic microreactor

A detailed investigation of the sound pressure field distribution in the ultrasonic microreactor was carried out using the multiphysics simulation software COMSOL. Particularly, we set up a model as shown in Fig. 10a in COMSOL and set parameters such as the airspace extent and the perfect matching layer. Our three-dimensional sound pressure simulation results revealed a highly directional acoustic pressure distribution. More specifically, the acoustic pressure was observed to occur in the longitudinal direction of the microreactor at regular intervals. In contrast, the acoustic pressure around the microreactor was considerably weaker (Fig. 10b). These results suggest that ultrasound energy can be efficiently focused on the microreactor, increasing its mass transfer performance. A more detailed analysis, based on a two-dimensional model (Fig. 10c), indicated that the highest acoustic pressure was concentrated near the microchannel surface, facilitating cavitation inside the microchannel. It should be noted that upon closer observation, we observed that the acoustic pressure curves near the microreactor's surface exhibited both high and low values at both ends, resembling the distribution of vibration displacements shown in Fig. 9c. Based on the simulation results, the coupling structure between the sandwich ultrasound transducer and the microreactor is reasonable.

Precise regulation of CDs’ multicolor spectra based on ultrasonic microreactor

Verification of ultrasonic enhancement

To evaluate the impact of ultrasonic enhancement, we compared CDs with and without ultrasonic treatment, keeping the reaction temperature constant at 180 °C and the flow speed at 0.2 mL/min (with the precursors and DMF being held constant). The precursor concentration was set at 1.0 mol/L, and two different ultrasonic frequencies (0 KHz and 20 KHz) were used, as detailed in Table S4. The CDs prepared with ultrasonic treatment were named W-CDs, while those prepared without ultrasonic treatment were referred to as N-CDs.

The crystal structure, surface morphology, and optical properties of the N-CDs and W-CDs were characterized as described in Fig. 11. The XRD diffraction pattern for the CDs is shown in Fig. 11a. They have similar single diffraction peak and are broad at 21.8° and 23.6°, respectively, exhibiting a graphitic structure with a small-sized feature [32]. No impurity peaks were observed simultaneously, confirming the high purity of the two products. The surface morphology of these two CDs was measured by TEM, as shown in Fig. 11b and 11c, the results reveal that both CDs are well dispersed without aggregation and are mostly spherical. The corresponding histograms of their size distributions are shown in Fig. 11d and 11e. N-CDs and W-CDs have an average diameter of about 2.69 nm and 2.02 nm, respectively. In addition, the high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images provided in the inset show that N-CDs and W-CDs have lattice spacing distances of 0.24 nm and 0.21 nm, which are consistent with the (1 0 0) and (1 0 3) facet of graphitic carbon, respectively. The W-CDs also show a narrower size distribution compared to the N-CDs, implying that the addition of ultrasound helps improve the uniformity of the CDs, enhancing their optical properties.

The UV–Vis absorption, PL emission, and PLQY spectra of the N-CDs and W-CDs were examined in more detail. As shown in Fig. 11f, the green and yellow-green luminescence of the N-CDs and W-CDs was observed under ultraviolet irradiation at 365 nm, respectively. As can be seen in Fig. 11g, they both have an intense absorption band in the range from 200 nm to 300 nm in the deep-ultraviolet range, corresponding to the C = C bond π–π* transition in the carbon domain of sp2 [6]. In addition, they also have characteristic solid absorption at 340 nm and are possibly attributed to the n–π* transitions of the surface states containing C = N and C = O structures [17]. The difference in characteristic absorption intensity between N-CDs and W-CDs depends on their carbon core and surface state. The N-CDs and W-CDs have a PL emission peak at 532 and 546 nm, respectively. Furthermore, W-CDs have smaller full widths at half maximum (FWHM) compared to N-CDs, which is beneficial for illumination and display applications. While it is worth noting that W-CDs have a smaller particle size, their PL emission spectrum is redshifted concerning that of the N-CDs, unlike the previous report that the smaller the particle size, the PL emission spectra are blue-shifted, which could be due to the addition of the ultrasound energy forcing the W-CDs significant changes have occurred with the carbon core of the N-CDs [33], [34], [35]. PLQY characterization was done to make a more intuitive comparison of their optical performance. Fig. 11i demonstrates that the PLQY of 83.1 % of the W-CDs is higher than that of the N-CDs (68.3 %). The enhancement of PLQY is due to the ultrasonic energy, which has fast heating and mass transfer properties and rapid mixing capability. The above results all show the addition of enhanced ultrasound treatment on the CDs properties.

To further elucidate the chemical structure of both N-CDs and W-CDs, we employed FTIR and XPS techniques for the identification of chemical bonding and compositions. These CDs had similar FTIR spectra, as shown in Fig. 12a. These analyses revealed distinct absorption peaks in the spectra, including O–H/N–H stretching vibrations at approximately 3450 cm−1, C = O vibrations at 1708 cm−1, C = C/C = N vibrations at 1652 cm−1, C–N = vibrations at 1398 cm−1, C–O vibrations at 1182 cm−1, and C–H bending vibrations at 868 cm−1 [16], [36]. Notably, the stretching vibrations corresponding to O–H/N–H were predominantly observed within the absorption band at 3450 cm−1, confirming the generation of –OH groups during the synthesis of the CDs. The presence of C = O functional groups was evidenced by absorption bands at approximately 1708 cm−1. Furthermore, the relatively weak absorption band centered at 1652 cm−1 was ascribed to the characteristic absorption bands of C = C and C = N functionalities. Throughout the synthesis process, the stretching vibrations corresponding to C–N= (1398 cm−1) and C–O (1182 cm−1) were detected, signifying the formation of polyaromatic structures within the two CDs. Upon conducting a more comprehensive examination, it was discerned that the stretching vibration intensities associated with C = C/C = N (1652 cm−1) exhibited a gradual reduction or shifted to higher wavenumbers as one progresses from N–CDs to W–CDs. In contrast, the stretching vibration intensities of C = O (1708 cm−1), C–N= (1398 cm−1), C–O (1182 cm−1), and O-H/N-H (3450 cm−1) experienced an augmentation, thereby signifying a progressive elevation in the abundance of nitrogen-doped polyaromatic structures and oxygen-containing functional groups [17].

The existence of chemical structures within both the N-CDs and W-CDs was further substantiated through XPS analysis. The complete XPS spectra of the two CDs displayed distinct peaks at 284.4 eV, 399.5 eV, and 531.2 eV, corresponding to C1s, N1s, and O1s (Fig. 12b), respectively, serving as evidence of the nitrogen atom doping within the CD framework. Further examination of the C1s band via high-resolution XPS spectra (Fig. 12d–i) unveiled three binding energy peaks at approximately 284.4 eV, 286.1 eV, and 287.8 eV, which could be attributed to sp2 carbon (C = C/C–C), sp3 carbon (C–N/C–O), and carbonyl groups (C = O/C = N), respectively. In the N1s spectra, three distinct component peaks emerged at around 398.5 eV, 399.5 eV, and 400.3 eV, corresponding to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, and graphitic N, respectively. Furthermore, the O 1 s band exhibited component peaks at 531.1 eV and 532.6 eV, corresponding to C = O and C–O functionalities, respectively. It is noteworthy that the N/C ratios for the N-CDs and W-CDs were 0.15 and 0.18, respectively, while the O/C ratios were 0.22 and 0.29, respectively (Fig. 12c), signifying a progressive increase in nitrogen and oxygen content from the N-CDs to the W-CDs. In summary, the FTIR and XPS data collectively illustrate that the introduction of ultrasound alters the surface functional groups of CDs and augments the nitrogen and oxygen doping levels.

Stability is a key factor in determining the reliability of fluorescent conversion materials [37]. The photostability and thermal stability of both N-CDs and W-CDs were evaluated by analyzing their fluorescence characteristics following long-term UV irradiation and heat treatment, as depicted in Fig. S6. The photoluminescence (PL) intensities of both CDs remained at approximately 85 % of their initial values after being exposed to 365 nm UV light at room temperature for 50 h, as illustrated in Fig. S6a. Furthermore, when subjected to 80 °C, the fluorescence intensities of both CDs gradually decreased over time, with W-CDs exhibiting a more significant decrease. Even after 35 h of heating (Fig. S6b), the fluorescence intensities of these CDs remained at 86 %. Consequently, these findings demonstrate the remarkable PL stability of both N-CDs and W-CDs, underscoring their potential for practical applications.

Effects of the reaction temperature in the ultrasonic microreactor on spectral properties of the CDs

By adjusting the supply voltage, the reaction temperature in the microreactor can be varied between 30 °C and 300 °C. For the synthesis of CDs, temperatures of 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C, 200 °C, and 220 °C were found to be effective. In addition to temperature, conditions such as flow velocity, precursor concentration, and ultrasound frequencies were also tested and found optimal at 0.2 mL/min, 1.0 mol/L, and 20 KHz, respectively, as detailed in Table S5.

The CDs' UV–Vis absorption spectrum and normalized PL emission spectrum are displayed in Fig. 13a-c, respectively. The first characteristic absorption range of the CD solution is between 300 nm and 500 nm, as shown in Fig. 13a, and the absorption peaks from the prepared CDs are distinct at different temperatures. In both cases, the absorption peak of CDs prepared at 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C, and 200 °C is about 340 nm. However, CDs designed at 220 °C have two absorption peaks centered at 318 nm and 430 nm, respectively. The appearance of these absorption peaks is attributed to the presence of the n–π* transitions of the surface states containing the C = N and C = O structures. Furthermore, there is intense absorption in the 200–300 nm range, related to the C = C bond π–π* transition in the sp2 carbon domain [38]. As shown in Fig. 13b, under UV irradiation at 365 nm, the CDs' solutions prepared at different temperatures emit blue, green, yellow-green, orange, and red light, respectively, demonstrating the rapid tuning of the multicolor spectrum of CDs. These colors correspond to emission peaks at 444 nm, 492 nm, 546 nm, 600 nm, and 630 nm, respectively, and the emission range covers the whole visible-light region. The broad regulation range, from 440 to 630 nm, is due to a unique combination of precursors, solvents, and reaction environments. This phenomenon is unlike anything we have observed in our previous studies, and it presents a fascinating new avenue for exploration in the field of regulation [6], [26]. Besides, we can observe that the CDs' FWHM gradually increases as a function of the redshift of the PL emission peak.

We also investigated the excitation-independent or excitation-dependent behavior of the PL emission for these CDs, as shown in Fig. 13d-h. The CDs prepared at 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C and 200 °C were found to exhibit pronounced excitation-dependent behavior; at the same time, as the excitation wavelength was increased, the PL emission peak was progressively redshifted, but the redshift distance also became shorter and shorter. These PL behaviors that depend on excitation relate to carbon bonds and surface functional groups and do not change CDs' chemical composition and size [16]. Notably, the fluorescence emission peak has a redshift with the excited state, and its fluorescence intensity decreased significantly, with most of the emission concentrated in the short wavelength region from blue to green. However, when the reaction temperature is 220 °C, as-prepared CDs show excitation-independent behavior. This excitation-independent feature was possibly caused by an eigenstate emission, closely related to the carbon core with a highly ordered graphitic structure, and is more similar to traditional inorganic phosphors than previously reported CDs [39], [40]. As a result, the emission spectrum of CDs can be tuned by changing the excitation wavelength or adjusting the reaction temperature.

Effects of flow velocity in the ultrasonic microreactor on the spectral properties of the CDs

The emission spectrum of CDs can also be changed by altering the flow velocity of the precursor solution in an ultrasonic microreactor. By setting the reaction temperature to 180 °C, keeping the flow velocity of precursor and DMF the same, a precursor concentration of 1.0 mol/L and ultrasound frequencies of 20 KHz were used, where the flow velocity of the precursor solution was varied between 0.2 mL/min and 1.0 mL/min, as presented in Table S6.

The research findings in Fig. 14a showed that both CDs exhibit a strong absorption band at 300–400 nm; however, the absorption peak of each CDs varies. As the flow velocity increased, the CDs' absorption peak shifted to 322 nm and 348 nm, increasing the absorption peak intensities. These CDs' absorption properties were similar to those prepared at different temperatures and exhibited significant absorption in the 200–300 nm range. When excited with 365 nm ultraviolet light, the CDs solutions prepared at different flow velocities emitted PL peaks at 546 nm, 480 nm, 458 nm, 450 nm, and 444 nm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 14b and 14c, indicating that the PL emission peak moves towards the blue region with an increase in flow velocity, possibly due to decreased concentration and size of the CDs [26]. However, obtaining full-color emission of CDs by regulating flow rates is difficult compared to CDs fabricated in different reaction temperatures, as the spectral range here is much smaller.

Characterization of the optical properties of CDs prepared under different flow velocities: (a) UV–Vis absorption spectra, (b) fluorescence image under UV light illumination, (c) PL emission spectrum; the excitation-dependent PL spectra of the CDs that were synthesized by (d) 0.2 mL/min, (e) 0.4 mL/min, (f) 0.6 mL/min, (g) 0.8 mL/min, (h) 1.0 mL/min flow velocity.

Upon further investigating the excitation-dependent behavior of PL emission for these CDs, we discovered some intriguing findings in Fig. 14d-h. It was observed that, unlike the fluorescence emission spectrum of CDs, which is redshifted by the increased reaction temperature, increasing the flow speed caused a blue shift in the fluorescence emission spectrum of the CDs while making the excitation-dependent behavior more apparent. Moreover, as we increased the excitation wavelength, the PL emission peaks from CDs prepared at different flow speeds were redshifted. Thus, these results show that different reaction conditions can change the optical behavior of CDs.

Effects of precursor concentration on spectral properties of CDs

We investigated the effect of the last key parameter, the precursor concentration, on the optical properties of CDs. Setting the ultrasound frequency at 20 KHz, the microreactor temperature at 180 °C, the precursor flow rate at 0.2 mL/min, volumes of ammonium citrate solution and DMF were the same, and the concentration of the precursor solution was in the range of 0.5 mol/L to 0.75 mol/L. mol/L, 1.0 mol/L, 1.25 mol/L, and 1.5 mol/L, respectively, as illustrated in Table S7.

Fig. 15a shows that the as-prepared tested CDs had a first characteristic absorption band between 300 and 400 nm. The absorption peaks of the CDs varied depending on the precursor concentration used to prepare them. For instance, CDs prepared at 0.5 mol/L had a first characteristic absorption peak at 352 nm, while CDs prepared at 0.75 mol/L had an absorption peak at 322 nm. CDs prepared at 1.0 mol/L had an absorption peak of 336 nm, CDs prepared at 1.25 mol/L had an absorption peak of 330 nm, and CDs prepared at 1.5 mol/L had an absorption peak of 338 nm. CDs solutions that were designed with different precursor concentrations produced luminescence of various colors, such as blue, green, yellow-green, orange-yellow, and deep red, when exposed to UV radiation at 365 nm, as seen in Fig. 15b. In addition, the corresponding PL emission peaks were located at 458 nm, 504 nm, 546 nm, 600 nm, and 648 nm (Fig. 15c), respectively, which is consistent with the relevant excitonic absorption band, revealing the band-edge emission properties of CDs [41], [42].

Characterization of the optical properties of CDs prepared under different precursor concentrations: (a) UV–Vis absorption spectrum, (b) fluorescence image under UV illumination, (c) PL emission spectrum, and the PL spectra as a function of excitation of the CDs that were synthesized by (d) 0.5 mol/L, (e) 0.75 mol/L, (f) 1.0 mol/L, (g) 1.25 mol/L, (h) 1.5 mol/L concentration of the precursor.

Furthermore, we also examined the normalized PL spectra of CDs prepared with different precursor concentrations at different excitation wavelengths. Fig. 15d-h show that as the precursor concentration increased from 0.5 mol/L to 1.0 mol/L, the CDs initially showed excitation-dependent behavior; when the concentration of the precursor continued to rise to 1.25 mol/L and 1.5 mol/L, the CDs again showed excitation-independence, similar to the previous effect of reaction temperature on the PL spectra of CDs. These findings suggest that tuning the precursor concentration makes achieving precise control of multicolor CD spectra possible.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated a novel method for co-regulating multicolor spectra of CDs by utilizing an ultrasonic microreactor. Inspired by plant leaves, we designed a bionic leaf vein microchannel with good fluidity and high energy transfer efficiency. The effects of fractal angle, depth-to-width ratio, and inlet angle on the flow uniformity of the microchannel were investigated using numerical simulations. Our results demonstrated that larger fractal angles produce more uniform fluid flow in the microchannel under similar conditions. Furthermore, we found that the velocity in the microchannel is stratified after the depth-to-width ratio exceeds 2.0 when keeping the same fractal angle. We also found that increasing the inlet angle leads to an increase in the velocity of the microchannel. To maximize ultrasonic energy transfer, we coupled the microreactor directly to a sandwich transducer and verified the microreactor's resonant state and uniform energy distribution through COMSOL simulation. Using the ultrasonic microreactor, we found that ultrasound promotes the redshift of the CDs spectrum and enhances its PLQY, reducing the FWHM. By adjusting key reaction parameters such as reaction temperature, flow velocity, and precursor concentration, we could precisely control the change of CDs’ emission spectrum from 444 nm to 648 nm, covering the entire visible area. We also observed that increasing the reaction temperature, decreasing the flow velocity, and increasing the precursor concentration all led to a red shift in the CDs spectrum, and the excitation-dependent or excitation-independent behavior of the CDs produced by different conditions differed. Our results suggest that the ultrasonic microreactor has excellent potential for synthesizing high-quality CDs with tunable emission spectra.
