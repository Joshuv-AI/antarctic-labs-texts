Commentary on the association between toothbrushing behavior and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in aging populations
Authors: Zeynep Afra Akbiyik Az
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Matters arising addresses the article titled “Association between toothbrushing behavior and cardiometabolic multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults in North China: a cross-section study”, published in BMC Oral Health 24, 1333 (2024). The study investigates the association between toothbrushing behavior and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in middle-aged and older adults, presenting valuable insights into the association between oral hygiene and CMM risk. While acknowledging its contributions to public health, the present commentary seeks to methodically identify areas that could be improved with regard to methodology and statistics. Firstly, it is recommended that more comprehensive exclusion criteria be applied to account for participants who may have conditions that could affect self-reported oral hygiene, such as psychiatric disorders or immunocompromised status. Furthermore, I propose that future studies employ a more rigorous multivariable regression model to address potential confounders collectively. This approach may help mitigate the limitations posed by wide confidence intervals, particularly within specific CMM subgroups. It should be noted that this commentary does not intend to refute or confirm the findings of the original study. Rather, our objective is to provide support for future research efforts in refining the association between oral health practices and CMM across diverse populations.Keywords: Matters arising, Toothbrushing behavior, Cardiometabolic multimorbidity

I read with great interest the article by Guo et al. entitled: “Association between toothbrushing behavior and cardiometabolic multimorbidity among middle aged and older adults in North China: a cross-section study”, published in BMC Oral Health on 1 November 2024 [1]. The authors investigated the relationship between toothbrushing frequency and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in middle-aged and older adults. The authors highlight a potential correlation between poor oral hygiene practices and an increased risk of CMM, a phenomenon frequently observed in individuals with a single chronic condition but less frequently investigated in the context of multimorbidity. This study contributes to the evidence on preventive oral health, especially in aging populations with rising multimorbidity rates.

However, while the findings are promising, several aspects require further clarification and consideration. I respectfully offer the following queries and suggestions, which may contribute to future research in this area.

Firstly, the authors mention excluding only participants with missing data. Given the study’s focus on the relationship between oral hygiene and CMM, implementing more comprehensive exclusion criteria could have enhanced the validity of the findings. Patients with psychiatric or neurological disorders, those who have recently undergone major surgery, or those with functional impairments affecting self-care may influence the reliability of self-reported oral hygiene. Moreover, immunocompromised patients, including those receiving cancer treatments, may exhibit distinct oral hygiene behaviors and health patterns that could confound the association between toothbrushing frequency and CMM. Could the authors clarify how they managed these groups, given their unique impact on both oral hygiene and CMM outcomes? While they do not explicitly identify these conditions as major confounders, their potential impact on oral hygiene behaviors and cardiometabolic multimorbidity should be considered in future research.

Additionally, although the authors acknowledged in the limitations section that the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, the study does not address the limitations arising from a lack of comprehensive multivariable logistic regression analysis. While age, sex, education, and other covariates were adjusted individually, assessing all potential confounders collectively in a single multivariable model could offer a clearer view of the independent link between toothbrushing frequency and CMM. A recent study examining the association between multimorbidity and tooth loss utilized hierarchical logistic regression to better isolate the effect of toothbrushing frequency from behavioral and sociodemographic factors [2]. This approach allowed for a more precise estimation of oral hygiene’s role in multimorbidity risk by accounting for interactions among multiple chronic conditions. Furthermore, a systematic review assessing the impact of oral hygiene practices on cardiometabolic outcomes underscored the necessity of comprehensive statistical modeling to distinguish direct effects from residual confounding [3]. Incorporating similar methodological rigor in the present study could enhance the robustness and interpretability of the findings. This limitation is further compounded by the wide confidence intervals reported for certain subgroups, suggesting possible issues with sample size adequacy and statistical power, particularly for specific combinations of CMM. Notably, several studies have demonstrated that periodontal diseases and CMM share common risk factors, such as smoking, diabetes, and advanced age [4, 5]. I suggest that future research adopt a more rigorous approach to statistical modelling to strengthen the evidence on this association.

Lastly, in addition to studies conducted in lower socioeconomic settings, it would be valuable to consider research from countries with better access to oral healthcare, such as European nations, Japan, and Canada. For instance, a study from the United Kingdom and the United States found that poor oral health indicators—such as tooth loss, periodontal disease, and dry mouth—were significantly associated with increased mortality, including all-cause, cardiovascular, and respiratory-related deaths [6]. Similarly, research from Japan demonstrated that inadequate oral hygiene practices, particularly infrequent toothbrushing, were linked to a higher risk of obesity, a key component of cardiometabolic multimorbidity [7]. Expanding the discussion to include such studies would provide a broader perspective on the generalizability of the findings and strengthen the argument for a global association between oral hygiene and cardiometabolic multimorbidity.

In conclusion, while the study provides valuable insights into the association between oral hygiene and CMM, clarifying these points could strengthen its contribution. I sincerely appreciate your consideration of our inquiries and look forward to your response.
