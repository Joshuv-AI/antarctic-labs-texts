An evidence-based comparison of rehabilitation strategies for diastasis recti abdominis in postpartum women: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Nahid Bigdeli, Ali Yalfani, Amin Doosti-Irani, Ali Qodrati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare the effectiveness of various physical therapy interventions, including exercise and non-exercise-based approaches, for improving intera recti distance (IRD) in women with postpartum diastasis recti abdominis (DRA). A systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted following PRISMA-NMA guidelines. Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, ISI Web of Science, Cochrane Library Databases, and manual searches were systematically searched from their inception until October 2024. A total of 27 RCTs involving 1340 postpartum women with DRA and 39 interventions were included. IRD was assessed via ultrasonography or calipers. Treatment effects on IRD were analysed using mean differences (MDs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and minimal clinically important differences (MCID) thresholds to determine clinical relevance. All networks revealed low heterogeneity and non-significant inconsistency (I2 = 26.80%). Combined deep and superficial abdominal muscle training versus no-treatment group was associated with a greater reduction in IRD compared to single-muscle approaches and non-exercise modalities, like TCMEB + KT(MD = -26.25, 95% CI = -29.41; -23.09, SUCRA = 0.96), TCME + BPFM + NMES (MD = -25.94, 95% CI = -32.33; -19.54, SUCRA = 0.95), NMES + TCME + MMT (MD = -24.62, 95% CI = -29.78; -19.47, SUCRA = 0.92) and NMES + TCME ( MD = -22.62, 95% CI = -26.58; -18.67, SUCRA = 0.88). The kinesio-taping (MD = -3.93, 95% CI = -6.20; -1.65, SUCRA = 0.18) and abdominal binders (MD = -10.32, 95% CI = -13.81; -6.83, SUCRA = 0.52) demonstrated moderate to low effectiveness. The overall quality of the evidence varied from low to High according to the GRADE approach. No serious publication bias was identified. Based on the findings of this study, comprehensive exercise programs that engage both superficial and deep abdominal muscles, especially when combined with neuromuscular electrical stimulation and breathing techniques, appear more effective than passive or isolated approaches in reducing inter-recti distance in postpartum women. Nevertheless, given the limited direct comparisons and low certainty in some evidence, clinical interpretation should remain cautious. Further well-designed studies with robust controls and direct comparison are needed to confirm and strengthen these findings. Introduction

Diastasis recti abdominis (DRA) is a common disorder in women during pregnancy and after that. As the fetus grows in the mother’s womb, the abdominal muscles begin to stretch and strain toward the outside. This disorder increases the length of the abdominal muscles, especially the Rectus abdominis, during pregnancy and separates them from the midline fascia, which is known as the Linea alba. DRA can appear as early as 16 weeks of gestation1. This disease affects a majority of pregnant and postpartum women, almost 70% in the late third trimester, 60% at six weeks after childbirth, and 30% at one year. According to Beer et al. (2009), a positive diagnosis of DRA is defined by an inter-recti distance (IRD) of the linea alba that is 2.2 cm (measured 3 cm above the umbilicus), 2.7 cm (at the umbilical level), and 1.0 cm (measured 3 cm below the umbilicus)2.

Many studies show that DRA leads to some complications after delivery, such as lower back and pelvis pain, changed trunk biomechanics, impaired lumbopelvic control and proprioception, disability in daily activities, decreased postural stability, reduced strength of the abdominal wall, and, in extreme cases, abdominal hernia3–5. Some studies have acknowledged that multiple pregnancies, maternal age, multiparity, and childcare obligations can be effective in the occurrence of DRA1,6,7. Considering the high prevalence of this disorder, limited research has been conducted on it. In general, research efforts on this topic are less than others.

A few strong randomized clinical trials have only focused on DRA. Some interventions for women with DRA are frequently used in clinical treatments, including therapeutic exercise, electrotherapy or electrical stimulation, types of abdominal braces or corsets, acupuncture, Kinesio taping, and a combination of these methods5,8–13. However, current guideline recommendations are heterogeneous and confuse clinicians and physical therapists when selecting the proper treatment method and which methods are probably the most effective.

Management of diastasis recti involves a range of strategies that can be broadly categorized into conservative and surgical approaches. The choice of Rehabilitation methods depends on the severity of the DRA, the patient’s symptoms, and individual preferences. Conservative Rehabilitation methods are often the first line of treatment in postpartum women, while surgical options are considered for more severe cases or when conservative methods fail to provide relief14. Different rehabilitation methods require careful consideration regarding their advantages and disadvantages. While exercise is the primary approach and foundation of IRD treatment, high variability in program design and inconsistency in the available evidence may lead to it sometimes being no more effective than no treatment at all and could even exacerbate the condition. Additionally, non-exercise physical therapies such as acupuncture and electrotherapy etc. offer alternative options for those unable to perform physical exercises. However, the evidence supporting these treatments is limited, and their effectiveness as stand-alone options is less established than that of exercise therapy6,7.

Determining evidence-based updates in the selection of the most appropriate DRA rehabilitation methods through a network meta-analysis will enable clinicians to make more accurate clinical decisions based on comprehensive data. It will also allow patients to make informed and effective choices to improve their quality of life by being aware of documented treatment options. Furthermore, this study can provide an opportunity for researchers to thoroughly assess gaps in the scientific literature and develop new treatment approaches. Currently, most systematic reviews and meta-analysis studies have only compared different treatment methods pairwise. None of these studies compared and classified rehabilitation methods with each other as a network meta-analysis4,6,14,15. The current study aimed to compare the available treatment methods for women with DRA in terms of positive and negative effects, via a systematic review with network meta-analysis (NMA). So, the research question for this systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) was: Which rehabilitation intervention is most effective in reducing IRD in women with postpartum diastasis recti abdominis?

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and NMA study was registered on PROSPERO with the code CRD42024593155 and the research ethics committee of Bu Ali Sina University- Hamadan with the code IR.BASU.REC.1403.010 and approved16. Also, the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) extension statement (Appendix 1- Table S1) and participants, interventions, comparators, and outcomes (PICO) were followed for reporting the current NMA study17.

Data sources, searches, and study selection

Two researchers (NB and AQ) searched five electronic databases from their inception until December 2023: PubMed (n = 415), Scopus (n = 2888), ISI Web of Science (n = 3610), Cochrane Library Databases (n = 2831), and manual searches in sites (eg; Google Scholar, ClinicalTrials.gov, ResearchGate, and by screening the reference lists) and review studies(n = 24). The researchers updated the search in October 2024 to check for new studies, and there were 4 findings. There was no restriction regarding the year of publication and language. Also, they reviewed eligible trial studies that were included in other review studies (Fig. 1). They used the keywords “diastasis recti” OR “inter recti diastasis” OR “rectus abdominis diastasis” OR “rectus diastasis” OR postpartum OR "rectus abdominis separation" OR “weakness of linea alba” AND “exercises” OR “rehabilitation” OR “abdominal exercises” OR “electrical stimulation” OR “kinesio taping” OR “abdominal belt”, etc. (Appendix 2- Table S2). The researchers checked the title and abstract and omitted duplicates or management of selecting RCTs; they used EndNote software®. All systematic reviews, cohorts, case reports, studies published in predatory journals, and observational studies related to DRA were omitted. The search strategy and further information for each database are available in Appendix 2- Table S3.

PRISMA flow chart of retrieving articles and assessing the eligibility criteria for NMA of rehabilitation methods in women with DRA.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

The current study included only RCTs with a parallel design that assessed the effectiveness of each treatment approach for women aged 18–45 with DRA one week to 12 months after delivery from 2007 to 2024. According to the PICOS framework, the study population consisted of postpartum women diagnosed with postpartum diastasis recti. The interventions evaluated included both non-exercise physical therapy and various exercise-based physical therapy approaches. The comparator group comprised individuals who did not receive any specific therapeutic intervention (No-treatment or RL). The primary outcome measure was IRD, and the study design for this NMA was based on RCTs. The inclusion criteria included studies related to postpartum DRA with IRD above 2.2 cm (2 cm above the umbilicus)2, RCT design and intervention type, which are reported in Table 1. The studies on Psychology, Abdominal surgery, and the RCT studies with baseline IRD below 2.2 cm were excluded from the study process (Appendix 16- Table S32). The effect of interventions on the DRA was assessed with the measurement of the distance between two balks of rectus abdominis muscle by a centimeter or millimeter scale at the 2 cm upper umbilicus, 2 cm below the umbilicus, and the umbilicus level5,9,15,18–21. The validity of assessing IRD using a digital caliper has been reported as 0.8421, whereas ultrasound imaging has demonstrated a validity of 0.9822. These values indicate that both measurement tools possess good validity, suggesting that both method offers a reliable assessment of IRD21,22.

Outcomes and time points

The primary outcome of this study was IRD intensity above 2.2 cm at baseline (e.g., measured using ultrasonography and a digital caliper)2. However, some RCTs had secondary outcomes like low back pain, disability, low quality of life, etc. According to the methodological expectations of Cochrane intervention reviews, Information was gathered at the baseline and post-intervention. Comparisons with follow-ups (eg, one or more months after intervention) were excluded.

Interventions and comparators

The researchers included RCTs that divided participants into several groups based on rehabilitation methods (non-exercise physical therapy, exercise physical therapy alone, or combined exercise and non-exercise physical therapy), as shown in Table 2. To evaluate the effects of interventions, all treatments were compared with a no-treatment group (RL), which included participants who did not engage in any intervention or specific physical therapy program and continued with their routine lives. This group of participants was considered a standard reference so that the relative effects of each exercise or non-exercise intervention could be evaluated against the no-intervention condition, compared within the framework of an NMA.

Geometry of the network

Interventions were categorized into five groups, and each group was assigned a distinct color for easier identification (Table 2). These methods included physical agents (especially electrotherapeutic agents), exercise therapy, mind–body practices, different types of abdominal belts and tape, exercise therapy combined with physical agents, exercise therapy combined with an abdominal belt (or tape), and no treatment (routine life and sham). The control group in each study included the usual methods of treatments (tested methods), ranging from some physical training to other modalities, daily routine (no intervention), and sham. The mind–body practices involve low-intensity and low-impact movements accompanied by controlled breathing and focused attention3,5,7–13,18–20,23–35. The electrotherapeutic agents use electricity and the electromagnetic spectrum to facilitate tissue healing, improve muscle strength and endurance, decrease edema, modulate pain, decrease the inflammatory process, and modify the healing process. The researchers also carefully reviewed the exercise interventions of each study and, after confirming the intervention methods and their effect on the inner (deep) and outer (superficial) abdominal muscles, or both of them, these modalities were categorized under a similar abbreviation. For example, three studies were reviewed, and each study aimed to strengthen the transverse abdominal muscle. So, the researchers grouped all three studies into the "Transverse Abdominal Strengthening (TrAS)" category, regardless of their original names. On the other hand, a study used a new method for this disorder, for the first time, so it was given only its abbreviations (ie, Bloom’s Method Exercise (BLME))27. Finally, all the studies were categorized through this method. This work helps researchers find the exact mechanism of different interventions in DRA improvement, or lack thereof.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

All extracted data were imported into an Excel sheet. Two researchers (NB and AQ) independently extracted data related to all the characteristics and outcomes of the selected studies (Kappa score: 0.841 in Appendix 3-Table S5). They used the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool (RoB 2 version) for the risk of bias. The RoB in the studies included in this NMA was evaluated based on the measurement of the primary outcome (IRD severity) at baseline and post-intervention, which is recognized as the key point for reporting the results. Because it is impossible to blind participants in clinical trials of exercise treatments or physical therapy, the risk of bias assessment focused on blinding the outcome assessor. Studies that clearly stated that outcome assessment was blinded were assessed as having low risk of bias with the following domains: 'Bias in the measurement of the outcome’. The studies were classified as high, low, or unclear risk (Appendix 3- Table S4, Figures S1, S2)36,37. Also, RoB disagreements were adjudicated by a third author (AY) if they did not reach a consensus. The researchers extracted study characteristics which include: the study number (id), name of first author, publication year, country, study design, parity, type of delivery, number of participants, interventions, study outcome, assessment tools, time of beginning intervention after delivery, intervention period, number of intervention sessions, number of intervention sessions per week, time of each session, intervention setting (eg, set, repeat and rest), demographic characteristics (eg, age, weight, BMI), type and number of experimental/control groups, and primary outcomes values (eg, mean, SD, MD, and SD difference of them). Furthermore, some studies evaluated only the upper umbilical region, so the researchers utilized data from the upper umbilical region’s IRD for the Homogenization of NMA (Appendix 4a- Table S6).

In this study, to facilitate the analysis of the effect of interventions, our preference was the mean difference (MD) between the pre- and post-test of each group and its standard deviation change (SDchange). Some data were reported as a median, interquartile range (IQR), and 95% confidence interval (CI)38–40. As a result, the MD with change score and SD estimates were calculated using the scatter index formula shown in Appendix 5- Table S9. A few studies reported the results through charts, and we extracted their data using the Get Data Software®9,25. Some studies did not report MDs and SDschange and did not respond to our request, so these variables were calculated using the following formula37,39.

Given the acceptable reliability of the instruments used in the studies, initial assessments have shown that the correlation between baseline and post-intervention values ​​is between 0.79 to 0.90. Therefore, a correlation coefficient of 0.8 was chosen for the following formula21.

Data synthesis and analysis

The NMA and systematic review estimates of DRA were calculated using the groups’ MDs and SD changes by AD-I and NB. The main study and patients’ characteristics were summarised narratively using descriptive synthesis and risk of bias assessment in Appendices 4a-c, Table S7, S8, and Appendix 3- Table S4. The extracted data from the included studies were described in terms of the main outcomes and secondary outcomes (rehabilitation methods related to DRA) as a reporting of pre- and post-treatment mean and standard deviation. Beyond the statistical evaluation, clinical significance was also assessed by applying the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). A 5–10 mm MCID for IRD was used as the benchmark for gauging treatment effect sizes, with study outcomes compared against this threshold to establish whether the observed changes held clinical importance41. The results, organized to highlight their practical clinical relevance, are detailed in Table 3 to ensure accurate interpretation of the data (Appendix 6- Tables S10, S11). Clinical interpretation based on the principles of Man-Son-Hing et al. classifies this result into one of four levels (definite, probable, possible, definitely not)42.

The size of each node and the thickness of lines between interventions in the networks are proportionate to the sample size and the number of available RCTs, respectively. Also, a random-effect NMA with a consistency model under the frequentist approach was performed to combine direct and indirect comparisons39. We presented effect sizes and mean differences of all interventions against a reference standard (RL) based on the additive model in each outcome network at all post-intervention times, according to the clinical relevance in Appendix 7 (Tables S13 to S15) at the NMA result and comparison result tables38. The statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. When the I2 value was 90% (ie, high degree of heterogeneity), we did not perform meta-analysis. Subsequently, treatment connections were graphically examined using network plots and ranked forest plots for each primary outcome post-intervention, incorporating all available data.

The treatments in each network were presented visually by network graph. The random-effects model was applied to estimate the direct and indirect effect sizes, as the direct intervention effect estimates were based on the common variance of the NMA. Subsequently, treatment connections were graphically examined using network plots and ranked forest plots for each primary outcome post-intervention, incorporating all available data. Treatments were ranked based on two indices: SUCRA and P-score, which both indicate the probability of treatment success in terms of relative effect, and the range of both is between zero and one. To assess direct and indirect evidence, a back-calculation model was employed to separate indirect evidence from direct evidence, allowing for the assessment of each intervention’s effect. Also, the researchers reported pairwise comparison by network forest plots (direct evidence) for each outcome using a random-effects model within a frequentist framework (Appendices 7- Table S15 and 8- Figure S7)38. Then, we showed the effect size of all treatment methods versus other treatments for outcomes at each post-intervention in a league table (Appendix 9- Table S16). To draw the network graph, we used Stata 17 software (Stata Corp, USA). All statistical analyses were done using the netmeta package in R software (version 4.3.2)43,44. Further, we used the Review Manager 5.4 (RevMan) for risk of bias assessment (RoB-2).

Assessment of transitivity (Similarity) assumptions

The transitivity assumption was assessed by comparing the clinical and epidemiological participant characteristics (Appendix 10- Table S17, Figure S8). At first, we evaluated the transitivity assumption defined as the balance between the distribution of potential effect modifiers (age, postpartum timing, parity of childbirth, type of delivery, length of treatment, number of participants, and baseline IRD intensity). Thus, the transitivity assumption was reviewed by exploring these variables through trials, intervention methods, and intervention nodes39,45,46.

Assessment of heterogeneity and consistency assumptions

The heterogeneity was assessed in pairwise comparisons and the networks of interventions. The chi-squared (Chi-2) test and I2 statistics were used to check heterogeneity. When the I2 value was 90% (ie, high degree of heterogeneity), we did not perform meta-analysis. Usually, heterogeneity between 0 to 40% is considered as “no serious concern,” 40% to 60% as “moderate concern,” 60% to 75% as “serious concern,” and 75% to 100% as “considerable heterogeneity”4,38,47. As well as network global inconsistency analysis was performed using the Design-by-Treatment Interaction method to investigate whether the effect of interventions varies in studies with different designs. This model is used as a general perspective to examine the discrepancy between direct and indirect evidence. Also, locally, inconsistency in closed loops was performed by applying loop-specific inconsistency models to identify any significant differences between the results obtained from pairwise comparisons (Appendix 11- Tables S18, S19)38.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

To assess the robustness of the results of the NMA, sensitivity analyses were performed. These analyses involved the removal of studies with a high risk of bias (Appendix 12- Tables S20, S21). Then, researchers performed subgroup analyses based on the duration of the intervention, categorizing studies into < 18 sessions versus ≥ 18 sessions (Appendix 13a- Figures S12, S13)46. Additionally, to account for potential confounding due to spontaneous recovery, the researchers conducted stratified analyses by postpartum timing, dividing interventions into three groups: immediate (< 6 weeks), early (6 weeks to 6 months), and late (> 6 months) (Appendix 13b- Figures S15, S17, S19).

Certainty of evidence and publication bias

In all consistent networks, we employed the Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to assess the certainty of evidence across four levels, ranging from very low (indicating a high likelihood that the true effect substantially differs from the estimated effect) to high (reflecting strong confidence that the true effect closely aligns with the estimated effect). To comprehensively interpret the findings, we systematically examined risk of bias (within-study bias), imprecision, indirectness, heterogeneity, inconsistency, and reporting bias using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) approach (Appendix 14- Table S30). Finally, to assess publication bias, a funnel plot was used to examine the balance of publication of studies, and the Egger test was used to detect statistical asymmetry in the data39,43,45. The results of these analyses are presented in Appendix 15- Figure S21.

Results

Study selection and General characteristics

The researchers found 27 RCTs out of 9,768 reviewed studies (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, the search strategy yielded 8,553 records. Screening titles and abstracts identified 8,258 irrelevant hits. The remaining 266 full-text articles were assessed, and 27 studies met the inclusion criteria (see Appendices 2- Table S2, S3, 16- Table S31, S32, and Fig. 1). Of these, one study was not included in the main network of this quantitative synthesis due to the inconsistency of its intervention with other studies. The number of participants was 1340, and their age, BMI, and baseline IRD were 29.92, 25.26, and 28.21 mm, respectively (Table 1). In the data extraction process, we identified the number of participants in the intervention groups: physical agents (n = 43)10,11,13,29,31,34,48–50, exercise therapy (n = 574)5,8,13,18,19,23–26,32,33,51, mind–body practices (n = 18)27,35, different types of abdominal belts and tapes (n = 135)3,8,9,20,25,28, exercise therapy combined with physical agents (n = 338)10,11,13,29,34,48–50, exercise therapy combined with belts (n = 86)3,8,29,30,50, and no treatment (n = 146)3,5,13,19,25,27,28,51. The characteristics of all included studies are detailed in Appendix 4a- Table S6.

Quantitative synthesis

Transitivity, heterogeneity, and inconsistency

No significant concerns were identified regarding the violation of the transitivity assumption in terms of clinical and epidemiologic features for participants of RCTs who were included in the NMA. There was only a slight concern about the duration of the interventions and the postpartum timing, which was resolved by subgroup analysis (Appendix 10- Table S17, Figure S8).

The heterogeneity of the studies indicated that the evidence was generally homogeneous (I2 = 26.80%, p = 0.1862, tau2 = 1.718). In two specific comparisons of NMES + TCME/TCME and TrAS/RL, the I2 was calculated to be 56.89% and 3.84%, respectively, suggesting probable superficial differences between studies in these cases. The inconsistency of the network evidence analysis revealed that the overall structure of the network was favorable in terms of consistency, with no significant difference between direct and indirect evidence (p = 0.1606). The global test of Design-by-Treatment Interaction confirmed that the overall network results were reliable and that there was no significant inconsistency in the association between interventions (p = 0.2029, tau2 = 2.953). Closed-loop analysis demonstrated that no significant inconsistency was observed in any comparisons, indicating both the stability of the combined results and the consistency of direct and indirect evidence in the meta-analysis network (p > 0.05). While this could suggest the absence of statistical heterogeneity among the studies in those comparisons, in this study, it was primarily due to the limited number of studies available for pairwise comparisons. To achieve stable estimates, at least 3 to 4 independent studies are needed in each comparison, which, in the present study, we faced this limitation due to the reduction of studies related to DRA therapeutic interventions. Finally, it is recommended to refer to Appendix 11- Tables S18, S19 for more complete information.

The results of networks

After the analysis of interventions, there were two separate sub-networks and the main network consisted of 26 studies. From 27 studies, the number of pairwise comparisons in the NMA was 42, and there were 31 treatments. The one study was a comparison of AB + LSE / Plank + AB + LSE from Awad et al.30, which was excluded from NMA. The direct and indirect comparison of treatments is shown in the league table (Appendix 9- Table S16) and forest plot (Appendix 8- Figure S7). The league table is a square matrix showing all pairwise comparisons in a network meta-analysis. Mean differences and their confidence intervals are provided in Appendices 7- Figure S3, S4, S6 and 13a-Figure S12, S13, S14 and 13b- Figures S15 to S20 to facilitate comparisons against the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for IRD measurements (Table 3). On the other hand, the additive network meta-analysis model was applied for comparisons of treatments versus RL (no-treatment), and these analysis results were reported in Appendix 7- Table S15.

Network meta-analysis of IRD

26 out of 27 studies constituted an intervention treatment network for DRA. The nodes for the DRA analysis were defined at the level of the rehabilitation methods. Each single or combined treatment was a separate node, and the researchers indicated each of them with one color. Different routes of administration for the same interventions (or combination) were merged into the same node. The Network color information is available in Table 2. Based on the results of this NMA, the largest node belongs to RL, which means that most treatments have formed a network with this variable. The RL is a “no treatment” and most of the interventions were compared with it in this NMA. After that, the other big node belongs to the TCME intervention. This is a traditional exercise in physical therapy and most RCTs compared the newest treatments versus it. In the end, the “TrAS” method, as the last big node of this network graph, is an exclusive exercise method for transverse abdominal (TrA) muscle practice that some therapists use. As it is clear, some treatments formed closed loops with them, and the size of these nodes indicates the number of repetitions in the NMA. Figure 3 shows forming loops in the network graph.

Treatment network graph for DRA. The node size is proportional to the number of RCTs in each intervention, and the line width is related to trials directly comparing the two interventions. colors refer to types of interventions.

The effect size of the treatments in this study is measured based on minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds. According to the study of Mota et al. (2018), the MCID of IRD is approximately between 5 and 10 mm (Appendix 6- Tables S10, S11)5,41. Thus, the mean difference observed in the studies pre- and post-intervention is quantified using the 95% confidence interval and the MCID, ensuring a robust assessment of the clinical relevance of treatment effects (Table 3). The results of pairwise comparisons between treatment interventions are also presented in the form of a league and NMA tables, including the mean difference in effectiveness between the two treatments and the 95% confidence interval. The league table allows for direct assessment of the observed differences between the interventions and helps to understand the extent to which each treatment is more effective than the others. Examination of the values ​​presented in the table indicates that some interventions had more significant effects than others, which can be taken into account in clinical decision-making. Their full details are provided in Appendix 9- Table S16. In the analysis of the results based on the interpretation of GRADE confidence coefficients for each pairwise comparison, as well as the clinical interpretation of the treatment effect in pairwise comparisons aimed at reducing IRD, it was observed that the greatest effect was in the comparison of TCMEB + KT versus RL (MD = –26.25). However, due to its low confidence, this result should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, the comparison of CSE-trx versus RL (MD = –25.14) was assessed with high confidence. Additionally, the comparisons of TCME versus RL (MD = –15.37), TCME + BPFM + NMES versus NMES (MD = –11.08), and REA versus TrAS (MD = –10.47) were also reported with high confidence. Meanwhile, the comparison of AB versus RL (MD = –10.32) showed moderate confidence, while the comparison of RL versus TrAS (MD = –10.15) was interpreted with low confidence. See Appendix 6- Tables S10, S11 and 14- Table S30 for further comparisons. According to Fig. 4, In the forest plot of DRA based on MCID threshold (All treatments Vs RL), it seems that the TCMEB + KT (SUCRA = 0.96, MD = -26.25)8, TCME + BPFM + NMES (SUCRA = 0.95, MD = -25.94)10, the CSE-trx (SUCRA = 0.94, MD = -25.25)5 and the NMES + MMT + TCME (SUCRA = 0.92, MD = -24.62)31 treatments versus the RL reduced the DRA more than others. It should be noted that exercise, physical therapy and physical agents were a main part of these treatments. Moreover, the DCSS + GCSS (SUCRA = 0.25, MD = -3.17)26, the Dynamic-plank (SUCRA = 0.23, MD = -2.60)32, the KT (SUCRA = 0.18, MD = -1.48)25,28, and the Static-plank (SUCRA = 0.17, MD = -1.10)32 indicated that they have a minimal effect on DRA than other interventions. On the other side, four interventions namely the GCSS (SUCRA = 0.11, MD = 0.28)26,33,48, Sham-KT (SUCRA = 0.10, MD = 0.52)28, CUAE (SUCRA = 0.09, MD = 1.00)12,19, and the SLRE (SUCRA = 0.02, MD = 3.86)12 were the weakest of these treatments, and not only did DRA not improve, but it seems they also caused that worse. Besides, there was no statistically significant difference between direct and indirect estimates of DRA (Appendices 7- Table S15 and 8- Figure S7).

The intervention duration subgroup

In the intervention duration subgroup, studies were divided into two groups above (22 studies) and below (5 studies) 18 sessions8,11,28,29,31. However, the network was formed only for interventions above 18 sessions, with 40 comparisons and 27 treatments (Appendix 13a). Based on the results of this NMA, the largest node belongs to RL. After that, the other big node belongs to the TCME intervention. In the end, the TrAS is the last big node of this network graph. As it is clear, some treatments formed closed loops with them, and the size of these nodes indicates the number of repetitions in the NMA. Appendix 13a shows the formed loops in the network graph.

In the forest plot of long-term interventions for improving DRA, it seems that the TCME + BPFM + NMES (SUCRA = 0.98, MD = -25.14)10, CSE-trx (SUCRA = 0.97, MD = -25.25)5, NMES + TCME (SUCRA = 0.91, MD = -21.89)29,31,34,49 and TCME + BPFM + KT (SUCRA = 0.89, MD = -21.17)50 treatments versus the RL reduced the DRA more than others. It should be noted that exercise, physical therapy and physical agents were a main part of these treatments. Moreover, the DCSS + GCSS (SUCRA = 0.26, MD = -2.94)26, the Dynamic-plank (SUCRA = 0.24, MD = -2.60)32, the KT (SUCRA = 0.18, MD = -1.24)25,28, and Static-plank (SUCRA = 0.16, MD = -1.10)32 indicated that they have a minimal effect on DRA than other interventions. On the other side, three interventions, namely the GCSS (SUCRA = 0.09, MD = 0.51)26,33,48, CUAE (SUCRA = 0.08, MD = 1.00)12,19, and SLRE (SUCRA = 0.01, MD = 3.86)12 were the weakest of these treatments, and not only did DRA not improve, but it seems they also caused it to worsen. Moreover, there was no statistically significant difference between direct and indirect estimates of DRA (Appendix 13a- Figures S12, S13, S14).

Five out of 27 studies evaluated improving the IRD with interventions under 18 sessions8,11,28,29,31. Due to the small number of studies, quantitative analysis was not performed and there is no certainty to compare short-term and long-term interventions. However, their results of IRD improvement were presented as follows: EA + TCME (MD = -6.41 ± 3.82) from Liu et al.11, TCMEB + KT (MD = -18.65 ± 4.80) from Bobowik et al.8, NMES + TCME (MD = -13.8 ± 3.49; -11 ± 4.85) from Chen et al.31 and Situt et al.29, NMES + MMT + TCME (MD = -15.8 ± 7.07) from Chen et al.31, KT + TCME (MD = -2.99 ± 4.99) from Situt et al.29, KT (MD = -2 ± 1.42) from Ptaszkowska et al.28.

The IRD timing of the postpartum subgroup

The timing postpartum, which was the second subgroup of studies, was divided into three groups: immediate time (< 6 weeks)3,8,9,20, early time (6 weeks to 6 months)5,10,12,13,23,25,26,29–35,48,49,51, and late time (> 6 months)11,18,19,27,28,50. This indicates a difference in the time intervals for treatment initiation and outcome assessment (Appendix 13b). This subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of intervention timing as a determinant of treatment effect size, while also distinguishing the intervention effects from the natural postpartum spontaneous recovery process. The findings indicated that the majority of treatments fell within the ‘early time’ category, signifying that interventions were generally administered within a moderate post-initiation timeframe, and their effects subsequently assessed. However, due to limited evidence and a lack of common pairwise comparisons across all three temporal groups, no conclusive insights could be drawn regarding the influence of intervention timing on treatment efficacy. In the analysis of the Immediate time, the comparison between TCME and RL yielded a P-score of 1.00 with a mean difference (MD = -13.10), indicating a definite clinical effect on IRD, and other treatments were possible effects. In the Early time subgroup, researchers observed a spectrum of effects from definite to probable, ranging from TCME + BPFM + NMES to TrAS + KT treatments. Finally, in the Late time subgroup, due to the absence of RL in pairwise comparisons, all treatments were evaluated against TCME, so they can not be compared to the two other groups. For a comprehensive overview, including NMA results for subgroups, network structures, ranking forest plots, and pairwise treatment comparisons, please refer to Appendix 13b- Figures S15 to S20.

Also, 20 out of 27 studies assessed secondary outcomes related to DRA3,5,10–12,18–20,23,25,26,28,29,31,34,35,48–50. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of high-risk studies on the overall results, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding these studies. After excluding high-risk studies, the heterogeneity (I2) value decreased from 26.80% to zero, indicating an improvement in the consistency of the data in the meta-analysis network model. However, this change was minor and did not have a significant impact on the overall results, indicating the robustness and reliability of the analyses performed. Detailed details of this analysis are provided in Appendix 12- Figures S9, S10 and Table S20.

Certainty of evidence and publication bias

In the GRADE assessment, most comparisons in this analysis network were based on a single study, except for two comparisons, RL/TrAS and NMES + TCME/TCME, which involved two studies, which limits the analytical power and the generalizability of the results. Because of the absence of complementary data, a more precise evaluation of heterogeneity and inconsistency in these comparisons was not achievable. Therefore, the findings from these comparisons (54.77% high confidence, 30.96% moderate confidence, and 14.28% low confidence) should be interpreted cautiously, and further research is advised to confirm or refute these results. Although relying on a single study may have limitations regarding generalizability, most comparisons were generally rated as moderate to high confidence due to well-designed and methodologically sound studies, low risk of bias, adequate precision (narrow confidence intervals), and close alignment with the research question. This suggests that despite being sourced from a single study in each comparison, the available evidence is of high quality. Hence, in the evidence analysis, 11 pairwise comparisons from two pilot studies were downgraded by one GRADE level due to limitations commonly associated with these types of studies (such as small sample sizes, preliminary experimental design, and the potential for greater imprecision). In other words, since these comparisons were based on pilot studies, they were reduced by one level according to the GRADE quality of evidence standard to reflect the possible weakening of the precision and generalizability of the results. In the GRADE table report, in the “Overall confidence” column for these 11 comparisons, the quality was specifically downgraded from high to moderate or moderate to low, while other similar comparisons based on RCT studies may be of higher quality. So, the GRADE judgments are reported in Appendix 14- Table S30 for the IRD network meta-analysis. As well as, the authors applied the Egger test and the funnel plot to assess publication bias (p = 0.552), and their result was reported in Appendix 15- Figure S21 and Fig. 5.

Discussion

It is contended that this is the first systematic review with network meta-analysis related to the comparative effectiveness of 31 different rehabilitation methods for treating the DRA and its secondary outcomes in 1,340 patients. The treatments were ranked based on their effect on IRD. Based on the GRADE assessment, roughly 45% of the studies exhibited moderate (30.95%) to low (14.28%) certainty. Consequently, although our results indicate a favorable effect of certain interventions on reducing IRD, these outcomes must be interpreted with caution. The uncertainty in quality, as well as the few number of evidence, suggests that generalizing the results to all populations or making broad use of evidence-based recommendations in clinical settings requires caution and requires future studies with more robust and reliable evidence. Based on our findings, exercise-based physical therapy, particularly TCME, when combined with breathing exercises and additional modalities targeting both the inner and outer core muscles, appears to offer superior benefits in reducing IRD compared to alternative approaches. While integrating exercise with other modalities can enhance treatment outcomes for DRA, some interventions proved ineffective or even detrimental. Comprehensive exercise programs demonstrated greater reductions in DRA than Non-comprehensive exercise programs (e.g., TrAS, CUAE, or SLRE), suggesting that the interconnected function of musculoskeletal units necessitates an exact and complete treatment strategy rather than focusing on a single muscle group and may even cause muscle imbalance. Furthermore, adding NMES to comprehensive exercise programs seems to further boost their effectiveness, thereby supporting the multimodal intervention concept46.

Due to the limited evidence available for interventions comprising fewer than 18 sessions, our network meta-analysis could not conclusively discriminate between short-term and long-term intervention effects. Consequently, they should be interpreted with relative caution. To examine the impact of postpartum timing on IRD improvement, the researchers conducted a subgroup analysis that categorized studies into three groups based on the initiation of intervention after delivery: "immediate," "early," and "late." Although due to the limited evidence and small number of comparisons in the immediate and late groups, we were unable to conclusively determine the effect of timing. Furthermore, the majority of the available evidence pertains to the early intervention group, which underscores the need for further investigation. Consequently, it can not be interpreted with confidence, and clinical recommendations should not be broadly generalized without taking these limitations into account.

Evidence on the IRD Improvement

It is unlikely that one kind of rehab method is the single best approach to treating DRA or other disorders, because of the difference between assessment tools, and sometimes, there are differences between results. In this NMA, there is moderately high confidence in the evidence indicating that exercise-based physical therapy combined with NMES is effective, particularly when delivered through progressive training programs that integrate both local and global core muscle exercises. Specifically, interventions such as TCME + BPFM + NMES, CSE-trx, and NMES + MMT + TCME demonstrated high-confidence evidence, whereas the TCMEB + KT protocol, although showing a strong effect (MD = –26.25), was supported by lower-confidence evidence. Notably, all these interventions involve exercises targeting both the deep (inner) and superficial (outer) layers of the core musculature. These findings should be interpreted with relative certainty, acknowledging the varying levels of evidence quality across the different modalities.

In the current study, the authors observed that the impact of comprehensive exercise types was greater than other modalities, such as types of belts, Kinesio tape, or exercises focused on one or two muscles, on the DRA. The first intervention in the ranking was TCMEB combined with KT, which had a significant effect based on clinical thresholds. Bobowik et al. (2018) started their interventions three days postpartum, and the duration of these interventions was only one session per week for six weeks8. Given the short duration of the intervention and its early onset after delivery, we hypothesized that this strong effect could relate to the spontaneous recovery of abdominal muscles after delivery. We conducted a subgroup analysis of postpartum time to determine the impact of the timing of intervention initiation as a covariate factor on the effect size of the interventions. However, due to the lack of sufficient evidence in the three timing categories studied and being a study in this pairwise comparison, this hypothesis remained inconclusive. On the other hand, based on the graded analysis (low confidence certainty), it is advisable to interpret the results of this intervention with caution. Based on the KT ranking score in the forest plot (Picture 4), adding KT to TCMEB had no impact on this collaboration. However, the breathing technique is helpful for DRA improvement and can be used in therapeutic exercises to facilitate a co-contraction of deep trunk muscles. Thus, integrating them with global and multimodal (Local and global) exercises can be efficient in improving DRA. Despite the strong recommendations for diaphragmatic rehabilitation for deeper abdominal muscle contractions52,53, they have never been studied postpartum. Hence, their function in DRA rehabilitation remains unknown.

Combining TCME and NMES and adding MMT or electromyographic BPFM modalities to them was better at decreasing IRD than TCME and NMES alone in women with DRA. These interventions had high certainty based on the GRADE analysis and can be interpreted with more confidence. Although muscle adaptations may result from BPFM loading through electromyographic biofeedback, in the KT + TCME/TCME + BPFM + KT comparison, it was observed that the MD with and without BPFM was 2.30 mm, indicating that this difference is very small and its effect size is not acceptable based on the clinical threshold. Liang et al.10 did not use electromyographic BPFM alone in another group. For this reason, it can not determine the exact impact of that. The next modality, the MMT, can increase blood supply to muscles and repair their tissues. However, in the comparison of NMES + MMT + TCME/NMES + TCME, the MD between the groups with and without MMT was about 2 mm, which indicates that this difference is very slight and, based on the clinical threshold, the effect size of MMT is not acceptable. Thus, the limited number of studies31 means that the effects on IRD remain uncertain, and further research is necessary to draw definitive conclusions. It should be mentioned that in this NMA, the TCME is more effective than NMES because of its ranking score in the forest plot. Therefore, in this collaboration, the effectiveness of TCME carries more weight than the NMES. Most of the top-ranked interventions demonstrated that incorporating comprehensive TCME exercises with other interventions, particularly NAMES, can have an acceptable impact more than others5,10,11,18,30,49,50,54.

Suspension training, like CSE-trx, focuses on inner and outer core muscles. It is a suspension TRX method and creates highly efficient pressure on the abdominal wall5. Suspension exercises effectively engage the entire abdominal muscle group by leveraging the natural function of these muscles. Also, this intervention applied a comprehensive approach through progressions of isolated muscular and functional exercises with abdominal breathing, respectively. CSE-trx showed an acceptable effect size compared to RL based on clinical thresholds and a probably acceptable effect size compared to TCME training. Although this comparison had a high confidence level based on GRADE analysis, it should be interpreted with some caution due to the lack of evidence. Furthermore, the STEP training also followed this principle (progressions of intervention) in its exercise design. So, based on the clinical threshold and high confidence GRADE, this exercise training versus RL could decrease the IRD18.

The NMES after delivery had an impact similar to the TCME in IRD, but combining both had a positive effect on their isolated application. The comparison of both interventions had moderate confidence based on the GRADE analysis, and therefore, interpretation requires sensitivity and caution. Also, the result of the RAE method, which trained global abdominal muscles, was close to them. Tadmare et al. (2024) and Situt et al. (2021), in justifying their intervention, acknowledged that NMES helps in activating larger type II nerve fibers with relatively low levels of stimulation29,34. Additionally, since NMES stimulates nerve fibers distributed throughout the muscle, it can activate deep muscle fibers at lower training intensities34. Moreover, electrical muscle stimulation activates a higher percentage of type II muscle fibers compared to voluntary exercise at the same intensity, as only high-intensity voluntary contractions typically engage type II fibers10,48. According to the MCID threshold of TCME + EA and TCME pairwise comparison, it sounds like EA has a slight impact on IRD improvement.

The RL/TrAS comparison with high confidence GRADE indicated that while transversus abdominis training has a moderately significant effect on IRD, it appears to exert minimal influence on improving IRD when compared to RAE training (RAE/TrAS). More evidence is needed to confidently support this interpretation. Simpson et al. observed that while the RAE group showed a reduction in diastasis, these exercises did not reduce the distortion of the linea alba. They hypothesized that activating the Transversus Abdominis (TrAS) would result in less distortion of the linea alba, but could not decrease IRD23. It appears that RAE primarily strengthens the rectus abdominis muscle and decreases the IRD but does not correct the linea alba distortion. Consequently, the reduction in diastasis recti may be reversible if the training is discontinued23. Therefore, isolated TrAS exercises alone are not sufficient to improve DRA. Abdominal oblique or OMAS exercises were slightly superior to TrAS, but this difference was not important. It seems the TrAS and OMAS training helps deeper abdominal and pelvic floor muscle contractions and reduces intra-abdominal pressure, elevating the pelvic viscera and relaxing the diaphragm. However, these exercises can not be a sufficient intervention for treating DRA because of their low impact and should be combined with superficial muscle training3,25,33,51.

The certainty of evidence for mindfulness exercises is low, reflecting methodological shortcomings that require careful interpretation. Consequently, the study’s results should be approached with caution and considered in light of these limitations. In these exercises, the results show that PPE was more effective in decreasing mild IRD than the BLME27,35. Pilates is believed to be an exercise program that connects the inner muscles of the anterior abdominal wall to the pelvic floor muscles and provides stability to the uterus and pelvic girdle with the coordinated movement of the limbs35. Also, it impacts the core’s inner and outer muscles, so it seems that one of the reasons for its similarity to TCME exercises is this issue. In addition, the related study on decreasing IRD found that BLME had a poor impact; therefore, more studies are needed in this field.

The comparison between AB and TG was supported by high confidence; however, clinically, there was no significant difference between these interventions, and both demonstrated only a small effect relative to the RL group. Since the interventions commenced three days postpartum, the magnitude of their effect remains controversial, and the limited evidence on the influence of timing further undermines the certainty of these findings. Based on the results, AB and TG could reduce IRD more than inner muscle exercise. However, improving IRD cannot be credited merely to AB, as there were some differences in duration, types of intervention design, etc. Depledge et al. (2023) reported that the mechanisms by which abdominal binding aids in the recovery from RA diastasis remain unclear9. Keshwani et al. (2019) acknowledged that abdominal binding might protect newly formed collagen and hold the abdominal wall muscles in a shortened position during movement3. This could facilitate a remodeling process within the muscle fibers by reducing the number of sarcomeres in series and shortening connective tissue fibers. Additionally, Tubigrip and belts may provide sensory feedback that alters muscle activation. However, excessive tightening can elevate intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) and increase the risk of future uterine prolapse by exerting pressure on the pelvic floor3,9,20,55. Finally, based on the studies, KT appears to have no visible effect on the DRA rehabilitation program. This treatment method was placed at the end of the ranking plot and before RL, and there are not enough studies to support the application of KT in DRA treating methods25,28. Hence, it cannot be mentioned as an appropriate intervention in DRA treatment.

Skoura et al., in a scoping review, acknowledged that "optimal isolated and synergistic contractions of inner unit muscles and tension-free diaphragmatic breathing should be prioritized during the immediate postpartum period for DRA. Additionally, LA tension through TrAS contraction promotes IRD closure7." This belief is correct but incomplete because, based on evidence, these contractions alone are insufficient, and the experts should apply exercises that engage both deep and superficial abdominal muscles. For example, the TrAS exercise led to a slight decrease in IRD7,14,15.

According to this NMA, isolated exercises for a specific muscle in the deep or superficial core region have a minimal effect, and these exercises show the most effectiveness when combined. This can be due to the strong fascial connection of LA between the entire abdominal muscles. Based on studies reviewed, therapeutic exercises should be advanced from inner unit muscles in the first phase into outer unit muscles in the next phases, and after that, both have to be integrated in the last phase. This process can be a comprehensive design for the treatment of DRA. According to the above description, isolated movement exercises such as SLRE or CUAE increased IRD approximately, and GCSS training was similar to the “no treatment” condition or the same as RL. In these exercises, a single movement was used to improve DRA, which had an inefficient effect. Therefore, it seems that performing these exercises alone and in the initial phases of rehabilitation can have a harmful effect. These exercises should be applied eccentrically and integrated with other deep and superficial core muscle exercises in the semi-advanced phase and concentrically in the advanced phase of DRA rehabilitation.

Implications for clinicians and experts

Overall, the findings suggest that integrating both superficial and deep abdominal wall contractions through a progressive training approach, particularly those engaging the entire core musculature using NMES or breathing techniques, may be beneficial in activating the rectus abdominis muscle without exacerbating the separation of the linea alba fascia. Based on the results of this NMA, exercise-based rehabilitation appears to be more favorable compared to other treatment modalities. Specifically, interventions that incorporate comprehensive local–global core exercises may be prioritized, followed by exercises targeting superficial muscles such as the rectus abdominis, and subsequently the deep core muscles. Nevertheless, isolated exercise modalities alone may not be sufficient for addressing DRA, as the current evidence does not consistently support their efficacy when used independently. Regarding non-exercise interventions, further high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to draw firmer conclusions. Within the scope of the current evidence base, NMES may hold more promise in managing DRA compared to other modalities. Although KT is commonly used in musculoskeletal rehabilitation for support, current findings indicate that KT, when applied alone, does not significantly improve IRD and therefore, may not be an effective standalone intervention for postpartum DRA.

In combined intervention strategies, the integration of structured exercise programs with NMES has shown comparatively better outcomes. It is important to note that factors such as study design, intervention intensity, and duration may influence the overall effectiveness of these interventions. However, given the limited number of studies and the variation in the GRADE certainty of the evidence, ranging from low to moderate-to-high confidence levels, clinical interpretation should be made cautiously.

Strengths and limitations

A primary limitation of our network meta-analysis is the sparse replication of direct treatment comparisons. Although the network comprises 42 pairwise comparisons, only 2 comparisons are reported in more than one study (RL vs. TrAS and NMES + TCME vs. TCME). The remaining 40 comparisons each appear in a single study. As a result, statistical estimation of comparison-specific heterogeneity (τ2 or I2) is unreliable for the vast majority of comparisons. We therefore relied on the common τ2 from the random-effects network model to characterize overall heterogeneity, and we have transparently reported this approach in the Methods. Future research with additional direct comparisons is needed to allow robust calculation of pairwise heterogeneity statistics.

Other limitations of this review were the time constraints associated with data collection and analysis. Given the timing of the study, it was not possible to conduct additional sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, new data published after the search cut-off date may influence the findings of this analysis. Additionally, due to the limited number of studies, it was not feasible to perform meta-regression to examine the effect of moderating variables. To address this limitation, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effects. Some studies reported an IRD mean below 2.2 cm in the baseline, which was a concern24,55–58. Hence, we did not apply them in this study. Also, another limitation we encountered when we started our study was the lack of use of databases such as EMBASE, CINAHL, and PEDro, which we were unable to search due to international restrictions imposed on our region.

Our study also had some strengths. It is the first NMA study in the DRA field, and this review followed the Cochrane Handbook and PRISMA-NMA reporting guidance17,59,60. The quality of studies was relatively high to moderate, except for a few. For a general search, strong selection criteria and detailed data extraction processes are required. The results are reported completely and transparently. The number of included trials grew into a compact and well-connected network for analyses. Finally, this network meta-analysis and systematic review helps as a valuable resource for comprehension of the rehabilitation methods of postpartum DRA, their advantages or disadvantages, and ranking of them in terms of effect power on IRD and secondary outcomes for more research and designing effective interventions in future DRA studies.

Future recommendations

Given the limitations of the current study, including a small number of studies, insufficient differentiation of each pairwise comparison, and a limited number of treatment comparisons, it is recommended that future research focus on increasing the sample size, applying more granular subgroup analyses (for instance, categorizing interventions as immediate, early, and late), and utilizing meta-regression to assess the influence of moderating variables such as the timing of intervention initiation and its duration. As mentioned, studies are very few and scattered, and it is necessary to validate existing rehabilitation interventions with further research. The methodological quality of the studies should be increased to increase their level of confidence. Studies should be clear and generalizable. High sample size and randomization should be considered seriously in future studies. It is necessary to follow the design principles of training in the rehabilitation exercises protocol.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis suggests that comprehensive exercise interventions targeting both deep and superficial abdominal muscles, especially when combined with neuromuscular electrical stimulation and breathing techniques, are more effective in reducing the distance between the rectus abdominis muscles in women with postpartum diastasis recti abdominis, compared to isolated exercises or passive treatments. Among non-exercise approaches, neuromuscular electrical stimulation showed supportive effects, while kinesio taping and abdominal binders demonstrated limited benefit. Although the overall heterogeneity was low and results were consistent, the certainty of evidence varied from low to high. Therefore, clinical application should be approached with caution. Further high-quality clinical trials are needed to strengthen these conclusions.
