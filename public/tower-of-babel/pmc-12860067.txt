Variation in caesarean section rates according to the Robson classification in four low- and middle-income countries: a meta-analysis of groups 1 to 4
Authors: Marion Ravit, Camille Etcheverry, Charles Kabore, Pisake Lumbiganon, Guillermo Carroli, Hung Mac Qoc Nhu, Ana Pilar Betrán, Celina Gialdini, Mike Robson, Alexandre Dumont
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaesarean section (CS) rates have increased globally, with substantial variations across healthcare settings. Monitoring CS rates using Robson’s Ten Group Classification System (RTGCS) provides insights into obstetric practices. This study examines the variation in CS rates among women classified in Groups 1 to 4 of the RTGCS across hospitals in four low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and explores hospital-level factors associated with these variations.MethodsThis observational study analyzed CS rates in 32 hospitals (8 per country) in Argentina, Burkina Faso, Thailand, and Vietnam. Data were collected from hospital records between January and December 2020. A meta-analysis assessed hospital-level heterogeneity in CS rates, and meta-regression models explored potential determinants, including hospital characteristics such as staffing levels, equipment availability, and birth volume.ResultsThe overall CS rate in the 32 hospitals was 45.4%, with nearly half (46.5%) of CS performed on women in Groups 1 to 4. There was substantial heterogeneity in CS rates among hospitals (I2 > 75%, p < 0.001). Factors associated with variations included country differences, staffing levels (midwife-to-delivery and doctor-to-delivery ratios at night), the presence of functional equipment (ultrasound and cardiotocograph), birth volume, and the possibility of labor companionship. Hospitals with higher birth volumes and greater staff availability had higher CS rates, while those permitting companionship during labor exhibited lower rates.ConclusionsHospital characteristics appear to influence CS rates among low-risk groups, highlighting the need to consider institutional factors when implementing CS reduction strategies. Routine monitoring using the RTGCS, alongside tailored interventions addressing hospital-specific challenges, could support efforts to optimize CS use in LMICs. Further research is needed to confirm the role of organizational factors and guide policy decisions.Trial registrationThe QUALI-DEC trial is registered with the Current Controlled Trials database (ISRCTN67214403), registration date: 30/03/2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08586-w.Keywords: Caesarean section, Hospital organization, Robson’s ten group classification system, Low- and middle-income countries

Background

During recent decades, caesarean section (CS) rates have increased worldwide, with no evident benefit for either mothers or newborns beyond a certain threshold [1–3]. Projections have shown that by 2030, 28.5% of women worldwide will give birth via CS, and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) will be major contributors to this trend [4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends Robson’s Ten Group Classification System (RTGCS) as an international reference system for monitoring CS rates and auditing obstetrical practices in health care facilities [1]. An overall reduction in CS will primarily arise from reducing the primary unnecessary CS rate in Robson Groups 1–4, accounting for approximately half of all CSs in LMICs [5, 6]. These women may be considered to be in “lower risk” groups, particularly for having a CS, from the perspective that they have a singleton pregnancy and with the foetus in cephalic presentation, which has reached at least 37 weeks of gestation, and with no previous CS.

The investigation and prioritization of women in Groups 1–4 is a feasible option to maximize the impact of beneficial interventions on safely reducing unnecessary caesareans in LMICs. Reducing CS rates among women with a previous CS (Robson Group 5) presents additional challenges in contexts with suboptimal availability of quality emergency obstetric care, particularly in low-income countries [7]. In addition, a large proportion of caesareans in Groups 6–10 (including multiple pregnancies and abnormalities) may be more difficult to reduce under suboptimal conditions in LMICs. For this reason, the “appropriate use of caesarean section through QUALIty DECision-making by women and providers” (QUALI-DEC) project aims to reduce CS rates among women categorized in Groups 1–4 of the RTGCS in participating hospitals throughout four LMICs (Argentina, Burkina Faso, Thailand and Vietnam) [8]. The multifaceted intervention combines four key active ingredients: opinion leaders to implement evidence-based clinical guidelines; caesarean audits and feedback to help providers identify unfulfilled indications for CS; a decision analysis tool (DAT) helping women in making an informed choice of mode of birth; and the implementation of WHO recommendations on labour companionship to support women during labour. Previous studies have shown that the CS rate is associated with hospital-level characteristics such as level of care, teaching status, and annual number of births [9–12]. Therefore, QUALI-DEC hospitals were selected to reflect the range of contexts.

The objective of this baseline study was to evaluate the variation in CS rates in Robson Groups 1–4 across 32 QUALI-DEC hospitals and to determine whether hospital-level characteristics may explain part of this variation.

Methods

This was an observational study using baseline routine data from QUALI-DEC participating hospitals in Argentina, Burkina Faso, Thailand, and Vietnam.

Targeted sites and participants

The study was conducted in 32 hospitals (8 hospitals per country) that participated in the QUALI-DEC project. Facilities were purposefully selected with country investigators who were striving to reflect the range of contexts, such as primary, secondary and tertiary levels of care; public and private hospitals; and teaching and non-academic facilities (Table 2). The selection was also made in collaboration with the health ministries of each country to reflect the ministries’ programmatic activities and priorities. The implementation of the RTGCS started in January 2020 in each hospital according to the WHO guidelines [1, 5]. Each woman admitted for delivery was classified into one of the 10 Robson groups that are mutually exclusive and totally inclusive. The 10 groups are based on six basic obstetric variables (parity, previous CS, onset of labour, gestational age, foetal presentation and lying and number of foetuses) that are routinely collected by midwives or physicians in the 32 participating hospitals.

Data sources and quality control

We used summary statistics (numbers of vaginal deliveries and number of caesarean sections according to the Robson group) that were collected daily from January to December 2020 in each hospital. The data were extracted from the hospitals’ birth registers and medical records by a trained health care provider and controlled quarterly by the country data manager. Monthly summary statistics were collected and managed by using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the Karolinska Institute and controlled at a monthly frequency by the principal data manager (MRa) for pending data and duplicated records.

Information on hospital-level characteristics was collected through baseline formative research conducted in 2020 as part of the QUALI-DEC project to adapt the intervention to the local context [13]. During one-day observation of the labour ward and use of a standardized questionnaire, a member of the research team explored available resources for obstetric care, physical space management, the health workforce and the implementation of labour companionship in practice.

Based on this information, we defined eleven hospital-level characteristics that have already been identified in the literature as being determinants of CS practice [10–12, 14–16]: the referral level (primary, secondary or tertiary level of reference), the type of facility (private or public), the teaching status (academic hospital or not), the presence of a functional ultrasound for obstetric examination, the presence of a functional CTG for electronic foetal monitoring, the organization of delivery rooms (individual or shared), the possibility of having a companion during childbirth (in terms of space and authorizations), the birth volume (deliveries/year) of the hospital, the ratio of births per obstetrician or per midwife (day and night shift, based on the median of births per staff), and the availability of an anaesthetist 24/7 for the delivery ward. The accuracy of the predefined characteristics of each hospital was controlled by country teams. We have also considered the country as being a determinant of CS practices because the political and societal context may explain differences between countries [4, 17].

Outcome

The primary outcome was the annual CS rate in Robson Groups 1 to 4. Monthly summary statistics were aggregated by hospital to estimate the proportion of caesarean deliveries in each group and for a one-year period. This allows accurate CS rates to be measured for specific groups of women, which may be underrepresented in low-volume hospitals such as Robson Groups 2 and 4.

Analysis

The annual CS rates for each Robson group in each hospital were meta-analysed, with percentage as the primary measure (numerator = number of CSs and denominator = number of women). Between-hospital heterogeneity was identified by using I2 statistics, and random effects models were used to account for between-hospital heterogeneity, where appropriate. I2 statistics are an approach used to quantify the effect of heterogeneity among hospitals and are based on the chi-square test (where Q is the chi-square statistic), which assesses whether observed differences in CS rates are due to chance alone. Therefore, the I2 provides the percentage of total variation across hospitals explained by heterogeneity rather than chance. For instance, an I2 of 0% indicates that all of the variability in hospital-based CS rates is due to sampling error and not to heterogeneity among hospitals. Conversely, an I2 of 75% suggests that three-quarters of the variability in CS rates can be attributed to inconsistency among hospitals. An I2 greater than 75% was considered to indicate high heterogeneity [18]. Forest plots were used to visually help in the interpretation of the data.

To consider the obstetrical characteristics of the women that can affect CS practices, analyses were conducted by Robson Groups 1 to 4.

A meta-regression was performed to explore the heterogeneity of CS rates among hospitals. The eleven hospital-level characteristics and country locations were assessed by using univariate meta-regression models. Univariate random-effects meta-regressions were conducted by using the “metareg” command in STATA IC 16 data analysis statistical software and weighted by using effect size standard errors. Regression results are presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals to reflect the relative difference in CS rates from the reference (for categorical covariates) or per-unit change (for continuous covariates) by using the “eform” option in STATA IC 16.

Results

Robson classification

We collected information on 159,612 deliveries, including 72,540 CSs (45.4%), from January 1 st to December 31 st, 2020. Table 1 reports the Robson Classification for the 32 participating hospitals during the study period. Women in Robson Groups 1–4 represented a large proportion of the obstetric population (67.9%). The CS rates among these women were relatively high and ranged from 14.7% in Group 3 to 74.0% in Group 2. Therefore, women in Groups 1 to 4 were the largest contributors to the overall CS rate (46.5%). Women with a previous CS (Group 5) had high CS rates (88.2%) and were the second largest contributors to the overall CS rate (33.3%).

Hospital characteristics

Table 2 presents the hospital-level characteristics. Most hospitals are public teaching facilities with functional ultrasound machines and cardiotocographs for electronic foetal monitoring. Twenty-three hospitals (72%) had delivery rooms that were shared with other women in labour. An anaesthetist was available 24/7 for the delivery ward in 12 hospitals (37%), and companionship during childbirth was possible in 18 participating facilities (56%). On average, there was approximately 1 woman per midwife/nurse and 3 women per obstetrician during the day and 4 during night shifts. However, there were major differences among the hospitals.

Meta-analysis

The pooled CS rates ranged from 28.4% (95% CI = 22.7–34.5) in Group 1 to 77.3% (95% CI = 72.1–82.1) in Group 2, 12.1% (95% CI = 9.2–15.4) in Group 3 and 54.4% (95% CI = 48.3–60.6) in Group 4 (Fig. 1). We observed a high level of heterogeneity among hospitals for all four Robson groups (I2 > 75%, p < 0.0001) by using a random effects model.

Discussion

Main findings

This analysis (including data from 32 hospitals in Argentina, Burkina Faso, Thailand and Vietnam) shows that CS rates are particularly high among women in spontaneous labour with a single foetus in cephalic position at term and who are considered a relatively low-risk group of women. Our meta-analysis also showed that CS rates among women in Groups 1, 2, 3 and 4 of the RTGCS are heterogeneous both among countries and among hospitals in the same country. Country, equipment, staffing level and the organization of delivery rooms to allow for companionship during childbirth may explain (to different degrees) the variation in CS rates within each group of the classification system. These findings are directly relevant for the QUALI-DEC intervention, which combines audit and feedback, peer review, training of health professionals and the promotion of companionship during labour. In particular, our results support the importance of implementing companionship, which was consistently associated with lower CS rates across Robson groups. They also highlight the need to consider hospital workload and staffing when adapting the intervention to different contexts. This evidence can guide refinements of the QUALI-DEC strategy to better address organisational barriers that contribute to unnecessary CS use.

Strengths and limitations

As part of the QUALI-DEC project, this study has several key strengths, including its large sample size, wide geographical coverage and diverse choice of hospitals. The use of RTGCS allows for comparisons of practices between hospitals and countries while considering the characteristics of women. However, this study had several limitations. First, we could not control for high-risk pregnancies within each of the low-risk groups. This would have been particularly relevant in Burkina Faso, where 56% of women admitted for delivery in participating hospitals in 2020 were referred from other health care facilities. Second, we did not measure other relevant hospital-level or provider-level characteristics, such as the availability of clinical guidelines, the seniority and training of obstetricians, labour pain management, the formal and informal costs of delivery, health care provider remuneration and the legal context. In addition, national clinical guidelines for caesarean section vary across—and sometimes within—countries, and their implementation is heterogeneous. Training of midwives and doctors regarding labour monitoring and decision-making for CS also differs substantially. These factors likely contribute to part of the unexplained heterogeneity in CS rates observed in this study. As a consequence, between 59 and 96% of the heterogeneity found in the CS rates in Robson Groups 1 to 4 could not be explained. In a complementary analysis, Etcheverry et al. [9] investigated how hospital organisation influences CS use in the same four countries, focusing on qualitative and survey data on organisational factors. In contrast, the present study provides a meta-analysis of Robson groups 1 to 4 based on hospital-level data, which allows us to quantify and compare CS rates across settings. Together, these two studies offer a broader understanding: Etcheverry et al. highlight organisational determinants, while our analysis quantifies variations in CS rates by Robson groups and hospital characteristics. The findings indicated that organizational factors linked to favoured resources, the absence of privacy that encourages women's preference for CS, and the burdensome workload of health care providers are the underlying causes of the excessive use of Caesarean sections in these hospitals. Third, disparities in data quality may also explain the heterogeneity. However, to minimize this bias, we ensured that we trained the data collectors in each hospital and checked the quality regularly.

Interpretation

Compared with those in other countries, the CS rates in all of the groups tended to be lower in Argentina. This difference may be explained by the specific characteristics of the participating hospitals in Argentina. All of the participating hospitals in Argentina are public, relatively well equipped, with low birth volume (< 2000 annual births), located in the Buenos Aires city area with a relatively well-performing health system and with companionship during childbirth in seven of the eight hospitals in the study. In contrast, hospitals in Burkina Faso are equally located in urban and rural contexts with suboptimal health care systems and a high proportion (56% in 2020) of women who were referred during labour from other health care facilities. Additionally, in Vietnam and Thailand, a lower fertility rate than in Argentina and Burkina Faso may add pressure to women and obstetricians in favour of primary caesarean birth [8, 19].

When considering the disparities between countries, two covariates related to equipment were significantly associated with hospital-level CS rates. First, we observed that the CS rate in Group 1 was greater in hospitals with ultrasound machines. Although ultrasound machine examination is very useful for screening for multiple pregnancies, breech presentations or abnormalities, its accuracy in estimating foetal birth weight is compromised by large intra- and interobserver variability [20]. Additionally, there is no evidence that routine use of ultrasound on admission in labour for cephalic presentation at term can improve birth outcomes [21]. We hypothesize that the inappropriate use of ultrasound for women in Group 1 may contribute to misdiagnosis of macrosomia, which could be associated with higher rates of unnecessary caesarean sections during labour, particularly in Vietnam. A recent systematic review showed that women with a false-positive diagnosis of foetal macrosomia based on estimated foetal weight are more likely to be treated with emergency CS [22]. In contrast, hospital-level CS rates in Group 4 were lower in hospitals with cardiotocographs (CTGs) than in hospitals without this device. The role of continuous electronic foetal monitoring (EFM) during labour remains debated: while it can benefit newborn health in high-risk pregnancies, it has also been associated with increased rates of caesarean and operative vaginal deliveries compared with intermittent auscultation [23]. Importantly, the WHO does not recommend continuous cardiotocography for assessment of fetal well-being in healthy pregnant women undergoing spontaneous labour [24]. Given the absence of data separating women with induced labour from those with planned CS in Group 4, our interpretations should be considered with caution. Further research is needed to better understand the potential contribution of CTG and ultrasound use to variations in CS rates.

Our results suggest that a greater number of deliveries in a hospital corresponded to a greater CS rate among women in Robson Groups 1 to 4. This is particularly pronounced in Vietnamese hospitals with more than 20,000 deliveries per year and overall CS rates of over 50% (Supplementary File SF1). In this context, CS overuse may be a response to major organisational constraints. Whereas a CS can be conducted in less than one hour, a vaginal birth will take several hours. A heavy workload in the labour ward may add pressure to health care providers in favour of planning a CS before labour or performing a CS during labour [16]. One of the hypotheses would be that the availability of an anaesthetist for a labour ward and a high density of obstetricians may encourage this behaviour.

Finally, our study showed that the possibility of nulliparous women having a companion during spontaneous labour is associated with lower CS rates in Group 1 than in hospitals where companionship is not possible. Our results are in accordance with those of a Cochrane systematic review of 26 studies [25], which demonstrated a 25% reduction in CS rates among women receiving continuous support via a companion of choice compared with those receiving usual care. Our findings also showed that the possibility of having a companion is associated with lower CS rates in Groups 2 and 4. These groups include women with planned CS before labour and women with induced labour. Planned CS is commonly performed on maternal request in LMICs, especially in Argentina and Thailand in our participating hospitals [9]. The main reason for this scenario is the fear of labour pain and the avoidance of negative experiences from vaginal birth [26]. In Thailand and Burkina Faso, women see companionship as an opportunity to reduce the amount of pain and to bridge communication gaps between them and health care providers [27, 28]. The possibility of having a companion may reduce the number of women who request a CS and may explain the low CS rates in Groups 2 and 4 in these hospitals.

Conclusions

In the 1-year preintervention period of the QUALI-DEC study, CS rates in Robson Groups 1 through 4 varied among participating countries and among hospitals within the same country. The between-hospital variation in CS rates could be partially explained by hospital-level characteristics such as equipment, workload, staffing level and the possibility of having a companion during childbirth. If some factors are well known, others need to be confirmed, and further research is needed. This study highlights the need to monitor CS by using the RTGCS in relation to information on the organization of obstetric care. Solutions that could emerge from analysing Robson data to reduce unnecessary caesareans should be adapted to the local context, depending on the organizational constraints and care identified as suboptimal. In addition, future studies comparing national guidelines and clinical practices across countries would provide valuable insights into how policy and training differences contribute to variations in CS rates.
