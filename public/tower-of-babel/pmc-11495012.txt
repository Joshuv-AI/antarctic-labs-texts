Temporary unilateral upper eyelid retraction: an unreported rare complication of rhinoplasty
Authors: Mehmet Fatih Kağan Değirmenci, Ayhan Işık Erdal, Serhat Şibar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUpper eyelid retraction may develop due to various neurogenic, myogenic, and mechanistic causes. Although the development of lagophthalmus after rhinoplasty has been described in the literature, upper eyelid retraction after uncomplicated rhinoplasty has not been reported before.Case presentationThis report presents a case of unilateral upper eyelid retraction observed six days after uncomplicated rhinoplasty. A 40-year-old female patient presented with left upper eyelid retraction on the sixth day of uncomplicated closed-approach structural rhinoplasty. The patient was able to close her eyelids completely and eye movements were normal in all directions of gaze. Anterior and posterior segment examinations were in normal range. We prescribed artificial tear drop and night-time liquid tear gel. After four weeks of conservative treatment, the upper eyelid retraction was improved without any complication. No recurrence was observed at 6-month follow-up.ConclusionThis is the first case presenting with upper eyelid retraction without lagophthalmus after uncomplicated rhinoplasty. Ophthalmologists should be aware that such a complication may occur quite rarely and may regress without sequelae with conservative treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-024-03734-w.Keywords: Eyelid retraction, Eyelids, Rhinoplasty, Postoperative complications

Background

Since rhinoplasty involves many surgical maneuvers on the nasal soft tissue envelope, cartilage, bone or septum, it is natural that it causes indirect effects such as edema and ecchymosis on the eyelids, which are the adjacent structures. These changes observed in the eyelids are almost always temporary. In addition to the most common edema and ecchymosis, rare complications such as lagophthalmos, emphysema and synkinesis have also been reported [1–7].

Eyelids are dynamic structures with complex anatomy. Therefore, their dysfunction causes both aesthetic and functional problems. These problems are also difficult to solve. The orbicularis oculi muscle is the primary protractor muscle of the eyelid. It is located under the upper and lower eyelid skin. It consists of three parts: pretarsal, preseptal, and orbital. The pretarsal and preseptal parts function in involuntary eyelid movements (blinking), whereas the orbital part is primarily involved in forced eyelid closure. The orbicularis oculi muscle is innervated by the branches of CN VII (the facial nerve). In recent cadaveric studies, new concepts of motor supply to medial periorbital area were defined [8, 9]. More recent one showed three types of motor sourcing for motor innervation of medial periorbital muscles by upper medial palpebral branch (UMPB) originating from the zygomatic/zygomaticobuccal branch of facial nerve [9]. Most commonly UMPB was formed by intraorbicularis motor components. In fewer number of cadavers intraorbicularis motor components and a branch from angular nerve both contributed to formation of UMPB. In a very small extent UMPB consisted only of the angular nerve branch. Dysfunction of the orbicularis oculi muscle may result in lagophthalmos (incomplete closure of the eyelids). Recent literature indicates that damage to the UMPB is commonly responsible for lagophthalmos following dacryocystorhinostomy or lateral osteotomy during rhinoplasty [4, 10]. The eyelid retractor muscles that open the eye are also crucial for balanced dynamic function. In the upper eyelid, retractors are the levator palpebrae superioris muscle and the superior tarsal muscle (Müller muscle). In the lower eyelid, the retractors are the capsulopalpebral fascia and the inferior tarsal muscle. The levator palpebrae superioris muscle is innervated by the CN III (the oculomotor nerve). Müller muscle is sympathetically innervated smooth muscle that provides 2–3 mm of upper eyelid elevation. Dysfunction of the upper eyelid retractor muscles may result in ptosis. Although eyelid ptosis is widely known and seen, its opposite condition, ‘upper eyelid retraction’, is much rarer.

The upper eyelid normally rests 1 to 2 mm below the corneal limbus. Upper eyelid retraction is defined by the abnormally high resting position of the upper eyelid. This abnormally high position produces an exposed sclera segment between the eyelid margin and the corneal limbus. Depending on its severity, it may also cause lagophthalmos. The most common causes are thyroid eye disease (Graves’ ophthalmopathy) and contralateral ptosis.Bartley divided the causes of upper eyelid retraction into three groups: neurogenic, myogenic, and mechanistic [7]. Various neurogenic, myogenic, and mechanistic causes have been described in the literature. However, there are no reports that it may develop after rhinoplasty. In this report, we present a case of unilateral upper eyelid retraction observed seven days after rhinoplasty.

Case report

A 40-year-old female patient underwent closed-approach structural rhinoplasty. She did not have any diagnosed disease. Surgery was started with bilateral marginal incisions. Lower lateral cartilages, upper lateral cartilages, dorsum and sidewalls were dissected in the subperichondrial and subperiosteal planes. The nasal hump was removed with scissors. Septoplasty was performed while preserving the L-strut. Transverse osteotomies were performed with a microsaw. Paramedial and lateral osteotomies were performed with a piezoelectric instrument. Bilateral spreader grafts were placed after osteotomies. The procedure was completed with tip maneuvers, further fine interventions, closure, and splint. After an uneventful postoperative follow-up, the patient was discharged the next day after surgery. At the visit on the sixth day after surgery, the abnormally high position of the left upper eyelid, and the asymmetry between the upper eyelid positions were noticed. In consultation with the ophthalmologist (MFKD), she was diagnosed with left upper eyelid retraction without lagophthalmos or corneal erosion. Best corrected visual acuity in decimals was 1,0 in both of the eyes. Eye movements in all direction of gazes were normal. Although the eyelid level on the left side is higher than on the other side, the patient was able to close both eyes completely. The presence of minimal lagophthamos was also specifically tested by asking the patient to close her eyes while downgaze and no space between the upper and lower eyelid margins was observed (Fig. 2). Direct and indirect light reflexes also did not show any abnormality. Anterior segment and dilated fundus examination in both eyes were in normal range. Lubricating artificial eye drop and gel were started considering the possibility of corneal involvement. Weekly follow-up examination revelaed regression of the eyelid retraction. At the 5th postoperative week visit, the left upper eyelid retraction showed complete resolution and the symmetry of the upper eyelids appeared to be restored (Fig. 3). She did not develop any additional pathology at the follow-up examinations including the six-month examination (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

Surgical trauma may cause dynamic changes in the surgical field and adjacent areas. It is conceivable that the activities of the protractor and retractor muscles of the eye may be affected by the effect of surgical trauma in rhinoplasty and dacryocystorhinostomy. Postoperative lagophthalmos cases due to dysfunction of the orbicularis oculi muscle, which is the protractor muscle, have been reported in the literature [2–4, 10]. In these former reports, the authors have emphasised that the UMPB, the main motor nerve of the upper eyelid, may have been damaged as an explanation for lagophthalmos. However, surgical trauma usually presents as a decrease in the activity of nerves and muscles in the affected area, and excessive activity is unlikely to occur. Since the retractors in the upper eyelid are the levator palpebrae superioris and Müller muscle, a possible mechanism of unilateral upper eyelid retraction could be an excessive contraction of one or both of these muscles. The authors think that the retraction in this case may be due to overstimulation of the Müller muscle, not the levator palpebrae superioris. Because the nerve of the levator palpebrae superioris, the oculomotor nerve, also innervates structures such as the pupil and extraocular muscles. So, its dysfunction can cause disorders in these innervated structures.

Although lagophthalmos was not observed, the left upper eyelid level was higher than the other eye in all gaze positions, which may indicate decreased activity in the orbicularis oculi of the left eye. From this point of view, another likely mechanism of unilateral upper eyelid retraction could be a partial UMPB neuropraxia causing decreased muscle tone of the pretarsal orbicularis oculi, leading to a relative increase in the action of its antagonist, levator palpebrae superioris muscle. This hypothesis may explain the higher left eyelid in all gaze positions, the absence of lagophthalmos on examination and eyelid retraction due to increased relative tone in the levator palpebrae superioris muscle. In brief, our patient may have presented with persistent unilateral upper eyelid retraction and very mild lagophthalmos that not visible on examination owing to neuropraxia of UMPB. Thus, neuropraxia of the UPMB may present as unilateral eyelid retraction rather than lagophthalmos in some cases.

Conclusions

Based on this case, ophthalmologists should be aware that upper eyelid retraction can develop after uncomplicated rhinoplasty and this situation is self-limiting and temporary. When faced with this situation, it may be useful to inform the patient that the situation is self-limiting to reduce the patient’s anxiety.
