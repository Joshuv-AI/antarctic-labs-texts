Climate change is leading to geographic expansion of tropical birds—range expansion and niche modeling in the White-browed Crake (Pololimnas cinereus)
Authors: Zhao Wanglin, Suo Lang, Wang Ting, Wang Yuan, Fan Liqing, Luo Tianxiang, Wang Xingxing, Yang Le, Zhang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe White-browed Crake (Pololimnas cinereus, family: Rallidae, hereafter WbC) is a climate sensitive bird with a tropical/subtropical distribution in Southeast Asia, Australasia, and the Philippines. Range expansion into higher latitudes would be predicted for this species in a warming climate. In this study, we first photographed a WbC in a park of Motuo County on the southeast Tibetan Plateau. Then we compiled geographic data from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) to illustrate its distribution characteristics. We also used a MaxEnt model to simulate its global suitable range under different future climate change scenarios.ResultsThe results showed: (1) this observation constitutes a new distributional record of the WbC on the Tibetan Plateau. This expanded northern boundary (29°19′25.40″N) increased the latitudinal limit of the species by 171.58 km. (2) The coldest monthly minimum temperature, the wettest seasonal precipitation, and the human footprint index were the main environmental factors affecting the distribution of WbC, the rise in the coldest monthly minimum temperature has facilitated the expansion of the WbC's habitat. (3) Future climate warming will lead to a significant increase of suitable areas for WbC, with its distribution center shifting 196.11 km and 153.80 km towards northwest in 2041–2060 and 2081–2100, respectively. Under the scenarios for the 2041–2060 and 2081–2100, the globally suitable distribution range of the WbC might expand by 1,125,400 km² and 1,275,200 km², respectively. In China, the corresponding expansion was 27,500 km² and 29,200 km², respectively, mainly distributed in Guangdong, Yunnan, Taiwan, Guangxi, Hainan, Xizang, and Fujian provinces.ConclusionsThe WbC photographed in Motuo County is a new distribution record of this species on the Tibetan Plateau, with Motuo County in Xizang being the northernmost boundary of the current WbC range. The wettest seasonal precipitation, and the human footprint index were the main environmental factors affecting the distribution of the WbC. Under future climate change scenarios, the WbC's range is expanding rapidly, and tends to dispersal in a northwesterly direction.

Introduction

According to the Sixth Assessment Report of the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the global surface mean temperature rose by 1.09 °C from 2011 to 2020 compared to the pre-industrial period (1850–1900), and is projected to reach 1.5 °C during 2021–2040 [1, 2]. The ongoing climate change has significantly impacted species distribution and migration [3, 4]. As one of the most sensitive biological groups in response to climate change, birds are important in monitoring the impacts of climate change [5]. Many studies have demonstrated that climate warming has led to significant range shifts in many bird species (e.g. Cettia cetti, Mergus merganser and Tragopan satyra), and the impacts of climate change on birds have received increasing attention [6–11]. In response to the pressure of global climate change, many bird species on North America are already migrating to polar regions and high altitudes, and 120 bird species distributed in China have experienced a northward or westward shift in their distribution ranges, with about 86.36% and 68.18% of the threatened birds on the Tibetan Plateau likely to dispersal to higher latitudes and altitudes, respectively [6, 12, 13]. Global warming is causing birds in Finnish nature reserves to shift northward [14]. Overall, climate warming has led to the expansion of bird distributions into higher latitudes [15]. Moreover, depending on the extent of habitat loss, each degree of surface warming could result in over 100 additional bird extinctions [8]. Accordingly, clarifying the variation of bird habitats may play a pivotal role in biodiversity conservation under scenarios of future climate changes [16].

In recent years, species distribution models (SDMs) have been widely used in the prediction of potential habitats of wild plants and animals [17, 18], in which the Maximum Entropy Modeling (MaxEnt) is the most widely used one at present [19, 20]. The MaxEnt model has a good simulation prediction ability and is well-suited for studying the potential distribution of many species. For instance, Zhang et al., (2019) utilized MaxEnt to evaluate and compare the habitat suitability of three raptors in the Sanjiangyuan National Park [21]. Li et al., (2023) used the MaxEnt to analyze the effects of global warming on the potential future habitat of the threatened birds on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau [6]. However more data is still needed to describe where and how birds are expanding.

The White-browed Crake (Pololimnas cinereus) belongs to the order Gruiformes (Family: Rallidae) and is distributed in humid, low-lying tropical and subtropical wetlands in South and Southeast Asia, Australasia, the Philippines, and the Solomon Islands [22], (https://avibase.bsc-eoc.org/). In China, it can only be seen in the warmer regions such as Guangxi, Hainan, Yunnan, Sichuan, Hong Kong and Taiwan [23–25]. In recent decades, the distribution range of the WbC has expanded from Southeast Asia to mainland China with a tendency to keep expanding northward [24, 26].

Under the joint influence of Indian monsoon from the Indian Ocean and the water vapor transport of the Yarlung Tsangpo River, the isotherms of the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon region have shifted significantly northward compared to the same latitude. The warm and humid climate of this region supports the most northerly tropical forest on the planet (29oN). Further, the strengthening of Indian monsoon during past decades [27], as well as the warming climate, enhances the possibility of the expansion of tropical birds into this area. However, to date evidence for range expansions of tropical species in Asia have been rare. Here, we present a new occurrence record of WbC in the eastern Himalaya, expanding the known distribution of this species. The WbC was first recorded in Motuo in 2022, followed by 23 and 2 additional sightings in Motuo during 2023 and 2024, respectively (https://www.birdreport.cn/home/habitat/page.html?taxonid=). Therefore, it is inferred that the WbC is not a vagrant in Motuo. We then compiled geographic data to illustrate its distribution characteristics, and employed MaxEnt model to simulate its potential suitable distribution area under different climate change scenarios. We aimed to answer: (1) What are the main factors that affecting the distribution of the WbC? (2) Will the range of the WbC continue to expand northward under future climate change scenarios? The results can be used for conservation planning and management of the WbC, safeguarding it from future climate change threats.

Methods

Occurrence records

The global distribution data of WbC are mainly derived from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBFI), published literature [22, 24, 25], and the observation record in Motuo (Fig. 1). Among these data sources which all provided precise longitude and latitude coordinates, 12,922 data entries were derived from GBIF (December 21, 2022), and 31 coordinates were from literature and observational records. After removing coordinates with obvious errors (located in the ocean), a total of 12,923 records were collected (Fig. 2). In the GBIF database, Poliolimnas cinereus, Amaurornis cinerea and Porzana cinerea are all referred to the same species, while Amaurornis cinerea is used uniformly, with an English name of WbC. The Chinese name is changed from ‘白眉田鸡’ to ‘白眉苦恶鸟’ since 2017 [28]. There is some controversy about the scientific name and genus position of the WbC. In the first and third editions of A Checklist on the Classification and Distribution of the Birds of China published in 2005 and 2017, the scientific name of the WbC was Porzana cinerea and Amaurornis cinerea, respectively, and the third edition classified it from the genus Zapornia to the genus Amaurornis. In 2020 some ornithologists were in favor of Poliolimnas cinereus [29]. The Handbook of the Birds of the World continued to use Porzana cinerea for a long time. “All the Birds of the World” uses Amaurornis cinerea but the updated Birds of the World which uses the latest Clements checklist uses Poliolimnas cinereus [30]. Therefore, this paper will use Pololimnas cinereus as the scientific name for the WbC.Fig. 1A White-browed Crake in Motuo (First shot is December 15, 2022)Fig. 2Global range and distributional records of White-browed Crake

To address potential overfitting in the simulation arising from spatial autocorrelation among distribution points, we used the ENMTools package in the R 4.2.3 software to remove redundant data within the same raster cell based on the spatial resolution (2.5′) of the environmental factors employed. This process ensured that each cell in the study area possessed a unique distribution point [31]. Consequently, 1,391 valid distribution points were eventually retained for modeling.

Environmental information

Little is known about the specific environmental variables affecting the distribution of the WbC. 25 variables broadly used to predict species distributions were chosen for the MaxEnt model. Subsequently, using the MaxEnt model, we identified the most significant variables contributing to the distribution of the WbC, thereby refining the model and interpreting results based on key ecological drivers.

In this study, four types of environmental variables were selected. (1) The bioclimatic variables contain 19 bioclimatic factors, which are derived from the WorldClim Database with a spatial resolution of 2.5’ (WorldClim, Version 2.1). The current bioclimatic variable data were the average of 1970–2000, and mean values of bioclimatic variables under four shared socio-economic pathways (SSPs: 126, 245, 370 and 585) climate scenarios from The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 6th (CMIP6) were selected for different future periods (2041–2060, 2081–2100) [1]). These combine Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) with Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), where higher numbers indicate greater climate challenges and mitigation difficulties. The SSP scenarios represent alternative climate change pathways: SSP126 (low emissions, ≤ 2.6 W/m² radiative forcing by 2100), SSP245 (intermediate, 4.5 W/m²), SSP370 (high, 7.0 W/m²), and SSP585 (very high, 8.5 W/m²). (2) Terrain variables include elevation, aspect, and slope. The elevation data were obtained from the WorldClim database, and the slope and aspect data were generated according to DEM (Digital Elevation Model). (3) Habitat variables were obtained from Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) data from the Global GIMMS NDVI3g v1 dataset (1981–2015). (4) Anthropogenic variables mainly include human footprint index (HFI) and land use type. The human footprint index integrates population, road distribution, etc., and is able to reflect the disturbance status of WbC and their habitats by anthropogenic activities, with data from An annual global terrestrial Human Footprint dataset during 2000–2018 [32]. Human Footprint Index has an important effect on the distribution of WbC. Even though it is not possible to predict the future HFI, the inclusion of the current HFI variable in the prediction of the future distribution of WbC was able to reduce the contribution of the climatic variables, which improved the accuracy of the model’s predictions to a certain extent. Land-use type data were extracted from Global land-use and land-cover change (LUCC) simulation product at a 1 km resolution under the SSP-RCP scenarios [33].

During the initial model construction, to mitigate the overfitting issue caused by multicollinearity among all environmental factors, we employed the Jackknife test in the MaxEnt software to determine the contribution rates of environmental variables to the model’s predictive outcomes. The environmental variables with large contribution were screened using Spearman correlation analysis to improve the simulation accuracy of the model, while those with minor contribution were eliminated [34]. First, 25 environmental variables were imported into MaxEnt, and the model was repeated 10 times. Second, the environmental factor information was extracted to sample points using the Extract Multi Values to Points module in ArcGIS 10.7, and then the initial environmental variables were subjected to the paired Spearman correlation test.

Species distribution model

The MaxEnt model is primarily constructed using latitude and longitude data of species distribution points, along with environmental factors that influence species survival. It quantifies habitat suitability in a probabilistic form, outperforming other models even in the scenarios with a small sample size of species points and unclear correlations among various climatic and environmental factors [35–37]. In this study, MaxEnt was used to assess the potential geographic distribution of the WbC. A dataset consisting of 1,391 distribution points and 8 environmental factors of the WbC was imported into MaxEnt, with 75% of the points randomly selected as the training set, and the remaining 25% used as the test set. To mitigate the randomness of the model and the uncertainty of the simulation results, the model was repeated 10 times, and the other parameters were adopted as the default settings [38]. The area of the graph enclosed by the ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve and the horizontal coordinate is called AUC (Area Under Curve), with a value range of [0,1]. Higher values indicate higher accuracy of the model prediction. When the value of AUC is greater than 0.8, it indicates excellent model results [39]. MaxEnt used a jackknife method to calculate the contribution of each environmental factor to the distribution of species, aiding in identifying the main influencing factors [40]. Ultimately, eight variables with AUC values exceeding 0.7, contribution rates above 1%, and absolute correlation coefficients greater than 0.8 were selected for model calculations.

According to the Maximum test sensitivity plus specificity Cloglog threshold division method [41], a distribution probability (P) threshold of 0.3 was obtained for WbC. Therefore, the suitable distribution area of the WbC was divided into four classes: P < 0.3 for the unsuitable area, 0.3 ≤ P < 0.5 for the low suitable area, 0.5 ≤ P < 0.7 for the medium suitable area, and P ≥ 0.7 for the high suitable area.

Migration directions and paths of the WbC suitable range were realized using the ArcGIS plugin SDMtoolbox [42]. The Centroid change function in SDMtoolbox was used to simulate the migration direction and path of suitable areas under different climate change scenarios in the future compared with those under the current climate scenario.

Results

New distribution record of White-browed Crake on the Tibetan Plateau

On December 15, 2022, a bird belonging to Rallidae was observed and recorded in Motuo County (95°19′56.83″N, 29°19′25.40″E, 1108 m), Xizang Autonomous Region. The bird was identified as the WbC (Pololimnas cinereus) after reviewing relevant literature (Fig. 1). And after checking the pertinent literature, China Bird Report (http://www.birdreport.cn/), and the eBird (https://ebird.org/), it was found that there was no prior record of the WbC distribution in Xizang Autonomous Region, making a new observation. According to the distribution map (Fig. 2), the WbC was mainly distributed between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, and the northern latitude did not exceed 23°26′N. Therefore, our observation recorded the northernmost latitude (29°19′25.40″N) of the WbC distribution. This record expanded the known northern boundary by a vertical distance of 171.58 km. According to the data of the nearest meteorological observation (2 km away from the record site) during 2011–2022, the minimum air temperature in the coldest month was 0.8–4.7 °C, which is assumed to be the thermal factor shaping the geographic distribution of the WbC. The geographical distribution characteristics of White-browed Crake

The distribution of the WbC is predominantly tropical with a northern limit of 23°26′N (Fig. 4). The eight variables in Fig. 4 reflect the habitat characteristics of the WbC from four aspects: climate, topography, vegetation cover, and human activity. We, therefore analyzed the primary environmental characteristics essential for the survival of the WbC based on data from its distribution points, including temperature, moisture, elevation, slope, slope, land cover type, vegetation index, and human footprint index. Regarding the main environmental factors impacting the distribution of WbC, it was found that the minimum temperature of coldest month was generally greater than 15℃ (Fig. 4a), and the precipitation of wettest quarter fell between 500 and 1500 mm (Fig. 4b). For the topography, the WbC was distributed at elevations generally less than 200 m (more than 80% of the selected sites, (Fig. 4c), at slopes less than 1 degree (Fig. 4d), and no significant difference in aspect (Fig. 4e). From the perspective of vegetation and land use types, the WbC preferred relative dense vegetation cover, dominated by cropland and forest vegetation (Fig. 4f), with the vegetation index generally higher than 0.4 (Fig. 4g). Compared with the suitable areas under the current climate, those under all future forcing scenarios showed an increasing trend, with percentage increases ranging from 24.01% to 40.69%. Changes in distribution center under different scenarios

Currently, the distribution center of the WbC in the world is located in the South China Sea, with coordinates at 5.52°N, 111.07°E. In the 2041–2060, the distribution center is forecasted to move towards the northwest, with an average distance of 196.11 km. In the 2081–2100, except for the SSP370 in which the center of distribution only shifted 19.85 km to the southwest, the distribution centers will move to the northwest continuously, with an average distance of 153.80 km (Fig. 8). Discussion

Effects of environmental variables on species distribution

The distribution patterns suggest the WbC predominantly inhabits warm climatic zones characterized by intermediate to moderately low precipitation levels, and generally grows in tropical and subtropical areas where human activities are more frequent, such as man-made bodies of water, rice paddies, swamps, wetlands, etc [43]. Our results indicated that the coldest monthly minimum temperature (51.20%), wettest quarter precipitation (17.70%), and human footprint index (12.90%) were the main environmental factors affecting the distribution of the WbC. These are closely related with the feeding habits of waterbird communities who are highly dependent on natural and man-made wetlands where food resources are rich. Concerning the WbC, the diet of this bird is mainly based on aquatic insects. First, the higher minimum temperature of the coldest month in winter ensures that aquatic insects do not freeze to death, providing a food source for WbC during the overwintering period. Second, although precipitation in the wettest season is a strong predictor, based on the species’ range and the response curve in Fig. 5b, the species does not prefer areas with large amounts of precipitation. The diet of the WbC is predominantly aquatic insects. are not sufficient during the wintering period [44].

Effects of climate change on the expansion of the distribution area of White-browed Crake

Climate change has caused the WbC’s range to shift to the northwest, i.e., expanding to higher latitudes in the northern hemisphere. Previous studies have revealed that climate warming is a major impact factor in changing the range of bird distribution [45]. Walther et al. (2002) suggested that climate warming altered climate thresholds towards polar regions or high latitudes, prompting birds and other species with limited dispersal and resource-use capabilities moving along climate thresholds [46]. Current research and observations proposed that climate change has already disrupted species distribution patterns, leading to species migrating to higher latitudes or altitudes in the Northern Hemisphere [6, 7, 9]. In our study, model analysis indicated that the migration rate of the WbC towards the northwest is 3.92 km/year over the next 50 years and 3.08 km/year over the next 100 years. This prediction is close with Hitch and Leberg (2007), who examined the changes in the distribution of 56 bird species and found that birds in the southern part of the country moved northward by 2.35 km per year. Whereas, a much faster rate was reported by Thomas and Lennon (1999), who found that bird species in the northern region of the UK had shifted northward by an average rate of 18.9 km per year [11, 47].

The northward expansion of the WbC’s range may be related to the adaptation strategy of birds migrating to higher latitudes prompted by climate warming [48, 49]. Range shifts of species are a general dynamic process in the evolutionary course of populations, and factors such as global warming, environmental and habitat changes, and the effects of anthropogenic activities have led to changes in the ranges of numerous bird species. Considering distribution ranges, birds in the Rallidae family have generally shown expansion patterns throughout their evolutionary process. In recent decades, the distribution range of the WbC has expanded from Southeast Asia to mainland China with a tendency to keep expanding northward [24, 26]. Valiela and Bowen (2003) linked the northward shift in the distribution of bird wintering grounds on Cape Cod, North America, to an increase in local winter minimum temperatures [50]. The minimum temperature of the coldest month plays a crucial role in shaping the distribution of the WbC. With global climate warming, particularly the continuous increase in the minimum temperature of the coldest month annually, the distribution range of the WbC has consequently expanded.

Ecological reasons for the Northern expansion of the White-browed Crake’s distribution range

The trend of birds moving northward and to higher elevations is likely an adaptation to mitigate climate pressures [6]. Winter warming and human-provided food sources have jointly enhanced the survival capabilities of these birds in high-latitude regions, driving their northward expansion [51]. The habitats of tropical species are closely linked to their heat tolerance. Consequently, lowland species may benefit from greater elevation gradients, enabling them to further occupy their potential thermal niches as temperatures rise by expanding uphill [52].

Uncertainty and perspectives

In this study, we only considered the effects of bioclimatic variables on the distribution of WbC. In fact, its distribution is also affected by various biotic (e.g., interspecific competition, predation, and disease) and abiotic factors (e.g., water level fluctuations, etc.) [53]. All of these factors may lead to differences between predicted and actual results. A more comprehensive ecological niche modeling approach is needed to elucidate the effects of all possible factors [54]. There are limitations to this approach, as future human footprint index data is not available, but in order to provide accuracy in the model predictions, we have included the current human footprint index in the future predictions.

The WbC is only one out of the 20 Rallidae species in China, and the knowledge of whether all the Rallidae species tend to expand under scenarios of climate change remains unclear. Comparisons among different bird species within a same family may help illustrate adaptation mechanisms of Rallidae species under future climate change. Besides, the distribution range of the WbC reflected a significant northwestward expansion in the Northern Hemisphere rather than in the Southern Hemisphere, which may be related to the small portion of terrestrial ecosystem in the Southern Hemisphere, as well as the slower response of bird range expansion to the maritime climate [55].

The Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon serves as an ecological corridor for bird migration, and the distribution area of the WbC may continue to expand northward along this corridor in the context of future climate warming. The current scarcity of WbCs on the Tibetan Plateau, the lack of clear information on population dynamics, and the overlap of their distribution areas with those of human activities make them vulnerable to disturbance by human activities. Therefore, it is recommended that population surveys, monitoring, assessment and protection of WbCs should be further strengthened.

The WbC exhibits distinct habitat preferences. It particularly favors diverse wetland types in tropical and subtropical regions, including marshes, lakes, and rice paddies. These environments typically provide abundant food resources (aquatic animals, insects, plant seeds, etc.), excellent concealment from predators, and suitable nesting sites. Naturally timid and alert, the species relies heavily on water bodies and dense vegetation. The WbC is a rare bird species within China, characterized by relatively low natural population numbers and fragmented distribution. This makes its population more vulnerable to external disturbances. Habitat loss and fragmentation, coupled with human disturbances (such as tourism development and noise pollution), severely impact its breeding, foraging, and resting activities. Only through sustained field surveys and ecological research can we gain a more accurate understanding of their population dynamics, distribution range, and behavioral patterns, providing scientific basis for developing effective conservation strategies. Additionally, strengthening habitat protection, establishing ecological corridors to connect fragmented habitats, and reducing human disturbance are all effective conservation measures.

Conclusion

In this paper, the WbC photographed in Motuo County is a new distribution record of this species on the Tibetan Plateau, with Motuo County in Xizang being the northernmost boundary of the current WbC range. The MaxEnt model simulated the global distribution of the WbC in the tropical subtropical region between the north and south Tropic of Cancer, with a suitable distribution area of 300.87 × 104 km2. The model results showed that the coldest monthly minimum temperature, the wettest seasonal precipitation, and the human footprint index were the main environmental factors affecting the distribution of the WbC, contributing 51.20%, 17.70%, and 12.90% to the model, respectively. Under future climate change scenarios, the WbC’s range is expanding rapidly, and tends to dispersal in a northwesterly direction. The results can be used for conservation planning and management of the WbC, safeguarding it from future climate change threats.
