Superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with intracranial hemorrhage in a patient on infliximab: a case report
Authors: Mohammad Rizwan Alam, Juan Corona-Ruiz, Joseph Cahill, William Goldsmith, Michael Sabra, Maro Issac, Peter Fahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuperior sagittal sinus thrombosis (SSST) is a rare but life-threatening form of cerebral venous thrombosis. Although several prothrombotic and autoimmune conditions are recognized risk factors, SSST occurring in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) receiving tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitor therapy remains exceptionally rare and poorly described in the literature.Case presentationWe present a 59-year-old Caucasian female with RA treated with infliximab who developed extensive superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with concurrent intracranial and subarachnoid hemorrhage. The patient was managed with anticoagulation, blood pressure control, and seizure prophylaxis, with subsequent clinical and radiologic improvement at one-month follow-up. Hypercoagulability evaluation was overall unrevealing, and no other clear provoking etiology was identified.ConclusionsGiven the rarity of this presentation, we report this case to raise awareness of a possible association between cerebral venous thrombosis and TNF-α inhibitor therapy in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. This is especially important given the increasing and widespread use of biologic agents in the treatment of autoimmune diseases.

Background

SSST is an uncommon form of cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT), accounting for approximately 0.5–1% of all strokes [1]. It can result in elevated intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus, venous infarction, and hemorrhagic conversion. RA is known to be associated with an increased risk of thrombosis [2], and thrombotic events have also been reported in patients receiving TNF-α inhibitors, such as infliximab [3]. The mechanisms by which infliximab may contribute to thrombotic risk remain unclear but could involve endothelial dysfunction, increased platelet aggregation, or alterations in the coagulation cascade [4, 9]. This case report describes a patient with SSST and ICH, with discussion of the possible contribution of infliximab therapy to thrombotic risk.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old Caucasian female with a history of hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis treated with infliximab 3 mg/kg every 8 weeks since approximately May 2022 without recent dose escalation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic opioid therapy presented with left-sided weakness. The patient denied hormone replacement therapy or oral contraceptive use.

Her husband reported that he heard erratic sounds while sleeping, after which she attempted to use the bathroom but fell, striking her head. He observed convulsive activity in her left arm and leg, prompting an emergency medical response.

Upon arrival, her vital signs were notable for hypertension with a blood pressure of 181/85 mmHg, with a normal heart rate of 82 bpm, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. She was afebrile at 98.0°F. She was noted to have a left parietal scalp hematoma. Neurological examination revealed that she was alert but slightly confused, oriented to person and place, but with difficulty recalling recent events. Cranial nerve examination demonstrated equal, round, and reactive pupils without papilledema, intact extraocular movements, and no facial asymmetry. Motor examination revealed left-sided weakness with 3/5 strength in both the upper and lower extremities, with preserved strength (5/5) on the right. A pronator drift was present on the left. Sensory examination showed decreased light touch and pinprick sensation on the left. Reflexes were brisk on the left (3 +), with an upgoing plantar response (Babinski sign) on the left. Coordination testing revealed dysmetria on the left with finger-nose testing, and she was unable to ambulate due to weakness. These findings correlated with an NIHSS score of 8, primarily driven by motor and sensory deficits. Other physical exam findings were unrevealing.

Laboratory workup (Tables 1 and 2) was unremarkable. The patient underwent a CT brain without contrast (Fig. 1) and an MRI brain without contrast (Fig. 2), showing a left Sylvian fissure SAH, a right posterior frontal intraparenchymal hematoma (1.4 × 2.1 cm), and a left parietal scalp hematoma. CT angiography (Fig. 3) was concerning for superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, but no other evidence of vascular malformations or aneurysms was noted. The patient was initially started on a nicardipine drip for blood pressure control and levetiracetam 1000 mg IV twice daily for seizure management and was admitted to the Neuro ICU. The patient was evaluated by neurology and neurosurgery.

The neurosurgical team noted mild to moderate brain compression without significant midline shift. Serial CT scans demonstrated stable hemorrhages, allowing initiation of anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin (Lovenox), later transitioned to apixaban 5 mg PO twice daily. Hematology was consulted for hypercoagulability studies, which were planned as an outpatient visit.

The patient's blood pressure was successfully managed with oral antihypertensives, and the nicardipine drip was weaned off. On hospital day 4, she was downgraded from the ICU, and by hospital day 6, she was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation.

At one-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant neurological improvement, though mild residual left-sided weakness and paresthesia persisted. She had a steady gait with preserved cognition and speech, and cranial nerve examination remained intact. Right upper and lower extremity strength was fully preserved, and left lower extremity strength had returned to baseline. Residual left upper extremity weakness persisted, with wrist extension 4/5, grip strength 4/5, intrinsic hand muscles 3/5, and finger extension 3/5. Sensory examination revealed intact light touch despite persistent mild residual left-sided paresthesia, with normal coordination testing. Repeat CT brain imaging demonstrated interval resolution of the previously identified right posterior frontal intraparenchymal hemorrhage and left Sylvian fissure subarachnoid hemorrhage, without evidence of new hemorrhage or worsening mass effect. She remained on anticoagulation with apixaban. Patient perspective

At follow-up, the patient reported gradual improvement in strength and functional recovery, although mild residual symptoms persisted.

Discussion

SSST is a serious and potentially life-threatening form of CVT [1]. In this patient, superior sagittal sinus thrombosis likely resulted in impaired venous drainage, venous hypertension, and hemorrhagic venous infarction, which may explain the right posterior frontal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Although the patient sustained a fall with an associated left parietal scalp hematoma, the frontal hemorrhage was anatomically remote from the site of external injury, making isolated traumatic hemorrhage less likely, although a traumatic contribution cannot be completely excluded. In contrast, the left Sylvian fissure subarachnoid hemorrhage was considered more consistent with traumatic injury following the neurological event.

Her underlying rheumatoid arthritis and anti-TNF-α therapy may both have contributed to her thrombotic risk, especially in the absence of other hypercoagulable findings.

This patient's focal neurological deficits, including left-sided weakness, hyperreflexia, and Babinski sign, are consistent with parasagittal cortical involvement due to SSST. The convulsive activity observed prior to the fall likely indicates cortical irritation secondary to venous congestion.

Despite the known association between rheumatoid arthritis and thrombosis, the patient did not demonstrate evidence of markedly active systemic inflammatory disease around the time of presentation. ESR and CRP on admission were 12 mm/hr and 1.5 mg/L, respectively. One month after discharge, ESR and CRP were 21 mm/hr and 2.5 mg/L, respectively. These findings suggest relatively controlled rheumatoid arthritis activity during the clinical course.

TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab, while reducing systemic inflammation, have paradoxically been implicated in thrombotic complications, including venous and arterial thromboses. Proposed mechanisms include endothelial dysfunction, immune-mediated coagulation abnormalities, alterations in cytokine balance, and, in some reported cases, induction of antiphospholipid antibodies [3, 10–12]. In the present case, antiphospholipid antibody testing was negative.

In a 2021 publication, Tatsuoka et al. described a case of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in a patient with Crohn’s disease receiving long-term high-dose infliximab therapy. Their literature review identified nine additional thrombotic events temporally associated with infliximab use, including three arterial and six venous thromboses [13]. These reported cases involved heterogeneous patient populations and underlying inflammatory diseases, most commonly Crohn’s disease rather than rheumatoid arthritis. Although rheumatoid arthritis itself is associated with increased thrombotic risk due to chronic systemic inflammation, our patient’s disease appeared relatively controlled, with low serial inflammatory markers. In addition, there was no evidence of malignancy, and both COVID-19 infection and vaccination were temporally remote from the thrombotic event. Although causality cannot be definitively established, these findings support the possibility that infliximab therapy may have contributed to the patient’s thrombotic presentation.

Given the unrevealing hypercoagulability evaluation and absence of other clear provoking factors, the patient’s underlying RA and TNF-α inhibitor exposure were considered possible contributing factors to her thrombotic presentation.

This case report has several limitations. As a single-patient observation, it cannot establish a causal relationship between infliximab therapy and superior sagittal sinus thrombosis. In addition, formal rheumatoid arthritis disease activity scores (e.g., DAS28) and complete longitudinal rheumatologic treatment records were unavailable, limiting definitive assessment of the relative contribution of underlying inflammatory disease versus infliximab therapy to thrombotic risk. Furthermore, Protein C, Protein S, and antithrombin III testing were performed one month following the acute thrombotic event while the patient remained on anticoagulation therapy and were not subsequently repeated, as additional testing was not recommended by hematology given the overall unrevealing evaluation and the plan for long-term anticoagulation.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of recognizing SSST in patients with RA, particularly those receiving TNF-α inhibitors. Although uncommon, CNS thrombosis may represent a potential complication of biologic therapy. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when encountering neurological symptoms in such patients. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanistic link between TNF-α inhibition and thrombosis, and to develop strategies for risk stratification and monitoring in vulnerable populations.
