Feasibility study of an integrated earthquake and tsunami early warning system
Authors: Raffaele Rea, Antonio Scala, Fabrizio Bernardi, Luca Elia, Stefano Lorito, Simona Colombelli, Gaetano Festa, Fabrizio Romano, Alessandro Amato, Aldo Zollo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent Tsunami Early Warning Systems (TEWS) aim to issue an initial alert within minutes after a seismic event, relying on rapid estimates of earthquake source parameters derived from teleseismic and regional seismic data. For coastal areas located in the near-distance range of a tsunamigenic source, reducing this alert time is critical to increase evacuation lead-time and enhance risk mitigation actions. In this study, we evaluate the integration of earthquake magnitude and location estimations from QuakeUp, an impact-based earthquake early warning system (EEWS), into a tsunami early warning system (TEWS) workflow. We investigate the trade-off between earliness and accuracy of the seismic source parameters and their impact on Probabilistic Tsunami Forecasting (PTF). A hindcast of the 30 October 2020 Mw7.0 Aegean Sea earthquake, whose tsunami reached nearby coasts within ~ 10 min demonstrated that QuakeUp can deliver stable and accurate hypocenter and magnitude estimates within ~ 40 s from origin time, comparable to those obtained by the Early-Est software within minutes, used operationally in the Mediterranean area. These rapid parameter estimates were used to run a PTF procedure, yielding runup predictions in reasonable agreement with observations. A second scenario, based on 150 simulated seismogram sets for Mw 6–7 earthquakes in the Messina Strait (Southern Italy) confirmed the ability of the integrated workflow to provide robust source estimates within one minute. This work represents the first demonstration of a combined EEWS-TEWS approach for near-field tsunamigenic earthquakes, highlighting its potential for dual risk mitigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25832-5.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Tsunamis consist of a series of long-wavelength sea gravity waves that may hit the coasts causing loss of lives and damages to buildings and other infrastructures. These events can be generated by different processes (earthquakes, submarine landslides, volcanoes, or peculiar meteorological conditions). In the last two decades, several earthquakes generated destructive tsunamis that caused hundreds of thousands fatalities1–3. The damages produced by this kind of events have been strongly mitigated by tsunami early warning systems (TEWS). These systems provide as fast and accurate as possible alerts to people on the coasts of the potentially impending waves. However, for coastlines very close to the epicentre, messages from the TEWS may be too late and the tsunami risk reduction must be based on people’s reaction to natural signs4.

In the framework of the North-East Atlantic, Mediterranean and connected seas Tsunami Warning and Mitigation System (NEAMTWS) coordinated by IOC-UNESCO, five tsunami service providers (TSP) provide tsunami alert services: the French Tsunami Warning Center (CENALT)5, the Italian Tsunami Alert Center (CAT-INGV)6, the National Observatory of Athens (NOA)7, the Instituto Português do Mar e da Atmosfera (IPMA)8 and the Kandilli Observatory and Earthquake Research Institute (KOERI)9. Each of them uses a different seismic network tuned for their different monitoring zones, different seismic velocity models and software for the characterisation of the seismic parameters and (slightly, in some cases) different decision matrices5–10. Among these TSPs, our analysis focuses on the Italian CAT-INGV. CAT-INGV issues tsunami alert messages based on earthquake parameter estimations provided by the Early-Est algorithm11. Early-Est uses regional and teleseismic data to estimate earthquakes magnitude and hypocenter coordinates, which in the Mediterranean basin require three/four minutes from the origin time (OT) of the earthquake. These estimations feed a very conservative decision matrix6 used to convert them into a tsunami warning. A new probabilistic tsunami forecasting algorithm (PTF) is expected to become operational in the CAT-INGV in 202512–14. For the chosen tsunami intensity metric (e.g. offshore amplitude), the PTF provides the probability distribution expected at predetermined forecast points along the coast. The probability is then converted into alert levels based on the user-defined precautionary level. The PTF is based on ensembles of precalculated tsunami scenarios weighted according to their probability of representing the real ongoing event, according to the preliminary estimates of the earthquake location, depth and magnitude. In this way, the initial uncertainty regarding the earthquake parameters (magnitude, hypocentre, strike, dip, rake, slip distribution) is propagated onto the tsunami forecast. The PTF workflow can be launched as soon as the estimations of magnitude and location of an ongoing earthquake are available, and it takes less than 30s to run.

When the earthquake hypocenter is located close enough to the coastline, tsunami waves may reach populated coastlines within a few minutes. This scenario typically occurs in the Mediterranean Sea15–17. The rapid characterization of the earthquake source is therefore crucial. Moreover, the cities located on the coasts are exposed to both the earthquake ground shaking and inundation risks.

An Earthquake Early Warning System (EEWS) could help to mitigate the strong shaking wave impact18. These automatic systems aim to rapidly detect a potentially damaging earthquake and to issue an alert to sites at risk, prior to the arrival of the strongest shaking waves, allowing for the activation of safety emergency measures19–23.

Several strategies have been recently proposed to obtain fast seismic source parameters estimation for TEWS applications24, and some are already operational, such as the system used by Japan Meteorological Agency25,26. However, these approaches are designed to use in near-real-time different kinds of data27 (seismic data, GNSS, GPS, etc.) and to handle events occurring in subduction zones, where the earthquake-generating fault may lie hundreds of kilometers offshore, allowing them to have minutes to characterize the earthquake source24. Focusing specifically on events occurring very close to the coast, in this work we explore a strategy to accelerate source parameter estimations in TEWS, proposing an integrated novel approach combining EEWS and TEWS. The two systems rely on different monitoring networks (local seismic networks for EEWS, regional/global for TEWS) and operate on different timescales, from seconds, using real-time information from the P-waves, to minutes or hours. In our framework the QuakeUp EEWS28 is used to trigger the tsunami warning workflow for potentially tsunamigenic events. This integration aims to deliver rapid alerts of strong ground shaking while extending the lead time for tsunami warnings, thereby allowing more time for protective actions such as evacuation. However, the effectiveness of such an approach must be carefully evaluated: EEWS perform best for inland events with dense seismic network coverage29,30, whereas offshore earthquakes are often poorly constrained by proximal sensors. Moreover, reducing warning time may compromise the accuracy of both earthquake source estimates and subsequent tsunami forecasts. The challenge is therefore to identify an optimal balance between earliness and accuracy in order to maximize the reliability and usefulness of near-field tsunami warnings6. We examined two different use-cases (Fig. 1). The first is the 30 October 2020 7.0, Samos (Aegean Sea) earthquake, which generated a moderate tsunami, with overall maximum observed runup exceeding 3.5 m at some locations16,17,31. For this event, we used waveforms from 97 accelerometric stations by both the Greek and Turkish seismic networks, including the ITSAK strong motion Network32, the National Observatory of Athens33, and Kandilli Observatory in Türkiye34. The second case involves 150 synthetic earthquake scenarios with magnitudes between 6 and 7 (with a 0.25 interval) of similar location simulating the 7.0, 1908, Messina Strait earthquake. This historical event caused estimated 80,000 fatalities35 from combined shaking and inundation. In our simulations, we excluded additional landslide tsunami source, which some studies identify as the main cause of the extended inundation in Sicily and Calabria in 1908 (e.g., Schambach et al.36). Synthetic ruptures were generated using the k-square model37,38 to emulate realistic heterogeneous slip distributions for magnitudes between 6 and 7, with fault plane length and width derived from the the Wells and Coppersmith empirical scaling relations39 (see Data and methods section for further details). Using the software AXITRA40, we simulated the velocity and acceleration earthquake records at all the locations of the RAN (Rete Accelerometrica Nazionale41 stations that are currently operating in real-time in the area.

Results

The results for the Mw 7.0, 2020 Samos (Aegean Sea) earthquake are presented both in terms of quickness and accuracy of the real-time source parameters estimations (epicentral coordinates, depth and magnitude), and in terms of alerting performance (timeliness and accuracy). The time-evolution of the errors on epicentral position and depth is shown in Fig. 2a; errors are computed as the distance in kilometers between the location estimated by EEWS QuakeUp and the value reported by the IRIS Bulletin42, that are here assumed as reference values. The system provides a stable location 42 s after the OT of the event (e.g., 32 s from the first P-wave detection at the network). The value of the final error on the epicenter is 4 km, while the error on depth is 0.3 km. Usually EEWS require a few seconds to have a stable magnitude and location estimations29,44. In this case, the unusually long time to obtain stable location estimation can be due to different factors linked to the network geometry, such as its sparsity (the average inter-station distance is 25 km) and the presence of only two stations within 20 km from the epicenter. The timeline plot in Fig. 2a continues in Fig. 2b, where we show the errors in epicentral position and depth provided by the algorithm Early-Est. Also in this case, we consider the values reported by the IRIS bulletin as a reference for error estimate. The time at which the source parameter estimations are provided by Early-Est is 180 s after the OT of the event and their error values are 9.7 km for the epicenter and 8 km for the depth. As for the magnitude, the real-time estimate initially increases with time and then it reaches a stable value after about 30 s from the OT. The magnitude estimate is obtained from the log-displacement amplitude measured in progressively expanded time windows and it therefore presents the typical pattern, with a sharp increase with time till a plateau is reached that indicates the final magnitude value (Fig. 2c). This behavior was already observed for other earthquakes and it is closely related to the time evolution of the earthquake moment rate function45,46. Specifically, the first magnitude value underestimates the IRIS reference value (Mw about 6.5), while the final plateau level is , consistent with the estimate provided by the IRIS bulletin40. The time needed to reach a stable magnitude value is also consistent with observations for similar size earthquakes46 and is associated with the duration of the moment rate function till its peak value47. The magnitude estimation provided by Early-Est is 7.0, which is the same reported in the reference bulletin (Fig. 2d), while it is obtained 3 min after the OT (red dot in the figure). The performance of the EEWS on the 2020 Aegean Sea event is evaluated also in terms of accuracy and rapidity of the alert release. To assess these parameters, following the approach by Festa et al.48 and Rea et al.29 we compared the predicted and measured intensities at each station and defined Successful Alert (SA), Successful No Alert (SNA), Missed Alert (MA), False Alert (FA), as described in the method section. By comparing the time at which the predicted intensity exceeds the selected threshold for the alert IMM=IV (corresponding to PGV=0.21 cm/s according to the formula proposed by Faenza and Michelini49) and the time at which the measured intensity exceeds the same threshold we can calculate the lead time provided by the system at each station in the area. In Fig. 3a we show the evolution of the percentage of each class in time, while in Fig. 3b, we show the available lead-times at the stations classified as SA.

In panel (b) the green dots represent the time at which the predicted intensity at each station exceeds the threshold and the red dots represent the time at which the measured intensity exceeds the same threshold at the same station. In Fig. 3a, we can see that from the beginning of the real-time simulation, the system provided the 92.7% of Successful Alerts and the 6.3% of the stations classified as Missed Alerts. This suggests that the ground motion prediction equation (GMPE), used by QuakeUp for stations not yet reached by the P-wave (see Methods), can predict the overcoming of the threshold from the first magnitude estimation. These predicted peak ground velocity (PGV) values change during the simulation. At 40 s from the OT of the event, the estimated magnitude reaches its final value, but the percentage of successfully alerted stations reached its minimum value (83.3%). This is because at this time many stations picked the P-waves, and the algorithm replaced GMPE predictions with those derived from P-wave amplitude parameters, where the station is located (see Data and Method section for details). These values, predicted from the P-waves, (to which the algorithm gives priority) could be smaller than the ones predicted by the GMPE as long as the P-wave peak amplitude is not approached, (as already observed in Rea et al., 202429 and Rea et al., 202550). It is worth clarifying that this is the reason why at this time the maximum percentage of Missed Alerts (15.7%) is reached. At the end of the simulation, 94.8% of the stations are classified as Successful Alert while 4.2% of them are classified as Missed Alert. 1% of the stations would receive a False Alert and this percentage does not change.

In Fig. 3b, the lead time provided by the system at the successfully alerted stations is shown as a function of the epicentral distance. The threshold is exceeded from the first alert (10 s after OT) at almost all the stations, allowing for about 20 s of lead-time at 150 km from the epicenter. Farther from the epicenter the lead time increases, reaching 50–70 s at 250 km. At 206 s from the OT, Early-Est provides its first estimation of location and magnitude which is routinely considered stable enough to be used for an alert, based on the trade-off between earliness and accuracy51 (blue horizontal line in Fig. 3b). The time at which Early-Est estimations are available is, therefore, too late for an effective earthquake early warning.

We also tested the QuakeUp method in a numerical simulation environment, for a different earthquake scenario: the 1908 Messina Strait earthquake. Different reasons are behind this choice. First, we want to understand if an impact-based EEWS can work properly also using a network geometry with important azimuthal gaps, such as the 94° gap in the analyzed case study. This is indeed very common in tsunamigenic scenarios. Second, the Messina event was one of the most destructive events in the Mediterranean Sea and hence it represents an interesting case study to propose a new methodology for a combined earthquake and tsunami alert.

With the aim of obtaining a larger data set to validate the method, we performed 150 simulated scenarios in the Messina Strait area (Italy). We simulated earthquakes of magnitude ranging from 6 to 7, with the hypocenter displaced at different locations along the fault associated with the 1908 earthquake43. The traces in acceleration and velocity for these events were first simulated at the stations working in real-time in the area (see Methods for details). Then, the entire chain of the QuakeUp methodology was run independently for each of the simulated scenarios. Results of this massive numerical experiment are summarized in Fig. 4, showing the distributions of the errors on source parameter estimations and the distribution of the time needed to obtain their stable values. The errors in magnitude are calculated as the difference between the true magnitude value set by the simulation, and the one estimated by QuakeUp, while the stabilization time is computed as the time at which the parameter estimation remains constant for at least 5 s. The mean and the median of these errors have the value of -0.3 and the standard deviation of this distribution is 0.25 (Fig. 4a). The negative value of the mean and the median indicates the system’s general tendency to underestimate the magnitude. This underestimation may come from the analyzed magnitude range (6–7) which requires many seconds of P-wave signal to have an accurate estimation, because of the duration of their relative source time function47. Figure 4d shows that QuakeUp needs an average time of 36 s from the OT to obtain a stable value of this parameter. As for the epicentral location, the errors are calculated as the distance in kilometers between the simulated epicenter and the estimated one. Figure 4b shows that the mean and the median of the errors have a value of 12 km, with a standard error of 2 km. The distribution of the error is not centered in zero, but this comes from the way we calculate this quantity, which can assume only positive values. Moreover, the geometry of the network affects these estimations. The average time needed to obtain stable estimations (Fig. 4e) is 35 s from the OT, which is much smaller than the time provided by the system used nowadays in a TEWS framework2. The method provided an average error of −2 km (Fig. 4c), showing good performance compared to other systems52,53. Considering that the depth error is calculated as the difference in kilometers between the true value and the estimated one, we can say that the system tends to overestimate the depth value. Discrepancy was expected from the general difficulties of estimating the depth of an event using only the P-waves arrival times. The average time needed to have this estimation is 35 s, which is significantly faster than the system currently used for an integration with the TEWS10,11.

Finally, the PTF algorithm was applied to both case studies to calculate the hazard curves at different target locations in the two regions. To do so, we used the hypocentral position estimated by QuakeUp and Early-Est (when available) as input for the PTF. Specifically, for the Messina Strait, we selected one of the 150 simulated scenarios that is representative of the ensemble. In particular, we chose the scenario that reproduces the historical event as reconstructed by Michelini et al.43.

We also calculated the theoretical tsunami travel time in both scenarios to identify the blind zones (the zones where the alert is expected to be issued after the tsunami has impacted) associated with the alert issued by the PTF using the source parameters estimations by QuakeUp and Early-Est, respectively. The travel times were calculated, using the software TTT54, from the fault models of the two scenarios (black dashed lines in Fig. 5a–d) and using a 15 arc-sec bathymetric model (SRTM15+55). The complete maps of the TTT are shown in Supplementary Material. The fault model used to calculate the tsunami travel-times in the Messina region was the same used to produce the seismograms for the magnitude 7 event, while the fault model of the Samos earthquake was obtained based on the focal mechanism proposed by IPGP56 and the fault dimensions computed by the Wells and Coppersmith earthquake relation39. The shape of the blind zones corresponds to specific times from the OT of the earthquakes. In both cases shown in Fig. 5 the combination of Early-Est and PTF provided a first stable tsunami alert in 3 min and 40 s from the OT. Using QuakeUp, these alert times are reduced to 66 s in the case of Samos (Fig. 5a) and 48 s in the Messina case (Fig. 5d).

In both case-studies we assumed no latency for the data transmission, assuming an ideal scenario. The exact latency time can be different at each station location and it would affect the time at which the systems obtain their final estimations depending on the robustness and speedness of the data transmission system. For this reason, we chose to neglect the data transmission latency in our simulations, that should introduce additional delays of the order of 1–2 s, with the existing technologies.

Discussion

For the two scenarios presented in this paper, we showed how a regional EEWS could significantly speed up the operation of TEWS, providing epicenter, depth and magnitude estimations up to two minutes before the currently used system. QuakeUp method proved capable of delivering source parameters estimations in less than one minute. It must be taken into account that the available seismic network is not always optimal for dealing with offshore, potentially tsunamigenic earthquakes.

For the 2020 Samos earthquake, using an intensity threshold of IMM=IV, QuakeUp could provide a successful alert at more than 90% of the stations in the area, issuing these alerts at sites located up to 300 km from the epicenter. The lead time provided for those sites ranges from a few seconds at an epicentral distance of about 50 km to more than a minute at 300 km. In a seismic risk-only defense perspective, these results are consistent with the ones provided for inland earthquake scenarios28,29,58.

The quickness in providing the source parameter estimations was confirmed by the system in the case of the simulated 1908 Messina earthquake. For this scenario, we generated 150 earthquakes with varying magnitude, hypocentral position and fault dimensions, and tested QuakeUp for each of these simulations, confirming that in all these scenarios the method could provide source parameters estimations in less than one minute.

To obtain these results, we assumed that the seismic network in both study areas were fully operational without any station outages. In reality, however, strong ground shaking during large earthquakes may cause some stations to malfunction, and others may already be temporarily inoperative for unrelated reasons. Previous studies28 have shown that QuakeUp estimates remain reliable even under such conditions. To further assess the robustness of the method we conducted simulations, for the Messina case, generating 1000 network configurations with 30% of stations failing, 1000 with 50% failing and 1000 with 70% failing. Although the accuracy of the estimations decreases as the network coverage is reduced, the system consistently provided reasonable location and magnitude values within less than one minute (see Sect. 3 of Supplementary Material).

Our results suggest that adopting a multi-hazard approach is crucial: EEWS source parameter estimations can be used to trigger tsunami alerts within TEWS workflows. Less than one minute is required for earthquake source characterization, which significantly reduces the time needed to issue a tsunami alert compared with the current operational systems. This reduction in warning time has a direct effect on the extent of blind zones in coastal areas, as shown in Fig. 5a-d, where large portions of the coastline (including tens of cities and thousands of people) fall into the area protected by earlier alerts.

Moreover, the combination of EEWS and TEWS brings additional societal benefits. First, EEWS alerts in coastal areas exposed to tsunamigenic earthquakes can provide seconds to minutes of warning for strong ground shaking, before the tsunami arrives. This dual alerting capability not only improves population preparedness for earthquake damage but also maximizes evacuation lead time against incoming tsunami waves. Second, the availability of EEWS information before tsunami arrival could support rapid civil protection actions, allowing authorities to anticipate operations for moving populations to safety along coastal areas. To make these alerts truly effective, however, structural strategies must be developed in tandem: adapted building practices to withstand strong shaking, and well-planned evacuation routes tailored to coastal communities at risk.

Finally, in this study we considered only a simplified, point-source characterization of the earthquake by its hypocenter and magnitude. However, there is significant potential for EEWS methodologies to provide early constraints on the geometry of the causative fault59. Such information could be integrated into the PTF14 frameworks to enable more refined and physically consistent tsunami scenarios, further improving the reliability of early warning systems.

The resemblance between the hazard curves calculated by the PTF (Fig. 5b–c), ensures the goodness of the robustness of combining QuakeUp with the tsunami forecasting workflows. The very small difference between the two curves confirms that the PTF can achieve nearly identical results when driven by either QuakeUp or Early-Est inputs. Considering, however, that QuakeUp provides these estimations much faster than Early-Est, its integration into TEWS could accelerate tsunami alerts without degrading their accuracy, representing a significant step forward in coastal hazard mitigation.

Conclusions

Current tsunami early warning systems may be too slow to provide effective alerts in near-coastal earthquake scenarios, as they rely on regional data and require minutes to stabilize source estimates., By adopting a multi-hazard perspective, we demonstrated that the QuakeUp EEWS can deliver reliable source parameters (location, depth and magnitude) within about 40 s from the origin time. Although similar to the method used by the operational systems at JMA25,26, the proposed novel approach is aimed at a faster source parameters estimation, useful for earthquakes occurring very close to the coast, where the time to issue a tsunami warning alert might be extremely short. The approach has been shown to be effective, also significantly increasing tsunami warning lead time in such scenarios. Indeed, tests on the 2020, Mw 7.0 Samos earthquake and on synthetic scenarios of the 1908 Messina event confirmed the robustness of the method, under even partial network failures, with epicentral errors of only a few kilometers and magnitude estimates within 0.1–0.3 units of reference values.

When integrated into a TEWS, these rapid estimates may enable earlier alerts that substantially reduce blind zones along exposed coastlines, potentially providing tens of seconds to minutes of additional lead time for evacuation and risk-mitigation actions. Beyond immediate warning, coupling EEWS and TEWS highlights the need for complementary strategies, including earthquake-resistant construction and well-planned evacuation routes, to effectively reduce losses in coastal areas.

Finally, while this study used simplified point-source descriptions, EEWS approaches hold promise for delivering early constraints on fault geometry, which could further refine Probabilistic Tsunami Forecasting. Together, these advances show that the integration of EEWS into TEWS represents a critical step toward faster, more reliable, and societally relevant tsunami hazard mitigation.

Methods and data

QuakeUp

The method is a P-wave shaking-forecast based Earthquake Early Warning method, that uses real-time recorded traces, from local to regional distances, to forecast the impact of an earthquake in terms of predicted peak ground velocity, within the area where the largest damage is expected. The algorithm is composed of different modules aiming to obtain the prediction of the ground shaking based on the P-wave signals recorded by a regional seismic sensors network.

An event is declared when a minimum of 5 P-wave arrival times are identified at the stations in the network by the FilterPicker algorithm60. This module uses a band-pass filter battery, STA/LTA based strategy to identify the P-wave arrival time on the vertical component of the real-time recorded traces. These data are used to locate the event using the algorithm RTLoc61, or MParLoc62 which are probabilistic methods, with RTLoc using both triggered and not-yet-triggered stations to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of hypocentral coordinates along with their uncertainties.

The P-wave peaks in acceleration, velocity and displacement () are measured as the maximum of absolute value on the vertical component on a progressively expanded time window. The measurements start at the identified P-wave arrival time and they are updated every 0.5 s. The iterative procedure stops after a predefined time from the first pick. The peaks and are then used to estimate the magnitude of the event and the PGV that will be recorded at the stations.

Probabilistic tsunami forecasting (PTF)

The methodology behind the TEWS currently under testing at the CAT-INGV is called probabilistic tsunami forecasting (PTF13. It is based on the idea of performing forecasting on a probabilistic base, accounting for the uncertainty inherited from the early estimation of the earthquake source. PTF provides as an outcome the probability of exceeding a Tsunami Intensity Measure (TIM, e.g. maximum run-up or near-coast wave amplitude) at multiple forecast points, as soon as an earthquake location and magnitude estimates are available, typically a few minutes after origin time. Early-Est

The software used to provide fast earthquake parameter estimations (hypocenter and magnitude) for the PTF is Early-Est6 and it uses real-time global teleseismic data. The location estimations are performed using the arrival times retrieved by the algorithm FilterPicker60. At each time step this algorithm provides the picks on the traces and Early-Est uses them to calculate the hypocentral location.

Early-Est also provides three types of magnitude estimations: mb, Mwp and Mwpd. Each time the algorithm decides which type of magnitude is more significant to describe an ongoing earthquake. Two main principles are used to decide which type of magnitude should be used: (1) the number of picked stations with a good signal-to-noise ratio and (2) the range of validity of each magnitude type.

To start the magnitude estimations, the system needs at least six picked stations. Once the data from the stations are available the system estimates the values of the three types of magnitude and assigns to the event the value mb if Mwp<5.0. The value of Mwp is used in the range 5.0 < Mwp<7.2, otherwise if Mwp>7.2 the value of Mwpd estimation is used5.

2020 Mw7.0 Samos earthquake

We tested the performances of the method presented in the previous paragraph on the tsunamigenic moment magnitude 7.0, 2020, Samos earthquake. The event occurred in the Aegean Sea, near the island of Samos on the 30th of October 2020 and subsequently a tsunami hit the coast of Türkiye and Greece. The geography of the area is characterized by the presence of numerous islands (under Greek jurisdiction) that are distant from each other. The aim of this test is to understand if an earthquake early warning system can be effective also using a sparse sensor network and if the estimation computed by this type of system can be helpful for the assessment of a tsunami alert. To understand this issue better, let’s consider that the previous cases in which QuakeUp was tested were inlands events with a dense network deployed around the fault, like for the 2016 Mw6.5 Norcia, Italy, earthquake. In the case of the 2023 Türkiye event the average inter-stations distance was 20 km29, while in the 2016 Norcia event the network available had an average inter-station distance of 9km28. In the case of Samos the average inter-stations distance is higher (25 km) and in the Messina Strait we have a large azimuthal gap (94 deg) in south-eastern direction.

The data recorded during this event are available at the IRIS website42. Using the accelerometric waveforms recorded at 105 stations in the area, we simulated a real-time performance of QuakeUp. The stations used for this simulation were located from 15 to 328 km from the epicenter estimated by the IRIS Bulletin. This range of distance can be considered as complementary to the one used by Early-Est, in fact the estimations provided by this method were based on data coming from stations located at more than 130 km.

The stations used for this study allow us to have real-time prediction of the seismic intensity. The maximum recorded intensity was VII in the epicentral area, after a few seconds from the origin time of the event, but values of intensity greater than V were recorded up to 269 km from the epicenter after 109 s from the origin time. The theoretical tsunami arrival time at the coast of Samos is about five minutes after the OT (calculated with TTT54. Considering that the theoretical estimations usually underestimate the actual inundation time, we can say that the nearest cost is reached by the tsunami waves in five minutes while the other coastal cities in the Dodecanese region were hit within an hour from the OT16,17,31.

Simulated events for the Messina Strait area

We simulated a set of tsunamigenic earthquakes in a coastal region where INGV deployed a dense on-shore seismic network. The region we chose is the Messina Strait, in southern Italy. In 1908 a magnitude 7 event took place, destroying the cities of Messina and Reggio Calabria and causing thousands of deaths. The earthquake also generated tsunami waves that hit the same cities a few minutes after the ground shaking. In the region, nowadays, a real-time-working dense seismic network is deployed on the mainland. In this context, we aim to investigate the efficiency of QuakeUp even when networks are characterized by significant azimuthal gaps (see Fig. 1b).

The possibility to simulate seismic events allows us to explore a multitude of scenarios in terms of magnitude and location, to give robustness to the results obtained on the single real events from a statistical point of view. Hence, we simulated 150 earthquakes in a magnitude range from 6 to 7 (with a 0.25 step) and tested the early warning method on them. The faults were simulated using the k-square model34, with the characteristics listed in Table 3. The values of length and width for each magnitude bin were calculated using the scaling relationship by Wells and Coppersmith37, but the orientation of the fault was kept the same for all the simulations following the idea that the same existing structure can produce earthquakes of different magnitudes depending on the dimension of the rupturing area.

In Table 3 is also listed the dimension of the square cells used to discretize the faults (spatial discretization ). We want to specify that this dimension decreases with the decreasing of the fault size, according to the principle explained by the Nyquist theorem65. We need at least 10 points to correctly reproduce the minimum wavelength (maximum frequency), but lower magnitude earthquakes produce higher frequency content in the seismograms. Therefore, once we established the minimum wavelength to reproduce depending on the expected source duration, we set appropriate spatial discretization.

For each fault we produced 10 stochastic slip distributions to enlarge the variability in the source modelling. For each slip distribution, we produced three different sets of seismograms, changing the position of the hypocenter on the fault. We placed the hypocenters in the southern part, in the center and in the northern part of the 5 simulated faults (one for each magnitude value), resulting in a total of 150 sets of seismograms at the 146 RAN (Rete Accelerometrica Nazionale39 stations in the area.
