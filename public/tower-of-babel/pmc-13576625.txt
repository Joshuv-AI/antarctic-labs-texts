Lignocellulosic waste biomass as a valuable energy source for bioethanol production: a review
Authors: Somia Mazhar, Isha Ijaz, Muhammad Nadeem Zafar, Zartasha Safdar, Farooq Ahmad, Muhammad Zubair, Syed Salman Sahfqat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global search for sustainable biofuels has accelerated due to the depletion of fossil fuel reserves and growing environmental concerns about greenhouse gas emissions. Because these are widely available and have the potential to lower carbon footprints. Bioethanol made from lignocellulosic biomass stands out among other biofuel options. Examining the composition, pre-treatment techniques, and biochemical conversion processes of lignocellulosic biomass, this work thoroughly examines its potential as a substrate for the generation of bioethanol. The importance of pre-treatment techniques, such as physical, chemical, biological, and hybrid approaches, has been assessed critically in terms of how they improve biomass digestibility and enhance sugar release. With an emphasis on optimising yield and process efficiency, developments in hydrolysis, fermentation, and ethanol recovery processes have also been covered. Notwithstanding its benefits, the manufacture of lignocellulosic bioethanol is fraught with technological and financial difficulties, requiring further research and development. In order to increase commercial viability, future prospects must have a strong emphasis on integrating sustainable biorefinery models, genetically engineered microbial strains, and affordable pre-treatment technologies. Lignocellulosic bioethanol has the potential to be a key component of the worldwide renewable energy transition, guaranteeing a more sustainable and greener future, provided that it continues to advance and receives regulatory support.

Introduction

In recent decades, due to increasing population, the world is facing a depletion in global oil resources.1–4 The production of energy using these sources leads to the emission of greenhouse gases, which causes global warming. In addition, while stratospheric ozone protects the Earth from harmful radiation, tropospheric ozone has adverse effects on human health. Furthermore, in urban environments, substantial quantities of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from gasoline- and diesel-powered vehicles are emitted, contributing to the exacerbation of tropospheric ozone.5 To avoid the alarming increase in environmental pollution and to combat the conventional oils run-off in the future, humans are adopting the use of renewable energy sources such as solar energy and wind energy.6 Biofuels can act as substitutes to fossil fuels. For this purpose, plant as well as algae biomass have been used as sources for biofuel (biodiesel and bioethanol) production in recent years.7,8

Bio-alcohols have been used as a fuel source for many years; traditionally, ethanol was used as drinking alcohol, but now ethanol is used in place of gasoline in vehicles to extensively reduce NOx, VOC and CO2 emissions.9 Compared to gasoline, bioethanol has advantages such as a higher octane number, broader flammability limit, and a greater heat of vaporization; however, pitfalls include a lower energy density and higher corrosiveness.10 Hence, bioethanol is the most widely used biofuel in the world.

The selection of a substrate for the production of biofuel is a crucial step and should be carried out cautiously, as depending specifically on a single type of substrate might render same concerns as faced previously.11–13 Bioethanol can be produced from rice straw,14 agricultural waste,15 microalgae16 sugar beet17etc. In this review, lignocellulosic biomass, in general, will be considered a substrate for bioethanol production. Lignocellulosic biomass consisting of cellulose, lignin and hemicellulose is the most widely present biomass on Earth.18 It can be pre-treated via several methods, including physical, chemical and biological methods, to make it available to enzymes. Transportation encompasses a significant portion of global greenhouse gas emissions, which demands the creation of sustainable fuels and low-carbon technologies. First-generation (1 G) raw material fuels, such as corn and sugarcane, still account for the largest share of bioethanol production; however, urgent concerns about food availability and feedstock prices have generated increased interest in second-generation (2 G) bioethanol created from lignocellulosic biomass.19 In 2022 alone, the production of bioethanol in the United States reached 58.1 million m3, in Brazil, 28.4 million m3, and in the European Union, 5.5 million m3.20 At the same time, lignocellulosic biomass is produced in billions of tons yearly globally, making it a convenient resource for biofuel production.21

There is extensive literature that has examined lignocellulosic biomass for bioethanol production, specifically focusing on technologies for pre-treatment, enzymes used in hydrolysis, pathways for fermentation and techno-economic issues related to lignocellulosic biomass-derived ethanol.19 Recent studies have discussed advancements in pre-treatment strategies as well as improvements in the potential for commercial success (2 G) of bioethanol produced from lignocellulosic biomass.22 Most of these assessments have, however, only focused on specific segments of the production process (e.g., pre-treatment, saccharification, or fermentation) or on one or two types of feedstocks at a time.23 Thus, a comprehensive review of all of the aforementioned directions (feedstocks, pre-treatment approaches, fermentation methods, processes for recovering ethanol, commercialization challenges, and future development/adoption strategies) is needed.24–26

The current review provides an all-inclusive and comparative assessment of the use of lignocellulosic biomass for the production of bioethanol. In contrast to previous literature reviews that concentrate mainly on pre-treatment methods or process optimization, the current review includes an assessment of a wide variety of feedstocks,27 including edible crops, non-food crops, hardwoods, softwoods, invasive plant species, horticulture crop waste, residual organic matter, and agro-industrial by-products.28 In addition to comparing pre-treatment methods (physical, chemical, biological, and integrated) across all feedstocks, the current review provides a thorough comparison of the impact of pre-treatment methods on the deconstruction of biomass into fermentable sugar and the yield of bioethanol produced.29

In addition to the technologies used for bioenergy production, as well as their potential effects on economy, this review offers a unique integration of all three perspectives; i.e., technological, economic, and sustainability.30 The review identifies key barriers to commercialization, including biomass recalcitrance, formation of inhibitory compounds in biomass feedstocks, variability of biomass feedstocks, high costs of enzyme production, and difficulties associated with the scale-up of production.31–33 In addition, emerging solutions to these barriers include integrated biorefineries (biomass-to-energy conversion facilities that also produce by-products such as fuel), advanced microbial engineering, and process enhancements to produce second-generation biofuel.34 The circular bioeconomy, which promotes the use of waste streams to create new products, among other innovative technologies, is also discussed. As a result, this review provides an integrated, comprehensive resource that can guide researchers, industrial stakeholders, and policy-makers towards the sustainable development and deployment of second-generation bioethanol technologies.35

Lignocellulose biomass as a substrate

Since the invention of the wheel, which laid the foundations of the modern era, advancements have been made to devise strategic pathways for biofuel production. Transportation is considered as the main reason for environmental pollution worldwide due to the rapid emission of harmful gasses (COx, SOx, NOx, etc.) directly into the air. Researchers have been trying to figure out the main reason, and surprisingly, petroleum-based fuels were found to be the major contributors to environmental pollution.36 This concern has driven the discovery of alternative, sustainable and eco-friendly fuels, as the dependence on transportation cannot be ignored. The most effective fuels in this aspect are alcohol-based biofuels, e.g., bioethanol, due to their higher oxygen-containing nature, which assists green combustion in engines.37

Bioethanol was first used by Morey in 1826;38 however, its production is quite challenging due to the complexity of the substrate required for its production. Conventionally, bioethanol was produced from sucrose and starch, but these are also used as food, which restricts their utility. The most important substrate is lignocellulosic biomass or lignocelluloses, which provides a smooth path for bioethanol production.39 It comprises three versatile polymers, namely lignin (10–35%), cellulose (25–55%) and hemicellulose (8–25%), as shown in Fig. 1, which are associated with each other in the form of a wide hetero-matrix. The composition of lignocellulosic biomass varies depending on the source from which it is extracted.36

Components of lignocellulose.

Cellulose is a polymer formed in a linear fashion by linking of glucose molecules via beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds, as shown in Fig. 1. It is the most stable component of lignocellulose and provides structural support. Moreover, it serves as a source of sugars to carry out fermentation into ethanol.40 20–30 wt% of lignocellulose is composed of hemicellulose, which is a complex branched heteropolymer containing pentoses (β-d-xylose and α-l-arabinose), hexoses (β-d-mannose, β-d-glucose, and β-d-galactose), glucuronic acid, and trace amounts of l-rhamnose and l-fucose units, together with xylans as the most abundant hemicellulosic polymers. Some important sugars that form hemicellulose are shown in Fig. 1. The third crucial component of lignocellulose is a phenol-based polymer, referred to as lignin, which is composed of three monolignol monomer precursors: p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol, and sinapyl alcohol, which provide structural support.41

Sources of lignocellulosic biomass (LCB)

The source of the lignocellulosic biomass plays a crucial role in its overall composition. Forest trees in this regard serve as the best LCB source. Undoubtedly, forests are very important for sustainability on Earth; therefore, cutting trees simply to obtain LCB is never considered. However, waste streams from various timber industries are vital sources of LCB. Forestry wastes, waste trunk from pulp mills, rejected logs from plywood mills and sawmills, or low-grade lumber are a rich source of LCB.42 Another important source of lignocelluloses is agricultural residues, i.e., rice husk, sugarcane bagasse, corn stover, cereal straw, and perennial crops such as poplar, miscanthus, and willow.

The features and structure of lignocellulosic biomass differ significantly from one feedstock to another, which affects pretreatment operations as well as the amount of ethanol ultimately produced. Feedstocks with high cellulose content have high amounts of fermentable sugars, which leads to higher theoretical yields of ethanol, while high lignin quantities make the biomass more difficult to process as they impose specific restrictions on enzymes.43 At the same time, hemicellulose content influences the choice of pretreatment techniques, as its removal improves cellulose availability, whereas excessive breakdown of hemicellulose can produce inhibitory compounds.33Table 1 presents a clear overview of the differences in composition and properties of lignocellulosic biomass, which depend on the type of biomass and largely govern the choice of pretreatment and the efficiency of bioethanol production. High cellulose levels indicate greater potential for bioethanol production, while high lignin levels make the biomass harder to treat and require intensive pretreatment. Similarly, the type of biomass also determines the process and challenges for its use in the industry. Thus, the comparison of biomass composition and pretreatment, as well as the theoretical bioethanol yield of biomass, works as a guideline in identifying suitable types of biomass and optimizing 2 G bioethanol production.

In order to obtain a meaningful comparison of ethanol production and lignocellulosic biomass, it is necessary to carefully consider the experimental conditions used for various steps of the process, such as pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and fermentation. Yield values reported in the literature differ because of differences in the level of biomass loading and the amount of enzymes used in the fermentation process, the type of microorganisms, and the reactor design and dimensions, and also the amount of fermentable carbohydrates in the substrate being used. Therefore, the interpretation of ethanol yields under different operating conditions must be done with caution in order to avoid confusing results regarding the superiority of one method over others. Indeed, one can increase the ethanol concentration by increasing the substrate loading; however, the increase can result in higher slurry viscosity and mass transfer limitations. Also, the level of enzyme loading has a more direct influence over the quantity of cellulose that can be hydrolysed in the process of fermentation. Finally, different fermentation strains have various capabilities to utilize various types of sugars, including glucose and xylose.

Pre-treatment methods for lignocellulosic biomass

Lignocellulosic biomass51 processing is the first, most important, and most expensive step of the whole process. Initially, for the hydrolysis of LB, acids and enzyme catalysts were used, but later the technique of enzyme immobilization was adopted to achieve higher efficiency. Pre-treatments are used to destroy the complex structure of lignocellulosic biomass and break the cross-linkages between their structural polymers, leading to a decrease in their polymerisation, as demonstrated in Fig. 2.

Schematic showing the structure of lignocellulosic biomass.

These methods not only decrease the polymerisation of lignocellulosic biomass but also reduce the size and enhance the surface area, making them more susceptible to enzymatic attack.52 Lignin causes irreversible inactivation of enzymes as it is a good adsorbent, and it also slows down the hydrolysis of polysaccharides to monosaccharides; therefore, lignin must be removed to enhance the efficiency of the process. Another challenge faced during pre-treatment of LB is the generation of limiting compounds such as furan derivatives, phenolic compounds, and weak acids. They decrease microbial activity and are highly undesirable, so they must be eliminated in pre-treatment steps.

Pre-treatment methods should be environmentally friendly, cost-efficient, easy to carry out, and enable management of the waste lignin produced by utilising the entire lignocellulosic biomass substrate; Table 2 presents these parameters. It should support large-scale loading of lignocellulosic biomass substrate and must avoid production of by-products that disrupt the hydrolysis and fermentation process. Commercially, a number of pre-treatment methods are used, including chemical, physical, and biological methods.

Physical pre-treatment methods

As the name indicates, physical pre-treatment methods involve the use of mechanical forces to reduce the size of the lignocellulosic feedstock.61,62 Mood et al. contemplated that these pre-treatment methods involve mechanical forces and pressure to reduce the size of biomass, to increase surface area, decrease crystallization and polymerisation of lignocellulosic biomass. Basically, the crosslinking between the polymeric structural network is broken down employing mechanical forces, irradiation, electromagnetic field, temperature, and pressure to increase their surface area and pore volume.52 Physical pre-treatment methods include grinding and milling, chipping, pyrolysis, extrusion, ultrasound treatment, microwave treatment, and pulsed electric field treatments, ranging from the oldest, most energy-intensive, cost-inefficient methods to such treatments that do not pollute the environment, are easy to carry out, and generate no harmful side products.34

Grinding and milling

This is the primary and oldest pre-treatment method; it is used to crush lignocellulosic biomass to reduce its size and polymeric cross-linkage, but a lot of energy is required for this process.66,67 For energy saving, Hideno et al. conducted a study regarding grinding of rice straw without sulphuric acid in wet disk milling that produced a slurry of rice straw that was then hydrolysed. This produced better results than the dry ball-milling process, indicating that wet ball-milling is the ideal pre-treatment for rice straw, producing ethanol yields of 116.65 and 147.42 mg g−1 for 2.5% DR (digested residue) and 10% DR, respectively, which might be regarded as a possible cellulosic feedstock; however, optimal conditions must be maintained for non-acidic and non-alkali pre-treatments.68

Sun et al. studied pre-treatment methods and proposed that, depending on the method, the final particle size can be reduced to 10–30 mm or even 0.2–2 mm; moreover, with ball milling, the bioethanol yields increased 1.1- to 14.8-fold compared to that for untreated biomass.69 For combined milling pre-treatment, steel balls are considered most efficient due to the smooth texture, maximum force and lowest friction; however, for solely milling, alumina balls are preferred.70,71 Although the energy consumption required for ball milling is high, by combining microwave irradiation with milling, the power consumption was reduced by 77.4%.72 Different methods of milling yield different depolymerisation products of cellulose (glucose and levoglucosan).52,73 Shen et al. also researched this and proposed that ball milling gives the highest yields of glucose and xylose after enzymatic hydrolysis.74

Extrusion

Extrusion is a thermo-physical process that breaks down the complex structure of lignocellulosic biomass both chemically and physically by combining intense shearing, mild heating, and quick mixing, resulting in increased sugar recoveries. It has, however, higher energy demands; hence, it is not an excellent alternative to old methods.75 Lamsal et al. found that hard red winter wheat bran pre-treated via extrusion led to higher reducing sugar yields than achieved by simple particle size reduction. After drying, the pellets of pre-treated biomass were formed. The production yield achieved by thermo-physical pre-treatment is composition-specific.71 Conan et al. suggested that by optimising the conditions of pre-treatment, notable yields can be produced, moreover extrudate shows clear changes in its morphological form, with a brittle surface that is rough and crumbly. Sometimes chemicals used in chemical pre-treatments react with moisture in the sample and produce empty spaces on the surface, leading to increased surface area post-extrusion.71 Wang et al. studied the extrusion of corn starch with different moisture content and concluded that the peak viscosity, trough viscosity, and final viscosity of the post-extrusion pastes varied. With higher moisture content, corn starch's gelatinization increased, whereas the crystallinity, order and packing decreased.76

Microwave treatment

Microwave-assisted treatment proves to be efficient due to its high heating rate and ease of operation, along with reduced energy requirements. Xiong et al. reported that upon microwave irradiation, the ultrastructure of cellulose and lignin breaks down, enhancing their susceptibility to hydrolysis using enzymes and catalysts.77 Similarly, Subha et al. found in their research that higher microwave intensity and greater irradiation time led to more cellulose removal and increased digestibility.78 Rezania et al. suggested in their work that microwave-assisted pre-treatment is consistent and selective, requires less energy, and takes less time to process, it is a possible substitute for traditional heating. Moreover, the rigid surface of LCB is cracked by irradiation with microwaves.79

Pulsed electric field

This novel pre-treatment method enhances biomass sensitivity by exposing it to higher voltages, ranging from 5.0 to 20.0 kV cm−1, for short-duration pulses (nano-to milli-seconds) to expose the cellulosic part of the biomass, and then cellulose is further broken down into sugars. This method can be carried out under ambient conditions and requires less energy because the electric field is applied for a shorter duration of approximately a few hundred microseconds.80 Putranto et al. analysed the lignocellulosic content of biomass using pulsed electric field, where the PEF apparatus had an output voltage of 8–31.36 kV and an electric field strength of 2.46–9.65 kV cm−1. Milled corncob was pre-treated at 5–9 kV cm−1 for 60–300 seconds and gave powdered corncob after the pre-treatment. They concluded that while untreated sample had a cellulose content of up to 22.5%, the cellulosic content increased to 56.63% ± 2.48–63.09% ± 1.73% after pre-treatment.81 Kumar et al. analysed changes in the structural properties upon application of a pulsed electric field to lignocellulosic biomass. This technique has low energy requirements, is non-destructive, requires simple instrumentation, and delivers enhanced cellulose hydrolysis, resulting in efficient conversion of the biomass into bioethanol.

Alawad conducted an experiment on flax straw and observed that PEF pre-treatment caused more porosity of the surface of the biomass, enabling increased glucose and xylose yields.82 With 2000 pulses and a field strength of 10 cm kV−1, the cellulose hydrolysis and ethanol yield improved because the electric field causes disruption of the membrane, resulting in loss of the cell's semi-permeability. Arumugam et al. studied the effect of PEF on sliced sugar-beet tails when an electric field of 5 kV was applied by comparing the juices obtained from untreated and pre-treated sugar beet. It was found that PEF-treated juices had double the sucrose content and almost 3.75 times greater ethanol yield as compared to untreated juices.83

Freeze pre-treatment

Freez pre-treatment method uses low temperature to crush lignocellulosic biomass, that significantly increases glucose and bioethanol yields. Barley straw was taken as a sample and pre-treated at 5.8 to −8 °C in winter. Samples were thawed and later hydrolysed via enzymes. Barley straw, being an agricultural waste with a high cellulosic content, is a good feedstock for bioethanol production. In this method, biomass was milled, mixed with water, and frozen to −18 °C, followed by defrosting to 22 °C repeatedly. The highest hydrolysis efficiency of 30.37% was achieved via freeze pre-treatment.84 This method has the least environmental impact because it does not require toxic chemicals. Dong et al. observed that enzymatic hydrolysis of rice straw was enhanced when the sample was pre-treated using the freeze method, rendering the biomass sensitive to enzymes and increasing the yield from 48% to 84%. Japonica rice was taken as samples, wherein untreated samples had a cellulosic content of 41%, and it increased to 64.26 per gram rice straw. Enzymatic hydrolysis of untreated samples produced 266.77 g kg−1 of reducing sugars, while the pre-treated samples produced 417.27 g kg−1.85

Chemical pre-treatment methods

Disrupting the lignocellulosic matrix is considered to be the most crucial and fundamental step in bioethanol production. Physical pre-treatment methods generally carry out this step inadequately, with a huge investment of energy, and extensive efforts are needed to operate the mechanical machinery involved. Therefore, alternative methods need to be adopted, wherein common chemical methods such as acid pre-treatment, alkaline pre-treatment or organosolv pre-treatment can achieve remarkable yields.86

Acid pre-treatment methods

It is commonly suggested that the most effective chemical pre-treatment method is acid pre-treatment using HCl, H2SO4, and H3PO4 to breakdown the lignocellulosic matrix via glycosidic bond cleavage. Suitable concentrations for acidic pre-treatment vary from 0.2 to 2.5% for higher temperature ranges of 120–210 °C. DAP is suitable for dilute pre-treatment conditions. Nutrients such as sulphur and phosphorus are produced at dilute acid conditions, which are useful for fermentation of bioethanol during its production.87 This step facilitates smooth conversion of polysaccharides into monomeric and oligomeric sugars. Pre-treatment of rye straw using dilute HNO3 was reported to be more useful for glucose conversion as compared to dilute H2SO4; however, use of dilute HNO3 has the major disadvantage of harmful side product generation. Hemicellulose is the major fraction of LCB that is primarily affected by acid pre-treatment.88 Similarly, Lenihan et al. analysed the use of dilute H3PO4 for the pre-treatment of potato peelings and found high cellulose to glucose conversion, but arabinose levels remained lower. To maintain arabinose levels, the temperature was increased to 150 °C; however, the reaction became very slow, and the thermal instability of the process rendered it difficult to manage. So this method cannot be used industrially, even with a theoretical yield of 82.5%.28

Microalgae were pre-treated via acidic pre-treatment with 1–10% v/v of acid by Harun et al. As compared to untreated microalgae, the acidic pre-treated microalgae produced a higher yield (52 g bioethanol per g of microalgae). Moreover, Solarte-Toro et al. concluded that lignin and cellulose are also solubilised by acid pre-treatment, resulting in the release of carbohydrates.89,90 Similarly, cheap agricultural waste, such as rice hulls, was used for bioethanol production, wherein the raw material was impregnated and distilled water was added to 7% of dry content and later placed in hot water for 3 hours so that complete hydrolysis occurs and the impregnated raw material is converted into monomeric sugars. In another series of experiments, a 3 : 1 acid-to-biomass composition was used, and in another series the time required for hydrolysis was varied. Sulphuric acid was used for hydrolysis, and the bioethanol yield was calculated to be 0.47 per gram for substrate.91,92

Ghazali et al. studied durian seed waste Durio zibethinus, which is produced on a large scale in Malaysia, and the waste has a high carbohydrate content of 43.6%. Initially, the seeds were washed and dried, followed by peeling and shredding for 4 h at 80 °C. Pre-treatment was carried out by treating with different concentrations of sulphuric acid and later neutralized with NaOH and fermented using α-amylase. Post-incubation, fermentation of the sugars was carried out. Glucose yields produced without acidic pre-treatments were recorded as 5.56 g L−1 at 90 minutes, while those pre-treated with sulphuric acid showed significant increases, at 50.1 g L−1 and 0.47% of bioethanol.54 Yildirim et al., in their investigation, used cotton straw and sunflower straw as raw materials and calculated the yield of produced bioethanol.93 The feedstock was pre-treated with dilute HCl and gave calculated yields of 0.41 g g−1 for cotton straw and 0.40 g g−1 for sunflower straw. Paramasivan et al. assessed samples such as narrow-leaved cattail, giant reed and hardy sugarcane, which are richer in lignocellulosic content, for bioethanol production. Materials with high LCB and low moisture content were placed in an ultrasonicator with 10% w/w sulphuric acid. The bioethanol yield produced was 81.91% ± 8.17% for narrow-leaved cattail, which is higher than found for other biomasses.94

Alkaline pre-treatment methods

A classic pre-treatment method for pulp and paper processing, alkaline pre-treatment methods also find uses in LCB pre-treatment. Woiciechowski et al. studied alkali pre-treatment, which solubilises the lignin fraction of LCB, by using NaOH, with a small effect on hemicellulose.95 It was deduced by Tu et al. that alkali pre-treatment technologies are effective at removing lignin, hydrolysing hemicellulose, and reducing the crystallinity of cellulose. Recent studies have shown an increase in efficiency when combining alkali pre-treatment with ionic liquids for the production of bioethanol from lignocellulosic biomass. Maryana et al. studied the effect of alkaline pre-treatment on sugarcane bagasse, wherein the raw material was heated with NaOH. The cellulose content was 66.16%, and lignin decreased by 40.6% after 15 minutes of heating; after 30 minutes, cellulose increased by 39.9% and lignin content decreased by 59.1%. Hence, alkaline pre-treatment caused a decrease in the crystallinity of cellulose. Wunna et al. conducted experiments on sugarcane bagasse and heated it with different concentrations of NaOH; with a 2% wt solution of NaOH and autoclaving for 60 minutes, the glucan and xylan concentrations were 37.3% and 33.2%, respectively. Lignin removal was seen as 83.7%. In this study, the optimal conditions were achieved with 10% solid loading in 2 wt% of alkali concentration for 30 min.96 Similarly, extending the options of raw materials and variation in acidic pre-treatments was performed on rice milling raw material for the production of bioethanol. Rice byproducts were dried and milled by hammer milling, and alkaline pre-treatment with sodium hydroxide and hydrogen peroxide was carried out. Analysis post-enzyme saccharification and fermentation showed a reasonable bioethanol yield of 55.1 g L−1. In a similar way, Ashoor et al.97 studied rice straw and its efficiency to produce bioethanol upon soaking different feedstock samples in variable concentrations of NaOH. The final yield produced was 3.3%.

Organosolv pre-treatment methods

Extensive advancements in pre-treatment techniques have presented another great method, named the organosolv pre-treatment method, which was studied by Duarte et al. They used organic solvents such as alcohols, ketones, ethers, organic acids and esters to carry out the whole process.98 Aihiokhai et al. conducted an experiment using these techniques that resulted in high yields of bioethanol. In 2025, a study was conducted that revealed that 100.00 mL of 0.5% H2SO4 pre-treatment of only 10.00 g of powdered rice husk yielded 17.59 mg L−1 bioethanol. Such a remarkable yield was due to the production of higher glucose content via acid pre-treatment.99 Lima et al. researched perola pineapple peel for bioethanol production and found that, upon pre-treatment with the organosolv method combined with dilute acid, the conversion of polysaccharides to reduced sugars from raw materials was 9.38 to 74.91 g L−1.100 The corresponding studies are summarized in Table 3.

Biological pre-treatment methods

As mentioned previously, physical and chemical pre-treatment methods have shortcomings of energy consumption and generation of harmful toxins, leading to their inefficiency prevailing despite good yields, so we shifted towards the use of live micro-organisms for pre-treatment of lignocellulosic biomass to avoid the negative impact of bioethanol production on ecosystems. In biological pre-treatments, life is used to generate fruitful compounds for the maintenance of life on this planet. These pre-treatment methods involve the use of ligninolytic (lignin-breaking) and cellulolytic (cellulose-breaking) microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) or enzymes to reduce the refractory nature of lignocellulosic biomass, thus breaking it into constituents that are sensitive to hydrolysis and enabling effective bioethanol production.

Some bacterial species that have proved to be effective for pre-treatment are Clostridium sp., Cellulomonas sp., Bacillus sp., Thermomonospora sp., and Streptomyces sp. Ummalyma et al. outlined that these methods are suitable as they are cost-efficient, López-Abelairas et al. quoted that they have the least environmental impact, and Vasco-Correa et al. summed that these methods are selective to nature and there is also no production of harmful substances. Sharma et al. put forward their observations that Cellulomonas fimi and Thermomonospora fusca are bacterial species that have been used for cellulose production, and that Paenibacillus campinasensis can live in harsh conditions and is used to pre-treat lignocellulosic biomass. Due to enhanced effects and the structured route for treating biomass, biological methods are combined with other pre-treatment methods to achieve greater yields.

Biological/chemical/oxidative/organosolv pretreatment

Si et al. reported that a combined bacterial and chemical pre-treatment resulted in enhanced scarification and found that after ligninolytic bacteria broke down lignin polymers, enzyme-catalysed hydrolysis of H-RS increased up to 40.9%. Saccharification of N-RS increased by 31.8%.108 Similarly, Meenakshisundaram et al. found that combining biological and alkaline pre-treatment can improve lignocellulosic complex delignification and help reduce alkaline treatment's chemical concentration, time, and temperature, which lowers operating costs.109 Paudel et al. stated that using other oxidizing agents and subsequent pre-treatment with microorganisms not only accelerated degradation but also prevented harmful products from being generated, resulting in higher sugar yields. This is the most efficient method in biological-chemical combined pre-treatments.

It was found that synergistic fungal-organosolv pre-treatment resulted in increased depolymerisation of carbohydrate and lignin production, which are both suitable for bioethanol production and cannot be achieved via a single pre-treatment method. It is noteworthy that although combined pre-treatments yield attractive results, their potential depends upon the strain type and its cultivation conditions, and on the type of the other pre-treatment method applied. These methods are by far the most dependable pre-treatment procedures for the degradation of lignocellulosic biomass for bioethanol generation.

Each pre-treatment has its own efficiencies regarding breaking lignocellulosic biomass, but there are also some negative consequences; for example, overconsumption of power or cost-ineffectiveness, which make it difficult to tag a single pre-treatment as the optimum. Advantages and disadvantages of various pretreatment methods

The current pretreatment technologies have varying merits and demerits with respect to their efficiency in lignin removal, cellulose recovery, enzymatic digestibility, inhibitor generation, severity of the process, and eco-sustainability features. Alkaline and organosolv pretreatments demonstrate high efficiency for delignification and thus better accessibility to cellulose. Acid pretreatment, in comparison, is the most effective method for hemicellulose extraction and quick sugar production, but produces some inhibitors (e.g. furfural or 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furaldehyde, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, HMF, and organic acids) under severe operational conditions. Physical technologies are effective in terms of increasing the surface area and reducing the size of particles; however, their energy consumption level significantly restricts their application. Biological pretreatment has advantages such as low inhibitor generation and mild conditions, but its productivity rate is its biggest disadvantage. Combined technologies are very effective in disrupting the lignocellulosic structure and increasing enzymatic digestibility, but their complexity, consumption of chemicals, and cost are obstacles to their applications. This is why none of the pretreatment processes alone can be regarded as the best; which method to apply should be decided based on the balance between the efficiency of delignification, cellulose recovery, number and character of inhibitors, enzymatic digestibility, severity, energy consumption, scalability, and ecological impact.115

General steps for bioethanol production

Pre-treatment methods are crucial for accessing the lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose fractions present in LCB. Moreover, those methods are crucial to resolve these fractions into further species that can be converted into bioethanol. This conversion to bioethanol is critical and comprises various steps, e.g., hydrolysis, fermentation and distillation. Each step is further categorized into a series of courses depending upon the type of biomass used. All these procedures are interlinked, where one forms the basis of the subsequent step. Pre-treatment forms lignocellulosic structures that are freely available for hydrolysis, which converts those structures into fermentable sugars. The sugars are then fermented to form various useful and sustainable products, of which bioethanol is most useful. The fermented broth is then distilled to separate and purify all the fractions. Detoxification of LCB

Detoxification is 1st step in bioethanol production, and it mainly aims to eliminate toxins from pre-treated biomass and fermentation inhibitors that can reduce the effectiveness of the enzymes and limit the development and activity of microorganisms during fermentation. Detoxification can be carried out separately or in combination with hydrolysis and fermentation. In combination with fermentation, a high dose of inoculum can yield more bioethanol along with decreased levels of furan derivative production.116

Hydrolysis of LCB fractions

Hydrolysis of different fractions of LCB is an imperative step for bioethanol production because it unfolds sugars by carrying out depolymerization of cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin.117 These readily available monomers are directed to fermentation. Hydrolysis of LCB can be achieved in three ways, i.e., acid hydrolysis, alkaline hydrolysis or enzymatic hydrolysis.118 In acid hydrolysis, a dilute or concentrated acid catalyst (HCl, H2SO4, H3PO4, etc.) is used to break down cellulose and hemicelluloses, giving enhanced yields. Dilute acid hydrolysis demands harsh temperature (160–220 °C) and pressure conditions, whereas concentrated acid hydrolysis can be carried out under mild conditions. High temperature and dilute acids can sometimes give rise to the formation of furfural, which acts as an inhibitory compound, reducing the overall yield.119 A study revealed that sugarcane bagasse, when hydrolysed with dilute H3PO4, gives 21.4 g L−1 of fermentable sugars at 100 °C. The inhibitory compounds formed in this case were remarkably low (<4 g L−1).120

Alkaline hydrolysis makes use of bases such as NH4OH, KOH, NaOH, Ca(OH)2, NH3, and (NH4)2SO3 to deform the structural fractions of LCB. Alkaline hydrolysis also carries out partial de-crystallization of cellulose and hemicellulose solvation, which remarkably enhances the yields. As per a previously conducted study, it has been observed that the use of NaOH to carry out hydrolysis of LCB enhances the wood degradability from 14% to 55%.121

As the name indicates, enzymatic hydrolysis requires enzymes that can catalyse the hydrolysis of LCB. In 2023, a study reported that barley straw, when pre-treated with aqueous NaOH for 10 min at 105 °C, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis, gave 86.5% yield of reducing sugars.122

Ethanol fermentation

In the lignocellulosic biomass under treatment, both pentose (5-membered ring sugars) and hexose (6-membered ring sugars) are available for fermentation and lead to the production of bioethanol molecules, as elaborated in Fig. 4 and quoted in the literature.

Simplified mechanism for the fermentation of pentose and hexose.

Bacterial species, such as Zymomonas mobilis, are used for glucose fermentation owing to their high ethanol production and sensitivity to ethanolic concentration (120 g L−1). But they are not naturally viable to ferment pentose, so this incapability leads to their inefficiency.123–125 To overcome this drawback of bacterial species, yeast species such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Candida shehatae, Pachysolen tannophilus, and Pichia stipitis can be efficiently used for fermenting pentose sugars; however, their susceptibility towards higher ethanolic concentrations as well as inhibition under glucose conditions leads to poor yields.123,126 As both bacterial and yeast species have certain drawbacks in the production of ethanol via fermentation under suitable conditions, the focus shifted towards genetically engineered microorganisms that exhibit desirable characteristics with the aim of achieving bioethanol yields that are sufficient to meet global demands for fuel and energy. A perfect genetically engineered organism not only gives high yields of bioethanol but also works at optimum pH, temperature and solvent conditions and is insensitive to increased alcohol concentration.30,127,128Z. mobilis gave higher fermentation productivity as compared to S. cerevisiae.129Saccharomyces cerevisiae,130Zymomonas mobilis,131 and Escherichia coli132 are the most commonly exploited microorganisms for bioethanol production, together with other yeast and bacterial species like Fusarium oxysporum133 and Corynebacterium glutamicum, respectively.134

To reduce the cost and increase bioethanol yield, the refineries make use of a series of fermentation strategies, as listed below:

• Separate hydrolysis and fermentation (SHF): both hydrolysis and fermentation occur in separate units. In the hydrolysis chamber, pre-treated carbohydrates in the biomass are broken down to monosugars and transformed into bioethanol via fermentation. This strategy is time-consuming and cost-ineffective, has high enzyme demands and energy consumption for both chambers separately.124

• Simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF): both hydrolysis and fermentation processes are carried out in one chamber, which results in better yield generation as compared to SHF because yeasts continuously remove sugars as the final product, so end-product inhibition is largely eliminated.135 The main drawback is the temperature of the reactor, as hydrolysis and fermentation require different optimum temperatures.

• Simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation (SSCF): both hydrolysis and fermentation happen in the same chamber along with synchronous co-fermentation of pentose sugar via respective strains, which results in degradation of lignocellulosic biomass to bioethanol, giving increased yields. The yield is, however, lower than that with SSF.

• Consolidated bioprocessing (CBP): one unit carries out hydrolysis, fermentation and enzyme production via genetically modified microbes (yeast strains and Clostridium thermocellum) that are potent enough not only to hydrolyse biomass but also to ferment ethanol.136,137 The strategy has reduced cost and energy requirements for the infrastructure of the procedure and is potentially the best suited among all.138–140

Distillation and dehydration

These steps are essential for extracting fuel-grade bioethanol from lignocellulosic biomass. Distillation enables separation of the target product as bioethanol from the fermentation mixture that is miscible with ethanol based on the difference in their vapour pressures.37 Products generated post-fermentation contain higher percentages of water, and concentrating alcohol in this mixture requires high energy consumption and cost.141,142 A simple process drives 37% alcohol towards a rectification column through which 95% alcohol exits and is further dehydrated to obtain a dry product that contains 99.5% ethanol.143

Separation of ethanol from the fermented mixture is a complex and energy-consuming process that can be undertaken by various procedures such as adsorption distillation, membrane processes, azeotropic distillation, diffusion distillation, extractive distillation, pervaporation, vacuum distillation, and chemical dehydration, with membrane distillation and pervaporation being the optimum among these mentioned techniques.144 Membrane distillation is a procedure that reduces energy consumption by using a membrane that separates the product from the fermented biomass mixture. The process is carried out at lower temperatures, thus concerns regarding heating and associated costs are eliminated. Another green side to this process is full recovery of non-volatile components. This is a continuous process, so fermentation and ethanol recovery both occur simultaneously.145

Pre-evaporation is another kind of membrane-based mechanism that is used to generate anhydrous ethanol. The mechanism is based upon the difference in affinity of ethanol and water towards the polymeric membrane, resulting in the generation of different concentrations of ethanol on either side of the membrane and a final ethanol concentration of 99.8%.

The various configurations for lignocellulosic fermentation differ not only in their benefits, but also in the productivity of ethanol, the quantity of enzymes needed, the complexity, and the industrial applicability of the methods. First, SHF is very flexible with regard to the two processes, since hydrolysis and fermentation can take place separately, but in most cases, such a method requires a much larger quantity of enzyme and has some additional operations. In contrast, SSF has advantages in terms of lower complexity of production technology and reduced sugar leakage, provided the conditions of the process are appropriate for the two kinds of microorganisms used. SSCF is another process that allows the use of the two types of sugars for the production of ethanol, thus increasing the yield of ethanol, but requiring special kinds of microorganisms, as discussed in Table 5. CBP results in the highest degree of process simplification, as the fermentation, enzymatic production and hydrolysis may take place at the same time in one system, which would minimize the technological expenses connected with it. At the same time, CBP has low competitiveness presently due to the low resistance of microorganisms, reduced efficiency and tolerance to inhibitors of the enzymes used, and limited scale-up of the process.146

Bioethanol production

This section discusses the production of bioethanol from several lignocellulosic biomass sources, including edible plants, non-edible plants and agricultural wastes that are rich in cellulose, lignin and hemicellulose contents. Growing energy demands from the global population and ongoing greenhouse gas emissions posing harmful effects on the environment have led the scientific community to look for biomass sources that could serve as feedstocks for bioethanol generation. Every day, scientists urgently search for new biomass sources to combat the energy crisis and reduce pollution. Starchy, non-starchy residues, industrial wastes, agro-industrial wastes, hardwoods, softwoods and even non-woody marine grasses are a few categories to mention that have proven to yield promising results for the production of bioethanol, and all the above-mentioned examples contain lignocellulose in their composition. By using genetically engineered grasses, seeds and residues and by using efficient strategies, scientists are making efforts to achieve maximum yield of bioethanol from plants and agricultural wastes. Edible plants

Many terrestrial crops, including grains, rice, wheat and corn, are rich sources of lignocellulosic biomass and are used extensively in bioethanol production. With growing global fuel demands, sufficient yields of bioethanol are produced from the above-mentioned sources, and global demands for energy are being satiated.150 Lignin, cellulosic and hemicellulosic contents serve as an excellent source of biomass for the production of bioethanol.151 Corn grains are extensively used for bioethanol production, previously with the assumption that only starch content is responsible for ethanol productivity, which was later contested by Gumienna et al., who studied a variety of corn grains (258 samples) and used Optimash to digest non-starchy polysaccharides while using protease for hydrolysis of peptide bonds. Fermentation was carried out with S. cerevisiae and the fermiol strain. It was deduced that lower starch levels and higher reducing sugar levels resulted in higher ethanol production. Corns with higher starch contents are not efficiently saccharified, yielding lower bioethanol production. However, PCA analysis revealed that the hybrid of corn with the maximum ethanol production had the male parent S80660A.152

Production of bioethanol from starch-based materials has also been examined previously by using grains of maize and tuber crops, etc. According to the Department of Energy (DOE) in the USA, from 1998 to 2006, bioethanol production increased to 12.9%, and bioethanol production from maize is currently positive, with a NEB ratio of 1.34%. But the energy efficiency of the system can be enhanced by the use of better maize varieties, good fertilizers, as well as better technological pathways.153 Sanni et al. examined cassava, sweet potato, corn and sorghum for bioethanol production, and these edible crops produced 64.41%, 41.96%, 40.89% and 34.11% as ROI values, being environmentally favourable, with reduced toxic emissions and energy requirements.154

Similarly, bioethanol production from rice and wheat straw was studied, noting that 668 million metric tons of straw can theoretically produce 282 billion litres of ethanol if appropriate technology is available. Singh et al. stated that burning of rice straw adds oxides of nitrogen and sulphur to air, which are pollutants, while using the same amount of rice straw for bioethanol production provides both economic benefits and environmental stability.155 Aziz et al. stated that 39% of sugarcane in India and 54.6% in Brazil are used for bioethanol production, but this may not be applicable for industrial levels due to insufficient information about the greenhouse gases of that particular system where sugarcanes are grown.156 Extensive research is required to conclude whether sugarcanes are trustworthy options for bioethanol production or not. Maria et al. showed that sufficient amounts of ethanol were produced in short incubation times by employing co-cultures of S. cerevisiae and A. niger on potato waste through SSF.157

However, the use of edible crop sources as biomass for bioethanol production is a controversial domain because major regions of the world suffer from poverty and hunger; in such cases, it will be morally incorrect to use food crops for the generation of bioethanol.158 Hence, this technique is worthy yet impractical due to ethical reasons. Scientists then shifted towards the generation of second-degree bioethanol from residues and waste straws left behind after crop harvesting that otherwise do not serve much of a purpose. However, second-degree bioethanol is not as efficient as first-degree production. Jakub et al. proposed that lignocellulosic biomass from sorghum (Poaceae family) proved to be an efficient source of bioethanol yield. Sorghum biomass was physically pre-treated by milling and washed with distilled water.159 SSF was the chosen technique for fermentation. Analytical techniques proved that sweet sorghum is characterised by higher bioethanol production.160

Non-edible plants

As quoted earlier, use of edible plants and grains for energy production may spike world hunger even more, so scientists have explored the use of agricultural wastes, sawdust and straws to generate second-degree bioethanol. Likewise, non-edible plants generally include the waste from sugarcane, maize, rice and wheat straw or other terrestrial crops. Grasses (Poaceae) and aquatic plants can also serve as sources of lignocellulosic biomass, thus generating bioethanol. Production of ethanol from agro-waste derived from Saccharum officinarum (sugarcane) and Zea mays (maize) include sugarcane bagasse, sugarcane bark, corncob, cornhusk, and corn husk, giving ethanol yields of 28.06%, 26.54%, 23.74%, 17.28%, 13.11%, 29.11%, 26.65%, 26.16%, 24.15%, and 23.34%. Raw materials were dried, ground, and pre-treated through acidic hydrolysis, then fermented with S. cerevisiae. Bioethanol was filtered, and a maximum yield was observed with 72 h fermentation.

In a similar fashion, grasses, either wild or cultivated, serve as excellent sources of lignocellulosic biomass for bioethanol production, as grasses are abundant and grow anywhere. Mohapatra et al. conducted an experiment that concluded that corn stover, eucalyptus, hardwood, rice straw, switch grass, and wheat straw produced 90 gal/dry ton, 130–142 gal/dry ton, 75 gal/dry ton, 60–80 gal/dry ton, and 97–105 gal/dry ton of ethanol yield, respectively. Switch grass, Napier grass and Bermuda grass all have potential to act as lignocellulosic biomass and advantages for energy production. Biomass was delignified via combined physicochemical methods, enzymatic saccharification, and hydrolysis to render the biomass ready to be fermented to bioethanol. Kongkeitkajorn et al.161 found that Napier grass produced sufficient ethanol yields of approximately 171 to 187 g of ethanol per kg of Napier grass after alkali pre-treatment and enzymatic hydrolysis followed by S. cerevisiae fermentation. Napier grass produced satisfactory results. The process yielded 171–187 g of ethanol per kg of Napier grass, with ethanol concentrations of 41–45 g L−1.

Among Thai grasses, the Tifton variety of Bermuda grass, vetiver grass and pearl millet were studied as lignocellulosic feedstocks for bioethanol production by Banka et al. and was seen as efficient sources of lignocellulosic biomass, yielding sufficient amounts of ethanol after various pretreatment, and several grasses such as arratum grass, bana grass, pangola grass and ruzi grass produced 0.05 g g−1, 0.11 g g−1, 0.03 g g−1, 0.04 g g−1 of bioethanol, respectively. Trejo et al. studied elephant ear plant, which is a semi-aquatic herbaceous species (family Araceae), as feedstock for bioethanol production. This was pre-treated in an autoclave and fermented via S. cerevisiae, followed by distillation to produce 1.13 mg mL−1 of bioethanol.162

Hardwood plants

In many parts of the world, there are hardwood forests consisting of trees that are a source of strong wood. Due to their faster growth and greater malleability, they are considered a more sustainable source and are of high industrial importance, and their processing generates extensive lignocellulosic biomass that is used for the production of second-degree bioethanol.52 El Hage experimented on acacia and eucalypt pulps to estimate bioethanol production, following the steam explosion as a pretreatment method for breakdown of lignocellulosic biomass and reduction of particle size. The bioethanol produced from acacia pulp was 16 499 tons per year, while that from eucalypt pulp was recorded as 117 216 tons per year.163

Similarly, Huo et al. also researched the use of hardwood plants for bioethanol production; plants chosen for this experiment included black locust, willow, and poplar.23 It was concluded that willow trees had the maximum cellulose and hemicellulosic content and the lowest lignin percentage composition, so it can serve best among all the hardwood plants for the generation of second-degree bioethanol production.164 One of the drawbacks of hardwoods is that their lignin content is generally higher, which is not favourable for the generation of bioethanol; therefore, this experiment suggested a promising use of willow tree as a lignocellulosic biomass.165 With the list going on, Song et al. also explored some facets of hardwoods for bioethanol production, where Chionanthus retusus, Zelkova serrata, Acer palmatum, Castanea crenata and Robinia pseudoacacia were sample woods that were chopped using a wood chopper. After that, the wood was pretreated by a chemical method using hydrogen peroxide-acetic acid. Subsequently, the pre-treated biomass was hydrolysed via cellulose and xylanase. P. stipites was used as a strain to separate the bioethanol that was produced, resulting in 12% more bioethanol production than obtained using conventional fermentation methods.166

Soft woods

Tandon et al. extended the scope of the investigation towards evergreen softwood plants that are used for furniture production. Softwoods were pre-treated via chemical methods and fermented using calcium chloride. Results were reported with different types of pre-treatments, and the enzyme hydrolysis yield spiked at 98% with the use of a sulphite pre-treatment method followed by the Organosolv method using an acidic catalyst, with 90% yield. Hence, it was summed up that by alternating the pre-treatment method used, the hydrolysis yield and bioethanol yields were greatly increased for softwoods.

Correspondingly, the concentration of the produced bioethanol via softwoods was also explored using mixed softwood sawdust waste (Cryptomeria japonica, Chamaecyparis obtusa). The mixture was pre-treated with muddy biomass via steam explosion at 25–55 atm for 5 minutes. Enzyme saccharification was favourable, with high steam pressure exhibiting maximum saccharification at 55 atm. SSF of a steam-explosion pre-treated sawdust mixture gave high concentrations of bioethanol, i.e. 66.7 g L−1. This concentration was very good and could be used on an industrial scale for future ethanol production from lignocellulosic biomass. Mikulski et al.166 extended this research to the use of microwave-assisted pre-treatment of softwood for bioethanol production using pine chips as a sample. The pine chips were pre-treated with microwaves combined with 0% w/v NaCS or 60% v/v EtOH, which helped obtain biomass with a high cellulose and low lignin content. This allowed extraction of biomass components to reach up to 46.5% and increased the production of bioethanol.

Organic waste

Achieving bioethanol production from different organic waste materials is gaining striking importance, as nothing is better than converting useless waste into energy to be consumed and fuel to run vehicles. Scientists came forward and devised a route to convert organic waste from agricultural, municipal, industrial and household sources into a fuel that could be of global benefit. Among the above-discussed categories of organic waste, agro-industrial wastes have been discussed previously with non-edible plants. The idea seems to offer a shining pathway to transform smelly, disease-causing, ecosystem-destroying waste that is being released into water bodies into something beneficial that serves humanity.

Agro-industrial waste, including raw residual coconut milk, pineapple juice, and tuna juice, was also used, with tuna juice and coconut milk having the highest carbohydrate concentration of 16 g L−1. Fermentation via yeast gave comparable ethanol production in all three samples at 36 hours, with 6.5% v/v in coconut milk, 8.39% v/v in pineapple juice, and 8.5% v/v in tuna juice, whereas the yield spiked at 48 hours with 20% v/v in coconut milk, 22% v/v in pineapple juice, and 12% v/v in tuna juice. Hence, studies have proved experimentally that these residues can serve as excellent sources for bioethanol production using the yeast S. cerevisiae.

Waste paper, such as exam sheets, newspapers, office papers, and pulp mill sludge, is pre-treated via a number of pre-treatment methods, and ethanol was produced, although the yield was not sufficiently higher. Acidic pre-treatments were used to deal with CaCO3 content in PPMs. Recycled paper sludge produced a maximum ethanol yield of 80.6% via the batch process/SSF technique.

Invasive and wild plant genera

Noxious invasive plant species that exist in Asian regions have the potential to infest millions of hectares, leading to a decline in crop production. The idea of utilising them to obtain biofuel laid the foundations for beneficial coping mechanisms.167,168Reynoutria x bohemica biomass, producing a 2.6 m3 ha−1 yield of bioethanol, provides evidence of employing invasive and wild plants as the best source of lignocellulosic biomass to obtain bioethanol. This remarkable yield provides a way to cope with threats imposed by such plants.169 The pre-treatment opened up the 21.5% lignocellulosic biomass of the raw material into hemicellulose-based saccharides, i.e., glucan, xylan, arabinan, acetyl groups, and Klason lignin as major products. To generate saccharides, 87% glucose was obtained by carrying out enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose by cellulase and β-glucosidase. Finally, saccharification followed by Saccharomyces cerevisiae-based fermentation resulted in 30 g L−1 of bioethanol as the final product.169,170

Another extensive study was undertaken by Borah and co-researchers on five invasive weeds, namely, Lantana camara, Arundo donax, Eichhornia crassipes, Mikania micrantha and Saccharum spontaneum, as feedstocks for bioethanol production. After acidic pretreatment (H2SO4) and detoxification of the acid hydrolysate by Ca(OH)2, H2SO4, and activated charcoal, in subsequent steps the residue was fed into a delignification step by ultrasonic cavitation techniques to remove 95% of the hemicellulose and the lignin part of the lignocellulosic biomass. Afterwards, enzymatic hydrolysis by cellulase and cellobiase gave 43.85 g/100 g of raw material fermentable sugars. Further respective protocols resulted in 27.36 g of bioethanol. The key advantage of this work was seen to be the simultaneous production of another advanced biofuel, i.e., biobutanol (17.96 g).

Invasive weeds have a major advantage that even new researchers can incorporate them in their laboratories to produce biofuels. In this context, a study revealed that freshwater hyacinth biomass (WHB) could undergo alkali pre-treatment with NaOH, and the hydrolysate could be collected after filtration.171,172 The hydrolysate was then submitted to enzymatic hydrolysis with xylanase and cellulase to yield 21.08 mg g−1 of reducing sugars and 236.56 mg g−1 of xylose. Pichia stipitis (fermenting yeast) was employed to carry out fermentation to yield 3.913 g L−1 of bioethanol. These studies show how a toxic genus of plant can be utilised to tackle worldwide energy issues. If proper attention is drawn to these techniques, the field of bioethanol production could be accelerated exponentially.173–175

Horticultural and landscaping waste

Deforestation to obtain wood and land has been seen to be increasing over the past few decades. Undoubtedly, this renders destructive outcomes to nature and habitat. One major issue that was being faced by the parties was horticultural waste resulting from deforestation. Turning a tree trunk into valuable furniture requires proper cutting that leads to a major proportion of wood going to waste. Since wood is considered to be a vital source of lignocellulosic biomass and affords a remarkable yield of bioethanol, the notion of utilising it to obtain biofuels is attractive. In this context, horticultural waste from Environmental Landscape Pte Ltd, Singapore, was taken and processed via the Organosolv pre-treatment method with ethanol at 70 °C for 1 hour. Such mild conditions effectively retained cellulose that then underwent enzymatic hydrolysis by cellulase to yield 25.06 g L−1 glucose after about 72 hours. S. cerevisiae then carried out conversion of the glucose at 12.39 g L−1. This study also showed that only 0.1 kg of horticultural waste is enough to generate 6.2 g of ethanol, even in the absence of commercial cellulase.

In 2021, Turner et al. revealed that perennial grass can also be employed to obtain high yields of bioethanol, as some species such as Miscanthus × giganteus have a dry weight (w/w) containing almost 23.8% lignin, 29.2% hemicelluloses, and 45.5% cellulose. So, acidic pre-treatment via 1% H2SO4 followed by cellulose-based enzymatic hydrolysis of Miscanthus cultivars resulted in 63.7–80.2% of glucose, which is one of the most revolutionary yields.176S. cerevisiae was used afterwards to convert glucose content into bioethanol, and it achieved 75.6% of bioethanol yield.177–179 Such high yields were achieved due to the early harvest of the substrate.180 In another study, two other genotypes of Miscanthus, named M. sinensis and M. sacchariflorus, along with Miscanthus × giganteus, were stabilised as biomass and achieved bioethanol yields of 234–253 g kg−1, 207–237 g kg−1 and 186–222 g kg−1, respectively. In 2022, Nugrahini et al. made use of landscaping flower waste (Chrysanthemum flower waste) to attain bioethanol. Sulphuric acid-based hydrolysis followed by S. cerevisiae fermentation yielded 13.53% bioethanol. They also found that by using these protocols, the bioethanol forms have 0.981 g mL−1 density, 12.711 gravity API, 0.981 specific gravity 10 422.2 kcal kg−1 caloric value. The findings of this study also revealed that the bioethanol that was obtained has a clear physical appearance, showing no impurity at all.181 Other genera of horticultural plants showed remarkable bioethanol yields.182 In Mesta crops, Hibiscus sabdariffa and Hibiscus cannabinus were studied by Lavanya et al. for the first time and revealed that 41.89–46.50% cellulose is exclusively present in these species. 38.71% of this cellulose was extracted by a cold alkali pre-treatment method, along with 27.27% lignin removal. Aspergillus niger SH3, a psychotolerant strain provided cellulase, whereas Pseudomonas lutea BG8 provided alpha-glucosidase for enzymatic hydrolysis. The hydrolysate was subjected to fermentation by S. cerevisiae, and 4.1 g L−1 of bioethanol was achieved.183

Agro-industrial by-product genera

The trends of agro-industries are set up to revolutionize daily life necessities. They have been rendering many great and valuable services, but the waste production from all the agro-industries is considered to be a hazard to different life forms in one way or another.184 Disposal of agro-industrial wastes is a very serious concern, but converting the waste into biofuel is the most reliable solution for this problem. The yield might be low, but optimization of conditions can enhance the productivity with time. In this regard, Gomes and co-workers conducted research on coconut cultivation wastes, e.g., coconut-tree leaf stalk (CLS), coconut-tree leaflets (CLL) and green coconut shells (GCS) to obtain bioethanol.

The biomass was subjected to two types of pre-treatments (hydrothermal treatment with distilled water and acidic treatment with H2SO4) separately. Enzymatic hydrolysis by Cellic Ctec2 released glucose from the wastes named above in yields of 45.39 g L−1, 21.31 g L−1 and 31.85 g L−1, respectively. Along with glucose, 7.93 g L−1 of xylose was also released. Kluyveromyces marxianus yeast was used on both hydrothermally pre-treated biomass and acid pre-treated biomass of CLS, CLL and GCS. The hydrothermally pre-treated biomass yielded 12.99 g L−1, 10.26 g L−1 and 8.83 g L−1 of bioethanol, respectively.185 On the other hand, acid-pretreated biomass of CLS, CLL and GCS yielded 7.44 g L−1, 7.01 g L−1 and 9.71 g L−1 of bioethanol, respectively.186 1.75 kg of coconut fruit is coir husk, which, in turn, contains 35% cellulose. This was submitted to multi-step pre-treatment methods and fed to enzymatic hydrolysis by cellulase. Afterwards, S. cerevisiae was used to carry out fermentation.

Different pretreated biomasses gave 0.19–0.25 mL of 2 G bioethanol. It was also suggested that Brazil produces 47.4 million m3 of 2 G ethanol each year from coconut husk. Although a sizable amount of its biomass is still inedible, the palm species known as coconut, or Cocos nucifera, has considerable agro-industrial value. In order to produce fermentable hydrolysates from the green coconut mesocarp using 18% (w/v) total solids (TS), a study investigated the optimisation of pretreatment and saccharification parameters, such as NaOH concentration, pretreatment duration, and enzyme load. Interestingly, hydrolysates retained the sugars dissolved during the alkaline pretreatment, since they were not detoxified.187 A decrease in enzyme loading from 15 to 7.5 FPU per g of biomass had no effect on the amount of ethanol produced in the end. Within 48 hours, hydrolysates with more than 7% (w/v) fermentable sugars were produced under ideal conditions. Using commercial strains of S. cerevisiae, further fermentation produced 3.73% (v/v) ethanol. Table 6 summarises more studies.

Bioethanol production from different sources

From the analyzed feedstocks, those based on sugar and starch gave higher, more stable ethanol yields than those from the lignocellulosic agricultural and forestry residues; the latter types are characterized by higher commercialization potential in the long run due to their availability and low cost. There are no treatment methods that could be considered as the most effective regardless of the raw material used; however, alkaline and combined methods seem to be effective for lignocellulosic waste, as they promote better delignification and enzyme accessibility, thus decreasing the recalcitrance of the biomass. When viewed from the commercialization perspective, agricultural residues and agro-industry waste can be promising because they are available, renewable, and may easily fit into existing biomass supply chains. It is also necessary to mention that the direct comparison between different yield values is complicated by the fact that the units used in the studies differ from each other. Therefore, the ideal situation would be to have information on ethanol yield expressed in the same units, such as grams of ethanol per gram of dry biomass. Ethanol concentration should be expressed as grams of ethanol in 1 L of the solution.

The high biomass loading leads to increased ethanol yield while, at the same time, resulting in increased slurry viscosity and a decrease in mass transfer, whereas the enzyme dosage would affect the saccharification degree. Similarly, the use of different yeast or bacterial strains may lead to variations in ethanol yield due to fermentation temperature, pH and tolerance to inhibitors. Reactor size should also be taken into account, since results obtained in small laboratory beakers cannot be applied directly to large-scale pilot or industrial manufacturing. Hence, in subsequent comparison studies, ethanol yields should be provided together with the biomass loading, enzyme dosage, strain, volume/scale of the reactor, and theoretical conversion efficiency. If applicable, ethanol yield should also be normalized considering the theoretical maximum based on fermentable carbohydrate content, so that comparison between different raw materials and processing technologies becomes more meaningful.217

Conclusion

The production of bioethanol is a sustainable option for the generation of fuel in order to save depleting fossil fuels and oil reservoirs. It can act as a substitute for petrol and alleviate the energy crisis in the coming decade, with reduced harmful emissions also reducing environmental pollution. An excellent source for the production of bioethanol under consideration recently is biomass that primarily consists of lignocellulosic contents. In this way, wheat straw, rice and wood extracts, and even household and industrial wastes rich in organic constituents can serve a greater purpose of fuel generation rather than lurk as a source of pollution. However, just like every coin's other aspect, the generation of ethanol from lignocellulosic biomass is not as flowery as it seems. This is an extensive process consisting of a series of steps spanning pre-treatment methods that require tremendous amounts of energy and high costs. Next to these methods is the phase in which detoxification begins and proceeds through hydrolysis, ending in fermented alcohol, which is ultimately refined. Bio-refineries require spacious setups and trained specialists to maintain optimum conditions, and they use genetically modified organisms for the utmost bioethanol yield. Intensive research is needed in the areas of pre-treatments to devise a combined pre-treatment method with the lowest energy consumption and minimal effects on the external environment. Strategies used to ferment bioethanol and refine it should be rooted in a way that simplifies the complexity along with reducing the time required to complete the process. In the coming years, we need to focus on the development of greener approaches, suitably modified microbes, and selection of environmentally friendly catalysts to make this approach worthy of the hype and potential.

Challenges

Bioethanol has tremendous potential to be used as a renewable and sustainable fuel source; however, there remain many technical, economic and operational issues associated with the commercial production of bioethanol from lignocellulosic materials that will continue to limit the production of bioethanol.

• The majority of these difficulties are due to the recalcitrant character of lignocellulosic materials. The three-dimensional structure of lignocellulosic materials (cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin) is highly complex and rigid, which prevents enzymes from gaining access to the lignocellulosic materials, thus inhibiting the hydrolysis of these materials into fermentable sugars. In order for enzymes to efficiently hydrolyse lignocellulosic materials into fermentable sugars, effective pretreatment of lignocellulosic materials will be required to change the three-dimensional structure through various forms of pretreatment.

• Another barrier to the production of bioethanol from lignocellulosic materials is that during the pretreatment of lignocellulosic materials, inhibitors can be produced. Different phenolic compounds and organic acids are typically produced from the degradation of lignocellulosic materials during pretreatment and may inhibit microbial growth. Therefore, detoxification of these inhibitors is typically necessary before fermentation, which adds complexity and time to the production process and increases costs associated with bioethanol production.

• Lignocellulosic biomass has a varying chemical composition due to many factors; i.e., the plant species it comes from, where it was grown, when it was harvested, and how it was stored. These factors create variability that directly impacts pretreatment process efficiencies and the number of sugars released during biomass pretreatment, resulting in inconsistencies in fermentation performance. Standardizing this pretreatment process will be difficult because of all these variations.

• The cost of collecting, transporting, storing, and processing large amounts of biomass adds significantly to the overall cost of producing lignocellulosic bioethanol. Converting current laboratory-scale processes into full industry-scale systems is still a challenge because of the equipment and energy requirements and the complexity of integrating the various processes. The low profitability on a per-gallon basis between lignocellulosic bioethanol and fossil fuels restricts the market opportunities for lignocellulosic bioethanol.

• Process intensification is a matter of concern, as independent hydrolysis, pretreatment, and fermentation stages increase energy consumption, the duration of the process, and operational costs. It is believed that SSF, SSCF, and CBP technologies can advance the effectiveness of the process. Even though high-solids fermentation can help increase ethanol concentration and decrease water consumption, challenges such as increased viscosity, unfavourable mixing, mass transfer issues, and reduced enzyme availability make it an imperfect method.

• Ethanol purification requires a lot of energy, as distillation is an energy-consuming process. There is a need for advances in membrane separation methods, pervaporation, and other low-energy technologies. Full-scale life-cycle assessments (LCA) should be conducted to identify environmentally significant points of biomass collection, pretreatment, fermentation, purification, and waste management with the aim of improving the carbon footprint. The cost of biomass supply chains can increase significantly due to feeding material collection, transportation, storage, seasonality, and variability in composition. Thus, logistics optimization and establishing decentralized preprocessing techniques are vital. Commercial implementation is restricted due to the considerable gap that exists between lab-scale effectiveness and industrial performance. A step-by-step approach from pilot-to commercial-scale is expected to close this gap.

Future prospects

Through creative developments in pre-treatment technologies,112,218–220 fermentation techniques,221–223 and bio-refinery integration,224 the generation of bioethanol from lignocellulosic biomass is expected to change in the future. The creation of economical and ecologically friendly pre-treatment techniques that effectively degrade the lignocellulosic structure while reducing the production of inhibitory chemicals will be one of the main areas of concentration. New green solvents that do not harm the environment, like traditional chemical pre-treatments, such as ionic liquids225 and nanomaterials,226 have the potential to improve biomass digestibility. Furthermore, by creating extremely selective and thermostable enzymes227 that can function in mild environments to maximise sugar release, enzymatic hydrolysis can be further optimised.228,229

Microbial engineering230,231 will be essential in fermentation to overcome inhibitor resistance and pentose sugar utilisation issues. The conversion rates of lignocellulosic sugars into ethanol may be further increased via co-culture fermentation, in which many microbial species cooperate.232 Additionally, combining consolidated bioprocessing (CBP)140,233,234 or simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF)235 will streamline operations and cut expenses and processing time.

In summary, ongoing research and development in feedstock utilisation, process optimisation, and regulatory support are critical to the future of lignocellulosic bioethanol. Bioethanol can become a practical and sustainable substitute for fossil fuels by overcoming the present technological and financial obstacles, greatly enhancing both environmental sustainability and global energy security.
