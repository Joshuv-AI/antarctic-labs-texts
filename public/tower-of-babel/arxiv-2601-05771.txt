Structural and extremal properties of l 1 l_{1} -Fiedler value
M. Rajesh Kannan, Rahul Roy

Abstract
The algebraic connectivity a ⁡ ( G ) a(G) , defined as the second smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix L ⁡ ( G ) L(G) , admits a well-known variational characterization involving the minimization of a quadratic form subject to an ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm constraint. In a recent work, Andrade and Dahl (2024) proposed an analogous formulation based on the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm, leading to the introduction of a new graph parameter b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) , referred to as the l 1 l_{1} -Fiedler value. In this article, we undertake a detailed investigation of the structural and extremal properties of b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) . We first derive a Nordhaus–Gaddum type inequality for b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) . For trees, we determine both global maximizer and minimizers of b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) , and present extremal constructions for trees with prescribed diameter, maximum degree, and number of pendant vertices. We further establish a connection between b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) and Laplacian matrices, and obtain a bound for b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) in terms of the edge connectivity, along with a complete characterization of the graphs attaining equality. We derive an explicit formula that describes the behaviour of b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) under the addition of pendant vertices. We also investigate the connection between b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) and the isoperimetric number.

Introduction

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Laplacian matrices of graphs play a fundamental role in spectral graph theory and have found wide-ranging applications in areas such as combinatorial optimization, electrical networks, graph signal processing, data science, and machine learning. In particular, the second smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix serves as a robust measure of the connectivity of a graph
[
6
]
. It also provides useful lower or upper bounds for several important graph invariants, including the isoperimetric number, maximum cut, independence number, genus, and diameter
[
10
,
11
,
13
]
. The associated eigenvector plays a central role in graph partitioning and data clustering, most notably in spectral clustering methods
[
13
,
14
]
. In his seminal work
[
6
]
, Fiedler introduced the term algebraic connectivity to describe the second smallest Laplacian eigenvalue.

The algebraic connectivity of a graph
G
, denoted by
a(G)
, admits a well-known variational characterization involving the
l_{2}
-norm in the objective function and the constraints. More recently, in
[
1
]
, Andrade and Dahl investigated analogous optimization formulations based on the
l_{1}
-norm and the
l_{\infty}
-norm. The resulting parameters are denoted by
b(G)
and
\gamma(G)
, respectively. They established a formula for
b(G)
in terms of the sparsest cut of
G
, which implies that computing
b(G)
is an NP-hard problem. In contrast, they showed that
\gamma(G)
can be computed via a linear programming formulation.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be an unweighted, undirected, and simple graph. The set
V(G)
contains the vertices, and
E(G)
contains the edges of
G
. The number of vertices, written as
|V(G)|
, is
n
. If two vertices
u
and
v
are adjacent, we write
u\sim v
. The
adjacency matrix
of
G
, denoted by
A(G)
, is the symmetric
n\times n
matrix whose
(u,v)
-entry is defined as

a_{uv}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }u\sim v,\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For a graph
G
, let
\Delta(G)
denote the
diagonal matrix
with the degrees of the vertices of
G
as its diagonal elements. The
Laplacian matrix

L(G)
is the
n\times n
matrix defined as

L(G)=\Delta(G)-A(G),

The eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix help in understanding several structural properties of a graph, such as whether it is bipartite or regular. They also give bounds for important graph parameters, including the chromatic number, clique number, and independence number
[
2
]
.
The eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix of a graph reveal information about the connectedness of the graph
G
. It is well known that
G
is connected if and only if
a(G)>0

[
6
,
7
]
.
An eigenvector corresponding to
a(G)
is called a
Fiedler vector

[
1
]
. The value
a(G)
can be computed using a standard optimization formula based on the Courant–Fischer theorem
[
8
]
. This formula is:

a(G)=\min\biggl\{\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}:\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}=0~\mbox{and}~\|x\|_{2}=1\ \biggl\},

where
\|x\|_{2}:=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}}
is the
l_{2}
-norm of the vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The vector
x
that gives the minimum in this expression has an important property: the squared differences
(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}
along the edges are as small as possible. This means that the values of
x
change in an optimal and smooth way across the edges of the graph. Andrade and Dahl
[
1
]
referred to this as the
\ell_{2}
-smoothing problem. They also studied similar optimization problems using the
\ell_{1}
-norm and the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm
[
1
]
. This led to the definition of two new graph parameters:

b(G)=\min\biggl\{\sum_{uv\in E(G)}|x_{u}-x_{v}|:\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}=0~\mbox{and}~\|x\|_{1}=1\biggl\},

\gamma(G)=\min\biggl\{\max_{uv\in E(G)}|x_{u}-x_{v}|:\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}=0~\mbox{and}~\|x\|_{\infty}=1\biggl\}.

Here,
\|x\|_{1}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|
denotes the
l_{1}
-norm, and
\|x\|_{\infty}:=\max\{|x_{i}|:1\leq i\leq n\}
denotes the
l_{\infty}
-norm of the vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. These optimization problems are well-defined because the constraint sets are compact and the objective functions are continuous. We refer to
b(G)
as the
l_{1}
-Fiedler value. The vectors that achieve the minima are called the
\ell_{1}
-Fiedler vector and the
\ell_{\infty}
-Fiedler vector, respectively.

The main objective of this article is to further investigate the quantity
b(G)
. We first establish a bound on
b(G)
in terms of the number of vertices and edges of a graph (Theorem
3.1
). Using this result, we derive a Nordhaus–Gaddum type bound for
b(G)
(Theorem
3.3
). For trees, we identify the global maximizer and minimizers for
b(G)
(Theorem
4.2
), and construct extremal examples for trees with prescribed diameter (Theorem
4.3
), maximum degree (Theorem
4.4
), and number of pendant vertices (Theorem
4.5
). A connection between
b(G)
and the Laplacian matrices of graphs is established in Theorem
5.1
. We then obtain a bound for
b(G)
in terms of the edge connectivity of
G
(Theorem
6.1
), and provide a complete characterization of the graphs for which this bound is sharp (Theorem
6.2
). Next, we derive a formula describing the change in
b(G)
under the addition of pendent vertices (Theorem
6.3
). In Section
6.2
, we investigate the relationship between the isoperimetric number and the quantity
b(G)
.

2
Preliminary results

We denote by
K_{n},C_{n},P_{n},
and
S_{n}
the complete graph, cycle graph, path graph, and star graph on
n
vertices, respectively. The degree of a vertex
v
in a graph is denoted by
\deg(v)
. A vertex
v
of a graph
G
is called a pendant vertex if
\deg(v)=1
. Let
d_{\min}(G)
denote the minimum vertex degree of
G
, and
d_{\max}(G)
is the maximum degree of any vertex in
G
. A graph
G
is said to be
regular
if all its vertices have the same degree. That is,
d_{\max}(G)=d_{\min}(G)
. The average degree of a graph
G
, denoted by
d_{\avg}(G)
, is the average of the degrees of all the vertices in
G
. It is given by:

d_{\avg}(G)=\frac{1}{|V(G)|}\sum\limits_{u\in V(G)}\deg(u).

If
G
has
n
vertices and
m
edges, then
d_{\avg}(G)=\frac{2m}{n}.

For a non-empty subset
S
of
V(G)
with
|S|<n
, the
cut
induced by
S
, denoted by
\partial S_{G}
, is
defined as follows:

\partial S_{G}=\biggl\{uv\in E(G):u\in S~\mbox{and}~v\in S^{c}\biggl\},

where
S^{c}=V(G)\setminus S
.
We write
\partial S
in place of
\partial S_{G}
when the underlying graph is clear from the context.

The
relative cut-size
is defined as

\xi(S)=\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}.

The
isoperimetric number
of
G
is defined as:

\iso(G)=\min_{S}\xi(S),

where the minimum is taken over all subset
S
of
V(G)
with
0<|S|\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor.
A set
S\subseteq V(G)
with
|S|\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
is
isoperimetric
if
\iso(G)=\xi(S).

The
edge density
or
sparsity
of a cut induced by
S
, denoted by
\rho(S)
, is defined as

\rho(S)=\frac{|\partial(S)|}{|S||S^{c}|}.

A cut is a
sparsest cut
if it minimizes
\rho(S)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. Then,

b(G)=\frac{n}{2}\min_{S}\rho(S),

where the minimum is taken over the non-empty subsets
S
of
V(G)
such that
S\neq V(G)
and both
S
and
S^{c}
induce connected subgraphs of
G
.

Next, we collect some of the known bounds on
b(G)
in terms of other graph parameters. The proofs can be found in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m

\lambda_{1}(G)
denote the largest eigenvalue of

. Then we have the following:

(a)

\frac{a(G)}{2}\leq b(G)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}(G)}{2}
.

(b)

\min\limits_{S}\xi(S)\leq b(G)\leq\frac{n}{2(n-1)}d_{\min}(G).

(c)

We can find the sparsest cut for trees explicitly as follows

is the subtree containing the vertex

, and
T_{v}=(V_{v},E_{v})
is the subtree containing the vertex

. An edge

\Bigl||V_{u}|-|V_{v}|\Bigl|\,
is the smallest possible.

Let

A
substar
in a tree

is a vertex-induced subgraph of
T
that is a star graph.

Theorem 2.4

(
[

)
.

Let

is calculated in

3
Nordhaus–Gaddum type bound

To start with, we derive an upper bound for
b(G)
in terms of the number of vertices and edges of the graph
G
, and characterize the extremal graphs.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
m
edges. Then

b(G)\leq\frac{m}{n-1}.

Furthermore, equality holds if and only if
G\cong K_{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle b(G)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2(n-1)}d_{\min}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2(n-1)}d_{\avg}(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2(n-1)}\frac{2m}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n-1}.

If equality holds in the above, then
d_{\min}=d_{\avg}
. That is, the graph
G
is regular.

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph. Then

b(G)=\frac{nr}{2(n-1)},

and, by Theorem
2.1
, the minimum edge density is
\frac{r}{n-1}
. Let
u
and
v
be two adjacent vertices, and
H
be the subset consisting only of the vertices
u
and
v
, along with the edge connecting them. Then

\rho(H)=\frac{2r-2}{2(n-2)}=\frac{r-1}{n-2}.

Since

\frac{r-1}{n-2}\geq\frac{r}{n-1},

we obtain

nr-n-r+1\geq nr-2r.

That is,
r\geq n-1
. Thus,
r=n-1
, and hence
G\cong K_{n}
.

If
G\cong K_{n}
, then, by Theorem
2.5
, we have

b(K_{n})=\frac{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2(n-1)}=\frac{m}{n-1}.

∎

From Theorem
3.1
, we can conclude that among all the connected graphs on
n
vertices, the complete graph
K_{n}
is the unique maximizer for
b(G)
. Next, we discuss the minimizers.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. Then
b(G)\geq b(P_{n})
, where
P_{n}
denotes the path graph on
n
vertices.

Proof.

Let
S
be a subset of
V(G)
that induces a sparsest cut. Then

b(G)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S|}{|S||S^{c}|}.

The minimum value of
b(G)
is obtained when the numerator is as small as possible and the denominator is as large as possible. Note that, the minimum value for
|\partial S|
is
1
, and the maximum value of
|S||S^{c}|
is
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
if
n
is even, and
\frac{n^{2}-1}{4}
if
n
is odd. These two conditions are simultaneously satisfied for the path graph
P_{n}
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Note that the path graph
P_{n}
is not a unique minimiser for
b(G)
. Consider a pendant vertex of
P_{n}
,
n\geq 6
. If this vertex is removed and attached to another vertex on the same side of the centre edge, then
b(G)
remains unchanged. In fact, later in Section
4
, we will see the construction of other minimisers for
b(G)
.

Note that for any graph
G
on
n
vertices, Theorem
3.1
implies that
b(G)\leq\frac{n}{2}
. The following result states that even the sum of
b(G)
and
b(G^{c})
is at most
\frac{n}{2}
. This is a Nordhaus-Gaddum-type bound for
b(G)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
vertices. Then

\frac{1}{2}<b(G)+b(G^{c})\leq\frac{n}{2}.

The equality on the right side holds if and only if
G\cong K_{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, we have
b(G)\leq\frac{m}{n-1}
and
b(G^{c})\leq\frac{1}{n-1}(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-m)
. Adding both inequalities gives the desired result. Equality holds if and only if
b(G)=\frac{m}{n-1}
and
b(G^{c})=\frac{1}{n-1}(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-m)
. By Theorem
3.1
,
G
must be
K_{n}
.

From Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.6
, we obtain

b(G)+b(G^{c})\geq\frac{a(G)+a(G^{c})}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

By Theorem
2.6
, the equality on the right side holds if and only if
G
or
G^{c}
is isomorphic to the join of an isolated vertex and a disconnected graph on
(n-1)
vertices.
Without loss of generality, let
G=\{v\}\vee H
where
H
is a disconnected graph on
(n-1)
vertices. Let
S\subseteq V(G)
induces a sparsest cut in
G
with
|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}
.
If
S
does not include the vertex
v
, then
|\partial S|\geq|S|
as every vertex of
S
is adjacent to
v
. If
S
includes
v
, then also
|\partial S|\geq n-|S|\geq|S|
. Therefore

b(G)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S|}{|S||S^{c}|}\geq\frac{n}{2}\times\frac{1}{|S^{c}|}>\frac{1}{2}.

Thus,

b(G)+b(G^{c})>\frac{1}{2}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the lower bound in the previous theorem is asymptotically tight, i.e., there exists a class of graphs for which the bound is achieved asymptotically as
n\rightarrow\infty
. For, consider the star graph on
n
vertices. Then,

b(S_{n})+b(S_{n}^{c})=b(S_{n})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2(n-1)},

as
S_{n}^{c}
is disconnected. Hence
b(S_{n})+b(S_{n}^{c})\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

4
Extremal Trees

In this section, we study extremal problems for trees. We begin by proving a strengthened version of Theorem
2.3
. The proof is similar to that of Theorem
2.3
in
[
1
]
, for the sake of completeness we include a proof here. Let
T
be a tree, and
uv\in E
. Define
T_{u}=(V_{u},E_{u})
and
T_{v}=(V_{v},E_{v})
are the two disjoint subtrees obtained by removing the edge
uv
such that
u\in V_{u}
and

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree. Let
uv\in E
. Then

b(T)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|V_{u}|}+\frac{1}{|V_{v}|}\right)

if and only if
uv
is a centre edge in
T
.

Proof.

Let
uv\in E
and

b(T)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|V_{u}|}+\frac{1}{|V_{v}|}\right).

Suppose that
uv
is not a centre edge of
T
. Then, there exists an edge
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
in
T
such that
\Bigl|\,|V_{u^{\prime}}|-|V_{v^{\prime}}|\Bigl|\,<\Bigl|\,|V_{u}|-|V_{v}|\Bigl|\,
. Since
|V_{u^{\prime}}|+|V_{v^{\prime}}|=|V_{u}|+|V_{v}|=n
, we must have
|V_{u^{\prime}}||V_{v^{\prime}}|>|V_{u}||V_{v}|
. Therefore

\frac{n}{2}\rho(V_{u^{\prime}})=\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{|V_{u^{\prime}}||V_{v^{\prime}}|}<\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{|V_{u}||V_{v}|}=b(T),

which is a contradiction.

Conversely, let
uv
be a centre edge in
T
. Then, by Theorem
2.1
,
b(T)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|V_{u}|}+\frac{1}{|V_{v}|}\right).
Suppose that
b(T)<\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|V_{u}|}+\frac{1}{|V_{v}|}\right).

Let
S\subseteq V
be such that both the subgraphs induced by
S
and
S^{c}
are connected in
T
, and
b(T)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S|}{|S||S^{c}|}.
Since connected subgraphs of a tree are also a tree, both the induced subgraphs are subtrees of
T
. Hence
|\partial S|=1
. Therefore,
S
and
S^{c}
are obtained by deleting an edge, say
wz
, from
T
. Without loss of generality, let
S=V_{w}
and
S^{c}=V_{z}.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{|V_{u}||V_{v}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|V_{u}|}+\frac{1}{|V_{v}|}\right)

\displaystyle>b(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S|}{|S||S^{c}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial V_{w}|}{|V_{w}||V_{z}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{|V_{w}||V_{z}|}.

Therefore
|V_{u}||V_{v}|<|V_{w}||V_{z}|
. Again, by using
|V_{w}|+|V_{z}|=|V_{u}|+|V_{v}|=n
, it follows that

\Bigl|\,|V_{w}|-|V_{z}|\Bigl|\,<\Bigl|\,|V_{u}|-|V_{v}|\Bigl|\,
, contradicts the fact that
uv
is a centre edge in
T
.
∎

.

We now prove a couple of results that facilitate the analysis of extremal trees with fixed diameter, maximum degree, and number of pendant vertices.

Lemma 4.1

Let
T=(V,E)
be a tree. Let

, and let the subtree

∎

Lemma 4.2

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

be a tree, and

In the following theorem, we study extremizers for trees with a fixed diameter.

{D+1}

∎

Let

In the theorem below, we discuss extremal trees when the maximum vertex degree is fixed.

5
Connection with Laplacian matrices

In this short section, we establish a connection between the quantity
b(G)
and the Laplacian matrix of the graph
G
. Note that, if
S
induces a sparsest cut, the associated
l_{1}
-
Fiedler vector

[
1
]
is given by

x_{v}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2|S|}&\mbox{if }v\in S,\\
-\frac{1}{2|S^{c}|}&\mbox{if }v\in S^{c}.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
S\subseteq V(G)
induce a sparsest cut in
G
. Let
x
be the associated
l_{1}
-Fiedler vector. Then we have the following:

(i)

\sum\limits_{u\in S}(L(G)x)_{u}=b(G),

(ii)

\sum\limits_{u\in S^{c}}(L(G)x)_{u}=-b(G)
,

where
L(G)
is the Laplacian matrix of
G
.

Proof.

Let
l_{ij}
denotes the
ij
-th entry of
L(G)
. For a vertex
u\in S
, let
|N_{S}(u)|
and
|N_{S^{c}}(u)|
represent the number of neighbors of
u
in
S
and
S^{c}
, respectively. Therefore,

\displaystyle(L(G)x)_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V(G)}l_{uw}x_{w}

\displaystyle=l_{uu}x_{u}+\sum_{w\neq u}l_{uw}x_{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{\deg(u)}{2|S|}-\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim u\\
w\in S\end{subarray}}x_{w}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim u\\
w\in S^{c}\end{subarray}}x_{w}\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\frac{\deg(u)}{2|S|}-\Bigl(\frac{|N_{S}(u)|}{2|S|}-\frac{|N_{S^{c}}(u)|}{2|S^{c}|}\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\frac{|N_{S^{c}}(u)|}{2|S|}+\frac{|N_{S^{c}}(u)|}{2|S^{c}|}

\displaystyle=|N_{S^{c}}(u)|\frac{n}{2|S||S^{c}|}.

By the symmetry, we get

b(G)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S^{c}|}{|S||S^{c}|}
.
So we have

(L(G)x)_{u}=b(G)\frac{|N_{S^{c}}(u)|}{|\partial S^{c}|}.

Similarly, if
u\in S^{c}
, we have

(L(G)x)_{u}=-b(G)\frac{|N_{S}(u)|}{|\partial S|}.

By summing the above expression over all vertices of
S
and
S^{c}
, respectively, we get the result.
∎

In this section, we establish connections between the quantity

and two fundamental graph parameters: the edge connectivity and the isoperimetric number of the graph.

6.1

The edge-connectivity of a graph

is the minimum number of edges whose removal disconnects

. In the following result, we establish an upper bound for

in terms of edge connectivity.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let

be a connected graph on

k
. Then

b(G)\leq\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}.

Proof.

If
G
is the complete graph
K_{n}
, then the result is immediate. Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, other than
K_{n}
. Let
S\subseteq V(G)
be such that
b(G)=\frac{n}{2}\rho(S)
. Add an edge between two non-adjacent vertices of
G
, and let
G_{1}
be the resultant graph. Then,

\displaystyle b(G_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G_{1}}|}{|S|(n-|S|)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G}|+1}{|S|(n-|S|)}

\displaystyle=b(G)+\frac{n}{2|S|(n-|S|)}

\displaystyle\leq b(G)+\frac{n}{2(n-1)}.

So if we add
k
edges to the graph
G
, then for the resulting graph
G_{k}
, we have
b(G_{k})\leq b(G)+\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}
.

Let
G
be a graph with edge-connectivity
k
. Then there exists a set of
k
edges such that the graph obtained from
G
by removing these edges, say
G_{-k}
, is disconnected. As
G_{-k}
is disconnected,
b(G_{-k})=0
. Thus,

b(G)\leq b(G_{-k})+\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}=\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}.

∎

Note that the above bound is tight. Among trees, equality holds only for star graphs (see Theorem
4.2
). The bound also holds with equality for the complete graph
K_{n}
. In the next theorem, we characterize the structure of all the graphs attaining this bound.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with edge-connectivity
k
, and define
G_{0}=G
. Then there exists a set of
k
edges such that by removing these
k
edges one after another, the resultant graph
G_{-k}
is disconnected. Let
G_{-s}
denote the graph obtained by deleting
s
edges from the graph
G
in this process, for
s\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. Then

b(G)=\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}

if and only if

(a)

there exists an isolated vertex
v
in
G_{-k}
such that adding all the
k
edges to
v
gives the graph
G
, and

(b)

in every step
\{v\}
induces a sparsest cut in each of the graph
G_{-k+i}
for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}.

Proof.

Let
v
be such an isolated vertex in
G_{-k}
. We add
k
edges and then obtain
G
. In the last step, also
\{v\}
induced a sparsest cut.
Therefore
b(G)=\frac{nk}{2(n-1)}
. Thus, we establish the sufficiency part.

To prove the necessary condition, in Theorem
6.1
we need the equalities to be held at every step as we add the edges to
G_{-k}
in reverse order. Let
S
induce a sparsest cut in
G_{-k}
. Consider the first step, if
b(G_{-k+1})=\frac{n}{2(n-1)}
, by Theorem
6.1
, we must have

b(G_{-k+1})=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G_{-k+1}}|}{|S|(n-|S|)},

(1)

\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G_{-k+1}}|}{|S|(n-|S|)}=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G_{-k}}|+1}{|S|(n-|S|)},

(2)

and

\frac{n}{2|S|(n-|S|)}=\frac{n}{2(n-1)}.

(3)

From (
3
), we have
S
must be a set with single vertex in
G_{-k}
, and it is an isolated vertex in
G_{-k}
. As equality holds in (
2
), we add an edge to
v
to get
G_{-k+1}
. From Equation (
1
), the vertex
v
induces a sparsest cut in
G_{-k+1}
.

Now it is easy to see that the very next edge we should add to
\{v\}
. If not, let the edge not contain
v
as an endpoint, then after removing
k-2
edges, it leads to
G_{-k+2}
, and removing the very first edge that we have added to
v
, makes the graph
G
disconnected. So,
G
has edge connectivity strictly less than
k
. Thus the result follows.
∎

A natural question that arises at this stage is how the parameter
b(G)
changes when new vertices are added to the graph. Next, we show that adding pendant vertices strictly decreases the quantity
b(G)
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
vertices. Let
G^{k}
be a graph obtained by adding
k
pendant vertex to the graph
G
. Then we have

b(G^{k})\leq b(G)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{(n+i)^{2}}\Bigl).

Proof.

First, we consider the effect of adding a pendant vertex
v
to
G
. Let the resultant graph be
G^{1}
. Let
S
induces a sparsest cut of
G
with
|S|\leq|S^{c}|
.

Case 1:
Let the neighbor of the new vertex
v
lie in
S
. Consider the set
S^{\prime}=S\cup\{v\}
. Then

\displaystyle b(G^{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S^{\prime}_{G^{1}}|}{|S^{\prime}|(n+1-|S^{\prime}|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{(|S|+1)(n-|S|)}.

Case 2:
Let the neighbor of the pendent vertex lie in
S^{c}
. Then

\displaystyle b(G^{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G^{1}}|}{(|S|)(n+1-|S|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{|S|(n+1-|S|)}.

From the last two cases, we have

b(G^{1})\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\max\{\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{|S|(n+1-|S|)},\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{(|S|+1)(n-|S|)}\}.

Note that if
|S|=|S^{c}|
then we have
n=2|S|
, which means
|S|(n+1-|S|)=(|S|+1)(n-|S|)
.

If
|S|<|S^{c}|
then we have
|S|(n+1-|S|)<(|S|+1)(n-|S|)
. Together we get

\displaystyle b(G^{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{|S|(n+1-|S|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{|\partial S_{G}|}{|S|(n-|S|)}\frac{(n-|S|)}{(n+1-|S|)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{2b(G)}{n}\frac{n-1}{n}

\displaystyle=b(G)(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}).

The desired result follows by repeating the process
k
times.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

Note that the upper bound can be attained; one such example is
S_{n}
where the pendant vertex is attached to the center of the star, resulting in creating
S_{n+k}
. It is easy to check that
S_{n}
is the only tree for which the inequality turns into equality(See Remark
4.1
).

In both corollaries above, we have
b(G)=\iso(G)
. The following results provide a method for constructing graphs where
b(G)
coincides with
\iso(G)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and
S
induces a sparsest cut with
|S|=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Then
S
is an isoperimetric set.

Proof.

Let
T\subseteq V(G)
with
|T|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Then we have

\frac{|{\partial S}|}{|S|(n-|S|)}\leq\frac{|{\partial T}|}{|T|(n-|T|)}.

Multiplying both side by
n-|S|
, we get

\frac{|{\partial S}|}{|S|}\leq\frac{|\partial T|}{|T|}\frac{(n-|S|)}{(n-|T|)}\leq\frac{|\partial T|}{|T|}.

Hence,
S
must be an isoperimetric set.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, where
n
is an even number. If
S
induces a sparsest cut with
|S|=\frac{n}{2}
, then
\iso(G)=b(G)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, we have that
S
is an isoperimetric set. Hence

\iso(G)=\frac{|{\partial S}|}{|S|}=\frac{n}{2}\times\frac{|\partial S|}{\frac{n}{2}|S|}=\frac{n}{2}\frac{|\partial S|}{(n-|S|)|S|}=b(G).

∎

Remark 6.3
.

From Theorem
6.6
, it is easy to see that if we take two graphs with an equal number of vertices and then join them by exactly one edge, the resultant graph
G
will satisfy
\iso(G)=b(G)
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
G
be a graph such that
S
is an isoperimetric singleton set. Then
S
induces sparsest cut in
G
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{v\}
. Then for any subset
T
of
V(G)
with
|T|\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we have

|\partial S|=\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}\leq\frac{|\partial T|}{|T|}.

But

\rho(T)=\frac{|{\partial T}|}{|T|(n-|T|)}\geq\frac{|{\partial S}|}{|S|(n-|T|)}\geq\frac{|{\partial S}|}{|S|(n-|S|)}=\rho(S).

Hence,
S
induces sparsest cut.
∎

We now derive a lower bound on
b(G)
in terms of the isoperimetric number for regular graphs.

Let
S\subseteq V(G)
, volume of
S
, denoted by
\vol S
is defined as follows:

\vol S=\sum_{u\in S}\deg(u).

The
Cheegar’s constant
of
G
is defined as follows
[
3
]
:

h(G)=\min_{S}\frac{|\partial S|}{\min(\vol S,\vol S^{c})}

Theorem 6.8

(
[
3
, Corollary 2.9]
)
.

For an r-regular graph
G
, we have

r\cdot h(G)\geq\inf\limits_{x}\frac{\sum\limits_{u\sim v}|x_{u}-x_{v}|}{\sum\limits_{u\in V(G)}|x_{u}|}\geq\frac{1}{2}r\cdot h(G),

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\sum_{u\in V(G)}x_{u}=0.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
G
be a regular graph. Then

b(G)\geq\frac{\iso(G)}{2}.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
r
-regular graph. Let
x
be an
l_{1}
-Fiedler vector. Then,

\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}=0,~~~~~~\sum_{v\in V(G)}|x_{v}|=1,~~~\mbox{and}~~~b(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}|x_{u}-x_{v}|.

Now,

\displaystyle h(G)

\displaystyle=\min_{S}\frac{|\partial S|}{\min(\vol\,S,\vol\,S^{c})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\min_{S}\frac{|\partial S|}{\min(|S|,|S^{c}|)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\min_{S}\Bigl\{\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}:|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}\Bigl\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\iso(G)}{r}.

Then, by Theorem
6.8
,

b(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}|x_{u}-x_{v}|\geq\frac{1}{2}r\cdot h(G)\sum_{v\in V(G)}|x_{v}|=\frac{r\cdot h(G)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\iso(G).

∎
