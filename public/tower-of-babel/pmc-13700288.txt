Value-based payment capability maturity model: a conceptual framework
Authors: José Crispim, Ana Moreira, Nazaré Rego
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo develop a Value-Based Payment Capability Maturity Model (VBP-CMM) that systematizes and guides healthcare organizations in transitioning from volume-based to value-based payment (VBP) systems, providing a structured framework for assessing, developing, and institutionalizing value-based capabilities.Data sourcesStudies published in sources indexed in PubMed, Scopus, or Web of Science (2015–2025), including conceptual or empirical research addressing the structure, implementation, or evaluation of VBP or alternative payment models (APMs).Study designA structured scoping literature review following JBI’s Population, Concept and Context (PCC) guidelines was conducted. An inductive thematic analysis identified core capabilities required for effective VBP management. These findings informed the conceptual development of a maturity model. An illustrative questionnaire and assessment approach were then proposed to demonstrate potential model operationalization.Principal findingsThe VBP-CMM structures capabilities progression across five maturity levels, from initial awareness to full integration, supporting continuous improvement. The model offers flexible and restrictive approaches to maturity progression, being adaptable to varied organizational realities. It enables systematic measurement of existing capabilities, prioritization of strategic actions, and alignment between financing, care delivery, and patient value. By diagnosing capability gaps and guiding targeted development, the VBP-CMM can institutionalize value-based approaches in healthcare, fostering sustainable and equitable improvements in clinical outcomes and patient experience. Future research should focus on test model implementation, standardized metrics development, systemic influences evaluation, training, cost analysis, and stakeholder engagement.ConclusionsThe proposed model provides healthcare organizations, particularly hospitals, with a systematic and customizable framework to build and evolve VBP capabilities, supporting the broader adoption of value-based healthcare. The accompanying questionnaire and assessment logic should be interpreted as supporting tools for future application and validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00807-y.

Introduction

The global expansion of interest in Value-Based Healthcare (VBHC) reflects a fundamental paradigmatic transition in the delivery and financing of healthcare systems [1]. Nonetheless, the definition of “value” within healthcare remains contested, owing to its intrinsically multidimensional nature that extends beyond clinical outcomes to encompass patient-centredness, empowerment, and shared decision-making [2, 3]. Porter’s value agenda [4] continues to constitute a seminal theoretical foundation; however, it has been subject to critique for its limited operational guidance on utilising outcomes for quality improvement, insufficient attention to the cultural transformation required, and lack of a comprehensive strategy to enable a systemic shift from volume to value [5]. In this context, Value-Based Payment (VBP) models have become central to the implementation of VBHK. These models aim to realign financial incentives with quality and efficiency by tying reimbursement to performance metrics [6, 7]. Various VBP models, ranging from bundled payments to shared savings and population-based schemes, have differed in scope, design, and risk-sharing mechanisms [8, 9]. Although VBP models have demonstrated improvements in clinical outcomes and cost-efficiency, implementing them is complex and often hindered by administrative burden, stakeholder resistance, and potential inequities [10, 11, 12]. Frameworks have been proposed to guide VBP implementation in diverse contexts, including oral health [13], state-level policy environments [14], and healthcare system-wide reforms [15]. Yet, the absence of a unified, consensus-based framework limits the translation of evidence into scalable practice.

To move beyond fragmented analyses and toward strategic advancement in VBHC financing, a structured framework is necessary to assess organizational capabilities and guide the implementation of VBP models. In this context, the development of a VBP Capability Maturity Model (VBP-CMM) is essential for healthcare systems, particularly hospitals, to effectively transition from volume-based to value-based care. In this study, VBP is approached primarily from the perspective of healthcare provider organizations, particularly hospitals, as actors that must develop internal capabilities to design, implement, govern, and sustain value-based payment arrangements.

The foundational structure for most maturity models is the Capability Maturity Model (CMM), originally developed to help organizations select improvement strategies by assessing process maturity and identifying critical development areas [16]. The proposed VBP-CMM is theoretically driven as it is not based on primary empirical data but rather on a structured synthesis of findings from the published scientific literature on VBP models. Grounded in a systematic scoping review (ScR) of this evidence, the model organizes key capabilities into progressive maturity levels, providing a conceptual roadmap for healthcare institutions seeking to adopt or enhance VBP approaches. The ScR maps all available evidence from the literature published with the aim of answering the following question: What are the key elements and capabilities related to the design, implementation, and governance of VBP models?

This study primarily aims to develop a conceptual VBP-CMM grounded in a structured scoping review of the literature. To demonstrate the practical applicability of the resulting framework, the study also includes an illustrative operationalization through a draft questionnaire and an example of capability assessment logic. These supporting tools are not presented as fully validated standalone instruments, but rather as preliminary mechanisms to show how the conceptual model may be applied in organizational settings.

The ScR was conducted following the JBI’s Population, Concept and Context (PCC) guidelines [17] and is reported through the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses – Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [18].

Maturity models (MMs) assess the evolution of processes, capabilities, and technologies within organizations, promoting gradual and systematic improvements. Most MMs have been designed in the context of information systems. These models are often generic and thus misaligned with the specificities of the healthcare sector, such as regulation, clinical integration, and sensitive data management [19–21]. Assessing maturity in complex domains, such as healthcare, requires adapted models that can act as intermediaries between general abstractions and practical applications [22].

A few healthcare-specific MMs are emerging: (1) the Digital Health Capability Maturity Model [21], developed to overcome the limitations of traditional models such as CMMI, which mainly prioritize technical metrics, including interoperability and data management; this model integrates communication, engagement, and governance as central domains, promoting a more holistic assessment of digital health maturity; (2) a MM to evaluate the maturity of omnichannel strategy adoption in healthcare services [20] to guide institutions toward sustainable and effective omnichannel implementation, a continuous process requiring monitoring, strategic adjustments, and integration across strategy, processes, systems, data, and people; (3) a MM to assess the implementation of circular economy practices in operating rooms [23]; (4) MM framework for integrated virtual care to support remote patient monitoring [24]; and (5) a MM to assess business intelligence [22].

Studies on MMs in healthcare have predominantly been conceptual, except for Sgambaro et al. [23], who provide exploratory empirical evidence by applying the model in a real-world context. That application required adaptation to the setting through partnerships with clinicians, managers, and frontline teams.

Although the application of MMs in healthcare has been growing, in alignment with digital transformation and the need for sustainability, a model for value-based healthcare implementation has not been available yet. The proposed theoretical CMM is comprehensive, integrating a data collection instrument with a fuzzy multi-criteria method for data aggregation, as demonstrated through an illustrative example. It offers a robust foundation for further research, practical validation, and policy development in healthcare payment reforms.

Method

Search strategy

This study explores published structured frameworks and models for designing and implementing VBP models. It outlines the key components, development phases, core activities, essential capabilities, resources, and other elements involved in both the design and execution of these models. The research is based on a ScR conducted to map the existing literature to develop a theoretical CMM. The search strategy followed the PRISMA-ScR [18] and the JBI’s PCC guidelines [17] (Population → Healthcare organizations/hospitals; Concept → Competencies, maturity, capability models; Context → Value-Based Healthcare / VBP). The review was designed to identify capabilities relevant to the organizational implementation of VBP, with healthcare organizations and hospitals as the primary unit of analysis. ScRs are well-suited for systematically identifying, evaluating, and synthesizing relevant literature in emerging fields, such as VBP. The literature search was conducted across three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, using the terms “value-based payment,” “value-based reimbursement,” “healthcare,” and “conceptual model,” as adapted for each database as needed. Boolean operators were used to maximize the specificity and relevance of the results (details are available in the supplementary materials). Studies were included if they examined the structure, development, implementation, or evaluation of VBP or alternative payment models (APMs). Both conceptual frameworks and empirical analyses addressing components, processes, resources, or capabilities relevant to the design and operationalization of VBP were considered, regardless of the healthcare setting, including primary care, emergency services, and hospice care. Only studies published between 2015 and 2025 and written in English were included, including peer-reviewed publications and analytical grey literature. Articles focused exclusively on fee-for-service models, opinion pieces without empirical or conceptual analysis, and studies not substantively addressing key elements of VBP or APM design and implementation were excluded. Study selection began with screening titles and abstracts for relevance, followed by full-text eligibility assessment independently performed by two reviewers (AM and JC). Classification was also independently conducted, with disagreements resolved through joint discussion and consensus. A PRISMA flow diagram summarizes the study selection process (Fig. 1). Data extraction was undertaken systematically using a standardized template capturing key study attributes, including bibliographic details, study aims, identified VBP components, required resources, core capabilities, and steps involved in design and implementation. Relevant elements were identified from explicit statements and contextual evidence, sometimes inferred from methodological descriptions or conceptual frameworks. The principal investigator performed the initial data extraction, which the other authors reviewed. Any discrepancies were resolved through collaborative discussion until a complete consensus was reached.

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis is a widely utilized method for examining qualitative data, characterized by systematically identifying, analyzing, and reporting recurring patterns or themes within a dataset [25]. While commonly applied to empirical data such as interviews or focus groups, thematic analysis has also been effectively employed for analyzing scientific literature in constructing theoretical models, owing to its flexible and interpretative nature. Braun and Clarke [25] describe thematic analysis as a method that facilitates the descriptive summarization of data and the deeper interpretative understanding of its meanings through code selection and theme development emerging from the sources under analysis. A theme is a “patterned response or meaning” pertinent to the research question and from the data. It is important to distinguish between themes and categories: while categories serve to organize the manifest content of the data descriptively, themes require a higher level of abstraction and integration, revealing more comprehensive and interpretative meanings [26]. A main feature of thematic analysis is its epistemological flexibility, allowing it to be applied across various theoretical and methodological frameworks [27]. This versatility is particularly suitable for integrative and systematic reviews, especially in contexts that aim to develop conceptual models or analytical frameworks, such as maturity models. The identification of themes is not contingent on the frequency of occurrence of an idea, but instead on its analytical relevance and capacity to elucidate the research question [26]. The conducted inductive approach emphasizes themes emerging directly from the data without being constrained by pre-existing theoretical frameworks [28]. This approach is particularly advantageous when the objective is to capture a broad and spontaneous range of meanings and is well-suited for identifying capabilities to be validated through maturity model development. Each theme must encompass internally consistent data and be sufficiently distinct from others to justify its separation [29]. In the present study, coding was performed based on the capabilities necessary for managing the design, implementation, and governance of VBP models. These capabilities were organized into themes and subthemes designated as focus areas and key components, respectively. The coding process, along with the definition of themes (focus areas) and subthemes (key components), was an iterative and independent process conducted by two reviewers (NR and JC).

Research design

The study comprises two analytically distinct contributions: (1) the conceptual development of the VBP-CMM from the literature, and (2) a proposed operationalization approach for assessing organizational maturity using structured data collection and multicriteria aggregation. The design of this study followed a multi-phase approach to develop and operationalize a value-based payment (VBP) maturity model. In Phase 1, the structure of the model was defined through an extensive literature review and thematic analysis. This process identified and consolidated the hierarchical levels of the model, beginning with (a) the definition of the main themes, referred to as focus areas; (b) the identification of key components of VBP associated with each focus area; (c) the specification of core activities required for the implementation of these components; and (d) the determination of the organizational capabilities necessary to execute the activities. In cases where the literature presented gaps, ambiguities, or excessive aggregation, deductive reasoning and expert discussion were employed to infer or clarify missing elements. In Phase 2, a set of instruments was developed to enable data collection in the context where the maturity model is intended to be applied. These included a mapping table linking activities to capabilities, the definition of maturity levels to be adopted within the model, and a diagnostic questionnaire designed to capture the current capability levels within the organization. Finally, Phase 3 focused on testing the practical application of these instruments and discussing strategies for the model’s operationalization—specifically, identifying the most suitable methods for gathering information on existing competencies and their levels of development (e.g., a) respondent perceptions, b) review of internal documentation and reports, and c) analysis of information systems) and determining how best to aggregate this information to derive an overall maturity score or level. Main themes

In the literature analysis, activities, capabilities, and VBP components were identified as distinct yet interrelated elements. Activities correspond to the core tasks carried out within payment reforms, while capabilities reflect the organizational and human competencies required for their effective execution. VBP components, in turn, represent the structural mechanisms through which these reforms are operationalized. Through thematic analysis, these three elements were integrated into higher-order themes, capturing coherent and conceptually consistent dimensions of value-based payment implementation. This methodological approach provides a structured framework linking practical actions, enabling capacities, and system-level components to the key conceptual dimensions of the reform.

From key components to core activities and associated capabilities

The literature highlights that the successful development and implementation of value-based payment (VBP) models rely on an articulated set of core activities, structural components, and organizational capabilities. A total of 28 core activities were identified, with the most prominent being system and organizational assessment [8, 30, 32, 33, 35, 38] and the definition of quality and performance metrics [15, 37, 39, 40]. Other critical activities include analyzing implementation drivers, defining the target population and scope of services, designing the payment model architecture, and planning the reform’s execution [8, 15, 30, 34, 35, 38]. Complementarily, the literature identifies 17 key components to sustain VBP models. Among the most recurrent are care coordination [30, 34, 38], equity strategies [13, 35], payment model alignment [13, 35], and quality measurement through validated indicators such as PROMs [13, 30]. Other components highlight the importance of participatory governance, stakeholder engagement, accountability mechanisms, and the promotion of cost-effective innovation [8, 15, 34, 38]. These activities and components are sustained by critical capabilities spanning strategic, organizational, clinical, and patient-centered dimensions. Strategic leadership and governance emerged as the most frequently mentioned capability, stressing vision, goal alignment, and reform leadership [8, 32, 39]. Other capabilities include adaptive implementation and organizational learning [8, 32], the use of design thinking for iterative model refinement [33, 41], and stakeholder-aligned strategies and engagement [32, 34]. Evidence-based clinical practice, patient-centered care coordination, and patient empowerment were also highlighted as determinants of success [33, 34]. Although some key components are conceptually related, they were retained as analytically distinct because they represent different organizational functions within VBP implementation. For example, participatory governance refers to formal decision-making structures, inclusion mechanisms, and shared accountability in governance processes, whereas stakeholder engagement refers to the active mobilization and alignment of actors during implementation and care delivery. Likewise, innovative care delivery concerns the design or adoption of new service delivery approaches, whereas care coordination refers to the operational capability to connect providers, teams, and services in daily practice. These distinctions were maintained to preserve the diagnostic specificity of the maturity model. For more details, please see Table 1.

Value-based payment capability maturity model

Maturity model structure

The proposed VBP-CMM can support organizations (particularly hospitals) in systematically building and advancing the capabilities needed for effective VBP reforms. This model serves as a framework to assess current capability levels and guide structured progression, focusing on continuous improvement in strategic, organizational, clinical, and patient-oriented dimensions of VBP implementation. Our comprehensive ScR provided a foundation for structuring the CMM around four core elements: maturity focus areas, key implementation factors, maturity capabilities, and maturity progression levels. It also guided the design of our model within a phased implementation framework (Fig. 4) that comprises the following main phases: (1) the structure model (focus areas, key components, and capabilities), (2) the data collection instrument, and (3) the capabilities assessment tool (aggregation methods and levels of maturity). The VBP-CMM main structure resulting from the thematic analysis is presented in Fig. 5.

Following the identification of themes in the literature, these were organized into five focus areas and 17 key components (Fig. 3). A focus area is a well-defined partition of a functional domain: (1) Strategic Foundations and Governance incorporates high-level (strategic) decisions related to the development of strategy, governance, and value creation practices; (2) Population and Equity-Centered Design includes multilevel (strategic and operational) decisions aimed at aligning payment and care delivery models with the needs of the population, promoting equity, population risk focus, and user-centeredness; (3) Model and Service Architecture incorporates multilevel (strategic and operational) decisions that shape how payment models and healthcare services are structured, financed, and adjusted with a focus on value; (4) Integrated Care Delivery involves clinical and organizational decisions related to the coordinated, continuous, and efficient delivery of healthcare services; and Measurement, Evaluation, and Learning involves operational decisions that systematically use data and evidence to monitor, evaluate, and learn from organizational performance.

Each maturity key component functions as a specific indicator, assessed based on its level of implementation within the corresponding focus area. Consequently, these key components form the foundation of the maturity assessment elements used in the evaluation tool. The allocation of key components to the focus areas ensures that each area is associated with a set of core activities and capabilities (Table 1). The level of implementation of these capabilities determines the value of the respective key factor and, through a final aggregation process, the overall maturity stage of the organization.

Maturity levels

Regarding the maturity levels for classifying organizations and guiding future decisions and investments in capabilities, we suggest five levels (Table 2): (1) Initial; (2) Value definition; (3) Design; (4) Implementation; (5) Scaling and Integration. The number of levels is common in similar maturity models; for example, in the few MMs identified in healthcare, the levels ranged from 4 [24] to 7 [21]. Having more levels enables a more refined evaluation that captures progression, but it can also introduce greater complexity and benchmarking challenges. Capabilities’ assessment tool

Linguistic Z-number

Natural language is frequently employed to represent real-world information precisely because of its inherent imprecision, characterized by both fuzziness and partial reliability. Fuzziness pertains to using soft, non-binary constraints in the valuation of variables, whereas partial reliability concerns the degree of confidence in the knowledge or intuition from which such information is derived. To jointly address these two dimensions of uncertainty, Zadeh [46] proposed the Z-numbers, which formalize uncertain information as an ordered pair Z = (A, B), where A expresses the constraint (i.e., the fuzzy restriction on a variable) and B is the reliability of A. Importantly, Z-numbers do not merely consist of two independent fuzzy sets; instead, they represent a structured framework incorporating an underlying probability distribution that links the two elements, thereby representing human reasoning under uncertainty. Building on this framework, Wang et al. [47] introduced the linguistic Z-number (LZN), in which both A and B are expressed in qualitative linguistic terms rather than quantitative values. For instance, a Z-number applied to a capability might take the form (“Established”; “very likely”), indicating that the capability at stake is considered well-developed, and there is a high level of confidence in this judgement. This approach enables experts to provide evaluations grounded in domain knowledge without the burden of precise quantification, thereby aligning the model more closely with real-world expert judgement. The use of LZNs in a maturity model supports evaluators in expressing the perceived existence of capabilities within an organization and the degree of confidence in these perceptions. While a percentage score facilitates straightforward aggregation of responses, it does so at the cost of granularity and contextual adaptation. In contrast, Linguistic Fuzzy Numbers (LFNs) offer richer informational content but introduce higher computational complexity, particularly during the aggregation phase. This trade-off underscores the practical relevance of LZNs in improving the interpretability and decision-making value of models dealing with uncertain or subjective expert input.

Fuzzy Z-TOPSIS

The Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), developed by Hwang and Yoon [49], is a well-established method for addressing multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) problems using crisp (i.e., precise) data. When the evaluation involves input from multiple experts assessing several alternatives across diverse criteria, the process becomes one of multi-criteria group decision-making (MCGDM). Among MCDM techniques, TOPSIS is particularly valued for its conceptual clarity, computational simplicity, and capacity to rank alternatives based on their relative closeness to an ideal solution. The underlying rationale is that the best alternative should simultaneously exhibit the minimum distance from the positive ideal and the maximum distance from the negative ideal. To accommodate uncertainty and imprecision in expert evaluations, especially when responses are formulated as Z-numbers, a modified version known as Z-TOPSIS has been developed [50]. This adaptation is especially relevant in contexts where both the fuzziness of assessments and the reliability of information sources must be considered. The present study selected Z-TOPSIS due to its ability to integrate expert judgements expressed as LZNs while maintaining a structured and interpretable decision-making process. Z-TOPSIS facilitates the aggregation of expert input through a transparent, stepwise methodology. This study compares organizational capabilities to ideal reference points, enabling decision-makers to identify which capabilities are furthest from the ideal and guiding prioritization of investments in capability development. This targeted approach supports more efficient allocation of resources in the quest for organizational maturity. An example using synthetic responses from 10 hypothetical experts (Appendix B) was used to describe the steps of Z-TOPSIS (Appendix A).

Illustrative example

In relation to the percentage type, the aggregation of the responses was carried out through a weighted average based on each expert’s weight. The final average yielded 65.98%, corresponding to a maturity level of 3 - Design. In turn, considering uncertainty and reliability in the responses using the Z-TOPSIS approach resulted in a lower outcome of 0.51, corresponding to level 2 – Value definition.

If progression between levels depends on achieving specific capabilities, advancement requires all experts (assumed to be managers) to rate at least E – Established for the capability to be considered attained. In this example, moving to level 2 requires the capabilities assessed in level 1 be attained, which is not the case, so the organization remains at maturity level 1 – Initial.

The approach to follow to interpret the results should be selected carefully. Each organization should choose the approach that best reflects its reality: a more restrictive approach, which considers advancement in the maturity levels only if specific capabilities are present, or a more flexible one, which proceeds with the implementation of the models even if some capabilities are still weak. In any case, knowledge about the organization’s capabilities is obtained.

Conclusion

The proposed VBP-CMM contributes to systematizing and guiding healthcare organizations through the transition from volume-based to value-based payment systems. The model is structured around five critical focus areas and 17 key components that span strategic, operational, clinical, and patient-centered dimensions. Each component is a specific capability indicator, enabling a granular evaluation adaptable to the organization’s context. Furthermore, the model operates across five progressive maturity levels, from initial awareness to complete integration, supporting continuous improvement based on defined capabilities. Clinical condition context acts as a major contingency factor in VBP design and maturity. A payment strategy that is appropriate for a discrete, well-defined, and measurable episode may be unsuitable for more heterogeneous, chronic, or multimorbid conditions. Therefore, maturity in VBP requires not only general organizational readiness, but also condition-sensitive design and implementation capability.

From a practical perspective, the VBP-CMM is intended for use by healthcare organizations, particularly hospitals and integrated provider networks, as well as policymakers and purchaser organizations seeking to assess readiness for value-based payment implementation. The model may be used in different ways depending on organizational maturity and purpose: as a diagnostic framework for identifying capability gaps, as a strategic planning tool for prioritizing capability development, or as a comparative structure for monitoring progress over time. In early-stage applications, the model may be used qualitatively to support managerial reflection and stakeholder dialogue. In more advanced applications, the accompanying assessment instrument may support structured measurement, provided that future empirical validation is conducted. For that the VBP-CMM incorporates a data collection instrument and methods for information analysis, such as fuzzy linguistic numbers, which enhance the CMM’s applicability in the face of uncertainties inherent to the assessment process. The possibility of adopting either a flexible or restrictive approach to maturity level progression enables different organizational realities to find the model a practical and realistic tool. In this sense, the VBP-CMM facilitates the measurement of an organization’s existing capabilities and shapes strategic decisions for institutional development, reinforcing the alignment between financing, care delivery, and the value generated for patients.

The main limitations of this study stemmed from: (a) the difficulty in selecting the final set of articles due to the broad range of topics within value-based studies; although the followed methods aimed at identifying all relevant studies, there is a possibility that some important study may have been left out of our screening; (b) in an attempt to develop a theoretical maturity model that could serve as a comprehensive foundation for the management of VBP models (i.e., regardless of geographic region or the nature and type of healthcare organization), and intending to keep the statements as close as possible to those presented by their original authors, we may have incurred a high degree of generalization (vagueness) in the definition of the capabilities; and (c) there may have been some interpretation bias when analyzing the literature. The proposed questionnaire and aggregation approach should be interpreted as preliminary operational tools. They were developed to illustrate how the conceptual model may be translated into practice, but they have not yet been empirically validated. Future studies should test their feasibility, reliability, and usefulness across different organizational contexts.
