Efficient Rare-Event Simulation for Random Geometric Graphs via Importance Sampling
Sarat Moka, Christian Hirsch, Volker Schmidt, Dirk Kroese

Abstract
Random geometric graphs defined on Euclidean subspaces, also called Gilbert graphs, are widely used to model spatially embedded networks across various domains. In such graphs, nodes are located at random in Euclidean space, and any two nodes are connected by an edge if they lie within a certain distance threshold. Accurately estimating rare-event probabilities related to key properties of these graphs, such as the number of edges and the size of the largest connected component, is important in the assessment of risk associated with catastrophic incidents, for example. However, this task is computationally challenging, especially for large networks. Importance sampling offers a viable solution by concentrating computational efforts on significant regions of the graph. This paper explores the application of an importance sampling method to estimate rare-event probabilities, highlighting its advantages in reducing variance and enhancing accuracy. Through asymptotic analysis and numerical studies, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our methodology, contributing to improved analysis of Gilbert graphs and showcasing the broader applicability of importance sampling in complex network analysis.

Introduction

Random geometric graphs have emerged as powerful mathematical models for representing spatially embedded networks in various fields such as wireless communication, sensor networks, and materials science, see e.g.
Baccelli and Błaszczyszyn (2001)
;
Baccelli and Błaszczyszyn (2009a)
;
Baccelli and Błaszczyszyn (2009b)
;
Baumeier et al. (2012)
;
Franceschetti and Meester (2007)
;
Kenniche and Ravelomananana (2010)
;
Thiedmann et al. (2009)
.
These graphs are defined by distributing nodes at random in a metric space, connecting pairs of nodes within a certain distance threshold, and forming a network that captures spatial relationships. In particular, in this paper, we consider random geometric graphs on a
d
-dimensional subset of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any fixed integer
d\geq 1
, where a graph is created by a collection of (random) points with an edge between any two points that are within the (Euclidean) distance of one length unit from each other. Such a graph is also known as Gilbert graph
(
Gilbert, 1961
)
. For a wide and deep mathematical treatment of Gilbert graphs, we refer the reader to
Penrose (2003)
.

Accurate estimation of key characteristics in Gilbert graphs, such as the mean values of the size of the largest component or the maximum degree, is crucial for understanding the behavior and performance of systems modeled by these graphs. However, except for a few isolated special cases, such fundamental characteristics cannot be computed in closed form. On the other hand, Monte Carlo simulation has become a central tool in the study of such networks; see e.g.
Baccelli and Błaszczyszyn (2010)
;
Błaszczyszyn et al. (2013)
, where the simplicity of the Gilbert graph model makes it possible to estimate the typical behavior of large random networks by standard Monte Carlo simulation to a very high precision. However, in many applications, it is not enough to know the average case. We need to understand how the system behaves with respect to rare events, where a rare event is an event that occurs infrequently but can have a significant impact. For instance, in the context of telecommunication networks, it is not enough that the network provides good service on average; rather, users expect it to work well with very high probability. These challenges have been the motivation for questions of rare-event probabilities in spatial random networks, which is now a vibrant research field. While the network models encountered in practice are more complex than the Gilbert graph, we believe that our results represent a first step toward rare-event analysis of more realistic spatial random networks, with significant potential in combining the importance-sampling schemes developed in this paper with large-deviation principles.

To understand the scope and complexity of such research problems, we first note that investigating rare events is a challenging topic even for basic random graph models that do not involve any form of geometric information, such as the Erdős-Rényi graph. For example, there is a series of papers that investigate probabilities of large deviations for the number of triangles in this type of graphs, see
Ganguly et al. (2024)
;
Chakraborty et al. (2026)
;
Stegehuis and Zwart (2023)
. Also, the largest connected component was recently investigated, see
Andreis et al. (2023)
;
Jorritsma et al. (2026)
. While
Andreis et al. (2023)
deals with only the Erdős-Rényi graph, the more recent paper by
Jorritsma et al. (2026)
considers more general (kernel-based) random graphs.

However, for Gilbert graphs, rare events are harder to analyze. For instance, while it is easy to analyze the probability of having atypically many edges in the Erdős-Rényi graph, this problem is difficult in the case of Gilbert graphs
(
Chatterjee and Harel, 2020
)
. Loosely speaking, the large deviations are governed by a condensation effect, i.e., the most likely reason for observing too many edges is to have a larger number of nodes in a small area that gives rise to a clique. In contrast, the behavior in the regime of lower large deviations is completely different. There, the most likely reason to observe too few edges comes from consistent changes throughout the sampling window
(
Hirsch et al., 2020
)
. Only very recently it became possible to understand the rare-event behavior of the largest connected component in spatial models of complex networks
(
Andreis et al., 2021
)
.

In the light of these challenges, the overall aim of our paper is to show the effectiveness of the powerful technique of importance sampling for the purpose of rare-event analysis in the context of the Gilbert graph. More precisely, we pursue the following goals:
(i) We propose conditional Monte Carlo and importance sampling estimators for a variety of rare events in the Gilbert graph such as the question whether edge count, maximum degree or clique count are below a fixed threshold. (ii) While these estimators are of a general abstract form, we present a specific grid-based scheme, which we show is easy to implement.
(iii) Our paper is the first one which gives an estimator with bounded relative error in the context of the Gilbert graph. More precisely, in Theorem
3
we show that under mild conditions, for a fixed sampling window both our proposed conditional Monte Carlo and the importance-sampling estimator have bounded relative error. (iv) Finally, we illustrate this in a scaling regime of a growing window, where we can prove that the importance sampling estimator exhibits a bounded relative error, whereas the conditional Monte Carlo does not. This illustrates that the more complicated importance-sampling scheme holds the promise of more substantial reduction in variance, where the theoretical results are also supported by an extensive simulation study.

To summarize this, we can state that
importance sampling offers a promising approach to address the computational challenges associated with estimating properties of Gilbert graphs. By assigning appropriate weights to samples, importance sampling focuses computational effort on regions of the graph that contribute significantly to the desired property, thus improving the efficiency of estimation.
The present paper explores the application of importance sampling techniques for estimating key graph properties in Gilbert graphs, where we explore the theoretical foundations of importance sampling, emphasizing its benefits in reducing variance and improving the accuracy of estimators. Additionally, we discuss the intricacies of adapting importance sampling to the specific characteristics of these graphs, considering factors such as spatial distribution, distance metrics, and connectivity constraints.
Through analysis and simulations, we demonstrate the effectiveness of importance sampling in providing more efficient and accurate estimates of critical graph properties. We specifically identify two key regimes to illustrate asymptotic efficiency of the proposed importance sampling estimator. Our findings not only contribute to the methodological toolbox for analyzing random geometric graphs but also shed light on the broader applicability of importance sampling in the realm of complex network analysis.

The subsequent sections of this paper are organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce some notation that is useful throughout the paper. The problem setup of rare-event simulation for Gilbert graphs along with important examples is presented in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we summarize two existing methods for rare-event simulation, namely naïve and conditional Monte Carlo methods, and then introduce the general framework of our importance sampling approach. The focus of Section
5
is put on implementation of the importance sampling method using blocking regions on the sampling window. In Section
6
, we compare the variances of all the methods and study the asymptotic efficiency of the proposed method over two important regimes. Simulation results are presented in Section
7
, whereas Section
8
concludes.

Notation and Efficiency Notions

Throughout the paper, the underlying probability space is denoted by
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
.
The sets of real numbers and integers are denoted by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Z}
, respectively, while the sets of non-negative real numbers and non-negative integers are correspondingly denoted by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
For any probability measure
\mu
and random element
\boldsymbol{X}
, we write
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\mu
to denote that
\boldsymbol{X}
is distributed according to
\mu
. The distribution of a Poisson random variable with rate parameter
\beta>0
is denoted by
\textsf{Poi}(\beta)
.
For a real-valued random variable
X
, its expectation and variance are denoted by
\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(X)
, respectively.
When necessary, to emphasize the dependency on a measure
\mu
, we use the notation
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}
and
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\mu}
to make it clear that probability, expectation and variance, respectively, are taken under the measure
\mu
. For any fixed integer
d\geq 1
and any Borel set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its volume by
\mathsf{Vol}(S)
which is, formally speaking, the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of the set
S
.
In particular, we denote the volume of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean sphere of unit radius by
\upsilon_{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\upsilon_{d}=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)},

(1)

where
\Gamma:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the gamma function.

For the asymptotic analysis considered in Section
6.2
, we use two standard notions of efficiency. Suppose that
\left\{Y_{t}:t>0\right\}
is a family of (real-valued) estimators parameterized by
t>0
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=0
. We say that the family has an asymptotic bounded relative error as
t\rightarrow\infty
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(Y_{t}\right)}{\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]^{2}}<\infty.

(2)

A slightly weaker notion is logarithmic efficiency, which holds if

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\log\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(Y_{t}\right)|}{2|\log\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]|}\geq 1,\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(Y_{t}\right)}{\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]^{(2-\varepsilon)}}=0,

(3)

for each
\varepsilon>0
. Here, “weaker” means that logarithmic efficiency implies an asymptotic bounded relative error. Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=0
, the variance
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left(Y_{t}\right)
in Eqs. (
2
)
and (
3
)
can be replaced by the second moment
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t}^{2}\right]
. For more details on these notions of efficiency, we refer to
Asmussen and Glynn (2007)
and
Rubinstein and Kroese (2017)
.

Rare Events in Gilbert Graphs

In this section, we introduce the notions of Gilbert graphs and of rare events associated with this type of graphs. We further provide some examples of rare events.

Consider the
d
-dimensional sampling window
{W=[0,\lambda]^{d}}
, for some
\lambda>0
and some integer
d\geq 1
. Then, for each
{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, let
\mathscr{G}_{n}
be the family of all finite subsets of size
n
on
W
, i.e.,

\mathscr{G}_{n}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}:x_{i}\in W\,\,\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,n\Big\},

where
n=0
corresponds to the empty set. Put
{\mathscr{G}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\mathscr{G}_{n}}
and
notice that the elements of
\mathscr{G}
are so-called simple point patterns, i.e., they do not have multiple points.

A point process is a random element
{\boldsymbol{X}:(\Omega,\mathcal{F})\to(\mathscr{G},\mathcal{G})}
, where
\mathcal{G}
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathscr{G}
.
By
\rho:\mathcal{G}\to[0,1]
we denote the probability measure on
\mathscr{G}
under which, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
the restriction
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}\in\mathscr{G}_{n}
of
\boldsymbol{X}
to
\mathscr{G}_{n}
is a point process which consists of
n
independent and uniformly distributed points in the window
W
.
Furthermore, a point process
\boldsymbol{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}\in\mathscr{G}
is called a
\kappa
- homogeneous Poisson point process on
W

with intensity
{\kappa>0}
if for the (random) total number of points
N
it holds that
{N\sim\mathsf{Poi}(\kappa\lambda^{d})}
, and for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, conditioned on
N=n
, we have
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\rho
.

From now onward, to simplify the notation, we put

\displaystyle\beta=\kappa\,\mathsf{Vol}(W)=\kappa\lambda^{d},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad q_{n}=\exp(-\beta)\frac{\beta^{n}}{n!}\quad\mbox{for each \,$n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}$}.

(4)

That is,
\{q_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
is the probability mass function of the Poisson distribution
\textsf{Poi}(\beta)
. Furthermore, by
F_{\textsf{Poi}}:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to[0,1]
we denote the cumulative distribution function of
\mathsf{Poi}(\beta)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{\textsf{Poi}}(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}q_{i}\quad\mbox{for each $n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}$.}

(5)

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, let
G(\boldsymbol{x})
be the graph constructed by taking the points in
\boldsymbol{x}
as nodes and connecting every two distinct points
{x,x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{x}}
by an edge if and only if
\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq 1
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A random graph
G(\boldsymbol{X})
is called a Gilbert graph if the set of nodes
\boldsymbol{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
constitutes a
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process in
W
for some
\kappa>0
. Then, for any
B\in\mathcal{G}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in B\right)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\,\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in B),

(6)

with
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\sim\rho
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Two realizations of Gilbert graphs on a bounded subset of the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Example realizations of Gilbert graphs on a 2-dimensional window
W=[0,\lambda]^{2}
, where black points represent the nodes, red lines represent the edges, and each circle centered at a node has a unit radius. Small intensity
\kappa
typically leads to few nodes and few edges as in (a) while large
\kappa
typically leads to a bigger graph with more edges as in (b).

Consider a
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process
\boldsymbol{X}
in
W
and let
A\in\mathcal{G}
be a non-empty set of realizations of
\boldsymbol{X}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
is close to zero, i.e., the occurrence of
A
is very unlikely. Then,
\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\}
is called a rare event. However, note that this definition contains a certain degree of vagueness from a mathematical point of view.
Furthermore, assume that
A\in\mathcal{G}
satisfies the hereditary property, i.e.,
for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{G}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}\subseteq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in A
implies that
\boldsymbol{x}\in A
.
A consequence of the hereditary property is that for any sequence of points
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\in W
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in A)\geq\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}\in A)\qquad\text{for each $n\geq 1$,}

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\mathbb{I}(B):\Omega\to\{0,1\}
denotes the indicator of the
event
B\in\mathscr{F}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{I}(B)(\omega)=1
if
\omega\in B
, and
\mathbb{I}(B)(\omega)=0
otherwise.

When
A
is taken to be the set of all the configurations of the Gilbert graph with no edges, the corresponding rare event probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
appears as the grand partition function of the popular hard-spheres model in grand canonical form. This model has many applications in various disciplines, including physics, chemistry, and material science; see e.g.

Krauth (2006)
;
Moka et al. (2021)
. In particular the probability density of the hard-spheres model is given by
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{x}\in A)/\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, and efficient estimation of
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
is crucial for understanding key properties of the model
(
Döge et al., 2004
)
.

We will now give five more examples of sets
A\in\mathcal{G}
that satisfy the hereditary property, and describe situations where the probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
is close to zero.
Note that, if we take the threshold parameter
\ell=0
in these examples, then the probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
is the same for the first four examples,
being equal to the grand partition function of the hard-spheres model.

Example 1

(
Edge Count).

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, the number of edges in
G(\boldsymbol{x})
will be denoted by
\mathsf{EC}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Furthermore, for a given threshold
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:\mathsf{EC}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\ell\}
be the event of interest.
Then, the value of
\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in A\right)
, i.e., the probability that the number of edges in the Gilbert graph
G(\boldsymbol{X})
is at most
\ell
, can be very small for values of
\kappa
and
\ell
such that
\ell
is much smaller
than the expected number of edges
{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{EC}(\boldsymbol{X})\right]}
.

Example 2

(
Maximum Degree).

We say that two nodes of a graph are adjacent if there is an edge between them.
For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, the degree of a node
x\in\boldsymbol{x}
of
G(\boldsymbol{x})
, denoted by
\mathsf{Deg}(x)
, is the number of nodes
x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{x}
adjacent to
x
, i.e., such that
0<\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq 1
.
The maximum degree of the graph
G(\boldsymbol{x})
is given by

\mathsf{MD}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\max\{\mathsf{Deg}(x):x\in\boldsymbol{x}\}.

Consider the event

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:\mathsf{MD}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\leq\ell\}
that the maximum degree is less than or equal to
\ell
, for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Then, for values of
\kappa
and
\ell
such that
\ell
is much smaller than the expected maximum degree
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{MD}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\right)]
, the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in A\right)
can be very small.

Example 3

(
Maximum Connected Component).

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, two nodes
x,x^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{x}
of
G(\boldsymbol{x})
with
x\not=x^{\prime}
are said to be connected if there is a sequence of nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in\boldsymbol{x}
for some integer
n>1
such that

x_{1}=x
,
x_{n}=x^{\prime}
, and for all
k=2,\dots,n
,
x_{k}
and
x_{k-1}
are adjacent to each other.
A subset of nodes
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\subseteq\boldsymbol{x}
of
G(\boldsymbol{x})
is called a connected component if all the nodes in
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
are
connected with each other and none of the nodes in
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
is connected with any node in
\boldsymbol{x}\setminus\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathsf{MCC}(\boldsymbol{x})
be
the size of the largest connected component in
G(\boldsymbol{x})
, where the size of a connected component is the number of nodes belonging to this connected component.
Consider the event

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:\mathsf{MCC}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\leq\ell+1\}

for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
\ell
is much smaller than
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{MCC}(\boldsymbol{X})]
.
Then, also in this case, the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in A\right)

that the size of each connected component is at most
\ell+1
, can be very small.

Example 4

(
Maximum Clique Size).

A clique of a graph is a subgraph that is complete, which means that any two distinct vertices in the subgraph
are adjacent. Denote the
maximum clique size by
\mathsf{MCS}(\boldsymbol{x})
and consider the event

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:\mathsf{MCS}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\ell+1\}
,
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
which is much smaller than
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{MCS}(\boldsymbol{X})]
.
The probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in A\right)

that all cliques are at most of size
\ell+1
, is then close to zero.

Example 5

(
Number of Triangles).

Another important quantity is the number of triangle subgraphs in an undirected graph, where
a triangle is a clique with
3
vertices and
3
edges.
Let
\mathsf{NTG}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the number of triangles in
G(\boldsymbol{x})
, and consider the event

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:\mathsf{NTG}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\leq\ell\}

for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Then, the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in A\right)
that the number of triangles in
G(\boldsymbol{X})
is not larger than
\ell
, can be very small if
\ell
is
much smaller than

\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{NTG}(\boldsymbol{X})]
. Note that
instead of triangles, we can consider cliques of any fixed size
k\geq 3
and the event that the number of cliques of size
k
is at most
\ell
.

Example 6

(
Planarity).

A graph is called
planar
if it can be embedded in the plane without edge crossings.
Consider the event

A=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}:G(\boldsymbol{x})\text{ is planar}\}
.
Since any subgraph of a planar graph is planar, the event
A
satisfies the hereditary property; refer to
Diestel (2024)
for more details. By Kuratowski’s theorem
(
Kuratowski, 1930
)
, a graph is non-planar if and only if it contains a subdivision of
K_{5}
(the complete graph on five vertices) or
K_{3,3}
(the complete bipartite graph with two sets of three mutually connected vertices) as a subgraph.
For sufficiently large intensity
\kappa
, the graph
G(\boldsymbol{X})
almost surely contains a
K_{5}
or
K_{3,3}
minor and the probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
is close to zero.

4
Estimation Methods

In this section, we first review two existing methods for estimating probabilities of the form given in Eq. (
6
), namely naïve Monte Carlo and conditional Monte Carlo.
Then, we present a general framework of the proposed importance sampling method, which consistently exhibits a variance less than or equal to that of the two existing methods. The following simple observation helps in understanding why the proposed method is efficient.

Observation 1
.

For any event
B\in\mathscr{F}
, let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}:\Omega\to[0,1]
be two random variables such that
{\xi_{1}=\mathbb{I}(B)}
and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]=\mathbb{P}(B)
. Then,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\xi_{2})\leq\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\xi_{1}),

which easily follows from the fact that
\xi_{2}^{2}\leq\xi_{2}
almost surely and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}^{2}]
.
This essentially suggests that for estimating the probability
\mathbb{P}(B)
, instead of using samples of
\xi_{1}
, it can be more efficient to use samples of
\xi_{2}
whenever possible.

For any fixed
B\in\mathscr{F}
,
a simple and basic method for estimating the probability

\mathbb{P}(B)

is naïve Monte Carlo.
To see this, let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process in the window
W
and consider
{B=\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\}}
for some

A\in\mathcal{G}
and the random variable

\displaystyle Y=\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A).

(8)

Now, for any integer
m\geq 1
, let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (iid) copies of
Y
. Then, the sample mean

\displaystyle Z_{\tt NMC}=\frac{1}{m}(Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{m})

(9)

is an unbiased estimator of
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
; i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Z_{\tt NMC}]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Y]=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
. Note that it is easy to generate a sample of
Y
. Namely, one only needs to generate a sample

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}

of the
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process
\boldsymbol{X}
in the window
W
,
and take
Y=1
if
\boldsymbol{X}\in A
, otherwise, take
Y=0
.

On the other hand, for the edge count problem (see Example
1
), a
conditional Monte Carlo estimator was recently proposed by
Hirsch et al. (2022)
. We now state this method more generally in our set-up. Suppose that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots:\Omega\to W
is a sequence of independent random points that are uniformly distributed in
W
. Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and

M=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)=1\}
for some

A\in\mathcal{G}
which satisfies the hereditary property.
Then, from Eq. (
6
) we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\,\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{M}q_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F_{\textsf{Poi}}(M)\right],

(10)

where
F_{\textsf{Poi}}

is the cumulative distribution function of
\textsf{Poi}(\beta)

given in Eq. (
5
).
Now, consider the random variable
\widehat{Y}:\Omega\to[0,1]
which is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\,\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)=F_{\textsf{Poi}}(M),

(11)

and, for any integer
m\geq 1
, let
\widehat{Y}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Y}_{m}
be a sequence of iid copies of
\widehat{Y}
.
Then, from Eq. (
10
) we have

\displaystyle Z_{\tt CMC}=\frac{1}{m}(\widehat{Y}_{1}+\cdots+\widehat{Y}_{m})

(12)

is also an unbiased estimator of
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
.
Furthermore, by Observation
1
, it is evident that the variance of the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
Z_{\tt CMC}
does not exceed that
of the naïve Monte Carlo estimator
Z_{\tt NMC}
given in Eq. (
9
), because for the random variables
Y
and
\widehat{Y}
introduced in Eqs. (
8
)
and (
11
), respectively, it holds that

Y\in\{0,1\}
and
\widehat{Y}\in[0,1]
, while
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{Y}]
; see also Proposition
1
later in Section
6
.

However, note that the estimator
\widehat{Y}
given in Eq. (
11
)
is still a weighted sum of Bernoulli random variables. Using importance sampling,
we now construct an estimator for
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
with a possibly further reduced variance, where each Bernoulli random variable

\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)
in Eq. (
11
) will be replaced by a non-binary random variable, which takes values in the interval
[0,1]
and has the same expectation.

Recall that in both methods considered above, i.e., for getting the naïve and conditional Monte Carlo estimators for
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
, the random points
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots:\Omega\to W
were independent and uniformly distributed in the cubic sampling window
W=[0,\lambda]^{d}
. Instead of this sampling scheme, i.e., instead of generating each of these points independently of the other points,
we now use a different procedure, where each point generation can depend on the locations of already generated points.
Formally, we are no longer considering
the probability measure
\rho:\mathcal{G}\to[0,1]

introduced in Section
3
,
under which
the restriction
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}\in\mathscr{G}_{n}
of the
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process
\boldsymbol{X}
to
\mathscr{G}_{n}
is
a point process that consists of
n
independent and uniformly distributed points
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
But, instead of
\rho
, we consider a new probability measure
\mu:\mathcal{G}\to[0,1]
such that
\rho
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
on
A\in\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho(A^{\prime})=\int_{A^{\prime}}L(\boldsymbol{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\mbox{for each $A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}\cap A$.}

(13)

Since
\widetilde{Y}

is an unbiased estimator for
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
under
\mu
,
from Observation 1, requiring
L\leq 1
ensures
\widetilde{Y}\in[0,1]
, which guarantees

\mathrm{Var}(\widetilde{Y})\leq\mathrm{Var}(Y)
. We then get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})],\quad n\geq 1.

(14)

This equality can be used in order to construct a third unbiased estimator for
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
, besides the estimators
Z_{\tt NMC}
and
Z_{\tt CMC}
discussed above. For this, let

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\,\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}),

(15)

where it follows from Eqs. (
6
) and (
14
) that

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}].

Furthermore, for any integer
m\geq 1
, let
\widetilde{Y}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{Y}_{m}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed copies of
\widetilde{Y}
.
Then,

\displaystyle Z_{\tt IS}=\frac{1}{m}(\widetilde{Y}_{1}+\cdots+\widetilde{Y}_{m})

(16)

is an unbiased estimator for the probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
.

Proposition
1
in Section
6
establishes the relationship between the variances of the three estimators
Z_{\tt NMC}
,
Z_{\tt CMC}
and
Z_{\tt IS}
, showing that the variance of
Z_{\tt IS}
does not exceed the variance of
Z_{\tt CMC}
, which in turn does not exceed the variance of
Z_{\tt NMC}
, where it is our goal to select
\mu
so that the event
\{\boldsymbol{X}\in A\}
is not rare under
\mu
, and hence from now on we refer to
\mu
as the importance sampling measure.

5
Importance Sampling Using Blocking Regions

In Section
4
, we presented a general idea of importance sampling to estimate the probability
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
, where
A\in\mathcal{G}
is some rare-event of interest.
We now present an example of an importance sampling measure
\mu
, where the choice of
\mu
is inspired by the perfect sampling method for hard-spheres models proposed by
Moka et al. (2021)
.

The key idea of this importance sampling method is to generate points sequentially so that each point falls
outside a certain blocking region created by the existing points.
Specifically, for any existing configuration
{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}
,
we say that a region
{B(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\subseteq W}
is blocked by
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
if any new point
x

selected in
B(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
is guaranteed to satisfy
{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\cup\{x\}\notin A}
.
That is, selecting the next point over the blocked region results in a configuration outside
A
.
Note that
B(\boldsymbol{x})
, possibly the whole window
W
, is defined for any configuration
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, not just for configurations in
A
.

Since random points
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
generated under
\rho
are independent and uniformly distributed in the cubic sampling window
W=[0,\lambda]^{d}
, we have that

\rho(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x})=(1/\lambda^{nd})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}_{n}
and
for all integers
n\geq 1
, where
\lambda^{d}
is the volume of
W
.
Suppose now that for every configuration
{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}}
, a blocking region
B(\boldsymbol{x})
can be identified easily and its volume can be computed exactly. Then, under
\mu
, random points
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are sequentially
generated such that
X_{n}
is uniformly generated on the non-blocking region
{W\setminus B(\boldsymbol{X}_{n-1})}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{X}_{n-1}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}\}}
is the set of already generated points.
We stop the procedure when either
{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\notin A}
or
{B(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=W}
.
Then, with
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
denoting an empty set of points, the Radon–Nikodym derivative
L:A\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
introduced in Eq. (
13
) is given by

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac{\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))}{\mathsf{Vol}(W)}\Big),\qquad\mbox{for any $n\geq 1$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in A$,}

(17)

where
B(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\varnothing
; i.e., the blocking region is empty when there are no points.
Note that the term
\left(1-\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))/\mathsf{Vol}(W)\right)
in Eq. (
15
) is the ratio of the uniform density

1/\mathsf{Vol}(W)=1/\lambda^{d}
on the whole window
W
and the uniform density
1/(\lambda^{d}-\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})))
over
the non-blocked region
{W\setminus B(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}
for the
(i+1)
th point.
Given that
\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{x}))\leq\lambda^{d}
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}
, we ensure that
L(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in A
, as desired.
With this choice of the importance sampling measure
\mu
, the estimator
Z_{\tt IS}
for
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
, introduced in Eq. (
16
), is
determined by
\widetilde{Y}
given in Eq. (
15
) with
L:A\to[0,1]
taken to be as in Eq. (
17
); see also
Algorithm
1
below.

Algorithm 1

Importance Sampling Method

1:

B\leftarrow\varnothing

2:

n\leftarrow 0
and
L_{0}\leftarrow 1

3:

repeat

4:

n\leftarrow n+1

5:

L_{n}\leftarrow L_{n-1}\times\left(1-\frac{\mathsf{Vol}(B)}{\mathsf{Vol}(W)}\right)

6:

Generate the next point
X_{n}
uniformly on
W\setminus B

7:

\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\leftarrow\left\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right\}

8:

Update the blocking region
B
for the next point

9:

until

B=W
or
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\notin A

10:

\widetilde{Y}\leftarrow\sum_{i=0}^{n}q_{i}\,L_{i}

11:

return

\widetilde{Y}

Ideally, in each iteration of the algorithm, we would like to identify the blocking region with the maximum possible volume;
see Figure
2(a)
. In that case, we can remove
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\notin A
from the stopping condition as it never happens when
B\neq W
. Unfortunately, it can be computationally challenging to identify such a maximal blocking region
and to compute its volume exactly so that a uniformly distributed point can be generated over the region outside the blocking region. However, since any subset of a maximal blocking region is also a valid blocking region, we can identify such sub-blocking regions in a computationally easy way.
For this, we use a
grid
on
W
.
In particular, we partition the window
W
into a cubic grid of size
K^{d}
. Each cell in the grid is uniquely indexed by a vector of dimensions
d

(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}^{d}
so that
\times_{j=1}^{d}\left[k_{j}{\lambda/K},\,(k_{j}+1){\lambda}/{K}\right]
is the cell with index
(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
.

To implement Algorithm
1
, the first point
X_{1}
is generated uniformly on the window
W
. At the
n
-th iteration, suppose that
\boldsymbol{X}_{n-1}
denotes the set of points generated in the previous iterations. To generate the
n
-th point, we identify a set of cells that are completely covered by the maximum blocking region and take the blocking region
B(\boldsymbol{X}_{n-1})
as the union of these cells. Then, we generate the next point
X_{n}
uniformly over the non-blocking cells.
See Figure
2
for an illustration of this grid-based approach to find blocking regions.

Remark 1

(Graphs with a Fixed Number of Nodes)
.

Recall that the total number of nodes
N
in the Gilbert graph is a Poisson random number. Now suppose that the graph is constructed with the number of nodes fixed, say
N=n
. Then, from Eq. (
11
), the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{Y}
becomes identical to the naïve Monte Carlo estimator
Y
given by Eq. (
8
) (i.e.,
F_{\textsf{Poi}}
is replaced by the degenerative distribution with all the mass at
n
). That is

Y=\widehat{Y}=\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A),

where for the configuration
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
of
n
points it holds that
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\sim\rho
. Thus, the conditional Monte Carlo method brings no variance reduction in this scenario; i.e.,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\widehat{Y})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(Y)
. On the other hand, the proposed importance sampling can still reduce the variance, because

\widetilde{Y}=\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}),

where
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\sim\mu
.
Since
L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\in[0,1]
, we have
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\widetilde{Y})<\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(Y)
, except for trivial cases with values of
n
, where
L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=1
almost surely.

6
Efficiency Analysis

In this section, we first demonstrate that our importance sampling estimator achieves the lowest variance among the three estimators presented in Section
4
. We then illustrate its asymptotic efficiency in comparison to the other methods through two interesting scenarios: one with a fixed sampling window and the other with a growing window. For this, to simplify the notation, let

\displaystyle p_{n}=\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)\quad\text{and}\quad p^{*}=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}p_{n},

(19)

for a rare event
A
of interest that satisfies the hereditary property, where we recall that
\boldsymbol{X}
denotes a
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process on the sampling window
W=[0,\lambda]^{d}
.

6.1
Variance Comparison

Proposition
1
demonstrates that the variance of the importance sampling estimator is lower than that of the conditional Monte Carlo estimator, which, in turn, is lower than that of the naïve Monte Carlo estimator. Here, the notation
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\mu}(\widetilde{Y})
emphasizes that
\widetilde{Y}
is constructed using points that are generated under the importance measure
\mu
, as in Eq. (
15
), while
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(\widehat{Y})
and
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(Y)
emphasize that both
\widehat{Y}
and
Y
are constructed using points that are generated under
\rho
as in Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
8
), respectively.

Proposition 1
.

For any intensity
\kappa
and window size
\lambda
, we have

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\mu}(\widetilde{Y})\leq\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(\widehat{Y})\leq\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(Y).

Remark 2

(Relationship with Optimal Importance Sampling)
.

Using Theorem 1.2 in Chapter V of
(
Asmussen and Glynn, 2007
)
, we can conclude that the optimal (i.e., zero-variance) importance sampling measure
\mu^{*}
for the rare-events considered in this paper has a Radon–Nikodym derivative
L^{*}:A\to[0,1]
given by

L^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=p_{n}\quad\text{for all}\,\,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in A\,\,\text{and}\,\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

where
p_{n}
is given in Eq. (
19
).
Unfortunately, sampling from such optimal measure is impractical as it involves the unknown probabilities
p_{n}
. If we had access to all
p_{n}
, we could directly compute
p^{*}=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
exactly using Eq. (
19
).
Our proposed importance sampling measure
\mu
strikes a balance between practicality and variance reduction.
Specifically, the importance sampling estimator retains a positive, yet minimized, variance, as demonstrated in Proposition
1
.
This is achieved by bringing the values of
L(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
closer to
L^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=p_{n}
, as supported by Observation
1
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

First observe that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Y]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}]
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Y^{2}]=p^{*},

(20)

where the equality
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Y^{2}]=p^{*}
holds, because
Y
is a Bernoulli random variable.
The second inequality
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Y^{2}]
follows from Observation
1
.
To prove the inequality
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]
, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}q_{m}\,\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\in A)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\,p_{n}+2\sum_{m<n}q_{m}q_{n}\,p_{n},

(21)

where we used the fact that
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A\}\subseteq\{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\in A\}
for all
m<n
. Then, using Eq. (
14
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}q_{m}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}\right]+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right].

(22)

Since
0\leq L(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\leq 1
for all
i
, this gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right]+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}\,\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)

(23)

which is equal to
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]
from (
21
), and hence completing the proof.
∎

Proposition
1
established that in general our importance sampling method is
superior to or, at the very least, as effective as the conditional Monte Carlo method in minimizing variance.
Our next result, Proposition
2
, provides more insights on this aspect.
To this end, for
m\leq n
, define

\displaystyle\gamma_{m,n}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)]}{p_{n}}.

(24)

That is,
\gamma_{m,n}
is the conditional expectation of
L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})
given
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A
, where
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
is a set of
n
uniformly and independently
distributed points on the observation window
W=[0,\lambda]^{d}
.

Proposition 2
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}p_{n}+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}p_{n},

(25)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\gamma_{n,n}p_{n}+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}\gamma_{m,n}p_{n}.

(26)

Proof.

The expression in Eq. (
25
) is established as Eq. (
23
) in the proof of Proposition
1
. To prove Eq. (
26
), consider Eq. (
22
), and then using the definition of the Radon-Nikodym derivative
L
, we can write for every
m\leq n
that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\right].

Furthermore, using Eq. (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}^{2}\,p_{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\right]}{p_{n}}+2\sum_{m<n}q_{n}q_{m}\,p_{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\in A)L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\right]}{p_{n}},

which is identical to Eq. (
26
) from the definition of
\gamma_{m,n}
given by Eq. (
24
).
∎

To see an implication of Proposition
2
, take
\ell=0
in any of the first
four examples in Section
3
. Then,
A
is the set of all the hard-spheres configurations. If we further assume that the cell edge length, which is
\lambda/K
, in the grid is selected sufficiently smaller than
1
,
then as in
(
Moka et al., 2021
)
, for every
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in\mathscr{G}_{n}\cap A
, we can show that

\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}))\geq nc\,\upsilon_{d}
,
for a positive constant
c
with
\upsilon_{d}
denoting the volume of a unit radius
d
-dimensional hyper-sphere given by Eq. (
1
). The value of
c
can increase with the refinement of the grid used in importance sampling.
Therefore, using the definition of
L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})
, for all
m,n
with
m\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{m,n}\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{ic\,\upsilon_{d}}{\lambda^{d}}\right)^{+},

(27)

where
r^{+}=\max(0,r)
for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note from Bernoulli’s inequality that
1+rt\leq(1+t)^{r}
for all
r\geq 0
and
t\geq-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma_{m,n}\leq\left(1-\frac{c\upsilon_{d}}{\lambda^{d}}\right)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(i-1)}=\left(1-\frac{c\upsilon_{d}}{\lambda^{d}}\right)^{m(m-1)/2}.

Thus, for any
n
,
\gamma_{m,n}
decays with a rate faster than exponential in
m^{2}
to reach zero for large
m
. As a result, from Proposition
2
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]
can be much smaller than
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]
, or equivalently,

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\mu}(\widetilde{Y})
can be much smaller than
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\mu}(\widehat{Y})
, as supported by the simulation results in Section
7
.

6.2
Asymptotic Analysis

As mentioned above, in this analysis our goal is to theoretically illustrate the limiting performance of the proposed importance sampling method in two asymptotic regimes where
p^{*}
approaches
0
. In the first regime, with a fixed observation window, we show that both the conditional Monte Carlo estimator and the proposed importance sampling estimator are efficient, whereas in the second regime, with a growing window, we show that only the importance sampling estimator retains efficiency.

Towards this end, let
M
be a integer-valued random variable with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M=n)=\frac{q_{n}p_{n}}{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{i}p_{i}},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(28)

where
q_{n}
and
p_{n}
are defined in Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
19
) respectively.
In other words,
M
is the number of points in a realization
\boldsymbol{X}
of the points of the Gilbert graph conditioned on
\boldsymbol{X}\in A
.

Suppose an asymptotic regime is parameterized by a non-negative parameter
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that the rare-event probability
p^{*}=p^{*}(t)
tends to
0
as
t\to\infty
.
As an example of such a regime, we can fix both the window size
\lambda
and threshold
\ell
, and increase the intensity
\kappa
to
\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Another regime could be where the intensity
\kappa
is constant and both
\ell
and
\lambda
go to
\infty
. In general, an asymptotic regime consists of changing combination of these parameters such that
p^{*}
goes to zero asymptotically.

It is easy to see that the naïve Monte Carlo estimator
Y
, defined in Eq. (
8
), exhibits neither the bounded relative error nor the logarithmic efficiency over any regime where
\lim_{t\to\infty}p^{*}=0
. This is true because
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(Y)=p^{*}(1-p^{*})
and

{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}_{\rho}(Y)}/{(p^{*})^{(2-\epsilon)}}={1}/{(p^{*})^{(1-\epsilon)}}-(p^{*})^{\epsilon}

goes to
\infty
as
t\to\infty
for all
0<\epsilon<1
. Therefore, the naïve Monte Carlo estimator is not efficient over any asymptotic regime where the rare-event probability
p^{*}
goes to zero.
Thus, we focus only on the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{Y}
, defined in Eq. (
11
), and the importance sampling estimator
\widetilde{Y}
, defined in Eq. (
15
).

Recall that both
\widehat{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
are unbiased estimators of
p^{*}
. As a consequence, from Proposition
1
, we can easily notice that in any regime, if
\widehat{Y}
has asymptotic logarithmic efficiency (respectively, bounded relative error), then
\widetilde{Y}
also has asymptotic logarithmic efficiency (respectively, bounded relative error).

6.2.1
A Regime with a Fixed Sampling Window

Theorem
3
establishes the asymptotic efficiency of both
\widehat{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
over an asymptotic regime. Corollary
1
presents an interesting application of this theorem where the window
W
and the threshold parameter
\ell
are fixed and the intensity
\kappa
increases unboundedly.

Theorem 3
.

Consider an asymptotic regime parameterized by
t
, where
\lim_{t\to\infty}p^{*}=0
, and for each
t>0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
be the support of
M
defined in Eq. (
28
) and denote its cardinality by
|\mathcal{S}_{t}|
. Furthermore, suppose for all sufficiently large
t>0
that
|\mathcal{S}_{t}|\leq n_{0}
for some
n_{0}<\infty
and
\min_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}p_{n}>0
. Then, the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{Y}
and the importance sampling estimators
\widetilde{Y}
exhibit bounded relative error as
t\to\infty
.

Before proving Theorem
3
, we first provide a specific corollary of the theorem.

Corollary 1
.

Consider the regime where
\lambda
and
\ell
are fixed. Then, for all examples stated in Section
3
, both
\widehat{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
exhibit bounded relative error as the intensity
\kappa\to\infty
.

Proof.

Note that
\kappa
appears in the definition of Poisson distribution
\{q_{n}:n\geq 0\}
; see Eq. (
4
). Hence,
\kappa
does not influence
p_{n}
.
From the hereditary property,
p_{n}
is decreasing in
n
. Furthermore, since the window size
\lambda
and threshold
\ell
are fixed, for all examples stated in Section
3
, for sufficiently large values of
n
, every configuration
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{G}_{n}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}\notin A
. Thus, there exists
n_{0}
such that
p_{n}=0
if and only if
n>n_{0}
. That is, the support of
M
is
\{0,\dots,n_{0}\}
for all
\kappa>0
. Thus,

{p_{M}\geq\min_{n=0,\dots,n_{0}}p_{n}=p_{n_{0}}>0},

almost surely,
and thus we complete the proof of the corollary using Theorem
3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We only need to show that
\widehat{Y}
has bounded relative error as it implies bounded relative error for
\widetilde{Y}
. From Eq. (
2
), we need to show that

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]/(p^{*})^{2}<\infty
.
Now let
N
and
N^{\prime}
be independent and identically Poisson distributed random variables, with distribution given by Eq. (
5
). Then, with the notation
a\vee b=\max(a,b)
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}q_{m}p_{n\vee m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N\vee N^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}<N)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N^{\prime}}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}\geq N)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}<N)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}\leq N)\right],

where in the second expectation of the last equality, we swapped
N
and
N^{\prime}
as they are identically distributed. As a consequence, it follows that

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}<N)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}\leq N)\right].

(29)

Let
n_{t}=\max\mathcal{S}_{t}
be the maximum element of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
.
From the hereditary property in Eq. (
7
), for each
t
,
p_{n}\geq p_{n^{\prime}}
whenever
n<n^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{0,1,\dots,n_{t}\}
and hence,
\mathbb{P}(N\in\mathcal{S}_{t})=\mathbb{P}(N\leq n_{t})
.
Note that for each
t
, we have

\displaystyle p^{*}=\mathbb{E}[p_{N}]=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}q_{n}p_{n}\geq p_{n_{t}}\,\mathbb{P}(N\leq n_{t}),

(30)

and

\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N\geq N^{\prime})\right]=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}q_{n}p_{n}\mathbb{P}(n\geq N^{\prime})\leq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}q_{n}\mathbb{P}(n\geq N^{\prime})\leq\mathbb{P}(N\leq n_{t})^{2},

where the first inequality holds because
p_{n}\leq 1
and the second inequality is a simple consequence of the fact that
n_{t}
is the maximum element of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
. Thus,

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]}{(p^{*})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{P}(N\leq n_{t})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(N\leq n_{t})^{2}}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}<\infty,

where
c=\min_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}p_{n}
and the first inequality holds because
p_{n_{t}}\geq c
.
∎

6.2.2
A Regime with a Growing Sampling Window

To demonstrate that over some regimes the importance sampling estimator
\widetilde{Y}
can be efficient while the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{Y}
fails to be efficient, we now concentrate on the hard-sphere scenario, by setting
\ell=0
in Examples
1
through
4
of Section
3
. Note that for this hard-sphere scenario,
p^{*}
is the probability that every pair of points in a
\kappa
-homogeneous Poisson point process on the window
W=[0,\lambda]^{d}
is separated by at least a unit distance. Recall that
\beta
is the expected number of nodes of the Gilbert graph, as defined in Eq. (
4
). For the asymptotic regime here, we assume that the volume of the window
W
is
\beta^{\delta}
(that is,
\lambda=\beta^{\delta/d}
) and the intensity
\kappa=\beta^{1-\delta}
for some
\delta>1
, and study the asymptotic behaviors of
\widehat{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
as
\beta\to\infty
.
Note that, since
\delta>1
, the volume of the window
W
increases faster than the expected number of nodes of the graph.
The limit point
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}p^{*}
and the rate of convergence to the limit point vary depending on the value of
\delta
. In particular, Lemma
1
is a result from
Moka et al. (2021)
and it implies that
p^{*}
is a rare-event probability for
1<\delta<2
as
\beta\to\infty
. For simplicity of analysis, we assume the ideal case where the importance sampling uses maximal-volume blocking regions.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 1 of
Moka et al. (2021)
)
.

Suppose
\lambda=\beta^{\delta/d}
with
\delta>1
. Then, for the hard-sphere case (i.e., setting
\ell=0
in Examples
1
through
4
),

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta^{2-\delta}}\log p^{*}=-\frac{\upsilon_{d}}{2},\quad\text{if}\,\,1<\delta<2.

and

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}p^{*}=\begin{cases}\exp(-\upsilon_{d}/2),&\quad\text{if}\,\,\delta=2,\\
1,&\quad\text{if}\,\,\delta>2,\end{cases}

Theorem
4
shows that the importance sampling estimator
\widetilde{Y}
is efficient asymptotically as
\beta\to\infty
while the conditional Monte Carlo estimate
\widehat{Y}
is inefficient.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
\lambda=\beta^{\delta/d}
with
1<\delta<2
. Then, for the hard-sphere case (i.e., setting
\ell=0
in Examples
1
through
4
), as
\beta\to\infty
, the following is true:

(i)

The importance sampling estimator
\widetilde{Y}
exhibits logarithmic efficiency.

(ii)

The conditional Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{Y}
exhibits neither bounded relative error nor logarithmic efficiency.

We prove statements (i) and (ii) of Theorem
4
separately. The following Lemma
2
is useful for establishing (i).

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\lambda=\beta^{\delta/d}
with
1<\delta<2
. Then, for the hard-sphere case (i.e., setting
\ell=0
in Examples
1
through
4
),

\sup_{n\leq 2\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}}{p_{n}}=\exp\left(O\left(\beta^{2-{\delta(d+1)/d}}+\beta^{3-2\delta}\right)\right).

In the lemma,
O(\cdot)
denotes the standard big
O
notation: A function
h(\beta)
said to be
O(g(\beta))
for a non-negative function
g(\beta)
if
{\limsup_{\beta\to\infty}h(\beta)/g(\beta)}
is finite. A proof of the lemma is provided in Appendix
9
.

Proof of Theorem
4
(i).

Because of Eq. (
3
), we need to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]}{(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}}=0,

(31)

for all
\epsilon>0
.
Under the importance sampling, we have
L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\notin A
. Thus,

\widetilde{Y}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}).

Furthermore, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widetilde{Y}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}q_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}q_{m}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})^{2}]}=\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}\right)^{2}.

Thus, we establish Eq. (
31
) by showing that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\left[\frac{1}{(p^{*})^{1-\epsilon}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}\right]=0.

(32)

Now for each
\beta
, if we take
n_{\beta}=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:p_{n}\neq 0\}
, then
p_{n}\neq 0
if and only if
n\leq n_{\beta}
. Since
p^{*}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}p_{n}
, with the notation
a\wedge b=\min(a,b)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{*}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}p_{n}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{n\leq 2\beta}q_{n}p_{n}}\sum_{n\leq 2\beta}q_{n}p_{n}\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}}{p_{n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{1}{p^{*}}\sum_{n>2\beta}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{p_{M}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{M})^{2}]}\,\,\bigg|\,\,M\leq 2\beta\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{1}{p^{*}}\sum_{n>2\beta}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]},

(33)

where
M
is distributed as Eq. (
28
). From Lemma
2
, we get that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{p_{M}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{M})^{2}]}\,\bigg|\,M\leq 2\beta\right]=\exp\left(O\left(\beta^{2-{\delta(d+1)/d}}+\beta^{3-2\delta}\right)\right).

From Lemma
1
,
p^{*}=\exp\left(-\frac{\upsilon_{d}\beta^{2-\delta}}{2}(1+o(1))\right),

and thus for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle(p^{*})^{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{p_{M}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{M})^{2}]}\,\bigg|\,M\leq 2\beta\right]

\displaystyle=\exp(-O(\beta^{2-\delta}))\exp\left(O\left(\beta^{2-{\delta(d+1)/d}}+\beta^{3-2\delta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0,

(34)

as
\beta\to\infty
,
because
2-\delta>2-{\delta(d+1)/d}
and
2-\delta>3-2\delta
.
Furthermore, since
\mathbb{P}(N>2\beta)=\sum_{n>2\beta}q_{n}
is the tail probability of a Poisson random variable with mean
\beta
, from the Chernoff bound it follows that
\mathbb{P}(N>2\beta)\leq\exp(-c\beta)
for some fixed constant
c>0
; see, e.g.,
Short (2013)
. Thus, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{n>2\beta}q_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}}{(p^{*})^{1-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(N>2\beta)}{(p^{*})^{1-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-c\beta)\exp\left(\frac{\upsilon_{d}\beta^{2-\delta}(1-\epsilon)}{2}(1+o(1))\right)\longrightarrow 0,

(35)

as
\beta\to\infty
, because
2-\delta<1
. From Eqs. (
34
), (
35
) and (
33
), we obtain Eq. (
32
) and thus Eq. (
31
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
(ii).

Since the bounded relative error implies the logarithmic efficiency, it is enough to show that

\limsup_{\beta\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\widehat{Y}^{2}]/(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}\right)=\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
.
Let
N
and
N^{\prime}
are iid Poisson random variables with distribution given by Eq. (
5
). Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}<N)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[p_{N}\mathbb{I}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta)\right]\geq p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta),

where
\lceil a\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater than
a
and the last inequality follows from the fact that
p_{n}
decreases with
n
. Therefore, from Eq. (
29
), for any
\epsilon>0
, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\widehat{Y}^{2}\right]}{(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}}\geq 2\frac{p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}}{(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}}\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta).

(36)

We complete the proof by showing that
\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta)\to 1/8
and
p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}/(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}\to\infty
as
\beta\to\infty
.
Note that

\mathbb{P}(\max(N^{\prime},N)\leq\beta)=\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta)+\mathbb{P}(N<N^{\prime}\leq\beta)+\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=N,N\leq\beta).

Since
N^{\prime}
and
N
are independent and identically distributed,
\mathbb{P}(\max(N^{\prime},N)\leq\beta)=\mathbb{P}(N\leq\beta)^{2}
and the first two terms on the right of the above expression are identical. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{P}(N\leq\beta)^{2}-\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=N,N\leq\beta)\right).

(37)

It is easy to see that

\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=N,N\leq\beta)\leq\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=N)=\exp(-2\beta)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\beta^{2n}/(n!)^{2},

where the summation term is well-known as the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order zero, denoted as
I_{0}(2\beta)
. Hence,
\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=N)=\exp(-2\beta)I_{0}(2\beta)
.
From
Kasperkovitz (1980)
, we get that
\exp(-2\beta)I_{0}(2\beta)\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
. We also know from
Short (2013)
that

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N\leq\beta)=1/2
.
Therefore, from Eq. (
37
), it follows that

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}<N\leq\beta)=1/8
.

Now to complete the proof, from Eq. (
36
), it remains to show that

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}}{(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}}=\infty.

Since the blocking volume added
i
points is at most

, from Lemmas
3
and
4
in Appendix
9
, we have

\displaystyle p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil\beta\rceil-1}i\upsilon_{d}\right)\exp(-O(\beta^{3-2\delta}))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\lceil\beta\rceil(\lceil\beta\rceil-1)\upsilon_{d}}{2\beta^{\delta}}\right)\exp(-O(\beta^{3-2\delta})).

From Lemma
1
, we know that
p^{*}=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2-\delta}\upsilon_{d}}{2}(1+o(1))\right)
for
\delta>1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\lceil\beta\rceil}}{(p^{*})^{2-\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(\frac{\beta^{2-\delta}\upsilon_{d}}{2}(2-\epsilon)(1+o(1))-\frac{\lceil\beta\rceil(\lceil\beta\rceil-1)\upsilon_{d}}{2\beta^{\delta}}\right)\exp(-O(\beta^{3-2\delta})).

(38)

Since
\lceil\beta\rceil(\lceil\beta\rceil-1)=O(\beta^{2})
and
2-\delta>3-2\delta
, by selecting
\epsilon<1
, we see that the limit of the term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
38
) tends to infinity as
\beta\to\infty
.
∎

7
Simulation Results

In this section, we illustrate the effectiveness of our importance sampling estimator by comparing it to the naïve and conditional Monte Carlo estimators through simulation results in eight different settings, covering the edge count, maximum degree, maximum connected component, maximum clique size, number of triangles, and planarity examples. The Python implementation of our simulation study is available at
https://github.com/saratmoka/RareEvents-RandGeoGraphs
.

We recall that
Z_{\tt NMC}
is the sample mean naïve rejection estimator of
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X}\in A)
, defined by Eq. (
9
). That is,
Z_{\tt NMC}=(Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{m})/m
, where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
are iid copies of
Y
. Similarly, the sample mean conditional Monte Carlo estimator
Z_{\tt CMC}
and the sample mean importance sampling estimator
Z_{\tt IS}
are defined by Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
16
), respectively. We use
\mathsf{RV}_{\tt NMC}
,
{\sf RV}_{\tt CMC}
and
{\sf RV}_{\tt IS}
to denote the estimated relative variances of

,
\widehat{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
, respectively. For instance,

where
\widehat{Y}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Y}_{m}
are samples used for
Z_{\tt CMC}
. Similarly, we compute
{\sf RV}_{\tt NMC}
and
{\sf RV}_{\tt IS}
. To ensure that all the sample mean estimators
Z_{\tt NMC}
,
Z_{\tt CMC}
and
Z_{\tt IS}
have the same confidence intervals, the number of samples
m
is selected such that estimates of the relative variances of these sample mean estimators fall below a small fixed value (
0.001
in our numerical studies). For instance, since
{\sf RV}_{\tt CMC}/m
is an estimate of the relative variance of the conditional Monte Carlo estimator
Z_{\tt CMC}
, copies of
\widehat{Y}
are generated until the value of
{\sf RV}_{\tt CMC}/m
becomes smaller than

.

Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the problem of estimating rare-event probabilities for random geometric graphs, also known as Gilbert graphs. We proposed an easily implementable and efficient importance sampling method for rare-event estimation. Using analysis and simulations, we compared its performance with existing methods: naïve and conditional Monte Carlo estimators. In particular, we showed that the importance sampling estimator always exhibits smaller variance than the other two methods. Furthermore, we established an asymptotic regime where the importance sampling estimator is efficient while the other estimators are inefficient. Our simulation results show that the proposed estimator can have variance thousands of times smaller than the other estimators when the rare-event probabilities are extremely small.

Proof of Lemma
2

We now provide a proof of Lemma
2
. For this we use two lemmas stated below: Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
. Note that the blocking volumes are monotonically non-decreasing. That is,
B(\boldsymbol{X}_{i-1})\subseteq B(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})
is for each
i
. Also, the blocking volume added by a point is at most
\upsilon_{d}
, the volume of a unit radius sphere. In other words, with

V_{i}=\mathsf{Vol}(B(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})),

we have
V_{i}-V_{i-1}\leq\upsilon_{d}
,
where the equality holds when the center of the
i
-th sphere is one unit away from the boundary of the window and
2
units away from the centers of the other spheres.
As a consequence, we get that

\displaystyle V_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(V_{j}-V_{j-1}\right)\leq i\upsilon_{d}.

(39)

Throughout the section, we assume that
\lambda=\beta^{\delta/d}
with
1<\delta<2
.

Lemma 3
.

For all sufficiently large values of
\beta>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V_{i}]\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right),\quad n\leq 2\beta.

Proof.

Recall from Eq. (
17
) that

L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac{V_{i}}{\lambda^{d}}\Big)^{+}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac{V_{i}}{\beta^{\delta}}\Big)^{+}.

From Eq. (
39
), for all
i<n\leq 2\beta
, we have

V_{i}\leq i\upsilon_{d}<n\upsilon_{d}\leq 2\beta\upsilon_{d}.

Since
\delta>1
, for sufficiently large values of
\beta
, we have

2\beta\upsilon_{d}<\beta^{\delta}
, and thus,
V_{i}/\beta^{\delta}<1
for all
i<n\leq 2\beta
.
Consequently, using the Taylor expansion of
\log(1-u)
,
u<1
, and the definition of
p_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log(1-V_{i}/\beta^{\delta})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{V_{i}^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right)\right],

for large values of
\beta
with
n\leq 2\beta
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle p_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}V_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{V_{i}^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right)\right],

Then, from Eq. (
39
),

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}V_{i}\right)\right]\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V_{i}]\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right),

where the last inequality
is a consequence of Jensen’s inequality since
\exp(\cdot)
is convex.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For any
n\leq 2\beta
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\right)\right]}=\exp\left(O\left(\beta^{2-{\delta(d+1)/d}}+\beta^{3-2\delta}\right)\right),

(40)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}=O(\beta^{3-2\delta}).

(41)

Proof.

For each
i=1,\dots,n-1
, define the indicator random variable

I_{i}=\mathbb{I}(V_{i}-V_{i-1}<\upsilon_{d})
.
Then,

\displaystyle i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(\upsilon_{d}-(V_{j}-V_{j-1})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{i}I_{i}\left(\upsilon_{d}-(V_{j}-V_{j-1})\right)\leq{\upsilon_{d}\sum_{j=1}^{i}I_{j},}

(42)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\leq\upsilon_{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}I_{j}=\upsilon_{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)I_{j},

for each
i=1,\dots,n-1
.
Note that
I_{i}=1
means the
i^{th}
point
X_{i}
is generated within
1
unit from the boundary of the window
W
or within
2
units from the centers of all the existing
i-1
points. Observe that for any
j=1,\dots,i-1
, the probability of
\|X_{i}-X_{j}\|_{2}\leq 2
is at most
2^{d}\upsilon_{d}/(\beta^{\delta}-V_{i})
. This is because,
X_{j}
is generated on non-blocking region created by the points
1,\dots,i-1
and
x_{i}
has to be within two units distance from
X_{i}
. Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(I_{i}=1|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}\right)\leq\frac{\beta^{\delta}-(\beta^{\delta/d}-2)^{d}}{\beta^{\delta}-V_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{2^{d}\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}-V_{i}}.

Since
V_{i}\leq i\upsilon_{d},
with
r_{i}=\frac{\beta^{\delta}-(\beta^{{\delta/d}}-2)^{d}}{\beta^{\delta}-i\upsilon_{d}}+\frac{i\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}-i\upsilon_{d}}
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(I_{i}=1|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}\right)\leq r_{i}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)I_{j}\right)\right].

Observe that the right side of the above inequality is equal to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}(n-j)I_{j}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}I_{n-1}\right)\big|X_{1},\dots,X_{n-2}\right]\right]},

which can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}(n-j)I_{j}\right)\right]\left((1-r_{n-1})+r_{n-1}\exp(2\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta})\right).

By repeating the same procedure for all the terms in the above expectation and substituting the values of
c_{i}
’s, we establish that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1+r_{n-i}(e^{2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta}}-1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}r_{n-i}(e^{2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta}}-1)\right).

(43)

Then, notice that since
\delta>1
, for each
i<n\leq 2\beta
,
iv/\beta^{\delta}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
. Furthermore,

\beta^{\delta}-(\beta^{\delta/d}-2)^{d}=O(\beta^{\delta(d-1)/d})
, and hence,

r_{i}=O\left((\beta^{\delta(d-1)/d}+i)/\beta^{\delta}\right)
.
Also,

\displaystyle\exp(2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta})-1=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{j!\beta^{j\delta}}=\frac{2i\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{(j+1)!\beta^{j\delta}}\leq\frac{2i\upsilon_{d}}{\beta^{\delta}}\exp(2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta}).

Since
\exp(2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta})\to 1
as
\beta\to\infty
for all
i<n\leq 2\beta
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}r_{n-i}(\exp(2i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta})-1)=O\left(\frac{n^{2}\beta^{\delta(d-1)/d}}{\beta^{2\delta}}+\frac{n^{3}}{\beta^{2\delta}}\right)=O\left(\beta^{2-{\delta(d+1)/d}}+\beta^{3-2\delta}\right),

which completes the proof of (
40
) using (
43
).
Now to prove (
40
), observe for large
\beta
that if
i<n\leq 2\beta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}=\frac{\upsilon_{d}^{2}}{\beta^{2\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}=\frac{\upsilon_{d}^{2}}{\beta^{2\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^{2}\frac{1}{1-i\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta}}\leq\frac{\upsilon_{d}^{2}}{\beta^{2\delta}}\frac{1}{1-n\upsilon_{d}/\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^{2},

where we used
i\upsilon_{d}<n\upsilon_{d}
in the last inequality.
We complete the proof by observing that
n/\beta^{\delta}\leq 2\beta/\beta^{\delta}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
and

\sum_{i<n}i^{2}\leq\sum_{i<2\beta}i^{2}=O(\beta^{3}).

∎

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}V_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V_{i}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(V_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V_{i}])\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V_{i}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\right)\right],

where the last inequality follows from the upper bound in Eq. (
39
). Thus, using Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[L(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})^{2}]}}{p_{n}}\leq\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(i\upsilon_{d})^{j}}{\beta^{j\delta}}\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2}{\beta^{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(i\upsilon_{d}-V_{i})\right)\right]}.

(44)

We now complete the proof using Lemma
4
.
∎
