An enhanced-permeability amphiphilic nanogel loaded with sulforaphane for synergistic anti-oxidative and anti-ferroptotic therapy of corneal alkali burn
Authors: Chengcheng Yang, Yuxi Huang, Yichi Zhang, Yubo Zhang, Wenting Luo, Xiaomei Ke, Songying Pi, Hui Chen, Shushan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlkali-induced ocular injury is a severe blinding disease where oxidative stress and ferroptosis drive corneal neovascularization and fibrosis. The natural compound sulforaphane (SFN) can inhibit these processes but its efficacy is limited by poor solubility, inadequate corneal penetration, and short ocular retention.MethodsSFN-loaded liposomes (LPS) were prepared and incorporated into a poloxamer-based thermosensitive hydrogel (HP@LPS). Its gelation properties at ocular surface temperature and in vitro release were characterized. The antioxidant and anti-ferroptosis effects of LPS were evaluated in vitro, while therapeutic efficacy of HP@LPS in inhibiting neovascularization, fibrosis, and promoting corneal repair was assessed in a rat alkali burn model.ResultsHP@LPS was successfully developed, undergoing in situ gelation for sustained SFN release, with LPS ensuring efficient corneal penetration. In vitro, LPS attenuated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, upregulated ferroptosis-inhibitory proteins, and suppressed neovascularization and fibrosis. In vivo, HP@LPS ameliorated inflammation, neovascularization, and fibrosis through synergistic antioxidative and anti-ferroptotic mechanisms, with favorable bioavailability and minimal toxicity.ConclusionsHP@LPS enhanced corneal retention and stromal penetration of SFN, promoting corneal repair and regeneration. This system offers a novel therapeutic strategy for corneal alkali burn and represents a promising candidate for clinical translation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08122-0.Keywords: Corneal alkali burn, Hydrogel, Liposomes, Oxidative stress, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Ocular chemical burns are a frequent ophthalmic emergency, with alkali injuries exhibiting particularly severe tissue destruction. Such insults provoke a cascade of inflammatory responses in the cornea, and delayed intervention often leads to irreversible visual impairment [1]. Corticosteroids are commonly employed to mitigate inflammation [2]; however, their use is constrained by adverse effects, including impaired epithelial repair and even corneal perforation, resulting in unsatisfactory therapeutic outcomes [3]. Oxidative stress has been recognized as a central mechanism driving disease progression after corneal alkali burn [4]. It induces the accumulation of lipid peroxides in corneal epithelial and stromal cells, together with aberrant Fe²⁺ deposition [3], thereby precipitating ferroptosis-associated irreversible cellular injury [3, 5]. In addition, oxidative stress activates signaling cascades such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) [6] and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [7], leading to the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which ultimately promotes corneal neovascularization and fibrosis. The combined effects of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-induced corneal injury are critical factors leading to vision loss and compromised prognosis in alkali burn cases.

Extensive studies have demonstrated that inhibiting oxidative stress produces beneficial therapeutic effects in ocular disorders [8, 9]. Although conventional exogenous antioxidants can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), their efficacy is limited by poor bioavailability and insufficient penetration [10]. More importantly, excessive suppression of ROS may disrupt redox homeostasis and impair corneal repair. Sulforaphane (SFN), a natural isothiocyanate with potent antioxidant activity, not only enhances ROS clearance and attenuates inflammation [11, 12] but also inhibits ferroptosis by disrupting mitochondrial function [13], thereby promoting corneal repair. Consequently, SFN, as a novel candidate, shows considerable promise for the treatment of corneal alkali burn.

Traditional drugs for alkali burn, such as dexamethasone eye drops, carry the risk of inducing corneal melting and perforation [3]. Recent studies have demonstrated that SFN exhibits superior efficacy and lower incidence of complications for the treatment of ocular diseases [11, 14, 15]. Topical delivery via eye drops avoids first-pass metabolism and enables rapid drug accumulation on the ocular surface [16]. However, conventional eye drops are limited by short ocular residence time due to blinking and tear turnover, often requiring frequent instillation that compromises patient compliance. More importantly, the poor solubility and low corneal permeability of SFN present significant formulation challenges and restrict its ocular application [12, 17]. Recently, thermosensitive hydrogels, which exhibit temperature-responsive sol-gel transition behavior, have emerged as excellent candidates for sustained ocular drug delivery [18, 19]. Poloxamer 407 (P407), a biocompatible FDA-approved polymer, possesses a unique three-dimensional porous structure that enables efficient drug loading [20] and temperature-triggered gelation, facilitating sustained release upon ocular administration [21]. Simultaneously, liposomes have also been applied in the clinic owing to their favorable stability and biosafety. Among them, cationic liposomes can electrostatically adsorb to the negatively charged corneal epithelium cells [22, 23], thereby improving resistance to tear clearance and extending ocular surface retention [23]. Additionally, the amphiphilic nature of liposomes enhances the apparent solubility of SFN. This improvement facilitates its incorporation into the hydrogel delivery system and increases its permeability within ocular tissues [24], offering a synergistic strategy for optimized ocular therapeutics.

Based on this, we designed a biocompatible thermosensitive nanohydrogel-based SFN eye drops (HP@LPS) to repair corneal stromal cells and remodel corneal structure in alkali burn. These hydrogel eye drops were constructed primarily using P407 to deliver SFN-loaded liposomes (LPS). Benefiting from in situ gelation and structural stability, HP@LPS enabled sustained release and enhanced SFN penetration. The PEG-modified liposomes exhibited unique electrostatic adsorption and amphiphilic properties [25], facilitating efficient epithelial penetration and prolonging drug release, thereby significantly improving the therapeutic efficiency of SFN. The topical application of HP@LPS alleviated corneal damage and neovascularization. This therapeutic effect was mediated by synergistic anti-oxidative stress and anti-ferroptosis mechanisms, which upregulate the expression of key proteins including SOD2, SIRT3, GPX4, and FSP-1. Importantly, the SFN-loaded eye drops exhibited minimal ocular surface and systemic toxicity. Overall, our study indicates a strategy to encapsulate SFN-loaded liposomes into a thermo-sensitive hydrogel to promote corneal repair and regeneration over a long period. This system represents a promising therapeutic option for corneal alkali burn with potential for clinical translation.

Cell culture

Human Corneal Fibroblasts (HCFs) were previously acquired from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Simian Virus 40 (SV40)-transformed Human Corneal Epithelial cells (HCEs) were provided by RIKEN Biosource Center (Tokyo, Japan). HCFs were grown in MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). HCEs were grown in DMEM-F12 medium supplemented with 15% heat-inactivated FBS, 10 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor, 40 µg/mL gentamicin, 0.1 µg/mL cholera toxin, and 5 µg/mL bovine insulin. Macrophage cells were grown in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cell lines were incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Human vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in EGM-2 complete medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. When cell confluence reached 80%, 0.25% trypsin was used for digestion and passaging, followed by subculture at a ratio of 1:2 to 1:3.

Synthesis of liposomes, LPS, HP@S and HP@LPS

Blank liposomes (LP): A total of 30 mg of DOTAP (1,2-Dioleoyl-3-triethylaoniu-propane): PC(Phosphatidylcholine): Cholesterol: DSPE-PEG2000 (molar ratio 25:40:30:5) was dissolved in 10 mL of chloroform, and mass are as follows 8.02 mg, 12.83 mg, 4.91 mg and 4.23 mg. Then, 2 mL of the solution was taken to form a lipid film via rotary evaporation, followed by vacuum drying. The film was hydrated with PBS at 60 °C, dispersed by probe sonication (120 W, pulse mode), and sterile filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain blank LP with a particle size of 100–200 nm.

SFN-loaded liposomes (LPS): After vacuum drying the lipid film, based on a pre-experiment encapsulation efficiency of 50%, the film was hydrated with 5 mL of PBS containing 16 µM SFN at 60 °C in a water bath for 15 min under light protection and nitrogen atmosphere. Monolayer liposomes with a particle size of 100–200 nm were obtained by probe sonication (200 W, intermittent sonication in an ice bath) and concentrated to a final volume of 2 mL. After purification by ultrafiltration centrifugation, the encapsulation efficiency was detected by high-performance liquid chromatography. The preparation was stored at 4 °C in the dark, and its stability was periodically tested. The final formulation was sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and reserved for subsequent use in animal experiments. For cell experiments, the concentration of the stock LPS solution was proportionally increased and diluted with culture medium to achieve a final concentration of approximately 10 µM LPS.

Hydrogel based on P407 (HP), HP encapsulated SFN (HP@S) and HP encapsulated LPS (HP@LPS): First, 10 µM SFN was dissolved in 1 mL of water; then 25% (w/v) P407 was slowly added to the SFN solution (or LPS suspension), with gentle mixing to avoid bubble formation. The mixture was placed in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight, with intermittent vortexing until P407 was completely dissolved, forming a homogeneous and transparent sol system. The final obtained HP@S or HP@LPS hydrogel was allowed to stand at 4 °C for at least 24 h to ensure sufficient polymer hydration and gel stability.

Characterization of LPS

The average diameter, zeta potential, and size distribution of LP and LPS were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern Ltd., UK). Morphological examination was further performed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with negative staining. Before analysis, test samples were placed on a carbon-coated copper grid, stained with 1% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid, and air-dried prior to TEM observation. The absorption and fluorescence spectra of SFN were determined using a UV-Vis spectrometer (UV-Probe-1700, Shimadzu, Japan) and a multi-function plate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of SFN in formulations was determined using Sephadex G-100 microcolumn centrifugation. After establishing the Sephadex G-100 microcolumn, 0.2 mL of LPS was added to the top of the microcolumn, which was then centrifuged at 900 × g for 3 min, and the separated liquid was collected. Then, 0.2 mL of pH 7.4 buffer was added, and the column was centrifuged using the same method to elute the liposomes. This process was repeated twice, and the eluent was collected. Methanol was used to dilute the eluent to a volume of 10 mL to calculate the concentration encapsulated inside the liposomes (CEN). Another 0.2 mL of liposomes was directly diluted to 10 mL without microcolumn centrifugation, and the sample was determined as the total concentration (CTotal). The concentration of SFN was measured using a UV-Vis spectrometer at λmax of 280 nm. The EE of SFN was calculated as follows: EE (%) = CEN / CTotal × 100%.

Characterization of HP@LPS

After hydrogel preparation, gelation times were determined via a vial-tilting method. Different concentrations of P407 were placed in 2 mL vials. The sol–gel transition was identified as the point when no flow was observed upon vial inversion, and the time was recorded as the gelation time. Each value represents the average of three independent measurements. The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and Z-average of 2%, 4%, and 6% HP solutions were measured at 20 °C by DLS. The injectability of HP@LPS was visually documented with a digital camera. For morphological characterization, hydrogels were dehydrated by lyophilization. The microstructure of the freeze-dried samples was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Phenom Pure). The dynamic rheological behavior of HP@LPS was evaluated with a strain-controlled rheometer (Waters DHR-2, USA). Approximately 1 mL of hydrogel was placed between parallel plates (40 mm in diameter, 1 mm gap), and kinetic viscosity was measured from 0.1 to 100 Hz at a constant strain (1%) and temperature of 37 °C.

In vitro drug release

For LPS (liposomes encapsulating 10 µM SFN), the drug release profile was explored in vitro using dynamic dialysis methods. Briefly, 8 mL of free SFN or LPS was placed into a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) and immersed in 30 mL of 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution as the release medium at 37 °C. Aliquots of 1 mL release medium were extracted at 10 min, 20 min, 30 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h, and an equal volume of dissolution medium was immediately supplemented. The extracted medium was freeze-dried and then redissolved in 0.4 mL methanol. The amount of released SFN was determined by HPLC in triplicate. Cumulative release rates were normalized.

For HP@LPS, 1 mL of HP@LPS hydrogel containing 10 µM LPS was stored at 37 °C in a Falcon tube to facilitate gelation. Then, 500 µL of 37 °C PBS was layered on top of the gel. At predetermined time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 24, 48, 72, 120, and 144 h), 10 µL aliquots were collected and supplemented with 10 µL PBS. The concentration of LPS in each sample was quantified using UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 275 nm. A standard curve was established using known concentrations of LPS under consistent conditions. The LPS concentrations in the experimental samples were determined by interpolation from this standard curve.

Cellular uptake

HCFs were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in a 12-well plate. After culturing for 24 h, cells were incubated with FITC-loaded liposomes (LP-FITC) or hydrogels encapsulated with LP-FITC (HP@LP-FITC) for different time points (0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h). Both treatments contained an identical final concentration of FITC (2.5 µM). The experiment was stopped by rinsing the HCFs three times with PBS to remove non-intracellular FITC or LP@FITC. Finally, HCFs were photographed using a fluorescence microscope. Cellular uptake efficiency was evaluated by the fluorescence intensity within HCFs.

Cytotoxicity assays

HCFs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well and serum-starved for 24 h upon reaching 95% confluency. Cells were then treated with varying concentrations (0, 5, 10, and 20 µM) of SFN/LPS for 72 h in serum-free MEM. Cell viability was determined by incubating with CCK-8 reagent for 4 h at 37 °C, followed by absorbance measurement at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Live/dead assay

HCFs were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in a 12-well plate with 900 µL of basal medium overnight. Subsequently, varying treatments were added to each well. After another 24 h of incubation, HCFs were washed and co-stained with calcein-AM (final concentration 0.67 µM) and propidium iodide (final concentration 50 µg/mL) for 30 min. Then, green (live) or red (dead) cells were observed and photographed with a fluorescence microscope.

FITC-dextran permeability assay

The paracellular permeability of corneal epithelia was evaluated using a 4 kDa FITC-dextran (FD) flux assay, as previously described [26]. Briefly, HCEs were pretreated with PBS, SFN, or LPS for 6 h, followed by exposure to 50 µM H2O2 for 4 h. For permeability measurement, 200 µL of FD or FD-loaded liposomes (LP-FD) (3 mg/mL) was added to the apical chamber and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The fluorescence intensity of the basolateral supernatant was then measured at 485/528 nm (excitation/emission) using an EnVision microplate reader (PerkinElmer). HCFs were photographed using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate by the fluorescence intensity within HCFs.

Matrigel tube formation

A 96-well plate was pre-chilled, and 20 µL of matrigel was added per well and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well were suspended in 100 µL of a 1:1 mixture of supernatant and complete medium and divided into four groups: (1)treated with supernatant from unstimulated HCFs (NC group); (2)treated with supernatant from H2O2-stimulated HCFs (AB group); (3)treated with supernatant from SFN-pretreated, H2O2-stimulated HCFs (SFN group); (4)treated with supernatant from LPS-pretreated, H2O2-stimulated HCFs (LPS group). The mixture was then gently added onto the polymerized Matrigel. After 6 h of incubation, images were captured under a microscope, and tube formation was quantified using the angiogenesis analyzer tool in Image J software.

Collagen gel contraction assay

The three-dimensional free-floating collagen gel contraction assay was performed by mixing bovine-derived type I collagen (3 mg/mL) with 10× MEM, neutralization buffer, and HCFs suspension (1.1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) in a 7:1:1:2 ratio. Following polymerization in BSA-coated 24-well plates (500 µL/well), gels were treated with PBS, TGF-β (5 ng/mL), SFN, or LPS in serum-free MEM. Gel contraction was monitored daily through diameter measurements, with conditioned media collected for extracellular matrix protein analysis.

Immunoblot analysis

Protein extraction from corneal tissues or HCFs was followed by quantification using a BCA assay (Abbkine, KTD3001). Proteins were separated by 10-12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk (1 h, RT), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ZO-1 (Abcam, 1:500), Occludin (Abcam, 1:500), GPX4 (Huabio, 1:1000), FSP-1 (Abiowell, 1:1000), SIRT3 (Wanleibio, 1:1000), SOD2 (Wanleibio, 1:1000), GAPDH (Huabio, 1:5000), and β-tubulin (Huabio, 1:5000), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, 1:4000, 1 h, RT). Protein bands were visualized using ECL (Abbkine, BMU102) and quantified by densitometry.

Fluorescent probe detection

HCFs were seeded onto slides and, following appropriate treatment, then incubated with FerroOrange fluorescent probe (DOJINDO, F374, 1 µM), Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit with BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Beyotime, S0043S, 5 µM), dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe (Biorigin, BN11008, 5 µM), and Tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate (TMRE, MCE, HY-D098, 10 µM) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to assess levels of Fe2+, lipid peroxidation, ROS, and mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in HCFs. Imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

HCFs were seeded on slides and serum-starved for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were pretreated with free SFN or LPS for 6 h, followed by incubation for 48 h with or without 5 ng/mL TGF-β. Following the treatments, HCFs were then fixed (4% PFA, 20 min) and permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100, 15 min) at room temperature (RT). Between each step, HCFs were washed with PBST (0.1% Tween in PBS). Subsequently, 3% BSA was used to block unspecific antibody adsorption for 15 min, followed by incubation with anti-Filamentous actin (F-actin) antibody (1:140 dilution) for 30 min. Afterward, cells were incubated with DAPI for 10 min at RT in the dark. Finally, images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

Serum-starved HCFs were cultured in 6-well plates and pretreated with 10 µM SFN or LPS for 6 h prior to stimulation with 50 µM H2O2 for 24 h. Cells were then harvested and analyzed for apoptosis using Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining followed by flow cytometry. Under the aforementioned treatment conditions, the conditioned medium was collected following a 48-hour stimulation with 50 µM H2O2 and centrifuged (300 × g, 10 min) to remove cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of RPMI-1640 complete medium. RAW264.7 macrophages were then treated with this mixed medium and cultured for an additional 48 h. Finally, the expression levels of M1/M2 polarization markers were quantitatively analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACS Canto II). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8 software, with isotype controls and compensation standards set for quality control.

In vivo drug uptake

Experimental animals were randomly divided into groups, and 4 µL of ICG-loaded liposomes (LP-ICG) or hydrogels encapsulated with LP-ICG (HP@LP-ICG) at equal concentration (0.3 mg/mL) was applied to the corneal surface of each group. Sequential imaging was performed at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 40 min post-administration using a small animal in vivo imaging system. Prior to imaging, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane, and imaging parameters were set with an excitation wavelength of 745 nm, an emission wavelength of 800–850 nm, and automatic optimization of exposure time (typically 100–500 ms), ensuring consistent imaging parameters across all time points. The average fluorescence intensity in the corneal region was quantitatively analyzed using Living Image software (or equivalent analysis system) through the ROI method. Data were expressed in terms of radiant efficiency (photons/sec/cm²/sr), with background fluorescence (untreated control group) subtracted.

Induction of corneal alkali burn in rats

This study was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. 00723). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 200–250 g) provided by the Guangdong Experimental Animal Center (Foshan, Guangdong) were selected for the experiment. During the experiment, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. After instilling 0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride three times at 5-minute intervals on the surface of the right eye, a 5 mm diameter filter paper soaked in 1 mM NaOH solution was placed on the central cornea of the right eye for 20 s. The eye was then immediately rinsed with 50 mL of physiological saline for at least 5 min to thoroughly remove chemical residues on the ocular surface. The successful establishment of the corneal alkali burn model was determined when corneal edema, turbidity, and a grayish-white circular burn mark with a diameter consistent with the filter paper (5 mm) appeared. Rats were randomly allocated into five experimental groups (n = 10): (1) NC group (without alkali burn); (2) AB group (alkali burn with PBS treatment administered); (3) SFN group (alkali burn with 10 µM SFN treatment); (4) HP@S group (alkali burn with 10 µM HP@S); and (5) HP@LPS group (alkali burn with 10 µM HP@LPS). All treatments were initiated immediately post-injury and maintained throughout the experimental period.

Clinical observation of cornea after alkali burn

On the day of modeling and on day 0, 2, 4, 7, and 14 post-modeling, the progression of corneal lesions in rats was dynamically observed using a slit-lamp microscope system, and anterior segment photography was performed to record the degree of corneal opacity and the growth of neovascularization. Simultaneously, the extent of corneal epithelial defects was assessed under cobalt blue light after staining with 1% sodium fluorescein. After image acquisition, two researchers who were blinded to the grouping independently performed clinical scoring of corneal opacity, and quantitative analysis of corneal epithelial defect area and neovascularization length (CNV-L) was conducted using ImageJ software (v1.53a, NIH, USA). All measurements were independently repeated four times.

Histological examination of the cornea

On day 14, eyeballs and organs were enucleated immediately and fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h after euthanizing the rats, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, embedding in paraffin, and sectioning into 4 μm thick continuous slices. The morphological structure of corneal tissue was evaluated using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining (Solarbio, G1120). Meanwhile, immunohistochemical staining was employed to detect vascular and fibrosis markers CD31 and α-smooth muscle actin (SMA), ferroptosis-related markers GPX4 and FSP-1, oxidative stress indicators SOD2 and SIRT3, as well as Prussian blue staining to analyze Fe2+ deposition. Scans were performed using a P250 FLASH high-resolution digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Corneal cryosection and staining

After enucleation, fresh rat eyeballs were immediately placed in OCT embedding medium and rapidly frozen at -80 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 12 μm thick corneal sections were prepared using a cryostat and mounted on adhesive slides. Apoptosis detection (TUNEL assay): After equilibrating the sections to room temperature, they were fixed directly with pre-chilled methanol (-20 °C) for 10 min, rinsed three times with PBS, and treated with permeabilization solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (4 °C, 5 min). After PBS wash, sections were incubated with the labeling solution according to the TUNEL kit instructions in the dark (37 °C, 1 h), counterstained with DAPI, and then coverslipped. ROS Detection: After methanol fixation, sections were incubated with 5 µM DHE at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS three times to remove excess probes. Immediate observation was performed using a fluorescence microscope (DHE: Ex/Em = 518/605 nm; DCFH-DA: Ex/Em = 488/525 nm). All operations were conducted in the dark, and the control group was treated with ROS scavengers (e.g., NAC) to verify specificity.

Corneal TEM examination

First, freshly obtained corneal tissue was immediately placed into pre-cooled PBS buffer and quickly cut into small tissue blocks of approximately 1 mm³ using sharp ophthalmic scissors. Then, tissue blocks were rapidly transferred into a specialized electron microscopy fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde + 2% paraformaldehyde, prepared in 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4) and fixed at 4 °C for 24 h. After fixation, tissue blocks were rinsed three times with 0.1 M PBS, each for 15 min. Subsequently, post-fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, followed by gradient ethanol dehydration (50%-100%), propylene oxide substitution, and Epon 812 resin infiltration and embedding. Finally, embedded blocks were placed in a 60 °C oven for polymerization for 48 h, and 70 nm sections were cut using an ultramicrotome. After double staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, sections were observed and photographed under TEM.

Measurement of MDA, 4-HNE, and VEGF secretion

HCFs were pretreated with PBS, SFN, or LPS for 6 h and then stimulated with 50 µM H2O2 for 24 h. Clarified supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 120 × g for 5 min and stored at -80 °C. MDA, 4-HNE, and VEGF released into the supernatant were quantified using ELISA kits. For in vivo studies, rat corneal tissues were collected at 14 day post-treatment. The tissues were homogenized, and the levels of MDA, 4-HNE, and VEGF were measured using ELISA kits.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that followed a normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were made using an independent samples t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used following confirmation of homogeneity of variance, with Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test applied for multiple comparisons. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction (Dunn-Bonferroni test) for post-hoc comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of HP@LPS

SFN has demonstrated promising potential in treating various ocular inflammatory disorders [14, 27]. However, its clinical application is hampered by poor aqueous solubility, short corneal residence time, and rapid clearance, leading to low bioavailability [15]. To optimize the therapeutic effect of SFN, we developed a multifunctional liposome-hydrogel eye drops for efficient delivery of SFN (Fig. 1A). SFN-loaded liposomes (LPS) were prepared using a conventional thin-film hydration method. TEM revealed that both LP and LPS exhibited typical spherical morphologies with uniform size distribution and intact structure (Fig. 1B). The mean diameter of LPS increased after loading with SFN and PEG modification, likely due to surface coating and drug incorporation (Fig. 1C). DLS confirmed this size increase, with LPS measuring 114.6 ± 5.1 nm compared to 96.6 ± 4.4 nm for unmodified liposomes (Fig. 1D)-a size range conducive to enhanced drug penetration and retention [28]. Zeta potential values were 29.62 ± 3.29 mV for liposomes and 26.2 ± 5.3 mV for LPS (Fig. 1E). The decrease in surface charge is attributed to the shielding effect of PEG, which also improves colloidal stability [29]. The remaining positive charge facilitates electrostatic adsorption to negatively charged corneal epithelium cells, thereby enhancing corneal retention [22, 23]. UV spectrophotometry was used to determine the drug loading capacity (6.85%) and encapsulation efficiency (84.13%) of SFN in LPS. The intensity of the characteristic absorption peak at 275 nm decreased over time (Fig. 1F). Capitalizing on the advantages of hydrogels for ocular drug delivery, we developed a thermosensitive in situ hydrogel system using P407. Gelation time at corneal surface temperature (35 °C) decreased significantly with increasing P407 concentration: from 120 s at 18% to 22 s at 25% (Fig. 1J). A concentration of 25% P407 was selected to balance rapid gelation and injectability. DLS analysis indicated that P407 micelles began to form at concentrations between 2% and 6%, with a size of 55 nm and PDI of 0.39 at 6% (Fig. 1K, L). HP@LPS appeared as a light green colloidal solution at room temperature and transitioned rapidly into a gel network upon heating to 35 °C (Fig. 1G). It demonstrated excellent injectability through a 24G needle (Fig. 1H). SEM of the lyophilized hydrogel revealed a highly porous microstructure with uniform dispersion of LPS (Fig. 1I). Rheological studies showed liquid-like behavior (G″ > G′) at temperatures below 27 °C, a sol-gel transition point at 27 °C (crossover of G′ and G″), and stable gel formation (G′ > G″) at 35 °C (Fig. 1M, N). The hydrogel also exhibited shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing markedly as shear rate increased from 0.1 to 100 s⁻¹ (Fig. 1O), confirming its suitability for topical application. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the HP@LPS eye drops possess desirable thermosensitivity, stability, and rapid in situ gelation capacity at ocular surface temperature, enabling sustained drug release and making it a promising candidate for the treatment of corneal alkali burn.

Drug release and uptake of HP@LPS

To further evaluate the drug uptake capacity of corneal stromal cells for the synthesized hydrogel eye drops system, we prepared LP-FITC and HP@LP-FITC. Fluorescence analysis revealed significantly enhanced cellular uptake for both formulations. Over 50% uptake was achieved within 4 h, with HP@LP-FITC showing superior uptake, reaching nearly 100% at 8 h and sustaining until 12 h. The fluorescence intensity of HP@LP-FITC was 2.5-fold higher than that of LP-FITC (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, LP-ICG and HP@LP-ICG were synthesized to monitor in vivo release behavior using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Both formulations retained stability on the ocular surface; however, LP-ICG fluorescence intensity decreased by 61.67% within 40 min, while HP@LP-ICG decreased by only 18.06% over the same period (Fig. 2B, C), indicating a 3.2-fold prolongation of corneal retention. In vitro release profiles demonstrated that HP@LPS released approximately 48% of SFN within 24 h and reached a cumulative release of 90% by day 6 (Fig. 2D). These in vivo and in vitro results collectively confirm the sustained drug release capacity of HP@LPS.

The ocular surface barrier plays a critical role in protecting visual function. However, lipophilic corneal epithelium impedes the penetration of hydrophilic drugs [30]. Concurrently, degradation in the stromal layer enhances its negative charge, posing challenges for drug delivery [31]. To address this, an in vitro transwell co-culture model [32] of corneal epithelial cells and HCFs was established to evaluate the permeability of cationic liposomes across the epithelial barrier. Using 4 kDa FD as a tracer [33], FD or LP-FD was added to the upper chamber containing epithelial cells, while HCFs were cultured in the lower chamber (Fig. 2E). The results showed that LP-FD significantly enhanced trans-epithelial transport, with a 3.04-fold higher fluorescence intensity in HCFs compared to FD after 24 h (Fig. 2F), along with increased HCFs uptake (Fig. 2G). Moreover, alkali burn was found to downregulate tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin [34] (Fig. 2H), which was reversed by SFN and LPS (Fig. 2I). In vivo imaging at 1 h post-administration revealed intense FITC fluorescence penetrating deep into the stroma and reaching the endothelium after alkali burn (Supplementary Fig. K). In summary, the PEG-modified cationic liposomes leverage the negative charge of the corneal epithelium for enhanced electrostatic adhesion. Additionally, the amphiphilic nature of LPS enhances the apparent solubility of SFN and facilitates its incorporation into the hydrogel delivery system, thereby increasing its permeability within ocular tissues and ultimately offering a synergistic strategy for optimized ocular therapeutics.

LPS attenuates alkali burn-induced oxidative stress and ferroptosis to reverse corneal injury in vitro

Previous studies have demonstrated that marked oxidative stress occurs in corneal alkali burn, and oxidative stress serves as a principal inducer of ferroptosis. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent mode of programmed cell death [35], is a critical contributor to corneal cell death during alkali injury. SFN exerts potent antioxidative [36] and anti-ferroptotic [37] effects. It has been proposed that SFN may attenuate corneal damage by suppressing ROS [13, 37]. However, its effect in the context of alkali burn remains unclear. Building on previous research, we investigated the role and underlying mechanisms of SFN in corneal alkali burn. To mimic the oxidative stress environment of alkali injury, HCFs were treated with 50 µM H2O2 to establish an in vitro model. Administration of SFN at varying concentrations revealed extensive HCFs death at 20 µM (Supplementary Fig. A-C); we selected 10 µM as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. FCM demonstrated that H2O2 stimulation markedly increased late apoptosis in HCFs (Fig. 3A, B), whereas treatment with LPS significantly alleviated apoptosis compared to other groups. Consistent results were obtained from live/dead staining (Fig. 3C, D) and CCK-8 assays (Fig. 3E), both indicating that LPS attenuated H2O2 -induced cell death and enhanced cell viability. These findings suggest that LPS exhibits protective effects on HCFs. We further explored whether LPS reverses HCFs injury via dual suppression of oxidative stress and ferroptosis. In H2O2 -treated HCFs, ROS production (Fig. 3F, G) and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3J, K) were markedly elevated, accompanied by increased secretion of the lipid peroxidation metabolites 4-HNE and MDA (Fig. 3H, I). LPS significantly suppressed ROS generation, reduced lipid peroxidation, and lowered the levels of these metabolites. Furthermore, H2O2 treatment induced intracellular Fe2+ accumulation in HCFs (Fig. 3L, M), which was diminished by approximately 50% following LPS treatment. Western blot analysis further revealed that LPS simultaneously upregulated the antioxidant proteins SOD2 and SIRT3, as well as the ferroptosis-inhibitory proteins FSP-1 and GPX4 (Fig. 3N). Collectively, these results indicate that LPS attenuates HCFs injury by suppressing oxidative stress and ferroptosis under alkali burn conditions.

LPS suppresses alkali burn-induced angiogenesis and fibrosis in vitro

Previous studies have shown that corneal alkali burn induces marked upregulation of VEGF in corneal epithelial cells, stromal cells, and macrophages [38], thereby driving the migration of limbal vascular endothelial cells into the stromal layer [39, 40], ultimately resulting in neovascularization and fibrosis. Further evidence identifies SFN as a potent inhibitor of neovascularization and fibrosis, primarily through the downregulation of VEGF expression [11, 41, 42]. Consistently, ELISA results revealed that LPS attenuated H2O2 -induced VEGF expression in HCFs (Fig. 4D), thereby inhibiting tube formation in HUVECs (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, FCM analysis indicated that co-culture of HCFs with macrophages following LPS treatment markedly reduced macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 4C, E, F, G). Furthermore, given that neovascularization is closely associated with corneal fibrosis, 3D culture assays further demonstrated that LPS robustly suppressed HCFs-mediated collagen contraction (Supplementary Fig. D, E) and downregulated fibrotic marker F-actin compared to SFN (Fig. 4H, I). While M2 polarization plays a mild reparative role early after tissue injury, its excessive activation drives pathological CNV and fibrosis by secreting VEGF and TGF-β1 [43, 44]. Our LPS treatment reduced M2 polarization both in vitro and in vivo, downregulating VEGF and TGF-β1 to break this immune-stromal pathological crosstalk, thereby inhibiting CNV and alleviating corneal excessive fibrosis. Overall, these findings indicate that LPS effectively attenuates corneal neovascularization and fibrosis induced by alkali burn, thereby facilitating the repair of HCFs.

HP@LPS attenuates alkali burn-induced angiogenesis and fibrosis to promote corneal repair in vivo

Encouraged by the pronounced therapeutic efficacy of LPS observed in vitro, we further evaluated the therapeutic potential of the HP@LPS hydrogel system in a rat model of alkali burn. The corneal alkali burn model was successfully established using NaOH [45], and SD rats were randomly assigned into five groups (n = 10): NC, alkali burn (AB), SFN, HP@S, and HP@LPS. All treatment groups receiving SFN were administered a consistent final concentration of 10 µM SFN. We selected a 14-day continuous dosing regimen, based on the clinical evidence that the early stage of corneal alkali injury (0–14 days) represents the optimal intervention window, during which the initial pathological damage can be most effectively blocked. Treatments were applied topically (4 µL per dose) every 12 h for 14 days. Corneal structural injury was assessed on day 2, 4, 7, and 14 post-treatments. Under low-dose and low-frequency administration, both HP@S and HP@LPS groups exhibited marked inhibition of neovascularization and fibrosis, with HP@LPS demonstrating a more pronounced effect (Fig. 5A). Slit-lamp microscopy revealed significantly enhanced corneal clarity in the HP@LPS group, and quantitative analysis showed that its transparency score (0.75 ± 0.50) was lower than those of the HP@S (1.50 ± 0.58) and SFN (2.25 ± 0.50) groups (Fig. 5B, D, F). Moreover, the fibrotic marker α-SMA was reduced following HP@LPS treatment (Fig. 5I). Fluorescein sodium staining further showed that HP@LPS treatment promoted corneal epithelial regeneration during the early phase (days 2 and 4) (Fig. 5C, G). In addition, the neovascular length in the HP@LPS group (1.18 ± 0.25 mm) was significantly shorter than that in the HP@S (2.13 ± 0.17 mm) and SFN (2.85 ± 0.13 mm) groups (Fig. 5E, H). ELISA analysis further confirmed that VEGF levels (Fig. 5J) and endothelial marker CD31 (Fig. 5I) were both reduced in the cornea following HP@LPS treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HP@LPS markedly attenuates pathological neovascularization and fibrosis induced by alkali burn, thereby facilitating corneal repair and regeneration. Finally, we verified biosafety in vivo, the results revealed all the treatments were non-toxic to normal corneas (Supplementary Fig. H); in addition, no significant changes were observed in the heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys, liver, or serum parameters on day 14 after different treatments (Supplementary Fig. I, J). However, clinical translation will require further studies on manufacturing, storage stability, and regulatory aspects of this combination product, which will advance this therapeutic platform toward potential clinical applications.

HP@LPS repairs alkali burn-induced corneal injury by alleviating oxidative stress and ferroptosis in vivo

Finally, we explored the intrinsic mechanisms by which HP@LPS ameliorates corneal alkali burn in vivo, with a particular focus on its modulation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-mediated irreversible injury. In vivo experiments demonstrated that HP@LPS effectively mitigated alkali burn-induced cell death, as evidenced by reduced apoptotic markers in immunofluorescence assays (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, HE staining revealed that HP@LPS restored orderly stromal structure and multilayered epithelial morphology (Fig. 6C). In addition, HP@LPS attenuated alkali burn-driven oxidative stress, evidenced by reduced ROS accumulation within the stromal layer (Fig. 6D, E) and decreased lipid peroxidation products 4-HNE and MDA (Fig. 6F, G). Synchronously, HP@LPS diminished aberrant Fe2+ deposition in the stroma (Fig. 6H) and alleviated mitochondrial damage (Fig. 6I; Supplementary Fig. F, G). Importantly, HP@LPS exerted dual regulation of ROS and ferroptosis: both immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses revealed upregulation of the antioxidant proteins SOD2 and SIRT3 (Fig. 6I, J), accompanied by increased expression of ferroptosis-suppressive proteins GPX4 and FSP-1. Notably, the therapeutic efficacy of HP@LPS was substantially superior to that of SFN and HP@S. In summary, these findings indicate that HP@LPS exerts a dual inhibitory effect on both ROS accumulation and ferroptosis. We acknowledge that inhibitor or gene knockdown experiments would provide definitive causal evidence. Nonetheless, the strong correlations observed in our study, together with extensive literature supporting SFN’s anti-ferroptotic effects [46], suggest the specificity of our findings. Current studies have also shown that significant ferroptosis and oxidative stress damage occur during corneal alkali burn [3]. Ferroptosis, as an iron-dependent programmed cell death, triggers an inflammatory cascade, contributing to corneal damage. Given that ferroptosis is a key therapeutic target for corneal alkali injury, and conventional anti-inflammatory drugs are difficult to inhibit this pathway, the synergistic effect of HP@LPS in suppressing oxidative stress and ferroptosis is further highlighted. Therefore, SFN synergistically alleviates stromal damage and promotes tissue regeneration after corneal alkali burn by inhibiting the ferroptosis pathway.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both oxidative stress and ferroptosis are pivotal in the progression of corneal alkali burn. We propose SFN, a natural antioxidant, as a potential therapeutic candidate. Given its limited corneal penetration and ocular surface retention, a biocompatible nanohydrogel-based SFN eye drops were developed in our study. We found that HP@LPS exhibited dual antioxidative and anti-ferroptosis effects, thereby restoring corneal regeneration and integrity over a long period. This system could be a promising therapeutic option for corneal alkali burn and shows translational potential.
