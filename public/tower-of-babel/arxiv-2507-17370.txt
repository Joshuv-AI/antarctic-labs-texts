Clustering, order conditions, and languages of interval exchanges
Sébastien Ferenczi, Luca Q. Zamboni

Abstract
. For any interval exchange transformation T T (standard or generalized), if we define a morphism ϕ \phi from the set of letters to the set of the return words of a word in the natural coding, respecting the lexicographical order, the word ϕ ​ v \phi v clusters (for the Burrows-Wheeler transform) for the permutation of T T if and only if the word v v clusters for the permutation of the induced map of T T on the cylinder [ w ] [w] . When T T is symmetrical, all such natural codings are rich languages, and this implies that the two orders above are the same if w w is a palindrome. Finally, we generalize the result, proved by using the clustering of a word w w , that w ​ w ww is produced by a generalized interval exchange transformation if and only if w ​ w ww is produced by a standard interval exchange transformation, to non-clustering w w : in the symmetric case, w w is produced by a generalized interval exchange transformation if and only if w w is produced by a standard interval exchange transformation.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 68R15, Secondary 37B10

In this paper, we start by exploring the connection between a purely word combinatorial notion used in data compression, the
clustering
for the
Burrows-Wheeler transform
(see Section 3) and a much studied class of dynamical systems, the
interval exchange transformations
(see Section 2), the main tool being a property of languages and symbolic dynamical systems we call the
order condition
(Section 3). Our first motivation was a question of M. Lapointe
[
19
]
on the clustering of
return words
in the natural codings of symmetric (standard) interval exchange transformations, and its partial answer by F. Dolce and C.B. Hugues
[
7
]
, where
it is shown that every return word of a standard interval exchange transformation without connection is clustering, but for an unknown permutation. In the present paper we prove that every return word for every interval exchange transformation (standard, affine, generalized,
with or without connections) clusters for the permutation defining the interval exchange transformation (Corollary
4
), and no other permutation if the interval exchange transformation is minimal and aperiodic and the return word is long enough (Proposition
12
). More generally, if we define a morphism
\phi
from the set of letters (or a subset of it) to the
set of the return words (or a subset of it) of a word
w
in the natural coding of an interval exchange transformation
T
, respecting the lexicographical order, the word
\phi v
clusters for the permutation of
T
if and only if the word
v
clusters for the permutation of the induced map of
T
on the cylinder
[w]
. None of these results is still true for interval exchange transformations
with flips
(Proposition
13
).

Thus the morphism
\phi
does not exactly preserve clustering, except if the permutation of the induced interval exchange transformation is the same as the permutation of the original interval exchange transformation. In the important case of symmetric interval exchange transformation, we can determine for which words
w
this will happen, namely those whose
shortest bispecial extension is a palindrome (Proposition
10
). This uses the full combinatorial theory of
rich languages
, to which we add the new result that the language of every symmetric interval exchange transformation is rich (Theorem
8
); we also give estimates of the palindromic complexity of such languages.

The notion of clustering gives new insights on a difficult and much-studied question, namely the difference of behaviour between standard and generalized interval exchange transformations, see
[
13
]
for references. As was shown by the same authors in
[
10
]

[
11
]
, the clustering of a primitive word
w
is linked to a combinatorial property, called the order condition, of the word
ww
, which is also equivalent to the fact that this word
ww
is produced by an interval exchange transformation. In Section 5, we use this result to show that a primitive word of the form
ww
is produced by a generalized interval exchange transformation
if and only if it is produced by a standard interval exchange transformation. Then we examine what happens when
w
, but not
ww
, satisfies the order condition, thus extending the theory of
[
13
]
from infinite languages to finite words. Then the order condition for
w
, or for a finite set of words
W
, is always equivalent to its production by a generalized interval exchange transformation, but only in the symmetric case we can determine if
W
is generated by a standard interval exchange transformation; in particular, a single word
w
is generated by a standard symmetric interval exchange transformation if and only if it is generated by a generalized symmetric interval exchange transformation (Theorem
19
) while this is not the case for a set of two or more words.

2.
Interval exchanges

All intervals we consider are closed on the left and open on the right.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite alphabet. A generalized interval exchange transformation is a map

The

If the restriction of

If the restriction of

is an affine map of slope

The endpoints of the

, resp.

, resp.

In this definition, the defining intervals

are not necessarily the intervals of continuity of

and

are adjacent and sent to adjacent intervals in the correct order, thus

may be extended to a continuous map on their union. Thus in the present paper an interval exchange transformation

is always supposed to be given
together with its defining intervals
as keeping the same
T
but changing the defining intervals would change the coding.

Definition 2
.

For a generalized
internal exchange transformation

, coded by

, its natural coding is the language

generated by all the trajectories, namely the

(x_{n},n\in{\mathbb{Z}})\in\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}

We say a finite set of words

.

Definition 3
.

A language

is a grouped coding of an affine interval exchange transformation if it is the natural coding of a generalized interval exchange transformation where

is piecewise affine on each defining interval.

Definition 4
.

A generalized interval exchange transformation defines an (oriented) pair of orders

on

•

is strictly to the left of the interval

•

The orders we consider on alphabets are always strict and total.

The orders defined by

which are numbered from

and then rearranged according to another permutation

; then

\pi_{0}^{-1}x<\pi_{0}^{-1}y

while

; note that sometimes the orderings are by

The following condition was first mentioned explicitly, for the symmetric case, in

]
, where it is related to the Markoff condition

]
, and
[

]

Definition 5
.

A bispecial word

If a language

If the natural coding of an interval exchange transformation

The main result (Proposition 8 in the easy direction, Theorem 19 in the difficult one) of

Let

3.
Clustering

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{r}\}

If

Then the Burrows-Wheeler transform of

In other words,

We say

, where

is a permutation on

and

in

\pi x_{i}=x_{r+1-i}

.

Equivalently, we shall also say that

is clustering for the pair of orders
(<_{A},<_{D})
if

is the order on

denoted above by

, and

if
\pi^{-1}x<\pi^{-1}y
.

From now on, we shall always use a pair of orders to define both clustering and interval exchange transformations
instead of the more traditional ways, still used in the introduction of the present paper, of an order and a permutation, or a pair of permutations.

Non-primitive words

[

.

4.
Rich languages and identification of orders

Starting for pair of orders for

, the orders of the induced map of

on

is in the

of the permutation giving

from

, and that all permutations in that class can be reached

For this, we shall use the theory of

, defined and named in
[

, though this notion was also studied under different names in
[

and some similar ideas appear already in
[

]
.

Definition 9
.

A language
L
is rich if all full return words to palindromes are palindromes.

Lemma 6
.

Let
x
be a finite or infinite word on an ordered alphabet
A
satisfying the symmetric order condition. Then for each
a\in A,
each full return word to
a
in
F({x})
is a palindrome.

Proof

Here we denote by
<
the order
<_{D}
, and thus by
>
the order
<_{A}
.
Assume that
x
contains a complete first return to a letter which is not a palindrome. Let
w
be a shortest such complete return and let
0
denote the corresponding letter. Thus
w=0axb0
with
a,b
distinct letter each different from 0, and
x
(possibly empty) a word in
A^{*}
not containing the letter
0.
Without loss of generality we may assume that
a<b.
Note that the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
0a,b0
implies
a>0\Leftrightarrow b>0.

First suppose
x
is empty, i.e.,
w=0ab0.
If
a>0,
the the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
0a,ab
implies
b\leq a,
a contradiction. If
a<0,
then
b<0
and the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
ab,b0
implies
b\leq a,
a contradiction. This proves that
x
is non-empty.

Thus we can write

w=0ax_{1}x_{2}\cdots b0=0a\cdots y_{2}y_{1}b0

with
a<b
and
a,b,x_{i},y_{j}\neq 0.

We now claim that
x_{k}>0
and
y_{k}<0
for each odd integer
k.
We proceed by induction on
k.
For
k=1,
the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
ax_{1},b0
implies
x_{1}\geq 0
which in turn implies
x_{1}>0.
Similarly, the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
y_{1}b,0a
implies
y_{1}\leq 0
and hence
y_{1}<0.
For the inductive step, suppose
k\geq 1
is odd and we have
x_{i}>0
and
y_{i}<0
for each
1\leq i\leq k
odd. Then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{k}x_{k+1},0a
implies
x_{k+1}\leq a.
If
x_{k+1}<a,
then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{k+1}x_{k+2},ax_{1}
implies
0<x_{1}\leq x_{k+2}.
If
x_{k+1}=a,
then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{k+1}x_{k+2}=ax_{k+2},b0
implies
x_{k+2}\geq 0
and hence
x_{k+2}>0.
Similarly, the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
y_{k+1}y_{k},b0
implies that
b\leq y_{k+1}.
If
b<y_{k+1},
then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
y_{k+2}y_{k+1},y_{1}b
implies
y_{k+2}\leq y_{1}<0
and if
b=y_{k+1},
then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
y_{k+2}y_{k+1}=y_{k+2}b,0a
implies
y_{k+2}\leq 0
and hence
y_{k+2}<0.

It follows from the previous claim that
|x|
is even and that
w=0ax_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{2k}b0
for some
k\geq 1
and with
x_{i}>0
for
i
odd and
x_{i}<0
for
i
even. Now if
a>0,
then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
0a,ax_{1}
implies
x_{1}\leq a
while the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
ax_{1},x_{1}x_{2}
implies
a\leq x_{1}
and hence
a=x_{1}.
But the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{1}x_{2},b0
implies
b\leq x_{1}=a,
a contradiction. On the other hand if
a<0,
then
b<0
and the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
b0,x_{2k}b
implies
b\leq x_{2k}
while the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{2k}b,x_{2k-1}x_{2k}
implies
x_{2k}\leq b
and hence
b=x_{2k}.
But then the symmetric order condition applied to the pair
x_{2k-1}x_{2k}=x_{2k-1}b,0a
implies
a\geq b,
a contradiction.
This completes the proof.∎

Let

Theorem 8

Let

be a language satisfying a symmetric order condition. Then

is rich.

Proof

We have to show that if

is a complete first return to a palindrome

then

is a palindrome. We will prove by induction on

that if

is a language on an ordered alphabet satisfying the symmetric order condition and

is a palindrome. The base case of the induction, i.e., when

is precisely the content of Lemma
6

and assume that if

is a language on an ordered alphabet satisfying the symmetric order condition and

of length less than

is a palindrome. Let

of length

We will show

is a palindrome. Let

denote the first (and hence last) letter of

Then both

are each concatenations of prefix first returns to

(respectively

with respect to

(possibly empty) not containing the letter

Finally, by Lemma

is a palindrome on the derived alphabet. Thus writing
