Cell-Based Regenerative Strategies for Knee Cartilage Repair: A Scoping Review of Autologous Cartilage, Mesenchymal Stromal Cells, and Hybrid Approaches
Authors: Birzhan Suiindik, Kazybek Baktybergen, Yerik Raimagambetov, Gulzhanat Korganbekova, Dina Saginova, Arman Batpen, Karlygash Suiindik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCell-based approaches for focal knee cartilage repair include cartilage- and chondrocyte-based procedures, mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based strategies, and combinations of these approaches. However, the amount and type of evidence available for these treatments differ considerably.ObjectiveTo map the clinical and preclinical evidence for cell-based regenerative strategies used for focal chondral and osteochondral defects of the knee, compare cartilage-based, MSC-based, and hybrid approaches, and identify areas where evidence remains limited.MethodsThis scoping review was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework and reported according to PRISMA-ScR. PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for studies published from January 2000 to December 2025. Eligible studies evaluated in vivo cartilage-derived, MSC-based, or hybrid regenerative interventions for focal knee chondral or osteochondral defects. Two reviewers independently screened the records and charted study characteristics and outcomes using a standardized form. Because of substantial heterogeneity in interventions, study designs, models, outcomes, and follow-up periods, the findings were summarized descriptively rather than pooled.ResultsSeventy-five publications met the eligibility criteria. Cartilage-based approaches were evaluated in 29 publications, including 21 clinical and 8 preclinical studies. MSC-based approaches were evaluated in 43 publications, including 12 clinical and 31 preclinical studies. Three publications examined hybrid strategies, including two clinical reports from the same 35-patient cohort and one preclinical study. Cartilage-based evidence was therefore mainly clinical (72.4%), whereas MSC-based evidence was mainly preclinical (72.1%). Clinical studies used a wide range of outcome measures, and comparative studies did not show a consistent advantage of one more complex cell-based approach over another. Evidence for hybrid strategies remained limited to one preclinical study and one clinical programme reported at 18 months and 5 years.ConclusionThe available evidence differs considerably across cartilage-based, MSC-based, and hybrid approaches. Cartilage-based techniques have been studied more extensively in clinical settings, whereas most MSC research remains preclinical. Hybrid cartilage and MSC strategies have been investigated in only a small number of studies. A framework based on structural or chondrogenic input, biological signaling, and the combination of these components may be useful for organizing future research, but it should currently be considered a hypothesis rather than a validated treatment model.

Introduction

Focal chondral and osteochondral defects of the knee are commonly identified during arthroscopy and remain clinically important because mature articular cartilage has a limited capacity for durable repair.1,2 Established procedures, including marrow stimulation, osteochondral transplantation, and autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI/MACI), can improve symptoms in selected patients. However, each approach has limitations related to repair tissue quality, donor-site morbidity, graft availability, procedural complexity, cell expansion, regulatory requirements, or the need for staged surgery.2,3 These limitations have encouraged continued development of cell-based regenerative treatments for the knee.

Current cell-based treatments can be divided into three broad groups. Cartilage- and chondrocyte-based techniques use autologous cartilage fragments, chondrons, or expanded chondrocytes as a source of chondrogenic cells and extracellular matrix.2,4,5 MSC-based strategies use culture-expanded cells or point-of-care products such as bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) and stromal vascular fraction (SVF). Their proposed effects include trophic, immunomodulatory, and matrix-regulatory signaling.6-10 These products differ in their biological characteristics and regulatory status and should not be considered equivalent.11,12 A third approach combines a cartilage-derived component with an MSC-based component within the same treatment strategy.13-15

Although these approaches have the same general goal of restoring knee cartilage, they have often been studied separately and are supported by different amounts of clinical and preclinical evidence.5,7 This makes it difficult to compare how far each approach has progressed toward clinical use and where important evidence gaps remain. This scoping review therefore aimed to map and compare the clinical and preclinical evidence for cartilage-based, MSC-based, and hybrid regenerative strategies for focal knee cartilage repair. Accordingly, the review question was: what clinical and preclinical evidence is available for cartilage-based, MSC-based, and hybrid regenerative strategies for focal chondral and osteochondral defects of the knee, and what are the principal clinical and translational evidence gaps across these approaches? Methods

Study Design and Registration

This scoping review was conducted using a structured scoping-review methodology and reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). 16 The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework on 17 May 2026 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/Q52T8). The choice of a scoping design reflected the breadth of the review question, the heterogeneity of interventions and evidence types, and the aim of mapping the evidence landscape rather than estimating a single pooled treatment effect. The descriptive analytical approach is consistent with JBI scoping-review guidance, which permits structured mapping and presentation of heterogeneous evidence when meta-analysis is not the review objective.17,18

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility was restricted to in vivo clinical and preclinical studies of regenerative treatment for focal chondral or osteochondral defects of the knee. Information Sources and Search Strategy

PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for publications from January 2000 through December 2025. Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were also screened for additional records. The last search was performed on 1 December 2025. The detailed search concepts and Boolean search blocks are provided in Appendix 1.

Study Selection

Records were exported to Rayyan and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria after a pilot exercise involving 50 randomly selected records. The same reviewers independently assessed potentially eligible full texts. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (Y.R.). The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of the study-selection process is presented in Figure 2.Figure 2.PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of study selection

Data Charting

A standardized Microsoft Excel charting form was pilot-tested on five randomly selected studies and refined iteratively. Data were charted independently by two reviewers, cross-checked for consistency, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion or third-reviewer adjudication. Charted variables included publication year, study design and clinical level of evidence, human or animal model, defect characteristics, intervention and comparator details, follow-up, clinical outcomes, MRI outcomes, histological outcomes, failures/adverse events, and key findings.

Synthesis and Presentation

The included studies differed substantially in design, animal models, interventions, comparators, outcome measures, and follow-up. For this reason, treatment effects were not statistically pooled. The evidence was summarized descriptively by reporting the numbers and proportions of clinical and preclinical studies in each treatment group. Comparative findings, reported outcomes, mechanistic observations, and remaining evidence gaps were then summarized narratively. The heterogeneous before-and-after data were not treated as comparable estimates of treatment effectiveness.17,18

Critical Appraisal of Individual Sources

Formal risk-of-bias assessment was not undertaken because the primary aim was evidence mapping rather than pooled effectiveness estimation. To provide readers with context regarding clinical evidence maturity, clinical publications were classified using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine 2011 Levels of Evidence framework. 19 No equivalent level-of-evidence grade was assigned to preclinical animal studies.

Results

Study Selection and Overall Evidence Map

The search identified 2,783 records. After duplicate removal, 1,919 records underwent title/abstract screening and 221 reports underwent full-text assessment. A total of 146 full texts were excluded (reviews/editorials/conference abstracts, n=42; not relevant to the review question, n=47; insufficient methodological or outcome data, n=33; in vitro studies without in vivo translational evaluation, n=19; and non-English full texts, n=5). Seventy-five publications were included.

The evidence base was distributed unevenly across the three prespecified domains (Table 2). Cartilage-based approaches comprised 29 publications, of which 21 (72.4%) were clinical and 8 (27.6%) preclinical. MSC-based approaches comprised 43 publications, of which 12 (27.9%) were clinical and 31 (72.1%) preclinical. Cartilage- and Chondrocyte-Based Strategies

Twenty-nine publications evaluated cartilage-derived approaches: 21 clinical and 8 preclinical. Clinical follow-up ranged from approximately 12 months to 9 years, with levels of evidence spanning randomized trials, comparative observational studies, and case series. Rather than treating within-group pre/post improvement as evidence of comparative effectiveness, the revised synthesis focuses on the pattern of comparative findings. Comparative studies reported mixed findings, with no consistent advantage of one procedure across the reported clinical outcomes.22-26,40-54

The eight preclinical cartilage-based publications used porcine, ovine, leporine, minipig, and mouse models. Several experiments supported the biological plausibility of autologous cartilage fragments as a repair substrate; for example, cartilage-chip constructs produced greater hyaline-cartilage proportions or type-II collagen expression than selected control strategies in some models.55-62 These findings were retained as preclinical evidence and were not interpreted as direct estimates of clinical effectiveness.

MSC-Based Strategies

Forty-three publications evaluated MSC-based approaches, including 12 clinical and 31 preclinical studies. The clinical literature encompassed culture-expanded bone-marrow, synovial, and adipose-derived stromal cells as well as point-of-care BMAC and SVF products.28-39 Because these interventions differ substantially in composition, processing, dose, and regulatory status, they were mapped within one broad domain for scoping purposes but were not considered therapeutically equivalent.11,12 Clinical outcome instruments included KOOS, IKDC, pain scores, and imaging measures; however, heterogeneity in study design and reporting precluded a valid pooled estimate across products.63-77

The preclinical MSC literature was the largest single body of evidence in the review (31 publications).78-93 It included culture-expanded MSCs, BMAC/SVF-related strategies, three-dimensional spheroids or constructs, and extracellular-vesicle-based approaches delivered by injection or scaffold-assisted implantation. While many studies reported histological improvement relative to selected controls, others found no added benefit of MSC supplementation or reported adverse tissue responses such as fibrosis.78,82 Cell-tracking and donor-DNA observations also indicated that durable structural engraftment is not a universal explanation for observed effects.14,15,64,90,91

Hybrid Cartilage–MSC Strategies

Only three publications explicitly combined a cartilage-derived component with an MSC-based component. Ba et al. 13 evaluated a preclinical cartilage/SVF construct. The clinical evidence consisted of two reports from the same 35-patient programme combining recycled autologous chondrons with allogeneic bone-marrow-derived MSCs.14,15 At 18 months, the programme reported improvement in KOOS and pain scores together with compositional MRI and tissue analyses; at 5 years, the cumulative failure rate was 14.3% (5/35).14,15 Because the two clinical papers represent follow-up of the same cohort, the hybrid domain should be interpreted as one clinical programme rather than two independent clinical replications.

Discussion

Principal Findings and Clinical Relevance

This scoping review identifies a marked difference in translational maturity across three cell-based knee cartilage-repair domains. Nearly three quarters of cartilage-based publications were clinical, whereas nearly three quarters of MSC-based publications were preclinical. Hybrid cartilage–MSC strategies were represented by only three publications and only one independent clinical cohort. This distribution is clinically relevant because the volume of literature around a technology should not be equated with the maturity or comparative strength of its clinical evidence.

A second clinically important finding is that improvement in patient-reported scores within uncontrolled cohorts cannot, by itself, establish superiority of one regenerative strategy over another. Several comparative studies in the cartilage-based literature reported similar outcomes between technically different procedures, including minced cartilage versus ACI, biphasic cartilage repair versus microfracture, and minced cartilage versus microfracture or AMIC.23,48,50,54 Accordingly, the revised interpretation emphasizes comparative design, clinical evidence maturity, and structural outcome assessment rather than the simple frequency of statistically significant pre/post improvement. 94

Clinical and Structural Outcomes Should Be Interpreted Separately

Across the clinical literature, symptomatic and functional improvement was not consistently mirrored by complete structural restoration on MRI. MOCART remains widely used for morphological assessment, but its relationship with clinical outcomes is limited and it does not directly establish hyaline-cartilage composition.95-99 Follow-up also ranged widely, which makes direct comparison of structural scores at different time points problematic. Compositional techniques such as T2 and T1ρ mapping may provide complementary information about tissue characteristics, but they were reported in only a subset of studies.14,31,98,99 For clinical interpretation, patient-reported and structural outcomes should therefore be reported as related but distinct domains rather than as interchangeable markers of regeneration.11,12,100-104

MSC Evidence: Product Heterogeneity and Mechanistic Interpretation

The MSC literature requires particular caution because the term “MSC-based” encompasses products with different cellular composition and manufacturing pathways. Culture-expanded stromal cells, BMAC, SVF, spheroids, and extracellular-vesicle approaches were grouped for mapping, not because they are biologically interchangeable.11,12 Mechanistically, the historical concept of MSCs as direct cartilage-forming replacement cells has evolved toward a broader model of trophic and immunomodulatory signaling.8-10 Findings in the included literature are compatible with this interpretation: donor MSC DNA was not detected in long-term repair tissue in the hybrid clinical programme, and selected tracking studies did not demonstrate persistent localization of injected cells within the defect.14,15,64,90 These observations support a signaling hypothesis but do not prove a single mechanism across all MSC products.

Hybrid Evidence and the Proposed Three-Component Model

Beyond the three therapeutic domains used to organize the evidence, the findings suggest a hypothesis-generating three-component model for cartilage regeneration comprising (1) a structural substrate, (2) a cellular or chondrogenic input, and (3) a supportive biological signaling environment. Hybrid strategies may represent one approach to integrating these components, although current clinical evidence is insufficient to establish whether such integration improves outcomes. The limited overlap between cartilage-based and MSC-based research suggests that these approaches may play different roles in cartilage repair. Cartilage-derived strategies mainly provide cells and matrix with chondrogenic potential, whereas MSC-based approaches may influence the local biological environment through cell signaling. Hybrid strategies combine these two components within the same treatment. This framework may help guide future comparative studies and generate new hypotheses, but it should not be considered a validated biological model or a basis for clinical recommendations. Evidence for hybrid approaches remains very limited, with only one preclinical study and one clinical programme identified in this review. Therefore, any potential benefit from combining these approaches remains uncertain.13-15

Implications for Clinical Research

The mapped evidence suggests several priorities. First, future knee cartilage trials should distinguish culture-expanded MSC products from point-of-care concentrates and other biologics rather than pooling them under a single therapeutic label. Second, comparative studies should report both patient-centered and structural outcomes using consistent time points and definitions, with longer follow-up where feasible. Third, hybrid strategies require independent replication and adequately powered comparative trials before any claim of added value over established cartilage-repair procedures can be made. Finally, clinical interpretation should prioritize comparative evidence over uncontrolled pre/post score changes when judging whether additional cellular complexity produces meaningful benefit.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. The included evidence was heterogeneous in design, follow-up, patient populations, animal models, intervention composition, and outcome reporting, which precluded pooled comparative effect estimates. The broad MSC category included culture-expanded cells, BMAC, SVF, adipose-derived products, and extracellular-vesicle strategies; grouping them was useful for evidence mapping but does not imply equivalence. Only English-language publications were included, and grey literature was not analyzed. Most included publications reported favorable findings, raising the possibility of publication or selective-reporting bias. 105 Finally, the hybrid domain was represented by only three publications, including two reports from the same clinical cohort, and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

For focal knee cartilage repair, the available evidence differs across cartilage-based, MSC-based, and hybrid approaches. Cartilage-based techniques have been studied more extensively in clinical settings, whereas most MSC studies remain preclinical and include a wide range of cell products. Evidence for hybrid cartilage and MSC approaches is still very limited. The proposed three-component framework may be useful for planning future studies, but it requires further testing before it can be considered a validated model.
