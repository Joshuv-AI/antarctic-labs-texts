Molecular mechanisms by which C1orf112 promotes endometrial cancer progression and the development and validation of a clinical scoring model
Authors: Lan Wang, Xuan Meng, Yiping Sun, Siling Ren, Yi Lan, Liangdan Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the expression profile, clinical significance, and underlying molecular mechanisms of C1orf112 in endometrial cancer (EC).MethodsWe performed bioinformatics analyses using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas to evaluate C1orf112 expression and its association with clinicopathological parameters in EC. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses, as well as protein–protein interaction network analysis, were conducted to explore the functional roles and regulatory pathways of C1orf112. A multi-omics integrative approach was used to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying C1orf112 expression. Furthermore, we constructed and validated a prognostic scoring model incorporating C1orf112 expression.ResultsC1orf112 was found to be significantly highexpressed in EC tissues and its expression was closely associated with clinical stage, histological grade, and patient prognosis. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that C1orf112 and its co-expressed genes are primarily involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, and the p53 signaling pathway. Notably, our bioinformatics predictions suggest that C1orf112 may be subject to bidirectional regulation by RNA-binding proteins, including LIN28B and SRRM4, potentially establishing a regulatory balance between oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways. Multi-omics analysis demonstrated that C1orf112 expression is co-regulated by genetic alterations and epigenetic modifications, with promoter DNA methylation levels showing a strong inverse correlation with transcriptional activity. However, further molecular and phenotypic validation is required to confirm these interactions. The prognostic scoring model incorporating C1orf112 expression exhibited robust predictive performance.ConclusionOur findings highlight the potential clinical utility of C1orf112 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in EC, and provide new insights into its regulatory molecular network. This study proposes a conceptual framework for understanding EC pathogenesis and guiding the development of targeted therapies. Nonetheless, further prospective clinical studies and mechanistic investigations are warranted to validate these findings.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02003-x.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the fastest-growing gynecologic malignancies worldwide in terms of both incidence and mortality. In 2020, 417,000 new cases were diagnosed, and the global age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) has continued to rise [1]. The 5 year survival rate for patients with advanced EC (stage III–IV) is only 18% [2], posing a serious threat to women’s health. The median survival time for patients with metastatic EC is less than 12 months [3], with a 5 year survival rate below 15%, in sharp contrast to approximately 80% in non-metastatic cases. The mortality rate of EC has continued to increase, rising by an average of 1.7% per year globally from 2010 to 2019 [4]. Moreover, metastatic EC accounts for approximately 70% of EC-related deaths and remains a major clinical challenge [5]. Notably, the recurrence rate of EC is rising, the overall survival of relapsed patients is declining, and the treatment costs for recurrent or metastatic EC are 3 to 5 times higher than those for early-stage EC [6–8]. Meanwhile, the quality of life of these patients has markedly decreased [9]. Given this pressing clinical context, it is crucial to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying EC and to identify effective therapeutic targets and prognostic models.

Traditional histopathological classification has limited capacity to guide individualized treatment decisions for EC. Although molecular classification has enhanced risk stratification, it remains inadequate to fully support precision medicine strategies.In particular, the absence of effective biomarkers means that many advanced-stage patients miss the optimal window for treatment. Recent multi-omics studies have revealed several molecular mechanisms involved in EC pathogenesis, including activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [10], mismatch repair deficiency [11], and epigenetic reprogramming [12]. However, the regulatory mechanisms of key drivers in EC progression remain poorly understood, posing a major obstacle to the development of precision therapies.

C1orf112 is an open reading frame (ORF) gene mapped to chromosome 1. Recent pan-cancer analyses suggest that C1orf112 may contribute to tumorigenesis by modulating cell cycle checkpoints, particularly at the G2/M transition. C1orf112 has been found to be significantly overexpressed in breast, cervical [13], and gastric cancers [14], with expression levels positively correlated with tumor stage. Experimental studies have shown that C1orf112 promotes the G1/S phase transition, supporting its oncogenic role and implicating it in the initiation and progression of EC. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which C1orf112 contributes to EC remain unclear, particularly regarding its potential functions beyond cell cycle regulation. Further studies are needed to elucidate its upstream regulatory network and evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target.

In this study, a multi-dimensional strategy was employed to investigate the expression pattern, biological roles, and clinical relevance of C1orf112 in EC. Initially, differential expression analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset was performed to identify genes closely associated with C1orf112. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were subsequently conducted to explore potential biological processes and key signaling pathways. Furthermore, survival analysis and multivariate Cox regression were applied to construct a C1orf112-based prognostic model, which was then validated in an independent cohort. To further elucidate its regulatory mechanisms, multi-omics datasets—including somatic mutations, copy number variations, and DNA methylation—were integrated to identify potential upstream regulators of C1orf112. Finally, in vitro assays—including RT-qPCR, CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation—were conducted to validate the oncogenic role of C1orf112 in EC cell proliferation. Using this integrative approach, we systematically characterized the molecular, functional, and clinical implications of C1orf112 in endometrial cancer.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

This study utilized the TCGA pan-cancer dataset, which includes 33 tumor types and matched normal tissues, to systematically analyze the expression profile of C1orf112. RNA-seq data were normalized using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), and differentially expressed genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05. The expression pattern of C1orf112 in endometrial cancer was validated using the GEPIA2 platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/), and its diagnostic performance was assessed by ROC curve analysis using the pROC package with 2,000 bootstrap iterations.

Diagnostic and prognostic analysis of C1orf112 in endometrial cancer

Clinical data comprising 35 normal endometrial tissues and 548 EC samples were obtained from the TCGA database to explore factors associated with overall survival (OS) in endometrial cancer. The clinical variables analyzed included age, weight, body mass index (BMI), clinical stage, and histological grade. Survival metrics—namely OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI)—were employed to assess the prognostic significance of C1orf112 expression in endometrial cancer. The diagnostic value of C1orf112 was evaluated by ROC curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Samples were stratified into high and low C1orf112 expression groups based on the median expression level. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the survminer and survival R packages to visualize the prognostic impact of C1orf112. Kaplan–Meier analysis was conducted to compare survival distributions between the high- and low-expression groups, with the corresponding curves plotted. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05, with multiple comparisons adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Differential gene expression analysis

RNA-seq data obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were preprocessed using the limma package in R, which included background correction and quantile normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and microRNAs (DEmiRNAs) between endometrial cancer (EC) and normal endometrial tissues, C1orf112 high and low expreeson group were identified based limma package. To explore the EC dysregulation genes associated with high C1orf112 expression, we used the median C1orf112 expression value as a reference. C1orf112 expression values higher than the median were defined as high C1orf112 expression, while values lower than the median were defined as low C1orf112 expression; this median distinguishes ICC samples into those with high-low C1orf112 expression. Wwe performed correlation analysis on the DEGs with adj.p.Val < 0.05, logFC > 0.5, and logFC < −0.5 and the target gene C1orf112. The genes significantly correlated with the C1orf112 (p value < 0.01) were defined as EC genes related to C1orf112. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05, corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Functional enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on EC genes related to C1orf112 using the clusterProfiler (v4.0) package in R. These analyses included functional annotation of biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and signaling pathways. Significant enrichment terms were identified using hypergeometric testing with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05, and redundant terms were removed using topological simplification algorithms. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using MSigDB gene sets: c5.bp.v7.0 (biological processes) and c2.cp.kegg.v7.0 (KEGG pathways). The input dataset consisted of a normalized gene expression matrix and a continuous phenotype variable stratified by C1orf112 expression levels. Enrichment scores (ES) were calculated using 1000 phenotype-based permutations. unctional modules significantly associated with elevated C1orf112 expression were identified according to normalized enrichment scores (|NES|> 1.6, p.adj < 0.05). A pathway activity scoring model was constructed by weighting gene-level Z-scores to quantify the regulatory activity of key signaling pathways. Functional interaction networks were visualized using the enrichplot package, in which node density represented enrichment significance, and edge thickness indicated the strength of functional associations. This integrative approach enabled a systematic characterization of the molecular mechanisms by which C1orf112 modulates multiple pathways involved in the malignant progression of endometrial cancer.

LinkedOmics correlation analysis

Correlation between C1orf112 expression and selected cell cycle regulators (CCNB1, CDK1, CDC25C) was evaluated using the LinkedOmics LinkFinder module (TCGA-UCEC cohort). Pearson correlation was applied for continuous expression data, whereas Spearman correlation was used when data did not meet normality assumptions. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Correlation plots and complete results are provided in the Supplementary Figure S2.

Construction and validation of a C1orf112-based clinical risk scoring model

The EC-related genes associated with C1orf112 were subsequently assessed for their associations with overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS). Genes exhibiting significant prognostic value (p < 0.01) were retained, resulting in a panel of 50 candidate genes. Along with C1orf112, we defined these genes as C1orf112-related prognostic genes. Model performance was evaluated using the rms package, incorporating Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (log-rank test), time-dependent ROC curves (AUCs at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals), and calibration plots to comprehensively assess the model’s predictive accuracy for OS and RFS.

Analysis of the upstream regulatory network

Upstream regulatory factors of the C1orf112-related prognostic genes (51 genes) were identified through hypergeometric enrichment analysis,utilizing data from LncRNA2Target v3.0, TRRUST v2.0, and POSTAR2. The analysis encompassed lncRNA–mRNA interactions,transcription factor (TF)–promoter binding events,and RNA-binding protein (RBP)–transcript associations,with statistical significance set at FDR < 0.01. The regulatory impact was quantified using Spearman correlation analysis, (|ρ|> 0.6, p < 0.001), esulting in the construction of a regulatory network.

Multi-omics integration analysis

Somatic mutation profiles were analyzed using the maftools (v2.14) package, based on data from the TCGA-UCEC cohort, applying a filtering threshold of MAF > 0.01. Rainfall plots were generated to visualize mutation hotspots and potential driver genes within the risk-score gene set (MutSigCV q < 0.1). Copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed using the GISTIC 2.0 algorithm, with significant amplifications or deletions defined by a q-value < 0.25. Chromosomal alterations were visualized using Circos plots. The association between DNA methylation and transcription was evaluated using the MethylMix (v2.12) package, based on the inverse correlation between promoter β-values and gene expression levels (Pearson r < − 0.5, FDR < 0.05). Hypomethylation-driven overexpressed gene clusters were identified and integrated into a three-dimensional multi-omics regulatory network.

Cell culture

EC cell lines Ishikawa and HEC-1-B were cultured (both obtained from Cellcook, China). Cells were maintained in DMEM (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and 95% air.

siRNA transfection

Small interfering RNAs(siRNAs) targeting C1orf112 were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai,China). The sequences were as follows:NC-siRNA(5'-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3'); si-C1orf112-1(5'- AUAUUAGCACUAAGUACAG − 3'); si-C1orf112-2 (5'- UUACAAUGCACAAAUGUG − 3'). siRNAs were transfected into Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Three siRNA sequences targeting C1orf112 were designed according to the manufacturer's instructions, with careful avoidance of SNP regions and predicted secondary structure hotspots to maximize knockdown efficiency. All siRNAs were initially screened for knockdown efficiency at both mRNA and protein levels. In cases where knockdown at the protein level was suboptimal or not statistically significant (e.g., siC1orf112-2), additional siRNA sequences were considered for redesign. Each experiment was performed with at least five independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3) to increase statistical power, and effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to further evaluate knockdown efficacy.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative PCR (Q-PCR) analysis was conducted using the StepOne™ real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system. The mRNA expression levels were quantified using the cycle threshold (Ct) method and normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences were as follows: C1orf112 forward: 5’-GAGGACTGTCAAGCCAAAATTT-3’, reverse:5’- AGATATGCTGAACACATCCTGT − 3’; GAPDH forward: 5’-CCCGAGCCGTGTTTCCT -3’, reverse: 5’-GTCCCAGTTGGTGACGATGC-3’。

Western blot analysis

Transfected Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China). Total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (GLPBIO, USA). Protein samples were denatured at 95 °C after addition of loading buffer. Subsequently, the protein was dissolved with 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto the PVDF membrane for 2 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h.Membranes were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Monad, China). Protein expression levels were analyzed using gel imaging software.

CCK-8 assay

Transfected Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cells in logarithmic growth phase (1.0 × 104 cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (GK10001, GLPBIO, USA) and 90 μL of medium were added per well daily from day 0 to day 4, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Transfected Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cells were seeded at 500 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 10 days in complete medium. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted.

EdU assay

Cells were incubated in complete medium containing 10 μM EdU (Beyotime) for 2 h. After removing the medium, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min. The click reaction was performed in the dark for 30 min using a mixture of Alexa Fluor 594-azide (1:500), CuSO₄ (100 mM), and sodium ascorbate (100 mM). After DAPI staining, five random fields (200 × magnification) were selected under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83). The EdU-positive rate was calculated as the number of red fluorescent cells divided by the total number of blue-stained cells × 100%. Experiments included untreated, transfection reagent, and siRNA negative control groups, each comprising three independent biological replicates.

Statistical methods

Bioinformatics analyses were performed using the Bioinforcloud platform (http://www.bioinforcloud.org.cn). Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.5. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA or two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare quantitative variables, whereas the chi-square (χ2) test was employed for categorical data. P-values for all statistical analyses are provided in the corresponding figure legends.Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).

Results

Expression of C1orf112 in tumor tissues and its clinical relevance

To assess the expression pattern and potential clinical relevance of C1orf112 across cancers, transcriptomic profiles from 33 tumor types in the TCGA database were analyzed. C1orf112 expression was significantly upregulated in multiple tumor tissues compared to their corresponding normal counterparts, in KICH, the expression level of C1orf112 was significantly higher in the control group than in the disease group (Fig. 1 A, B). Notably, C1orf112 expression was markedly increased in EC tissues compared to normal endometrium (Fig. 1C), which also had a high diagnostic accuracy of C1orf112 in EC (AUC = 0.908, Fig. 1D). Further analysis demonstrated a significant association between C1orf112 expression and histological grade in EC. Specifically, C1orf112 expression was lowest in well-differentiated (G1) tumors, higher in moderately differentiated (G2), and highest in poorly differentiated (G3) tumors. For histological subtypes, the mean H-score was significantly higher in non-endometrioid than in endometrioid carcinomas (p < 0.001). Clinical stage analysis showed that patients with advanced-stage EC (stage III–IV) had significantly higher H-scores than those in early stages (stage I–II) (p < 0.01; Fig. 1F–G). C1orf112 overexpression was more common in patients older than 60 years (p < 0.001; Fig. 1H). Overall, C1orf112 is highly expressed in EC and is closely associated with tumor differentiation, clinical progression, and poor prognosis.

The C1orf112-based clinical model demonstrates significant diagnostic efficacy

Based on Cox analysis, we constructed a C1orf112-based risk score and subsequently visualized its distribution across samples in the TCGA cohort. (Fig. 4A). A total of 31 genes that significantly contributed to the score were included in the scoring model constructed based on Cox regression analysis (Table S1). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis reveals that patients in the high-score group exhibit significantly reduced OS compared to those in the low-score group (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, we observed that both the Cox model score and C1orf112 expression were positively correlated with clinical parameters, including OS, tumor incidence, stage, recurrence rate, and recurrence time. These findings suggest that higher model scores are associated with an overall increase in adverse clinical outcomes (Fig. 4C). We presented the significantly DEGs within the C1orf112-related prognostic gene set, whose expression alterations may be assocated with the expression level of C1orf112. (Fig. 4D). GO semantic similarity analysis indicates that CCNE1, TTK, TPX2, and SPDL1 share strong biological similarity with C1orf112 (Fig. 4E). We further constructed a clinical prognostic model and found that the C1orf112-based score, together with stage and survival events, served as independent prognostic factors for esophageal cancer patients. Moreover, a higher risk score derived from this model remained significantly associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 4F). Discussion

This study systematically elucidated the regulatory role of C1orf112 in EC cell proliferation through the integration of multi-omics data and functional experiments. Aberrant C1orf112 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in EC patients and may contribute to tumour progression by modulating key signalling pathways, particularly the cell cycle.These findings are consistent with previous studies on the role of cell cycle-related genes in EC [17]. The clinical relevance of C1orf112 was further validated, offering new insights into precision therapy and personalised management of EC.

Firstly, we found that C1orf112 expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues from EC patients in the TCGA dataset, and that its overexpression was strongly associated with poor prognosis, indicating its potential clinical utility for both diagnostic and prognostic purposes.Survival analysis revealed that elevated C1orf112 expression was associated with worse OS, DSS, and PFI in EC patients [18].This finding aligns with previous studies that associate oncogene overexpression with adverse clinical outcomes across a range of malignancies [19]. Additionally, ROC curve analysis (AUC = 0.908) demonstrated the high sensitivity and specificity of C1orf112 for distinguishing high-risk EC patients. However, this study primarily relied on retrospective data from the TCGA database, and external validation using independent cohorts is still lacking. Therefore, the clinical utility of C1orf112 as a biomarker requires further validation in large-scale, multicentre prospective studies. Furthermore, the association between C1orf112 expression and key clinicopathological features (e.g., stage and grade) suggests a potential role in risk stratification and prognostic assessment [20], thereby offering a novel molecular basis for optimising precision management of EC [21]. Future studies involving diverse populations and detection platforms are warranted to comprehensively assess the translational potential of C1orf112 as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. Secondly, C1orf112 may contribute to the malignant phenotype of EC by promoting the aberrant activation of genes involved in mitotic nuclear division, DNA replication, and the p53 signaling pathway, thereby forming a pro-oncogenic molecular network and acting as a central regulator of the cell cycle.

Further mechanistic investigations suggest that C1orf112 may promote aberrant proliferation and malignant progression of EC cells by modulating key molecular networks, including the cell cycle, DNA replication [22], and the p53 signalling pathway [23]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that genes co-expressed with C1orf112 were significantly enriched in biological processes such as mitotic nuclear division, DNA replication within the cell cycle, and the p53 signaling pathway [24], consistent with previous findings regarding cell cycle-related genes. Notably, this study is primarily based on bioinformatic analyses and in vitro experiments, and lacks in vivo or clinical validation of the direct regulatory effects of C1orf112 on these pathways. Therefore, the current interpretations should be viewed with caution. Differential expression profiling revealed that the gene regulatory network in the high C1orf112 expression group exhibited characteristics associated with reduced fidelity of DNA replication and evasion of cell cycle checkpoints in tumor cells [25]. Future studies should further investigate the functional mechanisms of C1orf112 in tumour biology, particularly its specific roles in cell cycle regulation and metastasis-related signalling pathways, to provide a theoretical basis for developing targeted interventions to improve EC prognosis.

The differential inhibitory effects of C1orf112 knockdown observed between Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cells may be attributed to several factors. First, baseline expression of C1orf112 varies between cell lines, with HEC-1-B generally exhibiting higher levels, potentially resulting in greater dependency and sensitivity to gene silencing. Second, the distinct molecular backgrounds of these cell lines—specifically, Ishikawa’s ER-positive, low-grade characteristics versus HEC-1-B’s high-grade features—may underlie divergent cell cycle regulation and checkpoint responses. Third, C1orf112 has been implicated in replication stress management and the DNA damage response by modulating RAD51 filament dynamics. Thus, differences in DDR pathway competency or inherent replication stress between the two cell types may influence their proliferation response upon C1orf112 depletion. Further studies involving basal doubling time assessment and cell cycle profiling post-knockdown could provide mechanistic insight into these cell line-specific effects.

Cox regression analysis revealed that high C1orf112 expression serves as an independent prognostic risk factor in EC, suggesting its potential utility in clinical risk stratification. C1orf112 expression was incorporated into a clinical risk model,and its prognostic performance was subsequently evaluated. Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the C1orf112-based model conferred greater net clinical benefit in predicting OS and RFS compared to conventional models, including FIGO stage, histological grade, and the metabolic risk score (MRS) [26]. Moreover, the C1orf112-based model demonstrated superior predictive performance in terms of C-index and AUC values. Consistent with existing evidence that molecular alterations influence tumour biological behavior [27] and therapeutic heterogeneity [28], our model supports a potential regulatory role for C1orf112 in the context of tumour heterogeneity. This study identified that a high C1orf112-based risk score was significantly associated with poor prognosis in EC patients, exhibiting robust predictive performance for recurrence risk and OS.

This study systematically analysed the upstream regulatory network of the C1orf112-based clinical scoring gene set and identified RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), such as LIN28B and SRRM4, as potential regulators of specific gene subsets within the network [29]. By applying hypergeometric testing and bioinformatics database analysis, we preliminarily identified the potential regulatory roles of RBPs in modulating the expression of C1orf112 and its associated genes. The results showed significantly elevated LIN28B expression in the high-C1orf112 group, with a similar trend observed for SRRM4 [30], suggesting that these RBPs may contribute to tumour progression in EC by regulating the C1orf112-associated network. The expression levels of RBPs are key variables in post-transcriptional regulation. Because their binding sites are often highly degenerate and in excess, competition between different RBPs can occur. Consequently, changes in their expression levels can significantly impact target gene regulation [31]. Lin28 is a prominent RBP, having been shown to bind to thousands of transcripts and regulate their abundance and/or translation [32, 33]. Fluctuations in LIN28 expression levels influence gene expression not only directly but also indirectly [34]. Most notably, LIN28 inhibits miRNA biogenesis, with the let-7 miRNA family being a key target. The let-7 family is a highly conserved regulator of growth, differentiation, and metabolism. Its expression, regulated by LIN28, may profoundly impact multiple cellular processes [35, 36]. Studies in EC have shown that overexpressed LIN28B binds to and stabilizes the long noncoding RNA PCAT5, thereby enhancing the stability of IGF2BP3. IGF2BP3, in turn, promotes aerobic glycolysis, proliferation, migration, and invasion of EC cells by stabilizing the key glucose metabolism enzymes HK2 and PKM2 [29]. Furthermore, Diao et al. reported that LIN28B promotes endometrial cancer progression by upregulating MYC and is closely associated with remodeling the immune microenvironment [37]. These findings are consistent with the high expression of LIN28B observed in EC samples in this study, suggesting that it may play an important role in EC development through multiple mechanisms. Several studies have demonstrated that silencing of SRRM4 can promote disease progression [38, 39]. However, its role in EC has rarely been reported. We therefore hypothesize that the downregulation of SRRM4 may also contribute to the progression of EC and may represent a potential therapeutic target. However, direct experimental evidence supporting the involvement of C1orf112 in EC progression via these molecular mechanisms remains lacking. According to previous studies, LIN28B enhances the stability of target mRNAs via m6A modification [40], and our analysis suggests that it may regulate C1orf112 through a similar mechanism. In contrast, SRRM4 may promote the degradation of C1orf112 by recruiting RNA deadenylation complexes; however, this antagonistic mechanism requires further experimental validation in the context of EC. The current conclusions are primarily derived from bioinformatic predictions and synthesis of existing literature. Further validation using molecular assays, such as RIP-qPCR and ChIP, is warranted to elucidate its specific regulatory functions. Overall, this study provides preliminary evidence that C1orf112 may coordinate multiple signaling pathways to modulate the malignant phenotype of EC. Nevertheless, its underlying molecular mechanisms warrant further investigation and functional validation. In our analysis, we identified that the EC-related genes associated with C1orf112 may be regulated by RBPs; however, no upstream lincRNAs or miRNAs were retrieved from the reference database. This absence may be attributed to limitations of the background dataset, such as insufficient data volume, restricted coverage, or incomplete annotation of non-coding RNA regulatory interactions in esophageal cancer. Therefore, the upstream regulatory findings in this study remain incomplete and warrant further validation through larger-scale multi-omics datasets or experimental approaches.

Integrative multi-omics analysis revealed that the expression of C1orf112 is regulated by both genetic alterations, including somatic mutations and CNVs, and epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation. At the genomic level, C1orf112 and its co-expressed genes (e.g., LTBP2, ATAD2, and ESPL1) frequently exhibited somatic mutations and CNVs. However, the precise impact of these alterations on C1orf112 expression and function remains unclear and requires further validation through functional assays. Epigenetic analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between the methylation levels of key CpG sites (e.g., cg23244997 and cg21909475) within the C1orf112 promoter region and its transcriptional expression, suggesting that promoter methylation may suppress transcriptional activity and thereby regulate gene expression. Notably, we hypothesise that the gene dosage effect in amplified genomic regions may partially counteract the suppressive influence of promoter methylation. However, this assumption currently lacks direct experimental validation and is primarily based on bioinformatic predictions, thus warranting cautious interpretation. In addition, the spatial heterogeneity of the methylation regulatory network remains speculative due to the lack of spatial transcriptomic or multi-region sampling data. Overall, this study proposes a novel mutation–CNV–methylation interaction model that underlies the regulation of C1orf112, offering a multi-omics perspective on its molecular control in EC. Nevertheless, the causal relationships and specific biological effects require further mechanistic studies and experimental validation [41].

In addition, this study not only elucidated the molecular regulatory mechanisms of C1orf112 in EC using multi-omics data and bioinformatic analysis, but also confirmed its functional role through in vitro experiments. Specifically, an siRNA-mediated knockdown strategy was employed to achieve efficient silencing of C1orf112 in Ishikawa and HEC-1-B EC cell lines. qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed a significant reduction in C1orf112 expression in the transfection group, validating the efficacy of siRNA-mediated silencing. Functional assays, including CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation, consistently demonstrated that C1orf112 knockdown significantly suppressed EC cell proliferation. These findings provide direct evidence that C1orf112 promotes EC cell proliferation and further support its proposed role as a potential oncogenic factor. Notably, these in vitro experiments not only validated the predictive conclusions of the bioinformatic analysis, but also laid the groundwork for subsequent mechanistic investigations and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. However, it should be noted that the cellular functional assays in this study primarily focused on the malignant phenotype of cell proliferation and did not investigate the effects of C1orf112 on other tumour-related phenotypes, such as migration, invasion, apoptosis, and tumour stemness in EC cells. In addition, whether the results of in vitro experiments can be replicated in vivo (e.g. in animal models), and the specific regulatory mechanisms of C1orf112-associated signalling pathways,remain to be explored. Future studies should integrate in vivo tumorigenesis models, transcriptomic sequencing, and pathway intervention strategies to comprehensively elucidate the role of C1orf112 in EC progression and its translational relevance. Interestingly, the degree of proliferation inhibition following C1orf112 knockdown varied between Ishikawa and HEC-1-B cell lines in the CCK-8 assay. Several factors may contribute to this differential sensitivity: First, baseline expression levels of C1orf112 differ between the two cell lines, as suggested by preliminary qPCR and Western blot analyses, with HEC-1-B cells generally exhibiting higher endogenous C1orf112 expression. This may render HEC-1-B cells more reliant on C1orf112 for proliferation and thus more sensitive to its depletion. Second, the distinct molecular backgrounds of the cell lines could play a role. Ishikawa cells represent an ER-positive, low-grade EC subtype, whereas HEC-1-B cells display features typical of higher-grade tumors. These intrinsic differences may influence cell cycle regulation and checkpoint robustness, affecting the cellular response to C1orf112 knockdown. Third, C1orf112 has been implicated in the regulation of replication stress and the DNA damage response, particularly through modulation of RAD51 dynamics during homologous recombination repair. Differences in the integrity of DNA damage response pathways or baseline replication stress between the two cell lines may further account for the observed variation in proliferation inhibition. Collectively, these findings indicate that the differential sensitivity to C1orf112 knockdown primarily reflects the intrinsic biological characteristics of the two EC cell lines rather than experimental variability. Specifically, Ishikawa cells, being well-differentiated and highly proliferative, show a more apparent suppression upon C1orf112 depletion, whereas HEC-1-B cells, characterized by greater migratory potential but slower proliferation, exhibit a comparatively modest response. This biological heterogeneity between EC cell lines underscores the complex functional role of C1orf112 in distinct molecular subtypes of EC.To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, future experiments such as measurement of basal doubling time and cell cycle profiling by flow cytometry (PI staining or EdU/BrdU-FACS) before and after C1orf112 knockdown are warranted.

This study has several limitations.First, the findings are primarily based on retrospective analyses of TCGA data and lack validation in independent external cohorts. The clinical utility of C1orf112 as a biomarker requires further confirmation in large, multi-centre prospective studies. Second, the proposed functional mechanisms of C1orf112 are primarily inferred from bioinformatic predictions and existing literature, with limited in vivo and in vitro experimental validation in the context of EC. Third, the regulatory interactions within the RBP network require molecular validation, such as RIP-qPCR and RNA pull-down assays, to confirm direct binding and functional relevance. Fourth, the spatial heterogeneity of C1orf112 expression regulation and its role within the tumour microenvironment remain poorly characterised and require further investigation using single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomic approaches.

Future research should focus on validating the clinical utility of C1orf112 as a biomarker through prospective multi-centre studies; secondly, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of C1orf112 in EC progression using in vivo and in vitro systems, such as organoid and PDX models; thirdly, exploring the dynamic balance of the RBP regulatory network and its contribution to tumour heterogeneity; fourthly, developing targeted therapeutic strategies based on C1orf112-associated pathways and evaluating their translational potential. These efforts will elucidate clarify the biological function of C1orf112 in EC and identify novel molecular targets for precision diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusion

This study highlights the clinical potential of C1orf112 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in EC, elucidates its regulatory molecular network, and proposes a conceptual framework for understanding EC pathogenesis and guiding the development of targeted therapies. Nevertheless, these findings require further validation through prospective clinical studies and in-depth mechanistic investigations.
