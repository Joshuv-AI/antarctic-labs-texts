Enhancing healthcare classification with hybrid multimedia data processing and deep learning TNBO FCNN approach in IoT-enabled environments
Authors: Leeladhar Chourasiya, Umesh Kumar Lillohre, Abhishek Kumar Pandey, Sarita Simaiya, Shimaa A. Hussien, Ehab Seif Ghith, Monish Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of IoT-based healthcare systems has led to an explosion of multimodal patient data (e.g., voice, images, signals), raising concerns about accurate classification, real-time processing, and data security. Existing machine learning approaches often suffer from limited feature extraction, high computational demands, and inadequate privacy protection, particularly in decentralised and resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, the lack of robust routing and data integrity mechanisms hinders their deployment in real-world healthcare applications. To address these challenges, we propose a hybrid deep learning framework that integrates Tunable Nonlinear Bayesian Optimisation (TNBO) for efficient routing and hyper-parameter tuning with a Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) for robust classification of multimodal healthcare data. Additionally, we embed blockchain technology to ensure the security, transparency, and immutability of patient data across decentralised IoT environments. Our method demonstrates superior performance in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity when compared to traditional models such as RTS-DELM and SCA_WKNN. We optimise the proposed architecture for energy efficiency, scalability, and real-time healthcare monitoring, positioning it for deployment in telemedicine and smart healthcare systems. Experimental validation on benchmark datasets confirms the efficacy of our model, while ethical implications and future research directions are also discussed to support broader applicability. The performance of the proposed model was evaluated against existing state-of-the-art techniques, including Hybrid Multimedia Data Processing, RTS-DELM, Multimedia Data Sharing Systems, and SCA_WKNN. The results indicated that the TNBO + FCNN model achieved an accuracy of 0.924, a sensitivity of 0.921, and a specificity of 0.926. Furthermore, the model outperformed comparative approaches by significant margins, achieving an F1-score of 0.915 and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.950. These findings validate the proposed model’s effectiveness in enhancing healthcare data classification, making it a promising solution for real-time monitoring and decision-making in healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33141-0.

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) has significantly impacted sectors such as agriculture, smart homes, and especially healthcare by enabling devices to seamlessly connect and exchange data1,2. In healthcare, IoT supports remote monitoring, efficient diagnostics, and treatment tracking through a network of embedded, interconnected devices3,4. Applications like smart grids and IoT-based patient monitoring systems are transforming healthcare delivery and addressing global health disparities5. Continuous IoT advancements have enabled real-time data transmission, boosted diagnostic accuracy and improved patient outcomes6,7.

Blockchain technology further enhances this ecosystem by securing electronic health records and enabling privacy-preserving, tamper-proof data exchange8,9. Its immutable and decentralised nature strengthens trust in data-driven medical analytics and reduces the need for excessive medical interventions in chronic illness management10. In parallel, multimodal healthcare systems which integrate images, audio, tabular data, and time series support complex decision-making by simulating clinical reasoning. Such approaches have shown promise across various domains, including radiology, cardiology, psychiatry, and public health8. As healthcare becomes more digitized and decentralized, risks to data privacy rise due to software vulnerabilities, insider threats, and insecure smart devices11. Blockchain addresses these challenges by using cryptographic hashes to ensure data integrity.

Several recent models, such as RTS-DELM12 and SCA-based Weighted K-NN13,15, demonstrate the evolution of intelligent classification and prediction systems. However, limitations remain in handling multimodal inputs and securing data effectively. To address this, our research proposes an IoT-blockchain-based system comprising device, communication, blockchain, and user interface layers. In the computation layer, spiral handwriting images and voice signals undergo processing, while the TNBO algorithm guarantees energy-efficient routing. The proposed FCNN model16 follows preprocessing, data augmentation, and feature extraction for classification.

Challenges in existing methods

Despite the widespread adoption of machine learning and deep learning in healthcare data classification, several limitations persist. Conventional models often lack effective feature extraction for complex multimodal inputs (e.g., audio, images), and manual feature selection hampers performance17. High computational demands and the need for large training datasets restrict real-time applicability. Many healthcare datasets suffer from class imbalance, leading to biased predictions and reduced sensitivity toward minority classes. Furthermore, inadequate privacy protections and centralised data storage expose patient data to security risks. Most existing models also struggle to generalise across datasets and fail to integrate multiple data modalities effectively, resulting in inconsistent classification performance4,11.

The rapid advancement in AI and deep learning has significantly transformed healthcare applications, particularly in disease classification and diagnosis. Nonetheless, current classification models face numerous challenges, including ineffective feature extraction, high computational demands, concerns regarding data security, and difficulties in managing multimodal medical data, which encompasses audio and image records22. These challenges hinder the timely and accurate prediction of diseases, underscoring the need for a more robust, efficient, and secure classification model. This study is driven by several critical factors: the demand for Enhanced Classification Accuracy. Current models demonstrate limited accuracy due to subpar feature extraction from healthcare data. A deep learning approach, such as TNBO + FCNN, can optimise feature representation and improve classification outcomes. Challenges in Managing Multimodal Healthcare Data. Existing models frequently handle these modalities in isolation, resulting in less effective decision-making.

A multimodal learning framework is essential for integrating diverse data types to improve classification accuracy. Literature review

The integration of IoT and blockchain in healthcare enhances patient-provider interaction, enables real-time monitoring, and supports cost-effective treatment delivery. This study proposes a hybrid deep learning (DL) model for classifying healthcare data, emphasising secure, efficient, and scalable systems.

Blockchain technology is recognised for ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and real-time control in healthcare environments. Taloba et al.1 introduced a blockchain-based security architecture using hashing to detect data breaches in multimedia health content. Rehman et al.2 combined blockchain with RTS-DELM and IDS to create a health monitoring framework for healthcare 5.0, although it lacked robustness against suboptimal data input. Similarly, Xie et al.3 demonstrated AI-enhanced medical data categorisation supported by blockchain for privacy and decentralised data exchange. Xi et al.4 and Tanwar et al.5 highlighted blockchain’s tamper-proof and traceable nature, making it suitable for secure EMR and EHR management. Sharma et al.6 extended this by integrating blockchain with IoMT to enhance medical data security and access. De Aguiar et al.7 emphasised blockchain’s role in interoperability and secure information exchange, while Goel and Neduncheliyan8 proposed DBDH—a deep learning-based blockchain security framework.

Recent advancements in IoT-enhanced healthcare systems notably include frameworks for personalised medication management, such as the MediServe project, which leverages deep learning for elderly care (Kapse et al., 2024)32. Similarly, AI of Things applications have shown promise in the early detection of brain tumours via MRI imaging, further exemplifying multimodal diagnostic capabilities (Khekare et al., 2025)33. Moreover, optimising diagnostic accuracy for chest X-rays by fusing hyper-parameter tuning with transfer learning approaches has led to more reliable disease classification systems (Wajgi et al., 2024)34. The integration of smart IoT-enabled diagnostic tools continues to evolve, promoting better healthcare outcomes (Yenurkar et al., 2024a)35, while IoT-based blood pressure monitoring systems are transforming chronic heart disease management practices (Yenurkar et al., 2024b)36.

Methodology

The selection of TNBO for hyper-parameter optimisation and FCNN for handling complex, high-dimensional healthcare data forms a robust framework for multimedia health data classification. Their combined use offers superior computational efficiency and accuracy compared to traditional models. Additionally, integrating blockchain technology into this framework enhances data integrity and security by preventing unauthorised modifications. While blockchain does not directly impact classification accuracy, it ensures the trustworthiness and consistency of training data, ultimately supporting more reliable model outcomes in healthcare applications.

Data collection

The Parkinson’s Drawings dataset19 provided the hand-drawn spiral picture used in this evaluation, while the mPower speech dataset20 provided the speech signal. The study evaluates the effectiveness of the suggested TNBO + FCNN-based healthcare classification model using the Parkinson’s Drawings dataset and the mPower Voice dataset. The Parkinson’s Drawings dataset comprises images of healthy individuals and patients with Parkinson’s disease sketching waves and spirals, which are used for training and testing sets to compare the outcomes with those of the original publication.

On the other hand, the mPower Voice dataset is a mobile device-based database (downloaded through https://www.synapse.org/#!Synapse:syn4993293) used to explore new techniques for tracking key indicators of Parkinson’s disease progression and identification, as well as matching standard behavioural symptoms with metrics gathered from sensor-rich mobile devices. These datasets are chosen for the study due to their relevance in providing diverse and comprehensive healthcare data, enabling the evaluation of the proposed model’s effectiveness in processing and classifying healthcare data from different modalities.

Proposed FCNN-based healthcare classification

This study focuses on applying IoT blockchain technology within a four-layer framework for the multimodal classification of healthcare data. The proposed health monitoring system, which is based on blockchain and IoT, is structured into four distinct layers. The device layer is responsible for collecting data, which is then sent to the communication layer for secure transmission. The TSNBO algorithm is utilised in this layer, incorporating performance metrics such as delay, distance, and throughput to identify the most efficient routing path. After processing in the communication layer, the data is securely stored in the blockchain layer as immutable blocks. The cloud layer then handles various multimodal healthcare inputs, including voice signals and spiral handwriting images. The voice signal undergoes preprocessing with a Kalman filter11.

Following this, feature extraction is performed using methods such as Multiple Kernel Weighted Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficient (MKMFCC), spectral centroid, mean square energy, spectral bandwidth, pitch chroma, fluctuation index, spectral flux13, and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC)14, resulting in Output (1) In parallel, the spiral handwriting image is preprocessed using a median filter, followed by image augmentation techniques that include flipping, rotation, and shearing. Feature extraction techniques, such as Complete Local Binary Pattern (CLBP)15, Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF)16, and statistical feature analysis25, are then applied, producing Output (2) Ultimately, outputs 1 and 2 are input into the proposed FCCN model for classifying healthcare data. First layer (device layer)

The system comprises a network of intelligent devices designed to monitor and manage patients’ health conditions, including implantable sensors, actuators, and wearable technology. These smart devices collect and store data in homes, hospitals, or other environments before transmitting it to routers for further processing and analysis. These devices fall into several key categories, including electrocardiography (ECG), electromyography (EMG), glucose monitoring, blood oxygen level monitoring, and blood pressure sensors. Additionally, smart environmental sensors track factors such as room temperature, air quality, humidity, and noise levels. Meanwhile, smart activity sensors monitor patient behaviours, including step count consistency, water intake, posture, and other daily activities, providing comprehensive insights into their health.

Detailed description of the TNBO algorithm

The TNBO algorithm is a unique optimisation technique inspired by the foraging behaviour of the Namib Desert beetle, which utilises environmental moisture for survival. The TNBO combines principles from the TSA and the NBO algorithm, converging into an efficient search method suitable for routing and data optimisation in IoT blockchain-based healthcare systems. The TNBO algorithm offers several advantages over traditional optimisation methods like genetic algorithms and PSO9,10. Its robustness in dynamic IoT environments makes it ideal for healthcare systems with variable conditions, such as fluctuating node availability and energy levels. TNBO focuses on minimising path length, delay, and energy consumption, providing targeted routing efficiency not addressed by general-purpose algorithms. The integration of TSA’s efficient search mechanism ensures fast convergence, crucial for real-time healthcare applications. Third layer, i.e. Block chain layer

Block chain technology has been implemented to enhance the security of IoT systems, enabling the integration of various networks, including Long-Term Evolution (LTE), sensor networks, and Enhanced Machine-Type Communication (eMTC), to facilitate seamless interactions among IoT devices. Every node upholds the entire functionality of block chain systems24. In this architecture, all nodes are organized as independent Control Units (CUs), each comprising several nodes. Due to their limited computational capabilities and memory, these nodes are unable to manage the entire block chain efficiently.

Consequently, a consensus mechanism is essential to ensure the proper functioning of each CU within the block chain ecosystem. The system’s design includes Function Nodes (FNs) and User Nodes (UNs). FNs act as block chain services, supplying critical data for creating, maintaining, and validating blocks. On the other hand, UNs represent users responsible for data generation and verification. A visual representation, as shown in Fig. 5, highlights a block chain-based healthcare application, emphasizing its significance in securing and optimizing healthcare data management32–35.

Block chain Integration for Data Security: The healthcare data collected from IoT devices are encrypted and stored securely on a block chain network, which provides immutability, traceability, and decentralized control. Each data entry is hashed, and the hashes are stored on the block chain, ensuring that any tampering can be easily detected. This architecture guarantees data privacy and integrity throughout the data lifecycle, supporting reliable training and evaluation of the classification models. Importantly, the block chain does not directly influence the feature extraction or training processes but ensures the quality of data feeding into these stages.

Integration of TNBO and FCNN for efficient routing and feature extraction

The Fully Convolutional Neural Network and Tunable Nonlinear Beetle Optimisation are utilised in the proposed healthcare classification system to facilitate reliable multimodal feature extraction and efficient data routing. TNBO optimises routing patterns between IoT devices at the communication layer by reducing latency, increasing throughput, and limiting distance. This guarantees that only pertinent, high-quality medical data—such as audio signals and images—are sent to the calculation layer.

The FCNN then processes the optimal feature set acquired by TNBO-guided routing. Across various modalities, the FCNN performs feature extraction, fusion, and classification. To predict healthcare outcomes, its design combines convolutional layers for processing audio and picture input, followed by deconvolution and classification layers.

Hyperparameter tuning strategy for TNBO and FCNN

A structured optimisation process was employed to fine-tune the hyperparameters of both the TNBO and FCNN models, aiming to achieve optimal performance. For TNBO, key hyperparameters, such as population size, maximum iterations, switching probability, and escape probability, were chosen based on previous research and then refined using a grid search, which systematically evaluated the search space for combinations that minimised energy consumption, routing delay, and distance. For FCNN, Bayesian optimisation was employed to tune critical hyperparameters, including learning rate, batch size, dropout rate, kernel size, and the number of filters in convolutional layers. This probabilistic approach enabled efficient exploration of the hyperparameter space, striking a balance between training time and model complexity22–24.

To ensure generalisation and prevent overfitting, the tuning process used 5-fold cross-validation. High classification accuracy and stability across multiple datasets were achieved, primarily due to this dual-optimisation technique. While the tuning approach is methodologically sound, the justification for selecting specific parameters for TNBO and FCNN, such as population size, number of iterations, and kernel sizes, lacks detailed empirical support or ablation analysis to validate their effectiveness31.

Blockchain integration for data privacy and security

In the proposed healthcare classification framework, blockchain technology is incorporated to provide secure and immutable storage and sharing of sensitive medical data across IoT networks. By leveraging a decentralised ledger, blockchain prevents unauthorised modifications and data tampering, thereby protecting patient privacy. Each data block contains encrypted hashes and digital signatures, ensuring data integrity during transmission and storage.

However, while blockchain improves trust and transparency, it also presents inevitable trade-offs. The verification and consensus processes can increase computational overhead and latency during data handling. Lightweight consensus mechanisms addressed these issues and streamlined smart contracts to minimise unnecessary processing. Despite these trade-offs, the security advantages of blockchain surpass the additional overhead, making it an essential element in securing healthcare data within the TNBO-FCNN system. While blockchain integration is a central claim of the framework, its contribution is currently limited to conceptual discussions around data integrity and privacy. The manuscript would benefit from stronger experimental validation or performance simulations quantifying blockchain’s impact, such as latency, throughput, or processing overhead on the overall system.

Ethical and privacy and real-world scenarios

The integration of blockchain technology into the TNBO + FCNN framework introduces significant ethical and privacy implications. While blockchain ensures data integrity and transparency, its immutable nature poses challenges to regulations such as the GDPR’s “right to be forgotten.” To address this, sensitive patient records should be stored off-chain in encrypted form, with only cryptographic hashes maintained on-chain. Furthermore, the system must comply with healthcare privacy regulations (e.g., HIPAA, GDPR) by implementing strong encryption, access control, and anonymization techniques. Ethical AI practices are equally critical; the proposed model must be trained on diverse datasets to prevent bias in predictions and subjected to regular fairness audits. Transparent consent protocols should inform patients about data usage, storage mechanisms, and the blockchain’s role in safeguarding their information. These measures collectively ensure that technological innovation does not compromise patient trust or ethical standards.

The proposed TNBO + FCNN framework has strong potential for deployment in various healthcare contexts. In telemedicine, it could facilitate remote diagnostics by integrating audio and imaging analysis to assist clinicians in real time. Hospitals could leverage the system as a decision-support tool, enabling radiologists to quickly identify anomalies in medical scans. Edge computing implementations would allow continuous patient monitoring in rural or resource-limited areas, while blockchain integration ensures secure sharing of electronic health records across institutions. However, real-world deployment presents challenges such as variability in data quality across devices, the need for regulatory approval, and potential bias if training datasets are not sufficiently diverse. Infrastructure constraints and initial implementation costs may also limit adoption in certain regions. Addressing these challenges will be essential for achieving scalable, equitable, and effective healthcare impact.

Discussion

The proposed TNBO + FCNN model demonstrated significant improvements in accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity over traditional methods like RTS-DELM and SCA_WKNN, showcasing the effectiveness of hybrid deep learning in multimedia healthcare classification within IoT environments. Unlike previous approaches that struggled with scalability, our four-layer architecture enabled efficient and secure processing of large datasets. However, the limited diversity of the dataset is a constraint, and future studies should validate the model across broader demographics and conditions to enhance generalizability. Integrating block chain technology could further strengthen data security, particularly in IoT applications where privacy concerns are critical.

We believe the TNBO + FCNN framework holds strong potential for real-time health monitoring in telemedicine platforms, with implications for policy development in healthcare data regulation.

Conclusion and future directions

Conclusion

The reliance on speech and images in healthcare classification poses limitations due to the narrow scope of available data and the diversity of medical conditions that require varied data types for accurate representation. This approach may lead to challenges in data quality, generalizability, and integration with other healthcare information. To enhance classification effectiveness, authors should broaden the dataset to include diverse medical data, such as electronic health records, diagnostic imaging, and wearable device inputs. A multimodal data strategy will enhance model robustness and accuracy, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes. In the context of future scope, exploring potential advancements in security models for multimedia data sharing in IoT would be beneficial. This could involve further research into enhancing the robustness and efficiency of security measures, addressing emerging threats, and adapting to evolving IoT technologies. Additionally, considering the rapid development of IoT and multimedia technologies, future research could focus on integrating advanced encryption techniques, block chain technology, or artificial intelligence for more secure and efficient multimedia data sharing in IoT environments. Block chain provides a secure backbone for data management, enhancing trust in the model’s outputs by ensuring high-quality, unaltered data input.

The proposed TNBO + FCNN model demonstrated significant improvements in healthcare data classification, achieving an accuracy of 92.4%, sensitivity of 92.1%, and specificity of 92.6%. These results surpass existing methods such as RTS-DELM and SCA_WKNN, confirming the model’s effectiveness in handling multimodal healthcare data. The high-performance metrics affirm the model’s potential for reliable real-time disease diagnosis and decision-making in IoT-enabled healthcare environments. Future work will focus on further optimizing the model’s “The combined TNBO and FCNN model’s inference complexity predominantly depends on the architecture of the FCNN, which, with optimized design, offers rapid predictions suitable for real-time healthcare monitoring. The inference operation’s complexity is proportional to the number of input features and neurons, generally resulting in low latency on modern hardware. Meanwhile, the TNBO optimization phase, which involves hyper-parameter tuning through iterative evaluations, is computationally intensive but occurs offline, thereby not impacting real-time deployment. This makes the model feasible for real-time systems, especially when deployed on edge or cloud servers, provided that the FCNN is designed with efficiency considerations in mind.”

Limitations and future work

While the proposed TNBO + FCNN framework demonstrates strong performance in classifying multimodal healthcare data, several limitations merit further consideration. First, the integration of TNBO introduces computational overhead, which may pose challenges for real-time or resource-constrained healthcare environments. Additionally, the blockchain layer, though beneficial for data integrity and security, can increase latency and processing complexity. The current evaluation is based on publicly available datasets, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity and noise present in real-world clinical data. Furthermore, the lack of clinical validation or field deployment limits the assessment of the model’s scalability, interoperability, and robustness in practical healthcare systems.

Future work should focus on optimizing the model for real-time edge computing, evaluating cross-institutional generalizability, and incorporating federated learning techniques to enhance privacy while reducing transmission costs. Extensive clinical trials, longitudinal data testing, and hardware-aware implementations are also necessary to bridge the gap between theoretical performance and real-world applicability.
