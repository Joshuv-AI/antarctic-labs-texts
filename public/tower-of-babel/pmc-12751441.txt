Growth and physiological responses of Grammosciadium platycarpum seed treated with seaweed bio-stimulant under salinity stress
Authors: Monib Lotfi, Ghasem Eghlima, Mohammad Hossein Mirjalili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Improving seed germination indices is one of the factors for achieving high yields in agriculture, which is affected by environmental stresses, especially salinity. Seed priming is one of the methods for improving seed germination parameters to cope with salinity stress. Seaweeds can maintain plant growth under stressful conditions due to their bioactive compounds, including hormones, vitamins, nutrients, and secondary metabolites. The aim of the present study was to investigate the effect of seed pretreatment with seaweed extract (0, 10, 20, and 30 g L− 1) on germination, growth, and biochemical indices of Grammosciadium platycarpum seedlings under salinity stress (0, 2, and 4 dS m− 1). The results showed that seaweed extract priming enhanced key germination traits, under non-salinity conditions. For seedling growth traits, although conditions were better in the absence of salt stress, seed pretreatment significantly reduced the effects of salt stress compared to the control condition. The highest levels of chlorophyll a and b were observed at 30 g L− 1 in seeds treated with seaweed extract. The highest levels of MDA and antioxidant enzymes occurred under 4 dS m− 1 salinity. Overall, seaweed extract, with its bioactive compounds as an organic, cheap, and accessible input, appears to be an effective strategy to mitigate plant in low-fertility and saline lands.

Introduction

The genus Grammosciadium, belonging to the Asteraceae family, comprises nine species, three of which, G. pterocarpum, G. scarbridum, and G. platycarpum, are found in Iran. These species typically inhabit temperate to cold temperate regions and mountainous pastures [1]. G. platycarpum is a perennial plant with a height of 40–100 cm, which is known in Iran as mountain parsley or mountain dill and is distributed in Turkey, Iran, Iraq, and Armenia. G. platycarpum is used as an infusion to reduce blood glucose plus lipids and it is consumed as food in some regions of Iran [2]. This variety is known for its appealing taste and fragrance is used as a vegetable and food additive in different regions in spring. The fruit of G. platycarpum has an essential oil content ranging from 0.81 to 1.6%, with linalool (65.9–81.6%) and limonene (9.73–15.34%) being its primary components [3]. The analysis of G. platycarpum leaf and fruit essential oil revealed that 26.1% of the leaf essential oil and 53.9% of the fruit essential oil were linalool [4].

Today, salinity is considered an abiotic stress and one of the most important and widespread agricultural problems worldwide [5]. About 20% of irrigated land and 1.2% of dry land in the world are under salinity stress, and this problem is expanding [6]. Salinity stress reduces the growth and quality of crop plants and ultimately their death through adverse effects on biochemical and physiological processes such as interference with photosynthesis, respiration, and absorption distribution, as well as a decrease in chlorophyll [7]. The high content of salt ions reduces the amount of water absorption by roots by causing osmotic stress and, on the other hand, causes the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which ultimately leads to a decrease in plant growth and yield [8]. Germination is the first and most sensitive developmental stage in the growth cycle of any plant and a key process in seedling emergence, which plays a major role in determining the final plant density [9]. At this stage, salinity stress is one of the limiting factors for seedling germination and establishment [7]. Therefore, various strategies are used to improve germination, seedling growth, and seed productivity under saline conditions, one of the most effective of which is seed priming with various solutions of natural and synthetic compounds [10]. Seaweed extract, with its priming effects, can increase seedling germination and growth under saline stress [7, 11].

Seaweed extract, due to its numerous chemical compounds including vitamins, minerals, amino acids and phytohormones, improves the growth, quantitative and qualitative characteristics of agricultural products by inducing numerous physiological processes in plants [12, 13]. According to studies, seaweed extract increased the germination rate and growth parameters of Citrullus lanatus seedlings [7], Pisum sativum [11] and Lactuca sativa cultivar New Red Fire [14] under salt stress. Therefore, the present study was conducted with the aim of determining the effect of seed priming with seaweed extract on germination indices and enzymatic activity of G. platycarpum plants under salt stress. The results of this research can help in cultivating G. platycarpum in low-yielding and saline lands to provide raw materials for the food and beverage industries.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and seaweed

The seeds of G. platycarpum used in this experiment were collected in August 2024 from its habitats in the northwest of Iran, from the city of Mahabad (longitude: 45°40′, latitude: 36°41′, altitude: 1626), located in West Azerbaijan Province. Taxonomic identification and authentication of the plant specimens were conducted by Dr. Ali Sonboli. A representative voucher specimen has been archived under the voucher number MPH-3249 at the herbarium of Shahid Beheshti University, Iran. Seaweed extract with the brand Armadine was obtained from Arman Sabz Adineh Company, Tehran, Iran. G. platycarpum seeds priming with seaweed and NaCl treatments

In order to improve the germination indices of G. platycarpum seeds under salinity conditions, a factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design with two factors: salinity stress at three concentrations (0, 2, and 4 dS m− 1) and seed priming with seaweed at four concentrations (0, 10, 20, and 30 g L− 1) with three replications was conducted in the Ecophysiology Laboratory of the MPDRI, Shahid Beheshti University in 2024. Before the experiment, in order to disinfect the seeds collected from nature. Seeds were surface-sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite, followed by 70% ethanol for 5 min each, and rinsed several times with distilled water. Forty seeds were placed on filter paper in each Petri dish. For priming treatments, concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 g L− 1 of seaweed were prepared and seeds were soaked in it for 48 h. For salt stress, concentrations of 2 and 4 dS m− 1 NaCl were prepared and the determined concentrations were used for irrigation until the end of germination. For the control, distilled water was used for irrigation. Petri dishes were incubated a temperature of 25 °C, a relative humidity of 60%, and 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness.

Germination determination

Germinated seeds were counted daily 24 h after the start of the experiment. Seeds with visible roots (i.e., about 2 mm long) were counted as germinated seeds and the results were recorded. Germination percentage, germination rate, mean emergence time, and seed vigour were calculated using formulas 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively (Table 2). Fresh and dry weights of seedlings were measured after 45 days using a digital scale (with an accuracy of ± 0.001). Seedlings were dried in an oven at 50 °C for 48 h.

Chlorophyll content measurement

The method of Arnon [18] was used to measure chlorophyll content. 0.1 g of fresh seedling tissue was ground in 80% (v/v) acetone in the dark on ice. The resulting mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was used to measure chlorophyll content. The absorbance of the supernatant was read spectrophotometric ally at 645 and 663 nm for chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, respectively. Malondialdehyde (MDA) and antioxidant enzyme activities

The activities of major antioxidant enzymes were assessed spectrophotometrically. Fresh leaf tissue (500 mg) was ground in potassium phosphate extraction buffer (pH 7.0). The homogenized sample was subjected to centrifugation, after which the clear supernatant was carefully separated and used to assay the activities of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), as well as to determine total protein content. SOD activity was determined using the Beauchamp and Fridovich approach [19]. APX activity was assayed by a decline in the absorbance of oxidized ascorbate at 290 nm, according to Jebara et al. [20]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content evaluated at a wavelength of 390 nm in step with the technique of Loreto and Velikova [21]. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by determining malondialdehyde levels following the procedure of Wang et al. [22]. Leaf tissue, pre-treated with liquid nitrogen, was homogenized in a solution of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The reaction mixture was analyzed spectrophotometrically at 532 nm (SCINCO S-3100), and MDA concentration was calculated accordingly.

Statistical analysis

The study employed a factorial experimental design arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD), incorporating four replicates per treatment. Statistical analyses were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with mean comparisons performed via the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 0.05 significance level, utilizing SAS software version 9.4. Assumptions of ANOVA were verified prior to analysis using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances. Origin 2021 software become used to plotting graph.

Results and discussion

Seed germination indexes

The results of two-way ANOVA of the data showed that the effect of seaweed and salinity stress treatments and the interaction of their effects on germination percentage, germination rate and mean germination time were significant (p < 0.01). The results showed that by increasing the seaweed concentration to 20 g L− 1, the germination percentage under salinity stress increased. The highest germination percentage (96.56%) was observed in the 20 g L− 1 seaweed treatment in the absence of salinity stress and the lowest germination percentage (45.75%) was observed at the highest salinity level (4 dS m− 1) (Fig. 1a). Increasing the seaweed concentration from 10 to 20 g L− 1 increased the germination rate, but increasing the concentration of seaweed to 30 g L− 1 decreased the germination rate. On the other hand, salinity significantly reduced the germination rate of G. platycarpum seeds compared to the control. The results of the study of the interaction effects of seaweed and salinity showed that the highest germination rate (32.83 seeds day− 1) was obtained in the 20 g L− 1 seaweed treatment, and the lowest germination rate (33.22 seeds day− 1) was related to the control (Fig. 1b). The application of seaweed at a rate of 20 g L− 1 under 4 dS m− 1 stress significantly increased the germination rate, which was not significantly different from the control and moderated the effect of salinity stress. The results of the study of the interaction effects of seaweed and salinity showed that the lowest average germination time (4.33 days) was obtained with the application of 20 g L− 1 seaweed and no salinity conditions, and the highest (15.03 days) was related to salinity stress (4 dS m− 1) (Fig. 1c).

Salt stress adversely affects the germination and growth of G. platycarpum by disrupting growth parameters and metabolic processes. To mitigate these effects, seaweed extracts have been suggested as a priming method to enhance cultivation in saline environments. Studies show that low osmotic potential from salt solutions primarily inhibits seed germination [11], while toxic NaCl ions can limit water uptake in plant cells [8]. Furthermore, the suppression of germination due to NaCl is linked to the downregulation of gibberellin genes, leading to lower levels of gibberellic acid and decreased α-amylase activity [23]. Seaweed priming enhances metabolic activity and antioxidant enzyme production, which are crucial for managing salt stress. The positive impacts of algal extracts on germination and plant growth are attributed to enhanced seed permeability to salts and their high concentrations of bioactive compounds, such as glycine betaine, phenolics, and ascorbic acid, which alleviate salinity stress [7]. Consequently, priming with seaweed extracts can trigger physiological and biochemical adaptations that improve germination rates and seedling vigor, illustrating their potential to promote growth in various plant species facing abiotic stress [7, 11, 24, 25].

Vigour index and growth parameters

Based on the results of two-way ANOVA, the effect of seaweed and salinity stress and their interaction on seed vigor index, fresh and dry weight of seedlings had a significant effect (p < 0.01). The results of comparing the means indicated a significant increase in seed vigour index with the use of seaweed. The highest seed vigour index (2110.67) was obtained in the treatment of 30 g L− 1 seaweed and no salinity stress. Salinity stress caused a decrease in the seed vigour index, so that the lowest seed vigour index (32.539) was obtained at the highest salinity level. The highest seed vigour index (45.1542) under salinity stress was related to the application of 30 g L− 1 seaweed (Fig. 2a). The highest fresh (1.67 g) and dry (0.34 g) seedling weights were observed in the treatment of seed priming with seaweed at a rate of 30 g L− 1, which increased 1.35 and 1.28 times, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 2b and c). Salinity reduced the fresh and dry weights of seedlings by 41.09 and 45.57%, respectively, compared to the control. The reduction in seedling length, fresh and dry weight is due to excessive salt accumulation in the cell wall, which reduces the flexibility of the cell wall and alters metabolic activities [26]. Salinity stress reduces the efficiency of transport and absorption of stored nutrients [27], resulting in reduced growth of G. platycarpum seedlings. The present findings showed that the fresh and dry weight of G. platycarpum seedlings decreased with increasing levels of salinity stress compared to the control. This could be related to the stress that salt imposes on the metabolic activities of the seedling through the creation of ionic and osmotic stress and ultimately the reduction of water potential [8]. Oxidative stress causes damage to plants by producing ROS. There are several mechanisms to reduce the damage caused by oxidative stress, one of which is the use of seaweed extracts, which reduce the effects of salinity stress. The use of seaweed priming to tolerate environmental stresses has attracted attention in agricultural production due to its unique bioactive components such as essential nutrients and micronutrients, growth regulators, choline chloride and glycine betaine [28, 29]. These seaweeds have plant growth-stimulating effects as well as elicitor activity, which causes plants to activate defense mechanisms in response to abiotic challenges such as drought and salinity [11]. The significant increase in vigor index, fresh and dry weight of G. platycarpum seedlings compared to the control could be due to the fact that ascorbic acid, minerals, glutathione and proline may play a role in the induction of antioxidant enzymes that reduce ROS levels in plants under salt stress [30, 31].

Photosynthetic pigments

According to the results of two-way analysis of variance, the amount of chlorophyll a and b in mountain dill seedlings was affected by the simple and interactive effects of seaweed and salinity (p < 0.05). The highest amount of chlorophyll a (45.26 µg g− 1 FW) and chlorophyll b (41.80 µg g− 1 FW) was obtained in the absence of salinity stress conditions in seeds treated with seaweed extract 30 g L− 1. In addition, the lowest concentration of chlorophyll a (22.06 µg g− 1 FW) and b (16.01 µg g− 1 FW) was observed in the salinity stress conditions of 4 dS m− 1 (Fig. 3). Environmental stressors such as drought, salinity, heavy metal contamination, and temperature fluctuations significantly challenge the photosynthetic apparatus. The observed increase in chlorophyllase activity, which leads to the breakdown of pigment proteins and a subsequent decline in chlorophyll levels within plants, may account for the decrease in chlorophyll content linked to salt exposure in this study. Increased chlorophyllase activity, which deteriorates pigment proteins and eventually reduces chlorophyll content in plants, could explain the salt-induced reduction in chlorophyll content described in this work. Salinity-induced chlorophyll reduction could be largely related to either the slowdown in chlorophyll biosynthesis or accelerated enzymatic chlorophyll degradation, as well as the degeneration of thylakoid membrane and the breakdown of chlorophyll via oxidative damage-induced ROS, in addition to the modifications in chlorophyll protein complexe. Furthermore, salinity has been shown to reduce the expression of several Mg-chelatase subunit-encoding genes and the concentration of chlorophyll biosynthetic intermediates [11].

Additionally, oxidative damage resulting from ROS can lead to the disruption of thylakoid membranes and further degradation of chlorophyll, alongside alterations in chlorophyll-protein complexes [32]. In this investigation, the application of seaweed extract to G. platycarpum seeds demonstrated a beneficial effect on the levels of photosynthetic pigments. Notably, G. platycarpum seedlings subjected to salt stress exhibited elevated concentrations of these pigments when treated with seaweed extract. This finding indicates that seaweed extracts may enhance salt tolerance in G. platycarpum plants, potentially due to bioactive compounds present in these algae that improve plant resilience and performance under saline conditions [33]. Moreover, the presence of various bioactive constituents in algal extracts, such as minerals, amino acids, and betaines, may contribute to their ability to mitigate pigment degradation and enhance photosynthetic capacity in plants experiencing stress [7].

MDA and antioxidant enzymes

The MDA level showed significant changes under the influence of seaweed and salinity (p < 0.05). Seaweed reduced the MDA level by 33.34% compared to the control. However, the MDA level under salinity stress increased by 95.83% compared to the control (Fig. 4a). With increasing salinity in the cultivation medium of G. platycarpum seeds, the activity of SOD and APX in the seedling increased sharply, so that the highest activity of these enzymes (SOD and APX were 58.97 and 0.44 µg g− 1 FW, respectively) was at the highest salinity level (4 dS m− 1). The use of seaweed treatment at different levels of salinity stress caused a decrease in SOD and APX enzymes in G. platycarpum seedlings (Fig. 4b & c). The lowest activity of these enzymes SOD and APX were 17.12 and 0.08 µg g− 1 FW, respectively) was in the treatment with 30 g L− 1 of seaweed in non-stressed conditions. The amount of H2O2 accumulation in G. platycarpum seedlings increased with increasing salinity stress. The highest (40.20 µmol mg− 1 FW) was related to the 4 dS m− 1 treatment. The use of seaweed treatments at different levels of salinity stress caused a significant decrease in H2O2 accumulation in mountain dill seedlings. The lowest concentration (0.85 µmol mg− 1 FW) of H2O2 was obtained in the 30 g L− 1 seaweed treatment (Fig. 4d). Increasing the activity of antioxidant and non-antioxidant enzymes increases stress tolerance [7]. The role of antioxidant enzymes in increasing resistance to salt stress in rice [34], Citrullus lanatus [7] and radish [35] plants has been reported. Seaweed extract neutralizes the harmful effects of abiotic stress by increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Antioxidant enzymes reduce the harmful effects of stress by inhibiting ROS [7].

Although the present study demonstrates the positive effects of seaweed extract on germination, growth, and antioxidant responses of G. platycarpum under salinity stress, the experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully represent field environments. Moreover, the study focused on a single seaweed concentration range and did not explore the long-term physiological responses of mature plants. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of seaweed priming under field conditions, investigate different extraction methods and application timings, and examine the molecular mechanisms involved in stress mitigation. Such investigations would help optimize the use of seaweed extracts as a sustainable strategy for enhancing crop tolerance to salinity.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that seed priming with seaweed extract effectively improved germination, growth performance, and physiological responses of G. platycarpum under salinity stress. Seaweed extract, particularly at concentrations of 20–30 g L⁻¹, enhanced key germination traits and seedling vigor, increased fresh and dry biomass, and maintained higher levels of chlorophyll a and b even under salt stress. In addition, seaweed priming mitigated oxidative damage by reducing MDA and H₂O₂ accumulation while modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby improving the plant’s capacity to cope with salinity-induced oxidative stress. Overall, the findings indicate that seaweed extract, as an inexpensive and accessible bio-stimulant rich in bioactive compounds, can serve as a promising strategy to enhance salt tolerance and support the establishment of G. platycarpum in saline and low-fertility environments. Future studies evaluating field performance and broader physiological responses will help optimize its application for sustainable cultivation.
