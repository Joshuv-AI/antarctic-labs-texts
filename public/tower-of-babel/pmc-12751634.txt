Advanced machine learning techniques for coronary artery disease risk prediction in early middle-aged adults
Authors: Georgios Ntritsos, Erez Ornan, Eran Feldhay, Gil Chernin, Evangelos Evangelou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death globally, with early risk prediction being vital for timely intervention. In this study we utilized advanced machine learning techniques, incorporating clinical, lifestyle, and Genetic factors with the aim to enhance the 10-year risk prediction of CAD in early middle-aged adults (40–55 years).MethodsFor developing the machine learning models, we used data from the UK Biobank, a large cohort of over 500,000 participants. Among participants aged 40 to 55, we identified 3,012 CAD cases and 155,176 controls, without any prior CAD diagnosis. More than 700 clinical and lifestyle variables were evaluated, alongside a polygenic risk score (PRS) derived from more than 560,000 genetic variants. Forward feature selection was performed, followed by the application of a stacking ensemble model combining Random Forest and Deep Learning Neural Networks as base learners with a Logistic Regression model serving as a meta-learner. The model’s performance for the 10-years prediction of CAD risk was evaluated using Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and calibrated against the ACC/AHA guidelines.ResultsForward feature selection identified 22 CAD risk factors, including the PRS. The final model yielded an AUC of 0.81 in the training dataset and 0.80 in the testing dataset. At the 7.5% risk threshold, the model demonstrated higher sensitivity (0.511 vs. 0.346) and comparable specificity (0.863 vs. 0.866) compared to the ACC ASCVD model. The Net Reclassification Index (NRI) improved by 13.79%, emphasizing the model’s accuracy and alignment.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the potential of combining Genetic information with clinical and lifestyle factors through machine learning to improve CAD risk prediction in early middle-aged adults. The use of a stacking ensemble approach enabled modeling of complex relationships, yielding superior performance over standard risk models. This framework supports more precise and actionable 10-year risk stratification, reinforcing the value of PRSs when combined with traditional factors. Future research should validate these methods across diverse populations and clinical environments.Keywords: Coronary artery disease (CAD), Machine learning, Risk prediction, Polygenic risk scores (PRS)

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1], and early detection of individuals at high risk is crucial for the clinicals with the intention of guiding them to a healthier lifestyle and proper medication [2, 3]. The development of CAD is a complex process that involves an interplay among clinical, lifestyle and genetic factors [4].

Clinical and lifestyle factors can enlighten the screening and comprise an important role in the prediction of CAD, including age, sex, race and ethnicity, smoking habits, total cholesterol, low-density and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, use of medications, blood biochemistry measurements, physical activity related parameters and mental health related parameters. Risk prediction models have been developed and widely used for both 10-year and lifetime risk prediction of CAD [5, 6], embracing these factors, including the Framingham Risk Score [7], the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) Risk Calculator, proposed by the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines [8] and the QRISK score [9]. Moreover, over the last decade, the addition of genetic factors, in the light of polygenic risk scores (PRSs), to those risk models, have shown improved performance and accuracy on the CAD risk prediction [10, 11].

Lately, machine learning models have been proposed aiming to improve the accuracy of risk prediction models for several diseases [12–14]. Recent studies have demonstrated how artificial intelligence and integrated machine learning pipelines can significantly enhance the prediction of complex disease outcomes across various medical domains, such as oncology and immunotherapy [14–16].

Regarding CAD, the contribution of machine learning techniques has been assessed in several studies, with the goal of improving the accuracy of clinical diagnosis or prediction of diseases [17–22]. In today’s medical landscape, there are numerous modern diagnostic tests available, and when paired with machine learning, these tests become powerful tools for cardiologists and can play a crucial role in both preventing and diagnosing coronary artery disease [23].

Despite these advances, early middle-aged adults remain significantly underrepresented in the derivation and validation cohorts of most cardiovascular risk models [24, 25]. Models such as the ASCVD Risk Calculator [8] and the Framingham Risk Score [26] were primarily developed in middle-aged and older populations, resulting in decreased predictive accuracy among younger subgroups. Recent efforts, such as QRISK3-Young [27] and PREVENT [28], have aimed to address this gap by tailoring prediction tools to younger populations. However, these models still rely predominantly on traditional clinical risk factors and do not fully account for the complex interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental exposures that contribute to early-onset CAD [29]. Furthermore, the integration of polygenic risk scores and lifestyle factors into predictive modeling for early middle-aged adults remains limited, underscoring a significant methodological gap [30].

In the present study, we integrated a large number of clinical and lifestyle factors combined with genetic risk, using aggregated and composite machine learning models, developed and validated on a large biobank with ~ 500,000 participants. Our main target was to assess the 10-year risk prediction of CAD in early middle-aged adults, aged 40 to 55 years, a population in which CAD often progresses more aggressively, as individuals in this age group with premature atherosclerosis have been shown to experience higher relative risks and worse outcomes compared to older individuals [31, 32]. Furthermore, this age group presents a key period where the initiation of statin therapy and lifestyle modifications, such as improved diet, increased physical activity, and smoking cessation, can yield significant benefits in both reducing disease progression and preventing adverse cardiovascular events [33, 34]. Finally, the predictive performance of our model was systematically evaluated against the well-established and widely utilized CAD risk prediction model developed by the ACC [8].

Methods

Study participants

The analysis was performed in the UK Biobank (UKB). UKB data were derived under the Application ID 64,586. UKB is a population-based cohort of more than 500,000 individuals, aged 40–69 years, with rich phenotypic, demographic, clinical and -omics data such as genomics and metabolomics [35]. Participants attended assessment centers at which baseline data were collected on their medical history, lifestyle, body composition and environment. DNA for Genotyping was collected as well. For the validation of the Genetic risk model, we used a subset of unrelated individuals from multiple ethnicities, by removing one of each pair of 1st and 2nd degree relatives, using the centrally provided kinship data from UKB [36].

CAD incidence in the UK biobank

Data on CAD incidence in UK Biobank were available for each participant based on measurements and self-reported data collected during their first visit and, longitudinal general practice (GP) data including medications, clinical data such as measurements and diagnoses made prior to the 1 st visit and, in addition, data from reports derived from Hospital Episodes Statistics. We focused on early prevention in early middle-aged adults. We identified 3012 CAD cases, with a CAD diagnosis according to ICD-10 (I25.1) code or a death caused by CAD within a period of 10 years counted from the patient’s first visit time registered in the UKB. Patients with an age more than 55 years at baseline date were excluded from the analysis. Patients with CAD diagnosis (I25.1), MI diagnosis (I21.0, I21.1, I21.2, I21.3, I21.4, I21.9, I22.0, I22.1, I22.8, I22.9, I23.0, I23.1, I23.2, I23.3, I23.5, I23.6, I23.8, I25.2) or stroke (I63.0, I63.1, I63.2, I63.3, I63.4, I63.5, I63.6, I63.8, I63.9) prior to the baseline date or up to 3 months after the baseline date were also excluded. We moreover selected 155,176 controls, aged less than 55 Years at baseline date, that were not diagnosed for CAD in a period of 10 Years from the baseline date. We therefore included 158,188 participants in our analysis.

Clinical and lifestyle features included

To develop our prediction model we examined a list of more than 700 clinical and lifestyle factors including blood biochemistry (e.g. HDL, LDL, A1c and many more), blood count biomarkers (e.g. red and white cell count, basophil count and many more), blood pressure measurements (SBP, DBP), body size measurements, smoking status at baseline date, medications used on baseline date, diagnoses made prior to baseline date, physical activity related self-reported parameters, mental health related self-reported parameters and sleep related self-reported parameters. Participants with missing HDL measurement at baseline date were excluded from the analysis.

Polygenic risk score

On top of the factors for our prediction model, we included the Polygenic Risk Score (PRS). PRS represents a sum across single-nucleotide proteins (SNPs) of the number of effect alleles at that SNP, weighted by their effect size estimates (beta coefficients). For our developed PRS, we used the beta coefficients from a large independent multiethnic GWAS meta-analysis of ~ 9.4 million Genetic variants, including 68 K CAD cases and 117 K controls [37]. Genetic principal components (PCs) to account for population stratification were centrally computed, in order to adjust for the several ethnicities included in our dataset. We used PRS-CS software [38] for Linkage Disequilibrium (LD), using the p-value threshold of 0.5 and the defaults in all the optional parameters. The PRS was standardized per unit increase.

Development of the machine learning models

To build the machine learning models, the dataset of 158,188 participants was split randomly into two halves, a training and a testing dataset, each containing 1,506 CAD cases and 77,588 healthy controls respectively.

The whole process started with feature selection, performed in the training dataset with more than 700 factors examined, including the PRS and the PCs. For each pair of highly correlated factors (Pearson’s correlation coefficient > 0.9), the one demonstrating the higher discriminative ability, as measured by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) derived from a logistic regression model with CAD events as the dependent variable, was retained. Continuous factors were standardized to have zero mean and unit variance, and categorical/binary factors were transformed using one-hot encoding prior to model training. Binary factors with the less common value rate < 1% were also removed. Missing data for a given factor were imputed using median values when the proportion of missing values was ≤ 40%; otherwise, the factor was excluded from the analysis.

A forward feature selection was performed with criteria of AUC increase of at least 0.05% when running a logistic regression over selected factors and the CAD phenotype (ICD10 code I25.1) as the dependent column. LDL was added to the feature list to allow monitoring and manipulating its effect on the risk. Diabetes diagnosis replaced the original elected A1c levels as the AUC difference was negligible between the two and diabetes diagnosis is more commonly available for individuals both in the dataset and in real life applications.

We then applied several aggregated machine learning techniques. A stacking Ensemble Learning model was then built, composed of two base models - a Random Forest model and a Deep Learning Neural Network model with the rationale of capturing nonlinear and complex relationships between features for prediction improvement, and a Logistic Regression model as meta-learner, accepting as input the probabilities derived from the base models as well as the selected features.

Hyperparameter tuning for each model was performed using a random search strategy. For the Random Forest model, the optimal hyperparameters were determined as follows: 500 trees, maximum tree depth of 10, minimum number of samples required to split an internal node set to 2, minimum number of samples required at a leaf node set to 10, maximum number of features considered at each split set to the square root of the total number, entropy criterion for information gain, and bootstrap sampling enabled.

For the Deep Learning Neural Network, the optimal architecture included three hidden layers comprising 53, 41, and 57 nodes respectively, with the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) used as the activation function and trained over 8 epochs. Probability outputs from the two base models were combined with the independent variables derived from the forward feature selection. To construct the meta-learner logistic regression classifier, features with p-values greater than 0.05 were excluded. For the logistic regression model, hyperparameter tuning selected a regularization strength (C) of 0.021, an L2 penalty, and the “saga” solver for optimization. The AUCs of the base models and the final model were assessed on the training set using 20-fold cross-validation while performance on the test set was assessed using 100 bootstrap iterations.

Finally, the risk estimates from the logistic regression model were calibrated to match the slope of the ACC/AHH ASCVD 10y risk score and compared to the widely used model recommended by the ACC/AHA Guidelines on the Assessment of Cardiovascular Risk (the ACC ASCVD model). The comparison was based on sensitivity, specificity, and Net Reclassification Indices (NRIs) across several clinically relevant risk thresholds, including the 7.5% statin initiation threshold recommended in current U.S. guidelines [39]. Model performance evaluation

To evaluate model performance beyond traditional discrimination metrics, we examined precision-recall curves (PRCs), which are particularly informative in contexts of class imbalance, such as CAD occurring in early middle-aged adults. The PRCs were constructed by plotting precision (positive predictive value) against recall (sensitivity) across a range of decision thresholds. To explore potential variation in model behavior across subgroups, we also stratified PRC analyses by age deciles, providing further insight into the discriminative capacity of the models among younger individuals.

Model calibration was assessed both visually, through calibration plots, and quantitatively, by calculating the calibration slope, intercept, R², and the Brier score, separately for the training and testing datasets.

Model explainability and clinical utility

To evaluate the potential clinical benefit of our model, we performed Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). Net benefit was calculated across a range of threshold probabilities (from 0 to 0.20), allowing comparison between our model and the standard ACC/AHA ASCVD risk score. The resulting decision curves were compared to the default strategies of “treat all” and “treat none”, to evaluate the practical utility of each model in informing treatment decisions.

In parallel, model interpretability was assessed using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values. SHAP analysis was conducted via the LinearExplainer module from the SHAP Python package (version 0.47), producing summary plots illustrating the relative contribution of each feature to the model’s output.

Results

Our training dataset in the UK Biobank consisted of 1,506 CAD cases and 77,588 healthy controls, with a mean age of 48.3 Years and 55.35% of participants being female. The forward feature selection process identified 22 factors for CAD risk prediction and achieved an AUC of 0.806. The selected factors included a PRS, consisting of 564,856 variants, at the p-value threshold of < 0.5, the third genetic PC, and clinical, lifestyle and mental health related factors. We also compared the results of the calibrated classification model against the ACC ASCVD performance scores at four risk levels: 5%, 7.5%, 10% and 20%. (Table 4). Overall, our classification model demonstrated better performance with higher sensitivity, specificity, positive NRI and better NRI events at all four risk levels. Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of various clinical and lifestyle factors, combined with Genetic risk, using aggregated and composite machine learning techniques, to enhance 10-year CAD risk prediction in early middle-aged adults. The analysis was conducted on a vast dataset obtained from a large biobank, with more than 150,000 participants, and our model achieved an AUC of 0.80 and outperformed the ACC ASCVD model across multiple risk levels.

Our study focused on early middle-aged adults, where CAD progresses more aggressively and early prediction is critical to prevent severe consequences [40, 41]. Moreover, timely identification allows for effective interventions [42] and preventive strategies, including statins, can alter the disease trajectory and have been shown to be more effective in this age group, yielding better long-term cardiovascular outcomes [43].

Several previous studies have utilized UKB data to develop machine learning models for CAD risk prediction [44–46]. However, our study focused exclusively on individuals under 55 Years old, addressing the need for early detection, and utilized a PRS comprised of more than 560,000 variants enhancing the precision of genetic assessment. Furthermore, the implementation of a stacking ensemble model combining Random Forest and Deep Learning Neural Network techniques provided enhanced predictive accuracy. Finally, in our study we calibrated our model to align with the ACC/AHA guidelines, ensuring enhanced clinical relevance and practical applicability in real-world settings.

Our research highlights several notable strengths that enhance robustness and practical significance in a clinical context. First, the use of UKB, encompassing over 500,000 participants with rich phenotypic, clinical, lifestyle, and genomics data, enhanced the robustness of our analysis. Τhis extensive dataset facilitated the assessment of an extensive range of more than 700 clinical and lifestyle factors, combined with a polygenic risk score (PRS) derived from over 560,000 genetic variants. The integration of these features provided a comprehensive approach to risk prediction, reflecting the multifactorial nature of CAD. Furthermore, the implementation of machine learning techniques, specifically the stacking ensemble model, enabled us to identify non-linear relationships between the predictive features, further improving the overall performance of the model.

Beyond discrimination, model calibration was assessed using both graphical and statistical measures, including slope, intercept, Brier score and R². The model demonstrated strong calibration performance, supporting its suitability for clinical deployment in line with accepted risk communication standards. Additionally, PRC analyses showed that our model outperformed the ACC/AHA score across a range of clinically relevant precision thresholds. This advantage persisted even when stratifying by age deciles, highlighting the robustness of our model across early middle-aged subgroups, a critical consideration given the typically lower event rates in this population. Moreover, DCA confirmed the clinical utility of our model across relevant risk thresholds, while SHAP analysis highlighted the predominant contribution of sex, age, and polygenic risk, supporting both practical applicability and interpretability.

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly the application of temporal models such as Transformer-based architectures, have shown promising potential in capturing dynamic risk trajectories over time for cardiovascular diseases [47, 48] Integrating such temporal models into CAD risk prediction frameworks for early middle-aged adults could offer additional prognostic value by modeling the evolving nature of risk factors [49, 50]. Furthermore, while recent frameworks like QRISK3-Young [27] have tailored traditional risk calculators to younger populations, our approach differs by incorporating both polygenic risk and lifestyle-environmental interactions, offering a more comprehensive assessment of early-onset CAD risk.

While our study demonstrates significant strengths in predicting the 10-year CAD risk among early middle-aged adults, it is essential to address certain limitations. The extensive biobank dataset provided invaluable insights, yet variations in data collection methodologies may have introduced inconsistencies. Additionally, some clinical variables included missing values, requiring imputation strategies that could influence the results. Finally, reliance on self-reported questionnaires for several factors introduces the potential for reporting bias and reduced accuracy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of aggregated machine learning approaches in improving CAD risk prediction in early middle-aged adults. By incorporating a broad spectrum of clinical, lifestyle, and genetic factors, our model offers improved discriminative ability and calibration compared to widely used clinical tools. These findings support the utility of integrative predictive frameworks for early detection and prevention. Further research is warranted to validate model performance across diverse cohorts and assess real-world clinical implementation.
