Laser interstitial thermal therapy for treatment of a recurrent skull base chordoma: illustrative case
Authors: William D. Chow, Vance T. Lehman, Jamie J. Van Gompel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe authors applied laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) to a recurrent skull base chordoma, which has not been previously described.OBSERVATIONSA 63-year-old man was initially diagnosed with an 8-cm destructive clival chordoma, which was aggressively resected endoscopically but recurred despite multiple operations, proton radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. The patient underwent uncomplicated LITT for a subtemporal mass, which palliated the tumor for 10 months. However, it did not ultimately prevent the need for resection, although it may have delayed the surgery.LESSONSThis case study demonstrates the feasibility and safety of LITT in treating these typically surgically challenging tumors. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24534

Chordomas are rare bone tumors found throughout the craniospinal axis, arising from the skull base and vertebral bodies.1 These tumors have a slight predominance in males, accounting for 60% of cases, and the median age at diagnosis is around 60 years.1, 2 The primary effect of these tumors arises from the invasion and destruction of local tissues, although metastases have been noted later in the disease course at a rate of 30%–40%.1 The current first-line treatment for these lesions is en bloc resection with negative surgical margins.2 However, the location of these lesions often requires technically challenging procedures, resulting in incomplete resections. Thus, adjuvant treatment of chordomas is frequently required, yet radiation, proton therapy, and/or conventional chemotherapy have shown limited efficacy.3, 4 Laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) is an emerging surgical treatment for various central nervous system lesions. While initially described in 1983, recent advances in intraoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques have allowed for real-time thermometry, enabling more exquisite control of the extent of tissue ablation.5–7 LITT uses light energy to heat and coagulate surrounding tissue with precise margins. Its minimally invasive nature allows for a short postoperative hospital course and a faster return to primary treatment.8, 9 This paper reports the first use of LITT in treating a recurrent skull base chordoma. Given the slow progression of recurrent chordomas and their relative resistence to radiation, LITT may play a role in salvage treatment and potentially palliate intervals between surgery.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 63-year-old man had an extensive history of recurring skull base chordoma after multiple transnasal and open resections along with adjuvant radiation therapy and imatinib. He initially presented in 2014 with nasal stuffiness for 7 months, accompanied by anosmia and mild dysphagia. Computed tomography imaging revealed a large, 8-cm destructive clival mass, and a subsequent biopsy confirmed a chordoma (Fig. 1A). Complicating features of this lesion included bilateral carotid and basilar artery involvement, bilateral petrous apex extension, bilateral cavernous sinus extension, left-sided pterygoid and foramen ovale extension, and brainstem compression. A transnasal resection was performed, and gross-total resection was achieved (Fig. 1B). The patient received adjuvant proton radiation therapy with a total dose of 70 Gy. No evidence of recurrence was noted for 5 years after initial surgery and proton beam therapy. Following this initial period, recurrence was noted in the occipital condyle and inferior clivus, with intracranial involvement. A transpterygoid resection and frontotemporal craniotomy were performed 6 years after initial diagnosis to manage recurrences, and postoperative imatinib chemotherapy was initiated following the second procedure. Tumor stability was achieved until the 7th year after initial diagnosis, when recurrence required a repeat transpterygoid resection. Further tumor progression was noted in the left subtemporal area in the 8th year after the initial diagnosis (Fig. 2). Given the specific tumor location and the tumor’s clear propensity for recurrence, LITT was performed approximately 9 years after the initial diagnosis. The techniques for fiber placement, ablation, and damage assessment have been previously described.10 A single laser fiber was placed within the subtemporal mass, and MRI confirmed the correct placement. The fiber was activated at 7.5 W for 132 seconds and then increased to 12.15 W for 154 seconds. The fiber was then retracted slightly and reactivated at 12 W for 55 seconds (Fig. 3A and B). Postoperative imaging confirmed a zone of ablation over the entire left subtemporal mass (Fig. 3C). The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on postoperative day 1. The patient continued maintenance imatinib and was evaluated at 3-month follow-up appointments, revealing stability of the tumor (Fig. 4A and B); however, at 10 months, there was mild disease progression (Fig. 5A and B). After discussion with the patient and new lesions that developed near the clinoid, a repeat frontotemporal operation was performed to resect the mass, place fat, and biopsy the clinoidal mass, consistent with a separate focus on chordoma. Discussion

Observations

Chordomas are rare yet locally infiltrative bone tumors with limited available therapies outside of resection. Their tendency to appear in anatomically complicated locations, combined with a general resistance to traditional therapies and a propensity to recur, makes these lesions particularly difficult to eliminate.3 The persistent recurrence of this chordoma in our patient despite multiple operations, adjuvant proton radiation therapy, and chemotherapy led to the use of LITT ablation before deferring to a second craniotomy. Reported uses of LITT in the treatment of chordomas are extremely limited. A PubMed search conducted on June 30, 2024, using the search terms “laser interstitial chordoma” led to a single reported use of LITT in the treatment of a recurrent chordoma metastasis to the cervical spine, along with adjuvant stereotactic radiosurgery. In this case report, Williams et al. reported notable symptom improvement, with a complete treatment response on imaging after the combined use of the two treatment modalities.9 While the use of LITT with continued maintenance imatinib was ultimately unsuccessful in controlling the progression of our patient’s chordoma, the procedure was well tolerated, requiring only a postoperative hospital stay of 1 day with no complications. Intraoperative monitoring confirmed excellent distribution of the ablation zone, with full coverage of the subtemporal mass. Williams et al. noted in their case report the potential for decreased heat conduction due to the intrinsically increased lipid content of chordomas.9 However, test activation demonstrated expected heating at the laser tip on intraoperative monitoring and precise heating of the desired areas. A second concern raised in their report was the possibility of tumor seeding along the fiber tract. While this was not directly assessed, postoperative imaging did not indicate a recurrence of the chordoma along the procedure site.

Lessons

While the use of LITT could not prevent the need for additional surgery and potentially delay the next open operation, this case report helps demonstrate both the safety and feasibility of using LITT as a method for accessing otherwise difficult-to-treat chordomas. Chordomas can be demanding to surgically resect due to their natural anatomical locations; however, we used LITT to treat the lesion accurately with little damage to surrounding structures. Available therapies for treating chordomas still need to be expanded despite an increasing understanding of the underlying biology of these lesions. In advancing targeted therapy and immunotherapy options,3 LITT can serve as a feasible and well-tolerated adjuvant therapy for treating these neoplasms.
