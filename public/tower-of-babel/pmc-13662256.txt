Posterior Osteophyte Resection from the Medial Femoral Condyle Increases Medial Gap of Extension and Mid-Flexion in Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Ji Hyun Ahn, Ki-Tae Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe present study aimed to determine the effect of posterior osteophyte resection from the medial femoral condyle on the medial gap increase in varus osteoarthritis during posterior-stabilized total knee arthroplasty (TKA).MethodsOne hundred and eight cases of primary posterior-stabilized TKA for varus knee osteoarthritis were reviewed retrospectively. In a preoperative lateral view of the knee radiograph, 39 cases had the largest size osteophyte of the posterior femoral condyle > 5 mm (large osteophyte group) and 69 cases had 0 to 5 mm (small osteophyte group). After femoral component trial insertion, the posterior osteophyte of the medial femoral condyle was removed using a curved osteotome. Medial and lateral joint gaps at 0°, 45°, and 90° of flexion were measured intraoperatively before and after the posterior osteophyte resection from the medial femoral condyle.ResultsThe large osteophyte group showed a significantly larger increase in medial gap change at 0° and 45° of flexion than the small osteophyte group (medial gap increase at 0°: 0.2 ± 0.4 mm in the small osteophyte group and 2.9 ± 1.4 mm in the large osteophyte group, p < 0.001; medial gap increase at 45°: 0.3 ± 0.5 mm in the small osteophyte group and 1.7 ± 0.8 mm in the large osteophyte group, p < 0.001). At 0° and 45° of flexion, medial gap values after osteophyte resection were significantly larger than before osteophyte resection in both groups (p < 0.001). In linear regression analysis, posterior osteophyte size was also significantly associated with medial gap change at 0° and 45° of flexion (p < 0.001).ConclusionsResection of large posterior osteophytes > 5 mm from the medial femoral condyle can be expected to result in a larger increase in the medial gap than resection of osteophytes ≤ 5 mm. Surgeons need to pay close attention to these intraoperative changes in the medial gap after posterior osteophyte resection to achieve appropriate mediolateral balance during TKA.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Posterior osteophyte, Medial femoral condyle, Medial gap, Varus deformity

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis, providing pain relief and improved functionality.1) A crucial aspect of TKA is achieving proper alignment and balance in the coronal plane, which impacts the long-term success of the implant and patient satisfaction.2) Varus deformities are common in TKA candidates and pose a challenge in restoring proper alignment and balance.3)

Varus deformities result from medial compartment wear, ligament imbalance, and osteophyte formation, leading to increased mechanical stress on the medial side of the knee joint.4) Various techniques have been proposed to correct varus deformity in TKA, including medial soft-tissue release, peripheral osteophyte removal, and posterior condylar osteophyte resection.5,6,7,8) However, the optimal approach for balancing varus knees during TKA remains a topic of debate, with several authors proposing different techniques and their modifications.9,10,11) There are also studies that emphasized the importance of several techniques used for gap matching and contracture correction in TKA.12,13)

The removal of osteophytes, particularly those located at the posterior femoral condyles, has been suggested as a potential method to correct varus deformity in TKA.14,15,16) However, there is a lack of abundant evidence on the effect of osteophyte removal from the posterior femoral condyle on the correction of mediolateral gap imbalance during TKA. The current study aimed to determine the effect of posterior osteophyte resection from the medial femoral condyle on the medial gap increase in varus osteoarthritis during posterior-stabilized TKA. We hypothesized that resection of larger posterior osteophytes from the medial femoral condyle would result in a greater increase in the medial gap, thereby improving the mediolateral balance during TKA in patients with varus knee osteoarthritis.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangbuk Samsung Hospital (IRB No. 2026-01-044). Because of the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Patients and Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

This is a retrospective observational study based on routinely recorded intraoperative and clinical data. Patients who underwent primary posterior-stabilized TKA for varus osteoarthritis between May 1, 2021, and December 31, 2021, were enrolled in the current study. Patients with primary osteoarthritis and a minimum preoperative range of motion of > 90° were included, whereas those with traumatic osteoarthritis (n = 1), valgus deformity (n = 5), and inflammatory arthritis (n = 3), such as rheumatoid arthritis, were excluded. A total of 117 knees were identified, but 9 were excluded, leaving 108 knees eligible to participate in this study.

Fig. 1 shows the flow diagram of the selection and exclusion processes. One hundred and eight cases of primary TKA with posterior-stabilized prosthesis for varus osteoarthritis were reviewed retrospectively. The 108 knees consisted of 14 men and 94 women with an average age of 71 years (range, 61–84 years) at the time of surgery. In a preoperative lateral view of the knee radiograph, 39 cases had the largest size osteophyte of the posterior femoral condyle > 5 mm (the large osteophyte group) and 69 cases had 0 to 5 mm (the small osteophyte group). The cut-off value for posterior osteophyte size was based on a previous study, in which the mean maximum osteophyte size per case was approximately 5 mm.15) Medial and lateral joint gaps at 0°, 45°, and 90° of flexion were measured intraoperatively before and after the posterior osteophyte resection from the medial femoral condyle.

Surgical Procedure and Test Protocol

All procedures were performed by the senior author (JHA), using a hybrid resection approach. This hybrid resection technique included a measured resection technique for the distal femur cut and a modified gap technique for the posterior femur cut. The surgeon implanted the Anthem (Smith & Nephew) posterior-stabilized TKA design with fixed bearing in all patients. A medial parapatellar skin incision and mid-vastus approach were used in each case under tourniquet control. Following arthrotomy, an initial limited release of the deep medial collateral ligament (MCL) and the posteromedial capsule was systematically performed within approximately 2 cm distal to the joint line in all cases. Patellar resurfacing was not undertaken in any patient.

The distal femoral resection was executed perpendicular to the mechanical axis of the femur. Prior to tibial resection, the posterior femoral cut was performed using a predetermined external rotation angle. This rotational angle was set with reference to the posterior condylar axis of the distal femur, ranging from 0° to 7°, and was adjusted according to intraoperative assessment of the tibial longitudinal axis and the mediolateral gap at 90° of knee flexion. The size of the femoral component was determined using a posterior-referencing technique based on the AP dimension of the posterior condyles and the highest point of the anterolateral femoral cortex. The tibial resection was subsequently carried out perpendicular to the mechanical axis with a posterior slope of 3°, using an extramedullary alignment guide.

After completion of femoral and tibial bone preparation, a femoral trial component was inserted. Mediolateral joint gaps were assessed using an offset-type tensor device (B. Braun) in conjunction with an offset-type spreader (Scott femoral tibial tensor; INNOMED). All intraoperative gap measurements were recorded in 1-mm increments according to the scale of the measuring devices. Measurements were obtained at 0°, 45°, and 90° of knee flexion. All intraoperative gap assessments were performed by a single senior surgeon (JHA) using a standardized protocol, and gap measurements were obtained under a standardized distraction force of 20 lb.17)

Subsequently, the posterior osteophyte of the medial femoral condyle was removed meticulously using a curved osteotome (Fig. 2). Medial and lateral gap measurements at 0°, 45°, and 90° of flexion were repeated after the posterior osteophyte resection from the medial femoral condyle. If a narrow medial gap with > 2 mm of mediolateral gap imbalance was detected, the additional pie-crusting release was done around the medial epicondyle of the distal femur with the step-by-step technique. An 11-gauge spinal needle (J-type bone marrow needle; Argon Medical) was used to perform gradual release of the deep MCL. After the pie-crusting, the mediolateral gap was reassessed, and additional punctures were applied as needed to achieve a gap difference of < 2 mm.18)

The posterior osteophyte of the medial femoral condyle was removed meticulously using a curved osteotome.

Radiographic Evaluations and Functional Assessments

Preoperative varus deformity was assessed radiographically using a standing AP radiograph of the whole lower extremity. Preoperative knee mechanical axis was evaluated using the hip-knee-ankle angle, measured with an AP radiograph of the standing lower extremity. The centers of the femoral head, distal femoral intercondylar notch, and talus were identified and connected to determine the mechanical axis, and the angle between these lines was defined as the knee mechanical axis angle (KMAA). Postoperative knee mechanical axis was also assessed with a method similar to that for preoperative KMAA at 1 year postoperatively. Preoperative lateral radiographs were used to evaluate the posterior osteophyte of the medial femoral condyle. The measurement of the farthest distance from the most posterior point of the femoral cortex connected to the Blumensaat line to the osteophyte is defined as the size of the posterior osteophyte (Fig. 3). Knee function was evaluated by the preoperative and 1-year postoperative Knee Society Score (KSS) and Knee Function Score (KFS).

The size of the posterior osteophyte (white arrow) is defined as the farthest distance from the most posterior point of the femoral cortex connected to the Blumensaat line to the osteophyte.

Sample Size

The authors calculated the sample size based on the data from a pilot study with 5 knees in each group. In the present pilot study, the mean medial gap increase was 0.2 ± 0.4 mm at 0°, 0.3 ± 0.5 mm at 45°, and 0.0 ± 0.1 mm at 90° in the small osteophyte group. In the large osteophyte group, the mean medial gap increase was 1.7 ± 0.8 mm at 0°, 1.8 ± 1.0 mm at 45°, and 0.3 ± 0.6 mm at 90°, respectively. A comparison of the mean medial gap increase at 90° was referenced to determine the sample size as this yielded the greatest number of samples needed. From the pilot study data with a standard power of 80%, type I error rate of 5%, and effect size of 0.697, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 34 using an independent t-test generated by G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universitat Dusseldorf). Considering unexpected non-measurable situations, 39 knees were included in the large osteophyte group. In the small osteophyte group, 69 knees were included; these were selected during the same period to match the large osteophyte group.

Data Analyses

We analyzed the statistical data using IBM SPSS version 29.0.0 (IBM Corp.). We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess whether the variables followed a normal distribution. Based on the result of the normality test, independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-tests were used to analyze continuous variables. A paired t-test was used to confirm the change in the medial and lateral gaps before and after osteophyte resection in the same knee. A chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare nominal variables between the 2 groups. In addition, linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between posterior osteophyte size, treated as a continuous variable, and medial gap change according to flexion angle. A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to adjust for preoperative alignment angle. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

The large osteophyte group showed a significantly larger increase in medial gap change at 0° and 45° of flexion than the small osteophyte group (medial gap increase at 0°: 0.2 ± 0.4 mm in the small osteophyte group and 2.9 ± 1.4 mm in the large osteophyte group, p < 0.001; medial gap increase at 45°: 0.3 ± 0.5 mm in the small osteophyte group and 1.7 ± 0.8 mm in the large osteophyte group, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in medial gap change at 90° of flexion between the 2 groups (Table 2, Fig. 4). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the lateral gap change between the small and large osteophyte groups at any flexion angle (Table 3).

At 0° and 45° of flexion, medial gap values significantly increased following osteophyte resection in both groups (all p < 0.001). In the small osteophyte group, the medial gap increased from 10.2 ± 1.6 mm to 10.4 ± 1.6 mm at 0°, and from 10.3 ± 1.5 mm to 10.6 ± 1.6 mm at 45°. In the large osteophyte group, the medial gap increased from 9.6 ± 1.5 mm to 12.5 ± 1.9 mm at 0°, and from 10.4 ± 1.6 mm to 12.1 ± 1.7 mm at 45°. There was no significant difference in medial gap values at 90° of flexion before and after osteophyte resection in both groups (Table 2). No significant differences in the lateral gap were observed before and after osteophyte resection at any knee flexion angle (Table 3).

In multivariable linear regression analysis, osteophyte size was significantly associated with medial gap change at 0° (β = 0.338, p < 0.001) and 45° (β = 0.172, p < 0.001) of flexion, whereas only a weak association was observed at 90° of flexion (β = –0.029, p = 0.016) after adjustment for preoperative alignment angle (Table 4, Fig. 5). The large osteophyte group had a significantly more varus preoperative knee mechanical axis (11.7° ± 5.5°) than the small osteophyte group (8.3° ± 4.0°) (p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in 1-year postoperative KMAA between the 2 groups. There was also no significant difference in the 1-year postoperative KSS and KFS (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

The principal finding of this study was that resection of posterior osteophytes from the medial femoral condyle resulted in a significantly greater increase in the medial gap at 0° and 45° of flexion in the large osteophyte group compared with the small osteophyte group. This finding supports our hypothesis and indicates that removal of larger posterior osteophytes plays a meaningful role in achieving improved mediolateral balance during TKA.

In TKA, osteophyte removal is considered a fundamental step, as residual osteophytes can interfere with implant positioning, soft-tissue balance, and knee kinematics.19,20) Although osteophyte resection is routinely performed during TKA, the specific effect of osteophyte removal on intraoperative joint gaps and coronal balance—particularly for osteophytes located at the posterior femoral condyle—has not been well characterized.

The present findings are in agreement with previous studies demonstrating that removal of posterior osteophytes of the femoral condyle influences medial gap balance during TKA.20,21,22,23) Seo et al.23) reported a significant increase in the medial joint gap following posterior femoral condylar osteophyte resection in varus knees undergoing TKA. Similarly, Leie et al.20) observed a mean increase of approximately 1 mm in the medial gap after resection of posterior osteophytes of the femoral condyle.

Because the large osteophyte group had greater baseline varus deformity in this study, adjusted analysis was additionally performed to address potential confounding. The association between osteophyte size and medial gap change remained significant at 0° and 45° of flexion after adjustment, supporting the robustness of the main findings. A major strength of the present study is that we quantified the effect of posterior osteophyte resection according to osteophyte size and showed that larger posterior osteophytes are associated with substantially greater medial gap increases, particularly in extension and mid-flexion.

Furthermore, a cadaveric study by Holst et al.16) has shown that posterior femoral osteophytes increase medial contact force from extension to 45° of flexion, and that posterior osteophyte removal significantly increases the medial joint gap and can affect medial gap balance during TKA. Therefore, the significant medial gap change observed at 45° of flexion in the present study may be clinically relevant, because unrecognized imbalance in this range could contribute to mid-flexion instability, which has been associated with suboptimal outcomes after TKA.24)

The increase in the medial gap observed after posterior osteophyte resection is likely multifactorial. In varus knee osteoarthritis, increased mechanical loading of the medial compartment promotes osteophyte formation at the posterior femoral condyle,25) where these bony overgrowths may function as a mechanical restraint on the MCL and posterior capsule, thereby contributing to medial tightness and gap imbalance.16) In addition, posterior osteophytes may interfere with accurate femoral component positioning during TKA, potentially affecting coronal alignment.15) This restraining effect may be more pronounced in extension and mid-flexion, whereas it may diminish in deeper flexion as the tension pattern of the posterior capsule and adjacent medial soft tissues changes. Consistent with these mechanisms, our findings demonstrate that removal of larger posterior osteophytes results in a more pronounced increase in the medial gap, highlighting their clinically relevant role in mediolateral balance restoration during TKA.19,26)

Although statistically significant differences in intraoperative medial gap change were observed between the 2 groups, no significant differences in postoperative clinical outcomes, including the KSS and KFS, were found. This result was consistent with that of a previous study.27) Therefore, our findings should be interpreted primarily as intraoperative gap-balancing data rather than as evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, because this was a retrospective study and all intraoperative gap measurements were performed by a single non-blinded senior surgeon, selection and observer bias cannot be excluded, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, prospective randomized studies with blinded assessment are warranted to further validate our results. Second, the study population was limited to patients with varus knee osteoarthritis undergoing posterior-stabilized TKA, and thus the findings may not be directly applicable to other coronal deformities or implant designs. Third, the sample size of the large osteophyte group was smaller than that of the small osteophyte group, which may have influenced statistical power. Accordingly, differences at higher flexion angles may not have been fully captured, suggesting a potential type II error. Nevertheless, the consistent and significant differences observed between groups suggest that the effect of posterior osteophyte size on medial gap behavior is clinically meaningful. Finally, long-term clinical outcomes and implant survivorship were not evaluated, and future studies should investigate whether the intraoperative gap changes observed in this study translate into sustained functional improvement and implant longevity.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that resection of large posterior osteophytes (> 5 mm) from the medial femoral condyle results in a significantly greater increase in the medial gap compared with resection of smaller osteophytes in varus knees undergoing posterior-stabilized TKA. These findings underscore the importance of posterior osteophyte size as a key determinant of medial gap behavior during extension and mid-flexion. From a clinical perspective, surgeons should carefully consider posterior osteophyte burden when performing gap balancing in TKA with varus osteoarthritis, as removal of large osteophytes can produce substantial medial gap opening and influence subsequent soft-tissue release decisions. Anticipating these changes may help avoid unnecessary medial release and facilitate more reliable mediolateral balance. By incorporating posterior osteophyte size into surgical planning, surgeons may better individualize their balancing strategy during TKA.
