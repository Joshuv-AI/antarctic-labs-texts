the harnack inequality without convexity for curve shortening flow
Arjun Sobnack, Peter M. Topping

Abstract
In 1995, Hamilton introduced a Harnack inequality for convex solutions of the mean curvature flow. In this paper we prove an alternative Harnack inequality for curve shortening flow, i.e. one-dimensional mean curvature flow, that does not require any assumption of convexity. For an initial proper curve in the plane whose ends are radial lines but which is otherwise arbitrarily wild, we use the Harnack inequality to give an explicit time by which the curve shortening flow evolution must become graphical. This gives a new instance of delayed parabolic regularity. The Harnack inequality also gives estimates describing how a polar graphical flow with radial ends settles down to an expanding solution. Finally, we relate our Harnack inequality to Hamilton’s by identifying a pointwise curvature estimate implied by both Harnack inequalities in the special case of convex flows.

Introduction

Given an open interval
{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, e.g.
{\mathcal{I}}={\mathbb{R}}
,
a smooth map
\gamma:{\mathcal{I}}\times(0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, parametrised as
\gamma(u,t)
and
defining a one-parameter family of embeddings

,
is said to satisfy curve shortening flow if

\Big\langle{\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}},\nu\Big\rangle=-\kappa,

(1.1)

where
\nu
is a unit normal to
\gamma(\cdot,t)
and
\kappa
is the corresponding geodesic curvature so that the geodesic curvature vector is
\vec{\kappa}=-\kappa\nu
.

If at some time
t
the parameter
u
is arc-length, we
have tangent vector
{\mathcal{T}}:=\gamma_{u}
. The (continuous) tangent angle
\psi
is determined up to an integer multiple of
2\pi
by the condition

{\mathcal{T}}=(\cos\psi,\sin\psi).

(1.2)

We adopt the sign convention that
\kappa:=\psi_{u}
(still for arc-length
u
) which, by differentiating (
1.2
), makes

\vec{\kappa}=\gamma_{uu}={\mathcal{T}}_{u}=\kappa(*{\mathcal{T}}),

where the Hodge star operator
*
gives an anticlockwise rotation to vectors,
so
\nu=-{*{\mathcal{T}}}
or equivalently
{\mathcal{T}}=*\nu
.

In the case of non-trivial solutions
that are convex, which we may take to mean that
\kappa(\cdot,t)>0
for all
t\in(0,T)
,
and that are parametrised so that
{\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}}=-\kappa\nu
, Hamilton’s Harnack inequality
[
14
]
, restricted to the case of evolving curves, tells us that

\kappa_{t}+\frac{\kappa}{2t}\geq\frac{\kappa_{u}^{2}}{\kappa},

(1.3)

still with arc-length
u
,
under reasonable conditions on the ends of the curves
\gamma(\cdot,t)
.
Hamilton noticed that if one throws away the non-negative right-hand side of (
1.3
) then this implies that
(\kappa t^{\frac{1}{2}})_{t}\geq 0
, which can be integrated in time to give pointwise curvature estimates.

In fact, a stronger inequality of this form arises by instead parametrising the flow by the tangent angle
\psi
rather than
u
. As we show in Section
5
, Hamilton’s Harnack inequality is then
equivalent
to the statement
(\kappa t^{\frac{1}{2}})_{t}\geq 0
, where the time derivative is now with
\psi
fixed rather than
u
fixed.
This fact is implicit in the work of Andrews
[
1
, Theorem 5.6]
.
Everything we have said so far generalises to convex solutions of mean curvature flow.

(1.4)

where

is the Hodge star operator on 2-vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, i.e.

*\colon\Lambda^{2}{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}

.

\gamma(w)

The swept area is invariant under rotations of
\gamma
about the origin, and negates under reflections across lines through the origin.
Suitably interpreted, it is also invariant under orientation-preserving reparametrisations of
\gamma
, and negates under orientation-reversing reparametrisations.

In the case that
u
is an arc-length parameter, we can write

{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{v}^{w}\langle\gamma,\nu\rangle\,du.

(1.5)

If we view the curve as lying within the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, then we can alternatively write

{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)=\Im\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int\bar{z}\,dz\right],

(1.6)

where the integral is performed on the part of
\gamma
parametrised between
v
and
w
, and
\Im
represents the imaginary part.

Remark 1.4
.

If we are given an immersed closed (piecewise) smooth curve
\gamma:S^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
then the total swept area can be written

{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma}*(\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma)\,du=-\frac{1}{2i}\int_{\gamma}\bar{z}\,dz,

because the real part of the integral in (
1.6
) vanishes.
If additionally
\gamma
is an embedding that traverses its image in an anti-clockwise direction (i.e. it has winding number
0
or
+1
around each point), then the total swept area will be the
negation
of the area of the region enclosed by
\gamma
.
The choice of sign convention for the swept area is fraught because we are using it to relate two different topics in which the standard sign conventions clash.

We write the swept area of an evolving curve
\gamma(\cdot,t)
as
{\mathcal{A}}(v,w,t)
.

Before giving the Harnack inequality, we give one of the applications.
Given a curve shortening flow with initially radial ends, but which can be arbitrarily wild otherwise, as in Figure

Before giving any further consequences of the Harnack inequality, we explain the Harnack inequality itself. In order to do that, we need one further definition.

Definition 1.6
.

Given a smooth immersed curve
\gamma:{\mathcal{I}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

{\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
an open interval,

\psi:{\mathcal{I}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

The turning function
\Psi

\psi

We write the turning function of an evolving curve

2
Evolution of the swept area, turning function and Harnack quantity

In this section we derive evolution equations for the swept area
{\mathcal{A}}
and turning function
\Psi
, and the Harnack quantity
{\mathcal{H}}
, as introduced in Definitions
1.3
and
1.6
, and in (
1.11
).
In order to derive these heat equations, we need notation for the Laplacian.
Given a curve
\gamma(u)
, with parameter
u\in{\mathcal{I}}
and generally also evolving in time,
if
f(u)
is a function having
u
as an
argument (possibly amongst others), then we write

\Delta_{u}f
for the Laplacian of
f
with respect to the pull-back of the Euclidean metric on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by
\gamma
. If
u
has been chosen as an arc-length parameter for
\gamma
at some time, then

\Delta_{u}f=f_{uu}

at that time.
Note that we can write curve shortening flow as

\gamma_{t}=\Delta_{u}\gamma.

It is well known how tangent angles evolve under curve shortening flow. For example, the following lemma is immediately implied by
[
11
, Lemma 3.1.5]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\gamma:{\mathcal{I}}\times(0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a smooth map
such that
\gamma(\cdot,t)
is an evolving immersed curve satisfying the curve shortening flow equation
\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}
, where
\vec{\kappa}
is the curvature vector of
\gamma(\cdot,t)
.
Let
\psi(u,t)
be the tangent angle and
\Psi(v,w,t)
be the turning function of
\gamma
, cf. Definition
1.6
.
Then

\psi_{t}=\Delta_{u}\psi

(2.1)

and, in particular,

\Psi_{t}=\Delta_{v}\Psi+\Delta_{w}\Psi

on
\Sigma\times(0,T)
.

We are now in a position to describe the PDE governing the swept area
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\gamma:{\mathcal{I}}\times(0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a smooth map
such that
\gamma(\cdot,t)
is an evolving immersed curve satisfying the curve shortening flow equation

\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}
, where
\vec{\kappa}
is the curvature vector of
\gamma(\cdot,t)
.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}(v,w,t)
denote the swept area of
\gamma(\cdot,t)

between
u=v
and
u=w
, cf. Definition
1.3
.
Then

{\mathcal{A}}_{t}=\Delta_{v}{\mathcal{A}}+\Delta_{w}{\mathcal{A}}+\Psi(v,w,t),

(2.2)

where
\Psi
is the turning function of
\gamma
.
In particular, the Harnack quantity
{\mathcal{H}}(v,w,t):={\mathcal{A}}(v,w,t)-t\Psi(v,w,t)

from Theorem
1.7
satisfies

{\mathcal{H}}_{t}=\Delta_{v}{\mathcal{H}}+\Delta_{w}{\mathcal{H}}.

(2.3)

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.1
,
it suffices to establish the equations at an arbitrary time
t=\tau\in(0,T)
, and we choose

u\in{\mathcal{I}}
to be an arc-length parameter at time
t=\tau
.
Compute at time
t=\tau

2{\mathcal{A}}_{w}=*\big[\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma\big](w)

and

2{\mathcal{A}}_{ww}=*\big[\gamma_{uu}\wedge\gamma\big](w)=*\big[\gamma_{t}\wedge\gamma\big](w),

(2.4)

since
\gamma_{t}=\gamma_{uu}
.
Similarly we have
2{\mathcal{A}}_{v}=-*\big[\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma\big](v)
and

2{\mathcal{A}}_{vv}=-\,{*}\big[\gamma_{t}\wedge\gamma\big](v).

(2.5)

Because we are fixing a parametrisation
u
, rather than trying to continually adjust to arc-length parametrisation as
t
changes as in
[
11
]
, we have
\gamma_{ut}=\gamma_{tu}
and can integrate by parts to compute

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{A}}_{t}

\displaystyle=*\int_{v}^{w}(\gamma_{tu}\wedge\gamma+\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t})\,du

(2.6)

\displaystyle=*\big[\gamma_{t}\wedge\gamma\big]_{v}^{w}+*\,2\int_{v}^{w}\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t}\,du.

Combining (
2.4
), (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) gives

{\mathcal{A}}_{t}={\mathcal{A}}_{vv}+{\mathcal{A}}_{ww}+*\int_{v}^{w}\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t}\,du.

Recall from Section
1
that at time
t=\tau
we have

\gamma_{t}=-\kappa\nu=\kappa(*\gamma_{u})
,
and so

*(\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t})=\kappa\ {*}(\gamma_{u}\wedge[*\gamma_{u}])=\kappa

because
\gamma_{u}
has unit length.
As

\Psi(v,w,\tau)=\int_{v}^{w}\kappa(u)\,du,

(2.7)

we obtain

{\mathcal{A}}_{t}={\mathcal{A}}_{vv}+{\mathcal{A}}_{ww}+\Psi(v,w,\tau),

(2.8)

which is (
2.2
) at our arbitrary time
t=\tau
.
The final conclusion (
2.3
) then follows immediately by combining with Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

If we fix
v_{0}\in{\mathcal{I}}
and define
\beta:{\mathcal{I}}\times[0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}
, up to a normalisation constant, by
\beta(w,t):=-2\,{\mathcal{A}}(v_{0},w,t)+V(t)
, where
V:[0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is chosen to satisfy
V^{\prime}(t)=-2\psi(v_{0},t)-{*[\gamma_{uu}\wedge\gamma]}(v_{0},t)
,
then the
\beta(w,t)
here is the
\beta
introduced in
[
16
, §3.1.2]
.

3
Proof of the alternative Harnack inequality

In this section we apply the evolution equations derived in the previous section in an unfamiliar maximum principle argument in order to prove
Theorem
1.7
.

We begin by simplifying the parametrisation
\gamma
without any loss of generality.
Since
\gamma
is a curve shortening flow with initially radial ends, and in particular is uniformly proper with bounded curvature, we can
reparametrise so that
\gamma
satisfies

\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}=-\kappa\nu
rather than the more general (
1.1
).
Next, we make a time-independent reparametrisation of
{\mathcal{I}}
to reduce to the case
that
{\mathcal{I}}=(0,1)
.

Define

\Sigma:=\{(v,w)\in(0,1)\times(0,1)\ :\ v<w\}.

(3.1)

In Theorem
2.2
we have shown that
{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies a heat equation on

\Sigma\times(0,T)
and we will aim to apply the maximum principle to
{\mathcal{H}}
on
\Sigma\times[0,T)
. This will require us to extend
{\mathcal{H}}
to the boundary edges in
\partial\Sigma\times[0,T)
and control it there. To do that we find a new heat equation that governs
{\mathcal{H}}
one dimension down on these boundary components and apply the maximum principle. Of course, this requires us to go yet another dimension down and understand how

{\mathcal{H}}
behaves on the boundary points of these one-dimensional edges, but this will result from our knowledge of the spatial asymptotics of
\gamma
.

In the following lemma
the tangent angle
\psi
and turning function
\Psi
are from Definition
1.6
, but because of the initially radial end at angle
\pi
, we are free to determine the choice of
\psi
(otherwise determined up to an integer multiple of
2\pi
) by the condition that
\psi(u,t)\to 0
as
u\downarrow 0
. Of course, the limit is
0
rather than
\pi
because we are moving in, rather than out, along the end.
Moreover, because the curve
\gamma
in the following lemma is embedded, and it exits along the radial line at angle
\alpha
, we must have

\psi(u,t)\to\alpha
as
u\uparrow 1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma:(0,1)\times[0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a curve shortening flow with initially radial ends at angles

\pi
and
\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi)
in the sense of Definition
1.2
,
that is smooth down to
t=0
.
Suppose further that
\gamma
satisfies

\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}
, i.e. it has no tangential reparametrisation that would be allowed by (
1.1
).

Then an appropriate choice of
\psi
extends continuously to
[0,1]\times[0,T)
, with

\psi(0,t)=0
and
\psi(1,t)=\alpha
. The resulting extension of

\Psi
to
\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
satisfies
\Psi(0,w,t)=\psi(w,t)
and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left({\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}-\Delta_{w}\right)\Psi(0,w,t)&=0&\quad w\in(0,1)\\
\Psi(0,0,t)&=0&\\
\Psi(0,1,t)&=\alpha,&\end{aligned}\right.

(3.2)

for all
t\in(0,T)
.
Moreover,
{\mathcal{A}}
extends continuously to

\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
,
and the resulting function
{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left({\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}-\Delta_{w}\right){\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t)&=\Psi(0,w,t)&\quad w\in(0,1)\\
{\mathcal{A}}(0,0,t)&=0&\\
{\mathcal{A}}(0,1,t)&={\mathcal{A}}(0,1,0)+\alpha t&\end{aligned}\right.

(3.3)

for all
t\in(0,T)
.
In particular, the Harnack quantity

{\mathcal{H}}(v,w,t):={\mathcal{A}}(v,w,t)-t\Psi(v,w,t)
defined in Theorem
1.7

extends continuously to
\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
and
{\mathcal{H}}(0,w,t)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left({\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}-\Delta_{w}\right){\mathcal{H}}(0,w,t)&=0&\quad w\in(0,1)\\
{\mathcal{H}}(0,0,t)&=0&\\
{\mathcal{H}}(0,1,t)&={\mathcal{A}}(0,1,0)&\\
{\mathcal{H}}(0,w,0)&={\mathcal{A}}(0,w,0)\end{aligned}\right.

(3.4)

for all
t\in(0,T)
.

Assuming Lemma
3.1
, keeping in mind the discussion of reparametrisation at the start of this section, an application of the maximum principle to
{\mathcal{H}}(0,w,t)
immediately gives:

Corollary 3.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
1.7
, we have

{\mathcal{A}}_{-}\leq{\mathcal{H}}(0,w,t)\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}

for all
w\in(0,1)
and all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

As discussed earlier, it is a property of curve shortening flows with initially radial ends within the half-lines
L_{\pi}
and

L_{\alpha}
that over compact time intervals
[0,T_{0}]
, (the ends of)
the ends can each continue to be written as graphs over (the ends of)
L_{\pi}
and
L_{\alpha}
.
If we parametrise the end of
L_{\pi}
or
L_{\alpha}
using an arc-length parameter
x\in[0,\infty)
and view the end of the curve as the graph of a function

y(x,t)
, then
y
solves the graphical curve shortening flow equation

y_{t}=\frac{y_{xx}}{1+y_{x}^{2}}\equiv(\arctan(y_{x}))_{x}.

(3.5)

Lemma
A.1
from the appendix then guarantees that
y
and all its derivatives decay exponentially as
x\to\infty
, uniformly in time
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.

If we choose
\psi(u,0)
so that
\psi(u,0)=0
for
u>0
sufficiently close to
0
,
then
\psi(u,0)=\alpha
for
u<1
sufficiently close to
1
.
It then follows from the exponential decay that the angle function
\psi(u,t)

satisfies
\psi(u,t)\to 0
as
u\to 0
and
\psi(u,t)\to\alpha
as
u\to 1
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.
This instantly gives the claimed extension and boundary behaviour for
\Psi
in (
3.2
).
Moreover, the claimed equation satisfied by
\Psi(0,w,t)=\psi(w,t)
follows instantly from the equation satisfied by
\psi
in Lemma
2.1
.

Turning to
{\mathcal{A}}
, we note that the exponential decay of
y(x)
and its derivatives given by Lemma
A.1
imply that the integrand
*(\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma)
of the swept area
{\mathcal{A}}
must decay exponentially in any arc-length parameter (or equivalently with respect to
x
) as we exit along either end.
Indeed, up to an ambient isometry,
we could write each end of the curve as

\gamma=(x,y(x))
, in which case
\gamma_{x}=(1,y^{\prime}(x))
so

|\gamma_{x}\wedge\gamma|=|y(x)-xy^{\prime}(x)|,

which decays exponentially as
x\to\infty
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T_{0}]
.

This immediately allows us to extend
{\mathcal{A}}
continuously to
\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
.

We next wish to establish the equation for
{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t)
at an arbitrary time
\tau\in(0,T)
.
To do this, we take an arc-length parameter
u
at time
t=\tau
and follow the proof of Lemma
2.2
verbatim, but with
v
replaced by
0
, computing always at time
t=\tau
. The exponential decay of
y
and its derivatives along the ends allows us to differentiate under the integral sign in the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t)
to give

\displaystyle 2{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t)

\displaystyle=*\int_{0}^{w}(\gamma_{tu}\wedge\gamma+\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t})\,du

(3.6)

\displaystyle=*\big[\gamma_{t}\wedge\gamma\big](w)+*\,2\int_{0}^{w}\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t}\,du,

replacing (
2.6
).
But from (
2.4
) we have

2{\mathcal{A}}_{ww}=*\big[\gamma_{t}\wedge\gamma\big](w),

and similarly to before we have

\Psi(0,w,\tau)=\int_{0}^{w}\kappa(u)du=*\int_{0}^{w}\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t}\,du,

which implies the PDE of (
3.3
) at our arbitrary time
t=\tau
.

If we make the same simplification for
w
as we did for
v
,
replacing
w
by
1
,
then the computation above simplifies
even more. We obtain

{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\mathcal{A}}(0,1,t)=*\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{u}\wedge\gamma_{t}\,du=\Psi(0,1,\tau)=\alpha,

(3.7)

which integrates to give the final part of (
3.3
).

The properties of
{\mathcal{H}}
claimed in (
3.4
) follow immediately from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

It remains to prove the main Harnack theorem, but
before doing so we briefly comment on the symmetries of the swept area and turning function mentioned in the introduction.

Remark 3.3
.

Recall that both the swept area
{\mathcal{A}}
and turning function
\Psi
of a curve
\gamma:(0,1)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

negate
under reflections and under orientation-reversing reparametrisations.
Specifically,
if
\bar{\gamma}
is the reflection of
\gamma
across any line through the origin (e.g. the
x
–axis), and
\bar{}{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bar{\Psi}
are the swept area and turning function of
\bar{\gamma}
respectively,
then
\bar{}{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)=-{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)
and
\bar{\Psi}(v,w)=-\Psi(v,w)
,
and if
\hat{\gamma}
is the backward parametrisation of
\gamma
given by
\hat{\gamma}(u)=\gamma(1-u)
, and
\hat{}{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\Psi}
are the swept area and turning function of
\hat{\gamma}
respectively, then
\hat{}{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)=-{\mathcal{A}}(1-w,1-v)
and
\hat{\Psi}(v,w)=-\Psi(1-w,1-v)
.
In particular, if we reflect
and
reverse
\gamma
to get a new curve
\hat{\bar{\gamma}}=:\widetilde{\gamma}
, and let
\widetilde{}{\mathcal{A}}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
be the swept area and turning function of
\widetilde{\gamma}
respectively, then
\widetilde{}{\mathcal{A}}(v,w)={\mathcal{A}}(1-w,1-v)
and
\widetilde{\Psi}(v,w)=\Psi(1-w,1-v)
.
Geometrically, reflecting and reversing
\gamma
has the effect of interchanging the ends of
\gamma
while keeping the global properties the same.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

As discussed at the beginning of this section, we are free to reparametrise so that

{\mathcal{I}}=(0,1)
and
\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}
.
This makes
{\mathcal{A}}
,
\Psi
and
{\mathcal{H}}
functions of
\Sigma\times[0,T)
, where
\Sigma
is defined in (
3.1
).
Because we are considering curve shortening flows with initially radial ends, by Lemma
3.1
the functions
{\mathcal{A}}
,
\Psi
and
{\mathcal{H}}
extend continuously to

\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
.

By Corollary
3.2
, we know that

{\mathcal{A}}_{-}\leq{\mathcal{H}}(0,w,t)\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}

for all
w\in(0,1)
and
t\in[0,T)
, and by reflecting and reversing
\gamma
as discussed in Remark
3.3
, we can also deduce that

{\mathcal{A}}_{-}\leq{\mathcal{H}}(v,1,t)\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}

for all
v\in(0,1)
and
t\in[0,T)
.

Trivially, we have that
{\mathcal{H}}(u,u,t)=0
for all
u\in(0,1)
and
t\in[0,T)
.
Combining all these facts implies that

{\mathcal{A}}_{-}\leq{\mathcal{H}}\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}

throughout
\partial\Sigma\times[0,T)
, and also on
\overline{\Sigma}\times\{0\}
, i.e. on the entire parabolic boundary of
\Sigma\times[0,T)
.

This allows us to apply the maximum principle to the heat equation
(
2.3
) governing the evolution of
{\mathcal{H}}
.
We deduce that (
1.12
) holds throughout
\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

By (
1.14
), in the context of Theorem
1.9

we have
{\mathcal{V}}(t)={\mathcal{A}}(0,\beta,t)
.
The formula (
1.18
) for the area then follows from (
3.3
) in Lemma
3.1
with
\alpha:=\pi-\beta
, after reparametrising as at the beginning of this section.

The main inequality (

) is a translation of (

) of Theorem

in the special case of polar graphical flows, since
{\mathcal{A}}_{-}=0
and

.

Denote by

, the radial polar coordinate of the

-wedge solution at time

.
The inequalities of (

Estimates on when solutions of curve shortening flow with radial ends become graphical

In this section we prove Theorem

, and use it to deduce Theorem

.

Theorem
1.8
will follow by applying a maximum principle argument (similar to that in the previous section), not to (
2.3
) as in the proof of Theorem

, which we require to deduce Theorem

from Theorem

, we need to understand the behaviour of
\Psi
at extremal points of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

.

\gamma:(0,1)\times[0,T)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

, and extend the turning function

and the swept area

continuously to

, as described there.
Then for any

,

(4.1)

(ii)

if

then

\Psi(v_{*},w_{*},t)\geq 0.

(4.2)

Lemma 4.2
.

In the setting of Lemma
4.1
,
for any
t\in(0,T)
,

(i)

if

is such that

{\mathcal{A}}(0,w_{*},t)=\sup_{w\in(0,1)}{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t),

then

\Psi(0,w_{*},t)\leq\alpha.

(4.3)

(ii)

if
w_{*}\in[0,1]
is such that

{\mathcal{A}}(0,w_{*},t)=\inf_{w\in(0,1)}{\mathcal{A}}(0,w,t),

(4.4)

4.1
Proofs of Theorems
1.8
and
1.5
assuming Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2

Before proving Lemmas

and

.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

As in the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we may assume without loss of generality that
{\mathcal{I}}=(0,1)
and
\gamma_{t}=\vec{\kappa}
, and that the turning function
\Psi
and swept area
{\mathcal{A}}
extend continuously to
\overline{\Sigma}\times[0,T)
.
We wish to apply the maximum principle to the equation (
2.2
) for
{\mathcal{A}}
over
\Sigma\times[0,T)
, but to do so we first need to understand how
{\mathcal{A}}
behaves on
\partial\Sigma\times[0,T)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and define

{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}(v,w,t):={\mathcal{A}}(v,w,t)-\alpha t-\varepsilon(1+t).

Then

{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}(v,w,0)={\mathcal{A}}(v,w,0)-\varepsilon\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}-\varepsilon

(4.5)

and, from (
3.3
), we compute that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}(0,w,t)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left({\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}-\Delta_{w}\right){\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}(0,w,t)&=\Psi(0,w,t)-\alpha-\varepsilon&\quad w\in(0,1)\\

{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}(0,1,t)&={\mathcal{A}}(0,1,0)-\varepsilon(1+t)\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}-\varepsilon&\end{aligned}\right.

(4.6)

for all
t\in(0,T)

Appendix A
Exponential decay of graphical solutions starting from half-lines

In this appendix we give a simple lemma that will be used to show that curve shortening flows with initially radial ends must remain exponentially asymptotic to the ends for positive times. Since the flow will remain graphical over ends of the ends over finite time intervals, this amounts to proving estimates on solutions to (
3.5
).

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
y:[0,\infty)\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}

is a solution to graphical curve shortening flow (
3.5
) with

.

to so-called Angenent ovals

14

describe a curve shortening flow.
Note that

1\geq\sin\tilde{y}=2e^{t-a}\cosh(x-a)\geq e^{t-a}e^{a-x}=e^{t-x}

for all
