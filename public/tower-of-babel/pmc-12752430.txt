Interpretable machine learning approach for optimizing hospice care predictions using health assessment data
Authors: Shih-Yu Cho, Wei-Shu Lai, Jui-Hung Tsai, Peng-Chan Lin, Hsin-Hung Chou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDetermining the appropriate end-of-life (EOL) care model within a short time frame is challenging and requires extensive experience. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have developed automatic systems for identifying the hospice care models: hospice home (HHC), inpatient (HIC), and shared care (HSC). This study aimed to determine the optimal hospice care model for EOL patients with machine learning (ML) methods based on health assessment data.MethodsWe employed high-performance ML methods to build prediction models that could predict the most appropriate hospice care service for each patient using their health assessment data. Furthermore, we employed the knowledge distillation technique to transfer knowledge from the best-performing ML model to a decision tree model for classification interpretation.ResultsExperiments were conducted on a dataset of 3,468 hospice patients from National Cheng Kung University Hospital (2005–2020). ML models were built and validated, achieving high performance, with a macro-F1 score of 0.88 and an area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.95. In addition, an interpretable decision tree model was generated, which maintained high performance while providing clear, visualizable decision paths for the best hospice care model.ConclusionML models were developed using health assessment data to explore their potential in guiding the selection of hospice care services for end-of-life patients. The findings demonstrate a data-driven approach that may support more informed and personalized clinical decisions, while representing an initial proof of concept for integrating ML into hospice care planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03289-w.Keywords: Machine learning, Knowledge distillation, Hospice care services, Health assessment data, Clinical decision

Background

According to the World Health Organization [1], the goal of hospice care is to enhance the quality of life for patients with life-threatening illnesses and their families by addressing their physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs. Currently, the major service delivery models of specialist palliative care, namely, outpatient palliative care clinics, inpatient palliative care consultation teams, acute palliative care units, community-based palliative care, and hospice care, complement each other to provide comprehensive, supportive care from diagnosis to end-of-life (EOL) [2]. In Taiwan, hospice care encompasses three models: hospice home care (HHC), inpatient care (HIC), and shared care (HSC). Currently, nurses determine patient care models predominantly based on clinical judgment. For instance, an experienced nurse may assess that a patient presenting with symptoms such as dyspnea, dysphagia, or other severe conditions is inappropriate for home care and requires hospitalization.

Statistics from the National Health Insurance Administration in Taiwan’s report showed an increase in Hospice Care usage from approximately 43,000 in 2017 to 63,000 in 2021 [3]. As demand grows and medical technology advances, artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly leveraged to enhance hospice care. Machine learning (ML) models have shown promise in predicting overall survival [4–6] and mortality rates [7–13]. Other applications include those described by Khayal et al. and Chi et al., who analyzed racial differences among hospice patients [14, 15]. In contrast, Forsyth et al. and Manukyan et al. utilized model-assisted labeling in hospice care research [16, 17]. Additionally, Soltani et al. proposed using deep learning (DL) models to predict individual- and population-level needs within HHC [18]. Rather than employing DL/ML methods, Lai et al. analyzed health assessment data from hospice patients using statistical methods to identify key factors for selecting an HSC, HIC, or HHC model for EOL patients [19]. However, their approach requires healthcare professionals to review extensive patient data, which is time-consuming to do manually.

To the best of our knowledge, ML methods that utilize health assessment data have not yet been applied to determine the most suitable hospice care model for EOL patients. The ML method can quickly classify patients based on prior data and patterns, offering a more efficient alternative to labor-intensive, manual data review. Building on these foundations, this study is the first to employ ML methods in developing models that predict the most appropriate hospice care service (HSC, HIC, or HHC) for each patient. By identifying critical predictive factors and developing interpretable models, such as knowledge-distilled decision trees, this research aimed to provide a robust, accessible tool for clinical decision-making. This work advances EOL care by offering a method for supporting healthcare professionals, fostering the development of personalized treatment plans, and ensuring timely, informed care decisions.

With the advancement of AI technology, the application of ML in palliative care data has become an important research field. Many studies have explored the use of ML/DL models as auxiliary tools to enhance the decision-making process in palliative care. The following are recent studies related to this topic.

Avati et al. [7] used deep learning models to predict patients’ mortality within three to twelve months, determining whether patients should be referred to palliative care. The model was trained based on electronic health record (EHR) data. In addition to demographic information, ICD codes, CPT codes (Current Procedural Terminology), and RXCUI codes (RxNorm concept unique identifier) were used as features. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUCPR) of the model on the validation dataset was 0.69, and the recall rate was 0.34 when the precision was 0.9. It is worth noting that although the model’s performance yielded higher false positive predictions (i.e., individuals who were predicted to die within one year but survived longer), these patients were still diagnosed with terminal diseases or utilized high levels of healthcare services.

Cary et al. [8] used logistic regression and a multilayer perceptron to predict mortality within 30 days and 1 year after treatment of hip fractures in patients aged 65 years and older. In addition to general demographic information, the study utilized eight chronic diseases, Functional Independence Measure (FIM) scores, and length of stay in a rehabilitation medical facility (IRF) as input features. Although no independent test set was used, the average performance of ten-fold cross-validation was reported, with AUROC ranging from 0.756 to 0.765.

Parikh et al. [9] compared logistic regression models, random forests, and gradient boosting trees for predicting six-month mortality in patients attending the University of Pennsylvania Health System (UPHS) affiliated oncology clinic. The data included demographic information, comorbidity index (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index), etc. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.89.

Wang et al. [10] developed a neural network model for predicting mortality in patients with dementia, using natural language processing techniques to extract data from clinical records in addition to standard demographic information. On the validation data, the model achieved AUROCs of 0.978, 0.956, and 0.943 for predicting mortality at six months, one year, and two years, respectively.

Berg et al. [11] compared various models, including logistic regression, naïve Bayes, gradient boosting trees, neural networks, and support vector machines (SVMs), to predict 15-month mortality in community-dwelling US Medicare beneficiaries. Other studies on predicting mortality or survival include Blanes-Sevla et al. [12], who predicted one-year mortality in hospitalized patients, and Elfiky et al. [13], who predicted thirty-day mortality in cancer patients receiving chemotherapy.

In addition to studies focusing on predicting patient survival or mortality, other applications include Soltani et al. [18], who propose two DL models based on long short-term memory (LSTM) for demand prediction at both individual and group levels. Individual-level models predict the type and timing of the next service a specific patient will need; population-level models help predict the need for various services in a center supporting a particular patient population over the next week. Khayal et al. [14] and Chi et al. [15] analyzed racial differences among hospice patients; Forsyth et al. [16] and Manukyan et al. [17] used models to assist in annotating data in hospice research.

The studies above mainly focused on predicting mortality or survival. However, at the EOL, in addition to considering survival or mortality, it is also necessary to determine the most suitable hospice care method for patients based on the characteristics of different hospice service models. The study by Lai et al. [19] presented the characteristics of different models through statistical analysis. In contrast, this study developed and compared multiple ML models to recommend appropriate hospice service models based on the physical, psychological, and spiritual needs of patients, including HSC, HIC, and HHC. Furthermore, this study provides an interpretable clinical tool through feature analysis to identify features associated with specific service models and the order of importance of these features, as determined by the algorithm, thereby providing more accurate decision support for medical staff.

Data sources and patient cohort

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital (B-ER-109-05). The data for this study were sourced from the Hospice Database of National Cheng Kung University Hospital, comprising health assessment records collected by well-trained palliative care nursing professionals. The dataset consists of data on 8,874 hospice care patients from 2005 to 2020, encompassing 36 features. Key features of the dataset include basic demographics, symptom and sign scores, medication, and psychosocial and spiritual issues. All patients included in the dataset were suffering from terminal illnesses, including cancer, and voluntarily received hospice care. The models of hospice care administered were categorized into three types: HSC (7,076 records), HIC (918 records), and HHC (880 records). The hospice care model for all patients in the dataset was determined based on current practice, using experienced nurses’ assessment. The characteristics of the hospice delivery model’s dataset are summarized in Table S1 in the supplementary file.

Data was cleaned (Method 1 in the supplementary file) to include patients managed under a single hospice care model. Palliative patients will eventually pass away, and symptom improvement often represents only a transient therapeutic effect. We consider an appropriate care model to remain consistent from the initiation of care through to the patient’s end of life. For patients whose discharge outcomes are neither deterioration nor death, it remains uncertain whether they will subsequently change their care model. Thus, we cannot determine what model is appropriate for him. We excluded patients without clear discharge outcomes (death, impending death, or symptom improvement) to ensure consistency in model training, since it remains uncertain whether they will subsequently change their care model. After data cleaning and removing features with more than 40% missing values, 3,468 patient records with complete data remained, comprising 713 HHC, 642 HIC, and 2,113 HSC records, each consisting of 29 features. Numerical data were standardized, and categorical data were one-hot encoded, resulting in 57 features.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Effective feature selection is crucial for improving model performance, as it helps identify relevant features and reduce noise [20]. We compared different statistical methods and ultimately identified and removed six nonsignificant features (p-value >0.05), retaining 51 features. The variance threshold (VT) method was used to eliminate low-variance features [21], whereas recursive feature elimination (RFE) iteratively removed less important features until the optimal feature set was generated [22]. For detailed processes, please refer to Method 2 in the supplementary file. To address class imbalance among the hospice care models, far more patients were managed under HSC (60.9%) than under HHC (20.6%) and HIC (18.5%), we employed various techniques: class weights balancing, edited nearest neighbor (ENN) for undersampling [23], a combination of ENN and the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) [24], and sample duplication via K-means clustering.

Missing values were treated as augmented data. Since many more records depicted use of the HSC model than the other two categories, we imputed only 32 HIC and 120 HHC data points with the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) Imputer [25], multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) [26], and Miss Forest methods [27]. After imputing the missing values, the total number of data points was 3,620. Notice that all preprocessing steps were performed within each cross-validation split, with parameters fit solely on the training data.

Model training and validation

We built and compared five ML and one DL method-based predictive models for hospice patient classification. The models included logistic regression [28], support vector machine (SVM) [29], random forest classifier [30], eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [31], categorical boosting (CatBoost) [32], and multilayer perceptron (MLP) [33]. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted via a grid search, optimizing the parameters of each model to enhance performance. Please refer to Method 3 in the supplementary file for detailed information on model training.

To avoid overfitting, we adopted a 5-fold validation approach to evaluate the performance of the ML models using efficiency evaluation metrics. In this way, 716 HSC records were combined with 642 HIC and 713 HHC records, equally divided into five groups for five-fold cross-validation, and the remaining 1,397 HSC records were reserved for training. To fairly evaluate the models, the data were evenly distributed across hospice models with K-means clustering [34]. As shown in Figure S1 in the supplementary file, the process first ensures that the data of the three hospice modes are evenly distributed across the five groups of data. Then, the dataset is split using K-means to evenly distribute the data into five mutually exclusive subsets with similar characteristics.

Given the multiclass classification task and the imbalanced dataset, model performance was evaluated with the macro-F1 score, which averages precision and recall across all classes. In addition to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), we utilized the AUPRC as a key metric, as recommended for imbalanced datasets [35].

Feature significance

To ensure reliable predictions and gain the trust of healthcare professionals, we selected the two best-performing models, CatBoost and XGBoost, and calculated the feature importance values for both. To improve stability, each model was run 100 times, and the feature importance scores were averaged to highlight key factors influencing the hospice care decisions from the models. Additionally, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to both models to provide a comparative interpretation of the feature importance, offering further insights into the critical determinants across the two models. This approach ensures a robust and interpretable analysis of influential features in hospice care.

Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation (KD), a model compression technique, transfers knowledge from a complex teacher model to a simpler student model, enhancing the performance of the latter while reducing complexity and computational costs. In KD, the predictions of the well-trained teacher model guide the student model’s learning process [36]. In clinical settings, model interpretability is crucial for increasing healthcare professionals’ confidence. Although the SVM model in this study performed well, its interpretability is limited. To address this, we employed a decision tree as the student model to provide a transparent decision path, thereby enhancing the clinical understanding of the predictive process of the SVM model. Specifically, an SVM was first trained as the teacher model to generate soft labels for the training data, which were then used to train the decision tree student model. Finally, both models were validated on test data to confirm that the student model could achieve similar predictive performance to the teacher model. Results

We statistically analyzed the data of 3,468 patients managed with an HHC, HIC, or HSC model. Numerical variables are presented as the means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented as the percentages and counts. Across the entire cohort, the average age was 67 years; among the hospice care groups, however, this value differed significantly (p-value < 0.001). In terms of clinical diagnoses, digestive system diseases were the most common across all groups; however, this difference was significant among the groups (p-value < 0.001). Additionally, symptom management differed significantly, particularly regarding pain, dyspnea, constipation, and dysphagia. Psychosocial and spiritual needs also differed among the groups (Table S1 in the supplementary file).

Model performance

We compared various data preprocessing methods and selected the process that yielded the best results at each stage (Figure S2). For details on the experimental comparisons, please refer to Tables S2-S7 in the supplementary file. In this study, we evaluated several models based on precision, sensitivity (also known as recall), specificity, the macro-F1 score, and the AUPRC to assess their suitability in predicting hospice care needs. The logistic regression model demonstrated moderate predictive ability, with a precision of 0.86, a sensitivity of 0.85, a specificity of 0.93, a macro-F1 score of 0.86, and an AUPRC of 0.92. The SVM model showed stronger performance, with a precision of 0.88, a sensitivity of 0.87, a specificity of 0.94, a macro-F1 score of 0.88, and an AUPRC of 0.94, indicating greater reliability than the logistic regression model.

The random forest classifier achieved similar, though slightly lower, scores, with a precision and sensitivity of 0.85, a specificity of 0.93, a macro-F1 score of 0.85, and an AUPRC of 0.93. XGBoost and CatBoost performed similarly, each achieving a precision and sensitivity of 0.88, a specificity of 0.94, and a macro-F1 score of 0.88. Specifically, both models boasted an AUPRC of 0.95, the highest among the models tested. Finally, the MLP model yielded a precision and sensitivity of 0.85, a specificity of 0.93, a macro-F1 score of 0.86, and an AUPRC of 0.92, comparable to those achieved with logistic regression. These results suggest that the SVM, XGBoost, and CatBoost models offered the most promising predictive performance, particularly in terms of the AUPRC and macro-F1 score (Table 1).

Model interpretation

The feature importance values from the XGBoost and CatBoost models are shown in Fig. 4. The XGBoost model assigned the highest importance to family spiritual support (0.1771), demonstrating its central role in hospice care decisions. The next-highest importance was assigned to family psychosocial support (0.0824), indicating the importance of addressing the family’s emotional needs. Patient spiritual care (0.0687) and insomnia management (0.0583) were also important, reflecting the model’s focus on patient quality of life and their spiritual well-being. The fifth most important feature was psychosocial care for the patient (0.053), indicating the importance of a holistic approach that considers both emotional and physical aspects of EOL care. Similarly, the CatBoost model placed the most significant importance on family spiritual support (8.1588), reaffirming its essential role in hospice care. Dyspnea (7.0491) was the next most important feature, emphasizing the importance of managing respiratory disorders to enhance patient comfort. Insomnia (5.3043) and age (4.8981) were next, indicating the importance of restful sleep and age factors in tailoring EOL care. The fifth-most important feature in the CatBoost model was dysphagia (4.4757), underscoring the need to address swallowing difficulties that commonly impact patients in their final stages.

For the XGBoost model, family spiritual support ranks highest (0.1771), followed by family psychosocial support (0.0824), patient spiritual care (0.0687), insomnia management (0.0583), and psychosocial care for the patient (0.053), underscoring a holistic approach to end-of-life (EOL) care. For the CatBoost model, family spiritual support is also most important (8.1588), followed by dyspnea (7.0491), insomnia (5.3043), age (4.8981), and dysphagia (4.4757), reflecting a focus on both emotional support and physical comfort for the patients

The comparison of feature importance rankings across models revealed notable differences between XGBoost and CatBoost (Fig. 4). These discrepancies arise primarily from the different algorithms each model uses to compute feature importance. XGBoost estimates feature importance based on metrics such as information gain or split frequency, which capture how often and how effectively a feature is used to split decision nodes. In contrast, CatBoost applies the PredictionValueChange method, which quantifies the magnitude of changes in model outputs attributable to variations in individual features. Consequently, while both methods highlight influential predictors, differences in their underlying criteria can yield distinct importance rankings, reflecting complementary perspectives on model interpretability.

Figure. 5 presents the results of the SHAP value analysis for the XGBoost and CatBoost models. The feature importance and SHAP analyses jointly identified family spiritual support, psychosocial support, and insomnia management as the most crucial factors in predicting the best hospice care needs. Family spiritual support was consistently the most important feature and was especially relevant in high-intensity and specialized settings (HIC, HSC). Meanwhile, family psychosocial support and insomnia management address emotional and physical aspects crucial to EOL care. Together, these features provide a comprehensive basis for tailoring hospice care decisions

Additionally, variations in feature rankings were also observed between the traditional importance metrics (Fig. 4) and SHAP-based importance (Fig. 5) for the same classifier. This divergence stems from the conceptual difference between the two approaches: split-based importance measures the overall usage frequency of a feature in decision-making, whereas SHAP interprets contributions at the prediction level using Shapley values from cooperative game theory. Despite the variation in ranking order—for instance, Age ranking higher in Fig. 4B but lower in Fig. 5B—the overlap among the top features remains substantial (11 out of 12), suggesting that both methods consistently identify the key predictors driving the model’s performance.

While analyzing essential features from the tree-based models, we also examined the SVM results. Although the SVM model demonstrated strong predictive performance, it lacks interpretability. Therefore, we employed knowledge distillation to extract the performance of the SVM and created a decision path diagram (Fig. 6). Preliminary analysis of the generated decision paths suggests that patients with family members experiencing spiritual issues (n = 2,298) may benefit from HSC or HHC services. Specifically, if the patient also has spiritual needs (n = 784) and considers signing a DNR order before receiving hospice care (n = 259), HHC may be more suitable. In contrast, if they prefer to consider a DNR order after receiving hospice care (n = 525), HSC is recommended. Furthermore, for patients with only mild insomnia (insomnia ≤ 0.199, n = 1205), HHC may suffice if their family members do not have psychosocial concerns (n = 11). If the patient has mild dyspnea (dyspnea ≤ 0.25, n = 45), HHC would likewise suffice; however, if the patient’s condition is more severe (insomnia ≥ 0.199 or dyspnea ≥ 0.25, n = 264) or if their family members have psychosocial concerns (n = 1194), HSC should be considered.

The decision path illustrates the outcome of employing knowledge distillation to train a decision tree based on the results obtained from the SVM model. While the complete tree has a depth of 18, we present a simplified example with four levels. The decision tree illustrates the decision-making process, highlighting its key components. Branches represent decision rules, internal nodes indicate additional splits, and leaf nodes represent the outcomes. In this diagram, the orange nodes represent class 0, which corresponds to HHC; the green nodes represent class 1, which corresponds to HIC; and the purple nodes represent class 2, which corresponds to HSC. Different color schemes represent different categories, or different care modes. The color depth is inversely proportional to the gini value, which represents the level of confidence in the assessment. The lower the gini value (the darker the color), the higher the confidence that the corresponding branch is. The “value” item in a node represents the list of sample numbers per class. The “class” item in an internal node refers to the class with the largest number of samples in that node. As for “gini” item, it refers to the gini function of the decision tree

For family members without spiritual concerns but with psychosocial problems (n = 987), HIC is likely more appropriate. In cases where the patient has not received any related psychosocial care (n = 568) or lacks awareness of their disease prognosis (n = 101), HSC is recommended. For patients whose family members do not have psychosocial issues (n = 335) and who have mild dyspnea (n = 293), HHC remains a viable option. Among patients who had previously received psychosocial care (n = 28), younger patients might consider HIC (n = 22), whereas older patients (n = 6) might consider HHC. Additionally, if the patient’s family has some understanding of the disease prognosis (n = 12), HSC could also be selected.

This study has some limitations. To better understand the causes of minor prediction errors, we conducted several interpretive analyses. For instance, we considered the length of case intake as a factor, comparing the outcomes for durations of 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. The results revealed an average F1 score discrepancy of nearly 10% between short- and long-term patients, with notable prediction errors for short-term patients receiving home care. Table S7 in the supplementary file presents a comparison of model performance for short- and long-term patients, using a 3-day threshold, with CatBoost. Next, we performed Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum and chi-square tests on short- and long-term patients in home care, and evaluated the results using a 95% confidence interval (two-tailed, p-value < 0.05). Table S8 in the supplementary file summarizes the distribution of significant characteristics, showing that short-term home care patients presented with more severe health conditions, such as dyspnea, edema, and incontinence, but fewer psychosocial issues—conversely, long-term home care patients presented with more insomnia and psychosocial problems.

Discussion

In this study, we built ML models to predict the most suitable hospice care services accurately. The models offer a data-driven approach to selecting the optimal hospice care service based on health assessment data, providing healthcare professionals with a valuable tool for informed decision-making in EOL care. In a cohort of 3,468 EOL patients, including those under HHC, HIC, and HSC models, ML models were developed and validated, achieving high performance, with a macro F1 score of 0.88 and an AUPRC of 95% across all three models. The key features influencing the model predictions included family spiritual support, family psychosocial support, and insomnia management.

This study comprehensively analyzes the characteristics of 3,468 patients across three hospice care models: HIC, HSC, and HHC. Significant differences in demographics and clinical diagnoses were observed among the groups. Across the entire cohort, the average patient age was 67 years (p < 0.001), and digestive system diseases were the most common diagnoses. Regarding model performance, the SVM, XGBoost, and CatBoost models achieved strong predictive scores of 0.88. Their inference times, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.58 s, respectively, demonstrate their effectiveness in potential clinical applications, completing data preprocessing and producing predictions in under one second.

Feature importance analysis of the XGBoost and CatBoost models revealed critical insights for healthcare professionals. Family spiritual support, psychosocial support, and insomnia management were identified as key factors in predicting hospice care needs, especially in high-intensity and specialized settings. These elements address both emotional and physical aspects, forming a comprehensive foundation for making personalized care decisions.

The results of this study emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary care, whereby interventions from social workers, physical therapists, spiritual caregivers, and psychologists enhance holistic patient management. Dysphagia and edema were identified as concerns that require careful monitoring, as they can lead to complications and indicate underlying health issues. Other important factors include incontinence, which affects patient dignity and increases the risk of infection, and constipation, which is a common issue among patients undergoing specific treatments. Addressing these issues can improve the quality of care in hospice settings. While tree-based models were the best-performing among the investigated models, the SVM model also showed strong performance but difficulty in terms of interpretability. Knowledge distillation was therefore utilized to extract insights from the SVM model, and a decision path diagram was developed. Preliminary analyses of this diagram suggested that patients whose family members face spiritual concerns may benefit from HSC or HHC services. Recommendations can be made based on patient considerations regarding DNR orders and symptom severity, guiding clinical decision-making for EOL care. Overall, this research enhances the understanding of hospice care and informs practical clinical decision-making.

This study has some limitations: (1) the findings are derived from a single institution and therefore require external validation with multi-institutional datasets; (2) the model represents a proof-of-concept rather than deployment readiness; (3) the distilled decision tree reflects key practice features rather than optimal care pathways, while offering value for clinical decision support; and (4) over-cleaning and over-simplifying data (from 8,874 to 3,468) may cause overfitting. Future studies should incorporate cross-context comparisons to differentiate generalizable features from those that are specific.

Conclusion

This study presents a proof-of-concept application of machine learning (ML) to support decision-making in hospice care. The proposed model demonstrates the potential to assist in predicting and recommending appropriate hospice services based on baseline patient characteristics and health assessment data. The results suggest that ML-based approaches can provide timely, data-driven insights that may complement clinical judgment in care planning. Furthermore, by employing knowledge distillation, the study offers an interpretable framework that enhances understanding of model decision paths, contributing to future efforts toward clinically applicable and transparent ML systems in hospice settings.
