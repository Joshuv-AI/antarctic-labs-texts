Neoadjuvant immuno-chemotherapy combined with antiangiogenic therapy for patients with resectable high-risk intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and excellent performance status: interim efficacy results from the ZSAB-neoGOLP trial
Authors: Marco Gerling, Hannes Jansson
Published: 2026

The ZSAB-neoGOLP trial by Shi et al. is the first completed and reported phase II–III randomized controlled trial of neoadjuvant therapy for patients with biliary tract cancer (BTC). Importantly, it is disease-specific for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), randomizing 178 patients with this relatively rare cancer type (1).

This is an impressive and clinically important achievement in light of previous trials that have included patients with BTCs arising in the gallbladder, intra-/extrahepatic bile ducts, and ampulla of Vater (2,3), despite significant biological differences between these diseases (4).

The study thus represents an important milestone toward advancing care for patients with iCCA specifically. Currently, most patients undergoing surgery for iCCA experience recurrence, and median overall survival after surgery is limited to less than 5 years (4), indicating a strong rationale to develop novel strategies, including neoadjuvant treatment.

The primary endpoint of the neoGOLP trial was event-free survival, defined as a composite of progression to unresectability, recurrence, development of another malignancy, or death.

This choice of outcome, together with a trial population consisting of patients with iCCA and oncological high-risk criteria (clinical staging with tumor size >5 cm, multiple lesions, vascular invasion, or lymph node metastases), were likely key factors in the successful accrual and completion of the trial, as the current literature justifies a high expected event rate, with a median-event free survival time well below 1 year for the trial control arm. The NEO-GAP phase II trial employed similar high-risk criteria and reported a median recurrence-free survival of only 7.1 months after treatment with gemcitabine, cisplatin and nab-paclitaxel, followed by surgery (5).

The importance of these design considerations is further illustrated by the challenges faced by contemporaneous neoadjuvant trials that employed broader eligibility criteria. A German phase III trial of neoadjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin in BTC (AIO-GAIN) was stopped prematurely due to slow accrual; results for 68 randomized patients, reported at the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2025, showed prolonged overall survival in the neoadjuvant arm (6). This study protocol did not select for higher tumor-node-metastasis (TNM)/American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage. With overall survival as the primary endpoint, the study protocol’s power calculation required a sample size of more than 300 participants, impacting feasibility.

The selection of a composite outcome with a high expected event rate supports the power and sample size calculations of the ZSAB-neoGOLP trial. The same investigators previously conducted the ZSAB-TransGOLP phase II trial of immuno-chemotherapy and antiangiogenic therapy for patients with unresectable localized BTC (7). The TransGOLP trial showed a promising 90% disease-control rate, with two-thirds of patients achieving conversion to resectability (7). Furthermore, a similar “GOLP” regimen [gemcitabine-oxaliplatin-lenvatinib-anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1)] was evaluated as a first-line systemic therapy in an iCCA-specific phase II trial led by the same primary investigators, with an objective response rate of 80% (8). While this quadruple combination does not align with the Western standard in the first-line palliative setting, which is gemcitabine-cisplatin-durvalumab/pembrolizumab based on the TOPAZ (9) and KEYNOTE-966 trials (10), the reported results strongly support the efficacy of the GOLP regimen.

In the neoGOLP cohort, the majority of tumors (72%) displayed vascular invasion or a diameter >5 cm (60%). Multiple tumors (17%) and radiological lymph node metastases (15%) were less prevalent. For comparison, in the NEO-GAP phase II trial from the United States, the median tumor number was 3.5 and 35% of patients had lymph node metastases (5).

The most pronounced treatment benefit was seen in the vascular invasion subgroup; notably, in prespecified subgroup analyses, no event-free survival benefit was observed for patients with clinical N1-status, multiple tumors or age ≥65 years. The cohort’s relatively low median age (59 years) and low rates of lymph node metastasis and tumor multiplicity suggest a risk for type II error in these specific subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, with a 66% reduction in risk for patients with vascular invasion, this factor appears to be a key marker of response. In the subgroup without vascular invasion, the control arm had numerically fewer events (hazard ratio =2.06; 95% confidence interval: 0.88–4.78). The risk reduction in patients with vascular invasion is clinically noteworthy and may reflect a biological rationale for vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) inhibition via lenvatinib—currently absent from current Western first-line regimens—in this subgroup. More broadly, both vascular invasion and elevated carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) showed comparable magnitudes of risk reduction, and their combined consideration as selection criteria warrants prospective evaluation in future neoadjuvant trials.

The strict inclusion criteria allowed only study participants with World Health Organization (WHO)/Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0, i.e., patients fully fit and able to carry out any pre-disease activity without restriction. While this may be motivated by the 49% grade 3 treatment-related adverse event risk reported in the preceding ZSAB-TransGOLP phase II trial (7), this selection affects generalizability. Another aspect worth discussing is the high prevalence of hepatitis B infection (22% with active hepatitis B), reflecting viral hepatitis as an important risk factor in high-prevalence regions and underscoring the need for international validation studies, as noted by the authors (1). The control group, treated with upfront surgery followed by eight cycles of adjuvant capecitabine according to the BILCAP protocol (2), reflects current clinical practice for resectable iCCA in many parts of the world. The Japanese adjuvant phase III ASCOT trial, which was positive in the intention-to-treat analysis comparing S-1 with observation, has provided further evidence of the efficacy of oral fluoropyrimidines after curative-intent resection for BTC (3).

For iCCA, the high-risk criteria used by both the NEO-GAP and the ZSAB-neoGOLP trial (corresponding to a TNM/AJCC 8th edition stage Ib or higher) identify patients with technically resectable tumors but poor oncological prognosis, which may be interpreted as a borderline resectability category. Rather than reflecting technical considerations, these criteria capture an adverse tumor biology, i.e., biological borderline resectability. Importantly, standardized terminology for borderline resectability in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) has yet to be established. In this context, it is important to note the limited sensitivity of current radiological staging modalities for detection of lymph node metastases in iCCA.

A recent phase II trial of hepatic artery infusion gemcitabine-oxaliplatin chemotherapy plus anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and lenvatinib (NEO-ERA-01), with preliminary data reported at ASCO 2025, employed the same high-risk criteria as the NEO-GAP and ZSAB-neoGOLP trials (11). Here, the disease control rate was 93% and the grade 3 treatment-related adverse event rate was 31% (11). NEO-ERA-01 allowed inclusion of patients with WHO/ECOG performance status 0–1 (12). The primary outcome was safety and feasibility (completion of both neoadjuvant therapy and resection), with event-free and overall survival as secondary endpoints (12); final results are awaited.

Consistent with neoGOLP, recent and ongoing neoadjuvant trials for BTC have selected patients with a higher clinical stage (5,11,13). The Japanese phase III NABICAT trial, comparing neoadjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin-S-1 to upfront resection for BTC, has now concluded recruitment, and results are awaited (14). NABICAT included patients with T2 status (perihilar and distal CCA), T3 status (iCCA, gallbladder cancer, ampullary cancer), or lymph node metastases (N1) (14). A preceding phase II trial of gemcitabine-cisplatin-S1 used fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) to assess lymph node positivity and included patients with lymph node metastasis and any T-status (13).

Looking forward, a central question is which regimen should be assessed in international, ideally multi-continental, validation trials of neoadjuvant systemic therapy for high-risk iCCA. With gemcitabine-cisplatin and immunotherapy established as the current palliative standard, a neoadjuvant trial of gemcitabine-cisplatin-durvalumab/pembrolizumab could be a justifiable option, while the addition of tyrosine kinase inhibitors merits consideration given the impressive efficacy of GOLP regimens. A second key question concerns the choice of adjuvant therapy in the control arm. Equipoise may have shifted so that it is time for a phase III randomized controlled trial of neoadjuvant and adjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin-anti-PD1 (± a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) versus upfront resection and adjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin-anti-PD1 in high-risk iCCA. For patients with perihilar and distal CCA, a Dutch multicenter phase IIb–III study (NEODISCO) is investigating neoadjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin-pembrolizumab with adjuvant pembrolizumab, although the comparison arm remains upfront surgery only, reflecting current standard of care in the Netherlands (15). For patients with iCCA specifically, most announced or ongoing neoadjuvant trials are conducted in Asia (seven out of ten studies listed on ClinicalTrials.gov). An Italian phase II–III trial (PURITY) of neoadjuvant gemcitabine-nab-paclitaxel for patients with high-risk BTC (gallbladder cancer or CCA) is ongoing and includes patients with iCCA following criteria similar to neoGOLP (N1/≥ T1b) (16). With regard to iCCA-specific neoadjuvant trials outside of Asia, a phase II trial of gemcitabine-cisplatin-durvalumab is currently ongoing in the United States (17); a phase II trial of neoadjuvant gemcitabine-cisplatin-nab-paclitaxel and immunotherapy with a bispecific antibody (Rilvegostomig) has been announced in Canada (18); and a phase II trial of neoadjuvant selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) plus capecitabine is recruiting in France (19).

The role of lenvatinib-containing combination therapy for iCCA remains an important question. The authors of neoGOLP cite synergistic action, multi-target effects, augmented immune response, promising phase II data, and the current trial results as arguments for combining anti-PD1 with lenvatinib (1). Whether vascular invasion is a predictive marker of lenvatinib response in iCCA is an open question. In retrospective surgical iCCA cohorts, varying rates of vascular invasion have been reported (20), often derived from postoperative pathological data rather than preoperative imaging. While lenvatinib is established as a first-line treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma, the role of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in iCCA remains unclear. Although several phase II–III studies evaluating lenvatinib in iCCA in phase are registered, all are conducted in Asia. This reflects both the urgent need for improved treatments in iCCA—work spearheaded by expert academic centers in a high-prevalence region, as exemplified by the ZSAB study group—and the necessity of further global collaboration and international validation studies.
