Periodontal Status of Medically Compromised Patients Visiting Teaching Hospitals in Riyadh City: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Mahesh Shenoy, Abdullah H. M. Alqahtani, Osama M. S. Alzabni, Abdulrahman A. H. Alsaqobi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Periodontitis is an infection of the tissues, surrounding and supporting the teeth. Certain systemic conditions affect periodontal tissues and vice-versa. The research investigates how systemic illness influence periodontal health of medically frail patients, came to obtain oral healthcare in teaching hospitals of Riyadh City.Materials and Methods:A retrospective study design was employed, analyzing patient files from the past 10 years. Patients diagnosed with medically compromised conditions (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, immunosuppressive disorders) and with complete periodontal records were included. Chi-square test was done.Results:Chi-square test was done to compare between the groups and the findings showed that overall there was no statistically significant difference among the groups except when compared on the basis of plaque index and percentage bone loss (P value. 002 and. 030, respectively).Conclusion:From the present study it was concluded that, though diabetes and hypertension, showed greater plaque build-up and poor periodontal health outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal health gets compromised when bacterial accumulations on the teeth start an inflammatory reaction, leading to alveolar bone destruction and loss of tissue attachment.[1] Periodontal disease range from, most prevalent, easily treatable gingivitis to more advanced irreversible periodontitis.[2] In the past three decades, marked advances have occurred in our understanding of the infectious agents of periodontal disease.[3] Presence of anaerobic bacteria P.gingivalis in non-oral tissues like atheromatous plaques,[4] significant prevalence of E.corrodens, T.denticola, C.albicans and P.gingivalis in diabetic patients, show strong coorelation between periodontitis and systemic diseases. A recent hypothesis links chronic subclinical inflammation with insulin resistance, initiating the development of type 2 diabetes. The triggers of inflammation are many and potentially include oral infection.[5] Conversely, hyperglycemia produce inflammatory mediators leading to increased vascular permeability, greater breakdown of collagen fibers, and accelerated destruction of both nonmineralized connective tissue and bone.[6] From the above-mentioned findings, it can be stated that a logical framework provided by elucidation of the mechanism behind periodontal infection and systemic conditions would aid in the search and a possible hypothesis unifying both these inflammatory diseases could offer a unique opportunity for improving complications associated with both diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the participating teaching hospitals, with the IRB approval number “FUGRP/2025/419/1246/1127”. Patient confidentiality was maintained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A retrospective study design was employed, analyzing patient files from the past 10 years. OPG Radiographs (retrieved from Dentoplus System) were used to evaluate bone levels and to determine the area most affected by periodontal disease. The study was held in Munesiya campus of Riyadh Elm University, Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Patients diagnosed with medically compromised conditions (e.g. diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, immunosuppressive disorders), patients with complete periodontal records and patients who received treatment or consultation at teaching hospitals in Riyadh City within the last 10 years were included in the study whereas Medically fit patients, patients with incomplete records, patients under 18 years of age, pregnant women (to eliminate hormonal bias) were excluded from the study. The sample size was calculated using a confidence level of 95% and a power of 80% with 394 being the required number. To ensure consistency, all examiners involved in data extraction underwent calibration training. Inter- and intra-examiner reliability was assessed using kappa statistics, with a target value of ≥0.80 (Chronbach’s value 0.951).

Statistical Analysis: Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical data. Chi-square tests and independent t-tests compare periodontal parameters (e.g. Plaque Index, Gingival Index, PSR) across systemic conditions.

RESULTS

In the present study, a total of 394 patients were screened. Participant’s age averaged at 40.1304 years (SD 6.9498). This indicates that the participant’s ages are likely close to the mean value. There were 39.1% males and 60.9% females. In terms of nationality, the predominant portion of the sample were Saudis (82.6%). Among the study population, Periodontal disease status was mild for (44%), moderate for (30%) and severe for (26%). Hypertension was most prevalent (29%) among the population, followed by diabetes (21%), asthma (17%), kidney disease (14%), cardiovascular diseases (12%), and others (7%). DISCUSSION

This study sought to determine the magnitude of systemic diseases among the study population, with a particular emphasis on its relationship with periodontal status and plaque index. The results gleaned from this study adds to the understanding of systemic conditions like diabetes, hypertension, asthma, kidney diseases and heart diseases in relation to the periodontal health.

Prevalence of systemic diseases

The present study revealed that hypertension-related complications were the most prevalent among the participants (29%) followed by diabetes (21%), asthma (17%), chronic kidney disease (14%) and coronary heart disease (12%). These findings correspond with the international data that classify hypertension and diabetes as two of the most common chronic conditions around the globe.

The findings were also consistent with the report made by Özçaka et al. (2014),[7] they stated that older adults with systemic ailments like hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes exhibited more advanced periodontal disease and tooth loss compared to relatively healthier individuals.

Periodontal status

The results of the periodontal assessment in the current study indicate that 44% of the participants had mild periodontal disease, 30% had moderate disease, and only 26% had severe periodontal conditions. These results demonstrate relatively favorable levels of periodontal health among the study population but also indicate that a good proportion of the population is still at risk.

The findings are in agreement with Genctoy et al. (2007),[8] they assessed gingival inflammation using the Löe and Silness Gingival Index and described how severe gingivitis was related to increase carotid intima media thickness, a measure for atherosclerosis. This supports our study’s reasoning that systemic effects, even from mild periodontal disease, are possible if not properly managed.

High plaque index and its association with systemic diseases

The current study results show that high plaque index was observed in diabetics (39%), followed by asthmatics (21%) and hypertensive patients (12%). This corresponds with the result of Ayan and Dayi (2023),[9] they reported significant higher plaque and gingival index values among obese individuals. This may be due to common inflammatory and metabolic derangements seen in diabetic patients. Manzi et al. (1999)[10] add further corroboration to the association by describing the presence of periodontal pathogens in subgingival and atherosclerotic plaques. Their work underpins the mechanism of systemic inflammation associated with oral microbial ecosystems, especially in people suffering from systemic diseases.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we have found that systemic diseases, especially diabetes and hypertension, are linked to greater plaque build-up and poor periodontal health outcomes. However in general, there was no significant difference in the periodontal status of patients with and without systemic disease.
