SeqBoost: a sequential explainable model for predicting ED revisits within 72 hours
Authors: Pat Vatiwutipong, Thanapon Noraset
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurately predicting emergency department (ED) revisits within 72 hours remains challenging due to irregular and short patient visit histories. This study investigates how temporal representations of historical ED utilization and sequential modeling can improve predictive performance and model explainability.MethodsWe evaluate two complementary approaches using the MIMIC-IV-ED dataset. First, we introduce interval-based temporal features that summarize the recency and spacing of prior ED visits, and compare them with conventional visit-count summaries. Second, we examine longitudinal visit-level representations and propose Sequential Boosting (SeqBoost), a gradient-boosting framework that incorporates historical visits sequentially without padding missing values. Models are evaluated using patient-level cross-validation and AUROC, and feature attributions are analyzed using SHAP.ResultsInterval-based temporal features consistently outperformed visit-count summaries, particularly for short histories. For patients with a single prior visit, AUROC increased from 0.590 to 0.641, and further to 0.657 when combined with visit counts. Incorporating longitudinal visit-level features improved performance to an AUROC of 0.691 at history length two. SeqBoost achieved predictive performance comparable to that of non-sequential longitudinal models while avoiding padded missing values. SHAP analyses showed that SeqBoost generated feature attributions based solely on observed visits, whereas non-sequential models relied heavily on missing historical features.ConclusionTemporal-interval statistics and longitudinal visit-level representations improve 72-hour ED revisit prediction in MIMIC-IV-ED. SeqBoost achieves competitive predictive performance while providing more stable, clinically interpretable feature attributions by eliminating artifacts caused by missing values. These findings support more reliable and interpretable clinical decision support for identifying patients at risk of return to the ED.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03582-2.

Introduction

Revisits to the emergency department (ED) within 72 hours are widely recognized as a commonly used proxy indicator of care quality, posing risks to patient safety and contributing to ED overcrowding [1, 2]. Reducing these revisits can improve patient outcomes, alleviate resource strain, and enhance overall care delivery. Understanding the causes of revisits requires analyzing detailed patient data, such as visit history, diagnoses, and treatment outcomes, recorded in Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [3]. However, analyzing the revisit data and modeling revisit risk are challenging due to the unequal lengths of patient records and irregular time intervals between visits. These variations make it challenging to capture meaningful patterns using conventional machine learning approaches. Figure 2 (left) illustrates the visit data of a few patients.

Most existing models for ED revisit prediction use machine learning techniques on EHR data, relying heavily on summary statistics of the number of visits, such as the number of visits in the past year or binary indicators of prior revisits [4–6]. Some studies incorporate broader features, such as diagnoses and treatment codes, and apply models ranging from logistic regression to tree-based ensemble methods, including random forests and XGBoost [7–9]. However, these methods typically treat historical data in aggregated or flat form, overlooking visit timing and sequencing.

To address the limitations of aggregated history in previous work, this paper explores a more comprehensive representation of patient visit history through two key approaches. First, we observe that the visit counts commonly used in prior studies reduce the entire history to a single number and therefore fail to represent how frequently, how recently, and how irregularly patients return to the ED. Thus, we introduce a set of temporal features based on the statistical distribution of time intervals between prior ED visits to capture recurring or clustered utilization patterns, even when visits fall outside conventional look-back windows (e.g., the past 6 or 12 months).

Second, we extend this idea by treating each prior visit as a distinct unit of information. A straightforward way to do this is to concatenate visit-level features across the truncated history, padding shorter sequences with missing values. Our experiments show that while this representation can improve accuracy over aggregated baselines, it also introduces systematic missing-value artifacts that inflate the apparent importance of absent visits. Moreover, we observed that the most recent visits carry the strongest predictive signal. Motivated by these observations, we propose incorporating historical information sequentially, allowing only existing older visits to contribute to the prediction. To achieve this, we introduce SeqBoost, a variant of gradient boosting [10] designed to process short visit sequences without padding the representation with artificial missingness.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:We propose a temporal representation of ED visits that captures recency and clustering of prior visits, using inter-visit interval statistics rather than simple visit counts (Sect. 3.2).We propose visit-level representations for short patient histories and introduce SeqBoost to incorporate historical visits without padding (Sects. 3.3 and 3.4).We evaluate our methods on the MIMIC-IV-ED benchmark [9], demonstrating that the proposed temporal features and the full visit-level representations improve the model performance (Sects. 5.1 and 5.2) and that the SeqBoost models have better explainability than traditional boosting models for the ED revisit prediction (Sect. 5.3).

Literature review

72-hour ED revisits

Emergency department (ED) revisits within 72 hours of discharge are widely used as quality metrics and patient safety indicators across many healthcare systems [11, 12]. These revisits may signal unresolved clinical issues from the index visit, suboptimal discharge planning, or failures in care transitions. Numerous studies have shown that early unplanned returns to the ED are associated with adverse outcomes, including increased risk of hospitalization, higher mortality, and poorer patient satisfaction [4–6, 12]. They also place additional strain on healthcare resources: each extra visit consumes significant medical resources and contributes to ED overcrowding [5]. Crowding, in turn, can degrade care quality by prolonging wait times and increasing the risk of treatment delays or errors [6]. Identifying patients at risk of early return has become a pressing operational and clinical priority.

Despite their clinical relevance, predicting 72-hour ED revisits remains particularly challenging. Unlike 30-day hospital readmissions, for which extensive research has identified strong predictors and structured interventions, 72-hour ED revisits occur over a much shorter time frame and are driven by a more heterogeneous set of factors [13]. Revisits may result from complications of the original condition, patient misunderstanding of discharge instructions, progression of disease, or failure to access outpatient care. Social and behavioral factors such as housing instability, substance use, and caregiver availability further complicate risk prediction but are often underdocumented in electronic health records (EHRs). This makes it challenging to predict revisits solely from current ED visits.

Predicting ED revisits

Numerous machine learning and statistical models have been proposed to identify patients at high risk for early ED return [4–9]. These models span a variety of algorithms, including logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, support vector machines, and, more recently, deep learning models. A recent scoping review of 72-hour revisit prediction methods identified 28 relevant studies, revealing considerable variation in study designs, feature sets, and performance metrics [13].

Most models use structured data from EHRs, such as age, triage level, vital signs, laboratory results, comorbidity indices, insurance type, and diagnosis codes. For example, a study using Veterans Affairs data demonstrated that adding prior-year healthcare utilization (e.g., number of ED visits) and comorbidities to demographic features improved predictive performance from an AUC of 0.55 to 0.74 [6]. Another study focused more broadly on frequent ED users, Doupe et al. [14] identified past healthcare utilization, particularly ED visits and hospitalizations in the previous year, as highly predictive of both short-term and long-term ED use. Similarly, Ryan et al. ryan2015understanding, in a study of over 1 million Medicaid ED records, found stark differences in revisit risk patterns between “frequent flyers” and first-time visitors. They showed that past revisits, temporal proximity, and encounter-specific features (e.g., alcoholism, distance to hospital) were strong predictors of repeat visits among frequent users. In contrast, a broader, more varied set of features was necessary for less frequent patients. The same study found that patients with a prior 72-hour revisit in the last six months had three times the risk of returning compared to others (13% vs. 4.4%).

However, other studies question the generalizability of such features. Xie et al. xie2022benchmarking used the MIMIC-IV-ED dataset and reported that prior-year utilization features contributed little to model performance for 72-hour revisit prediction. While such aggregated summaries are easy to implement and explain, they inherently overlook crucial nuances in the timing, sequence, and clinical context of each visit. Ryan et al. ryan2015understanding acknowledged these limitations, noting that simple historical visit counts fail to capture the complexity of revisit behaviors effectively. Similarly, Hu et al. hu2017predicting and Pellerin et al. pellerin2018predicting achieved moderate predictive results, but highlighted the potential shortcomings of ignoring detailed temporal patterns. These findings suggest that simply aggregating prior visits into a single count or score may not capture the full complexity of patient trajectories.

Our study directly addresses these limitations by explicitly modeling ED revisit data as sequences of visits. Our results on MIMIC-IV-ED suggest that, when temporal structure is modeled more explicitly, historical visit features contribute more strongly to 72-hour revisit prediction than suggested by Xie et al. xie2022benchmarking.

Explainability of a predictive model

In machine learning, explainability is defined as the level to which the reasoning behind a model’s predictions can be effectively articulated and communicated to human users through appropriate methods and tools [15–17]. Insufficient explainability of prediction models can hinder real-world adoption, weaken patient trust, and introduce potential legal or regulatory challenges [18]. In healthcare contexts, when clinicians cannot clearly understand the explanations underlying ML-based recommendations, this gap may unintentionally compromise clinical autonomy, accountability, and ethical compliance.

However, explainability is not determined solely by the choice of model architecture or explanation technique, but is also strongly shaped by data quality and preprocessing decisions [19, 20]. In particular, missing data and NaN values can substantially undermine model explainability, especially when post-hoc explanation methods such as SHAP are employed [21]. Both the presence of missing values and the strategy used to handle them, whether through imputation or direct modeling, have been shown to significantly affect Shapley values in terms of their magnitude, sign, and feature importance ranking [19, 21]. Notably, while models that natively handle missing values, such as XGBoost, may achieve strong predictive performance, they have been observed to induce the largest distortions in Shapley value distributions when compared to models trained on imputed data, revealing a fundamental trade-off between predictive accuracy and explainability under missing data [20–22].

ED revisit prediction

We propose recasting 72-hour ED revisit prediction as a sequence classification problem. Rather than collapsing historical visits into aggregate counts, we treat each patient’s visit history as a sequence of timestamped events with rich, structured descriptors (e.g., diagnosis codes, procedures, discharge status, medications, and triage category).

Interval-based temporal features

The feature set for each ED visit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{VF}(v_i^{(p)})$$\end{document}) includes the following feature groups: patient demographics, details of the ED visit, and clinical variables of that particular visit. The clinical variables are further divided into triage information, chief complaints, health signals (e.g., temperature and heart rate), and ICD-based information. We follow Xie et al. [9] feature extraction method to obtain them.

Visit count features (VC)

A typical set of summarized features are visit counts. For example, Hao et al. [23] showed that 30-day ED revisit risk increases with the number of ED visits in the prior 12 months and incorporated prior-year ED visit counts as a key predictor; Zhang et al. [7] reported that the number of previous ED visits was among the most informative features in an XGBoost model for predicting revisits within 72 hours and 9 days; Zhang et al. [24] included the frequency of ED visits in the prior year as part of their operations and utilization variables when predicting 72-hour return visit admissions; and Xie et al. [9] used previous ED visit counts over multiple retrospective windows (e.g., 30, 90, and 365 days) as summarized features for prediction tasks.

Interval statistics (ST)

In addition to historical visit counts, which have been widely adopted in prior work, we introduce interval-based temporal features that explicitly characterize the timing and spacing of previous ED visits. These features are designed to capture both visit recency and visit regularity, which are not fully represented by count-based summaries alone. First, for each historical visit t < i, the inter-visit interval is defined as 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta_t^{(p)} = t_\mathrm{in}(v_{t+1}^{(p)}) - t_\mathrm{out}(v_t^{(p)}),$$\end{document}

which measures the elapsed time between consecutive ED visits.

Truncation and padding for longitudinal feature construction

Although summary features (SF) provide a simple and compact way to represent longitudinal visit histories, they inherently remove the temporal dimension and may lead to a substantial loss of information from past visits. To better preserve historical information, an alternative strategy is to construct longitudinal features (LF) by concatenating visit-level feature vectors, where each visit is encoded according to Eq. 4.

When a patient has fewer than ℓ historical visits, the representation is left-padded with NaN values to obtain a fixed-length input.

A key limitation of the LF concatenation approach is that, for patients with short visit histories, a significant fraction of the input features are padded. As a result, (i) imputation is required before model training, which may introduce additional bias, and (ii) post-hoc feature attribution methods such as SHAP become difficult to explain, since importance scores may be assigned to artificial (imputed or missing) features rather than observed clinical events.

SeqBoost algorithm

To address the limitation of the fixed-length longitudinal feature representation (LF), we propose Sequential Boosting (SeqBoost). Given a truncated sequence of length ℓ, SeqBoost sequentially builds classifiers from the most recent visit to the least recent available visit. The model is trained via gradient boosting, where each stage incorporates information from an additional historical visit and learns to correct the residual errors of the ensemble constructed from more recent visits. At the boosting stage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k \in \{1, \ldots, \ell\}$$\end{document}, a weak learner is trained using features from the k-th most recent visit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{X}_{i,k}^{(p)}$$\end{document}), while the predictions from earlier stages are treated as fixed. The boosting stages are ordered in reverse chronological order, with the first stage being \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{X}_{i,1}^{(p)} \equiv \textbf{x}_i^{(p)}$$\end{document} (i.e., the most recent visit). This sequential construction allows the model to adaptively determine the contribution of each visit based on its temporal relevance, without requiring explicit imputation for missing visits. This ensures that each decision made by the model is based solely on real patient data, leading to more reliable SHAP value explanation and clinically meaningful feature importance rankings. In addition, since sequential feature feeding may lead to overfitting, early stopping was applied. We did not specify this in the Algorithm 1, but the standard technique in boosting frameworks can be used to monitor performance on a validation set and stop training when improvement stalls or degrades.

Dataset and experiments

Dataset

We evaluated the proposed method and feature representations using the MIMIC-IV-ED benchmark dataset [9]. Data access was obtained through PhysioNet under approved data use agreements [25, 26]. The dataset contains ED visit records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Massachusetts, United States, spanning the period from 2008 to 2019.

Following the benchmark protocol, we considered ED visits in which patients were discharged (i.e., not admitted to the hospital) and for which subsequent ED utilization could be observed. After preprocessing, the dataset comprises 220,485 visit-level instances, each corresponding to a single ED visit and serving as a prediction point. Specifically, for a patient with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p$$\end{document} visits, up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p - 1$$\end{document} visits contribute labeled instances, while the final visit is used only to define the outcome and is not included as a training or test instance.

The outcome of ED revisit within 72 hours is relatively rare. Consistent with prior work, the dataset is therefore highly imbalanced, with only a small fraction (3.39%) of visit-level instances labeled as positive (y = 1). As a result, and following the benchmark design, we report performance primarily using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), which is insensitive to the classification thresholding.

Figure 4 shows that visit histories in the dataset are generally short. More than half of the visit-level instances correspond to patients with only a single prior ED visit, and the frequency of longer histories decreases rapidly as sequence length increases. Histories of more than 5 visits are uncommon (17.14%), and very long histories (e.g., exceeding 30 visits) are rare (1.77%). This empirical distribution highlights a limitation of fixed-length longitudinal feature representations: for large truncation lengths ℓ, most feature dimensions are dominated by padded NaN values. Consequently, setting ℓ to a large value is unlikely to improve generalization and may instead amplify the impact of missing-value imputation.Fig. 4Empirical distribution of visit-lengths in the dataset

Summary of feature sets and model for experiments

Experiment 1: Evaluation of summarized features The first experiment investigates the predictive utility of the proposed interval-based temporal features, described in Sect. 3.2, by comparing models that use only visit-level features (VF) with those augmented by historical visit-count features (VC), interval-based features (ST), and their combination.

Using XGBoost [27] as the base classifier, we constructed multiple feature sets based solely on information available at the current visit and evaluated the incremental contribution of adding VC, ST, and both feature groups to 72-hour ED revisit prediction. The model was trained with a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum tree depth of 3, and 100 boosting rounds. The AUROC was used as the evaluation metric, and early stopping was applied if no improvement was observed for 10 consecutive iterations on a validation set.

Experiment 2: Modeling longitudinal visit histories. The second experiment evaluates modeling strategies that incorporate information from multiple historical visits. We compared two approaches: (1) a longitudinal-feature (LF) from Sect. 3.3 with XGBoost, which is a non-sequential model, and (2) with the proposed Sequential Boosting model (SeqBoost) from Sect. 3.4. Both approaches utilize the same visit-level, visit-count, and interval-based features; the key difference lies in how longitudinal information is processed. This experiment aims to assess whether SeqBoost captures temporal structure in visit histories more effectively while mitigating the limitations of the non-sequential model. The XGBoost model uses the same hyperparameters as in the previous experiment. For SeqBoost, the learning rate is initialized at 0.1 and reduced by a factor of 10 at each subsequent stage when incorporating an additional historical visit.

In both experiments, model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), which is appropriate given the class imbalance in the data, and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), which is consistent with prior work on the same prediction task. Data were split at the patient level to ensure that all visit-level instances from a given patient appeared exclusively in either the training or testing set. We employed 5-fold cross-validation, in which patients were randomly partitioned into five folds. Each fold served as the test set once, while the remaining folds were used for training, ensuring that each patient contributed to the evaluation exactly once.

To assess feature importance and improve explainability, we employed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values [28]. SHAP provides a theoretically grounded framework for attributing model predictions to individual features. In particular, it enables the examination of how different feature groups contribute to predicted revisit risk, facilitating clinical explanation and supporting transparent model analysis.

Both visit count (VC) and interval-based temporal features (ST) improved predictive performance relative to the VF baseline. Notably, the proposed interval-based features (ST) generally outperformed visit counts (VC) for short histories (lengths 2–6), with statistically significant gains at lengths 2, 3, and 6. The largest improvement occurred for patients with a single prior visit (length 2), where AUROC increased from 0.5903 (VC) to 0.6406 (ST). This result highlights the advantage of explicitly modeling the timing of recent visits, as visit count features alone encode only coarse binary information within predefined time windows.

Combining visit counts and interval-based statistics (VCST) yielded the best overall performance across most lengths. This longitudinal feature set significantly outperformed the VC baseline for lengths 1, 2, 3, and 5, indicating that the two feature types provide complementary information. For longer histories, AUROC values exhibited greater variability across feature sets, likely due to the substantially smaller number of patients with long visit sequences.

In the LF model, several historical visit count features (n_ed_30d1, n_ed_90d1, n_ed_365d1) appear among the most influential predictors. As shown in the top-left panel of Fig. 7 (the blue-colored bars), these features exhibit higher global SHAP values than their current-visit counterparts (n_ed_30d0, n_ed_90d0, n_ed_365d0). Taken at face value, this would imply that ED visits occurring before the previous encounter exert greater influence on 72-hour revisit risk than those occurring before the current encounter, a clinically implausible explanation. The issue becomes even clearer when examining predictions for new patients (bottom-left panel). For this group, all historical-visit features should be undefined. Yet SHAP identifies n_ed_30d1, n_ed_90d1, and n_ed_365d1 as some of the most important predictors, even though these features must necessarily be missing.

This behavior arises from how XGBoost handles missing values. When features are padded with NaNs due to differing sequence lengths, XGBoost internally assigns a default branch for missing values at each tree split. SHAP, in turn, treats the presence of a missing value (NaN) as a meaningful feature pattern and assigns it importance. As a result, high SHAP values for these historical-visit features do not reflect genuine clinical relevance but instead reveal the model’s heavy reliance on missing-value patterns. This severely undermines the explainability of the LF model, particularly in datasets like MIMIC-IV-ED, where the majority of patients have few or no prior visits.

In contrast, SeqBoost avoids this explainability failure by design. At each boosting stage, SeqBoost uses only features from visits that actually exist for a given patient. No NaN padding is required, and missing visit-level inputs are never introduced into the model. Therefore, SeqBoost does not provide the model with missing-value patterns that could be spuriously explained as predictive signals. As shown in the top-right and bottom-right panels of Fig. 7, SHAP values under SeqBoost correspond entirely to observed features, with no contributions from missing values. This design ensures that SHAP explanations reflect clinically meaningful relationships rather than artifacts of missing-value handling. SeqBoost correctly identifies real predictors of 72-hour ED revisit risk—such as recent utilization frequency and physiological markers—while avoiding misleading attributions to nonexistent historical features.

Discussion

Impact of the historical features

Prior work on the MIMIC-IV-ED benchmark, most notably by Xie et al. [9], reported that summarized historical utilization features, such as prior ED visit counts within fixed look-back windows, contributed little to predicting 72-hour ED revisits. However, this conclusion contrasts with earlier ED utilization studies, which consistently identified prior ED use and recent revisit patterns as important risk factors for short-term returns [4–6]. Our results suggest that this discrepancy is largely attributable to how historical information is summarized and used.

In Sect. 5.1, the results show that simple visit-count features yield only modest improvements, consistent with Xie et al.’s observations on MIMIC-IV-ED [9]. In contrast, interval-based temporal statistics that explicitly capture visit spacing and recency provide significantly stronger predictive gains, aligning more closely with prior evidence on temporal patterns of ED use [4, 5]. The complementary nature of visit counts and interval-based features, confirmed through correlation and SHAP analyses (Fig. 6), further explains why their combination consistently outperforms either representation alone.

Furthermore, the results in Sect. 5.2 extend these findings by adding longitudinal features. Models incorporating features from previous visits significantly outperform those relying solely on current-visit and summarized features (Table 3), supporting prior work that emphasizes the predictive value of richer longitudinal utilization histories for ED revisits and related outcomes [5, 6]. Together with the benchmark findings on MIMIC-IV-ED [9], our study suggests that the key to leveraging utilization history lies less in whether prior visits are used and more in how they are temporally encoded and integrated into the prediction model.

Explainability and clinical relevance

Beyond predictive performance, explainability is essential for building clinician trust and supporting safe deployment of machine learning models in emergency care, and has been increasingly emphasized in recent work on interpretable risk prediction and clinical deterioration models in the ED and related settings [29–32]. In line with studies that use SHAP to clarify model behavior and monitor data drift in deployed ED models, our approach focuses on feature–level attributions rather than model–specific internals.

However, prior work on 72–hour ED reattendance prediction has highlighted that models built primarily on past medical histories risk embedding systematic bias against patients with limited or missing hospital records, as predictions may be driven by the absence of prior visits or diagnoses rather than by a well–described clinical state [33]. Our work highlights this problem when the longitudinal features are used in addition to the summarized features. The SHAP analyses in Fig. 7 show that the model attributes the prediction to the missing values. Although SeqBoost does not consistently outperform the longitudinal model in predictive performance, SeqBoost yields attribution patterns that are more stable across different historical lengths and more closely aligned with clinical expectations.

Importantly, incorporating historical utilization does not displace established clinical predictors. Consistent with prior ED revisit studies emphasizing age, triage urgency, and vital signs [34, 35], our SHAP analyses (Sect. 5.3) show that variables such as last recorded heart rate, triage pain score, and body temperature remain among the most influential contributors to revisit risk. This suggests that temporal utilization features complement rather than obscure clinically meaningful signals, supporting their use in interpretable ED decision support models.

Limitations

Feature encoding and SHAP attribution

Diagnoses are subject to very high cardinality in clinical practice. For example, MIMIC-IV-ED records diagnoses as ICD codes primarily optimized for billing and administrative use, resulting in thousands of distinct categories that only loosely map to clinically homogeneous groups. In line with the MIMIC-IV-ED benchmark [9], this study applies the Charlson Comorbidity Index and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index to encode ICD codes to focus our study on historical visit information. However, such coarse or frequency-based encodings may attenuate the apparent predictive value of diagnosis and complaint variables, and partially explain their relatively modest SHAP importance compared with triage measures, vital signs, and utilization features. Similar patterns have been observed in ED prediction models where triage variables (age, acuity, vital signs) consistently dominate feature importance rankings, while diagnosis-related fields contribute less once basic clinical severity is captured [36, 37].

In addition, although SeqBoost improves the stability and clinical plausibility of feature attributions by avoiding padded missing values, the resulting explanations remain descriptive rather than causal. As emphasized by previous work [33, 38], SHAP-based explanations reflect the patterns learned by the model, not necessarily true clinical risk factors. In particular, associations between specific diagnosis or complaint categories and revisit risk may reflect latent confounders (e.g., frailty, access to care, or social support) that are not explicitly modeled.

Bias and generalizability

This study is evaluated exclusively on the MIMIC-IV-ED dataset, which originates from a single tertiary academic medical center in the United States. Practice patterns, patient demographics, discharge processes, and access to outpatient care may differ substantially across institutions and healthcare systems. Although the proposed interval-based temporal features and SeqBoost can be applied to other institutions, the observed predictive performance gains from may not directly generalize to other healthcare systems with different utilization dynamics.

As highlighted by Chmiel et al. (2021) [33], models that incorporate coded medical history and utilization features may implicitly discriminate against patients with limited or missing historical records, because predictions can be driven as much by the absence of recorded information as by true clinical risk. This issue is particularly salient for new patients, patients receiving fragmented care across institutions, or those with undocumented chronic conditions. While SeqBoost mitigates a specific failure mode observed in NaN-padded longitudinal model, it does not fully resolve the broader problem of information asymmetry. Patients with richer documented histories inherently provide more predictive signal through clinical and utilization features, whereas patients with sparse or no records may appear artificially low risk despite potentially high true risk.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the contribution of historical emergency department (ED) utilization to 72-hour revisit prediction depends fundamentally on how temporal information is encoded. While prior benchmark studies suggested limited utility of historical features, our results show that interval-based temporal statistics capturing visit recency and spacing provide consistent and statistically significant improvements over simple visit counts. On the MIMIC-IV-ED benchmark, interval-based temporal features consistently outperformed simple visit-count summaries, particularly for short histories. For patients with history length of 2, replacing visit counts with interval statistics increased AUROC from 0.590 to 0.641, and combining both feature types further improved performance to 0.657. Extending beyond summarized history, we further show that longitudinal visit-level representations improve predictive performance (AUROC increased from 0.657 to 0.691 at history length 2) but can introduce misleading explanations when padding and missing values are used. To address this, we propose SeqBoost, a sequential boosting framework that incorporates only observed historical visits, achieving comparable predictive performance while yielding more stable and clinically plausible SHAP attributions. Future work may extend this framework by integrating richer clinical representations, such as advanced diagnosis encodings or unstructured clinical text, and by validating interval-based temporal and SeqBoost across multi-institutional datasets with more diverse utilization patterns.
