Clinical characteristics and vascular involvement of Takayasu arteritis versus Kawasaki disease in infants and young children
Authors: Congying Wang, Yingjie Xu, Jianming Lai, Xiaohui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood-onset Takayasu arteritis (c-TAK) in infants and young children often presents with systemic inflammation rather than ischemic symptoms, and its clinical features overlap with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)-resistant Kawasaki disease (KD). To aid in early differentiation, this study compared clinical characteristics and vascular involvement between c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD in infants and young children.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included patients under three years old diagnosed with c-TAK or IVIG-resistant KD between June, 2016, and December, 2025. Demographics, clinical presentation, laboratory findings, vascular involvement, immunotherapy, and outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsA total of 144 infants and young children were enrolled, including 43 with c-TAK and 101 with IVIG-resistant KD. Compared to the IVIG-resistant KD group, the c-TAK group had a significantly higher proportion of infants (85.7% vs. 29.7%) and females (67.4% vs. 32.7%), longer fever duration (18 [9, 26] vs. 11 [9, 13] days), higher lymphocyte counts (7.09 [5.58, 9.02] vs. 2.92 [2.18, 3.91] × 10⁹/L), and more medium-to-giant coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs; 48.8% vs. 18.8%; all P < 0.001). At the baseline, 76.7% of c-TAK patients received IVIG but experienced recurrent fever. The imaging features in the c-TAK group included vessel wall thickening (95.3%), dilation (79.1%), and stenosis (41.9%). The most commonly involved arteries were the carotid (79.1%), abdominal aorta (69.8%), subclavian (67.4%), and coronary artery (67.4%). Conversely, the IVIG-resistant KD group mainly exhibited CAAs (29.7%). The median follow-up duration was comparable between the two groups (21.0 [11.8, 45.5] vs. 18.0 [12.0, 36.0] months, P = 0.952). By the final follow-up, vascular imaging abnormalities completely resolved in 10.3% of c-TAK patients and 96.9% of IVIG-resistant KD patients.ConclusionIn young children, particularly female infants, who present with prolonged fever and a poor response to IVIG, c-TAK should be considered as an important differential diagnosis alongside IVIG-resistant KD. The distribution of vascular involvement, detailed imaging characteristics and regular follow-up may be helpful for the differential diagnosis of these two diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-026-01222-7.

Introduction

Childhood-onset Takayasu arteritis (c-TAK) and Kawasaki disease (KD) are two primary childhood vasculitides that may involve multiple systems of the body and lead to severe vascular complications. Neither disease has specific diagnostic biomarkers, and diagnoses rely on a combination of clinical symptoms, physical signs, and vascular involvement features.

c-TAK is a chronic vasculitis primarily involving the aorta and its major branches [1]. In infants and young children, c-TAK often lacks typical ischemic symptoms and is instead characterized by systemic inflammatory features [2–5]. KD is an acute, self-limiting vasculitis of early childhood, and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is the standard initial therapy [6]. Approximately 10 ~ 20% of patients with KD exhibit persistent or recurrent fever after completion of IVIG treatment [7]. Most of these cases are diagnosed as IVIG-resistant KD. However, following re-evaluation, a subset is reclassified as an alternative systemic vasculitis, such as c-TAK [4, 8–11]. Early distinction between c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD remains considerably challenging.

Several case reports have revealed that misclassification between c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD occurs predominantly in children under three years of age, resulting in delayed diagnosis, inadequate immunotherapy, oversight of systemic arterial lesions, and a consequent increase in the risk of life-threatening vascular complications [8–11]. A small-scale study involving 7 young children with c-TAK and 12 with KD suggested that systemic arterial involvement may help distinguish c-TAK from KD [5]. However, all KD patients included in that study were IVIG-responsive, with no IVIG-resistant cases enrolled. Currently, a systematic comparative analysis of the clinical features, vascular involvement patterns, immunotherapy regimens, and prognosis between c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD in infants and young children is still lacking.

To address this gap, we compared children younger than 3 years of age with c-TAK and those with IVIG-resistant KD, aiming to identify clues that may facilitate the early differentiation of these two clinically overlapping vasculitides.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Capital Center for Children’s Health from June 1, 2016, to December 31, 2025. Eligible patients were those initially hospitalized in the Department of Cardiovascular and Rheumatology between June 1, 2016, and June 1, 2025, with a discharge diagnosis of c-TAK or IVIG-resistant KD.

Inclusion criteria required patients to be under 3 years of age and to meet one of the following clinical definitions: 1) For a diagnosis of c-TAK, patients were required to fulfill the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) /Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organization (PRINTO) /Pediatric Rheumatology European Society (PRES) classification criteria for c-TAK [12]; 2) For a diagnosis of IVIG-resistant KD, patients were required to fulfill the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for KD [6], receive standard IVIG therapy (2 g/kg), and subsequently meet the criteria for IVIG-resistance. IVIG-resistance was defined as persistent or recrudescent fever ≥ 36 h after the completion of the initial IVIG infusion [6]. Patients were excluded if they had malignant hematological diseases or immunodeficiency. After initial screening, two independent experts in pediatric rheumatology and cardiology reviewed the diagnoses of all enrolled patients, confirming that the classification of all included patients was accurate.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital Center for Children’s Health (SHERLL2025024). Informed consent was waived as all patients’ data were anonymized.

Baseline data collection

The initial hospitalization of the enrolled patients was defined as baseline. Baseline demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, vascular imaging findings, and treatment regimens were systematically extracted from electronic medical records. Patients with KD were further classified into complete KD and incomplete KD based on their clinical manifestation. Complete KD was defined as fever lasting at least five days, accompanied by at least four of the five principal clinical features: bilateral conjunctival injection, oral mucosal changes, rash, changes in the extremities, and cervical lymphadenopathy. Incomplete KD was defined as fever lasting at least five days with only two or three principal clinical features, and in the absence of an alternative diagnosis [6]. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, platelet count, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and hemoglobin concentration. Since the above laboratory parameters could be measured multiple times during the first hospitalization, we selected the peak values for white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, platelet count, CRP, and ESR, along with the lowest value for hemoglobin concentration, prior to the initiation of any immunotherapy, for further analyses. Immunotherapy was defined as the administration of IVIG, glucocorticoids, biological agents, or conventional immunosuppressants.

Baseline vascular evaluation

At the initial hospitalization, all patients underwent preliminary screening for potential vascular involvement by their attending physicians, based on clinical guidelines [1, 6, 13] and clinical judgment. Vascular involvement included wall thickening, stenosis, dilation, and aneurysm. Structural damage was defined as the presence of vascular stenosis, dilation, or aneurysm.

Considering that coronary artery involvement may occur in both c-TAK and KD, all patients were evaluated for coronary artery involvement via echocardiography. A coronary artery Z‑score ≥ 2.5 was used to diagnose coronary artery aneurysm (CAA). CAA was further classified as small (2.5 ≤ Z < 5), medium (5 ≤ Z < 10), or giant (Z ≥ 10) [6].

c-TAK primarily affects the aorta and its major branches. Accordingly, patients with c-TAK underwent systemic arterial evaluation using computed tomography angiography (CTA) or vascular ultrasound. CTA was employed to evaluate the involvement of central arteries, including the ascending aorta, aortic arch, thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery, renal artery, and pulmonary artery. Vascular ultrasound was utilized to assess the involvement of extracranial and peripheral arteries, including the common carotid artery, brachiocephalic trunk, subclavian artery, axillary artery, brachial artery, iliac artery, and femoral artery.

In addition to the coronary arteries, KD may affect the axillary, subclavian, brachial, iliac, and femoral arteries [14]. The overall prevalence of the non-coronary systemic arterial aneurysms (SAAs) in patients with KD is estimated to be less than 2% [15]. Currently, SAAs screening is not included in standard KD management protocols. At our center, only KD patients with medium-to-giant CAAs, who are at elevated risk for developing SAAs [14], underwent comprehensive multi-site arterial imaging. The imaging protocol applied to this subgroup of KD patients was the same as that established for c-TAK.

Follow-up data collection

Following the initial hospitalization, patients were regularly followed up through outpatient visits or subsequent hospitalizations. Patients with c-TAK underwent serial echocardiography at 1, 2, 3, and 6 months, and annually thereafter to monitor coronary artery involvement, and received systemic arterial assessment every 6 ~ 12 months. Patients with IVIG-resistant KD underwent echocardiography at the same interval as those with c-TAK. The method for systemic arterial assessment remained consistent with that used at baseline. For all patients with c-TAK, as well as IVIG-resistant KD patients who developed medium-to-large CAAs, pediatric rheumatologists or cardiologists could arrange additional follow-up and vascular involvement assessment based on the individual clinical course.

Data on immunotherapy regimens during the follow-up period were collected, along with vascular involvement assessed at 6 months post-discharge, at 12 months post-discharge, and at the final follow-up. Clinical outcomes included all-cause mortality, clinical remission and the regression of vascular involvements. Clinical remission in c-TAK was defined as the absence of disease-related clinical manifestations and signs, normalization of inflammatory markers, and no imaging evidence of progression in vascular stenosis or dilation [16]. Regression of vascular involvement was defined as the complete resolution of abnormal vascular findings, including vascular wall thickening, luminal stenosis, or luminal dilation. The final date for follow-up data inclusion was December 31, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Both c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD are rare pediatric conditions in infants and young children, with estimated incidences of approximately 0.04 and 6 ~ 10 per 100,000, respectively [17–19]. Prior studies on infants and young children with c-TAK reported small sample sizes ranging from 7 to 43 cases [2–5], underscoring the difficulty of conducting adequately powered prospective studies. Therefore, we conducted a 10-year retrospective review of institutional medical records, maximizing case accrual over an extended period. This approach yielded 101 cases of IVIG-resistant KD and 43 cases of c-TAK. Although the sample sizes remained limited, this represented the largest single-center cohort to date, permitting exploratory analyses.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (P25, P75). Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). Differences between the c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD groups were compared using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. To address the potential confounding effects of age on the comparison of laboratory parameters and the incidence of CAA between the two diseases, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age (infants vs. toddlers). A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to compare the duration of CAA regression between the c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD groups. The analysis was adjusted for potential confounding factors, including age, sex, and initial aneurysm size. The event of interest was defined as normalization of CAA diameter. Time zero was defined as the date of initial hospitalization. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.0).

Results

Comparisons of baseline demographics and symptoms

During the study period, a total of 144 patients younger than 3 years were enrolled, including 43 patients diagnosed with c-TAK and 101 patients diagnosed with IVIG-resistant KD. Patients in the c-TAK group were younger (0.22 [0.16, 0.48] vs. 1.42 [0.81, 2.25] years, P < 0.001), with the majority being infants (85.7%, 36/43). In contrast, the IVIG-resistant KD group consisted mostly of toddlers (71.3%, 72/101; Fig. 1). A female predominance was observed in the c-TAK group (67.4%, 29/43), whereas the IVIG-resistant KD group exhibited a male predominance (67.3%, 68/101; Table 1).

Fever was the most common symptom of c-TAK (86%, 37/43). Patients with c-TAK exhibited a significantly longer duration of fever compared to those with IVIG-resistant KD (18 [9, 26] vs. 11 [9, 13] days; P < 0.001). Furthermore, c-TAK patients presented with vascular signs that were entirely absent in the IVIG-resistant KD group, including hypertension (30.2%, 13/43), blood pressure discrepancy (16.3%, 7/43), decreased or absent pulse (9.3%, 4/43), and vascular bruits (4.7%, 2/43). Although 44.2% (19/43) of c-TAK patients exhibited rash, other characteristic KD manifestations were less frequent in this group, including cervical lymphadenopathy (18.6%, 8/43), bilateral conjunctival injection (9.3%, 4/43), oral mucosal changes (4.7%, 2/43), and changes in the extremities (4.7%, 2/43).

34.9% (15/43) of patients with c-TAK were initially diagnosed and managed as incomplete KD. However, these patients continued to experience recurrent fever and persistently elevated CRP levels following IVIG therapy. Given the atypical clinical presentation and poor response to IVIG, a more detailed physical examination and comprehensive vascular imaging assessment were undertaken. These evaluations revealed hypertension in 33.3% (5/15) of cases, asymmetric limb blood pressure in 13.3% (2/15) of cases, and multi-site arterial involvement in all 15 patients. Accordingly, the diagnosis was revised to c-TAK. Of note, none of the 43 patients with c-TAK met the diagnostic criteria for complete KD, whereas all patients in the IVIG-resistant KD group exhibited the complete phenotype.

Comparisons of laboratory values

In both the c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD groups, CRP and ESR levels exceeded the normal reference range (CRP < 8 mg/L; ESR 0 ~ 15 mm/h), with no significant differences observed between the two groups (both P > 0.05). Compared to the IVIG-resistant KD group, patients with c-TAK had higher lymphocyte counts (7.09 [5.58, 9.02] vs. 2.92 [2.18, 3.91] × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001) and platelet counts (677.00 [506.00, 880.00] vs. 340.00 [271.00, 426.00] × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001). Patients with c-TAK also had lower neutrophil counts (9.00 [5.38, 13.0] vs. 12.00 [9.54, 14.70] × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001) and hemoglobin levels (80.00 [73.50, 97.00] vs. 107.00 [99.00, 112.00] g/L, P < 0.001; Table 1). Given the higher proportion of infants in the c-TAK group, subgroup analyses were performed separately within infants (< 1 year) and toddlers (1 ~ 3 years), as described in the Methods. In both age subgroups, lymphocyte counts remained significantly higher in patients with c-TAK than those with IVIG-resistant KD (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1).

Comparisons of baseline vascular involvement

Vascular involvement features at baseline differed between the two groups (Table 2). In the c-TAK group, vascular lesions demonstrated heterogeneous imaging features, including vessel wall thickening (95.3%, 41/43), dilation (79.1%, 34/43), and stenosis (41.9%, 18/43). Arterial involvement was widespread, with the most frequently affected vessels being the carotid artery (79.1%, 34/43), abdominal aorta (69.8%, 30/43), subclavian artery (67.4%, 29/43), and coronary artery (67.4%, 29/43; Table 2).

Conversely, patients with IVIG-resistant KD mainly exhibited CAA (29.7%, 30/101). 19 patients with IVIG-resistant KD underwent comprehensive multi-site arterial imaging due to the presence of medium-to-large CAA at disease onset. In these patients, no SAAs were observed.

The c-TAK group demonstrated significantly higher rates of overall CAA (67.4% [29/43] vs. 29.7% [30/101], P < 0.001) and medium-to-giant CAA (48.8% [21/43] vs. 18.8% [19/101], P < 0.001) compared to the IVIG-resistant KD group. Taking into account that younger age is a risk factor for CAA development [20], we performed an age-stratified subgroup analysis (Supplementary Table 2). Among infants, although the overall incidence of CAA did not differ between the two groups (63.9% [23/36] vs. 40% [12/30], P = 0.091), the proportion of medium-to-giant CAA was numerically higher in the c-TAK group, showing a trend toward statistical significance (41.7% [15/36] vs. 16.7% [5/30], P = 0.053). In toddlers, c-TAK patients had higher rates of both overall CAA (85.7% [6/7] vs. 25.4% [18/71], P = 0.004) and medium-to-giant CAA (57.1% [4/7] vs. 8.45% [6/71], P = 0.003) compared to those with IVIG-resistant KD.

Comparisons of immunotherapy

During initial hospitalization, 76.7% (33/43) of patients with c-TAK received IVIG but had persistent or recurrent fever. Glucocorticoid therapy was administered to 44.2% (19/43) of c-TAK patients and 86.1% (87/101) of IVIG‑resistant KD patients (Table 1). Patients with c-TAK had a longer duration of glucocorticoid therapy (330.0 [134.0, 516.0] vs. 26.0 [20.2, 31.0] days, P < 0.001) than that in the IVIG-resistant KD group (Table 3).

90.7% (39/43) of c-TAK patients received biological agents, including infliximab (37.2%, 16/43) and tocilizumab (53.5%, 23/43). 10.9% (11/101) of IVIG-resistant KD patients received infliximab (Table 1). All patients with c-TAK who initiated biological agents at baseline continued to use them regularly during the follow-up period, with a median duration of biological agents of 450 (300, 780) days (Table 3). In contrast, none of the patients with IVIG-resistant KD who used biological agents at baseline continued such treatment during follow-up. In addition, 7.0% (3/39) of patients with c-TAK received conventional immunosuppressants during the follow-up period (Table 3).

Comparisons of follow-up

Following initial hospitalization, 4 patients with c-TAK and 5 with IVIG-resistant KD were lost to follow-up. The median follow-up duration was comparable between the c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD groups (21.0 [11.8, 45.5] vs. 18.0 [12.0, 36.0] months, P = 0.952; Table 3). One infant with IVIG-resistant KD complicated by giant CAA died suddenly one month after discharge. No patients with c-TAK died.

By the final follow-up, all patients with c-TAK achieved clinical remission. 10.3% (4/39) of the c-TAK group, and 96.9% (93/96) of the IVIG-resistant KD group achieved complete normalization of vascular imaging findings. Since CAA was the common vascular involvement in both groups, we compared their regression duration. After adjusting for age, sex, and initial CAA size, the time to CAA regression did not differ significantly between patients with c-TAK and those with IVIG-resistant KD (HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.31–1.57, P = 0.391).

Discussion

c-TAK presents with heterogeneous and atypical clinical manifestations, which may contribute to misdiagnosis during the early stages of the disease. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically address the differential diagnosis between two forms of childhood systemic vasculitis: c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD. Our finding indicated that although c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD in infants and young children shared features such as prolonged fever, mucocutaneous manifestations, elevated inflammatory markers and CAA, each conditions retained distinct clinical and vascular characteristics. Detailed physical examination, vascular imaging, and regular follow-up may facilitate differentiation between these two conditions (Fig. 2).

This study focused on children younger than 3 years of age, based on the epidemiological data, clinical observations, and evidence from previous studies. First, although c-TAK may occur across all pediatric age groups, IVIG-resistant KD is predominantly observed in infants and young children. Wang et al. reported a median age of 1.83 (0.92, 3.33) years among patients with IVIG-resistant KD [21], and Egami et al. revealed a median age of 2.08 (0.83, 3.00) years [22]. Our previous study similarly found a median age of 1.75 (1.00, 3.27) years among our IVIG-resistant KD cohort [23]. Children younger than 3 years represent the primary age range where c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD overlap in age distribution. Second, in clinical practice, we observed that c-TAK demonstrates age-specific clinical phenotypes: infant and young c-TAK patients primarily present with systemic inflammation, whereas older patients more commonly exhibit dizziness, headache, and fatigue, with fever being relatively uncommon. c-TAK cases with clinical features resembling IVIG-resistant KD are predominantly observed in children under 3 years of age. Third, previous studies have also identified this age group as the period with the highest potential for diagnostic confusion between these two diseases [4, 5]. Based on these considerations, we restricted our comparative analysis of c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD to patients younger than 3 years.

Consistent with previous studies [24–26], our analysis confirmed a female predominance in the c-TAK group, contrasting with a male predominance in the IVIG-resistant KD group. Our findings also revealed that patients with c-TAK demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of infants and a marked shift toward younger age groups compared to the IVIG-resistant KD group, whose age distribution remained concentrated in toddlers. These distinct demographic features indicated that c-TAK should be considered in the differential diagnosis when infants, particularly female infants, present with intermittent or recurrent fever following IVIG treatment.

Hypertension is an important indicator for identifying c-TAK. In this study, hypertension was the most common vascular sign in the c-TAK group, with an incidence of 30.2%; however, this rate is substantially lower than that reported in previous studies [27, 28]. Blood pressure measurement in infants and young children is highly dependent on both appropriate cuff size and operator technique [29, 30]. Moreover, no standardized reference values for normal blood pressure have been established for this age group [30], making the accurate detection of borderline or stage 1 hypertension particularly challenging. In the present study, as well as in studies by Zhao and Jin [3, 4], approximately one-half to two-thirds of infants and young children with c-TAK did not exhibit hypertension during the early stage of the disease. These findings underscore that normal blood pressure cannot be used to exclude c-TAK. The diagnosis of c-TAK requires a comprehensive evaluation of clinical features, treatment response, and vascular imaging.

Aligning with previous reports [31–33], we found that c-TAK, though classified as a large-vessel vasculitis, can involve medium-sized arteries such as the femoral, axillary, brachial, superior mesenteric, and coronary arteries. In this study, we observed that 69.7% of the c-TAK patients had CAA, and 48.8% had medium-to-giant CAA. The proportion of CAA in our study was higher than the incidence described in the overall c-TAK population [27, 28, 31, 34]. There are two possible reasons for the differences in CAA prevalence. Firstly, infant and young c-TAK patients with CAA were more likely to be admitted to the hospital for suspecting of KD, prompting comprehensive physical and cardiac evaluations. Those with medium-to-giant CAA, and poor response to IVIG subsequently underwent systemic arterial assessment, which facilitated a timely diagnosis of c-TAK. Conversely, patients without CAA may be misdiagnosed as having an infection in the early stage of diseases, and were not included in this study. As a result, the proportions of CAA reported here may be higher than the true prevalence in the real world. Secondly, our findings aligned with Zhao et al.‘s study of infant c-TAK patients [3]. While the rate of CAA in young c-TAK patients (40%) reported by Jin et al. [4] was lower than that in our cohort, it remained elevated compared to the general c-TAK population. Collectively, these observations suggest that younger age may be a risk factor for coronary artery involvement in c-TAK, a hypothesis that needs further investigation.

KD, a medium-vessel vasculitis mainly affecting the coronary artery, can also present with systemic arterial lesions [14, 35, 36]. Systemic arterial lesions occur more frequently in young children, particularly infants with KD. The vascular involvement sites in infants and young children with c-TAK and KD were not completely separate, complicating their diagnoses. In this study, we found that the imaging features of vascular lesions played a crucial role in early differentiation. In infants and young children, patients with c-TAK exhibited heterogeneous vascular abnormalities, including vascular wall thickening, stenosis, and dilation. In contrast, vascular lesions in IVIG-resistant KD patients primarily presented as aneurysms. Therefore, distinguishing between the two diseases should rely not only on the distribution of vascular involvement but also on detailed imaging characteristics.

c-TAK is a chronic granulomatous arteritis [12], whereas KD is an acute, self-limiting necrotizing arteritis [6]. Aligning with the distinct pathological nature of the two diseases, our study revealed that patients with c-TAK exhibited a significantly longer fever duration. Zhao et al. reported that approximately 80% of the systemic arterial aneurysms in KD resolved within 6 months of disease onset [14]. In comparison, only 10.3% (4/39) of the c-TAK patients in this study achieved complete normalization of vascular imaging findings after a median follow-up of 21 months, indicating a more persistent vascular involvement. Taken together, regular follow-up for infants and young children with c-TAK not only aids in monitoring disease progression but also provides important evidence for differentiating between these two diseases.

Glucocorticoids are the mainstay therapy for induction of remission in c-TAK. In this study, only 44.2% of patients with c-TAK received glucocorticoid treatment, a finding that reflected the treatment preference of our clinical center. Previous studies revealed that biologic agents such as infliximab or tocilizumab can serve as a promising alternative in c-TAK patients, particularly in patients with high disease activity in the early stage of diseases [37, 38]. Infancy and early childhood constitute a critical period for growth and development. Prolonged glucocorticoid exposure can adversely affect bone health and metabolism. Therefore, pediatricians at our center prefer to use biologics rather than glucocorticoids for remission induction. However, as this is a retrospective study, baseline characteristic, including age, sex, and extent of vascular involvement, differed among patients receiving alternative treatment regimens. The efficacy and safety of this center-specific treatment strategy require further validation in prospective studies.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, with all participants younger than 3 years of age. The generalizability of these findings to other ethnic populations, medical centers, or older age groups should be interpreted with caution. Second, there were obvious differences in the age and sex distribution between the c-TAK group and the IVIG-resistant KD group. Although age-stratified subgroup analyses were performed when comparing laboratory indicators and CAA incidence, these analyses cannot fully substitute for a rigorous age- and sex-matched study design. Due to the limited sample size, inherent to the rarity of c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD, matching by age and sex was not feasible in the present study. Multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these differences and provide more robust evidence. Finally, multi-site arterial imaging was performed selectively in KD patients presenting with medium-to-large CAAs or evidence of progressive coronary artery lesions—namely, those at elevated risk for developing non-coronary systemic arterial aneurysms. This targeted screening strategy may have led to an underestimation of the true burden of systemic arterial involvement within patients with IVIG-resistant KD.

Conclusion

This study provided a preliminary exploration of the differential diagnosis between c-TAK and IVIG-resistant KD in infants and young children. Beyond the distribution of vascular involvement, detailed imaging characteristics and findings from regular follow-up may provide additional diagnostic clues.
