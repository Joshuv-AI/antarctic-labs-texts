Curvilinear coordinates and curvature in radiative transport (Submitted for review to Journal of computational and theoretical transport on 8/28/25)
Johannes Krotz a and Ryan G. McClarren a

Abstract
We derive a general expression for the streaming term in radiative transport equations and other transport problems when formulated in curvilinear coordinates, emphasizing coordinate systems adapted to the geometry of the domain and the directional dependence of particle transport. By parametrizing the angular variable using a local orthonormal frame, we express directional derivatives in terms of curvature-related quantities that reflect the geometry of underlying spatial manifolds. Our formulation highlights how the interaction between coordinate choices and curvature influences the streaming operator, offering geometric interpretations of its components. The resulting framework offers intuitive insight into when and how angular dependence can be simplified and may guide the selection of coordinate systems that balance analytical tractability and computational efficiency.

Introduction

Equations of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\bm{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\Psi+\mathcal{G}[\Psi]=Q,

(1)

arise in a wide variety of physical and engineering contexts involving the directional transport of particles, energy or other agents. Here,
\Psi=\Psi(\mathbf{r},\bm{\Omega},t)
denotes a density or distribution function depending on spatial position
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, direction of movement or momentum
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and time
t
. The streaming term
\bm{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\Psi
captures advective transport along characteristics determined by
\bm{\Omega}
, while the operator
\mathcal{G}[\Psi]
accounts for local or nonlocal interactions such as absorption, scattering, collisions, or relaxation effects. The source term
Q
models external or internal production mechanisms.

Equation (
1
) and closely related formulations find application in a wide range of domains. Examples range from rarefied gas dynamics where it appears as the Boltzmann equation describing non-equilibrium molecular flows
Cercignani 1988
, to plasma physics, where the Vlasov equation models the evolution of charged particle distributions under electromagnetic fields
Nicholson 1983
. The phonon Boltzmann equation describes microscale heat transport in solids
Majumdar 1993
, and similar forms govern the propagation of cosmic rays in astrophysics
Schlickeiser 2002
. Radiative transfer models photon transport in stellar and atmospheric environments
Chandrasekhar 1960
, as well as in optical tomography
Arridge 1999
. While in the context of biological and social systems, kinetic equations with directional transport can be used to describe traffic flow, swarming, and crowd dynamics
Bellomo et al. 2013
.

Despite their disparate origins, these systems all share a common mathematical core structured around the advective transport term
\bm{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\Psi
. This term fundamentally couples direction and space, giving rise to anisotropy, long-range effects, and complex interactions that distinguish kinetic equations from classical PDEs in purely spatial domains.

As with many problems in physics and engineering, it is common practice to address this problem using coordinates tailored to the geometry of the domain. For instance, spherical coordinates for scenarios where most parameters exhibit rotational symmetry. Notably, equation (
1
) offers additional degrees of freedom in choosing coordinates for
{\bm{\Omega}}
, beyond those for
{\bm{r}}
. However, this introduces added complexity: the optimal representation of
{\bm{r}}
and
{\bm{\Omega}}
may depend on each other.

For
{\bm{\Omega}}
, a vector on the unit sphere, a common parametrization is the standard spherical coordinates (polar and azimuthal angles) or equivalently:

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}(\omega,\mu)=\mu{\bm{n}}+\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos(\omega){\bm{t}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\sin(\omega){\bm{b}},

(2)

where
\omega\in[0,2\pi]
,
\mu\in[-1,1]
, and
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
form an orthogonal basis. While this formulation may appear convoluted, it simply means that
{\bm{\Omega}}
is parametrized by its projection
\mu
onto a designated vector
{\bm{n}}
and a polar angle
\omega
measured in a plane orthogonal to
{\bm{n}}
from a reference vector
{\bm{t}}
in that plane. A visualization is provided in Figure
1
.

:

Given a parametrization, i.e., a set of coordinates for
{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, say
{\bm{r}}={\bm{r}}(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
, a natural choice for
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}
, and
{\bm{b}}
would be the canonical tangent vectors
{\bm{n}}=c_{1}\frac{\partial{\bm{r}}}{\partial{u^{1}}}
,
{\bm{t}}=c_{2}\frac{\partial{\bm{r}}}{\partial u^{2}}
, and
{\bm{b}}={\bm{n}}\times{\bm{t}}
, where the coefficients
c_{1},c_{2}
ensure normalization. Note that this canonical choice is not necessarily orthogonal; if it is not, additional modifications are required. Orthogonalization via Gram-Schmidt is one possible approach. Other choices in terms of contra and covariant coordinates of the canonical tangent vectors, guaranteeing orthogonality, are described in
Freimanis 2011
.
Moving forward, we will not rely on any specific choice of
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}
, and
{\bm{b}}
, but will assume they form an integrable orthonormal vector field.

The parametrization of
{\bm{\Omega}}
presented in (
2
) simplifies integrals over
{\bm{\Omega}}
, which frequently appear in scattering operators
\mathcal{S}
, as:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\cdot)\,d{\bm{\Omega}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\cdot)\,d\omega\,d\mu.

(3)

A more significant advantage, however, is that
{\bm{n}}
can often be chosen as the normal vector to level sets of
\Psi
, leading to cases where
\Psi
is effectively independent of
\omega
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Psi({\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}},E,t)=\Psi({\bm{r}},(\omega,\mu),E,t)=\Psi({\bm{r}},\mu,E,t).

(4)

This reduction in the dimensionality of the parameter space can greatly simplify solving equations (
1
). However, achieving this simplification with constant
{\bm{n}}
is generally only possible in trivial cases. In most scenarios,
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}
, and
{\bm{b}}
must depend on
{\bm{r}}
, making the computation of the term
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi
particularly cumbersome.

In the literature, the exact form of this term for a given choice of coordinates for
{\bm{r}}
and
{\bm{\Omega}}
is often derived through tedious geometric arguments. These derivations can be challenging to follow and even harder to adapt to slightly different geometries. Examples of such derivations can be seen in
Bell and Glasstone 1970
;
Larsen 2007

In the next section, we derive general expressions for
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi
for arbitrary choices of
{\bm{r}}
and
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}
in a purely analytical manner.
We denote by
\nabla^{{\bm{r}}}
the partial derivatives in space and identify

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi={\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla^{{\bm{r}}}\Psi+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mu}{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\mu+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\omega}{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\omega,

(5)

presenting alternative formulations for the terms
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\mu
and
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\omega
in section
2

We relate these expressions to the geometric properties of curves and surfaces embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which we believe will make the process of deriving
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi
more straightforward, in some cases, and provides additional intuition and guidance in general.
Namely we will rewrite the terms
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\mu
and
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\omega
as terms looking as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\mu=(1-\mu^{2})\framebox{
\begin{tabular}[]{c}\text{The curvature of}\\
\text{some manifold} $X$\end{tabular}}+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\framebox{ \begin{tabular}[]{c}\text{The curvature of}\\

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\omega=\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\framebox{
\begin{tabular}[]{c}\text{The curvature of}\\
\text{some curves in} $X$\end{tabular}}+\mu\framebox{
\begin{tabular}[]{c}\text{Non-curvature term}\\
\text{quantifying spinning of ${\bm{t}}$ around ${\bm{n}}$}\end{tabular}}.

In
Freimanis 2011

5

{\bm{r}}
, but restricted to two choices of
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}
relative to the chosen coordinates, and with focus on the coordinate representation alone. We pose severely fewer restrictions on
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}
and focus on interpretations regarding the embeddings into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
rather than purely on the coordinate level.
In section
3
, we provide examples illustrating this approach in some classical settings and some less common scenarios.

2
Derivation of
{\bf\Omega}\cdot\nabla\Psi

In this section, we set out to calculate an expression
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi
when
{\bm{\Omega}}
is parametrized as

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}({\bm{r}})=\mu{\bm{n}}+\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos(\omega){\bm{t}}+\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\sin(\omega){\bm{b}},

(14)

where
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and
{\bm{b}}({\bm{r}}):={\bm{n}}\times{\bm{t}}
are orthonormal, differentiable vector fields and thus
\Psi({\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}},E,t)=\Psi({\bm{r}},(\omega({\bm{r}}),\mu({\bm{r}})),E,t)
.
Given coordinates for
{\bm{r}}
it is not always the simplest choice to guarantee all these conditions. Seemingly obvious choices might have neither normality nor orthogonality. We will stick to these assumptions regardless and discuss possible fixes, if they do not hold in some of the examples in section
3
.

We will begin by establishing some notation and simple properties of these vector fields, as well as
\omega
and
\mu
, that will be helpful in later calculations. We will then proceed to calculate
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\Psi
, providing several alternative forms for most terms involved, allowing practitioners to choose the one best suited to their needs.
Throughout the bold letters will be used for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Further, given a vector
{\bm{h}}_{1},{\bm{h}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we denote by
{\bm{h}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{h}}_{2}
their standard inner product, by
||{\bm{h}}_{1}||=\sqrt{{\bm{h}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{h}}_{1}}
the euclidean norm and their normalization as
\hat{{\bm{h}}_{1}}:=\frac{{\bm{h}}_{1}}{||{\bm{h}}_{1}||}
.We will denote the directional derivative in direction
{\bm{h}}_{1}
as
\nabla_{{\bm{h}}_{1}}:={\bm{h}}_{1}\cdot\nabla
.
Lastly, let us define the projection of
{\bm{\Omega}}({\bm{r}})
onto the orthogonal complement of
{\bm{n}}
, i.e., onto the plane spanned by
{\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{b}}
, as
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}({\bm{r}})
. Specifically,

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}({\bm{r}}):=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\cos(\omega){\bm{t}}+\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\sin(\omega){\bm{b}}={\bm{\Omega}}({\bm{r}})-\mu{\bm{n}}.

(15)

We will analyze equation (
5
) piece by piece and start with the term
{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla\mu=\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\mu
in section
2.1
.

Between
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\mu
and
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\omega
the prior is arguably more important, since terms stemming from the second one can often be ignored if
{\bm{n}}
is chosen orthogonal to level sets of
\Psi
, which under mild conditions leads to
\partial\Psi/\partial\omega=0.
(Compare
A.1
)
For cases where
\partial\Psi/\partial\omega\neq 0
we will derive an expression for
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\omega
in section
2.2
. Most terms in the streaming will be related to curvatures of either curves or surfaces, or both, defined by the vectors
{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{b}}
, which lends itself to a more intuitive approach to choosing coordinates.

2.1
Derivation of
{\bf\Omega}\cdot\nabla\mu

In this section we set out to further evaluate and interpret the term
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\mu
in equation (
5
).

Since
\mu={\bm{\Omega}}\cdot{\bm{n}}
we first find

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\mu={\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}{\bm{n}}={\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot(\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}+\mu\nabla_{{\bm{n}}}{\bm{n}})+\mu{\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}{\bm{n}}

(16)

using the linearity of the derivative and the inner product. Note that the last term
\mu{\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}{\bm{n}}=0,
because
{\bm{n}}
is assumed to be normalized.
It also is a consequence of a more general property of orthonormal frames detailed in the following remark.

Remark 1
.

Properties of orthonormal frames

Let
{\bm{h}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and let
{\bm{u}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
{\bm{v}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be distinct, differentiable orthonormal vector fields, i.e.

{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{u}}={\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{v}}=1
and
{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bm{v}}=0
everywhere. Since these inner products are constant in space their derivatives disappear and therefore

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{u}}=0

(17)

and

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{v}}=-{\bm{v}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{h}}}{\bm{u}}.

(18)

Note that this holds for
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\{{\bm{n}},{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}\}.

Using Remark
1
we are left to simplify

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\mu={\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}+\mu({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{n}}}{\bm{n}})

(19)

The second term can be expressed in terms of the normal curvature of an integral curve.

Remark 2
.

Integral Curves and Normal Curvature

Given a differentiable vector field
{\bm{u}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
let
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}({\bm{r}},\tau)
be the integral curve of
{\bm{u}}
passing through
{\bm{r}}
, i.e. let it solve the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(\tau,{\bm{r}})}{d\tau}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}(\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(\tau,{\bm{r}}))

\displaystyle\quad\tau\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(0,{\bm{r}})

\displaystyle={\bm{r}}

(20)

Existence of
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}
is guaranteed by Picard-Lindelöff, but since we will only use this curve locally we simply note that we can solve this differential equation in a neighborhood of
\bm{\gamma}(0,{\bm{r}})
. We shall further, sloppily, refer to
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(0,{\bm{r}})
as
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}({\bm{r}})
. Since
{\bm{u}}
is a unit vector everywhere
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}
is parametrized by arc length and has a normal curvature vector

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}^{{\bm{u}}}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{d^{2}\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(\tau,{\bm{r}})}{d\tau^{2}}\bigg|_{\tau=0}=-\frac{d{\bm{u}}(\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}(\tau,{\bm{r}}))}{d\tau}\bigg|_{\tau=0}=-({\bm{u}}({\bm{r}})\cdot\nabla){\bm{u}}({\bm{r}})=-\nabla_{{\bm{u}}}{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}).

(21)

(The sign convention here is chosen such that were
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{u}}}
a curve on a sphere with outward normal
\bm{N}
, the value of
\bm{\kappa}^{{\bm{u}}}\cdot\bm{N}
would be positive.)

Setting
{\bm{u}}={\bm{n}}
in the previous remark, we find that the second term in equation (
19
) is

\displaystyle\mu({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{n}}}{\bm{n}})

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}(\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{n}}}{\bm{n}})=\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\left(\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}\right)

(22)

expressing the term entirely in terms of
\mu
and the curvature of the integral curve of
{\bm{n}}
in the direction of
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}
.
This term quantifies how much
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{n}}
curves in direction
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}
. It disappears, if
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{n}}
is a straight line. If we imagine a set of surfaces orthogonal to
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{n}}
along the curve, this is simultaneously a measure for how parallel such surfaces are along the curve.

As an example, if
{\bm{n}}({\bm{r}})
is chosen to be the normalized gradient of
f({\bm{r}})=||{\bm{r}}||^{2}
, the term
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}=0
everywhere, since the level sets of
f
are spheres centered at the origin and all these spheres are, locally parallel along
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{n}}}
. On the other hand, if
{\bm{n}}
is chosen to be the normalized gradient of
f({\bm{r}})={\bm{r}}^{\bot}{\bf A}{\bm{r}}
, where
{\bf A}
is some positive definite matrix with at least two distinct eigenvalues, then
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}
will not vanish. In the latter case, the level sets of
f
are ellipses, excluding spheres, which are not parallel along
\bm{\gamma}^{{\bm{n}}}
. This is illustrated in figure
2
. We will see these two specific examples in section

.

Having established the notion of curvature of integral curves we shall first express the remaining term in equation (

) in terms of integral curves as well. Recall the definition of
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}
and therefor note that its normalization is given by
\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{\parallel}=\cos(\omega){\bm{t}}+\sin(\omega){\bm{b}}
.
Thus using the linearity of the derivative and the inner product again we can now also tackle the first term in equation (

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}=(1-\mu^{2})\left(\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{\hat{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu^{2})\left[\cos(\omega)^{2}({\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{n}})+\sin(\omega)^{2}({\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}})+\sin(\omega)\cos(\omega)\left({\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}}+{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{n}}\right)\right]

(23)

Using Remark
1
the last set of derivatives can be rewritten as
{\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}}+{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{n}}={\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}}-{\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}}=0,
causing the last summand to disappear. Further using Remark
1
again we can replace
{\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{n}}=-{\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{n}}=-{\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{b}}
and get

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=-(1-\mu^{2})\left[\cos(\omega)^{2}({\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{t}})+\sin(\omega)^{2}({\bm{n}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{b}})\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\mu^{2})\left[\cos(\omega)^{2}{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}+\sin(\omega)^{2}{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}\right].

(24)

In the last step Remark
2
was used to identify
\nabla_{{\bm{t}}}{\bm{t}}={\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}
and
\nabla_{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{b}}={\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}
as the curvatures of the corresponding integral curves.

While the interpretation in terms of the curvature of integral curves already offers some intuition, we would like to note that
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}
and
{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}
can also be interpreted as the curvature of a manifold orthogonal to
{\bm{n}}
in direction
{\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{b}}
respectively, if such a manifold exists. We will continue to look at
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}
a little longer to make its relation to the shape of such manifolds orthogonal to
{\bm{n}}
clearer. To that end, we will establish under which conditions such orthogonal manifolds exist and introduce the shape operator of a manifold.

Remark 3
.

Induced Foliation

Let
{{\bm{V}}}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a differentiable normalized vector field and let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a connected open set. If
{{\bm{V}}}\cdot\text{rot}({\bm{V}})=0
on
U
, then for each
{\bm{r}}\in U
there is a manifold
X^{\bm{V}}_{\bm{r}}
such that
{\bm{r}}\in X^{\bm{V}}_{\bm{r}}
and so that
{\bm{V}}({\bm{r}}^{\prime})
is the normal vector of
{\bm{X}}
at all
{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\in{\bm{X}}^{\bm{V}}_{\bm{r}}
. Since
{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\in{\bm{X}}_{\bm{r}}^{\bm{V}}
implies
{\bm{X}}_{\bm{r}}^{\bm{V}}={\bm{X}}^{\bm{V}}_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}
this introduces an equivalence relation on
U
called a foliation, with equivalence classes, called leaves, being surfaces normal to
{\bm{V}}
. Due to this we will drop the
{\bm{r}}
from
{\bm{X}}_{\bm{r}}^{\bm{V}}
going forward and write
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{V}}
to mean the leaf of the foliation that is orthogonal to
{\bm{V}}
at
{\bm{r}}
. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
the condition
{\bm{V}}\cdot\text{rot}({\bm{V}})=0
is both necessary and sufficient for the existence of a foliation. This follows from Froebenius theorem
Frobenius 1877
;
Lee 2000
applied to vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

A maybe more applicable, yet still sufficient (not necessary condition), for the existence of
X^{\bm{V}}
can be expressed as follows: Let
{\bm{r}}(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
be a (local) parametrization of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e. let
(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
be the (local) coordinates.
If
0\neq{\bm{V}}\propto\frac{\partial{\bm{r}}}{\partial u^{1}}
then

{\bm{X}}^{\bm{V}}(u^{2},u^{3})={\bm{r}}(\bar{u}^{1},u^{2},u^{3})

is a parametrization of
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{V}}
containing the point
\bar{{\bm{r}}}={\bm{r}}(\bar{u}^{1},\bar{u}^{2},\bar{u}^{3})
, i.e. fixing one of the coordinates of
{\bm{r}}
, but altering the others generates a surface containing
{\bm{r}}
.

Remark 4
.

Manifolds and their Shape Operator

Let
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{N}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\supset U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the parametrization of an embedded surface
{\bm{X}}(u^{1},u^{2})
with parameters
(u^{1},u^{2})\in U
and tangent vectors
{\bm{T}}_{i}=\frac{\partial{\bm{X}}}{\partial u^{i}}
(
i=1,2
) and normal vector
{\bm{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.
For simplicity assume
{\bm{N}}
is not just defined and differentiable on
{\bm{X}}
, but on an open neighborhood
U
of of
{\bm{X}}\subset U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The metric tensor
g
of
{\bm{X}}
can be expressed in coordinates as the matrix
g_{\alpha,\beta}={\bm{T}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\bm{T}}_{\beta}
where
\alpha,\beta\in\{u^{1},u^{2}\}
, while the second fundamental form
h
of
{\bm{X}}
is given by the matrix defined through
h_{\alpha,\beta}=-\frac{\partial{\bm{T}}_{\alpha}}{\partial\beta}\cdot{\bm{N}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{X}}}{\partial\alpha\partial\beta}\cdot{\bm{N}}
.
From
g
and
h
the shape operator, or Weingarten map
S_{\bm{X}}
, can be calculated as
S_{\bm{X}}=g^{-1}h
. The shape operator contains information about the curvature of the manifold
{\bm{X}}
. Given a unit tangent vector
{\bm{u}}\in T_{\bm{X}}
the curvature in direction
{\bm{u}}
can be calculated as
{\bm{u}}^{\top}S_{\bm{X}}{\bm{u}}
. The shape operator shares its Eigenvalues with
h
, meaning Eigenvalues of
\mathbb{S}_{X}
are the principal curvatures of
{\bm{X}}
, its determinant is the Gauss curvature and its trace is the mean curvature of
{\bm{X}}
.

By Weingarten’s theorem, the coordinate representation of
S_{X}
can also be calculated as

\displaystyle(S_{X})_{\alpha}^{\beta}={\bm{T}}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{T}}_{\beta}}{\bm{N}}

\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\{u^{1},u^{2}\}.

(25)

Weingarten 1861
;
Kreyszig 2013

With notation from Remarks
3
and
4
and
{\bm{N}}={\bm{n}}
and
{\bm{T}}_{1}={\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{T}}_{2}={\bm{b}}
we can interpret the derivative terms in equation (
16
) as components of the shape operator
S_{{\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}}}
of the manifold
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}}
and thus

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}}{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=(1-\mu^{2})\left[(\cos(\omega),\sin(\omega))\cdot S_{{\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\omega)\\
\sin(\omega)\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=:(1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)

Conveniently the map

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}=\frac{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}-({\bm{t}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{b}}}{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}})}{1-{\bm{t}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}.

(55)

The shape operator can be transformed in a similar fashion, if the surface curvature form is preferred. The curvature

{\bm{b}}
or
\tilde{{\bm{b}}}

In order to calculate
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}
,
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}

{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}}

\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{t}}

Using this we can calculate
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}
and

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}=\frac{1}{k_{\bm{t}}}\begin{pmatrix}{a\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi)\sqrt{c^{2}\left(\left(a^{2}-b^{2}\right)\cos(2\phi)+a^{2}+b^{2}\right)}}\\
\\
{b\sin(\theta)\sin(\phi)\sqrt{c^{2}\left(\left(a^{2}-b^{2}\right)\cos(2\phi)+a^{2}+b^{2}\right)}}\\
\\
{c\cos(\theta)\sqrt{c^{2}\left(\left(a^{2}-b^{2}\right)\cos(2\phi)+a^{2}+b^{2}\right)}}\end{pmatrix}

(57)

with

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\rho\left(\cos^{2}(\theta)\left(a^{2}\cos^{2}(\phi)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi)\right)+c^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\theta)\left(a^{2}\cos^{2}(\phi)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi)\right)+c^{2}\cos^{2}(\theta)}

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}}=\frac{1}{k_{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}}\begin{pmatrix}{a\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi)\sqrt{a^{2}b^{2}\sin^{4}(\theta)}}\\
\\
{b\sin(\theta)\sin(\phi)\sqrt{a^{2}b^{2}\sin^{4}(\theta)}}\\
\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(58)

where

\displaystyle k_{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}=\rho\left(\sin^{2}(\theta)\left(a^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi)+b^{2}\cos^{2}(\phi)\right)\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\theta)\left(a^{2}\cos^{2}(\phi)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi)\right)}

Now

can be calculated through (

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}=\frac{1}{k_{\bm{n}}}\begin{pmatrix}a\cos(\phi)\sin(\theta)\left(b^{4}\left(c^{2}-a^{2}\right)\cos(\theta)^{2}\right.\left.+{c}^{4}\left({b}^{2}-{a}^{2}\right)\sin(\phi)^{2}\sin(\theta)^{2}\right),\\

We saw that going from spherical to elliptical coordinates significantly increased the complexity of the involved terms as well as the procedure of calculating them. Remark 5
.

Homothetic Scaling A shared feature of all the preceding examples is the appearance of the scaling factor
1/\rho
in every term. This is not a coincidence, but rather a direct consequence of the fact that in each case, the rescaled embedding satisfies
X_{\rho{\bm{r}}}^{\bm{n}}=\rho X_{\bm{r}}^{\bm{n}}
. In other words, the foliation induced by
{\bm{n}}
can be generated by scaling a single base manifold by a factor of
\rho
. Such families of manifolds are said to be generated by homothetic/self-similar rescaling. These commonly arise in coordinate systems and in the context of curvature flows.
Kohn 2008
;
Drugan et al. 2018

More specifically, for
{\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
{\bm{r}}_{2}=\rho{\bm{r}}_{1}
for some
\rho>0
, we observe that the normal, tangent, and binormal vectors satisfy
{\bm{n}}({\bm{r}}_{1})={\bm{n}}({\bm{r}}_{2}),{\bm{t}}({\bm{r}}_{1})={\bm{t}}({\bm{r}}_{2})
, and
{\bm{b}}({\bm{r}}_{1})={\bm{b}}({\bm{r}}_{2})
. Consequently, the associated quantities
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{u}}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{u}}
are simply rescaled by a factor of
\rho
when transitioning from
{\bm{r}}_{1}
to
{\bm{r}}_{2}
.

An illustrative 2D example of such homothetic rescaling is shown in Figure
8
.

Figure 8
:
2D example of homothetically rescaled manifolds. Base manifold
{\bm{X}}
in red, rescaled copies
\rho{\bm{X}}
in blue for various
\rho.

To see the effect on curvatures let

{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an embedded manifold parametrized by
u^{1},u^{2}\in U^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, if
{\bm{X}}
is a surface, and
u^{1}\in U^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}
, if
{\bm{X}}
is a curve.

Next, let
{\bm{Y}}=\rho{\bm{X}}
. If
{\bm{X}}_{i}=\frac{\partial{\bm{X}}}{\partial u^{i}}
with
i={1(,2)}
are picked as tangent vectors to
{\bm{X}}
, then If
{\bm{Y}}_{i}=\frac{\partial{\bm{Y}}}{\partial u^{i}}=\rho{\bm{X}}_{i}
are tangent vectors to
{\bm{Y}}
.
Since
{\bm{X}}_{i}
and
{\bm{Y}}_{i}
are colinear, the normal vectors
{\bm{N}}_{\bm{X}}={\bm{N}}_{Y}
are the same for fixed
u^{1}(,u^{2})
.
Consequently the metric tensors of
{\bm{Y}}

{g_{\bm{Y}}}_{\alpha,\beta}={\bm{Y}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\bm{Y}}_{\beta}

=\rho^{2}{\bm{X}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\bm{X}}_{\beta}=

\rho^{2}{g_{\bm{X}}}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a scalar multiple of the metric tensor
g_{\bm{X}}
of
{\bm{X}}
. Similarly for the second fundamental form:

{h_{\bm{Y}}}_{\alpha,\beta}={\bm{N}}_{\bm{Y}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{Y}}}{\partial\alpha\partial\beta}=\rho N_{\bm{X}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{X}}}{\partial\alpha\partial\beta}=\rho{h_{\bm{X}}}_{\alpha,\beta}.

This now finally leads to

\displaystyle S_{\bm{Y}}=(g_{\bm{Y}})^{-1}h_{\bm{Y}}=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(g_{\bm{X}})^{-1}\rho h_{\bm{X}}=\frac{1}{\rho}S_{\bm{X}}.

(61)

This relation between shape factors of scaled manifolds, intuitively implies that manifolds scaled by a factor
\rho
are flatter by a factor
1/\rho
. This applies to manifolds in arbitrary dimensions, but specifically to the curves and surfaces of interest for us here thus explaining the factor
1/\rho
in all terms from previous examples bar
{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{n}}{\bm{t}}
.
To explain the scaling of this factor consider
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{1},\tau)
and assume this is a parametrization by arc length. Then
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{2},\tau)=\rho\bm{\gamma}(r_{1},\tau/\rho)
is also an arc length parametrization. Noting that this implies
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}}^{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{1})=\frac{1}{\rho}{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{u}({\bm{r}}_{2})}
gives an additional explanation of the factor
1/\rho
in all curvature related terms.
However, we can also use this to see that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{n}}{\bm{t}}({\bm{r}}_{2})=\frac{d}{d\tau}{\bm{t}}(\bm{\gamma}^{n}({\bm{r}}_{2},\tau))\bigg|_{\tau=0}=\frac{d}{d\tau}{\bm{t}}(\rho\bm{\gamma}^{n}({\bm{r}}_{1},\tau/\rho))\bigg|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\tau}{\bm{t}}(\bm{\gamma}^{n}({\bm{r}}_{1},\tau/\rho))\bigg|_{\tau=0}=\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_{\bm{n}}{\bm{t}}({\bm{r}}_{1})

(62)

thereby explaining the
1/\rho
factor in the remaining term. Here we used
\bm{\gamma}^{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{2},\tau)=\rho\bm{\gamma}(r_{1},\tau/\rho)
in the first line and
{\bm{t}}({\bm{r}})={\bm{t}}(\rho{\bm{r}})
going from the first to the second line.

While the example of elliptical coordinates gave us a glimpse into how complex things can get one might find some relief in the fact that it suffices to handle the case
\rho=1
and simply scale everything in the end, when dealing with coordinates based on homothetic foliations.

3.5
Translating Graph coordinates

We will close the example section with a class of coordinates that is not based on homothetic foliation, but translated graphs instead. For this let
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
be twice differentiable function with graph
G_{0}(x,y)=(x,y,f(x,y))
and assume that
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is parametrized along translated copies of
G
:

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}(x,y,z):=G_{z}(x,y):=(x,y,f(x,y)+z).

(63)

An example is depicted in figure
9

:

We will consider the special case of a translating paraboloid first and then provide formulas for the case of general
G_{z}

given at the start of the example section in (

). As was the case for ellipses unfortunately none of the terms will end up being zero. However we will see that all terms in the streaming operator can be calculated with knowledge of
G_{0}
alone, i.e. independently of

Translating Paraboloid

In the case of
G_{0}
being a paraboloid
f
is of the form
f(x,y)=ax^{2}+by^{2}
(
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
). Tangent vectors parallel to the coordinate axis to
G_{z}
can by found by normalizing
\partial_{x}G_{z}
and
\partial_{y}G_{z}
respectively.

This intuitive choice leads to

\displaystyle{\bm{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}}\\
0\\
\frac{2ax}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{b}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}\\
\frac{2by}{\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

(64)

along with the normal vector

\displaystyle{\bm{n}}(x,y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{2ax}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}\\
-\frac{2by}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

(65)

However,
{\bm{t}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{b}}}
are generally not orthogonal. This can be corrected via Gram-Schmidt, keeping
{\bm{t}}
and replacing
\tilde{{\bm{b}}}
with:

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{4abxy}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy\right)}\\
\frac{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy}\\
\frac{2by}{\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy\right)}\end{pmatrix}

(66)

Using
\tilde{{\bm{b}}}
over
{\bm{b}}
has the advantage that
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{\tilde{{\bm{b}}}}}=\partial_{y}\tilde{{\bm{b}}}
, while
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}
is a slightly more complicated directional derivative of
{\bm{b}}
. Similarly calculating the second fundamental form needed for the shape operator is slightly easier obtained with respect to
\{{\bm{t}},{\bm{b}}\}.

The price to pay for this simplification is that terms calculated using
\tilde{{\bm{b}}}
will require correction via equations (
54
) and (
55
).

Either route leads to the shape operator with respect to
{\bm{t}}
and
{\bm{b}}
being

\displaystyle\left(S_{G_{z}}\right)_{{\bm{t}}{\bm{t}}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2a}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}

(67)

\displaystyle\left(S_{G_{z}}\right)_{{\bm{t}}{\bm{b}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{8a^{2}bxy\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}+4abxy\right)}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+4b^{2}y^{2}+1\right)}

(68)

\displaystyle\left(S_{G_{z}}\right)_{{\bm{b}}{\bm{t}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{8a^{2}bxy\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy\right)}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+4b^{2}y^{2}+1\right)}

(69)

\displaystyle\left(S_{G_{z}}\right)_{{\bm{b}}{\bm{b}}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2b\left(\frac{16a^{3}bx^{2}y^{2}}{\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}}+4a^{2}x^{2}\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}+\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\right)}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+4b^{2}y^{2}+1\right)}

(70)

The curvatures
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}
,
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}
and
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}
are equally lengthy so that for the sake of readability we will only provide their inner products with
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}
,
{\bm{b}}
and
{\bm{t}}
as needed.

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}=

\displaystyle\cos(\omega)\frac{4a^{2}x}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(4b^{2}y^{2}+1\right)}

\displaystyle+\sin(\omega)\frac{4by\left(-4a^{3}x^{2}+4a^{2}bx^{2}+b\right)}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(4b^{2}y^{2}+1\right)\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy\right)}

(71)

Conveniently

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}\cdot{\bm{b}}=0

(72)

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}\cdot{\bm{t}}=\frac{4aby}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}-4abxy\right)}

(73)

Lastly, we can find rather immediately

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{t}}{\bm{n}}=-{\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{b}}{\bm{n}}=

\displaystyle\frac{8a^{2}bxy}{\left(4a^{2}x^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}\left(4abxy-\sqrt{4a^{2}x^{2}+1}\sqrt{4b^{2}y^{2}+1}\right)}

General translating Graph

As final example we shall generalize the previous example by replacing the paraboloid by a general function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
. As shorthand let subscripts on
f
denote derivatives with respect to the subscript, i.e. for example
f_{x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
. We will again chose
{\bm{n}}
to be the normal vector of the graph of
f
given by

\displaystyle{\bm{n}}(x,y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{f_{x}}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\\
-\frac{f_{y}}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(75)

Like for the paraboloid

\displaystyle{\bm{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\\
0\\
\frac{f_{x}}{\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{b}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}}\\
\frac{f_{y}}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

(76)

are the canonical tangent vectors to
G_{z}
parallel to the
x
and
y

An appropriate, orthonormal choice is

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{f_{y}f_{x}}{\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\\
\\
-\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}\\
\\
\frac{f_{y}}{\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}}\end{pmatrix}

(77)

In this basis we can find the components of the shape operator by calculating the metric tensor and second fundamental form and get

\displaystyle(S_{G_{z}})_{{\bm{t}}{\bm{t}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{k_{S}}f_{xx}

(78)

\displaystyle(S_{G_{z}})_{{\bm{t}}{\bm{b}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{y}f_{x}f_{xx}-\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}f_{xy}}{k_{S}\left(\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}\right)}

(79)

\displaystyle(S_{G_{z}})_{{\bm{b}}{\bm{t}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}\right)\left(f_{y}f_{x}f_{xx}-\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)f_{xy}\right)}{k_{S}\left(f_{y}^{2}+f_{x}^{2}+1\right)}

(80)

\displaystyle(S_{G_{z}})_{{\bm{b}}{\bm{b}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{y}f_{x}\left(\left(f_{x}^{2}+\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}+1\right)f_{xy}\right.-f_{y}f_{x}f_{xx}-\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}f_{yy}\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}}{k_{S}\left(f_{y}^{2}+f_{x}^{2}+1\right)},

(81)

where

k_{S}={\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)}.

Again we will only provide the necessary inner products of
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}},{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}
and
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}
with
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel},{\bm{b}}
and
{\bm{t}}
.

\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}=\cos(\omega)\frac{f_{y}f_{xy}+f_{x}f_{xx}}{\left(f_{y}^{2}+1\right)\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}}+

(82)

\displaystyle\sin(\omega)\frac{\left(f_{yy}\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)-f_{x}^{2}f_{xx}\right)f_{y}-f_{y}^{2}f_{x}f_{xy}+f_{x}\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)f_{xy}}{\left(f_{y}^{2}+1\right)\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}\right)}

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{t}}}}\cdot{\bm{b}}=-\frac{f_{y}\left(\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}f_{y}^{2}+\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}+\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\left(-f_{y}\right)f_{x}\right)f_{xy}}{\left(f_{y}^{2}+1\right)\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)\left(f_{y}f_{x}-\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}}

(83)

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{b}}}}\cdot{\bm{t}}=\frac{f_{y}f_{xx}}{\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}\right)}

(84)

To wrap up we calculate the winding term as

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{t}}{\bm{n}}=-{\bm{t}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{b}}{\bm{n}}=-\frac{f_{y}\left(f_{y}f_{xy}+f_{x}f_{xx}\right)}{\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\left(f_{x}^{2}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(\sqrt{f_{y}^{2}+1}\sqrt{f_{x}^{2}+1}-f_{y}f_{x}\right)}

(85)

It should be noted that, while the general translating graph example expectedly looks more complicated than the paraboloid, the differences, formally, are not too big. Since the curvature terms consist of first and second derivatives only the paraboloid already exposes most of the complexity that is to be expected. Further note that none of the terms depend on
z
. Thus, despite none of the terms in these two example being particularly simple they all can be calculated using geometric properties of
G_{0}
alone.

4
Conclusion

We provided a derivation of the streaming operator
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi
in equation (
1
) with a high level of generality and freedom concerning the choice of parametrization of
{\bm{\Omega}}
. We also provided various interpretations of the resulting terms relating to the curvature of manifolds, which we believe to be more intuitive than pure calculus or complicated pictures. While we provided a handful of classical examples where the correctly parametrized form of
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi
can be almost intuited, we also demonstrated the complexity can increase tremendously once the parametrization of
{\bm{\Omega}}
becomes even slightly more complicated. Regardless, we believe the connection to curves and surfaces sheds light on the issue at hand from a new angle and might provide intuition and guidance to practitioners when deciding if the trade-off between increased complexity versus a potential reduction in dimension when using different coordinate systems is worth it or not. Beyond that we see use as a intuitive verification tool. While actual curvatures and thus the coefficients of terms in the streaming operator can be hard to calculate, whether or not they vanish can oftentimes be decided without any calculation.

\Psi
, i.e.
\nabla^{\bm{r}}\Psi({\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}},t)\propto{\bm{n}}({\bm{r}})

for all
{\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}}
, then the terms
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\omega}
are also zero under rather mild conditions on
X^{\bm{n}}.
These conditions are the following: Let
{\bm{v}}\in T_{\bm{r}}X^{\bm{n}}

be a tangent vector to
X^{\bm{n}}
at
{\bm{r}}
and let
Q\in SO(2)
. We require there exists a loop
\bm{\gamma}:[0,1]\to X^{\bm{n}}
on
X^{\bm{n}}
starting and ending at
{\bm{r}}
, such that parallel transport
Lee 2018
along
\bm{\gamma}
maps
v
to
Qv
. If this holds we say the Holonomy group of
X^{\bm{n}}
at
{\bm{r}}
equals
SO(2)
, or
Hol_{\bm{r}}=SO(2)
.
If this condition holds then for every
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, there is a loop
\bm{\gamma}\subset X^{\bm{n}}
such that
{\bm{\Omega}}(\mu,\omega_{1})
is turned into
{\bm{\Omega}}(\mu,\omega_{2})
while moving along
\bm{\gamma}
. But since
X^{\bm{n}}
was assumed to be a level set of
\Psi
this implies
\Psi({\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}}(\mu,\omega_{1}),t)=\Psi({\bm{r}},{\bm{\Omega}}(\mu,\omega_{2},t)
and therefor
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\omega}=0.
While sounding rather complicated the condition
Hol_{\bm{r}}({\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}})=SO(2)
holds for all orientable surfaces that are not a product space or have constant intrinsic curvature.
Berger 1955

While spheres are a notable exception whose holonomy group happens to be
SO(2)
despite having constant curvature, cylinders unfortunately are not.

A.2
Some comments on Conservation forms

In numerical applications people are often more interested in spatial and directional averages of
\Psi
and as such of
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi.
It is therefore convenient to rewrite
\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi
in conservation form
Bell and Glasstone 1970

\displaystyle v({\bm{r}})\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{\bm{r}}\\
\partial_{\mu}\\
\partial_{\omega}\end{pmatrix}\cdot{\bm{J}}({\bm{r}},\mu,\omega),

(86)

where
v({\bm{r}})
is the volume element with respect to the coordinates of
{\bm{r}}
and
{\bm{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
is some vector field whose components can be interpreted as flux of
\Psi
. If such a form exists the integral

\displaystyle\int_{V}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi=\int_{V}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\nabla_{{\bm{\Omega}}}\Psi v({\bm{r}})du^{1}du^{2}du^{3}d\mu d\omega.

(87)

is simplified tremendously via the divergence theorem.

We show through a simplified case that a reformulation such as (
86
) can not be expected in arbitrary coordinates.
Suppose that we have picked coordinates
{\bm{r}}(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3})
such that
\frac{d{\bm{r}}}{du^{1}}={\bm{n}}=\nabla\Psi
, that is
X^{\bm{n}}
are level sets of
\Psi
. Further, assume that the structure of
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}}
is sufficient for this to imply that
\partial\Psi/\partial\omega=0
. (Compare section
A.1
for details. Also take note, that this example specifically excludes cylindrical coordinates.)

We therefore have

\displaystyle v({\bm{r}})\nabla_{\bm{\Omega}}\Psi=v({\bm{r}})\mu\nabla_{\bm{n}}\Psi+v({\bm{r}})\left((1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}})\right)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mu}

(88)

as shown in section 2. To arrive at a conservation form of this we attempt the following Ansatz:

\displaystyle v({\bm{r}})\nabla_{\bm{\Omega}}\Psi=\frac{v({\bm{r}})\mu}{f}\nabla_{\bm{n}}(f\Psi)v({\bm{r}})\frac{\left((1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}})\right)}{g}\frac{\partial(g\Psi)}{\partial\mu}

(89)

with some functions
f,g>0
. For this to be in conservation form three conditions are required:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{n}}\frac{v({\bm{r}})\mu}{f}=0\Rightarrow\nabla_{\bm{n}}\frac{v}{f}=0

(90)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\frac{\left((1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}})\right)}{g}=0

(91)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{f}\nabla_{\bm{n}}f=-\frac{\left((1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}})\right)}{g}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu}

(92)

Where the last condition merely ensures equality of the right-hand sides of (
88
) and (
89
).

Through some manipulations and after abbreviating

K({\bm{r}},\mu,\omega)=\left((1-\mu^{2})C({\bm{r}},\omega)+\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}})\right)

conditions (
90
) and (
91
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{\bm{n}}v}{v}=\frac{\nabla_{\bm{n}}f}{f}

(93)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}=\frac{K}{g}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mu}

(94)

Plugging (
94
) into (
92
) then leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{\bm{n}}v}{v}=\frac{\nabla_{\bm{n}}f}{f}=\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mu}=C({\bm{r}},\omega)-\frac{1-2\mu^{2}}{\mu\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\parallel}\cdot{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}).

In this equation the left-hand side is independent of
\mu
and
\omega
, implying it can only hold if
{\bm{\bm{\kappa}}^{{\bm{n}}}}=0
for all
{\bm{r}}
and if
C({\bm{r}},\omega)=C({\bm{r}})
is itself independent of
\omega
.
The latter condition means the sectional curvature of
{\bm{X}}^{\bm{n}}
is the same in all directions, which from embedded surfaces leaves only planes and spheres. In both cases, conservation forms are known:

\displaystyle f_{\text{plane}}=1

\displaystyle g_{\text{plane}}=1

(95)

\displaystyle f_{\text{sphere}}=\rho

\displaystyle g_{\text{sphere}}=1.

(96)

All other cases fail to produce a conservation form via the chosen assumptions and Ansatz. Note that, while cylindrical coordinates, technically do not fulfill all assumptions used here, there still are conservation forms in the literature. These are however also based on a choice of
{\bm{n}}
where
X^{\bm{n}}
are planes.
Lastly note that this is not proof that conservation forms do not exist in general, but merely that, if they do, finding them is more complicated than using a simple Ansatz like we did.
