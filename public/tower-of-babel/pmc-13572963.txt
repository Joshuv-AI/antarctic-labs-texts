Prokaryotic Novelty and Metabolic Diversity in the Tagoro Shallow Hydrothermal System (Canary Islands, Spain)
Authors: Clàudia Pérez‐Barrancos, Eugenio Fraile‐Nuez, José Antonio Lozano‐Rodríguez, María Isabel Díaz‐Durán, Carmen Presas‐Navarro, Jesús M. Arrieta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe shallow hydrothermal system of the Tagoro submarine volcano (Canary Islands, Spain) has been discharging low‐temperature, nutrient‐rich, acidic fluids high in CO2 and H2S since March 2012. Despite influencing local microbial communities, the effects of these hydrothermal fluids on community structure and diversity across spatial gradients remain poorly understood. Using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, we observed significant differences in prokaryotic community structure from epipelagic waters to benthic habitats affected by hydrothermal activity. Prokaryotic richness and diversity decreased closer to hydrothermal sources, yet novel taxa were prevalent across all hydrothermally influenced habitats. Archaeal diversity was low and dominated by ammonia‐oxidising Nitrosopumilaceae, while bacterial communities were more diverse, including iron‐oxidising Mariprofundaceae, methane‐oxidising Methylomonadaceae and metabolically versatile Rhodobacteraceae. Molecular analyses revealed microbial specialisation within these families, complementing scanning electron microscopy observations of active bacterial biomineralization in chimney fragments linked to Mariprofundaceae members. Together, these findings offer new insights into the ecology and functional potential of microbial assemblages in the Tagoro hydrothermal system, suggesting their involvement in nitrogen, iron, sulphur and methane cycling. This study advances our understanding of microbial distribution patterns in shallow‐sea hydrothermal fields and highlights the high prevalence of novel taxa with potential biotechnological relevance.

Introduction

Marine shallow‐water hydrothermal systems (< 200 mbsl) are dynamic environments where geothermal activity interacts with the surrounding marine ecosystem. The simultaneous presence of light and geothermally reduced compounds makes these systems high‐energy environments where both photosynthesis and chemosynthesis take place (Tarasov et al. 2005; Price and Giovannelli 2017), contributing to enhance primary production in the surface layer (e.g., Buck et al. 2018; Bonnet et al. 2023; Tilliette et al. 2023; Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). These unique environments harbour diverse microbial communities adapted to extreme conditions (Caramanna et al. 2021) and sustain the food web by promoting the accumulation and transformation of organic matter (Gomez‐Saez et al. 2017). Additionally, microorganisms often develop new habitats (e.g., microbial mats and biofilms) (Danovaro et al. 2024) and establish potential symbiotic relationships with biota colonising these newly formed ecosystems (Dubilier et al. 2008).

While shallow‐water vents have been documented on several geological settings, they remain significantly less explored than their deep‐sea (> 1000 mbsl) counterparts (Price and Giovannelli 2017). The 2011 submarine eruption that took place just 1.8 km off the coast of El Hierro (Canary Islands), provided a rare opportunity to investigate underwater volcanism and its subsequent shallow hydrothermal activity in a relatively accessible system. The eruption lasted 5 months and formed the Tagoro underwater volcano (Álvarez‐Valero et al. 2023; Vázquez et al. 2023). In March 2012, the system evolved into a diffuse degassing stage, with the affected area concentrated within 0.5 km of the main crater, extending from approximately 127 mbsl (main crater) to 88 mbsl (summit) (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2016; Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024). This has resulted in a 7600 m2 shallow hydrothermal system covered with iron (Fe)‐oxide deposits (González et al. 2020) and characterised by thousands of vent orifices discharging low‐temperature (21°C–33.3°C) low‐pH (−1.37 average anomaly) fluids (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024), together with CO2 and reduced species degassing (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013, 2016). Vent structures include fissures, fractures and chimney‐like morphologies, often colonised by microbial mats (Danovaro et al. 2017; Sotomayor‐García et al. 2023; Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024).

Diffuse hydrothermal emissions from Tagoro have significantly altered the physical and chemical properties of seawater and marine sediments since the eruptive phase (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013, 2016; Santana‐González et al. 2017; Fraile‐Nuez et al. 2018; González et al. 2020; González‐Vega et al. 2020). Hydrothermal discharge creates persistent anomalies including elevated temperature, and reduced salinity and density in the water column (Fraile‐Nuez et al. 2018). These emissions also produce marked ORP anomalies and decrease in pH, driven by the release of reduced, acidic fluids and CO2‐rich gases (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013, 2016). Vertical water‐column profiles further revealed positive Fe2+ concentration anomalies, coinciding with significant pH reductions, in waters near the main cone (Santana‐González et al. 2017). Similarly, significant inorganic nutrient enrichments have been consistently observed in the water column (González‐Vega et al. 2020; Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024; Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025), with anomalies detected up to 50 m above the seabed and occasionally reaching the mixed layer and euphotic zone. Higher concentrations of dissolved PO4 and Si(OH)4 were detected in samples collected directly from the vents, whereas dissolved NO2 − + NO3 − was highest in the water column and decreased toward active venting areas (González‐Vega et al. 2020). Direct analyses of vent fluids indicate strong enrichments in dissolved Si(OH)4, PO4 and NH4 + relative to background seawater, reaching concentrations of up to 189, 1.60 and 1.97 μmol kg−1, respectively (González‐Vega et al. 2020). Dissolved Si(OH)4 has been identified as a conservative tracer of hydrothermal influence at Tagoro (González‐Vega et al. 2020), and long‐term time series of this compound show persistent mixing between hydrothermal fluids and surrounding seawater, with detectable hydrothermal signatures extending several kilometres from the main active venting zone (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Together, these geochemical conditions likely influence local biogeochemistry and provide electron donors and nutrients that influence the composition and functioning of microbial communities.

Despite its ecological significance and potential biotechnological relevance (García‐Davis et al. 2021), microbial communities in the post‐eruptive Tagoro system remain poorly characterised. The discovery of Candidatus Thiolava veneris highlighted the presence of pioneering microbial taxa colonising newly formed habitats (Danovaro et al. 2017), although it remains uncultured and its complete genetic sequence is still unknown. While macrofaunal colonisation of this newly formed benthic habitat has been more extensively documented (Sotomayor‐García et al. 2019, 2020, 2023), potential interactions between macrofauna and microbial communities remain unexplored. Furthermore, microbes involved in Fe, S and CH4 cycling have been detected in Fe‐rich sediments and mineral deposits (González et al. 2020), yet available microbiological data remain scarce, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive characterisation of the system.

Experimental evidence suggests that Tagoro hydrothermal inputs can enhance primary production in surface seawater, primarily mediated by phototrophic microbes (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Geothermal compounds may also fuel chemosynthetic communities in the water column (Ferrera et al. 2015; Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). These processes may contribute to the transfer of matter and energy to higher trophic levels, as reflected in the abundance, diversity, and isotopic composition of zooplankton at Tagoro (Fernández de Puelles et al. 2021; Herrera et al. 2024).

This study characterises epipelagic and benthic prokaryotic communities across hydrothermal habitats subjected to different degrees of hydrothermal influence within the Tagoro shallow‐water hydrothermal field, including seawater, microbial mats, rocks, sediments and chimneys. The community structure and diversity of both bacteria and archaea were determined using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data. Comparisons with background seawater allowed the identification of microbial taxa specifically associated with the Tagoro hydrothermal field. Comparative phylogenetic analyses provided insights into the taxonomic diversity and potential functional roles of prokaryotic microorganisms in the area. Additionally, bacterial biomineralization was examined through SEM analyses of mineral structures consistent with those formed by putative Fe‐oxidising bacteria. These findings are essential to better understand the role of microbes in benthic and epipelagic marine habitats affected by shallow hydrothermal activity and their contribution to local biogeochemical cycles.

Materials and Methods

Seawater, microbial mat, sediment, rock and chimney samples were collected to characterise the prokaryotic communities inhabiting the active hydrothermal vent system at Tagoro (Figure 1), and to evaluate their plausible contribution to biomineralization and local biogeochemical cycles. In this study, we analysed a total of 37 discrete biological samples obtained over 6 oceanographic cruises conducted between April 2017 and February 2022 onboard the R/V Ramón Margalef and R/V Ángeles Alvariño (Table S1).

Microbial and Geological Sampling

Discrete hydrothermally enriched seawater samples were collected from ~127 mbsl at two vertical hydrographic stations located above the main crater of the Tagoro shallow submarine volcano (Figure 1A). In this study, ‘hydrothermally enriched seawater’ is operationally defined as seawater exhibiting anomalies in temperature, pH and/or ORP relative to background conditions, indicating influence from diffuse hydrothermal discharge.

Seawater sampling was conducted using a rosette frame equipped with 24 12‐L Niskin bottles. Bottles were closed when real‐time anomalies in temperature, pH or ORP (indicators of active hydrothermal activity) were detected by an SBE 911‐plus CTD coupled with pH/ORP sensors. All sensors were calibrated before each oceanographic cruise. For comparison, reference seawater samples were also collected at ~127 mbsl from a control station located east of El Hierro, outside the influence of the Tagoro hydrothermal system (Figure 1A).

Additional hydrothermally enriched seawater, along with microbial mats, rocks, sediments and chimneys, were collected from visually active hydrothermal vents and areas adjacent to the main active crater using a remotely operated vehicle (ROV; Liropus 2000, IEO‐CSIC) (Figure 1B). The ROV was equipped with a range of sampling instruments specifically designed for each substrate type. Vent‐proximal fluids, microbial mats, sediments, and rocks were obtained using a suction system, which was upgraded in 2021 to a piston‐driven syringe sampler. Despite this improvement, vent‐proximal fluids represent in situ mixtures of hydrothermal emissions and ambient seawater due to unavoidable entrainment during sampling. Therefore, they are included in this study under the operational category of ‘hydrothermally enriched seawater’ to reflect their hydrothermal influence, as they still exhibit measurable physicochemical anomalies associated with hydrothermal activity. Chimney samples were collected using a drag mesh, as well as some sediment samples. The ROV was also equipped with robotic arms to collect additional sediment and rock samples. All samples were preserved at −20°C until further analysis.

The present sampling strategy builds on more than a decade of monitoring the Tagoro degassing stage, during which physicochemical gradients associated with hydrothermal activity have been extensively characterised (e.g., Fraile‐Nuez et al. 2018; González‐Vega et al. 2020; Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024; Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Accordingly, sample types were used as a proxy for hydrothermal influence, ranging from reference seawater, hydrothermally enriched seawater, microbial mat, sediment, rock, and chimney habitats. This classification follows previously described geochemical gradients and tracers of hydrothermal influence, such as dissolved Si(OH)4 (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024; Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025).

DNA Extraction, Amplification and Sequencing

Genomic DNA isolation and sequencing library preparation for 16S V4 gene sequencing were conducted at the Oceanographic Center of the Canary Islands (IEO‐CSIC, Tenerife, Spain). The OmniPrep Soil DNA kit (G‐Biosciences) was used following the provided guidelines to extract DNA from microbial mat, sediment, volcanic rocks, and chimney samples. Seawater samples were filtered through a 0.20 μm pore‐size filter before DNA extraction using the phenol‐chloroform method (Sambrook and Russell 2001). Sequencing libraries were prepared following the methods described by Pérez‐Barrancos et al. (2025). Briefly, the V4 region of the prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515F (GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 806R (GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT) (Apprill et al. 2015; Parada et al. 2016). Amplified products were cleaned, indexed and pooled in equal molar proportions and paired‐end sequenced (2 × 300) on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Macrogen, Korea). Sequence data (demultiplexed sequences with primers and adapters) are available in the NCBI public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) under accession number PRJNA1139066.

Sequencing Processing

Analyses were conducted in R v4.3.1 (R Core Team 2021) using tidyverse v2.0 (Wickham et al. 2019). Amplicons were processed with DADA2 v1.28 (Callahan et al. 2016) after primer removal using cutadapt v1.18. Sequence reads were trimmed using optimal parameters (truncLen 216,143 and maxEE 1) estimated using the FIGARO package (Weinstein et al. 2019). Sequences were pooled together for sample inference and chimeras were removed following the consensus approach. This process resulted in an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table comprising 7902 ASVs, representing 84% of the original reads. Rarefaction curves generated with ranacapa v0.1.0 (Kandlikar et al. 2018) indicated sufficient sequencing depth (Figure S1). Taxonomic assignment of prokaryotic sequences (including species level assignment) was performed according to Silva SSU version 138.1. About 5% sequences were removed as they were classified as eukaryotes, mitochondria or chloroplasts. The amplicon dataset was then rarefied to a sample size of 22,944 reads per sample to minimise library size differences using phyloseq v1.44 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). Two samples (S41 and S44, Table S1) were excluded due to insufficient read counts and 313 ASVs were lost during random subsampling.

Sequences unassigned at the genus level in the rarefied dataset using Silva were subjected to BLAST searches (Sayers et al. 2025) against the NCBI Nucleotide collection (nr/nt) to check for members of poorly studied groups not present in curated datasets like Silva but found elsewhere. Additionally, these unassigned sequences were compared against the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA sequences (Bacteria and Archaea) database to assess their potential relationship with cultured bacteria. To ensure the robustness of the results, the statistical significance (E‐value) and sequence similarity (Max Ident) of each match were evaluated. Results were further interpreted using the approximate taxonomic thresholds for bacteria and archaea defined by Yarza et al. (2014): 94.5% for genus level, 86.5% for family level and 82% for order level.

A phylogenetic tree was constructed for some of the most abundant taxonomic families in the rarified dataset using DECIPHER v2.24 (Wright 2016). For this purpose, sequences from each family were extracted from the rarefied dataset and visually inspected. Selected Silva sequences were incorporated into the respective datasets to serve as references, along with an outgroup bacterial or archaeal sequence that constituted the root of the phylogenetic tree (Table S2). After aligning the sequences, Silva pure sequences were shortened to match the length of the region sequenced in our study. The phylogenetic tree was constructed on phangorn v2.11.1 (Schliep 2011). Several substitution models were tested using a neighbour‐joining tree as a starting point, with the GTR + G + I maximum likelihood model consistently showing the best fit. Gamma distribution k = 4 and invariant sites inv = 0.2 parameters were applied before optimizing each tree without rearrangement. Bootstrapping was conducted to estimate the maximum likelihood (ML) tree up to 1000 times, ensuring the robustness of the phylogenetic analysis.

Diversity metrics, Chao1 (species richness) and Inverse Simpson (species diversity), were estimated using phyloseq v1.44 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). The statistical significance of alpha diversity differences between habitats was assessed using Games‐Howell post hoc tests following Welch's ANOVA. These statistical tests were chosen because data consistently met normality but not always homogeneity of variances. Analyses were performed with rstatix v0.7.2 (Kassambara 2021) and visualised using ggpubr v0.6.0 (Kassambara 2020).

Variations in microbial community structure among samples were evaluated through hierarchical clustering based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities using pvclust v2.2 (Suzuki et al. 2019). To quantify the uncertainty associated with clustering, multiscale bootstrap resampling was conducted up to 1000 times, providing approximately unbiased (au) values for each identified cluster. The results were visualised using ggtree v3.8.2 (Yu 2020).

The associations between species and specific habitats were assessed using the Indicator Value (IndVal) index, calculated through an indicator species analysis with indicspecies v1.7.15 (De Cáceres and Legendre 2009). This statistical index combines relative abundance and frequency of occurrence data to identify species strongly associated with specific habitats or environmental conditions (hereafter referred to as ‘indicator taxa’). To ensure the robustness of our results, we set a threshold of 75% or higher for the association value (stat) and a statistical significance (p) of 0.001. This approach allows the identification of prokaryotic species that may serve as potential biological markers of shallow submarine hydrothermal activity, providing insights into the ecological processes and dynamics of local microbial communities.

Petrographic Microscopy

A chimney fragment (S41, Table S1) was embedded in epoxy, and a thin section was prepared following standard procedures at the University of La Laguna. The thin section was studied under a Nikon Eclipse LV100N Pol polarisation optical microscope located at the Oceanographic Center of the Canary Islands (IEO‐CSIC, Tenerife, Spain). Images were acquired using a Pixelink camera and were processed with NIS‐Elements BR v5.42.06 software. Areas exhibiting dendritic features were photographed under 20× and 10× magnification in plane‐polarised light and selected for subsequent microanalyses.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

Textures and detailed morphology of individual filaments and globular structures were studied using a Zeiss EVO 15 scanning electron microscope (SEM) with 2 nm resolution equipped with 50 mm2 Oxford X‐max X‐ray scattering energy microanalyzer (EDX) from the General Research Support Service (SEGAI) of the University of La Laguna (Tenerife, Spain). Prior to analysis, the thin section was sputter‐coated with carbon in the first analytical session and with gold in the second session using a Quorum Q150R ES Plus system at a thickness of 15 nm. Additionally, a chimney fragment (also from sample S41) attached to an aluminium stub was coated with gold following the same procedure. Microanalyses were performed at a voltage of 20 kV and a working distance of 8.5 mm. Images of both the thin section and the chimney fragment were examined using the secondary electron (SE) detector at 20 kV and an average working distance of 7 mm.

Results

Prokaryotic Alpha Diversity

Chao1 richness and Inverse Simpson diversity differed significantly among habitats (Welch ANOVA, p < 0.001), indicating a marked decline in estimated richness and diversity with increasing proximity to hydrothermal vent emissions (Figure 2). Microbial mats exhibited the highest richness estimates but had lower diversity than seawater samples. Specifically, hydrothermally enriched seawater displayed greater richness and diversity than reference seawater. In contrast, rock samples exhibited lower richness than reference seawater and lower diversity relative to microbial mats. Sediment samples followed this decreasing trend, showing further declines in both richness and diversity, while chimney samples presented the lowest values overall. These contrasting variations in alpha diversity were particularly significant between microbial mat and chimney samples (Games–Howell post hoc, p ≤ 0.01).

Prokaryotic Chao1 richness and Inverse Simpson diversity estimates across habitats, ordered by increasing proximity to hydrothermal sources: Reference seawater, hydrothermally enriched seawater, microbial mat, rocks, sediments and chimney samples. Statistically significant differences between habitats, based on Welch ANOVA followed by Games–Howell pairwise post hoc tests, are stated at the top of each panel or indicated with brackets and asterisks (* for p ≤ 0.05 and ** for p ≤ 0.01) for each pairwise comparison.

Prokaryotic Community Structure

Distinct prokaryotic communities were observed between habitats based on hierarchical clustering of Bray–Curtis dissimilarities (Figure 3A). Seawater samples clustered separately from those samples collected in benthic habitats (microbial mats, rocks, sediments and chimneys), indicating a singular prokaryotic community in seawater. For instance, the archaeal class Nitrososphaeria, composed exclusively of the family Nitrosopumilaceae (Figure S2), was particularly abundant in seawater samples. This archaeal group accounted for 13% of the median relative abundance in reference seawater and 6% in hydrothermally enriched seawater (Figure 3B). Specifically, the genus Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus was consistently observed (Figure S3) (see Section 3.3).

Classes are arranged in decreasing order of median relative abundance across each habitat, with those contributing < 2% grouped as ‘Other’. Epipelagic Seawater

Reference and hydrothermally enriched seawater samples were analysed separately to assess the impact of hydrothermal influence on epipelagic prokaryotic communities. Both sample types shared similar dominant taxonomic groups, yet their community structures exhibited some differences. Gammaproteobacteria were more abundant in enriched seawater (37%) than reference seawater (15%) (Figure 3 A), whereas Alphaproteobacteria were more prevalent in reference seawater (31%) compared to enriched seawater (22%) (Figure 3 A). Nonetheless, Pseudomonadales remained the predominant Gammaproteobacteria order in seawater (Figure 3 B), with notable contributions from the families SAR86 clade, Pseudomonadaceae and Moraxellaceae (Figure S2). Similarly, the orders SAR11 clade and Rhodospirillales were consistently observed in both reference and enriched seawater (Figure 3 B), with Clade I and II , and AEGEAN‐169 marine group driving their relative abundances (Figure S2). Furthermore, indicator species analysis identified Rhodospirillaceae, a low‐abundance family (< 1%) within Rhodospirillales (Figure S2), as a strong indicator taxa of reference seawater (stat ≥ 0.92 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). Meanwhile, enriched seawater was particularly associated with several low‐abundance families within Alphaproteobacteria and other classes like Bacteroidia and Desulfobacteria (stat ≥ 0.82 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3).

Volcanic Rocks

In contrast to seawater samples, rock samples exhibited high variability in their prokaryotic composition (Figure 3A). Notably, no ASVs were identified as indicators of rock habitats (stat < 0.75 and p > 0.001; Table S3). While some rock samples clustered closely with microbial mats, others differed significantly.

Two rock samples, S43 and S50, formed a separate cluster that was significantly differentiated from all other benthic samples (Figure 3A). These samples were characterised by a high abundance of Alphaproteobacteria, particularly within the orders Rhizobiales (~12%–22%), Parvibaculales (~5%–9.5%) and Rhodobacterales (~4%–6.5%) (Figures 3B and S2). The order Steroidobacterales of Gammaproteobacteria was also notably present (up to 8%). These rock samples were also visually described as soft and easily disaggregated, with orange precipitates attached during on‐board sampling. In contrast, rock sample S42 showed greater similarity to sediment and chimney samples. This sample was predominantly composed of Gammaproteobacteria (Figure 3A), with a high relative abundance of the order Enterobacterales (up to 70%) (Figure 3B) and the Pseudoalteromonadaceae family (Figure S2). Lastly, the remaining rock samples clustered with or near microbial mats, displaying a notable presence of Zetaproteobacteria (30%) (Figure 3A), which in our dataset was exclusively composed of the genus Mariprofundus within the order Mariprofundales (Figures 3B and S3). However, the highest relative abundances of Zetaproteobacteria were observed in microbial mat samples (~32%) (Figure 3A), where 14 Mariprofundus ASVs were significant indicators (stat ≥ 0.84 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3 and Figure S3).

Microbial Mats

Microbial mats exhibited the highest number of ASVs serving as indicators, including members from 15 phyla, 22 classes and 32 orders (Table S3). In addition to Zetaproteobacteria, 15 Alphaproteobacterial ASVs were identified as indicators of this habitat (stat ≥ 0.76 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). Among these, 10 ASVs belonged to the family Rhodobacteraceae, which accounted for ~6% of the median relative abundance in microbial mats (Figure S2). Notably, the genus Roseobacter clade NAC11‐7 lineage was the most prevalent within this family and habitat (Figure S3). Several members of Gammaproteobacteria were also associated with microbial mats, with 18 ASVs highlighted as indicators (stat ≥ 0.79 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). The orders Enterobacterales and Pseudomonadales were the most consistent among these, comprising 5% and 2% of the median relative abundance in microbial mat samples, respectively (Figure 3B). In this particular habitat, Enterobacterales was mostly represented by the family Colwelliaceae, while Pseudomonadales by Halieaceae (Figure S2). Additionally, eight Bacteroidia ASVs were associated with microbial mats (stat ≥ 0.78 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), including five ASVs classified as Flavobacteriales (~1%). The class Bdellovibrionia (~1%) also exhibited 10 ASVs as indicators (stat ≥ 0.78 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), along with several other low‐abundance taxa (< 1%), such as members of the phyla Desulfobacterota, Campylobacterota, Firmicutes, Myxococcota and Patescibacteria (stat > 0.75 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3).

Sediment and Chimney Fragments

Sediment and chimney samples shared similarities in prokaryotic community composition that significantly set them apart from other benthic samples (Figure 3A). Both habitats were characterised by the presence of Gammaproteobacteria (28%–51%), Thermoanaerobaculia (2%–13%), Thermodesulfovibrionia (2%–18%), Alphaproteobacteria (7.5%–10%), Calditrichia (3%–4%), Anaerolineae (3%–4%) and Desulfobulbia (< 2%). However, as observed in other habitats, differences at lower taxonomic levels (Figures 3B, S2, and S3) and the identification of indicator taxa (Table S3) revealed unique microbial features specific to these habitats.

Sediment samples were particularly characterised by Thermodesulfovibrionia class, with notably higher relative abundances (18%) compared to chimney samples (2%). Among 19 ASVs classified within this class, 3 ASVs were significantly indicative of hydrothermally influenced sediments (stat ≥ 0.90 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), including one classified as the genus Thermodesulfovibrio. This ASV was the only one classified to the genus level, while the other 18 ASVs remained at the Thermodesulfovibrionia class level. Sediments also featured Desulfobulbia (~2%), which was exclusively composed of the order Desulfobulbales, with five ASVs acting as indicators (stat ≥ 0.90 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). Four of these ASVs were classified as the family Desulfobulbaceae, including two identified as the genus Desulfobulbus. Furthermore, two ASVs from low‐abundance orders (< 1%) within Anaerolineae (~2%), such as Anaerolineales (Figure S2), were identified as significant indicators of sediment (stat ≥ 0.97 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). Nonetheless, the order SJA‐15 predominated within this class in both sediment and chimney samples (~2%) (Figure 3B).

Chimneys, on the other hand, were characterised by the dominance of the class Thermoanaerobaculia (~13%) (Figure 3A), with the family Thermoanaerobaculaceae being the unique representative (Figure S2) and almost entirely represented by the genus Subgroup 10 (Figure S3). However, no Thermoanaerobaculia ASVs were found indicative of chimney samples (stat < 0.75 and p > 0.001; Table S3). In contrast, one ASV from the genus Subgroup 23 (< 1%) was indicative of sediment samples (stat ≥ 0.93 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), where this family constituted only 2% of the relative abundance (Figure S2). Similarly, the order Methylococcales of Gammaproteobacteria exhibited higher relative abundances in chimney (28%) than sediment (9%) samples (Figure 3B), yet an ASV classified as the low‐abundance Methylomarinum genus (< 1%) was found to be a sediment indicator (stat ≥ 0.95 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3). The gammaproteobacterial Nitrosococcales was also higher in sediment samples (~1%). Chimney samples were further distinguished by the order Chromatiales (4%) within Gammaproteobacteria (Figure 3B), which was rare in other habitats (< 1%), particularly by the genus Candidatus Thiobios (Figure S3). Additionally, 2 ASVs from Alphaproteobacteria were also identified as indicative of chimney samples (stat ≥ 0.90 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), with 1 ASV classified under the family Rhodobacteraceae (~2%). However, the median relative abundance of Alphaproteobacteria in chimney samples was relatively low (10%) compared to other hydrothermally influenced habitats (Figure 3A). Additionally, chimney samples exhibited the highest median relative abundances of Planctomycetales and Phycisphaerales (~1.5%) (Figure 3B), which were the relatively most abundant orders of the Planctomycetota phylum. Although each of these classes comprised a small proportion of the overall prokaryotic community (< 1%), one ASV from the order Phycisphaerales, classified as the genus SM1A02, was found to be indicative of chimney samples (stat ≥ 0.88 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3).

Few other ASVs, typically belonging to low‐abundance phyla (< 1%), such as Acidobacteriota, Myxococcota, SAR 324 clade (Marine group B), were also found to be indicative of either sediment or chimney samples (stat ≥ 0.85 and p ≤ 0.001; Table S3), yet none were classified beyond the order level.

Phylogenetic Overview of Dominant Prokaryotic Families

Four prokaryotic families accounted for the highest relative abundances across the hydrothermally influenced habitats at the Tagoro submarine volcano: Mariprofundaceae (16% of median relative abundance), Methylomonadaceae (5%), Rhodobacteraceae (3%) and Nitrosopumilaceae (1%) (Figure S2). Phylogenetic trees constructed for selected families (Figure 4) illustrate the evolutionary relationships among ASVs and are complemented by heatmaps and bar plots showing median and maximum relative abundances across habitats. Rhodobacteraceae were excluded from detailed phylogenetic interpretation due to their high diversity and substantial metabolic heterogeneity.

Given the heterogeneity of environments included in this study, results are presented in a descriptive framework to reflect observed patterns, consistent with trends described in Section 3.2, rather than inferred functional equivalence between habitats.

Mariprofundaceae Family (Zetaproteobacteria)

Mariprofundaceae was the most abundant family in the dataset, comprising 16% of the median relative abundance. Members of this family were particularly represented in microbial mat samples (see Section 3.2).

However, Mariprofundaceae exhibited substantial ASV richness, with 66 ASVs classified as Mariprofundus and most detected at low relative abundances (≤ 1%) across multiple habitats affected by hydrothermal activity (Figure 4A). For instance, ASV_4, ASV_3 and ASV_2 were present in all hydrothermal habitats and accounted for the majority of relative abundances (≥ 5%) (Figure 4A). These ASVs were most closely related to the cultured species Mariprofundus ferrooxydans (including strain PV‐1), M. micogutta and M. aestuarium.

Only ASV_11 was classified at the species level as M. micogutta. Among all hydrothermally influenced habitats, this species had the highest median relative abundance in chimney samples (~9%), with individual observations reaching up to ~16%.

Phylogenetic analysis did not reveal clear habitat‐specific clustering of Mariprofundus ASVs. However, a subset of low‐abundance ASVs (≤ 1%) was identified as microbial mat indicators through indicator species analysis (see Section 3.2). Among these 14 indicator ASVs, ASV_10 showed relatively higher median relative abundance in microbial mats (~6%) and was closely related to M. ferrinatatus. In addition, 7 of the 14 indicator ASVs formed a distinct cluster, significantly differing from other Mariprofundus ASVs and from most cultured references, suggesting the presence of uncharacterized diversity within the genus.

Methylomonadaceae Family (Gammaproteobacteria)

Methylomonadaceae comprised ~5% of median relative abundance in the dataset and included 29 ASVs (Figure 4B). Although no species were identified and 10 ASVs remained unclassified at genus level, most ASVs were associated with cultured species (Table S2) or classified taxa belonging to genera IheB2‐23, Methylomonas and Methylomarinum.

ASVs classified as IheB2‐23 formed a distinct phylogenetic cluster and were most frequently associated with chimney samples, accounting for ~5% of the median relative abundance. However, certain ASVs were also detected in hydrothermally enriched seawater, rock, and sediment samples, reaching values up to 38%.

The Methylomonas ASV exhibited a maximum abundance of 4% in a rock sample and was closely related to Methylomonas koyamae and Methylomonas methanica MC09. Yet, its overall median relative abundance remained low (e.g., 0.5% in microbial mats and 0.02% in chimneys).

Among the Methylomarinum ASVs, ASV_259 was the only indicator ASV within the Methylomonadaceae family. It was significantly associated with sediment samples, where it was exclusively found, reaching relative abundances up to ~8%. In contrast, the remaining Methylomarinum ASVs exhibited peak abundances in rock samples (~10%) and hydrothermally enriched seawater (40%).

Lastly, ASVs classified as Marine Methylotrophic Group 2 and pItb‐vmat‐59 formed a cluster closely related to Methylomarinum vadi and Methylomonas rubra. The Marine Methylotrophic Group 2 ASVs were particularly associated with microbial mats, with median and maximum relative abundances up to ~23% and 73%, respectively. Similarly, pItb‐vmat‐59 ASVs were most prevalent in microbial mats, accounting for ~2% of the median relative abundance, although they reached higher relative abundances in chimney and rock samples (2%–8%).

Overall, members of Methylomonadaceae were detected across all habitat types considered in this study, indicating a broad environmental distribution within the family.

Nitrosopumilaceae (Nitrososphaeria)

Nitrosopumilaceae represented 1% of the median relative abundance and was detected in all habitats affected by hydrothermal activity at Tagoro, with particularly high abundance in seawater (~6%) (Section 3.2). This family accounted for 147 ASVs, including 40 ASVs classified as Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus, 29 ASVs as Candidatus Nitrosopumilus, 2 ASVs as Cenarchaeum, and 76 ASVs as unclassified Nitrosopumilaceae taxa (Figure 4C). No ASVs were classified at the species level or identified as significant indicators of specific habitats (Table S3). However, phylogenetic analyses revealed two broad clusters with differing distribution patterns across benthic and pelagic environments (Figure 4C).

One cluster included mainly benthic‐associated ASVs related to Candidatus Nitrosopumilus and Cenarchaeum, with median relative abundances up to ~57% and ~2.5%, respectively. Within this group, ASV_2403 was closely related to Candidatus Nitrosopumilus piranensis and Candidatus Nitrosopumilus adriaticus yet exhibited low abundances (≤ 1%). Additionally, both ASV_16 and ASV_6770 were associated with Candidatus Nitrosopumilus maritimus, Candidatus Nitrosopumilus koreensis and Candidatus Nitrosopumilus sediminis. ASV_16 was widespread across all habitats, showing median relative abundances of 35%–57% in benthic samples and of 9%–15% in seawater, while ASV_6770 was low‐abundant (≤ 0.5%) and confined to microbial mats and rocks.

A second cluster comprised ASVs classified as Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus and unclassified Nitrosopumilaceae, which were overall predominant in seawater samples. For example, ASV_12 was associated with Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus brevis, displayed a median relative abundance of ~37%–41% in seawater samples, and reached a maximum relative abundance of 60% in a sample of hydrothermally enriched seawater.

These patterns indicate differential distribution of Nitrosopumilaceae lineages across pelagic and benthic habitats within the Tagoro system.

Unexplored Prokaryotic Diversity

Approximately, 66% of the sequences in the rarefied dataset remained unassigned at genus level, with 37% unclassified at family level, 22% at order level and 10% at class level. Notably, nearly half (around 48%) of the robust ASVs identified as indicators of specific habitats were unassigned to a known genus (Table S4). Direct BLAST analysis against the NCBI Nucleotide collection (nr/nt) showed that 99.9% of unassigned sequences matched sequences corresponding to uncultured prokaryotes from diverse environments around the world. The highest percentage of identity between each of our sequences and its best database match (i.e., their closest GenBank relative) was ≥ 94.5% for ~89% of the sequences, suggesting genus level similarity based on published thresholds (see Section 2.3). An additional ~11% had identities between 86.5% and < 94.5% (family level), and only ~0.1% fell between 83% and < 86.5% (order level). Meanwhile, BLAST searches against the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA sequences database showed that all unassigned sequences were distantly related to formally described prokaryotes, as the highest identity values were on average below 90% ± 4%, highlighting the novelty of these unassigned sequences. In both databases expectation values were low (E‐value ≤ 4e−51), indicating that the matches were reliable and highly significant. These results indicate that, although most sequences share high similarity with other environmental sequences, they remain taxonomically unassigned, highlighting the extent of novel diversity at Tagoro and particularly of novel prokaryotic genera yet to be formally described.

The class Thermodesulfovibrionia, characteristic of our sediment samples (Table S3), exemplifies this unexplored diversity. Among the 19 ASVs classified within this class, only one was classified to the genus Thermodesulfovibrio (ASV_170), while the remaining ASVs were classified only at class level. Direct BLAST of these 18 unassigned Thermodesulfovibrionia sequences against the NCBI nucleotide collection confirmed their presence in aquatic environments elsewhere (Table S4). These closest GenBank relatives of these sequences presented maximum identities ranging from 96% to 100%, suggesting a close relationship at the level of genus or species with our unidentified Thermodesulfovibrionia ASVs. However, BLAST against the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA sequences database yielded lower identity matches (83%–87%), further indicating that these sequences are highly different from those of formally described organisms available in pure culture (Table S4). All BLAST results showed extremely low expectation values, indicating that the matches were reliable and highly significant. These findings indicate that vast, unexplored microbial diversity awaits discovery in unique environments such as the Tagoro hydrothermal system.

Chimney Textures and Morphology

Directional dendritic features were frequently observed throughout the thin section using optical microscopy (Figure S4A–B), along with Fe‐rich bands forming stacked, dome‐shaped structures (Figure S4C). SEM images of these dendritic areas revealed complex structures and diverse morphologies, occasionally embedded within the Si‐Fe chimney matrix (Figures S4D–F and S5).

At higher magnifications, the chimney fragment exhibited mineralised surfaces with intricate textures, including twisted stalks and globular precipitates (Figure 5A,B). Filamentous structures were often observed interwoven within the mineral matrix, especially those with irregular and straight morphologies (Figure 5C). Twisted stalks were also individually identified throughout the chimney fragment (Figure 5D–F) and were sometimes partially covered by crystalline salt deposits (Figures 5F and S6).

(D, E) Examples of ribbon‐like twisted stalks (red arrows). (F) Twisted stalk partially covered by crystalline salt deposits (see Figure S6 for detailed chemical composition).

EDX analyses showed that twisted stalks were primarily composed of silicon (Si) and Fe, with a lower Si:Fe ratio (higher Fe content relative to Si) compared to the surrounding chimney matrix (Figure S5). Additionally, both regions contained calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), titanium (Ti) and potassium (K), with manganese (Mn) uniquely present in the filaments.

Discussion

The shallow‐water hydrothermal field of Tagoro, located at depths of 88–127 mbsl and just 1.8 km off the southern coast of El Hierro (Canary Islands, Spain), has been intensely monitored since its underwater eruption in October 2011 by the Spanish Institute of Oceanography (IEO‐CSIC). However, microbial community studies during the current post‐eruptive degassing phase remain limited compared to geological and oceanographic research. The role of geothermally reduced compounds in shaping local microbial communities is still being investigated, although recent experimental studies evidence their significant impact on surface seawater microorganisms (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Additionally, studies on seawater, microbial mats, and mineral deposits at Tagoro detected both novel and typical taxa associated with hydrothermal processes (Ferrera et al. 2015; Danovaro et al. 2017; González et al. 2020). However, most of these field studies were based on a limited number of microbiological samples, often derived from only a few samples or even a single sample, highlighting the need for more extensive research. By analysing 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of 37 samples (including seawater, microbial mats, sediments, rocks and chimney fragments), our study improves the characterisation of the epipelagic and benthic prokaryotic community structure and composition at the Tagoro hydrothermal field. Furthermore, our findings provide new insights into the potential biogeochemical processes mediated by prokaryotes shaping this dynamic ecosystem.

Prokaryotic Alpha Diversity

Species Chao1 richness and Inverse Simpson diversity decreased with increasing proximity to hydrothermal vents at Tagoro, in accordance with observations from hydrothermal diffuse flow environments worldwide (Huber et al. 2003; Campbell et al. 2013). This pattern is likely driven by geochemical conditions resulting from mixing between hydrothermal fluids and ambient seawater, which regulates the availability of electron donors (e.g., Fe2+ or reduced sulphur species) and electron acceptors (e.g., O2 or NO2 −), and therefore the range of metabolic processes that can be sustained. Under these conditions, habitats closer to vent influence are expected to favour a smaller number of taxa adapted to reduced and variable redox conditions. This is consistent with experimental evidence from Tagoro showing rapid changes in microbial community composition under small proportions of hydrothermal fluids (2%–16%) (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025).

Surface ocean variability (e.g., tidal and wind forcing, currents, vertical mixing, and weather patterns) may also influence alpha diversity estimates. The Tagoro hydrothermal system exhibits cyclic degassing behaviour (Fraile‐Nuez et al. 2018), while variability in mixed layer depth can intermittently enhance or reduce the coupling between hydrothermal fluids and surface waters (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Persistent anomalies in CO2, pH and reduced chemical species further indicate a sustained but dynamic perturbation of carbonate chemistry and redox structure (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013, 2016), constraining microbial diversity.

In addition, phylogenetic assignments based on average nucleotide identity of 16S rRNA sequences against NCBI sequence databases (Nucleotide collection and 16S ribosomal RNA sequences) revealed that most bacterial populations in the Tagoro environment are genetically distant from formally described species but closely related to sequences previously found in other hydrothermal settings. These findings suggest that shallow‐water hydrothermal systems may harbour a substantial proportion of novel microbial lineages, highlighting the need for improved diversity assessments in these underexplored ecosystems, as current estimates may be biased due to limited microbial data.

Prokaryotic Community Structure and Habitat Specificity

Epipelagic Seawater

Seawater enriched with hydrothermal fluids from Tagoro exhibited a distinct prokaryotic community structure compared to unaffected waters. This pattern is consistent with experimental findings by Pérez‐Barrancos et al. (2025), who reported significant shifts in surface microbial communities following small additions of hydrothermal fluids (2%–16% vent fluid, corresponding to mixing conditions within ~40–95 m from the main hydrothermal source). In our study, enriched and reference seawater samples shared similar dominant taxonomic groups, yet their overall community structure differed significantly. Furthermore, distinct low‐abundance taxa were associated with each environment, suggesting that hydrothermal activity may contribute to niche differentiation even among less abundant microbial populations.

Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria were dominant in hydrothermally enriched seawater samples, consistent with observations from shallow and deep‐sea hydrothermal vents worldwide (Price and Giovannelli 2017; Zeng et al. 2021). Alphaproteobacteria included groups typically prevalent in oceanic bacterioplankton communities, such as the SAR11 Clade I and II (Giovannoni 2017). Among Gammaproteobacteria, the order Pseudomonadales was notably abundant, suggesting that these bacteria benefit significantly from hydrothermal inputs at Tagoro. Families such as SAR86 clade, Pseudomonadaceae and Moraxellaceae contributed substantially to this gammaproteobacterial order. The SAR86 clade, along with photoheterotrophic microbes with genes for proteorhodopsin‐based phototrophy, were in fact previously linked to Tagoro (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). The availability of sunlight, combined with natural sources of inorganic and organic compounds, may provide an additional energy source for these photoheterotrophic taxa, potentially supporting their proliferation in shallow‐water hydrothermal systems.

The archaeal class Nitrososphaeria was also abundant in our seawater samples, with the genus Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus consistently observed. The notable presence of these aerobic ammonia‐oxidising archaea (Santoro et al. 2021) highlights their potential role in nitrogen cycling in hydrothermally enriched seawater, which will be further discussed in Section 4.3.

Several low‐abundance families were also characteristic of the prokaryotic community in seawater affected by the hydrothermal activity of Tagoro, including Oligoflexaceae (Oligoflexia), NS7 marine group (Bacteroidia), OCS116 clade (Alphaproteobacteria) and Desulfosarcinaceae (Desulfobacteria). Notably, the genus SEEP‐SRB1, an anaerobic sulphate‐reducing bacterial group within the Desulfosarcinaceae family previously associated with methane oxidation in deep‐sea environments (Knittel et al. 2003; Schreiber et al. 2010), was detected. SEEP‐SRB1 has been found in methane seeps, hydrothermal vents, and cold seep ecosystems, all characterised by low‐temperature, hydrocarbon‐rich fluid emissions. This group is capable of sulphate reduction coupled to the anaerobic oxidation of methane and other non‐methane hydrocarbons (Boetius et al. 2000; Kleindienst et al. 2012; Vigneron et al. 2017), often in syntrophy with anaerobic methanotrophic archaea (Boetius et al. 2000). The environmental conditions at Tagoro, involving the active discharge of diffuse low‐temperature vent fluids, presumably rich in reduced sulphur compounds (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013), may be consistent with the presence of sulphate‐reducing microbial activity in hydrothermally influenced waters. Additionally, the detection of SEEP‐SRB1 indicates the presence of microbial lineages that have been associated with methane‐rich environments and anaerobic methane oxidation in other settings. However, the occurrence of these taxa alone does not demonstrate methane availability or activity at Tagoro, and direct geochemical measurements would be required to assess the presence and ecological relevance of methane in the area.

Microbial Mats

Zetaproteobacteria, a group of obligate autotrophs capable of oxidising ferrous iron (Fe2+) to ferric iron (Fe3+), were the main prokaryotes in our microbial mat samples. These bacteria were first discovered in hydrothermal vents at Kamaʻehuakanaloa Seamount (formerly Lōʻihi) in Hawaii (Emerson and Moyer 2002), where distinct rust‐coloured Fe oxides suggested high concentrations of CO2 and Fe2+ (see Section 4.4). Notably, they are known to produce highly reactive Fe‐oxyhydroxides that can adsorb or precipitate nutrients and metals (McAllister et al. 2019). In our study, Zetaproteobacteria were exclusively represented by the lithotrophic iron‐oxidising genus Mariprofundus (McAllister et al. 2019). Approximately 22% of Mariprofundus ASVs were identified as strong indicators of microbial mat samples, highlighting their ecological significance in this benthic habitat. The presence of orange precipitates in rock samples, which resemble ferric hydroxide (Fe(OH)3) deposits found in seamounts influenced by active hydrothermal venting (e.g., Boyd and Scott 2001), reinforces our observations of iron‐oxidising bacteria contributing to mineral precipitation (see Section 4.4). Positive Fe2+ anomalies have been reported in hydrothermally influenced waters at Tagoro (Santana‐González et al. 2017). At iron‐dominated vents, Zetaproteobacteria can significantly shape the environment through the production of mineral precipitates and exopolysaccharides (McAllister et al. 2019), which contribute to the formation of microbial mats, impact the local geochemistry, and enhance microbial diversity. Our findings therefore indicate that dissolved iron concentration may be a major factor influencing the prokaryotic community structure at Tagoro, consistent with observations in hydrothermal vents worldwide (Hager et al. 2017; Fullerton et al. 2024). The role of bacterial iron oxidizers in iron cycling and biomineralization processes at the Tagoro submarine volcano is further discussed in Sections 4.3 and 4.4.

Our study also documented the high microbial diversity characterising microbial mats at Tagoro, with 21 bacterial classes, including Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia, identified as indicators of this habitat alongside Zetaproteobacteria. Within Alphaproteobacteria, the Roseobacter clade NAC11‐7 lineage was particularly associated with microbial mats, being the most abundant genus within the family Rhodobacteraceae. This lineage has been frequently linked to algae and algal blooms in surface seawater environments (Buchan et al. 2005; Teeling et al. 2012), suggesting that it thrives at relatively high concentrations of organic matter. Similarly, members of Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidia that are frequently associated with a copiotrophic lifestyle and the utilisation of organic matter, were consistently detected in our microbial mat samples. For instance, the ubiquitous genus Thalassotalea (Colwelliaceae family) and the cosmopolitan OM60/NOR5 clade (Halieaceae family) are well known for their polysaccharide‐degrading abilities (Taylor et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2020; Li et al. 2023). Their prevalence in the Tagoro hydrothermal system suggests that the region maintains high productivity rates, sustaining bacterial groups highly adapted to the utilisation of biogenic organic material inputs and contributing to biogeochemical nutrient cycling (e.g., Buchan et al. 2014). Notably, Rhodobacterales and Flavobacteriales have been shown to increase in response to the accumulation of transparent exopolymer particles (TEPs) produced by phytoplankton (Taylor et al. 2014). These findings align with previous experimental evidence at Tagoro, where hydrothermal inputs fuelled phytoplankton biomass, thereby increasing organic matter availability ready for microbial utilisation (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025).

Furthermore, indicator species analysis revealed that microbial mats at the Tagoro hydrothermal system contained specific bacterial groups linked to sulphur and nitrogen cycles, consistent with previous studies in the area (Danovaro et al. 2017; González et al. 2020). Notably, one of the most remarkable discoveries at Tagoro has been Candidatus Thiolava veneris, a novel bacterium belonging to the order Thiotrichales capable of utilising sulphur and nitrogen compounds, as well as organic and inorganic carbon, forming massive microbial mats of long, white filaments attached to the rock substrate (Danovaro et al. 2017). However, since the 16S rRNA gene sequence of Ca. Thiolava veneris was not retrieved from the metagenomic assembled sequences and the bacterium has not been isolated in pure culture (Danovaro et al. 2017), we were unable to confirm its presence in our samples. Instead, 10 Thiotrichales ASVs were detected with low relative abundances (< 1%) across our benthic samples (data not shown). Notably, our analysis also identified the genus Desulfoconvexum (Desulfobacterota) in microbial mats, which was unexpected given that only one species, D. algidum, has been isolated so far from permanently cold marine sediments in Norway (Könneke et al. 2013). This psychrophilic, sulphate‐reducing bacterium plays a crucial role in reducing sulphate (SO4 2−) to hydrogen sulphide (H2S) in strictly anaerobic environments. Likewise, the microaerophilic genus Sulfurospirillum (Campylobacterota) was strongly associated with microbial mats at Tagoro, including physiologically versatile species capable of sulphur oxidation and reduction, dissimilatory nitrate reduction, indirect Fe3+ reduction, and utilisation of toxic compounds (Goris and Diekert 2016).

These findings indicate that microbial mats at Tagoro harbour a complex microbial network shaped by the unique physical and chemical properties of this shallow‐water hydrothermal system. For instance, González et al. (2020) already discussed that sulphur‐oxidising bacterial mats are typically associated with higher‐temperature vents and chimney conduits, whereas iron‐rich bacterial mats tend to form in lower‐temperature, more diffuse venting areas (Vander Roost et al. 2017). At Tagoro, vent temperatures range from approximately 26°C to 29°C (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024), and while these differences are subtle, they may contribute to variations in microbial mat structure. Previous analyses also described a gradual increase in hydrothermal fluid temperatures from diffuse (~26.8°C) to transitional (~27.2°C) and rocky‐focused (~28.7°C) venting areas (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024), similar to temperature trends observed at the Lucky Strike hydrothermal field (Barreyre et al. 2012). This temperature gradient was particularly evident in the eastern region of the main crater of Tagoro, characterised by extensive areas of diffuse venting and low vent densities, where most of our microbial mat samples were collected. However, a temperature difference of less than 2°C across venting areas is unlikely to exert a direct physiological effect on microbial communities. Rather, we suggest that the observed bacterial heterogeneity across microbial mats is primarily driven by variation in the availability of electron donors and acceptors—controlled by the mixing ratio between hydrothermal fluids and ambient seawater—which determines the range of energetically favourable metabolic strategies at each microhabitat. In this sense, even subtle differences in hydrothermal fluid flow velocity and local hydrodynamics, as documented at Tagoro (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024), could translate into substantial variation in geochemical gradients and thus in microbial community structure. These findings underscore the need for more comprehensive geochemical and bioenergetic studies of the benthic compartment at Tagoro.

Rocks, Sediments and Chimney Fragments

Several bacterial taxa frequently associated with ammonia oxidation were also found in rocks, sediments and chimneys influenced by the hydrothermal activity at Tagoro, in addition to the archaea Nitrososphaeria. For instance, the order Nitrosococcales was identified in rocks and sediments, while Planctomycetales and Phycisphaerales were more abundant in chimney fragments. Notably, the genus SM1A02, a potential anammox bacterium of the order Phycisphaerales, was significantly associated with chimneys at the Tagoro hydrothermal field. Planctomycetes have previously been linked to iron hydroxide deposits at deep‐sea vents in the southwestern Pacific, where SM1A02 was also detected (Storesund et al. 2018). These findings provide evidence of diverse ammonia oxidation processes at Tagoro, including aerobic ammonia oxidation by archaea (such as Nitrososphaeria) and bacteria (Nitrosococcales), as well as anaerobic ammonium oxidation by anammox bacteria (Planctomycetales and Phycisphaerales) in low‐oxygen environments like hydrothermal vent chimneys.

Sediment samples were particularly characterised by strictly anaerobic sulphate‐reducing bacteria, such as members of Thermodesulfovibrionia and Desulfobulbia commonly isolated from hot springs. Thermodesulfovibrionia was among the most abundant taxa in the sediments from Tagoro, with several ASVs identified as significant indicators of sediment habitats influenced by hydrothermal activity. This class mostly encompasses thermophilic sulphate reducers that also oxidise organic compounds or hydrogen, producing hydrogen sulphide (H2S) as by‐product (e.g., Henry et al. 1994; Haouari et al. 2008). Even though they remain uncultured, these bacteria have gained increasing interest not only in microbial ecology but also in biotechnology due to their role in sulphur cycling and their potential applications in bioenergy or bioremediation. Notably, our study highlights Thermodesulfovibrionia as an example of yet undescribed bacterial diversity in this shallow‐water hydrothermal field. About 19 ASVs were classified within this class, but only one was identified beyond the class level as the genus Thermodesulfovibrio. Consistently, most strains within this genus have been isolated from hot springs in vent fluids, microbial mats and sediments (e.g., Henry et al. 1994; Sonne‐Hansen and Ahring 1999; Haouari et al. 2008; Sekiguchi et al. 2008). These findings indicate that active hydrothermal fluid entrainment, rich in sulphur compounds, may be prevalent in the benthic habitats of Tagoro and highlight the need for further research on sulphate availability on the area.

In addition to the sulphate‐reducing bacteria detected in sediments, chimney samples also exhibited a notable presence of sulphur‐oxidising bacteria, like the gammaproteobacterial order Chromatiales (Imhoff 2014). The genus Candidatus Thiobios, a chemolithoautotrophic sulphide‐oxidising ectosymbiont, was particularly abundant within this order. This genus is typically associated with Zoothamnium niveum, a sessile, giant, colonial marine ciliate from sulphide‐rich habitats that is consistently covered with chemoautotrophic, sulphide‐oxidising bacteria (Rinke et al. 2006). These bacteria, including Candidatus Thiobios, are responsible for the brilliant white colour of Z. niveum. These observations are consistent with potential sulphur‐rich conditions previously reported in hydrothermal fluids and vent‐proximal environments at Tagoro (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2013), which may promote symbiotic relationships between bacteria and eukaryotes. This finding underscores Tagoro as a hotspot for studying microbial symbiosis in sulphur‐rich environments and its potential contribution to marine eukaryotic evolution.

Furthermore, aerobic methane‐oxidising bacteria commonly found at shallow submarine hot springs were also detected in sediments and chimney samples, especially members of Methylococcales (e.g., Hirayama et al. 2013). This is consistent with previous studies in Tagoro that identified methane‐related microbes (methanogenic archaea and methanotrophic archaea and bacteria) in mineralised samples (González et al. 2020), emphasising the need for direct methane measurements in the area. The co‐occurrence of sulphate‐reducing bacteria and microbes previously associated with methane cycling may indicate potential microbial mutualism, similar to the syntrophic relationships observed in seawater, although such interactions at Tagoro would require functional and geochemical evidence.

The benthic habitats of Tagoro also harbour highly diverse microbial assemblages capable of expressing a wide variety of metabolic pathways. One such group is Anaerolineae bacteria, which can oxidise a broad range of carbon compounds, from simple sugars to complex organic molecules, and express both fermentation and carbon fixation pathways (Payne et al. 2024). Among Anaerolineae, the heterotrophic genus Thermomarinilinea was significantly associated with hydrothermally influenced sediments at Tagoro. The only cultured species within this genus was isolated from the Taketomi submarine hot spring field in Japan, where it relies on fermentation in anaerobic, high‐temperature (37°C–65°C), low‐pH (5.5–7.3) environments (Nunoura et al. 2013), similar to those conditions observed in Tagoro sediments.

Other abundant thermophilic, strictly anaerobic microbes involved in organic matter utilisation, such as Thermoanaerobaculia, were also detected, especially in sediments and chimneys at Tagoro. Thermoanaerobaculia strains are typically found in hot springs and exhibit great metabolic flexibility, from carbohydrate fermentation to chemolithoautotrophy (Stackebrandt 2014). In our study, Subgroup 10 accounted for most of Thermoanaerobaculia abundance and was strongly linked to hydrothermal vent chimneys. This supports previous observations of their adaptation to the extreme temperature and chemical conditions of vent chimneys, as the first cultivated representative of Subgroup 10 was an obligate anaerobe, capable of fermentative growth on complex proteinaceous substances, isolated from a deep‐sea hydrothermal vent chimney at the Hatoma Knoll in Japan (Izumi et al. 2012). In contrast, Subgroup 23 was more notably associated with sediment samples, suggesting a preference for slightly cooler or less hydrothermally impacted environments compared to the active chimneys. However, environmental sequences of Subgroup 23 have been found not only in marine sediments but also in hot spring microbial mats, and even freshwater hot springs, where the first cultivated member of this subgroup was isolated (Losey et al. 2013; Dedysh and Yilmaz 2018).

Altogether, these findings highlight the metabolic diversity of prokaryotic communities in the hydrothermal ecosystem of Tagoro, with specialised prokaryotes capable of utilising inorganic and/or organic compounds in habitats influenced by hydrothermal activity alongside cosmopolitan taxa. Furthermore, the presence of different microbial groups across these habitats emphasises the adaptability of these microbes to varying environmental conditions, making them key players in local biogeochemical processes.

Iron, Nitrogen, Sulphur and Methane Compounds as Main Sources of Energy

Our study revealed a remarkable presence of prokaryotes associated with iron, nitrogen, sulphur and methane cycling across the Tagoro shallow‐water hydrothermal field, expanding on the previous observations in the area and consistent with the broad range of redox‐active compounds documented in Tagoro hydrothermal fluids (González‐Vega et al. 2020; Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024). The co‐occurrence of microorganisms with contrasting metabolic strategies—including iron oxidizers, ammonia oxidizers, sulphate reducers, and potential methanotrophs—is a hallmark of hydrothermally influenced environments where the simultaneous availability of multiple reduced compounds and electron acceptors sustains diverse metabolic niches (Dick 2019; Price and Giovannelli 2017). The diverse microbial assemblages identified here are therefore consistent with the geochemical gradients generated by the mixing of hydrothermal fluids with oxygenated ambient seawater, as has been described in comparable shallow‐water hydrothermal systems (Gomez‐Saez et al. 2017).

Bacterial taxa involved in iron cycling were consistently identified at Tagoro, with a notable abundance of iron‐oxidising bacteria within the Zetaproteobacteria class. These chemolithoautotrophic bacteria were particularly dominant in hydrothermally influenced benthic habitats, consistent with their known ecological versality and widespread distribution in marine environments (McAllister et al. 2019). Zetaproteobacteria typically inhabit environments characterised by brackish to hypersaline water, abundant Fe2+ and predominantly micro‐oxic conditions. Therefore, the periodic release of shallow‐water vent fluids enriched in CO2 and Fe2+ (Santana‐Casiano et al. 2016; Santana‐González et al. 2017) may favour the proliferation of iron microbial mats and the colonisation of Fe‐rich mineral deposits at Tagoro.

Given that these conditions are widespread and found globally in diverse habitats, it is thought that the diversification and evolution of Zetaproteobacteria must be prominent. However, Zetaproteobacteria remain largely uncultivated, with only a small fraction of environmental sequences represented by cultured isolates (McAllister et al. 2018). In our study, Zetaproteobacteria sequences were exclusively assigned to the genus Mariprofundus within the family Mariprofundaceae. This group exhibited high ASV richness, with most ASVs closely related to cultured species such as M. ferrinatatus, M. ferrooxydans , M. ferrooxydans PV‐1, M. micogutta and M. aestuarium. Only one ASV was classified at the species level as M. micogutta, a species originally isolated from deep‐sea sediments in the hydrothermal field of Bayonnaise knoll in Japan (Makita et al. 2017). The high relative abundance of M. micogutta in Tagoro chimney fragments aligns with its known preference for microaerophilic, Fe2+‐rich environments at temperatures around 25°C and pH 6.4.

Nevertheless, three ASVs, consistently detected across all of our explored hydrothermal habitats, accounted for most of the relative abundance of Mariprofundus sequences. These ASVs were genetically similar to M. ferrooxydans , M. ferrooxydans PV‐1, M. micogutta and M. aestuarium. M. aestuarium and M. ferrinatatus were first identified in redox‐stratified waters of Chesapeake Bay, the largest estuary in the United States (Chiu et al. 2017). Chiu et al. (2017) reported that these species produce shorter filaments to avoid cell encrustation and, particularly CP strains, possess genes associated with biofilm formation, an adaptation that likely facilitates particle colonisation to pelagic Zetaproteobacteria. This may explain why the most abundant Mariprofundus ASV identified in our study as a significant indicator taxon of microbial mats in Tagoro, is closely related to M. ferrinatatus. Such biofilm‐forming traits could provide an advantage in benthic habitats of Tagoro, where notable rates of particle suspension and flux occur (Martín‐Díaz et al. 2024).

Phylogenetic analyses also revealed a consistent presence of ammonia‐oxidising archaea in seawater samples collected both directly from hydrothermal vents and throughout the water column at Tagoro. The detection of Nitrososphaeria, particularly members of the family Nitrosopumilaceae, in both pelagic and benthic habitats supports the hypothesis that ammonium (NH4 +) is the primary form of dissolved inorganic nitrogen released at Tagoro (González‐Vega et al. 2020). However, this ammonium may be rapidly oxidised to nitrite and nitrate by these archaea, explaining the enhanced concentrations of nitrate observed in the vicinity of Tagoro as compared to the surrounding waters. The release of NH4 + as a primary hydrothermal end‐product is a distinctive feature of the Tagoro system. While vent fluids at Kamaʻehuakanaloa Seamount (Hawaii) are also enriched in NH4 +, alongside CO2, CH4, Fe and Mn (Singer et al. 2013), the dominant microbial response at that system is iron oxidation by Zetaproteobacteria, with nitrogen cycling linked primarily to iron‐coupled denitrification and multi‐source nitrogen assimilation by iron oxidizers rather than to a nitrification pathway prominently run by Ammonia Oxidizing Archaea (Singer et al. 2013; Field et al. 2014). The strong signal of Nitrososphaeria throughout the Tagoro water column and benthic habitats therefore suggests that NH4 + at Tagoro is actively channelled into nitrification, a rare nitrogen cycling trajectory for a hydrothermal system. The substantial enrichment of NH4 + at Tagoro, reaching concentrations up to 1.97 μmol kg−1 in vent fluids compared to ~0.02 μmol kg−1 in background seawater (González‐Vega et al. 2020), likely reflects the particular geology and geothermal activity of this young volcanic system, in which high‐temperature alteration of organic‐rich marine sediments or volcanic rocks may generate ammonium. This elevated ammonium supply provides a significant and relatively unusual energy and nitrogen source that fuels the diverse nitrifying microbial community documented in our study. Future direct measurements of NH4 + in vent fluids across multiple sampling campaigns would substantially strengthen our understanding of nitrogen cycling at Tagoro and its role in supporting microbial diversity in this system.

Moreover, we observed habitat‐specific patterns in the distribution of Nitrosopumilaceae genera across Tagoro. Candidatus Nitrosopumilus and Cenarchaeum were more prevalent in hydrothermally influenced benthic habitats, while Candidatus Nitrosopelagicus and unclassified Nitrosopumilaceae sequences were consistently observed in epipelagic waters unaffected by hydrothermal discharge. These distinctions likely reflect adaptations to local physiochemical conditions. For instance, Ca. Nitrosopelagicus is known to dominate ammonia oxidation in epipelagic and upper mesopelagic waters of low and mid‐latitude open oceans, where it correlates with nitrification rates. Its low biomass nitrogen requirements, facilitated by a small genome and a higher molar growth efficiency, likely contribute to their ecological success in open oceans (Santoro et al. 2015). In contrast, Ca. Nitrosopumilus exhibits greater tolerance to elevated ammonium and nitrite concentrations, as well as higher temperatures and growth rates, factors that may provide a competitive advantage in the dynamic, warm, nutrient‐rich benthic habitats near hydrothermal vents.

In addition to iron and nitrogen cycling, our data revealed the presence of microbial taxa previously associated with methane and one‐carbon compound metabolisms within the Tagoro hydrothermal system, particularly in chimney and sediment habitats. Aerobic methane‐oxidising bacteria from the family Methylomonadaceae were the only methanotrophs identified, with 5 genera detected, including Methylomonas and Methylomarinum, which have cultured representatives (e.g., Boden et al. 2011; Ogiso et al. 2012; Hirayama et al. 2013).

Methylomarinum was primarily detected in rock, sediment, and hydrothermally influenced seawater samples, whereas other methylotrophic taxa, such as Marine Methylotrophic Group 2 and pItb‐vmat‐59, were mainly observed in microbial mats. However, Methylomarinum vadi, the only described species within the genus, was first isolated from a microbial mat in a shallow hydrothermal system off Okinawa, Japan (Hirayama et al. 2013). These observations highlight the diversity of methane‐related microbes in hydrothermal environments. In contrast, the uncultured clade IheB2‐23 was predominantly found in chimney samples, consistent with previous reports of its presence in methane seeps and plumes off the coast of Taiwan, in the northern South China Sea (Mau et al. 2020).

Previous studies at Tagoro also detected methanogenic archaea such as Methanosarcina sp. and Methanoccoides sp. in bedrock samples (González et al. 2020), suggesting a possible biogenic source of methane resulting from anaerobic microbial activity. This hypothesis aligns with our findings of abundant microbes capable of utilising organic matter, indicating that sediments at Tagoro are rich in organic material and thus underscore the high biological productivity at the hydrothermal system (Pérez‐Barrancos et al. 2025). Sulphur‐cycling microbes, including sulphate‐reducing and sulphur‐oxidising bacteria, were also detected in our study, and some likely rely on organic matter degradation and/or syntrophic associations (discussed in Section 4.2). Still, the presence, origin and metabolic fate of such compounds within the Tagoro hydrothermal system remain unresolved and require future geochemical and microbial research.

The geochemistry of shallow‐water hydrothermal systems therefore supports diverse microbial taxa with distinct metabolic strategies (Price and Giovannelli 2017), as demonstrated in our study. In contrast to deep‐sea vents, which predominantly rely on chemosynthesis, shallow‐water vents also exhibit phototrophic primary production. Recent studies indicate that chemoautotrophy plays a significant role in primary production within hydrothermally influenced coastal environments (Gomez‐Saez et al. 2017). The contribution of chemosynthesis to total primary production at shallow‐water vents has been reported to range from 1% to 50% (Tarasov et al. 1999), with experimental assessments suggesting even higher contributions (Gomez‐Saez et al. 2017). These findings highlight the potential importance of vent microorganisms in producing significant amounts of newly synthesized organic matter, using the energy derived from the oxidation of chemical compounds such as H2S, CH4 or Fe2+. Similar to their deep‐sea counterparts, these organic compounds may sustain higher trophic levels (Dick 2019). Given its potential impact on primary production, further research quantifying chemosynthesis rates in shallow‐water hydrothermal environments is essential to better constrain its ecological implications.

Bacterial Biomineralization

The identification of diverse biogenic structures, including twisted stalks and globular precipitates, in a chimney fragment collected in 2017 provides direct evidence of ongoing microbial Fe biomineralization in the active hydrothermal field of Tagoro. Twisted stalks are a characteristic vestige of Mariprofundus ferrooxydans (Emerson et al. 2007; Singer et al. 2013; McAllister et al. 2019), a Zetaproteobacterium that regulates Fe mineral growth near the cell surface while preventing encrustation (Chan et al. 2011). These structures also anchor cells to surfaces, promoting bacterial motility within Fe2+ and O2 gradients (Krepski et al. 2013), which may facilitate their proliferation in hydrothermal environments. Sheaths, another biomineralization structure, have also been linked to Zetaproteobacteria members and may function similarly to stalks (Fleming et al. 2013; Chan et al. 2016).

Such iron‐rich conditions are consistent with previously reported Fe2+ anomalies in hydrothermally influenced waters at Tagoro (Santana‐González et al. 2017). Our microbial analyses consistently identified Zetaproteobacteria as one of the most abundant taxa in microbial mat, rock, and sediment samples from active hydrothermal areas over 5 years (2017–2022) (see Section 4.2). These findings support the role of Zetaproteobacteria in Fe‐oxyhydroxide precipitation within this shallow‐water hydrothermal system. This contrasts with a 2014 survey, which reported a low relative abundance of Zetaproteobacteria alongside a high prevalence of twisted stalks and empty sheaths in mineralised samples from the summit and secondary vents of Tagoro (González et al. 2020). González et al. (2020) suggested that biogenic Fe oxidation may have occurred during past hydrothermal events and that hydrothermal fluid emissions through the sediments had ceased at the analysed locations. However, the presence of Fe‐rich precipitates on oceanographic instruments deployed at the site and the detection of iron‐oxidising microbial DNA suggest that iron‐related processes remain active in the system. In this context, Fe‐rich deposits in the Tagoro hydrothermal field are likely influenced by microbial activity, consistent with observations from diffuse low‐temperature hydrothermal vents worldwide (e.g., Johannessen et al. 2017).

Conclusions

Our study shows that hydrothermal venting at the Tagoro submarine volcano is associated with clear shifts in microbial community structure and diversity across epipelagic and benthic prokaryotic assemblages. Prokaryotic richness and diversity consistently decreased with increasing proximity to hydrothermal vent sources. Meanwhile, novel taxa were prevalent across all hydrothermally influenced habitats, unveiling an extensive, unexplored microbial diversity in the vicinity of this shallow submarine hydrothermal system. Closest GenBank relatives matched at taxonomic levels from order to genus, highlighting the vast unknown microbial diversity and the strong potential for discovering new metabolic capabilities in these unique hydrothermal areas. In hydrothermally enriched epipelagic seawater, Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria were dominant. Both pelagic and benthic compartments harboured photoautotrophic and chemoautotrophic populations, providing further evidence that sufficient light penetrates from the sea surface to the main crater floor at 127 m depth. Microbial mats were characterised by iron‐oxidising Zetaproteobacteria, which likely contribute to Fe‐oxyhydroxide precipitation. In benthic habitats (volcanic rocks, sediments, and chimneys), nitrogen‐ and sulphur‐cycling microbes, including ammonia‐oxidising archaea Nitrososphaeria, were prevalent. This supports previous hypotheses suggesting that nitrogen at Tagoro is emitted as hydrothermal ammonium and is rapidly oxidised to nitrite and nitrate by microbes, explaining the high nitrate concentrations reported in the area. Sediments were also enriched with bacteria involved in organic matter utilisation, indicating high organic availability and supporting earlier observations of increased productivity in the Tagoro marine ecosystem. Together, these findings indicate the involvement of prokaryotic communities in local biogeochemical processes within this unique environment and provide preliminary evidence that yet unexplored carbon and energy sources, such as methane, may be important in the area. Complex microbial interactions were also observed, including potential syntropic relationships between microbial groups and possible symbiotic associations with benthic microfauna. Overall, the Tagoro submarine volcano stands as a valuable natural laboratory for studying microbial diversity, biogeochemical cycling and intricate microbial relationships in underexplored shallow‐water hydrothermal systems.
