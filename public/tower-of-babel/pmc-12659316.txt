Background

Aortic dissection (AD) is a severe cardiovascular disease characterized by a tear in the intimal layer of the aorta, leading to the separation of the aortic wall [1]. In China, the incidence of AD is approximately 2.8 cases per 100,000 persons per year [2]. Early rapid diagnosis and appropriate treatment are crucial for the survival of AD patients [3]. This paper reports a case of giant syphilitic Stanford type A aortic dissection presenting initially with facial swelling, detailing its clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, imaging studies, and surgical management. The hospital’s ethics committee approved the presentation of the case. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of his case. A literature review was also performed to enhance the understanding of this condition.

Case report

A 62-year-old male presented with a 2-week history of facial swelling. He had a history of hypertension but had not been adherent to treatments. There was no family history available, and his sexual history was vague. The patient had no history of interventions. On admission, his vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.3 °C, heart rate 96 beats per minute, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute, blood pressure 148/103 mmHg, and SPO2 92%−96% on 2-L/min nasal oxygen. He appeared acutely ill with marked facial swelling and prominent jugular venous distention. Lung auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds bilaterally without significant crackles or wheezes. There was no lower limb edema.

Initial laboratory tests, including complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, electrolytes, immunological antibodies, and tumor markers, showed no significant abnormalities. Serological tests for syphilis were positive: the Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA) was positive, and the syphilis rapid plasma reagin test (TRUST) was positive at 1:32 dilution. Initial chest CT at a local hospital revealed a giant aneurysmal dilation at the beginning of the ascending aorta measuring approximately 5.1 × 5.1 cm, with an arcuate filling defect around it. There was severe compression and narrowing of the adjacent right pulmonary artery, which was poorly visualized. The patient was immediately transferred to our hospital for further evaluation, where CT angiography of the chest and abdomen confirmed the diagnosis of aortic dissection (Fig. 1).

There is a prominent dissecting aortic aneurysm at the aortic root, with ulcer formation observed in the aortic arch and descending aorta (a, b). The dissecting aortic aneurysm causes significant compression of the superior vena cava and the right pulmonary artery (c). Measurements show the maximum diameter is 87.7 millimetres, and the maximum circumference is 238.6 millimetres (d).

Two days after admission, considering the patient’s condition of massive ascending aortic dissection/hematoma with no abnormalities noted in the aortic valve and Valsalva sinuses but with diffuse ulceration in the aortic arch, the selected surgical approach involved ascending aortic replacement combined with total arch replacement and elephant trunk stent implantation. Prior to surgery, the superior vena cava pressure was approximately 65 mmHg. Intraoperatively, a massive dilation of the ascending aorta was observed, compressing the surrounding tissues and significantly impacting the superior vena cava. Upon opening the aorta, a roughly 5 × 3 cm oval-shaped tear was noted on the convex side of the aorta’s major curve (Fig. 2), characterized by rounded edges suggesting a chronic nature. There was extensive thrombus formation within the tear, which was carefully dissected and removed intact (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, the outer membrane of the original aortic dissection was closely adhering to the surrounding tissues, making separation difficult and risking iatrogenic damage to the adjacent blood vessels. Finally, the outer wall of the original aorta was preserved on the lateral side to prevent iatrogenic injury. During surgery, a 30-mm branch stent and 30-mm descending aortic branch stent were used, with femoral artery cannulation and selective cerebral perfusion via the unilateral innominate artery. The patient underwent 223 min of cardiopulmonary bypass, 136 min of aortic clamping, and 35 min of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest with selective brain perfusion. However, due to the adhesion of the surrounding tissue, the tumor was not completely removed, and the aortic wall near the vena cava was left out. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous monitoring and care. During anesthesia recovery, central venous pressure fluctuated between 15 and 19 mmHg under appropriate volume status. The patient was extubated 5 h after surgery and transferred to a general ward the following day. On the second day post-surgery, the patient began ambulating and experienced a smooth recovery. A follow-up thoracic aorta CTA and three-dimensional reconstruction were performed 1 week after surgery (Fig. 4b-c). The patient was discharged in a stable condition. A telephone follow-up at 3 months revealed no specific discomfort or facial swelling. CTA of thoracic aorta and three-dimensional reconstruction (Fig. 4e-f) were re-examined 6 months after surgery, and no special clinical symptoms such as facial swelling appeared again.

Discussion and conclusion

This paper reported a case of syphilitic aortic dissection with facial swelling as the initial symptom. This case underscores the rarity of syphilitic aortic dissection presenting as facial swelling. Timely recognition and individualized treatment strategies can achieve favorable outcomes.

An early study by Hirst et al. [4] demonstrated in the 1950 s a significant reduction in mortality among patients with aortic dissection 2 weeks after onset, guiding current medical and surgical treatment strategies for aortic dissection. Accurate collection of disease onset time is crucial for treatment decisions. Aortic dissection caused by syphilis often occurs in late-stage syphilis, typically within 5 years after infection, but its course shows considerable heterogeneity. In this case, the patient did not present with typical acute tearing chest or back pain characteristic of aortic dissection onset but initially presented with facial swelling and cough, highlighting the importance of determining the initial onset time for assessing the severity of aortic dissection. Syphilitic aortic dissection remains a rare entity, especially in the antibiotic era. Still, the recent cases suggest a long disease course without the characteristic tearing pain of acute aortic dissection onset [5–8], as observed here. Indeed, the presentation symptoms included shortness of breath [5], stroke [7], weakness and abdominal pain [8], or chest pain for 1 week [6], but the typical acute onset symptoms can still be observed in some cases [9–11].

The latest pathological perspectives suggest that chronic aortic dissection manifests as progressive intimal tear with floating thrombus, gradual fibrosis, thickening, natural remodeling of the aortic wall, and gradual calcification of the false lumen wall [12, 13]. Considering the patient’s history of syphilis, the aortic dissection, in this case, aligns with the characteristic features of syphilitic aortitis. Chronic aortic dissection or aneurysm is a common cardiovascular manifestation of cardiovascular syphilis [14]. The case reported here displayed rounded tear edges and a thrombus that suggested chronic aortic dissection. Treponema pallidum often enters the nutrient vessels of the aortic root through the mediastinal pulmonary lymphatic tissue, and the ascending aorta, which has abundant lymphatic and nutrient vessels, is frequently affected first [15]. Concurrent involvement of other vascular sites (such as coronary arteries) or compression by dissection/aneurysm can lead to varying symptoms and/or signs of syphilitic arteritis [15]. The most frequent symptom in patients with syphilitic aortic dissection/arteritis is long-standing chronic atypical chest pain, sometimes indistinguishable from angina pectoris [11, 15]. These patients may present with non-specific clinical symptoms related to respiratory distress, dizziness, heart failure, and neurological manifestations of neurosyphilis [16, 17], all of which lack specificity for aortic dissection. These clinical manifestations and the often-obscure history of sexually transmitted diseases contribute to potential delays in the diagnosis and management of this condition at the primary care level.

Individualized treatment is paramount in managing aortic diseases. In previous case reports, most surgeons have favored the Bentall procedure due to the frequent involvement of the aortic valve [10, 11, 18]. In recent years, aortic valve-sparing procedures have gained increasing attention, but whether valve-sparing techniques increase the risk of late recurrence in patients with syphilitic lesions remains unknown. In this case, the patient did not present with involvement of the aortic valve or coronary arteries but exhibited ulcerative lesions in the aortic arch. Therefore, surgeons opted for ascending aortic replacement combined with total arch replacement and elephant trunk stent implantation to address the vascular lesion. Additionally, using branched stents reduced the need for anastomosis with the left subclavian artery, partially shortening the duration of surgery. Regarding the patient’s recovery during hospitalization, the treatment outcome was satisfactory, but long-term effects require further observation.

In conclusion, this case underscores the rarity of syphilitic aortic dissection presenting initially with facial swelling. It is important to consider the possibility of chronic aortic dissection presenting with atypical symptoms. Testing for syphilis is important for management. Timely recognition and implementation of individualized treatment strategies can achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes, enhancing understanding of this disease and mitigating delays in diagnosis and treatment.
