Mutual perception among conspecific seeds enhances germination synchrony
Authors: Shengtianzi Dong, Tiantian Qin, Hongyang Chen, Hanyue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCooperation among conspecific seeds, which influences germination timing and quantity, can enhance the interspecific competitive ability and environmental adaptability of invasive plants. However, the specific underlying mechanisms require further investigation. This study investigates whether a chemical mechanism of mutual perception exists among seeds of the invasive plant Ambrosia trifida L. and its potential impact on seed germination.ResultsWe found that increased seed density and higher concentrations of seed extracts significantly promoted germination rates and reduced germination time in Ambrosia trifida L. seeds. Aggregated seeds germinated earlier and more synchronously than isolated seeds, indicating the presence of seed-to-seed communication mediated by chemical signals. Widely targeted metabolomic analysis identified 527 chemical compounds in the seed extracts, with 150 involved in key metabolic pathways. Notably, secondary metabolites in the shikimates and phenylpropanoids class were enriched, particularly angelicin. Quantitative analysis confirmed that angelicin significantly enhanced germination synchronicity when applied at various concentrations.ConclusionsOur research findings indicate that Ambrosia trifida seeds communicate through secondary metabolites, with angelicin playing a key role in promoting synchronized germination. This chemical communication among conspecific seeds facilitates the rapid and uniform establishment of populations. Understanding this mechanism provides new insights into plant ecology and offers potential strategies for managing invasive species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02468-5.

Background

Intraspecific cooperation, a fundamental ecological process, significantly enhances population fitness and resilience in fluctuating environments [5, 18, 33]. Such cooperative behaviors often rely on sophisticated communication systems, where individuals exchange information through chemical, visual, or physical cues [10, 44]. In the plant kingdom, chemical signaling is a primary mode of communication, influencing processes from defense against herbivores to resource allocation among kin [8, 19]. These interactions, mediated by chemical signals, can structure plant communities, facilitate kin selection, and ultimately determine the competitive success of a species within its ecosystem [6, 32].

Despite extensive research on cooperation among adult plants, the dynamics of such interactions at the seed stage remain largely unexplored [15, 39]. This represents a significant research gap, as the seed stage is a critical bottleneck in the life cycle of many plants, particularly for annual and invasive species [30, 41, 42]. Germination timing and synchrony are pivotal traits that dictate the initial success of a population [2, 4]. Synchronized germination, in particular, enables a nascent population to rapidly monopolize resources and preemptively outcompete neighboring species, a strategy that is especially advantageous for invasive plants aiming to establish a foothold in new environments [43]. The underlying mechanisms that orchestrate this critical synchronization, however, are not well understood.

A plausible mechanism for achieving coordinated germination is through density-dependent chemical signaling among conspecific seeds, a phenomenon analogous to quorum sensing in microorganisms [29]. This process, which we term “mutual perception,” involves seeds releasing chemical cues into the environment that signal the presence and density of neighbors [35]. When a critical threshold of these chemical signals is reached, it could trigger a synchronized germination response across the seed cohort. This strategy would ensure that germination occurs only when the population density is sufficient to mount a successful establishment. Therefore, we posit a central hypothesis: conspecific seeds engage in chemical communication via secreted secondary metabolites, which in turn orchestrates synchronized germination to enhance population establishment. Testing this hypothesis could reveal a novel mechanism underpinning the invasion success of aggressive plant species.

To investigate this hypothesis, we selected the highly invasive annual plant, Ambrosia trifida L. as our model organism. Native to North America, Ambrosia trifida has successfully invaded numerous regions worldwide, causing significant agricultural losses and public health issues due to its allergenic pollen [12, 24, 25, 28]. A key characteristic contributing to its invasiveness is its ability to form dense, fast-growing monocultures that outcompete native flora and crops [36, 40]. This rapid dominance is predicated on the mass synchronous germination of its seeds from the soil seed bank. The pronounced ecological impact and reliance on synchronized germination make Ambrosia trifida an ideal system to explore the mechanisms of seed-to-seed chemical communication.

This study aims to dissect the chemical basis of mutual perception among Ambrosia trifida seeds and its functional consequences for germination. We address the following specific research questions: (1) Does the germination timing and synchrony in Ambrosia trifida depend on seed density and exposure to conspecific chemical exudates? (2) What are the specific chemical compounds present in seed exudates that may mediate this communication? (3) Can we identify and quantify the key signaling molecules responsible for promoting germination synchrony? By answering these questions, we seek to provide direct evidence for chemically mediated germination synchrony in an invasive plant and offer new insights into plant ecology and potential avenues for invasive species management.

Results

Investigating seed-to-seed communication

Ambrosia trifida seeds exhibit a mutual perception mechanism, enabling rapid and synchronized germination through the release of signaling substances. This phenomenon was validated by a two-way ANOVA (Table 1), which revealed highly significant main effects for both seed density and extract concentration (P < 0.001 for both). Compared to isolated seeds (1 seed/dish), high-density aggregation (25 seeds/dish) not only significantly increased the total germination rate (77.0% vs. 29.1%, Fig. 1a) but also substantially shortened the time to first germination (meaning 6.1 days vs. 14.8 days, Fig. 1b) and the overall duration of germination. Crucially, a significant interaction effect between density and concentration was observed for both germination rate and time to first germination. Simple effects analysis (Table 2) indicated that high-density conditions enhanced seed sensitivity to the extract, with a lower concentration (T3) being sufficient to significantly promote germination, whereas the higher T4 concentration was required under low-density conditions. Furthermore, high density induced a “ceiling effect” on the germination-accelerating properties of the extract. Under high extract concentrations, aggregated seeds germinated 6 days earlier than their control group, while isolated seeds germinated 10 days earlier, suggesting that the potential for further acceleration was diminished when germination was already rapid. In contrast, there was no interaction effect on the duration of germination (P = 0.413); the effects of density and concentration were independent and additive. That is, as extract concentration increased, seeds under both density treatments exhibited a shorter, more synchronous germination period (Fig. 1c, d). Screening of chemical substances involved in seed-to-seed communication based on widely-targeted metabolomics

Metabolomic analysis of Ambrosia trifida seed extract identified 527 chemical substances, 150 of which were involved in KEGG pathway analysis, spanning 7 categories and ultimately enriching 55 pathways. To verify the mutual perception effect during seed germination, we divided the Ambrosia trifida seed extract into two treatment groups: Group A (5 g 100 ml−1) and Group B (25 g 100 ml−1), and conducted widely targeted metabolomic analysis, with Group A as the control and Group B as the experimental group. Widely targeted metabolomic detection revealed that Groups A and B shared 439 chemical substances, while 527 total substances were detected in Group B (Additional file 1: Table 1).

After preprocessing, we normalized all samples and created a clustering heatmap (Fig. 2a) for visual analysis. In the metabolomic data, Group B samples with higher extract concentrations showed significantly higher signal intensity than Group A samples with lower concentrations. Additionally, by combining univariate and multivariate statistical analyses, we identified differential metabolites. For Group A versus Group B, 334 differential metabolites were identified, with 330 upregulated. A volcano plot was used to visualize these differential metabolites (Fig. 2b). This analysis indicates that higher extract concentrations release more signaling molecules, enhancing mutual perception during seed germination. To further explore the biological functions of the chemical compounds in Ambrosia trifida seed extract, we conducted functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG pathway database. The KEGG database integrates a wide array of metabolic pathways and their corresponding regulatory proteins, based on gene and genome function information and metabolic reactions. In this study, 150 substances were included in the KEGG pathway analysis (Additional file 2: Table 2a), categorized into seven major groups: amino acids and peptides (10.7%), polyketides (5.3%), shikimates and phenylpropanoids (36%), alkaloids (22.7%), carbohydrates (6%), terpenoids (6%), and fatty acids (13.3%). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these substances identified 55 significantly enriched metabolic pathways Additional file 2: Table 2b). These enriched pathways encompass various aspects of plant secondary and primary metabolism, providing valuable insights into the chemical composition and biological functions of the Ambrosia trifida seed extract.

Discussion

Chemical communication among seeds promotes germination synchronicity

The conventional paradigm of plant chemical ecology has predominantly focused on interspecific interactions, particularly the allelopathic suppression of competitors [22, 38]. Our findings challenge this view by providing compelling evidence for sophisticated intraspecific chemical communication among Ambrosia trifida seeds. We demonstrate that seed density positively correlates with both the rate and synchronicity of germination, a phenomenon mediated by secreted secondary metabolites. This moves beyond the simple observation of density-dependent effects [35] by identifying angelicin, a coumarin, as a key signaling molecule. This discovery suggests a mechanism akin to quorum sensing in microorganisms, where individuals collectively regulate group behavior upon reaching a critical population density [26, 37]. In the context of seed ecology, we propose this as a form of “cooperative germination.”

This cooperative strategy likely confers significant evolutionary advantages. Synchronous germination allows the nascent Ambrosia trifida population to rapidly preempt resources such as light, water, and nutrients, effectively creating a “monoculture patch” that suppresses later-arriving competitors [16, 34]. Furthermore, by ensuring simultaneous emergence, the population may overcome the early-stage demographic challenges posed by the Allee effect, where individual fitness is reduced at low population densities due to factors like inefficient pollination or increased per-capita predation risk [1, 27]. This self-perception and coordinated response mechanism, therefore, represents a powerful strategy for successful population establishment and subsequent invasion, transforming our understanding of seed bank dynamics from a collection of independent individuals to an interactive, communicative system.

Our metabolomic analysis pinpointed angelicin, a compound primarily studied for its pharmacological activities in mammalian systems [20], as the pivotal germination synchronizing agent. To our knowledge, this is one of the first reports implicating angelicin as an endogenous signaling molecule in plant ecophysiology. This finding opens a new frontier for understanding the functional diversity of furanocoumarins, which are typically characterized as defense compounds against herbivores and pathogens [9]. Our study re-contextualizes angelicin as a potential phyto-regulator, acting as an external signal that modulates a fundamental developmental process.

The precise molecular mechanism underlying angelicin’s action remains to be elucidated. It may function by interacting with the canonical germination signaling pathways, potentially sensitizing the seeds to germination cues or modulating the balance between abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellins (GA) [7]. Alternatively, it could activate a novel, as-yet-unidentified perception and signal transduction cascade. This discovery not only adds a new player to the complex network of chemicals governing seed dormancy and germination but also highlights the need to explore the roles of secondary metabolites beyond defense, in the realm of developmental regulation and social communication in plants.

Application prospects of chemical communication among seeds in the control of invasive plants

The synchronicity of germination facilitates rapid population establishment in invasive plants, enabling them to form stable populations [13, 14]. Our research has unveiled a perception mechanism among Ambrosia trifida seeds, whereby intraspecific chemical communication allows for rapid germination under favorable environmental conditions. This leads to the establishment of high-density populations and confers a significant ecological advantage, which is likely a key factor underpinning its invasiveness. Consequently, understanding the chemical basis of germination synchrony in Ambrosia trifida holds profound implications for developing targeted and sustainable management strategies.

Furthermore, this finding offers new avenues for the control and management of Ambrosia trifida and other pernicious invasive plants. Conventional control measures for invasive plants primarily target established adult plants, such as through manual removal or chemical herbicides. However, relying solely on these methods is often costly and time-consuming [3, 17, 21]. Our study points towards a novel and efficient control strategy: inducing a mass, synchronized germination event to exhaust the soil seed bank in a single stroke, which can then be combined with eco-friendly, above-ground control techniques to achieve rapid and effective integrated management. For species with persistent seed banks, this strategy could significantly reduce long-term invasive pressure, serving as a powerful tool in integrated management.

By decoding the chemical language of seeds, we not only deepen our understanding of plant population ecology but also unlock tangible, mechanism-based avenues for the development of next-generation management tools for invasive species. Further research into the specificity and efficacy of these approaches is warranted to translate these ecological insights into practical and sustainable solutions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, mutual perception among seeds is a complex and intriguing biological phenomenon that has significant implications for both plant ecology and agricultural production. This study reveals the chemical communication mediated by secondary metabolites among Ambrosia trifida seeds and finds that angelicin can enhance the synchronicity of seed germination. This not only deepens our understanding of the ecological adaptation strategies of this invasive plant but also provides new ideas and methods for the control of invasive plants.

Methods

Seed collection and pre-treatment

In October 2023, Ambrosia trifida seeds were collected from the Yili River Valley region (83.25° E, 43.52° N) in Xinjiang, China. Seeds were air-dried, cleaned of impurities, and stored in paper bags at 4 °C until use. To break dormancy and ensure uniform germination, seeds were subjected to cold stratification by placing them between layers of moist filter paper in Petri dishes and storing them at 4 °C in the dark for 14 days, a standard protocol for this species [31].

Seed extract preparation

To investigate density-dependent chemical signaling, seed extracts were prepared to mimic different ecological scenarios. The stock concentration was based on the seed bank density observed during the rapid population expansion phase of Ambrosia trifida invasion (3rd year), which corresponds to approximately 1500 seeds/m2, or so a seed mass of 25 g/m2 [11].

To prepare the stock extract (25 g/100 ml), 25 g of stratified seeds were soaked in 100 ml of sterile deionized water in an autoclaved flask. The flask was sealed and incubated on a shaker at 25 °C for 48 h in the dark to facilitate the leaching of water-soluble metabolites [23, 31]. The resulting mixture was filtered through two layers of sterile gauze to remove the seeds and then centrifuged for 15 min to pellet fine debris. The supernatant was collected as the stock extract. The pH of the extract was measured (6.5 ± 0.2). To minimize microbial contamination, the incubator was cleaned and disinfected with 75% ethanol before use. All solutions were freshly prepared, and the cultures were monitored daily. No visible microbial contamination was observed throughout the experimental period.

Effect of seed extract concentration on germination

This experiment followed a completely randomized design. Effects of varying seed densities and seed extract concentrations on seed germination: 25 g of Ambrosia trifida seeds were soaked in 100 ml of distilled water for 48 h with continuous shaking to ensure uniform extraction. The seed density treatments included 1 seed per dish (with 25 petri dishes per group) and 25 seeds per dish. The germination experiment was conducted in a growth chamber set to a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod, with day/night temperatures of 25 °C and 10 °C, a light intensity of 6600 lx [31]. The control group received 4 ml of distilled water per dish, while the treatment groups received 4 ml of seed extract diluted to ratios of 1:100, 1:50, 1:10, 1:5, and 1:1 based on 25 g per 100 ml. Every 2 days, 2 ml of distilled water was added to maintain moisture. Each treatment was conducted with three biological replicates. Germination (defined as radicle emergence > 2 mm) was monitored daily, and the number of germinated seeds was recorded every 2 days. The experiment was concluded for each replicate after 7 consecutive days with no new germination.

Extraction and detection of metabolites in seed extracts

Sample preparation and extraction

Ambrosia trifida seed extract samples were divided into two groups: Group A (5 g per 100 ml) and Group B (25 g per 100 ml), with three biological replicates per group, totaling six samples. The samples were stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at − 80 °C for subsequent metabolomic analysis using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS).

Using Group A as a reference, the relative content of each target compound was calculated by dividing its quantitative value in Group B by the corresponding value in Group A. After thawing, samples were placed on ice and vortexed for 30 s. Samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and 4 ml of the supernatant was collected for lyophilization. The samples were reconstituted in 200 µl of extraction solution (methanol:water at a 3:1 volume ratio, containing an internal standard) and vortexed for 30 s. The samples were homogenized at 40 Hz for 4 min, then placed in an ice-water bath for ultrasonic treatment for 15 min, and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter, and the filtrate was transferred to 2-ml sample vials. Equal volumes from each sample were combined to prepare quality control (QC) samples.

Widely-targeted metabolomics analysis

Chromatographic separation of target compounds was performed using an EXION LC System (SCIEX) ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) on a Kinetex C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 2.6 µm). Mobile phase A consisted of an aqueous solution with 0.01% acetic acid, while mobile phase B was a 50% acetonitrile/isopropanol solution. The column oven temperature was maintained at 25 °C, the autosampler was set to 4 °C, with an injection volume of 2 µl, and a flow rate of 0.3 ml min−1. A SCIEX 6500 QTRAP + triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an IonDrive Turbo V ESI ion source was used for mass spectrometric analysis in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The ion source parameters were set as follows: IonSpray voltage (positive/negative): 5500 V/− 4500 V, curtain gas: 35 psi, temperature: 400 °C, and ion source gas: 50psi.

Targeted quantitative metabolomics analysis

(1) Reagent preparation: (a) Standard curve solution preparation: Prepare standard curve solutions with final concentrations of 0.5 ng ml−1, 2 ng ml−1, 5 n ml−1, 20 ng ml−1, 50 ng ml−1, 200 ng ml−1, and 500 ng ml−1. (b) Mobile phase preparation: The organic phase consists of 100% acetonitrile, while the aqueous phase is a 0.1% formic acid solution. Add 900 ml of ultrapure water to a 1-L volumetric flask, add 1 ml of formic acid, then dilute to 1 L with ultrapure water. Mix thoroughly by inversion. (2) High-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) analysis: (a) Liquid chromatography conditions: An Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (2.7 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm) was used, with a flow rate of 0.3 ml min−1, column temperature at 40 °C, and an injection volume of 2 µl. Mobile phase A consisted of a 0.1% formic acid solution, while mobile phase B was acetonitrile. (b) Mass spectrometry conditions: The capillary voltage was set at 1 kV, source offset voltage at 50 V, source temperature at 150 °C, desolvation temperature at 500 °C, cone gas flow at 150 L hr−1, and desolvation gas flow at 1000 L hr−1.

The analysis involved two distinct mass spectrometry systems. Wide-target metabolomic profiling: A SCIEX 6500 QTRAP + LC–MS/MS system was used for the initial screening and identification of differential metabolites between the high- and low-density leachates. Targeted quantitative analysis: An Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC coupled to an Agilent 6470 Triple Quadrupole LC/MS system was used for the absolute quantification of candidate signaling compounds identified in the profiling stage.

Effects of chemical substances on seed germination

Using metabolomic analysis, eight chemical substances were identified (Table 1a), and a multi-level orthogonal experiment with two factors—four concentration levels and eight chemical substances—was designed (Additional file 3: Table 3). Data were recorded every 2 days, with germination deemed complete when no new seeds germinated for 7 consecutive days. For each treatment group, germination rate, germination potential, germination duration, and time to first germination were calculated. Seed germination was defined as the emergence of the radicle through the seed coat.

To efficiently screen the effects of multiple candidate compounds, an L32(48) orthogonal array was designed. This allowed for the systematic testing of eight selected compounds (Factor A-H) at four different concentration levels (Factors 1–4). The main effects of each compound on seed germination were analyzed. The germination assay procedure was identical to that described in section effect of seed extract concentration on germination, with three biological replicates for each treatment combination.

Data analysis

To ensure data accuracy and reliability, the raw LC–MS/MS data were subjected to a rigorous pre-processing pipeline. Initially, peaks were filtered based on their relative standard deviation (RSD, or coefficient of variation) across biological replicates to remove noise and outliers. This was followed by a missing value filtering step, wherein peaks were retained only if they were detected in at least 50% of samples within a single experimental group or in at least 50% of all samples combined. For the remaining features, missing values were imputed by replacing them with one-half of the minimum positive value recorded for that feature. Finally, the data matrix was normalized using an internal standard to correct for systemic variations.

The pre-processed data matrix was then analyzed using multivariate statistical methods to identify differential metabolites between the high- and low-density groups. An unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was first performed to visualize the overall sample distribution and clustering. Subsequently, a supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to maximize the separation between groups and identify the variables responsible for it. Metabolites were considered significantly differential if they met two criteria: a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 from the OPLS-DA model and a P-value < 0.05 from Student’s t-test. To account for multiple comparisons, all P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

All germination data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Final germination percentages were arcsine transformed before analysis to meet the assumptions of normality. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test (P < 0.05) was used to compare germination rates among different treatments in Experiment 1. For the orthogonal design (Experiment 2), factorial ANOVA was performed to determine the main effects of each tested compound and its concentration.

Additionally, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using commercial databases such as KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).
