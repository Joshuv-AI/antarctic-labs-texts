Trends in incidence and DALYs of infectious disease from 2000 to 2023 and the attributable risk factors in China: an analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023
Authors: Liyan Zhou, Qiao Liu, Yaping Wang, Min Du, Chenyuan Qin, Xi Li, Wenxin Yan, Shimo Zhang, Weiyang Chen, Shunzhe Wu, Zhiyu Zhang, Lei Zhou, Yuantao Hao, Zhidong Cao, Sitong Luo, Weizhong Yang, Wannian Liang, Min Liu, Jue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundInfectious diseases remain a major source of health loss, yet long-term, cause-comparable assessments that jointly integrate incidence, disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), age patterns, and risk attribution for China are fragmented and not comprehensively synthesized within a unified framework. We aimed to evaluate the trends in incidence and DALYs of infectious diseases in China from 2000 to 2023 to inform current priority setting by clarifying long-term trends and changes observed during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsData on the number and rate of incidence and DALYs of five major infectious-disease cause groups (HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, enteric infections, neglected tropical diseases and malaria, and other infectious diseases) and 40 specific infectious diseases in China were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2023. Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) in age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) were calculated overall and by sex and age group to quantify temporal trends from 2000 to 2023. We conducted comparative risk assessment using population attributable fractions (PAFs) to quantify risk-attributable DALYs, and decomposition analyses to assess the contributions of population growth, population ageing and epidemiologic change to changes in incident cases, deaths, and DALYs. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to examine the robustness of the results.FindingsFrom 2000 to 2023, age-standardized DALY rates declined in four of the five major infectious disease cause groups in China. The largest decrease was observed for enteric infections, with DALY rates falling from 365.4 to 43.8 per 100,000 (EAPC −11.0% [95% CI −12.2 to −9.7]). Among all groups, respiratory infections and tuberculosis had the highest DALY burden throughout the study period, while their DALY rates decreased from 2559.5 to 688.1 per 100,000 (EAPC −8.1% [−9.2 to −7.0]). It’s estimated that HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections were the only cause group with an increasing DALY rate, rising from 79.7 to 103.7 per 100,000 (EAPC 1.0% [0.6 to 1.4]). The increase was greatest among adults aged 20–54 years, in whom DALY rates rose from 71.1 to 152.0 per 100,000 (EAPC 3.3% [2.7 to 3.9]). Risk attribution varied by cause and age group. Unsafe sex accounted for most DALYs for sexually transmitted infections excluding HIV and more than 70% of HIV/AIDS DALYs. For lower respiratory infections, child and maternal malnutrition accounted for 42.2% of DALYs in those aged < 20 years, whereas tobacco use accounted for 38.1% in adults aged 20–54 years. Enteric infection DALYs were mainly attributable to unsafe water, sanitation, and handwashing, accounting for more than 70% across age groups. Decomposition analysis showed that epidemiologic change was the main driver of changes in infectious disease burden from 2000 to 2023.InterpretationChina’s infectious disease DALY burden declined from 2000 to 2023, but progress was uneven across causes and increasingly concentrated in older adults. Persistent incidence–DALY discordance and heterogeneous age-specific risk profiles indicate that current priority setting should move beyond incidence-led targets towards reducing preventable disability and premature mortality through age- and mechanism-tailored prevention and care.FundingPrevention and Control of Emerging and Major Infectious Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project.Keywords: Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023, Infectious diseases, China, Incidence, Disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs)

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Web of Science for articles published up to Oct 1, 2025, using the terms “infectious diseases”, “disease burden”, “DALYs”, “incidence”, “trends”, “China”, and “Global Burden of Disease”, with no language restrictions. Previous studies have documented substantial progress in controlling specific infectious diseases in China, but most have focused on single diseases, selected populations, or incidence-based surveillance data. Few studies have provided a unified, cause-comparable assessment of infectious disease burden in China integrating incidence, DALYs, age-specific patterns, and risk attribution. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, interpretation of temporal trends has become more complex because of changes in pathogen circulation, health-care use, and reporting. A comprehensive current assessment using a unified burden framework has been lacking.

Added value of this study

Using GBD 2023 estimates, we provide a comprehensive assessment of infectious disease burden in China from 2000 to 2023, integrating incidence, DALYs, age patterns, cause-ranking dynamics, risk attribution, and decomposition analysis. We found that age-standardised DALY rates declined in four of the five major infectious disease groups, with the steepest decline in enteric infections, except for HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis remained the leading contributors to DALYs, and the burden shifted progressively towards older adults. Incidence and DALY rankings were often discordant: upper respiratory infections dominated incidence, whereas COVID-19 and lower respiratory infections were major drivers of DALYs in 2023, and HIV/AIDS showed declining incidence but rising DALY burden in working-age adults. Risk profiles also varied substantially by age and cause. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses suggested that the short-term fluctuations during the COVID-19 period for respiratory and enteric infections.

Implications of all the available evidence

China’s infectious disease transition is increasingly characterised by divergence between transmission frequency and health loss. Current priority setting should therefore move beyond incidence-based targets and focus more directly on preventing disability and premature mortality. Age-specific and mechanism-specific strategies are needed, particularly integrated services on prevention and care for older adults.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, global public health efforts have delivered major gains in infectious disease control. However, infectious pathogens remain a formidable threat to population health. Common infections—particularly lower respiratory infections—continue to cause substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide, and the COVID-19 pandemic has underscored persistent gaps in health-system preparedness for respiratory pathogens and emerging outbreaks.1, 2, 3 In China, rapid socioeconomic development and sustained public health investment have supported broad improvements in infectious disease control capacity, with strengthened routine prevention, surveillance systems, and access to essential public health services.4, 5, 6, 7 At the same time, rapid population ageing and evolving risk exposures are reshaping vulnerability to infection, challenging existing prevention and control strategies.8

In China, how infectious disease burden is distributed across causes and age groups under population ageing and changing risk profiles has not been comprehensively synthesized within a single analytical framework.1,9 Although quantitative evidence exists at both national and subnational levels, and in some cases with finer spatial granularity, most studies focus on specific pathogens or rely on statutory notification data that primarily capture incidence and therefore do not fully reflect health loss from disability and premature mortality.10, 11, 12 Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, changing pathogen circulation and shifts in population vulnerability further complicate interpretation of routine surveillance trends.12,13 A comprehensive and comparable assessment using a unified burden framework, integrating incidence, age-stratified patterns, and risk attribution, is needed to quantify these shifts and inform priority setting.14 Importantly, a DALY-anchored assessment captures dimensions of non-fatal health loss and premature mortality that are not evident from incidence trends alone. By integrating incidence, DALYs, age patterns, and attributable risks within a unified framework, this study extends beyond pathogen-specific or incidence-only analyses and provides a more comprehensive basis for priority setting in China.

To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of the infectious disease burden in China from 2000 to 2023 using estimates from the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2023.14,15 We examined temporal trends in incidence and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) across five major infectious disease categories and key Level 3 causes, with analyses stratified by age and attention to the COVID-19 period. We also quantified the contribution of modifiable risk factors to DALY burden using the comparative risk assessment framework.14 This study aims to provide a consistent evidence base to support priority setting and inform targeted prevention and control strategies in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Data source and study design

This study is a secondary analysis of the burden of infectious diseases in China using data from the GBD 2023. GBD 2023 provides a comprehensive, standardized assessment of health loss for 375 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2023.3,14 All estimates reported in this study represent model-derived approximations generated within the GBD analytical framework rather than direct measurements from primary surveillance or registry systems. The study adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement.16 We extracted data on incidence and DALYs for China (national level) from 2000 to 2023 using the GBD Results Tool.15 In GBD, incidence for some acute infectious diseases is episode-based (i.e., it counts disease episodes rather than individuals) and can therefore exceed 100,000 per 100,000 population.17 The GBD 2023 estimation incorporated data from over 120,000 cause-related sources, with more than 35,000 newly added since GBD 2021.14 For China, GBD 2023 estimates draw on multiple routinely collected sources (e.g., the Disease Surveillance Points system, CDC cause-of-death reporting, maternal and child health surveillance, cancer registries, and hospital inpatient data), with source documentation traceable through the GBD Data Input Sources Tool.15 To better reflect the COVID-19 pandemic period, GBD 2023 improved data availability and reassigned outcomes previously grouped as “other pandemic-related outcomes” to specific causes (including COVID-19) for recent years, thereby improving cause-specific attribution during 2020–2023.14

Classifications of causes and age groups

Infectious diseases were classified based on the GBD cause hierarchy and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Our analysis focused on five major Level 2 infectious disease categories: (1) HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections; (2) respiratory infections and tuberculosis; (3) enteric infections; (4) neglected tropical diseases and malaria; and (5) other infectious diseases. These categories reflect major clinically and epidemiologically relevant domains of infectious disease burden in China, corresponding broadly to sexually transmitted, respiratory, intestinal/faeco–oral, vector-borne/parasitic, and other infectious conditions. To identify leading drivers of burden, we further analyzed 40 specific Level 3 causes. The corresponding ICD-10 codes for each disease are listed in Supplementary Table S7. Analyses were stratified by age into four groups: all ages, <20 years, 20–54 years, and ≥55 years.

Estimation of disease burden and risk attribution

GBD 2023 estimates non-fatal outcomes using standardised modelling approaches (primarily DisMod-MR 2.1, with cause-specific updates described in the GBD 2023 appendices) and computes DALYs as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs).18,19 YLLs are derived from deaths and standard life expectancy, and YLDs are derived from sequela prevalence and disability weights with comorbidity adjustment.14 We used the GBD-reported mean estimates and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) based on 250 draws (2.5th–97.5th percentiles).3,14 Risk-attributable DALYs in 2023 were assessed under the comparative risk assessment (CRA) framework and reported as population attributable fractions (PAFs), representing the proportion of DALYs that would be reduced if exposure were shifted to the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL).14 PAFs therefore reflect theoretical burden reduction under a counterfactual minimum-risk exposure scenario, rather than directly achievable policy effects. PAF estimates (and corresponding risk-attributable DALYs) were directly extracted from the GBD Results Tool.

Statistical analysis

To analyze temporal trends, we calculated the Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) for incidence rates and DALY rates, including both age-standardized rates and age-specific rates (<20, 20–54, and ≥55 years), over 2000–2023. In addition, to assess the robustness and granularity of age-related patterns, we conducted a supplementary analysis using 5-year age groups. We used EAPC as a standardized summary metric to compare long-term rate trends across heterogeneous causes. To further examine the potential influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on temporal trends, we conducted a phase-specific analysis by estimating EAPCs separately for the pre-pandemic period (2000–2019) and the COVID-19 period (2020–2023) for both ASIR and ASDR. In addition, as a sensitivity analysis, we re-estimated EAPCs for 2000–2019 and compared them with those for the full study period (2000–2023) to assess whether inclusion of the COVID-19 period materially altered the overall long-term trend estimates. The EAPC is a summary statistic used to describe the magnitude and direction of trends.20 It was calculated using a regression line fitted to the natural logarithm of the rates:

with EAPC calculated as 100×(exp(β)−1) and 95% confidence intervals derived from the standard error of β. In contrast, uncertainty intervals (UIs) were used for GBD-derived rates, counts, and percent changes directly extracted from the GBD Results Tool. Age-standardized rates (ASRs) were directly extracted from the GBD Results Tool.3,14 To be specific, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for EAPCs are derived from regression-based estimation and reflect statistical uncertainty under model assumptions, whereas 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) reported for GBD estimates are generated through the propagation of uncertainty across multiple model components, including input data variability, model selection, and parameter estimation.

For absolute numbers (incident cases and DALYs), we summarized changes using percent change between 2000 and 2023 based on point estimates [(V2023−V2000)/V2000×100%]. Percent change reflects the overall relative difference between two time points (e.g., 2000 and 2023), whereas EAPC summarizes the average annual rate of change under a log-linear assumption across the entire study period. Because uncertainty intervals for overall (2000–2023) percent change were not directly available from the GBD Results Tool, we additionally reported period-specific percent changes with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for 2000–2010 and 2010–2023, as provided by the GBD 2023 Results Tool (Supplementary Table S5). Numbers reflect changes in both disease burden and population dynamics (population size and age structure) and should therefore be interpreted as changes in total burden rather than underlying risk.1 We further performed a three-component decomposition analysis to quantify the contributions of population growth, population aging (changes in age structure), and epidemiologic change (changes in age-specific rates) to changes in total incident cases, deaths, and DALYs between 2000 and 2023.21 Total burden was expressed as Bt=∑ara,t×Na,t, where ra,t denotes the age-specific rate and Na,t the population in age group a in year t. Contributions of the three components were estimated using a stepwise replacement approach averaged over all six possible factor-order permutations to obtain order-invariant estimates.22,23 For causes with zero estimates in early years (e.g., COVID-19), EAPC was estimated using years with available non-zero estimates. Therefore, the EAPC for COVID-19 reflects the pandemic period rather than a long-term trend over 2000–2023. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Results

Overall trends in incidence and DALYs from 2000 to 2023

As shown in Table 1, Table 2 and Fig. 1, age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) declined in four of the five major infectious-disease cause groups in China from 2000 to 2023, whereas HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections (HIV/AIDS and STIs) was the only group with an increasing ASDR. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis accounted for the greatest absolute burden over 2000–2023. Across the five major cause groups, temporal patterns differed between rates and absolute burden. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis had the highest total DALYs over 2000–2023. Their ASDR declined from 2559.5 to 688.1 per 100,000 (EAPC −8.1%, 95% CI −9.2 to −7.0), and total DALYs decreased from 21.6 million to 11.2 million (−48.3%). HIV/AIDS and STIs were the only group with an increasing ASDR (79.7–103.7 per 100,000; EAPC 1.0%, 95% CI 0.6–1.4). Total DALYs increased from 0.9 million to 1.5 million (+75.3%), whereas incident cases declined from 67.2 million to 57.2 million (−14.9%). Enteric infections showed the largest decline in ASDR (365.4–43.8 per 100,000; EAPC −11.0%, 95% CI −12.2 to −9.7), with total DALYs decreasing from 3.0 million to 0.5 million (−81.9%). Neglected tropical diseases and malaria (NTDs and malaria), as well as other infectious diseases, also showed declining ASDRs (EAPC −3.1% and −6.9%, respectively) and reductions in total DALYs (−39.5% and −74.3%), while changes in incident cases were comparatively modest (Table 1, Table 2; Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S3). In the sensitivity analyses, the trends of ASIR and ASDR during the pre-pandemic period (2000–2019) were consistent with those from the main analysis for 2000–2023 across the five major infectious disease groups (Supplementary Table S11; Supplementary Figs. S20–S22).

Sex and age-stratified trends and burden distribution

After stratification by sex, overall burden was higher in males than in females (Supplementary Table S6). Over 2000–2023, long-term trends across the five infectious-disease cause groups were broadly similar between sexes. Despite the overall decline in DALY rates from 2000 to 2023, the age distribution of infectious-disease burden shifted. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis remained the largest contributor to DALYs across all three age groups (<20 years, 20–54 years, and ≥55 years) and showed increasing incidence alongside declining DALY rates in each group (Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The burden of HIV/AIDS and STIs was concentrated in adults aged 20–54 years, with DALY rates increasing in both the 20–54 years and ≥55 years groups. Enteric infections and NTDs and malaria showed declining DALY rates across all age groups, with the largest reductions in those aged < 20 years, whereas other infectious diseases also declined across age groups but remained highest among children aged < 20 years (Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

(F–J) Numbers of DALYs by age group. (A, F) HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections. (B, G) Respiratory infections and tuberculosis. (C, H) Enteric infections. (D, I) Neglected tropical diseases and malaria. (E, J) Other infectious diseases. DALY, disability-adjusted life-years; GBD, Global Burden of Disease.

In those aged < 20 years, DALY rates declined across all major cause groups, with the largest reduction observed for enteric infections (629.1–59.3 per 100,000; EAPC −11.6%). DALY rates for respiratory infections and tuberculosis also decreased markedly (3563.6–413.8 per 100,000; EAPC −10.2%), despite an slightly increase in incidence over the same period (EAPC 0.7%). Among adults aged 20–54 years, HIV/AIDS and STIs showed the largest increase in DALY rate (71.1–152.0 per 100,000; EAPC 3.3%), whereas incidence declined (9082.3–7721.9 per 100,000; EAPC −0.7%). This age group continued to carry the highest burden of HIV/AIDS and STIs. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis also remained a major source of DALYs, with declining DALY rates (503.3–374.0 per 100,000; EAPC −3.6%) but increasing incidence. Among individuals aged ≥ 55 years, respiratory infections and tuberculosis had the highest DALY rate and showed the smallest decline (2277.8–1751.8 per 100,000; EAPC −4.0%). HIV/AIDS and STIs also showed an increasing DALY rate (46.6–83.0 per 100,000; EAPC 3.0%) despite a slight decline in incidence (36.8–34.1 per 100,000; EAPC −1.0%) (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

This finer 5-year age-group analysis showed that the broad age-patterns were generally consistent with those of the three-category stratification (Supplementary Fig. S3; Supplementary Table S4). In particular, DALY rates for respiratory infections and tuberculosis declined across all age groups, whereas incidence increased modestly in children and younger-to-middle-aged adults but stabilized or declined in the oldest groups; HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections showed increasing DALY rates across most age groups, and enteric infections showed the steepest declines in early childhood.

At the specific disease level, distinct distribution patterns were observed between incidence and disease burden rankings (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S2). Overall, age-standardized incidence and DALY rates for the majority of infectious diseases exhibited decreasing trends over the study period, with only a few specific causes, such as HIV/AIDS, showing sustained increases (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S5).

In 2023, COVID-19 ranked first for DALYs in the overall population and in adults aged 20–54 years and ≥55 years, surpassing lower respiratory infections (LRIs) and tuberculosis (Supplementary Fig. S2A, C, and D). By contrast, for incidence, COVID-19 ranked second in the overall population and across all age groups, after upper respiratory infections (Fig. 3). HIV/AIDS showed a marked divergence between incidence and burden rankings: although it did not rank among the top 10 causes for incidence in the overall population (Fig. 3A), its DALY ranking among adults aged 20–54 years rose from 4th in 2000 to 2nd in 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Consistent with declining incidence but increasing DALY rates over time (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S5).

Among other major causes, upper respiratory infections consistently ranked first for incidence in the general population and across all age groups, while ranking only 5th for DALYs in the overall population (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S2A). LRIs were the leading cause of DALYs in the overall and older populations in 2000, but fell to 2nd by 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S2A, D). Diarrheal diseases remained among the top three causes of incidence in the general population, while their DALY ranking remained 3rd from 2000 to 2023, with both incidence and DALY rates declining continuously (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S2A; Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. S5). Tuberculosis also showed declining burden across age groups; among adults aged 20–54 years, its DALYs ranking fell from 1st in 2000 to 5th in 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S2C), in line with declines in both incidence and DALY rates (Fig. 4). Several other causes, including acute hepatitis, sexually transmitted infections excluding HIV, and otitis media, also declined in ranking or DALY rates over time (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S2A; Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis before and during the COVID-19 pandemic

We examined the EAPC in age-standardized incidence and DALY rates before (2000–2019) and during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023). Overall, HIV/AIDS and STIs remained stable, with the ASDR EAPC shifting from 0.86% to 0.07%. NTDs and malaria showed a smaller decline in DALY rates during the pandemic (EAPC −1.0%) compared with before (EAPC −3.2%). For respiratory infections and tuberculosis, the ASIR EAPC changed from −0.01% (95% CI –0.03 to 0.01) in 2000–2019 to 11.3% (−7.1 to 33.3) in 2020–2023, while the ASDR EAPC changed from −9.5% (−9.9 to −9.2) to 16.4% (−31.1 to 96.7). Similarly, for enteric infections, point estimates for EAPC shifted from −6.0% (−6.8 to −5.2) to 24.9% (−26.0 to 110.8) for ASIR, and from −12.8% (−13.6 to −12.0) to 10.6% (−17.8 to 48.9) for ASDR, but these trends during the COVID-19 period were not statistically significant (Table 1, Table 2 and Supplementary Table S8 and S9; Supplementary Figs S4, S6, and S7).

Attributable risk factors for major infectious diseases

In 2023, population attributable fractions (PAFs) varied substantially across infectious disease cause groups and age strata (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figs. S8–S13).

Behavioral risks predominated for HIV/AIDS and STIs. Unsafe sex was the leading risk factor across all ages (Fig. 5A), accounting for nearly 100% (PAF) of DALYs for sexually transmitted infections excluding HIV (Supplementary Fig. S9) and over 70% (PAF) of DALYs for HIV/AIDS (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Among adults aged 20–54 years, drug use accounted for 25.2% (PAF) of HIV/AIDS DALYs.

For respiratory infections and tuberculosis, risk profiles exhibited distinct generational disparities (Fig. 5B). Among individuals aged < 20 years, child and maternal malnutrition accounted for 42.2% (PAF) of DALYs due to lower respiratory infections (Supplementary Fig. S10B). In adults aged ≥ 20 years, tobacco use accounted for the largest proportion of DALYs. Specifically, in the 20–54 age group, tobacco use accounted for 38.1% (PAF) of LRI DALYs and 31.0% (PAF) of tuberculosis DALYs (Supplementary Figs. S10C and S11C). High alcohol use accounted for 23.7% (PAF) of tuberculosis DALYs in adults aged 20–54 years (Supplementary Fig. S11C). Air pollution remained an important contributor across age groups, accounting for 21.9% (PAF) of overall LRI DALYs (Supplementary Fig. S10A).

For enteric infections, unsafe water, sanitation, and handwashing (WASH) accounted for more than 70% (PAF) of DALYs in the overall population, with similar proportions across age groups (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. S13). Overall, risk profiles differed by age: child and maternal malnutrition accounted for a larger share of LRI DALYs in those aged < 20 years, whereas behavioral risks (e.g., tobacco use, unsafe sex, and drug use) accounted for larger shares of DALYs in adults (Supplementary Figs. S8–S11).

Decomposition of changes in incidence and disease burden

Decomposition analysis revealed that epidemiologic change was the predominant driver of infectious disease trends in China from 2000 to 2023 (Table 3; Supplementary Table S10; Supplementary Figs. S14–S16). For incidence, the rise in respiratory infections and tuberculosis was primarily driven by epidemiologic change (87.4%) and population growth (31.8%), whereas the declines in enteric infections (145.3%), NTDs and malaria (138.8%), and other infectious diseases (117.5%) were mainly driven by epidemiologic change. Conversely, the incidence reduction in HIV/AIDS and STIs was dominated by population ageing (220.3%). For DALYs, epidemiologic change accounted for the largest share of change across most groups, including respiratory infections and tuberculosis (145.5%), enteric infections (97.5%), NTDs and malaria (146.1%), and other infectious diseases (103.6%), while it also drove the increase in HIV/AIDS and STIs (79.2%). Mortality trends followed a similar pattern driven by epidemiologic factors, with the notable exception of respiratory infections and tuberculosis, where deaths were mainly driven by population ageing (201.0%).

Discussion

Using GBD 2023 estimates, we assessed infectious disease burden in China from 2000 to 2023 across incidence, DALYs, age patterns, cause rankings, and risk attribution. From 2000 to 2023, age-standardized DALY rates declined in four of the five major infectious-disease cause groups in China, with the fastest decline in enteric infections, except for HIV/AIDS and STIs. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis remained the largest contributors to DALYs over the study period, while the burden shifted progressively from younger populations towards older adults. We observed that, for most infectious disease categories, the direction and magnitude of long-term trends during 2000–2019 were broadly consistent with the overall period (2020–2023), whereas the 2020–2023 estimates reflected short-term perturbations likely attributable to pandemic-related disruptions, which was similar with the results reported in other studies.3,9,12 It’s worth noting that EAPC-based trends rely on a log-linear assumption and may be sensitive to short-term shocks, particularly during the 2020–2023 period. Risk-attribution patterns varied substantially across causes and age groups. Decomposition analyses further showed that epidemiological change was the predominant driver of temporal changes in incident cases and DALYs. These findings suggest that priority setting should increasingly account for age-specific and cause-specific heterogeneity, with particular attention to the rising HIV burden in working-age adults (20–54 years) and the disproportionate burden of respiratory infections and their severe outcomes in older adults (≥55 years).

Among the five major infectious disease categories, age-standardized DALY rates declined in four. Enteric infections showed the steepest decline. HIV/AIDS and STIs were the only category with increases trends. Although the trends reached statistical significance (EAPC 1.0% [0.6 to 1.4]), their magnitude was modest and should be interpreted cautiously, as small relative changes may not translate into meaningful shifts in population health burden. China’s infectious-disease control capacity should also be interpreted in light of major health-system and surveillance upgrades after SARS, including nationwide real-time notifiable-disease reporting and early-warning capacities, alongside expansion of primary-care–oriented public health services following the 2009 health-system reform, which together strengthened routine prevention and outbreak response platforms.24,25 This uneven progress points to a clear contrast in the underlying drivers of control. Conditions linked to environmental exposure and poverty appear highly responsive to improvements in the built environment and public health systems. In rural China, sanitary latrine coverage increased markedly between 2006 and 2017, alongside decreases in diarrhoeal outcomes attributable to unsafe sanitation, illustrating how infrastructure gains can translate into rapid reductions in faeco–oral transmission.26, 27, 28 Decomposition analysis is consistent with this interpretation, showing that epidemiologic change was the predominant contributor to the decline in enteric-infection burden. Taken together, these findings suggest that improvements in sanitation, surveillance, and routine public health services reshaped the underlying risk environment, allowing reductions in burden that were not explained by demographic change alone.25,29 Similar system effects are evident for malaria, where the 1-3-7 surveillance and response model strengthened timely reporting, investigation, and focus response, and became a cornerstone of China’s elimination pathway.7,30 HIV/AIDS and STIs exhibited a divergence between incidence and DALYs, with important implications for interpretation and policy. Although incident cases decreased, DALYs continued to rise, particularly among adults aged 20–54 years. This pattern suggests that reductions in new infections have been offset by increases in years lived with disability, driven by prolonged survival among people living with HIV alongside persistent challenges such as late diagnosis, delayed treatment initiation, and the growing burden of non-AIDS comorbidities.31, 32, 33 As DALYs capture both premature mortality and non-fatal health loss, these findings highlight that improvements in survival can paradoxically increase overall burden when disability accumulates over time. HIV transmission remains strongly shaped by behavioural and structural determinants and is therefore less amenable to purely biomedical or engineering-based interventions.34 China’s national response, particularly the “Four Frees and One Care” policy introduced in 2003, substantially expanded access to testing, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, and free antiretroviral therapy, facilitating the transition of HIV into a chronic, manageable condition.35 However, in this context, declining incidence does not necessarily translate into reduced burden if late presentation and comorbidity-related disability persist. Future progress will therefore depend on integrated, person-centred strategies that extend beyond infection control to long-term disease management. These include earlier testing and rapid linkage to care, sustained treatment adherence, targeted prevention and harm reduction among key populations, stigma reduction, and systematic management of comorbidities within a chronic-care framework.36, 37, 38, 39 These findings underscore that temporal trends in incidence and DALYs may diverge, reflecting transitions in disease severity, survival, and health-care effectiveness, and highlight the importance of interpreting burden through integrated metrics rather than single indicators.

Respiratory infections and tuberculosis remained the dominant contributors to infectious disease–related health loss in China. China’s long-term TB burden decline is consistent with programmatic strengthening and large-scale implementation of WHO-recommended DOTS in the early 2000s, underscoring the role of organized service delivery and treatment success in reducing severe outcomes.40 Age-standardized DALY rates declined overall from 2000 to 2023, but showed marked perturbations during 2020–2023, and were reshaped in 2023 when COVID-19 reordered cause-specific burdens. These findings suggest that the main long-term burden patterns in China were already established before 2020. Although stringent non-pharmaceutical interventions temporarily suppressed the circulation of several common pathogens, as shown in previous studies from China, the pandemic period appears to have introduced mainly short-term perturbations rather than a fundamental redirection of the underlying burden trajectories.29 Incidence trends did not mirror these improvements: incidence did not decrease and rose modestly in some strata, whereas DALY rates continued to fall. This decoupling suggests that burden reduction was driven less by reduced transmission than by lower severity, improved survival, and shorter or less disabling disease courses.1,41 Decomposition analysis adds a complementary structural perspective to this pattern. For respiratory infections and tuberculosis, epidemiologic change remained the main contributor to DALY reduction, but changes in population age structure acted in the opposite direction and offset part of these gains. This finding suggests that, even when age-specific risks decline, shifts in the population age distribution can limit the pace of further burden reduction in conditions in which severe outcomes are concentrated in older age group.42 Non-pharmaceutical interventions during the pandemic were accompanied by temporary suppression of several respiratory and vaccine-preventable infections, followed by rebound after restrictions were relaxed, consistent with an “immunity gap” after a period of reduced circulation.43,44 The co-circulation of multiple respiratory pathogens in 2023 highlights how rapidly respiratory burdens can reconfigure once contact patterns and health-care seeking normalise.45, 46, 47 If the pandemic-period point estimates suggested a sharp rebound, particularly for respiratory burden, this should be interpreted cautiously. Because it is compatible with rapid re-expansion of pathogen circulation after policy adjustment and a period of reduced exposure, rather than with a stable reversal of the longer-term trend.13 China’s infectious-disease control capacity has also been shaped by regulatory governance and long-term national strategy. For example, the Vaccine Administration Law (adopted in 2019, effective Dec 1, 2019) strengthened lifecycle regulation of vaccines, reflecting an institutional push toward safer, more standardized immunization delivery. At the strategic level, the Healthy China 2030 plan (released in October 2016) elevated health to a national priority and provides an overarching policy frame for integrating prevention, primary care, and preparedness.4,40 Together, current priority setting should not be inferred from incidence alone, but should also consider severity and case-fatality, and strengthen resilient multi-pathogen surveillance and response to detect and manage rapid fluctuations.48,49

Beyond short-term pandemic effects, our findings indicate a longer-term shift in infectious disease–related health loss from children and adolescents towards adults aged 55 years and older. This pattern suggests that China is moving from a phase in which progress was driven mainly by reductions in severe childhood outcomes to one in which further gains will depend increasingly on preventing severe disease and premature death in older populations.50,51 The decomposition results reinforce this interpretation by showing that changes in population age structure were not merely a background demographic process, but an increasingly relevant component of burden change in some major disease groups. In this sense, China’s infectious disease profile appears to be entering a new phase, in which continued progress will depend not only on sustaining epidemiologic improvements, but also on adapting prevention and care strategies to an older and more clinically vulnerable population. The drivers are not limited to population ageing but also include age-related declines in immune function, increasing multimorbidity, and greater reliance on timely access to and continuity of care.52, 53, 54 Whereas childhood control can be achieved largely through immunization and improved nutrition, reducing health loss in older adults depends more on effective service delivery—early detection, timely treatment, management of comorbidities, and sustained follow-up.55 Priority setting should therefore shift from child-focused, one-off interventions towards integrated, life-course approaches that systematically include older adults in vaccination, screening, and longitudinal care, aligned with chronic disease platforms to reduce the risk of severe outcomes.56

At the disease level, contradictory trends (such as rising incidence and decreased DALYs) were shown for several diseases as well as the misaligned ranks of incidence and DALY for example URIs remained most frequent yet caused relatively little health loss. This discordance suggests that burden patterns are driven not only by transmission intensity but by how age-specific risks and care pathways translate infection into severe outcomes, premature mortality, and disability. Morever, declining DALY rates despite stable or growing incidence may indicate a drop in case fatality or disability weights over time. Therefore, current priority should be anchored in preventable health loss rather than case counts alone.14,57 It highlights the importance of interpreting burden through integrated metrics with both incidence and DALYs rather than incidence alone.

Risk attribution provides additional context for these divergent patterns but should be interpreted cautiously. PAF derived from the GBD comparative risk assessment framework represent counterfactual estimates under a theoretical minimum-risk exposure scenario, rather than the magnitude of burden reduction that can be achieved through real-world interventions. As such, they reflect the relative importance of risk factors under idealized assumptions rather than directly actionable policy effects. Within this framework, the drivers of DALYs varied substantially by disease and age group. For respiratory infections and tuberculosis, risk profiles differed across the life course, with nutritional vulnerability predominating among younger populations, whereas tobacco use and ambient air pollution contributed more prominently among adults. This pattern is consistent with high-incidence conditions contributing relatively less to overall DALYs in younger groups, while health loss becomes concentrated among adults with higher severity and mortality risks.41,58 For HIV/AIDS, behavioral risks predominated, and the combined contribution of unsafe sex and drug use is compatible with DALYs rising despite falling incidence when prevention, early diagnosis, and sustained care do not sufficiently reduce long-term disability and premature mortality.41 By contrast, enteric infections were dominated by risks related to unsafe water, sanitation, and inadequate hand hygiene, supporting more parallel improvements when environmental exposure is reduced.39,59

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive assessment to quantify how China’s infectious disease burden has evolved from 2000 to 2023 using a unified burden framework (incidence and DALYs) and integrating age-stratified patterns, cause-ranking dynamics, and comparative risk assessment–based risk attribution. This study also has several limitations. First, as a secondary analysis based on GBD outputs, our results depend on a complex modelling framework that integrates multiple data sources with varying quality and completeness. The reported uncertainty intervals primarily reflect model-based estimation and regression procedures rather than direct empirical observation, and therefore capture only part of the overall uncertainty that warrant cautious interpretation. In particular, relatively narrow 95% confidence intervals of EAPCs should not be interpreted as high empirical precision, as they do not fully account for structural uncertainty arising from model assumptions, data sparsity, and smoothing strategies. Second, analyses at the national level and for both sexes combined may mask subnational and sex-specific heterogeneity relevant to targeting. Third, risk attribution is constrained by the risks quantified in the GBD comparative risk assessment framework, population attributable fractions reflect model-based attributable associations rather than direct causality and cannot capture programmatic determinants. Fourth, EAPC-based trend summaries assume approximately log-linear change and may understate non-linear dynamics around major disruptions, including the pandemic period. Future work should extend these findings with subnational and sex-stratified analyses, incorporate finer age grouping where possible, triangulate modelled estimates with independent surveillance, vital registration, and health-service data, and evaluate how prevention and care cascades mediate the observed incidence–DALY discordance for priority conditions.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive age-stratified assessment of infectious disease burden in China from 2000 to 2023 using GBD 2023, integrating incidence, DALYs, cause-specific ranking patterns, and risk attribution. Although age-standardised DALY rates declined in most major cause groups, the burden became increasingly concentrated in older adults and more heterogeneous across causes. Respiratory infections and tuberculosis remained the leading contributors to health loss, particularly among adults aged 55 years and older, whereas HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections emerged as a growing source of DALY burden in working-age adults despite declining incidence. Enteric infections and other environmentally mediated infectious diseases declined substantially over time, but continued surveillance and sustained investment in water, sanitation, and hygiene remain essential to prevent resurgence. Overall, these findings underscore the need to look beyond incidence alone in setting current priorities and to align age-specific and cause-specific strategies with the dominant pathways of preventable health loss.
