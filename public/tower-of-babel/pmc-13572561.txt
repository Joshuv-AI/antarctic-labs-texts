Reconciling strong coordination and anion-reinforced solvation structure enables nonflammable electrolytes for potassium-ion batteries
Authors: Jie Wen, Maoting Xia, Hongwei Fu, Wang Lyu, Ling Fan, Jiawan Zhou, Apparao M Rao, Jiang Zhou, Shuangchun Wen, Bingan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonflammable electrolytes are pivotal for improving the safety of potassium-ion batteries. However, their broad adoption has been hindered by the inadequate electrode compatibility of conventional nonflammable electrolytes, which typically result from strong solvation and excessive free solvent molecules. Here, we employ a molecular design strategy that integrates strong coordination capability with anion-reinforced solvation structure, thereby enabling the effective deployment of nonflammable phosphate solvents. By incorporating lengthened functionalized side-chains into the phosphate backbone, the tailored molecule tri(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate achieves robust multi-site chelation via synergistic interactions between the ether oxygen (C-O-C) and the phosphoryl (P=O) groups. Concurrently, its large molecular structure effectively increases the anion-to-solvent ratio in the electrolyte, promoting the formation of stable electrode-electrolyte interphases, and resulting in high compatibility with both the negative and positive electrodes. Consequently, the optimized tri(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate-based electrolyte enables stable cycling of graphite negative electrode (92.7% capacity retention after 800 cycles at 100 mA g−1) and Prussian blue positive electrode (4.2 V, 88.6% capacity retention after 3200 cycles at 100 mA g−1). Furthermore, the assembled graphite||Prussian blue full cell also maintains 80.3% capacity retention after 1500 cycles at 500 mA g−1. This work provides a strategy for electrolyte design in potassium-ion batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

Potassium-ion batteries (PIBs) have attracted considerable interest as promising candidates for next-generation electrochemical energy storage, owing to their high operating voltage, cost-effectiveness, and natural abundance1–3. As the medium for ionic conduction, the electrolyte plays a decisive role in regulating ion transport between the paired electrodes, determining overall energy conversion efficiency4,5. However, conventional electrolytes face critical challenges that include continuous parasitic reactions, which result in unstable electrode-electrolyte interfaces (EEIs) and poor cycling stability, and the inherent flammability of organic solvents, which raises safety issues6,7. It is well known that nonflammable electrolytes can intrinsically eliminate the chain reactions required for combustion, thereby mitigating fire hazards and improving battery safety8–10. Although low molecular weight phosphate solvents (e.g., trimethyl phosphate (TMP) and triethyl phosphate (TEP)) are intrinsically nonflammable in nature, their conventionally low-concentration (1 M) formulations exhibit strong solvation tendency and low oxidation stability (<4.0 V vs K+/K), resulting in persistent electrolyte side reactions and rapid capacity fading11–13.

To address these limitations, extensive efforts have been devoted to manipulating the solvation structure in electrolytes14–16. In electrolytes, solvents and anions compete for coordination with cations; the resulting solvation structure depends on the outcome of this competition17. Among the various electrolyte design strategies explored, high-concentration electrolytes (HCEs) based on strongly coordinating solvents and weakly solvating electrolytes (WSEs) based on weakly coordinating solvents have emerged as mainstream strategies18–20. Their core goal is to promote the formation of an anion-rich solvation shell, thus promoting the formation of robust anion-derived inorganic-rich interphases21,22. Specifically, these HCEs systems effectively reduce the proportion of free solvents and enhance anion participation in the solvation structure, which facilitates the formation of a robust, anion-derived interphases including the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) on the high-voltage positive electrode and the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the graphite negative electrode, thereby alleviating side reactions at both electrode surfaces23,24. Unfortunately, they suffer from high salt cost and increased viscosity, which hinder their widespread adoption25. In WSEs systems, the cations coordinate with anions in the primary solvation shell by weakening the solvating capability of solvents26,27. However, the weak solvent-cation interaction also increases the population of free solvents, which may aggravate the interfacial reactivity at the positive electrode, especially under high operating voltage23,28. Therefore, designing solvent molecules that can simultaneously ensure compatibility with both electrodes, as well as high safety and high voltage stability, remains a challenge.

Here, we construct a molecular design concept for phosphate solvents, which integrates strong coordination and anion-reinforced solvation into a single molecule via side-chain engineering. This design leverages the advantages of both HCEs and WSEs while avoiding their inherent limitations. In this design, the tri(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBXP) solvent forms a multi-site, strong coordination configuration via the C-O-C groups in side-chains and the P=O group in the main-chain, thereby reducing the reactivity of ion-coordinated solvents. Meanwhile, its large molecular structure effectively increases the anion-to-solvent ratio, driving anions into the K+ solvation sheath and further facilitating the construction of stable EEIs. Consequently, the designed electrolyte based on TBXP solvent exhibits enhanced redox stability and is compatible with both graphite negative and high-voltage Prussian blue (PB) positive. As a result, this electrolyte enables the K||graphite cell to exhibit stable cycling over 800 cycles at 100 mA g−1. The 4.2 V-charged K||PB cell operates stably over 3200 cycles with a high average coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.7% at 100 mA g−1. This molecular design concept provides an effective strategy for developing electrolytes for PIBs.

Results

Electrolyte design

As the fundamental component for cell cycling, the electrolyte design is pivotal for determining the cell’s key performance metrics. To date, electrolyte design strategies have been governed by two mechanisms: manipulating K+ solvation structures29–31 and optimizing the electrochemical stability of solvents32–34. The solvation structure of an electrolyte arises from the competition between solvent molecules and anions for coordination sites around cations. Under fixed salt type and concentration, weakening the competitive coordination strength between solvent molecules and cations effectively promotes more anions to participate in the solvation shell of K+, thereby facilitating the formation of a stable, inorganic-rich interphase from anion-derived sources35. In addition to designing weak solvents that exhibit weak cation-solvent interactions, another effective strategy is to reduce the solvent molecule density in the electrolyte system. Consequently, we introduce an electrolyte design strategy that leverages increased solvent molecular size to lower their molar concentration, thus diminishing their competitive coordination (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2)19. This macromolecular solvent effectively promotes the entry of more anions into the K+ solvation sheath (Supplementary Fig. 3)36.

Specifically, using conventional phosphate solvent TMP as the backbone, we constructed the tributyl phosphate (TBP) solvent molecule by lengthening phosphate molecular backbones (Fig. 1a). Based on bond dissociation energy (BDE) calculations, the major parasitic reaction sites of phosphate solvents are predicted to be predominantly on the side-chains, and the computed BDE values show a progressive decreasing trend with increasing side-chain length (Supplementary Fig. 4)37. Considering the single phosphoryl (P=O) coordination characteristic of the TBP molecule, its divergent side-chain structure is unfavorable for the stability of the electrolyte system (Fig. 1b). To address this, we developed the TBXP solvent molecule by strategically incorporating -CH2CH2O- moieties into the three side-chains of the TBP molecule (Fig. 1a). Apart from increasing the molecular size, this design also positions the side-chain C-O-C moieties away from the backbone due to the spacing effect of the -CH2CH2- moieties. This effectively removes the intramolecular steric hindrance, thereby enabling unhindered coordination of K+ with these C-O-C groups. Consequently, multi-site chelation coordination between K+ and TBXP is achieved. To probe the respective contributions of the coordination sites, electrostatic potential (ESP) maps and Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy were performed (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, and Supplementary Data 1). The combined results suggest that both the main-chain P=O and side-chain C-O-C sites in TBXP serve as primary coordination sites of K+, consistent with the multi-site chelation design of the TBXP solvent molecule. The combined effect of the large molecular size and the multi-site strong coordination mechanism reduces the proportion of free solvent molecules in the electrolyte system. Notably, BDE calculations for K+-solvent clusters indicate that TBXP displays higher BDE values than TBP (divergent coordination structure), suggesting that the side-chain anchoring strategy may contribute to the enhanced stability of its side-chains (Supplementary Fig. 7). Therefore, this side-chains coordination effectively avoids the formation of divergent ion-coordinated solvent structures and improves the electrochemical stability of the bulk electrolyte (Fig. 1c).

To probe the reductive stability of solvent molecules, we employed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to estimate the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of electrolyte components (Supplementary Data 2). As shown in Fig. 2a, the LUMO energy levels of TMP (−0.20 eV), TBP (−0.17 eV), and TBXP (−0.19 eV) solvents are closely matched, which tentatively suggests comparable intrinsic reductive stability across these solvent molecules in their free state. In the electrolyte, solvent molecules ubiquitously coordinate with K+, which results in a reduction of the LUMO energy levels for the coordinated solvent species (Fig. 2a). To elucidate the coordination of K+ with the solvent side-chains as well as its impact on reductive stability, we computed the LUMO energy levels of the coordinated conformations of TBXP with K+ (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 8). The calculations indicate that when K+ coordination solely with the P=O site of TBXP solvent to form a divergent coordination configuration, the K+-TBXP conformation Ⅱ exhibits a LUMO energy level of −4.44 eV (Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, benefiting from the intrinsic side-chains coordination anchoring property of TBXP solvent, the additional coordination of K+ to the C-O-C site in its side-chains is calculated to elevate the LUMO energy levels to the range of −3.42 eV (conformation Ⅰ) to −3.51 eV (conformation Ⅲ) (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, TBP solvent exhibits severe steric hindrance from long alkyl side-chains structure, which restricts K+ coordination to the P=O site alone and forces the formation of a divergent coordination configuration. This unfavorable coordination mechanism results a much lower LUMO energy level of −4.50 eV for the K+-TBP conformation, and this may be unfavorable to reductive stability (Fig. 2a). To evaluate the oxidative stability of the electrolytes, we also calculated the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels (Supplementary Fig. 9). Given the high free solvent content in the TMP-based electrolyte and the strong coordination in the TBXP-based electrolyte system, we compared the HOMO levels of the TMP molecule and K+-TBXP conformations. The results show that the coordinated TBXP exhibits a lower (more negative) HOMO energy level than a free TMP solvent molecule, which may contribute to favorable oxidative stability for the TBXP-based electrolyte.

To gain the strength of K+-solvent molecule interactions, we calculated the binding energies of K+ with various solvent molecules (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Data 3). The binding energy for K+-TBP (−1.53 eV) is stronger than that for K+-TMP (−1.36 eV). In contrast, in the TBXP solvent molecule, the backbone P=O and side-chain C-O-C sites cooperatively form a multidentate chelation structure with K+ (conformations I, III, and IV), increasing the binding energy of K+-TBXP to a range of −2.35 to −2.59 eV (Supplementary Fig. 10). It should be noted that when no coordination exists between K+ and side-chain C-O-C groups (K+-TBXP, conformation II), the binding energy of K+-TBXP (−1.52 eV) is correspondingly weakened to a level similar to that of the K+-TBP conformation. The strong binding energy suggests an improved coordination capability, potentially reducing the reactivity of the ion-solvent-complexed solvents. To evaluate the intrinsic solvation capability of solvent molecules, we conducted 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis under a fixed stoichiometric ratio of bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide potassium (KFSI) to solvent. The strength of K+-FSI− interaction correlates directly with the magnitude of the observed 19F NMR peak field chemical shift38,39. As shown in Fig. 2c, at the same molar ratio of salt to solvent (FSI−:solvent = 1:5), the TBXP-based electrolyte exhibited a more shielded effect toward upfield shift (50.7 ppm) compared with the TMP-based electrolyte (51.8 ppm). This result indicates that the TBXP solvent has stronger coordination capability, thereby reducing the free solvent content in the electrolyte. This characteristic was also confirmed by the corresponding Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 11). To further probe insight into the influence of molecular size effects on electrolyte solvation structures, we conducted 19F NMR analysis on various electrolyte systems with an identical KFSI salt concentration (1 M). As shown in Fig. 2d, contrary to the results under the fixed stoichiometric ratio condition, the TBXP-based electrolyte exhibits a pronounced downfield chemical shift (52.1 ppm), indicating that TBXP solvent more efficiently promotes the FSI− anions into the primary K+ solvation sheath. Raman spectroscopy results further corroborated this conclusion. Figure 2e shows the Raman spectra and peak positions of FSI− anions, indicating K+-FSI− ion-pairing behavior40,41. Fitting analysis of the Raman peaks in this range showed that the TBXP-based electrolyte has a higher fraction of ion aggregates (AGGs, an anion coordinating to two or more K+) at 36.5%, compared to 10.4% for the TMP-based electrolyte. Further electrochemical tests demonstrate that the TBXP-based electrolyte enables enhanced cycling stability for both the graphite negative electrode and the PB positive electrode (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). To identify the critical role of C-O-C groups in the side-chains of TBXP solvent, we further introduced triheptyl phosphate (THP) solvent with identical side-chain length but without C-O-C groups as a control analogue. The results demonstrate that the THP-based electrolyte exhibits negligible reversible capacity in both the graphite negative electrode and the PB positive electrode, even at low current densities (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). This finding provides evidence for the feasibility of our design strategy, demonstrating that a solvent with both strong coordination and anion-reinforced solvation can be achieved through side-chain engineering, thereby effectively enhancing the electrochemical performance of PIBs.

The solvent species in electrolytes are a critical determinant of the battery safety risk42,43. To evaluate the safety of solvents, we conducted flammability tests. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, phosphate-based solvents exhibit a self-extinguishing time (SET) of 0 s g−1, indicating complete non-flammability under open flame conditions, in stark contrast to conventional carbonate- and ether-based solvents. The flame-retardant properties of phosphate-based solvents arise from the generation of PO• radicals under high temperature. These radicals can efficiently scavenge highly reactive H• and OH• radicals that drive the combustion chain reaction, thereby efficiently reducing heat release and suppressing flame propagation, thereby lowering the thermal runaway risk of batteries44,45. Notably, given the presence of ether oxygen groups in the side-chain of the TBXP solvent molecule, we also prepared a physically blended mixture of dimethyl ether (DME) and TBP (1:1 by volume) for flammability comparison. The results demonstrate that physical blending fails to preserve the non-flammability inherent to phosphate solvents (SET = 85 s g−1). To address the high-viscosity of macromolecular phosphate solvents, we introduced 30 vol% of a low-viscosity fluorinated ether (1,1,2,2,5,5,6,6-octafluoro-3-oxahexane, OOE) as a co-solvent to optimize the electrolyte system (Supplementary Fig. 17). The introduction of OOE co-solvent effectively mitigates the high-viscosity issue (Supplementary Fig. 18). Accordingly, we consider three electrolytes: 1 M KFSI in TMP-OOE, TBP-OOE, and TBXP-OOE, which are named KFSI-TMP, KFSI-TBP, and KFSI-TBXP, respectively. At 25 °C, the ionic conductivities were measured at 3.8 mS cm−1 for KFSI-TMP, 2.22 mS cm−1 for KFSI-TBP, and 1.75 mS cm−1 for KFSI-TBXP electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 19, and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the generally inferior ionic conductivity of PIBs electrolytes fundamentally stems from the limited dissociation degree of potassium salts. Although the TBXP-based electrolyte exhibits the lowest ionic conductivity, it is still sufficient to meet the requirements for battery operation at room temperature. Furthermore, Raman analysis revealed that the solvation structure evolution trend remained largely unchanged after the introduction of the OOE co-solvent, whereas the AGG ratio increased only slightly (Supplementary Fig. 20).

To obtain an atomic-scale understanding of the solvation structural characteristics of the three electrolytes, classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed (Supplementary Fig. 21, and Supplementary Data 4). For each electrolyte, twenty representative coordination configurations were sampled to systematically compare the variations in their solvation structures (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). We first determined the distribution of distances between K+ and the primary coordinating oxygen atoms (the Osolvent, P=O and C-O-C sites are denoted as OP=O and OC-O-C, respectively), and calculated a weighted average across all data. The average K+-OP=O distance in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte is 3.06 Å, which is greater than those in KFSI-TMP (2.89 Å) and KFSI-TBP (2.98 Å) electrolytes (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Fig. 25). Additionally, the average K+-OC-O-C distance in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte is 3.04 Å. Coordination involving both the P=O and C-O-C groups is observed, consistent with the multi-site chelation characteristic of the TBXP solvent, and the longer K+-Osolvent distance may favor FSI− anions into the K+ solvation sheath. To further coordination number (CN) analysis was performed. Compared to the KFSI-TMP (Fig. 2h) and KFSI-TBP (Supplementary Fig. 26a) electrolytes, the K+ in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibits a stronger preference for coordination with FSI− anions (Fig. 2g) and a higher proportion of AGGs (Fig. 2i, and Supplementary Fig. 26b). To probe the side-chain coordinating mechanism, we analyzed the radial distribution functions (RDFs) for K+ coordination with various O sites in the solvents (Supplementary Fig. 27). The results show a higher CN for K+-OC-O-C in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, which is primarily attributable to the contribution from the C-O-C groups on the side-chains of the TBXP solvent molecules. In contrast, solvent coordination in the KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes is predominantly localized at the P=O group (Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, the RDFs analysis of FSI− anions reveals a higher CN in both the KFSI-TBXP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes compared to the KFSI-TMP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 28). Notably, despite the inherent multidentate and strong coordination characteristics of TBXP solvent, its pronounced molecular size effect still effectively facilitates the formation of an anion-reinforced solvation structure, which highlights the beneficial role of the molecular size effect in regulating the solvation structure of the electrolyte.

Anodic stability and interfacial chemistry

We first investigated the formation of SEI on the Cu current collector surface in K||Cu cells using various electrolytes. Chronopotentiometry (CP) was applied at a constant current density of |I| = 0.1 mA cm−2 to cycle the K||Cu cells from 0.01 to 1.0 V. This protocol was designed to avoid bulk K metal deposition. A comparison of potential-time (E-t) profiles during galvanostatic cycling revealed that the K||Cu cells required the shortest time for the Cu electrode potential (ECu) drop to 0.01 V in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Fig. 3a). An analysis of the integrated charge (Q) over the first CP cycle reveals that the Qdischarge exceeds the Qcharge, pointing to irreversible reactions including electrolyte decomposition associated with SEI formation. Subsequent cyclic voltammetry (CV) analyzes further confirmed the mitigated reductive decomposition of the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 29). This indicates that the designed electrolyte underwent the lowest extent of irreversible reductive decomposition during SEI formation, resulting in a stable SEI.

Graphite is recognized as a negative material with potential for PIBs, leveraging its integrated advantages of low cost, a low operating voltage plateau, and high theoretical capacity46. To assess graphite negative electrode performance in the three electrolytes, K||graphite cells were cycled at 100 mA g−1 within a voltage window of 0.01–3.0 V. All electrochemical tests were conducted at 28 ± 1 °C. The K||graphite cell exhibited a high initial CE of 85.5% in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, compared with 78.1% and 62.3% for the KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes, respectively (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, severe electrochemical polarization in the K||graphite cell with KFSI-TBP electrolyte not only resulted in diminished reversible capacity at the K+ intercalation cut-off voltage of 0.01 V, but also accounted for its low ICE under such constrained voltage conditions (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). Figure 3c shows that the cycling stability of K||graphite cells in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte outperforms that in KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes. Specifically, the K||graphite cell in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibits 92.7% capacity retention after 800 cycles at 100 mA g−1, with an average CE of 99.4%. In contrast, the cell in the KFSI-TMP electrolyte shows a capacity retention of only 19.6% after 400 cycles, with an average CE below 99%. When using KFSI-TBP electrolyte, the low initial capacity and its subsequent rise are attributed to severe electrochemical polarization and sluggish electrode activation (Supplementary Fig. 32). The capacity gradually stabilizes at approximately 255 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, and the cell fails at the 189 cycle (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 30). Remarkably, the K||graphite cell shows stable cycling for over one year (>850 cycles) in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte at 50 mA g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 33). Furthermore, the K||graphite cell exhibits good rate performance in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 34).

To understand the role of the SEI formed on the graphite negative electrode using the three electrolytes used in this study, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted to inspect the morphologies of the cycled graphite negative electrode. Compared to the pristine graphite negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 35), no discernible cracking was observed on flake graphite cycled in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Fig. 3d). However, prominent cracks were evident on flake graphite cycled in KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes, resulting in compromised K+ storage performance (Fig. 3d). To further characterize the interphase, the physical morphology of the SEI formed on the graphite negative electrode was systematically investigated. Under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), a thin and uniform SEI of around 5 nm was observed on the graphite negative electrode in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Fig. 3e), indicating that the interphases provide effective chemical and electronic passivation to suppress continuous electrolyte decomposition. In contrast, a thicker and uneven SEI formed after prolonged cycling in the KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes.

A controlled experiment was designed to verify the effect of the SEI on the cycling stability of the graphite negative electrode. The K||graphite cell was pre-cycled in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte to form an SEI-coated graphite negative electrode, which was then reassembled and retested in a low-concentration TMP-based electrolyte (1 M KFSI salt in TMP solvent, referred to as 1 M KFSI/TMP) under identical testing conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 36, the pre-cycled graphite negative electrode displayed enhanced cycling stability and CE compared to the pristine graphite negative electrode in the 1 M KFSI/TMP electrolyte. Furthermore, SEM images reveal that the pre-cycled graphite negative electrodes maintain structural integrity after 20 cycles in 1 M KFSI/TMP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 37), indicating that the SEI formed on the graphite negative electrode in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte is sufficient to prevent the decomposition of the non-compatible electrolyte during cycling, thus avoiding structural damage to the electrode. These results indicate that our designed electrolyte effectively mitigates reductive decomposition, minimizes SEI growth, and protects the structural integrity of the graphite negative electrode.

To characterize the chemical composition of the SEI formed on graphite negative electrodes, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with Ar+ depth profiling was employed (Fig. 3f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 38). XPS analysis revealed a strong KF peak in the F 1s spectra for the SEI formed in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Fig. 3f), contributing to enhanced negative electrode cycling stability47,48. In S 2p spectra, the intensity of the strong SOx peak dominated the outer layer, while the KxSy peak intensified with increasing sputtering depth (Supplementary Figs. 38a–c). Furthermore, more accumulation of N-Ox species was observed in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, primarily attributed to FSI− anion reduction (Supplementary Figs. 38d–f). Analysis of P 2p spectra, a signature of phosphoric ester-based solvent (TMP, TBP, and TBXP) decomposition, demonstrated the lowest intensity for the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Fig. 3g), indicating enhanced suppression of solvent decomposition. This trend was also corroborated by O 1s spectra (Supplementary Figs. 38g–i). Quantitative elemental analysis further established that the SEI formed in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte contained a reduced atomic ratio of O and P but an elevated atomic ratio of S and N compared to KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes (Fig. 3h). Critically, whereas the P element originates from solvent decomposition, the S element derives exclusively from FSI− anion reduction. Thus, the average atomic ratio of S:P serves as a key metric for determining whether solvent- or anion-derived reactions dominate electrolyte decomposition. The elevated S:P average atomic ratio of 1.28 in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, compared with 0.24 in KFSI-TMP and 0.36 in KFSI-TBP electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 39), reflects a higher contribution of inorganic components derived from FSI− reduction. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte promotes the formation of an anion-derived, inorganic-rich SEI, effectively mitigating solvent decomposition side reactions.

To characterize the impedance behavior of graphite negative electrodes in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, we employed a three-electrode configuration with K@Cu as the reference electrode (Supplementary Fig. 40). Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis of the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results at different cycles was performed to probe electrode interfacial evolution. Rapid processes with relaxation times (τ1) < 0.01 s are primarily attributed to SEI properties49,50. As shown in Fig. 3i, j, the τ1 peak stabilized after 10 cycles and remained virtually unchanged beyond 100 cycles, demonstrating robust SEI formation on graphite negative electrodes in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte. This conclusion is also corroborated by the cycling performance of K||graphite cells, which show a CE of 99.4% after 10 cycles, further confirming the stability of the SEI.

High-voltage stability investigation of electrolytes

The advantages of the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte motivated the evaluation of its high-voltage stability. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) revealed improved oxidation stability for the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, which exhibits improved tolerance for high voltage, with an increase in leakage current at ~4.4 V. Both KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes present an increase in leakage current at 4.1 V and 4.2 V, respectively (Fig. 4a). To understand the corrosion behavior of the electrolyte towards the Al current collector under high voltage, K||Al cells were subjected to chronoamperometry (CA) at 4.2 V for 20 h (Fig. 4b). In the KFSI-TMP electrolyte, the leakage current initially increased before decreasing to approximately 0.35 mA cm−2 with prolonged holding time. The Al current collector suffered from severe corrosion, as shown in the SEM images after testing (Fig. 4b, c). In contrast, the use of the KFSI-TBP electrolyte yielded a leakage current of ~50 μA cm−2, with minor corrosion spots visible in the SEM image (Fig. 4b, c). With the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, the leakage current was ~25 μA cm−2, and the SEM image revealed a surface morphology closely resembling that of the initial Al current collector, which confirms the efficient suppression of Al corrosion (Fig. 4b, c).

To further evaluate the positive electrode performance in the three electrolytes, iron-based PB positive materials were synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 41), and K||PB cells were assembled. As shown in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 42, the K||PB cell using the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte delivered a reversible capacity of 102 mAh g−1 within the voltage range of 2.0 to 4.2 V at 100 mA g−1. Remarkably, it delivers a long cycling with a capacity retention of 88.6% and an average CE of 99.7% over 3200 cycles, with high reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 43). Simultaneously, the K||PB cells in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte can still cycle stably over 1600 cycles even at higher rates (200 and 500 mA g−1), while maintaining a high retention and an average CE of ~99.5% (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). In contrast, with the KFSI-TMP electrolyte, the cell demonstrated a CE consistently below 95% and failed rapidly after only 80 cycles. Similarly, the K||PB cell with the KFSI-TBP electrolyte experienced a sharp decline in CE after 600 cycles. Furthermore, the CV profiles of the K||PB cells in different electrolytes corroborate the performance advantages of the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 46). These observed deteriorations in CE are likely attributed to side reactions, such as the corrosion of Al foil or the oxidative decomposition of the electrolytes. Figure 4e and Supplementary Table 2 compare the electrochemical performance of the PB positive electrode in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte with previously reported values in the literature, demonstrating that our system achieves notable cycle number and capacity retention. In addition to improved cycling stability, our KFSI-TBXP electrolyte also exhibits good rate capability (Fig. 4f). The K||PB cell delivers reversible capacities of ~99.0, 89.1, 83.6, 75.9, and 65.8 mAh g−1 at 100, 200, 300, 500, and 1000 mA g−1, respectively. Moreover, a reversible capacity of 100 mAh g−1 could be nearly maintained when the specific current was returned to 100 mA g−1, with stable cycling over 1800 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 47). Encouragingly, the K||PB cell with the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte also presents favorable rate performance at a high mass loading of ~10.8 mg cm−2 at 100 to 500 mA g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 48). In contrast, the K||PB cells using KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes experienced overcharging failure upon returning to 100 mA g−1, which could be attributed to severe interfacial side reactions induced either from intrinsic parasitic reactions within the electrolytes or from structural evolution of the electrode material (Supplementary Figs. 49 and 50)51.

To investigate the contributions of electrolyte and anion decomposition to the detailed composition of the CEI, XPS analysis was conducted on the cycled positive electrode. It should be noted that the S and P elements in the CEI originate from the decomposition of phosphate-based solvents (TMP, TBP, and TBXP) and the FSI− anion, respectively. Thus, the atomic ratio of S and P elements can serve as an indicator of the decomposition tendency of either the solvent or the anion. As shown in Fig. 4g, the CEI formed in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibits a lower P content and a higher S content. Concurrently, the PB positive electrode cycled in KFSI-TBXP reveals higher atomic ratios of N, F, and S elements compared to KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes, confirming that the CEI derived from KFSI-TBXP is enriched in inorganic components (Supplementary Fig. 51a). Furthermore, the CEI formed in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibits a substantially higher S:P atomic ratio than its KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes, as confirmed by XPS result (Supplementary Fig. 51b) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy element mapping (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53). Consequently, the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte promotes the decomposition of FSI− anion more effectively than the KFSI-TMP and KFSI-TBP electrolytes, resulting in the formation of an inorganic-rich CEI on PB positive electrodes, which underpins its enhanced electrochemical performance.

Electrochemical performance of full cells

Given the beneficial effects of the designed electrolyte on both the negative electrode and positive electrode, the electrochemical performance was evaluated in full cells assembled with pre-potassiated graphite negative electrode and PB positive electrode within a voltage range of 1.8 to 4.0 V (Supplementary Fig. 54, and Fig. 5a). The rate performance of graphite||PB full cells was evaluated in the three electrolytes (Fig. 5b), with KFSI-TBXP exhibiting the favorable rate performance among all electrolytes. Specifically, the full cell using the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte delivered reversible capacities of ~101.8, 94.8, 88.3, 79.9, and 66.2 mAh g−1 at 100, 200, 300, 500, and 1000 mA g−1, respectively (Fig. 5c). Full cells using the KFSI-TMP electrolyte failed rapidly after only 3 cycles at 100 mA g−1, owing to its inadequate high-voltage stability (Supplementary Fig. 55a). In contrast, the poor rate performance associated with the KFSI-TBP electrolyte resulted from the limited K+ storage capability of graphite negative electrodes in this electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 55b).

We further evaluated the cycling performance of the graphite||PB full cell in KFSI-TBXP electrolyte. The full cell exhibits an average discharge voltage of 2.9 V and a high energy efficiency of 88% at 200 mA g−1. Notably, the energy efficiency was still maintained at ~85% after 250 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 56). At 500 mA g−1, the graphite||PB full cell using the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibits a capacity retention of 80.3% after 1500 cycles, with an average CE of 99.2%. In contrast, the full cell using the KFSI-TBP electrolyte exhibits a capacity retention of 70.1% after 120 cycles at 500 mA g−1, while the full cell using the KFSI-TMP electrolyte shows a CE below 85% after 450 cycles (Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Fig. 57). To further verify the feasibility of the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte under high mass loading conditions, a high-loading graphite||PB full cell was fabricated with a PB positive electrode mass loading of 21.5 mg cm−2. This cell delivers a high areal capacity of ~1.74 mAh cm−2 at 100 mA g−1 and exhibits stable cycling for more than 80 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 58). The present electrolyte system exhibits competitive cycling stability compared to previous reports (Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Table 3).

In addition, we also evaluated the electrochemical performance of the monolayer graphite||PB pouch full cell in the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 59). At a specific current of 200 mA g−1, the pouch full cell delivered a reversible capacity of 95.3 mAh g−1, which is comparable to that observed in coin cells under the same conditions. Furthermore, the graphite||PB pouch full cell maintained 96.8% capacity retention after 150 cycles, and it maintained a high and stable CE (Fig. 5f). The charge-discharge profiles of the pouch full cell almost overlapped from cycle 50 to 100, indicating favorable retention of energy density retention (Fig. 5g). We further evaluated the safety of pouch cells assembled with the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte. Firstly, under package clipping and flame exposure conditions, the pouch cell with the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte remained nonflammable, whereas the pouch cell with conventional carbonate-based electrolyte underwent intense combustion (Fig. 5h). Moreover, to evaluate the thermal behavior of the pouch cell under abusive conditions, the pouch cell with our designed electrolyte was charged to 4.0 V (100% state of charge, SOC) and then maintained under various overvoltage conditions via constant-voltage charging for 5 min, during which thermal imaging analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. 60). The results revealed absence of thermal runaway and a smaller temperature change (Fig. 5i). Subsequently, nail penetration short circuit test combined with infrared thermography imaging further confirmed its thermal safety. The pouch cell using KFSI-TBXP electrolyte maintained a much lower temperature during the short-circuit penetration process compared with the conventional carbonate-based electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 61). Furthermore, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was employed to assess the thermal abuse tolerance of both the potassiated graphite-electrolyte and charged PB-electrolyte mixtures. Compared to the KFSI-TBP and KFSI-TMP electrolytes, the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte exhibited a higher exothermic onset temperature and lower total heat release, indicating enhanced capability in mitigating thermal runaway (Supplementary Fig. 62). These findings demonstrate the enhanced safety of our designed electrolyte.

Discussion

In this work, we conceived and reported a molecular design strategy that integrates strong coordination capability with anion-reinforced solvation characteristics. This approach aims to mitigate the longstanding challenges of poor cycling stability and severe parasitic reactions in PIBs. By leveraging the spatial size effect and the multi-site coordination mechanism of solvent molecules, this strategy mitigates solvent decomposition at electrode interfaces, thereby facilitating the formation of anion-derived interphases. The TBXP solvent enhances battery safety through its intrinsic non-flammability, while also expanding the upper voltage limit for high-voltage batteries and improving the cycling stability of both electrodes. A K||graphite cell with this molecularly engineered electrolyte operates stably for over 800 cycles at 100 mA g−1, and a 4.2 V high-voltage K||PB cell delivers a capacity retention of 88.6% after 3200 cycles at 100 mA g−1. Furthermore, the graphite||PB full cells achieve stable cycling for over 1500 cycles at 500 mA g−1. This work presents an approach to solvent molecular engineering, offering fundamental insights into electrolyte design for PIBs.

Methods

Preparation of materials and electrodes

An iron-based Prussian blue analogue (PB) was synthesized at room temperature via a facile aqueous co-precipitation method. Specifically, solution x was prepared by dissolving 1 mmol of K4Fe(CN)6 (99.0% purity, Aladdin) in 160 mL of deionized water, while solution y was prepared by dissolving 2 mmol of FeCl3 (99.0% purity, Aladdin) in 40 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, solution y was added dropwise into solution x under continuous stirring, and stirring was continued for 2 h after the addition was complete. The resulting mixture was then allowed to stand at room temperature for 24 h. The blue precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed multiple times with deionized water and ethanol. It was then dried overnight in an oven at 80 °C.

The PB active material, Ketjen black (conductive agent, 99.0% purity, Canrd), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, binder, Mw~600,000 g mol−1, 99.9% purity, Canrd) were mixed at a mass ratio of 6:3:1 in anhydrous N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.9% purity, Canrd) to form a viscous slurry. This slurry was subsequently coated onto carbon-coated Al foil (99.6% purity, thickness: 12 μm and 1 μm for Al foil and carbon, respectively, Canrd) with a doctor blade coater, followed by vacuum drying in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. The high mass loading PB electrode was prepared by coating a slurry consisting of 80 wt% PB active material, 8 wt% Ketjen black, and 12 wt% PVDF onto carbon-coated Al foil. Unless otherwise noted, the average mass loading of the PB active material is approximately ~1.0–1.5 mg cm−2. The graphite electrode was prepared following the same procedure. Natural graphite (99.9% purity, XFNANO), Super P (99.5% purity, Canrd) and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, binder, 99.0% purity, Canrd) were blended at a mass ratio of 8:1:1, with anhydrous ethanol (99.7% purity, Aladdin) and deionized water (volume ratio of 1:4) serving as the mixed solvent to form a homogeneous slurry. The as-prepared slurry was uniformly coated onto Cu foil (99.8% purity, thickness: 9 μm, Canrd) using a doctor blade coater, and then vacuum-dried at 80 °C overnight. The average mass loading of graphite active material is about 0.8–1.2 mg cm−2. Prior to all electrochemical tests on CR2032 coin cells, these electrodes were punched into circular discs with a diameter of 12 mm using a battery slitter prior (Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd, MSK-T10).

Preparation of electrolytes

Potassium metal (K, 99.0% purity, Aladdin), Trimethyl phosphate (TMP, 99.0% purity, Aladdin), Tributyl phosphate (TBP, 99.0% purity, Aladdin), Tri (2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBXP, 98.0% purity, Adamas), 1,1,2,2,5,5,6,6-octafluoro-3-oxahexane (OOE, 99.5% purity, Aladdin), Ethylene carbonate (EC, 99.9% purity, Shanghai Songjing New Energy Co., Ltd), Dimethyl carbonate (DMC, 99.9% purity, Shanghai Songjing New Energy Co., Ltd), Diethyl carbonate (DEC, 99.9% purity, Shanghai Songjing New Energy Co., Ltd), Ethylene glycol diethyl ether (DEE, 99.5% purity, Aladdin), 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME, 99.0% purity, TCI), and Dimethyl Sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6, 99.9% purity, Aladdin) were used for electrolyte preparation and were directly utilized without further purification. The battery-grade bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide potassium salt (KFSI, 99.0% purity, DoDo Chem Co., Ltd) was dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 80 °C prior to use. All procedures, including the unsealing of all solvents and KFSI salt and the preparation of the electrolyte, were performed in an argon-filled glove box.

Preparation of a representative KFSI-TBXP electrolyte involved dissolving 0.219 g of KFSI salt in 0.7 mL of TBXP solvent. Following complete dissolution of the KFSI salt, the resultant KFSI/TBXP mixture was introduced into 0.3 mL of OOE solvent under stirring to form the 1 M KFSI/TBXP-OOE (named KFSI-TBXP) electrolyte. The concentration of KFSI salt in the final electrolyte was calculated to be 1 M. Molarity (M, defined as the number of moles of salt per liter of solution, with the unit of moles per liter, denoted as mol L−1) is used to express the concentration of salt in the electrolyte. The 1 M KFSI/TMP-OOE (named KFSI-TMP) and 1 M KFSI/TBP-OOE (named KFSI-TBP) electrolytes were prepared using the same method as the KFSI-TBXP electrolyte, in which TBXP was replaced by TMP and TBP, respectively. To validate the mechanism, single-solvent electrolytes were also prepared. These included 1 M KFSI salt solutions in TMP, TBP, and TBXP solvents (designated as 1 M KFSI/TMP, 1 M KFSI/TBP, and 1 M KFSI/TBXP, respectively), as well as electrolytes with a constant molar ratio of salt to solvent (KFSI: solvent = 1:5). All electrolytes were prepared and stored in an argon-filled glove box under an atmosphere containing below 0.01 ppm O2 and H2O.

Electrochemical measurements

All CR2032 coin and pouch cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box. Each CR2032 coin cell was fixed with 80 µL of electrolyte prior to electrochemical performance testing. A single layer of glass microfiber paper (Whatman, GF/D, diameter: 19 mm, thickness: 260 μm, porosity 84%, pore size 2.7 μm) was used as the separator between the working and counter electrodes. The rate and cycling performance of K||graphite cells, K||PB cells, and graphite||PB full cells were evaluated using a Neware battery testing system (BTSCT-3008-TC 5.X., Shenzhen, China). Specific testing conditions are provided in the discussion section or corresponding Fig. captions. All electrochemical measurements were conducted in a temperature-controlled chamber, with the temperature kept constant at 28 ± 1 °C throughout all tests. The LSV, Cyclic voltammetry (CV), and EIS were measured on a CHI660e electrochemical system. Specifically, LSV measurements were conducted on K||Al cells over a voltage window of 2.5–5.0 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1, while CV measurements were performed on K||Cu cells over a range of 0.01–2.5 V at 0.1 mV s−1 and on K||PB cells over a range of 2.0–4.2 V at 0.05 mV s−1. EIS measurements were performed on K||graphite cells after different cycling numbers at 100 mA g−1. All impedance measurements were carried out under potentiostatic mode with a signal amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range of 10 mHz to 10 kHz. The data sampling rate was set to 12 points per decade, and no pretreatment was conducted prior to these measurements. Before each measurement, the cells were recorded after a 2 min open-circuit voltage stabilization period. Ionic conductivity was measured in a custom‑built setup using the same EIS parameters, except that the frequency range was 10 Hz to 10 kHz. To assess the corrosion of Al current collectors by electrolytes under high voltage, K||Al cells were subjected to a constant voltage hold at 4.2 V for 20 h in different electrolytes.

Prior to the assembly of graphite||PB full cells, the graphite negative electrodes were subjected to 5 pre-cycles at 100 mA g−1 for electrochemical potassiation to achieve a fully potassiated state. For the graphite||PB pouch cells, all the electrodes, separators, and electrolytes were identical to those used in the CR2032 coin cells. The graphite negative electrode of the pouch cell was chemically pre-potassiated via the direct contact method. The K metal piece was rolled into a 300 μm metal foil, which was then stacked with the graphite negative electrode and electrolyte to form a tri-layer configuration. After 4 h of static contact, the pre-potassiated graphite negative electrode was immediately transferred into the pouch cell assembly. In this work, all full cells were fabricated with a negative-to-positive capacity (N:P) ratio of approximately 1.6. Prior to pouch cell assembly, the graphite negative electrode underwent a contact pre-potassiation treatment. Specifically, bulk K metal was rolled into a 300 μm-thick K foil, and the graphite-electrolyte-K foil was assembled into a sandwich structure, which was then left standing for 4 h to obtain the pre-potassiated graphite negative electrode. The monolayer pouch cells with a capacity of 1.3 mAh were assembled. The PB positive electrode dimensions are 3.0 cm × 4.0 cm with a total mass loading of approximately 13.5 mg, while the graphite negative electrode measured 3.2 cm × 4.2 cm, and a glass microfiber membrane was employed as the cell separator. The electrolyte was uniformly dropped onto the separator via a micropipette at a dosage of approximately 0.6 mL per cell. After sufficient electrolyte wetting, the assembled pouch cells were transferred to a vacuum sealing machine. Prior to final heat sealing, degassing was conducted under a vacuum of −0.1 MPa for 10 s to eliminate residual air bubbles inside the cell. During the cycling stability tests, a constant external pressure of approximately 45 kPa was applied to the pouch cell to maintain stable and intimate contact between electrodes.

Characterizations

Cycled electrodes were obtained by disassembling coin cells in an argon-filled glove box, followed by washing three times with the corresponding pure solvent (TMP, TBP, and TBXP) prior to characterization. The structure and microstructure of electrode materials were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-7610F Plus) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F), respectively. The interfacial composition of cycled graphite negative and PB positive electrodes was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi), with binding energies calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Samples were transferred directly from the argon-filled glove box to the XPS analysis chamber under vacuum transfer, thereby preventing exposure to moisture and air. Physicochemical properties of the electrolyte were characterized using a confocal Raman microscope (Alpha300, WITec R) with 532 nm laser excitation. The Solvation environments of the electrolytes were monitored by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR, 400 MHz, Bruker AVANCE III HD 400) at 298 K using Wilmad coaxial NMR tubes. DMSO-d6 served as the internal standard, while an external reference was established by encapsulating DMSO-d6 in a capillary isolated from the electrolyte. A thermal imager (FOTRIC 285) recorded the pouch cells’ temperatures after each constant-voltage overcharge test. Briefly, after 5 pre-cycling cycles, the pouch cells were charged to 4.0 V (100% SOC), followed by constant-voltage overcharge tests at 4.0 V, 5.0 V, and 6.0 V (each lasting 5 min). After the 5 min test under each voltage, the thermal imager recorded the cells’ temperature. DSC measurements were performed on a thermal analyzer (China-DZ-DSC300C). Potassiated graphite negative electrodes were retrieved from cells discharged to 0.01 V at 20 mA g−1 in the corresponding electrolytes, while charged PB positive electrodes were obtained from cells charged to 4.2 V at 100 mA g−1. Both electrodes were carefully rinsed with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) three times to remove residual electrolyte. After overnight drying, the electrode powders were mixed with the selected electrolyte solutions and sealed in 100 µL high-pressure gold DSC capsules. The DSC profiles were recorded from 30 to 400 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1.

Theoretical calculations

The DFT calculations were carried out on idealized structural models. Parameter fitting was performed with the Gaussian 16 C.01 package using the B3LYP exchange-correlation functional combined with Grimme’s DFT-D3(BJ) empirical dispersion correction52,53. The self-consistent field (SCF) computations employed the def2-TZVP basis set for neutral molecules54 and the ma-TZVP basis set for the FSI− anion55,56. Atomic partial charges were derived following the RESP2(0.5) scheme57, which integrates gas-phase and implicit solvent (polarizable continuum model) charges, using both Gaussian 16 and the Multiwfn program. Electrostatic potentials were calculated with Multiwfn via the highly efficient algorithm of Zhang and Lu58, and visualized with VMD59. When performing molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of low-dielectric-constant solvents with non-polarizable all-atom force fields, it is essential to account for electronic polarization effects. Accordingly, the partial charges of the charged ions were uniformly scaled by a factor of 0.8 to approximate electronic screening, a correction that has been shown to improve the predicted interionic interactions.

For various complexes, the energies of the HOMO and the LUMO were computed, together with the complexation energies corrected for the basis set superposition error (BSSE). All these calculations employed the ma‑TZVP basis set.

The OPLS-AA force field60 was employed throughout this work, incorporating fitted parameters for K+ (obtained from the Biochemical and Organic Simulation System, BOSS, program)61, FSI− anions62, and the solvent molecules TMP, TBP, TBXP and OOE (generated via LigParGen)63. All MD simulations were conducted with the LAMMPS package64, while initial configurations were constructed using Packmol65 and Moltemplate66. Three simulation cells were constructed, corresponding to the electrolytes 1 M KFSI/TMP-OOE, 1 M KFSI/TBP-OOE, and 1 M KFSI/TBXP-OOE, each containing the appropriate numbers of salt and solvent molecules. The systems were equilibrated via a simulated annealing protocol that consisted of an initial 2 ns NPT equilibration at 298 K, heating to 500 K over 2 ns (NPT), a 2 ns NPT relaxation at 500 K, cooling back to 298 K over 2 ns, and a final 20 ns NPT equilibration at 298 K. Production trajectories of 20 ns were subsequently collected at 298 K in the NVT ensemble. All simulations employed a 2 fs timestep and a pressure of 1 atm. Temperature and pressure were controlled using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat and barostat with damping times of 0.2 ps and 2 ps, respectively. Radial distribution functions were obtained from the production data using LAMMPS, and cation solvation coordination numbers were extracted by integrating the first RDF peak up to the first minimum. The populations of SSIPs, CIPs, and AGGs in the initial solvation sheath were determined with an in‑house Python script.

Limitations statement: The MD simulations were performed with the OPLS-AA force field, a well-established non-polarizable model whose fixed-charge description represents a mean-field treatment of electrostatic interactions rather than capturing instantaneous charge polarization. Electrostatic potentials and energy levels were computed using DFT with the implicit solvation model (SMD) implicit solvation model. While continuum solvation models are known to be less accurate for ionic species than for neutral molecules, the SMD approach has been widely validated and yields adequate accuracy for our screening purposes. An explicit-implicit hybrid solvation scheme would offer higher fidelity but would come at a steep increase in computational cost and data-analysis complexity, rendering it impractical for ESP screening.
