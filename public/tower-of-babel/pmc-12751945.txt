Novel hypoxia–immune biomarkers predict response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with laryngo-hypopharyngeal cancer
Authors: Lingwa Wang, Haiyang Li, Ling Feng, Jugao Fang, Ru Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe hypoxic immune microenvironment has clinical significance in predicting sensitivity to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in patients with laryngo-hypopharyngeal cancer (LHC).MethodsIn this study, RNA sequencing was performed to screen out differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Logistic regression analysis was used to construct a predictive model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to investigate the differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between different risk groups. Immune infiltration analysis was performed using the “ssGSEA” method. The association between biomarkers and drug sensitivity was assessed using the CellMiner database.ResultsWe screened 40 hypoxia immunity-related DEGs and constructed a five-gene prediction model with an area under the curve of 0.854. The high-risk group had a worse OS and PFS than the low-risk group in both our data and TCGA–HNSC external data set. Furthermore, immune cell infiltration played a role in the efficacy of NAC, specifically through its impact on the level of natural killer cell infiltration and migration ability. Finally, we validated gene expression in an independent clinical cohort. We also observed that patients with increased AREG expression showed sensitivity to the anticancer therapeutic drugs afatinib and sapitinib.ConclusionsOur hypoxia–immune panel can accurately predict response to NAC and OS in patients with LHC and may have implications for the development of novel biomarkers and targeted therapies. Background

Laryngeal and hypopharyngeal (LH) cancers are prevalent types of head and neck cancer, with an increasing incidence worldwide. The risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis in patients with advanced-stage LH cancer is as high as 15–40%, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of only approximately 25–35% [1]. Treatment modalities for LH cancer include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or a combination thereof, depending on the tumor stage, size, and location. Despite therapeutic advances, patients still experience long-term complications that negatively affect their quality of life, and their 5-year survival rate remains low. The primary treatment goal for locally advanced LH cancer is to preserve the larynx [2, 3]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has emerged as a promising treatment approach; it involves the administration of chemotherapy prior to surgery or radiation therapy to reduce tumor size and preserve organ function [4]. Nevertheless, ongoing research is required to optimize the use of NAC and determine the patient population that would benefit most from this approach.

The hypoxic immune microenvironment significantly influences tumorigenesis, cancer progression and metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance [5]. Hypoxia, a hallmark feature of many solid tumors, modifies gene expression and metabolism that enhance tumor cell survival and proliferation. Moreover, hypoxia can create an immunosuppressive environment, limiting the immune system’s ability to detect and destroy malignant cells. While tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) exhibit antitumor activity, immune checkpoint proteins such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 repress TIL function, promoting immune escape [6]. Immunotherapeutic modalities, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and combination therapies targeting both the tumor-specific microenvironment and intrinsic cancer pathways, exhibit potential in treating various malignancies, including melanoma, lung cancer, and bladder cancer [7–9]. Growing evidence across multiple cancer types underscores the critical link between hypoxia-related immune interactions and therapy response. For instance, in colorectal cancer, molecules (such as HEPACAM2) and non-coding RNA (such as hsa_circ_0005939) are key regulators within the tumor microenvironment, influencing immune infiltration, stemness, and ultimately patient prognosis and drug sensitivity [10, 11]. Similarly, in cervical cancer, zinc finger proteins like ZNF695 have been shown to correlate with immune cell infiltration and serve as potential prognostic biomarkers and immunotherapeutic targets [12]. Furthermore, hypoxia is linked to chemotherapy resistance due to its role in changing tumor cell metabolism, angiogenesis, and reducing drug uptake by cancer cells [13, 14]. The interplay between hypoxia and the immune landscape, as reflected by the diversity and clonality of immune repertoires in cancers, such as B-ALL, further highlights its profound impact on disease progression and treatment efficacy [15]. However, further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms behind the hypoxic immune microenvironment and develop effective treatment strategies for LH cancers.

Our objective was to create and validate innovative hypoxia-related immune biomarkers that accurately represent and quantify the LH cancer environment. Building on the expanding understanding of hypoxia–immune axes in other malignancies [10–12, 15], we conducted in silico modeling to identify a hypoxia–immune model with heightened sensitivity to NAC. Subsequently, we used qRT–PCR to evaluate these biomarkers in tissue samples, facilitating their eventual implementation in clinical practice.

Methods

Patient information and data preparation

This study included a cohort of 51 patients diagnosed with LHC obtained from the Beijing Tongren Hospital. The diagnostic confirmation of all specimens was carried out using pathological biopsy, and all patients underwent NAC. The NAC regimen consisted of two cycles of the TPF protocol (docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil). Specifically, the regimen was as follows: Paclitaxel (135 mg/m2) administered on day 1; Cisplatin (30 mg/m2) administered from day 2 to day 4; and 5-fluorouracil (500 mg/m2) administered from day 2 to day 6. A second cycle was started on day 21: Paclitaxel (135 mg/m2) on day 21; Cisplatin (30 mg/m2) from day 22 to day 24; and 5-fluorouracil (500 mg/m2) from day 22 to day 26. Pre- and post-chemotherapy imaging data were evaluated by two experienced radiologists to assess therapeutic efficacy. Patients were categorized into two groups based on their response to NAC: sensitive and insensitive. The evaluation was performed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 guidelines. Patients achieving a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) were classified as NAC-sensitive, whereas those with stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) were classified as NAC-insensitive. Comprehensive clinicopathological characteristics, such as age, sex, tumor location, clinical stage, and TNM stage, were carefully extracted from the patients’ medical history records. Furthermore, the TNM stage of each patient was precisely determined according to the International Union Against Cancer standards. Before surgical intervention, all patients signed an informed consent form in writing to utilize their tissues in our research study. Our study protocol was endorsed by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (protocol code: TREC2022-KY018.R1, approval date: 2022-4-21), and conducted in strict compliance with the ethical standards enshrined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. All patients signed informed consent before operation. All cancer tissues were procured prior to NAC, and RNA sequencing was performed to determine the gene expression levels. The RNA sequencing data generated and analyzed during this study have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and are publicly accessible as of February 14, 2024. The data can be retrieved using the accession number GSE184072.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

We conducted a thorough differential expression analysis to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the NAC-sensitive and -insensitive groups using the “DEseq2” R package [16]. The threshold values were set as |log2 fold change (FC)|> 1 and p value < 0.05 to ensure the robustness of our findings. We utilized the “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” R packages to generate volcano plots and heatmaps, respectively [17, 18]. To better understand the intricate protein–protein interactions (PPI) among these DEGs, we employed the STRING program (version 11.5) to construct a PPI network, which was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape software [19, 20]. In addition, we performed gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis utilizing the “clusterProfiler” R package to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the biological significance of these DEGs [21].

Construction of a hypoxia–immune prediction model

We extracted the hypoxia-related genes from the hallmark gene set in the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, www.gsea-msigdb.org) and the HIF-1 signaling pathway in the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/) to obtain a comprehensive list of 258 hypoxia-related genes [22, 23]. In addition, we acquired 1793 immune-related genes from the ImmPort (http://www.immport.org/) database [24]. We intersected the hypoxia- and immune-related genes with the DEGs to obtain a candidate gene set of 40 genes. To construct a parsimonious and robust predictive model for NAC sensitivity, we used a two-step logistic regression approach. First, univariate logistic regression was performed on the 40 candidate genes to identify factors significantly associated with NAC response (p < 0.05). Subsequently, these significant variables were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. To select the most relevant predictors and avoid overfitting, we applied a backward stepwise selection procedure based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The final model was constructed using the glm function in R, and the regression coefficients from this model were used to calculate the risk score for each patient. Patients were then divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score.

Evaluation and analysis of the hypoxia–immune prediction model

To evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the hypoxia–immune prediction model and compare it with five separate genes and a clinical index, we utilized receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) via the “pROC” R package [25]. In addition, we used decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess the net benefit and clinical usefulness of the model through the “rmda” R package. The TCGA–head–neck squamous cell (HNSC) data set, which contained 544 HNSC samples, was used as the training cohort. We downloaded TCGA–HNSC expression profiles and clinical data from the TCGA data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Finally, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to investigate the differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between the high- and low-risk groups using the “surv_cutpoint” function in the “survival” and “survminer” R packages.

Immune cell infiltration analysis and drug sensitivity prediction analysis

We performed immune infiltration analysis using the “GSVA” R package and the “ssGSEA” method [22]. The analysis was based on a gene set that defines 28 types of immune cells, as previously described by Bindea et al. [26]. This gene set includes markers for T cells (e.g., CD8 + T cells, T helper cells), B cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and other key tumor-infiltrating immune populations. We calculated the signature scores of the different types of immune cells using the “z-score” method to further analyze the differences between the high- and low-risk groups and different NAC outcomes. Furthermore, we conducted Pearson correlation analysis to calculate the correlation between the infiltrating immune cells and the expression of five genes, applying p < 0.05 as our threshold criterion to screen significant immune cell markers. To assess the association between the five candidate biomarkers and drug sensitivity, we utilized the CellMiner database [27, 28]. We downloaded gene expression data and the growth rate values of the NCI-60 cancer cell line for this purpose.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

To validate the expression of the five candidate biomarker genes, we collected an additional 36 independent LHC samples from Beijing Tongren Hospital. We extracted the total RNA from these tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). After performing reverse transcription, we examined the expression levels of the target genes in triplicate using the SYBR Green method. The primers utilized in this study are provided in Table S1. The qPCR results were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Establishment of a novel hypoxia–immune model to predict the response to NAC

Hypoxia and the immune microenvironment are hallmarks of solid tumors that have been linked to chemosensitivity [29, 30]. We detected 40 hypoxia–immune-related genes in our data set, comprising 36 immune genes and 4 hypoxia genes (Fig. 2a). Figure 2b displays their expression levels in our data set. We then subjected these 40 genes to logistic regression analysis to identify crucial predictors of NAC outcomes. Based on the coefficient values, we constructed a predictive model that included one risk gene and four protective genes (Fig. 2c, d). We then calculated the risk scores for all samples using the following formula: risk score = 35.0573 + 0.5348 * GPC4 − 3.0743 * PTGDR2 − 0.445 * HSPA2 − 2.3286 * GPHA2 − 0.3089 * AREG. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on their median risk scores. The results of the correlation analysis between the risk score and five predictors are shown in Fig. 2e. The NAC-insensitive group had significantly higher GPC4 expression, whereas the NAC-sensitive group had a higher expression of PTGDR2, HSPA2, GPHA2, and AREG (Fig. 2f). Patients with poor NAC efficacy demonstrated higher risk scores, as many patients in the high-risk group responded poorly to NAC (Fig. 2g, h). Predictive performance of the hypoxia–immune model in the discovery and TCGA–HNSC data sets

Our hypoxia–immune model yielded an AUC value of 0.854 when predicting NAC outcomes (Fig. 3a), outperforming the individual genes or clinical indexes (Fig. 3b–d). Patients were grouped into low and high risks based on their median scores. Figure 3e illustrates the risk score, clinical status, and five-gene expressions among these groups in our data set. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that the high-risk group had a worse OS and PFS than the low-risk group (Figs. 3f, g and S2). We then confirmed the prognostic efficacy of our hypoxia–immune model in the TCGA–HNSC data set. Patients with higher risk scores had increased mortality, and those in the high-risk group had worse survival than those in the low-risk group (Figs. 3h, i, j and S3). Immune cell infiltration analysis of the hypoxia–immune model

We also compared the levels of 28 types of immune cells infiltrating each sample in our data set between the different risk groups and NAC outcomes. Heat maps displaying immune cell infiltration levels are presented in Fig. 4a, b. Natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages were notably more abundant in the high-risk group, while monocytes and effector memory CD4 T cells were more abundant in the low-risk group (Fig. 4c). The percentages of NK cells and T follicular helper cells were significantly higher in the NAC-insensitive group (Fig. 4d). Given the high infiltration of NK cells in both the high-risk and NAC-insensitive groups, we investigated the correlation between the expression of the five genes in our model and NK cell markers to explore the potential molecular mechanism underlying the effect of NK cells on chemotherapy efficacy. We observed a strong correlation between CXCL1 and the expression of the five genes in our model (Fig. 4e). NK cells participate in tumor immune responses by killing their target cells and producing cytokines, and their migration ability can be influenced by chemokines [31]. Prediction of potentially effective chemotherapeutic drugs based on our hypoxia–immune model

Using the CellMiner NCI-60 database, we examined potential therapeutic drugs for the five hypoxia–immune biomarkers. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted between each gene and the activities of various drugs. Subsequently, we compared the drug sensitivities of the different gene expression groups based on their median expression levels. Patients with high GPC4 expression exhibited lower drug sensitivity to 6-thioguanine (Fig. 6a), while those with high PTGDR2 expression were more sensitive to pipobroman, hydroxyurea, valrubicin, oxaliplatin, and XK-469 (Fig. 6b–f). High HSPA2 expression correlated with low sensitivity to pyrazoloacridine, RH1, belinostat, intedanib, and MK-8033 (Fig. 6g–k), and low GPHA2 expression led to lower teglarinad drug sensitivity (Fig. 6l). In addition, high AREG expression was associated with increased sensitivity to afatinib, sapitinib, and BMS-690514 (Fig. 6m–o). Discussion

NAC is an effective treatment method for patients with locally advanced LHC as it reduces tumor size and preserves organ function well. A retrospective single-center cohort study showed that among 100 patients with stages III–IV LHC who underwent NAC therapy followed by transoral robotic surgery, the 2-year OS rate was as high as 75%, while the PFS rate reached 65% [4]. However, not all patients are suitable candidates for this approach due to their varying sensitivities to NAC, and accurately predicting its efficacy in advance is difficult [32, 33]. Therefore, identifying molecular biomarkers for predicting the effectiveness of NAC is crucial. In this study, we aimed to mine comprehensive transcriptional profiles to construct a tool that can help address this critical clinical issue.

Heterogeneity among different tumor entities may lead to inter-individual variability in response to chemotherapy. Differences in the tumor microenvironment and dominant signaling pathways regulating cellular proliferation can exist across individuals, even for the same pathological type and location [34]. Therefore, identifying predictive biomarkers for chemotherapeutic sensitivity is critical for personalizing treatment and improving patient outcomes. Previous studies have identified several predictive biomarkers for chemotherapeutic sensitivity, such as the abnormal activation of the CPSF1–p62–miR-124-3p signaling axis in breast cancer, harmful mutations in the ERCC2 gene in bladder cancer, and Src expression in esophageal adenocarcinoma [35–37]. Identifying predictive biomarkers for chemotherapeutic sensitivity is crucial for selecting patients likely to benefit from treatment while minimizing toxicity. Moreover, selecting predictive biomarkers for chemosensitivity can build an accurate prediction model, enabling precise stratification, diagnosis, and treatment based on biopsy samples before therapy.

Hypoxia and immune evasion are critical factors that affect patient prognosis in different tumor environments. For example, targeting HIF-1α can promote the infiltration of immune cells, such as NK and CD8+ T cells, into the tumor microenvironment by increasing the release of the chemokine CCL5, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [38]. Wenhao et al. established a 27-gene prognostic risk score incorporating hypoxia, immune, and EMT gene signatures that could predict OS in patients with lung adenocarcinoma and guide treatment [39]. In addition, Likun et al. identified six hypoxia–immune-related long non-coding RNAs that can potentially predict colorectal cancer survival and sensitivity to immunotherapy [40]. Previous studies have also explored the hypoxia–immune typing of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. For example, Jill M et al. developed and validated a hypoxia–immune prognostic transcriptional classifier that may have clinical application for guiding the use of hypoxia modification and targeted immunotherapies in treating head and neck cancers [41]. Huan et al. constructed a model consisting of 14 characteristic factors, including hypoxia-related and EMT-related genes, to evaluate preoperative survival in patients and guide subsequent treatment plans [42]. However, no studies reported predicting NAC response in patients with LHC.

In this study, we have developed and validated a five-gene hypoxia–immune model that predicts the response to NAC and OS in patients with LHC. The AUC of the model reached 0.854, which outperformed those of the individual genes and clinical indexes. Moreover, our analysis indicated that high-risk patients had worse prognoses than low-risk patients in both our data set and the TCGA–HNSC external data set. Our approach aligns with the growing trend of constructing multi-gene biomarker panels that capture complex tumor microenvironment interactions, as seen in the identification of immune repertoire-based biomarkers in B-ALL [12] and comprehensive profiles of molecules, such as HEPACAM2 in CRC [13]. Among the five genes, GPC4 (glypican-4), a member of the membrane protein polysaccharide family (GRIPS), is of particular interest. In colorectal cancer, the interaction between CD36 and GPC4 can promote the proteasome-dependent ubiquitination of GPC4, inhibition of β-catenin/c-myc signaling, and suppression of downstream glycolytic pathways [43]. Given that glycolytic switch is a hallmark of hypoxia and contributes to immunosuppression, GPC4 dysregulation in our model may signify a crucial link between hypoxia-driven metabolic reprogramming and an impaired anti-tumor immune response, ultimately affecting chemosensitivity. Plasma GPC4 levels are significantly elevated in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, and this increase is associated with a significantly worse prognosis [44]. In addition, genetic polymorphisms and GPC4 alleles are associated with susceptibility to Epstein–Barr virus-associated gastric cancer [45]. PTGDR2 (prostaglandin D2 receptor 2) is a G protein-coupled receptor predominantly expressed in CD4+ effector T helper 2 (Th2) cells. PTGDR2 expression is reduced in gastric and colon cancer tissues, and this decrease is correlated with poor patient prognosis [46, 47]. The loss of PTGDR2, a receptor for the pro-resolution lipid mediator PGD2, may predispose the TME toward a pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive state, potentially characterized by dampened Th2 responses and altered macrophage polarization. This immune dysregulation potentially presents a key mechanism by which NAC efficacy is influenced. In addition, the disease-free survival (DFS) of patients with head and neck cancer with PTGDR2 methylation was significantly worse than those without methylation, underscoring its potential as an alternative screening method [48, 49]. HSPA2 (heat shock protein family A member 2), also known as HSP70-2, is frequently overexpressed in various human malignancies and solid tumors. E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF144A can suppress the aggressive phenotype of breast cancer by controlling HSPA2-mediated oncogenic activities. Furthermore, altered levels of HSPA2 methylation have been associated with attenuated chemotherapy effects [50, 51]. Critically, HIF-1α specifically binds to the HSPA2 promoter in hypoxic environments, thereby increasing tumor cell resistance to hypoxia-induced apoptosis [52]. This direct hypoxic regulation positions HSPA2 as a central node in our model, directly transducing the hypoxic signal into pro-survival and chemoresistant programs in cancer cells. GPHA2 (glycoprotein hormone subunit alpha 2) is a member of the dimeric glycoprotein hormone family. It is a novel limbal progenitor cell surface marker and plays a crucial role in corneal immune and angiogenic processes [53, 54]. However, there is currently no research on GPHA2 in tumors. AREG (amphiregulin) is the most extensively studied among the five genes; it serves as a ligand for EGFR, which is involved in the initiation, progression, and metastasis of various tumors. Moreover, AREG plays a crucial role in tumor therapeutic resistance [55]. AREG can induce tumor cell proliferation, invasion, migration, or resistance to apoptosis via EGFR-mediated signaling. Moreover, the EGFR–AREG interaction influences the tumor microenvironment from pretreatment to post-treatment among responders and nonresponders [56, 57]. In addition, AREG controls the production of chemokines that attract monocytes and activate the proangiogenic functions of macrophages [58]. This suggests a dual mechanism in NAC response: intrinsic cancer cell survival via EGFR and extrinsic reshaping of the immune TME toward a pro-tumorigenic phenotype, which may collectively blunt the efficacy of chemotherapy.

This study exemplifies the powerful trend of moving beyond single-gene biomarkers toward integrated models that fully capture the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. The prognostic and therapeutic relevance of such multi-faceted signatures is increasingly evident, as seen in immune repertoire dynamics in B-ALL [15] along with the multi-omics profile of HEPACAM2 [10] and circRNA-mediated regulatory axis hsa_circ_0005939 in CRC [11]. In this context, the ability of our hypoxia–immune model to predict patient-specific drug sensitivities (e.g., AREG to afatinib/sapitinib) aligns with the capacity of ZNF695 expression to correlate with distinct drug responses in CESC [12], collectively highlighting the immense potential of such models in personalizing cancer therapy.

In this study, we developed a five-gene hypoxia–immune model that can predict the sensitivity and prognosis of patients with locally advanced LHC treated with NAC. Our findings were validated using both the TCGA–HNSC external data set and an independent clinical sample cohort. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the association between the five-gene signature and immune infiltration, particularly the paradoxical role of NK cells, remains correlative. As noted, we lack direct functional evidence, such as GSVA of NK cell-related pathways or experimental validation of NK cell cytotoxicity, to definitively establish the mechanistic link. Second, the precise molecular mechanisms by which the individual genes regulate the hypoxic immune microenvironment to confer chemotherapy resistance require further investigation through regulatory network analysis and functional assays in future studies. Third, the data sets suitable for our research were limited and difficult to obtain from public databases, which may have restricted the comprehensiveness of this study. In addition, we only validated the expression of genes in the model through qPCR analysis in another cohort, so further molecular biological and cellular experiments are required. Finally, future clinical trials are necessary to refine patient selection criteria and maximize the therapeutic potential of NAC in the treatment of locally advanced LHC.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a five-gene hypoxia–immune model can predict the sensitivity and prognosis of patients with locally advanced LHC treated with NAC. Establishing this novel model provides valuable insights into the mechanism underlying the hypoxic immune microenvironment and can assist in selecting more appropriate treatments to maximize benefits for these patients.
