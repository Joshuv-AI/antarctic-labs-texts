Diagnostic accuracy of IHDS and MoCA in screening for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia
Authors: Mulualem Kelebie, Gebresilassie Tadesse, Setegn Fentahun, Girum Nakie, Birtukan Fasil, Gidey Rtbey, Dawed Ali, Girmaw Medfu Takelle, Yeneneh Workie, Zemene Yiglet, Mulu Getnet, Getasew Kibralew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) remain prevalent among adults living with HIV, even in the era of sustained antiretroviral therapy (ART). Accurate screening for HAND remains a challenge in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia, as comprehensive neuropsychological assessments thought effective are often limited by their high demands for time, specialized personnel, and logistical resources. The International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) are brief cognitive screening tools commonly used in clinical settings; however, their diagnostic accuracy for detecting HAND in the Ethiopian population has not been well established. This study aims to evaluate and compare the sensitivity and specificity of the IHDS and MoCA in identifying HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia.MethodsA multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted among 290 adults living with HIV attending three primary hospitals in Ethiopia. Participants aged ≥ 18 years were recruited using systematic random sampling. Clinical data, including baseline CD4⁺ T-cell counts and recent viral load measurements, were extracted from medical records. Cognitive function was assessed using the IHDS and MoCA, with the Frascati criteria used as the diagnostic reference standard, and factors independently associated with IHDS scores were identified through multivariable linear regression.ResultsThe study included 290 adults living with HIV, with a mean age of 40.6 ± 8.04 years. Based on the Frascati criteria, 43.4% of participants exhibited HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND), with asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment being the most common subtype (25.4%), followed by mild neurocognitive disorder (13.3%) and HIV-associated dementia (4.7%). Using the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS), 47.9% of participants were identified as cognitively impaired, indicating possible HAND, whereas the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) identified 36.2% as impaired. The IHDS demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, with an optimal cutoff score of ≤ 10 (sensitivity 85.3% and specificity 77%), compared to the MoCA at a cutoff score of 21 (sensitivity 62.1% and specificity 81%). Multivariable linear regression analysis revealed that higher educational attainment (β = 0.30, p = 0.005) and male sex (β = 0.41, p = 0.045) were independently associated with better neurocognitive performance, while being unmarried (β = −0.29, p = 0.01) and a longer duration of antiretroviral therapy (β = −0.26, p = 0.04) were significantly associated with poorer cognitive outcomes.ConclusionHIV-associated neurocognitive disorders remain highly prevalent among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. The IHDS demonstrated better sensitivity than the MoCA for detecting HAND, making it a more suitable screening tool in resource-limited settings. Educational status, sex, marital status, and duration of ART were significant predictors of cognitive performance. Integrating routine cognitive screening using IHDS may improve early identification and management of HAND.Keywords: HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, International HIV dementia scale, Montreal cognitive assessment, HIV/AIDS, Ethiopia

Introduction

HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) encompasses a spectrum of cognitive, behavioral, motor, and autonomic impairments resulting from HIV-related central nervous system pathology, either through direct viral neurotoxicity or indirectly via opportunistic infections, with milder forms remaining prevalent despite the marked reduction in the incidence of HIV-associated dementia (HAD) in the era of highly active antiretroviral therapy [1–3]. It has significantly altered the progression of HIV-associated dementia (HAD), with global evidence demonstrating improvements in cognitive and neuropsychological functioning across various antiretroviral regimens; notably, early initiation—regardless of traditional clinical or immunological criteria—has been associated with a decreased incidence and severity of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders [4–6].

HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) remain a significant global public health challenge, as cognitive impairment continues to affect a substantial proportion of people living with HIV (PLWH), even in the era of widespread access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) and effective viral suppression [7, 8]. It is a major contributor to morbidity and long-term disability in this population, with detrimental effects on cognitive functioning, daily activities, and overall quality of life [9, 10]. Before 2007, the lack of standardized definitions for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) led to considerable variability in reported prevalence across different populations and regions [11, 12]. The Frascati criteria introduced a uniform framework for the diagnosis of HAND in adults, enhancing comparability across studies and facilitating research into its clinical consequences [13, 14].

The criteria mandate comprehensive neuropsychological assessment across at least five cognitive domains, evaluation of functional abilities in activities of daily living, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and confirmation that HIV is the primary contributor to cognitive decline [15, 16]. HAND is stratified into three severity levels: asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI), mild neurocognitive disorder (MND), and HIV-associated dementia (HAD) [17, 18]. ANI and MND are defined by performance ≥ 1 SD below normative means in two or more cognitive domains, with MND additionally associated with mild functional impairment, whereas HAD requires ≥ 2 SD deficits with marked functional decline [19–21].

Globally, the prevalence of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) among individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) shows considerable heterogeneity, with reported rates ranging from 20% to 50%, and some studies documenting prevalence as high as 69% [22]. A recent meta-analysis estimated the overall pooled global prevalence of HAND to be approximately 50.41%, with region-specific estimates of 50.015% in Europe, 49.566% in Africa, 52.032% in Asia, and 50.407% in the United States [23]. In Africa context, reported prevalence rates of HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment (HAND) vary substantially, ranging from 14% to 88%, with meta-analytic estimates indicating a pooled prevalence of 46.34% across the continent, approximately 53% in sub-Saharan Africa, and 39.15% in Ethiopia, highlighting notable regional disparities in the burden of HAND [24–26].

The gold standard for assessing cognitive impairments in individuals with HIV is the administration of a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, which offers high sensitivity and specificity and evaluates multiple cognitive domains—including memory, executive function, and attention—for the classification of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) according to the widely endorsed Frascati criteria [27, 28]. Nevertheless, the implementation of this approach in routine clinical practice is often limited by its substantial requirements for time, specialized personnel, and logistical resources [21, 29]. As a result, several brief cognitive screening instruments have been developed to enable the early detection of cognitive impairment in individuals living with HIV/AIDS [30, 31]. Among these, the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the HIV Dementia Scale (HDS) are specifically designed to evaluate subcortical cognitive deficits, which are frequently observed in this population [32].

Furthermore, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) originally validated for the detection of mild cognitive impairment across various neurological disorders, has gained increasing utility in the evaluation of HAND due to its broad assessment of multiple cognitive domains and its ease of administration in clinical settings [33–35]. In a study conducted among HIV-positive individuals in the United States, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS), with sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 87%, respectively, versus 88% sensitivity and 57% specificity for the IHDS [32].

A study conducted in China among male HIV patients and matched controls reported that a MoCA cut-off score of < 26 yielded a sensitivity of 79%, specificity of 91%, and a Youden index of 0.70, while an IHDS cut-off score of ≤ 10 demonstrated a sensitivity of 68%, specificity of 87%, and a Youden index of 0.55 [36]. In a cohort study from Germany, the IHDS cut-off of ≤ 10 showed improved diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 94%, specificity of 86%, and a Youden index of 0.80 [37]. Similarly, a study involving a Brazilian cohort of HIV-positive individuals found that an IHDS cut-off score of ≤ 10 resulted in a sensitivity of 55% and specificity of 80% [38]. Meta-analyses indicated that the sensitivity of the IHDS for detecting HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) varied considerably across studies, ranging from 45% to over 80%, with a pooled sensitivity of 62%, suggesting moderate overall diagnostic utility [30].

The international HIV dementia scale demonstrates moderate sensitivity for detecting advanced cognitive impairment, such as HIV-associated dementia (HAD), but shows limited sensitivity for milder HAND subtypes, including asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI) and mild neurocognitive disorder (MND) [39, 40]. Conversely, although the MoCA is a valid tool for assessing mild cognitive impairment, its diagnostic utility may be limited by educational and cultural biases, as well as susceptibility to confounding by psychiatric conditions such as depression, thereby reducing its accuracy and interpretability in low-resource, culturally diverse settings [41–43]. The International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) are widely utilized for the screening of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND); however, their diagnostic performance has shown variability across different populations [44, 45].

In Ethiopia, factors such as low educational attainment, limited access to specialized healthcare, and significant linguistic and cultural diversity highlight the need for population-specific validation of neurocognitive screening tools. Although the IHDS and MoCA are widely used globally, their diagnostic accuracy—including sensitivity and specificity—for detecting HAND in the Ethiopian context remains unclear. Validation is crucial to ensure that these tools accurately identify true cases of HAND (sensitivity) while minimizing false-positive results (specificity) within this population. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate and compare the sensitivity and specificity of the IHDS and MoCA in detecting HAND among Amharic-speaking adults living with HIV, with the ultimate goal of guiding the implementation of reliable, culturally appropriate, and feasible screening strategies in low-resource settings.

Methods and materials

We conducted a multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study among HIV-infected adults attending three primary hospitals in Ethiopia. The study was carried out between April 15 and May 28, 2023, and aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) for screening HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. The study sites were selected primary hospitals located in the South Gondar Zone of the Amhara Region, Ethiopia. This zone comprises 13 districts (woredas) and seven town administrations, with an estimated population of approximately three million [46, 47]. The local healthcare system includes nine hospitals (eight primary hospitals and one comprehensive specialized hospital), 109 health centers, and 402 health posts. The Amhara Region bears approximately 28% of Ethiopia’s HIV burden, with an estimated 171,555 people living with HIV/AIDS out of a national total of 612,925 [48, 49].

Three primary hospitals—Addis Zemen, Nifas Mewicha, and Ebnat—were randomly selected from the eight primary hospitals providing HIV care services using a lottery-based sampling technique to ensure representativeness. Participants were recruited using a systematic random sampling technique. Eligible patients attending the ART clinics for routine follow-up visits during the study period were considered for inclusion. Based on an estimated patient flow of approximately 400 patients per month, a sampling interval of four was determined for each of the three primary hospitals. A random starting point was selected from among the first four patients, after which every fourth patient presenting to the outpatient department (OPD) was approached for eligibility assessment and potential enrollment. To ensure that participants were selected at regular and unbiased intervals throughout the data collection period, thereby minimizing the risk of selection bias and all patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria at the specified interval were invited to participate in the study.

Participants

A total of 294 HIV-positive adults receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) were enrolled in this cross-sectional study. Eligible participants were aged 18 to 64 years and actively engaged in routine ART follow-up in the three selected primary hospitals. Inclusion criteria required a confirmed HIV diagnosis, ongoing ART in accordance with the Ethiopian national HIV treatment guidelines, consistent clinic attendance, and the capacity to provide informed consent. Participants were also required to be physically and cognitively capable of completing standardized neurocognitive assessments. Exclusion criteria comprised the presence of severe acute medical conditions and major psychiatric disorders (schizophrenia and bipolar disorder), neurodevelopmental disorder like intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorders, a documented history of cerebrovascular accidents (stroke), or significant sensory impairments (profound visual or speech deficits) that could interfere with the validity of cognitive evaluations. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Gondar.

Prior to participation, all individuals received comprehensive information regarding the study’s objectives, methodology, potential risks, and anticipated benefits. The authors confirm that all procedures contributing to this research were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2013 [50]. Participants were informed of their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty and were encouraged to ask questions about the study procedures. All information was collected anonymously, and participant confidentiality was strictly protected throughout the study. Participation was voluntary, and no form of coercion or compensation was used. All data were collected, managed, and securely stored by the primary investigators. Four participants were excluded because they were unable to provide informed consent, primarily due to significant cognitive impairments that hindered their comprehension of the study information and their ability to demonstrate understanding during the consent process.

Measures and procedures

Comprehensive demographic and clinical data were collected from all participants, including age, sex, educational level, and occupational status. Clinical assessments encompassed HIV-related immunologic and virologic markers, including baseline CD4⁺ T-cell counts extracted from medical records at ART initiation and plasma viral load measurements obtained from the most recent laboratory evaluations. Additionally medical illnesses were systematically taken from patient charts as part of the clinical assessment. Detailed information on antiretroviral therapy (ART), including the specific regimen and duration of treatment, was also obtained. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a psychometrically robust, self-report instrument widely utilized in both clinical and research settings for the screening, diagnosis, and longitudinal evaluation of depressive disorders [51].

The PHQ-9 consists of nine items that align with the diagnostic criteria for Major Depressive Disorder as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 27. Symptom severity is categorized as minimal [1–4], mild [5–9], moderate [10–14], moderately severe [15–19], and severe [20–27]. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 is commonly used to identify individuals with clinically significant depressive symptoms [52, 53]. In addition, participants’ substance use history was assessed, with particular emphasis on the consumption of alcohol, khat, and tobacco.

A neurocognitive batteries with normative data validated for use in Ethiopia was administered to all participants in their native language, Amharic [2, 54, 55]. The International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) is a brief screening tool designed to assess cognitive impairment associated with HIV [32, 54]. It evaluates three cognitive domains: memory registration and delayed recall of four words, motor speed through timed finger tapping, and psychomotor speed. The total score ranges from 0 to 12, with a score of ≤ 10 indicating potential cognitive impairment [32].

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a brief, standardized cognitive screening tool developed by Nasreddine et al. to detect mild cognitive impairment (MCI ) [33]. It consists of 12 subtasks assessing key cognitive domains, including visuospatial and executive functioning, short-term memory, attention, concentration, working memory, language, and orientation. The one-page, 30-point instrument takes approximately 10 min to administer and includes tasks such as delayed recall of five nouns (5 points), clock drawing (3 points), cube copying (1 point), Trail Making Test B (1 point), phonemic fluency (1 point), abstraction (2 points), serial subtraction (3 points), target tapping (1 point), digit span forward and backward (1 point each), animal naming (3 points), sentence repetition (2 points), and orientation to time and place (6 points). A cutoff score of 26 is typically used to differentiate normal from impaired cognitive function [56].

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a validated cognitive screening tool, has been culturally adapted and linguistically validated for use in the Ethiopian context by translating it into Amharic and implementing specific modifications—including substituting English alphabet characters with their Amharic equivalents, replacing letters in verbal fluency tasks with phonemically corresponding Amharic letters, and culturally contextualizing abstraction items—to ensure both linguistic accuracy and cultural relevance while maintaining the original instrument’s conceptual integrity and psychometric validity [57–59]. All participants were assessed under standardized conditions by psychometrists who are qualified psychiatrists trained in administering the International Neurocognitive Normative Study test battery [60, 61]. Standard neuropsychological assessments included the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which were administered sequentially during a single visit following standardized procedures [62–64].

Gold standard assessment

In this study, the diagnosis of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) was established using the Frascati criteria, which served as the gold standard for evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [11, 65–67]. The Frascati criteria are internationally endorsed diagnostic guidelines that classify HAND into three categories based on the severity of cognitive impairment and functional status: asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI), mild neurocognitive disorder (MND), and HIV-associated dementia (HAD) [68, 69]. Diagnosis was based on a standardized neuropsychological battery that assessed performance across seven cognitive domains in accordance with the Frascati criteria [16, 70, 71].

Visual learning and memory were evaluated using the Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Test, while verbal learning and memory were assessed with a Story Recall test [72–74]. Language function was measured using the Category Fluency test, and motor speed was assessed with the Grooved Pegboard Test. Speed of information processing was evaluated using the Trail Making Test Part A, the Symbol Digit Modalities Test, and the Stroop Color–Word Test [75, 76]. Attention and working memory were assessed with the Digit Span Test, and executive functioning was evaluated using the Trail Making Test Parts B and C, the Clock Drawing Test, and the Stroop incongruent condition [77–79].

A diagnosis of ANI or MND requires performance at least 1 standard deviation (SD) below the normative mean in a minimum of two cognitive domains; however, MND is distinguished by evidence of mild interference in daily functioning, whereas ANI presents without functional complaints. HAD, representing the most severe form of HAND is defined by performance ≥ 2 SD below the mean in at least two cognitive domains, accompanied by significant functional impairment [80, 81]. Additionally, at least one impaired domain must lie outside of motor or sensory-perceptual functioning, and alternative etiologies—such as psychiatric disorders, substance use, or other neurological conditions—must be excluded [82, 83]. These diagnostic frameworks were used as reference standards to assess the sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic performance of the IHDS and MoCA in detecting HAND among Ethiopian adults living with HIV [84, 85].

Data analysis

All collected data were rigorously checked for completeness and internal consistency before being entered into EpiData version 4.6.02 and subsequently exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed using the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) as continuous variables, with cognitive impairment classified according to the Frascati criteria as the state variable. Diagnostic performance was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive and negative likelihood ratios across a range of cutoff scores for both tools. Optimal cutoff points were identified using Youden’s J index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1), with cutoffs considered adequate if sensitivity exceeded 0.80 and specificity was greater than 0.60, ensuring high detection capability with an acceptably low false-positive rate [86]. Additionally, multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors independently associated with IHDS scores, with all model assumptions verified prior to interpretation.

Results

A total of 290 HIV-positive individuals were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 40.6 years (SD = 8.04; range: 18–64 years). The prevalence of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) was 47.9% based on the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and 36.2% according to the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Using the Frascati criteria, 56.6% of participants demonstrated normal neurocognitive functioning, while 43.4% met the criteria for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders. Among those with impairment, asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment was the most common subtype (25.4%), followed by mild neurocognitive disorder (13.3%) and HIV-associated dementia (4.7%). Regarding educational attainment, 43.1% of participants had no formal education, indicating a high prevalence of limited literacy. Females represented the majority of participants with primary (≤ grade 8; 60.3%) and secondary (grades 9–12; 69.6%) education, whereas males predominated among those with a diploma or higher education (64.0%). Overall, females accounted for 60.0% of the study population (Table 1).

Regarding the clinical characteristics of the participants, the majorities were classified as WHO clinical stage I (85.5%) and exhibited CD4 counts exceeding 500cells/mm³ (44.5%). Participants had a substantial duration of antiretroviral therapy exposure, with a mean ART duration of 10.9 years (SD = 4.32). The majority of the cohort had long-term ART exposure, with 42.1% receiving ART for 11–15 years, followed by 31.4% with mid-term exposure (6–10 years). A smaller proportion of participants had very long-term exposure (> 15 years; 14.1%), while 12.4% had short-term ART exposure (≤ 5 years). Comorbid medical conditions were absent in most participants (85.5%). Viral load assessment showed that 46.6% of participants achieved viral suppression, defined as a viral load < 50 copies/mL. Additionally, participants with a viral load < 1000 copies/mL had lower proportions of HAND (ANI, MND, and HAD) compared to those with a viral load ≥ 1000 copies/mL. Despite this, a substantial proportion (62.8%) exhibited suboptimal medication adherence, and 32.8% were clinically identified with depressive symptoms. Additionally, over half of the participants reported substance use, with alcohol (23.5%), cigarette smoking (20.3%), and khat consumption (14.1%) being the most prevalent. The majority of participants were gainfully employed, with 51.4% engaged in private employment and 23.4% employed in the government sector, while 25.2% were unemployed. Most participants (84.1%) were living with family, indicating strong household support, whereas 15.9% lived alone or with other relatives (Table 2).

The psychometric evaluation of the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) among HIV-positive patients in South Gondar primary hospitals demonstrated varying diagnostic performances across different cutoff scores. In our study, the optimal cutoff point for the IHDS was identified as ≤ 10, yielding a sensitivity of 85.3%, specificity of 77%, Youden’s J-index of 0.62, positive predictive value (PPV) of 71.2%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 88.7%, indicating a well-balanced diagnostic utility. A slightly higher cutoff of 10.5 showed comparable sensitivity (86.4%) but lower specificity (68%), with a Youden’s J-index of 0.54, positive likelihood ratio (PLR) of 2.7, negative likelihood ratio (NLR) of 0.2, PPV of 71.7%, and NPV of 88.8%.

The MoCA, the optimal cutoff identified in our study was ≤ 21, which provided a better balance between sensitivity and specificity than the conventional threshold of ≤ 26. At this cutoff, MoCA achieved a sensitivity of 62.1%, specificity of 81%, Youden’s J-index of 0.43, PLR of 4.26, NLR of 0.47, PPV of 68.6%, and NPV of 76.2%. In contrast, at the traditional cutoff of ≤ 26, MoCA demonstrated higher sensitivity (86.2%) but reduced specificity (54.6%), with a Youden’s J-index of 0.40, PPV of 55.9%, and NPV of 85.6%. Reliability analysis showed acceptable internal consistency for both tools, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.79 for the IHDS and 0.70 for the MoCA, supporting their use as effective screening instruments for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) in this setting (Tables 3 and 4).

Factors associated with international HIV dementia scale scores

A multiple linear regression analysis indicated that several factors significantly affected neurocognitive performance, as measured by IHDS scores, among HIV-positive patients in South Gondar primary hospitals. Specifically, higher educational status (β = 0.30, p = 0.005) and being male (β = 0.41, p = 0.045) were significantly associated with better cognitive function. In contrast, being unmarried (β = -0.29, p = 0.01) and having a longer duration on ART (β = -0.26, p = 0.04) were linked to lower IHDS scores, suggesting poorer cognitive outcomes. Other variables—including occupation, living conditions, CD4 count, medication adherence, and viral load—did not show statistically significant associations, although medication adherence (β = 0.34, p = 0.07) showed a positive trend toward improved cognitive performance ( Table 5).

Discussion

A total of 290 HIV-positive individuals were enrolled in the study; the prevalence of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) was 47.9% based on the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and 36.2% according to the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Using the Frascati criteria, 56.6% of participants demonstrated normal neurocognitive functioning, while 43.4% met criteria for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders. Among those with impairment, asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment was the most common subtype (25.4%), followed by mild neurocognitive disorder (13.3%) and HIV-associated dementia (4.7%). Despite being considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of HAND, the Frascati criteria are limited by their reliance on attributing cognitive impairment directly to HIV infection, particularly in cases of asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI), which may be confounded by multiple factors such as substance use, depression, aging, and low educational attainment, thereby potentially leading to misclassification of HIV-related cognitive disorder [13, 66, 87, 88].

The findings also indicate that the IHDS is a more effective screening tool than the MoCA for detecting HAND among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. The IHDS demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, with higher sensitivity (85.3% vs. 62.1%) and a larger area under the curve (AUC = 0.81 vs. 0.71), making it particularly suitable for identifying cognitive impairment in resource-limited settings. Although the MoCA exhibited slightly higher specificity, its lower sensitivity limits its utility as a screening instrument for HAND. These results highlight the variability in psychometric performance between the IHDS and MoCA and underscore the importance of selecting context-appropriate tools for neurocognitive assessment in HIV-positive populations [54, 89, 90].

The International HIV Dementia Scale is specifically designed to detect more advanced stages of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND), such as HIV-associated dementia, and has demonstrated higher sensitivity for identifying moderate to severe cognitive impairment. However, it is less effective in detecting milder forms, such as asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI) [55, 91]. In contrast, the MoCA is a general cognitive screening tool that evaluates a broader range of cognitive domains and is better suited for identifying mild cognitive impairment [91, 92]. Nevertheless, in the context of HIV—particularly in resource-limited settings—the MoCA often shows lower sensitivity compared to the IHDS, limiting its effectiveness in detecting the early stages of HAND [93, 94].

A systematic review and meta-analysis identified an optimal IHDS cutoff score of 10.127 for screening HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND), with a sensitivity of 0.65, a specificity of 0.647, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.693 [65]. In a study conducted in Thailand involving 74 participants, the MoCA, but not the IHDS, significantly differentiated asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI) from cognitively normal (CN) individuals, with AUCs of 0.71 and 0.56, respectively, although both tools demonstrated poor sensitivity and specificity [91]. In Tanzania, a study of individuals with HAND (n = 118) found that an IHDS cutoff score of ≤ 9 produced a sensitivity of 60.9%, specificity of 77.7%, and a Youden’s J-index of 0.454 [95]. A cohort study in East Africa reported that applying the standard IHDS cutoff of 10 resulted in a sensitivity of 91%, specificity of 17%, and an AUC of 0.63 [96]. Conversely, a validation study conducted in a German-speaking HIV outpatient clinic demonstrated that the same IHDS cutoff of 10 yielded a sensitivity of 94% and specificity of 86% for detecting HIV-associated dementia [37].

Similarly, a study from South Africa reported that an IHDS cutoff of 10.5 achieved optimal diagnostic utility, with a sensitivity of 69%, specificity of 74%, and the highest sensitivity observed for HIV-associated dementia [97]. The variation in sensitivity and specificity of both the IHDS and MoCA across different studies and countries is primarily attributed to socio-cultural differences, educational disparities, healthcare system characteristics, and linguistic or cultural adaptation of the tools [98–100]. These contextual factors influence test comprehension and performance, thereby affecting diagnostic outcomes [101, 102]. Furthermore, differences in antiretroviral therapy (ART) status, demographic profiles, sample sizes, and the reference standards used to classify cognitive impairment contribute to the heterogeneity in diagnostic accuracy [103, 104].

The differing sensitivity and specificity of the IHDS and MoCA observed in this study may be partly explained by the distinct neurocognitive domains each tool is designed to assess [105]. The IHDS is primarily intended to detect subcortical dementia, which is characteristic of more advanced HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND), such as HIV-associated dementia [90, 106]. In contrast, the MoCA is more sensitive to cortical dysfunction and is better suited for detecting milder forms of impairment, including asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment [42, 107]. This fundamental difference in diagnostic focus may contribute to the variation in their performance, highlighting the importance of selecting appropriate screening tools based on the clinical presentation and stage of HAND.

Higher educational attainment was significantly associated with better neurocognitive performance (β = 0.30, p = 0.005), consistent with the cognitive reserve hypothesis, which suggests that education strengthens the brain’s capacity to withstand neuropathological damage. Higher education may enhance neurocognitive resilience by promoting the development of more efficient neural pathways, increased synaptic connectivity, and improved adaptive strategies [2, 108]. Additionally, individuals with greater educational attainment often demonstrate higher health literacy, better healthcare engagement, and more consistent adherence to antiretroviral therapy, all contributing to favorable neurocognitive outcomes [109, 110]. In contrast, lower education is associated with socioeconomic disadvantage, limited healthcare access, and reduced cognitive stimulation, increasing susceptibility to HAND [111, 112]. Furthermore, lower education has been associated with structural brain differences, such as reduced cortical thickness and frontal lobe volume, which are linked to impaired memory and executive function [113, 114].

This study found that male sex was significantly associated with better neurocognitive performance than female sex (β = 0.41, p = 0.045), suggesting a higher risk of HAND in women. This disparity may be driven by hormonal and immune differences; sex steroids like estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone influence cognitive function, with hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle, menopause, and pregnancy adding complexity for women living with HIV [115, 116]. Estradiol also impacts viral transcription and neurocognitive processes, while both estradiol and progesterone modulate immune responses via cytokines and chemokines [117]. Women tend to exhibit heightened immune activation and elevated inflammatory markers such as sCD163, and plasma neopterin levels have been linked to neurocognitive impairment in women but not men, indicating that sex-specific immune mechanisms may contribute to increased vulnerability to HAND in females [118–120].

In addition to biological factors, sociocultural determinants may also contribute to sex differences in neurocognitive outcomes. In many resource-limited settings, including Ethiopia, women often have lower levels of formal education and reduced access to educational opportunities compared with men, largely due to entrenched gender norms and socioeconomic constraints [121–123]. Men are more likely to engage in cognitively stimulating activities, participate in formal employment, and assume social roles that enhance cognitive reserve, whereas women frequently bear disproportionate caregiving responsibilities and have limited access to educational and economic resources [124–126]. Such disparities in education, literacy, and cognitive engagement may exacerbate neurocognitive vulnerability among women, increasing their risk of developing HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND).

This study found that being unmarried was significantly associated with poorer neurocognitive outcomes among HIV-positive individuals (β = −0.29, p = 0.01), reinforcing existing evidence that marital status may play a protective role in cognitive health. Being married is often associated with enhanced social support, emotional stability, and engagement in healthier lifestyle behaviors, which may help mitigate the progression of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) [89, 127]. In contrast, unmarried individuals—particularly those who are widowed, divorced, or living alone—may be more vulnerable to psychosocial stress, social isolation, and reduced motivation to engage in health-promoting activities, all of which are established risk factors for cognitive decline [128]. Chronic exposure to stress and a lack of meaningful social interactions may adversely affect neural integrity over time, potentially accelerating neurodegenerative processes [129–131].

A longer duration of antiretroviral therapy (ART) was significantly associated with poorer neurocognitive performance, suggesting a potential cumulative effect of HIV-related neuroinflammation or ART-related neurotoxicity over time (β = −0.26, p = 0.04). Given that prolonged ART exposure typically coincides with advancing age, it is plausible that the neurocognitive decline observed in older HIV-positive individuals may, in part, be mediated by extended ART use [132, 133]. Age itself is a well-established risk factor for HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) [134]. However, this association may be confounded by the increased prevalence of cerebrovascular risk factors in older adults, including hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia [135]. Data also indicate that older age, elevated systolic blood pressure, increased body mass index (BMI), high serum cholesterol, and a diagnosis of AIDS are independently associated with poorer global neuropsychological functioning [136, 137]. These findings suggest that neurocognitive impairment in older HIV-positive individuals likely arises from a multifactorial process involving vascular comorbidities, the chronic effects of HIV infection, and the potential neurotoxic burden of prolonged ART exposure [8].

Limitation

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the study population was restricted to Amharic-speaking individuals, which limits the generalizability of the results to Ethiopia’s broader ethno-linguistic and cultural diversity. Second, substantial variability in educational attainment and sociocultural background—particularly among older and rural participants—may have influenced performance on cognitive screening tools such as the MoCA and IHDS, despite efforts at linguistic translation and cultural adaptation. Third, the study did not systematically assess or control for key confounding factors, including psychiatric comorbidities such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and anxiety, all of which are known to affect neurocognitive performance and may have introduced residual confounding, thereby reducing internal validity. Fourth, the absence of a demographically matched HIV-negative control group limits the ability to establish causal associations and further constrains external validity. Fifth, daily functioning was determined using interview-based clinical ratings; however, because self- and informant-reported functional difficulties distinguish ANI from MND, classification may be vulnerable to reporting inaccuracies, and ANI diagnoses may partly reflect limited insight into functional impairments in PLWH. Finally, the finger-tapping task in the IHDS may have introduced variability due to unstandardized finger separation, potentially affecting motor scores, and future studies could use motion-tracking or computer vision tools to improve accuracy.

Conclusion

HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) remain highly prevalent among adults living with HIV in Ethiopia. Both the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were useful screening tools; however, the IHDS demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, with higher sensitivity and greater overall accuracy. This makes it a more practical and context-appropriate options for resource-limited settings, particularly among populations with lower educational levels. Additionally, factors such as educational status, sex, marital status, and duration of antiretroviral therapy were significantly associated with cognitive performance. Incorporating routine cognitive screening using the IHDS into HIV care may enhance early detection and management of HAND, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Further validation across diverse populations is recommended to strengthen screening strategies and support timely clinical interventions.
