Self‐administered tele‐ultrasound in fetal telemonitoring: Prospective study
Authors: Shariva S. Kariman, Sophie Lekkerkerker, Judith Ummels, Bauke Adriaanse, Josephus F. M. van den Heuvel, Mireille N. Bekker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDigital innovations in ultrasound are enabling tele‐ultrasound and remote guidance, offering new opportunities to strengthen hospital‐at‐home care. This study evaluated the feasibility and user experience of self‐administered ultrasound by pregnant women with real‐time guidance.Material and MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included pregnant women with uncomplicated singleton pregnancies between 24 and 37 weeks of gestation. Participants used the portable tele‐ultrasound device Pulsenmore ES (Pulsenmore Ltd., Omer) at home to perform clinician‐guided, real‐time transabdominal 2D scans twice weekly, completing eight scans over a 28‐day period. Real‐time guidance was provided by experienced obstetric healthcare professionals. Primary outcome was feasibility, including image acquisition, interpretability, interobserver agreement for saved images, and technical aspects. Secondary outcomes included the System Usability Scale and the self‐developed Telemonitoring Survey for user experience. Additionally, a focus group meeting assessed obstetric healthcare professionals' perspectives on the system.ResultsThirty pregnant women participated, with a mean age of 32.6 years, median body mass index of 23.3 kg/m2, and a mean gestational age of 29 weeks (range 24.3–34.5). All participants completed eight ultrasound scans, resulting in 240 tele‐ultrasound examinations with a 100% success rate for predefined parameters, including fetal position, fetal heart activity, deepest pocket of amniotic fluid, and placental location. Across the study, remote connection and image acquisition times shortened to a median of 5 and 4 min, respectively. Retrospective analysis was possible for 226 scans, yielding a 99.9% parameter identification rate and perfect interobserver agreement (100%). Technical errors occurred in 16.3% of examinations, mainly due to connection issues. No association was found between scan duration and maternal or pregnancy characteristics such as gestational age, body mass index, and placental location. Participant experience was highly positive, with a mean System Usability Scale score of 80.7 and a satisfaction score of 4.4/5 on the Telemonitoring Survey, reflecting excellent usability. Ultrasound experts found the system user‐friendly, with sufficient image quality and functionality.ConclusionsSelf‐administered fetal tele‐ultrasound is feasible and associated with high user satisfaction and usability. This study supported its potential as a complementary tool in obstetric care, highlighting tele‐ultrasound's promise to enhance accessibility, patient engagement, and strengthen hospital‐at‐home strategies.

Key message

Self‐administered tele‐ultrasound with real‐time clinician guidance is feasible, achieves high‐quality image acquisition, and is associated with high user satisfaction. This approach may support hospital‐at‐home care by improving accessibility and patient engagement in obstetric practice.

INTRODUCTION

As healthcare systems face rising demands driven by workforce shortages, increasing care costs, and an increasing burden of chronic disease, hospital‐at‐home models offer a safe alternative for delivering patient‐centered care. 1 In obstetric care, telemonitoring embodies this shift by enabling pregnant individuals to remain in their home environment while still receiving continuous, expert oversight. Research shows that patients value the convenience of telemonitoring and the comfort of being at home, contributing to a positive patient experience. 2 These factors support better adherence, greater engagement, and an enhanced sense of control over their care. 3 , 4 Understanding patient experiences is especially important in high‐risk pregnancies, where stress and anxiety are common. In this context, successful integration of tele‐ultrasound into prenatal care requires not only technological feasibility but also careful assessment of patient experience. 1 , 2 , 3

Telemonitoring in high‐risk pregnancies has been well studied and is increasingly integrated into standard clinical practice. Evidence shows that remote antenatal monitoring, including cardiotocography transmission, symptom reporting, and blood pressure monitoring, is as safe as in‐hospital admission. 1 The integration of digital health into obstetric care has demonstrated a valuable tool for improving prenatal care, patient experience, and satisfaction, and cost‐effectiveness. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

Ultrasound is the primary imaging modality to assess the pregnancy, forming a cornerstone of obstetric care. 6 Traditionally, ultrasound examinations are conducted in hospital settings by trained healthcare professionals. Recent digital innovations have expanded the ability to perform and interpret ultrasound outside the traditional clinical setting, giving digital health and mobile medical devices a possible role in obstetric care. 7 Technologies such as tele‐ultrasound now enable remote guidance and evaluation of acquired US images, improving the accessibility of obstetric services, facilitating remote healthcare, and task shifting. 8

However, research on self‐administered tele‐ultrasound remains limited. This study aims to assess the feasibility and user experience of self‐administered routine transabdominal ultrasound by pregnant women, with real‐time guidance from an obstetric healthcare professional.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and setting

Between November 2024 and May 2025, a single‐center, prospective observational cohort study was conducted at the University Medical Center Utrecht (UMCU) in the Netherlands, involving pregnant women with a gestational age between 24 and 37 weeks. The study was exempted from approval by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC) NedMec (24U‐1645).

Participants

Women over 18 years of age with an uncomplicated singleton pregnancy between 24 + 0 and 36 + 6 weeks of gestation were recruited at the outpatient clinic and through social media. Women with a twin pregnancy, a body mass index (BMI) over 35, fetal anomalies, or those who were not familiar with smartphone use were excluded from the study.

Intervention

Tele‐ultrasound was the intervention in this study, using a portable ultrasound device: the Pulsenmore ES (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). This system consists of a wireless ultrasound transducer (central frequency of 3.5/2–5 Mhz) that can be coupled with a smartphone and its corresponding application and a clinician's web‐based dashboard. This combination enables fetal ultrasound imaging from home. The participants could connect an Android smartphone to the cradle containing the ultrasound transducer to serve as a probe. The device operates in two modes: the app guided mode, with instructions through videoclips and post scan assessment, and clinician guided mode with real‐time guidance and assessment. The mode used in this study was the clinician guided mode. The ultrasound examination can be initiated by the participant, and the functionality was accessible through a videocall in the corresponding application. The clinician can assess the videocall and ultrasound interface via the clinician's web‐based dashboard. The application and web portal allow participants to obtain two‐dimensional ultrasound images, which are visible in real‐time for both the participant and the clinician while communicating within the videocall. During the videocall and ultrasound examination, the clinician can adjust ultrasound settings in the dashboard. In addition, the web portal enables recording and downloading of ultrasound images and videos after the examination.

Study procedures and equipment

Each participant received the Pulsenmore ultrasound device, if needed an Android smartphone, for a period of approximately 28 days and were instructed to perform two ultrasound examinations per week, with a total of eight scans, from their homes. Instructions for using the Pulsenmore device were given directly in person or through an online videocall by the obstetric health care professional. Additionally, a written instruction manual was provided to each participant.

An experienced obstetric sonographer performing advanced ultrasound examinations guided the participant throughout the ultrasound examination to visualize the predefined sonographic parameters. If a decline in fetal status was suspected, the participant was advised to undergo same‐day in‐hospital assessment to evaluate fetal wellbeing.

Ultrasound examinations were performed on preset date and time using the application, initiated by the obstetric healthcare professional. During the meeting, the participant was at home, while the clinician was in the outpatient clinic. Each meeting started with a videocall, accessible via the Pulsenmore application for the patient and the Pulsenmore web portal for the obstetric healthcare professional. The videocall facilitated real‐time audiovisual communication. Within the meeting, the ultrasound examination could be started by the obstetric healthcare professional via the web portal.

The patient was instructed to place the probe on the abdomen, and the images were automatically displayed real time within the videocall, visible to both the health care professional and the participant. Communication was possible while the images were displayed, enabling instructions and questions. With the real‐time guidance of the obstetric health professional, the predefined sonographic parameters were made visible. All connections of the obstetric healthcare professional were initiated at the hospital site, with a LAN internet connection. Ultrasound images and videos were recorded, pseudonymized, and stored in a database by the healthcare professional who performed the ultrasound examination.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics were self‐reported using a questionnaire containing gestational age, ethnicity, educational level, parity, and obstetric history. During the ultrasound examination, healthcare professionals guided the patient through image acquisition, obtained ultrasound images, and documented the process in Castor EDC (Castor Electronic Data Capture, 2021). The captured images were saved to a web‐based database and subsequently downloaded into a secure research file, making them available for further assessment. All surveys were filled out online via an email link with direct entry into the data capturing tool Castor EDC, Castor Electronic Data Capture, 2021b. 9

Outcomes

The primary outcome was feasibility, a composite of three components: image acquisition, interpretability, and technical aspects.

Image acquisition was defined as the ability to visualize predefined sonographic parameters under the guidance of real‐time instructions and to save those images for later assessment. The predefined sonographic parameters were evaluated in real‐time by the obstetric healthcare professional.

Interpretability was defined as the post scan identification of predefined sonographic parameters by two ultrasound experts independently, each blinded to the other's observations.

Technical aspects were assessed by measuring the time from the start of the videocall to the initiation of the ultrasound examination, the time required to acquire the sonographic parameters, and the duration of the total meeting. In addition, all technical issues that required resolution during the meetings were analyzed and documented, noting whether they occurred prior to, during, or after the image acquisition.

The secondary outcomes included user experience, evaluated through self‐assessment questionnaires. After the fourth and eighth ultrasound examinations, patients were asked to complete two questionnaires. The first is the validated self‐assessment questionnaire evaluating usability using the System Usability Scale (SUS) in Dutch (Appendix S1). 10 The SUS is a 10‐statement survey designed to assess the learnability, reliability, and usability of products. Raw scores are transformed to a percentile rank (0–100) along with an associated letter grade (A+ to F), with A+ being the highest usability score. A score of 84.0 or higher indicates excellent usability, corresponding to an A grade; scores between 74.1–84.0 (B or B+) represent good usability, 65.0–74.0 (C or C+) okay usability, 51.7–62.6 (D) poor usability, and 0–51.6 (F) awful usability. The second questionnaire, the Telemonitoring Survey, was developed by the research team (Appendix S1). The Telemonitoring Survey examined patient satisfaction and usability. This survey contained eight statements with five answer options (varying from strongly disagree to strongly agree), three questions using a 10‐point Likert scale, and an open comment form.

A focus group was conducted including five involved obstetric healthcare professionals with experience in basic and advanced ultrasonography to explore their perspectives on the use of tele‐ultrasound. The discussion took place in a single session lasting approximately 45 min and was guided by structured, theme‐based questions addressing advantages and disadvantages, as well as perspectives on the future role of tele‐ultrasound in clinical practice.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics of the study population were reported using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD or medians and ranges. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while the chi‐squared test was used for the categorical variables. The image acquisition and post‐scan interpretability were analyzed and reported using descriptive statistics. The interobserver agreement was assessed by using the Cohen's kappa statistic, which was interpretated as follows: values ≤0, no agreement, 0.01–0.20, none to slight agreement and 0.21–0.40 as fair, 0.41–0.60 as moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 as substantial agreement, 0.81–1.00 as almost perfect agreement. The technical aspects and results of the questionnaires were reported using descriptive statistics. Correlations between the ultrasound duration and baseline characteristics including gestational age and BMI were analyzed using the Spearman's Rho test, as variables were not normally distributed. To examine the relationship between ultrasound duration and placental localisation, a Mann–Whitney U test was conducted, as the placental localisation groups were of comparable size. All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics version 30.0.0.

The overall SUS score for all patients was 80.7, indicating excellent usability of the ultrasound device. The reference standards for SUS scores are detailed in Table 5. The ultrasound device was easy to use, and the majority of participants (93.6%) felt confident during the self‐assessment and would like to use the device more frequently in the future (83%). This questionnaire also included an open‐ended section for user feedback, with clear themes emerging: the device was considered easy to use; pairing the device with the phone did not always proceed smoothly, and the process would have been easier without the need for a loan smartphone to use the ultrasound device. With clear instructions and a suitable phone, the device was reported to be pleasant and convenient to use.

Overall satisfaction with the tele‐ultrasound, as evaluated in the Telemonitoring Survey, was high, with a mean score of 4.4 out of 5 across eight questions (Table 6). Most women (95.8%) reported feeling more engaged in their obstetric care through use of the ultrasound device, and 89.4% indicated they would use the device for a longer period if recommended. In the open‐ended section for user feedback, overall, experiences were generally positive. Many participants appreciated the guidance of a healthcare professional, which facilitated their understanding of the ultrasound. They reported that seeing their baby was reassuring and that the device enhanced their sense of connection and engagement with the pregnancy. Some participants mentioned the compatibility with an Android smartphone as a possible barrier for future users.

Perspective of obstetric healthcare professionals

Five experts evaluated the tele‐ultrasound system as user‐friendly and capable of providing sufficient image quality for basic home‐based obstetric assessments, though detailed structural evaluation remains limited. The experts noted that tele‐ultrasound may have the potential to support remote guidance, triage, and patient reassurance, particularly in monitoring of reduced fetal movements, fetal growth restriction, and stable PPROM, possibly complementing home‐based CTG and blood pressure monitoring. Potential benefits include increased patient autonomy, reduced hospital visits, and more efficient workflows for clinicians and healthcare organizations. Limitations involve unequal access for patients with low health or digital literacy, connectivity issues, and lack of electronic medical record integration. Future implementation was expected to encompass staff training, technical support, and focus on workflow integration and reimbursement. Tele‐ultrasound is expected to play a central role in hospital‐at‐home models, with greatest benefit for motivated, digitally capable patients requiring frequent monitoring, and less benefit for those with high anxiety or limited technical skills.

DISCUSSION

The use of self‐administered fetal tele‐ultrasound was found to be a feasible and acceptable approach for routine transabdominal ultrasound examinations in a low‐risk population. Images acquired by pregnant individuals were highly interpretable, and predefined sonographic parameters could be consistently identified retrospectively in nearly all cases. Favorable user experiences, reflected in a usability score (80.7, A) and a high overall satisfaction score (4.4/5), support its potential as a complementary tool in obstetric care. Ultrasound experts' experiences were assessed in a focus group, highlighting clinical advances as well as limitations for future adoption. These findings highlight the promise of tele‐ultrasound in enhancing accessibility and patient engagement while maintaining diagnostic quality.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we did not conduct in‐depth interviews, which may have reduced our ability to capture more nuanced insights and patient experiences. Additionally, the educational level of participants was relatively high, with over half having completed secondary education or higher, which may have positively influenced their ability to perform self‐administered measurements. Furthermore, given the small sample size, the subgroup analyses examining associations between ultrasound duration and maternal or pregnancy characteristics should be considered exploratory. Finally, the exclusion of individuals with language barriers (i.e., those not fluent in Dutch or English) may have limited the representation of participants from diverse ethnic backgrounds.

Literature on self‐administration of obstetric ultrasound with real‐time guidance is limited. A randomized controlled trial by Mor et al. enrolled 50 women with recurrent pregnancy loss early in gestation, randomized to high‐risk prenatal care alone or supplemented with physician‐guided tele‐ultrasound. Scans assessed fetal heart rate, movements, and amniotic fluid. The intervention group showed lower anxiety and stronger maternal‐fetal attachment. These findings align with our results, where patients reported a positive experience and noted feeling comfortable seeing their baby and more engaged during pregnancy. 11

Other studies using patient‐operated tele‐ultrasound with real‐time guidance align with our findings. Axelrod et al. evaluated women with gestational diabetes (≥31 weeks) using alternating in‐person and remote visits, the latter including maternal and fetal assessments with real‐time DVP measurement using the same device as in our study. Patients reported a very positive experience (6.6/7) and an excellent Net Promoter Score (9.94). 12 Nir et al. similarly showed that all 10 participants were able to obtain satisfactory DVP images after real‐time instruction. Both studies, like ours, demonstrated positive user experiences. 13

A few studies have examined self‐administered obstetric ultrasound without real‐time guidance. In a retrospective cohort of 4460 singleton pregnancies (≥14 weeks), women performed short home scans using a standardized six‐step protocol, with images reviewed by sonographers. Although intended to improve reassurance and access, home ultrasound use was not significantly associated with maternal or neonatal outcomes. 14 In a study conducted with 100 pregnant women, a portable abdominal ultrasound device was used for self‐use several times a day over a period of 1–2 weeks. Participants of 16–40 weeks of gestational age were instructed to perform scans according to a standardized six‐step protocol. The scans were not interpreted until after delivery and were subsequently evaluated by two independent experts. The success rate in identifying standard obstetric ultrasound parameters, such as fetal heart activity, amniotic fluid volume, and fetal body movements, was high and comparable to our study, underscoring the feasibility of self‐administered fetal ultrasound. 15

Self‐performed scans of fetal heartbeat and DVP, by 46 participants without real‐time guidance, had a success rate of 52% in the first session. 16 The majority of women expressed a preference for real‐time support from a physician during self‐administered ultrasound; only one‐third of participants reported feeling comfortable using the tele‐ultrasound device, compared to 93.6% in our study. 16

A recent study involving 28 participants performing self‐administered ultrasounds demonstrated that fetal heart rate and DVP measurements can be reliably obtained using tele‐ultrasound systems, even at 40 + 1 to 40 + 6 weeks of gestation. These ultrasound examinations were retrospectively assessed. In our study, fetal heart rate was not quantified; however, incorporating more exact or more complex measurements could further broaden the clinical applicability of tele‐ultrasound. 17

Further research including a broader range of gestational ages and maternal BMI is needed to evaluate feasibility, as advancing gestation and higher BMI may introduce additional imaging challenges.

The observed reduction in scan and acquisition time over the study period suggests a learning effect, with participants becoming more efficient with repeated use. Future studies should explore models with limited guidance followed by more independent scanning to assess feasibility in less resource‐intensive settings.

The adoption of digital healthcare solutions continues to grow as health systems respond to workforce shortages, the increasing prevalence of chronic conditions, and rising costs. 18 Tele‐ultrasound could enable task‐shifting, allowing individuals without advanced ultrasound skills to perform routine scans under remote guidance, expanding the workforce and enabling care in diverse locations to support hospital‐at‐home models. In obstetrics, hospital‐at‐home programs currently focus on monitoring high‐risk pregnancies with devices like cardiotocography and blood pressure monitors. Integrating tele‐ultrasound could extend these programs by enabling imaging‐based assessments outside the hospital, enhancing patient‐centered care, supporting clinical decision‐making, and further decentralizing specialist services, while reducing travel time and associated costs for patients. 19

Future research is needed to explore the integration of tele‐ultrasound into routine clinical practice, including self‐administered applications. Self‐administered obstetric ultrasound may reduce maternal anxiety and enhance maternal–fetal attachment in women with a history of recurrent pregnancy loss, 11 and it could potentially be applied to pregnancies complicated by e.g. fetal growth restriction, as an extension of safe and effective hospital‐at‐home‐models. 1 Studies should also assess cost‐effectiveness, patient experience, and overall clinical impact. This study identified several technical shortcomings that may affect the performance and reliability of the system in its current form, underscoring the importance of evaluating such technologies within the specific context in which they are intended to be used. We therefore recommend that clinicians conduct performance assessments within their own clinical setting prior to implementation. At the same time, manufacturers should continue to invest in the development of robust and stable software to minimize technical issues. Addressing these challenges is essential for the successful integration of tele‐ultrasound into routine clinical practice and warrants further investigation given its wide range of potential applications. Further technological advancements may enable more comprehensive ultrasound assessments, including biometric measurements. Additionally, the integration of AI‐driven clinical support tools during image acquisition could further enhance usability and diagnostic quality. 20

CONCLUSION

Our findings demonstrate that self‐administered fetal tele‐ultrasound is feasible and associated with high user satisfaction and usability. This study supported its potential as a complementary tool in obstetric care, highlighting tele‐ultrasound's promise to enhance accessibility, patient engagement, and strengthen hospital‐care‐at‐home strategies.
