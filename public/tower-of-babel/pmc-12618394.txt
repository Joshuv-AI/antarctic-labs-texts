Biofunctional semi-interpenetrating gellan gum and silk sericin scaffolds encapsulated with betel leaf extract-β-Cyclodextrin inclusive complexes for wound healing
Authors: Thanyaluck Thanyacharoen, Piyachat Chuysinuan, Kriengsak Lirdprapamongkol, Chalinan Pengsuk, Supanna Techasakul, Jisnuson Svasti, Patcharakamon Nooeaid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic wound treatment presents a significant challenge, requiring bioactive scaffolds that facilitate effective wound repair and promote skin regeneration with normal functionality. In this study, gellan gum (GG) networks were formed via physical crosslinking with divalent cations, while silk sericin (SS), as the linear phase, molecularly penetrated the pore volume of the GG network, resulting in the formation of semi-interpenetrating polymeric networks (semi-IPNs). The GG/SS scaffolds were enriched with betel leaf extract-encapsulated β-cyclodextrin complexes (B-ICs) to preserve the bioactive substance, improve the controlled release, and provide antibacterial, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Characterization through XRD, FTIR, and thermal analyses confirmed successful encapsulation and enhanced thermal stability, while SEM imaging revealed well-formed microporous structures. Mechanical testing showed that B-ICs significantly improved the compressive modulus and strength of the scaffolds. Additionally, the controlled release behavior of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, confirmed by the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, suggested anomalous transport as the release mechanism, aligning with the faster in vitro degradation rate. The scaffolds exhibited high phenolic content, resulting in excellent free radical scavenging activity to minimize oxidative stress and support an optimal wound healing environment. In vivo skin irritation test in rabbits confirmed that B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were non-irritant, suggesting the dermal safety and biocompatibility of the materials, a critical requirement for clinical translation. As a result, the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds would be a promising candidate for wound healing and tissue engineering applications. Keywords: Wound healing, Tissue engineering scaffolds, Semi-interpenetrating polymeric network, Betel leaf extract, Inclusive complexes

Introduction

The wound healing process is a complicated physiological response of a living system to proceed with tissue regeneration [1]. An insufficiently disordered wound healing process, for example, overexpression of the inflammatory response and bacterial infection, often leads to delayed wound repair, and consequently chronic wounds [1]. Complete chronic wound treatment is characterized by the restoration of full-thickness skin tissue with normal functions, which has recently remained a significant challenge. Tissue engineering has been introduced as a more efficacious wound repair therapy to promote the regeneration of the full dermal layer. Three-dimensional (3D) interconnected porous scaffolds were engineered with the integration of autologous cells or stem cells and biological factors to mimic the native extracellular matrix (ECM), change the wound environment, and stimulate cell proliferation and migration [2]. Hydrogels are one of the most promising scaffolds due to their structural networks are similar to natural ECM. The hydrogels also provide excellent biocompatibility, high water absorption capacity, and water retention ability, which are the physical features desired for ideal wound dressings. However, hydrogels have limitations in insufficient mechanical properties and stability. Several strategies, such as polymer blends, copolymers, hybrid materials and composites have been developed to overcome these limitations. Interpenetrating polymeric networks (IPNs) are an alternative strategy for tailoring and improving the physical and chemical characteristics of polymers by combining two different crosslinked networks of polymers [3]. Most IPNs were chemically produced via chemical crosslinking, and which IPNs obtained are limited to biodegradation, cytotoxicity, and cost-effectiveness [3]. Therefore, biopolymer-based IPNs produced through physical crosslinking are a solution for overcoming these drawbacks. Semi-IPNs have been considered a novel system for the development of tissue engineering scaffolds, which are composed of a crosslinked network and an interpenetrated linear phase [4]. The linear phase in semi-IPNs can release or dissolve.

FDA-approved gellan gum (GG) is a bacterial exopolysaccharide that has been widely used for tissue engineering due to heat stability, non-toxicity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, renewability, tunable mechanical properties, and thermosensitive gelling characteristics [5]. A crosslinked GG network is generated via electrostatic interaction between the carboxylate groups of the polymer and divalent cations, forming stable networks. For instance, halloysite nanotubes/GG composite hydrogels showed tunable physical and mechanical properties, and biocompatibility with human fibroblasts that were suitable for soft tissue engineering [6]. GG and silk sericin hydrogels were developed for retina tissue engineering, which promoted cell proliferation and differentiation [7]. Silk sericin (SS), derived from silk cocoon waste, exhibits plenty of advantages for tissue engineering, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, hydrophilicity, and moisture absorption. It was investigated that SS enhanced the mechanical properties of polysaccharide-based hydrogels [8]. Nevertheless, the most naturally derived polymer-based scaffolds did not exhibit antibacterial and antioxidant properties, which are unsuitable for bacterial-infected chronic wounds. Therefore, the antibacterial activity of the scaffolds can be functionalized by using various antibacterial agents, such as antibiotics, synthetic particles and phytochemicals. Utilization of antibiotics like vancomycin and ciprofloxacin led to the development of drug resistance in Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, respectively [9]. Synthetic nanoparticles (i.e., zinc oxide, silver nanoparticles) have negative side effects, such as skin sensitization and environmental pollution, which do not satisfy the customers’ demand [9]. Notably, plant extracts with inherent antibacterial and antioxidant properties are gaining prominence as biopharmaceuticals, particularly for using in open skin wounds and skin infections [9]. Betel (Piper betel L.) leaf extract has been investigated for antibacterial and antioxidant capacities against different pathogens, including P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, S. pyogens, C. albicans, and E. coli [10]. Betel leaf extract has been added to poly(vinyl alcohol) electrospun nanofibers-based wound dressings showing antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli bacteria [10]. Many studies faced a significant limitation that plant extracts lack stability under conditions of heat and light, resulting in the reduction of bioactivity and therapeutic effectiveness. To address this issue, encapsulation technology emerges as a potent approach, offering protection to biological substances while facilitating controlled drug release profiles.

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first report on the fabrication of biofunctional semi-interpenetrating GG/SS scaffolds encapsulating betel leaf extract–β-cyclodextrin inclusion complexes (B-ICs). This hybrid design combined the structural integrity and hydrophilicity of GG, the bioactive and antioxidant nature of SS, and the inclusion-complexation ability of β-CD to stabilize and control the release of phytochemicals. Such a synergistic system offers a novel strategy for developing multifunctional, naturally derived wound healing scaffolds that integrate moisture management, antioxidant protection, and sustained phytochemical delivery. The physicochemical properties of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were investigated in comparison with the properties of B-GG/SS, GG/SS and GG scaffolds. In addition, in vitro cell culture test, anti-inflammatory test and in vivo irritation test in rabbit models were performed to evaluate the suitability of the scaffolds for wound healing and skin tissue engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Preparation of betel leaf extract-encapsulated β-cyclodextrin inclusive complexes (B-ICs)

β-cyclodextrin (β-CD; Sigma Aldrich, USA) solution at a concentration of 10%w/v was dissolved in deionized (DI) water/ethanol (3/1 v/v) at temperature of 55 °C. Betel leaf extract (B; Thai-China Flavors and Fragrances Industry, Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Thailand) was introduced into the β-CD solution at a ratio of 2/1 w/w. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 18 h to form inclusive complexes (ICs). The mixture was then frozen at a temperature of −20 °C and subjected to lyophilization at temperature of −45 °C under vacuum for 48 h to produce dried betel leaf extract-encapsulated ICs (B-ICs) particles.

Preparation of B-ICs incorporated gellan gum/silk sericin (B-ICs-GG/SS) scaffolds

Gellan gum (GG; Chemipan Co., Ltd, Bangkok, Thailand) was dissolved in DI water at a concentration of 2%w/v under stirring at a temperature of 90 °C until obtained homogeneous solution. 0.1%w/v CaCl2·2H2O (Sigma Aldrich, USA) was added to the GG solution and continuously stirred at temperature of 70 °C for 30 min, to generate the physical formation of a crosslinked GG network. Silk sericin (SS) was extracted from Thai silk cocoons using the autoclave method, followed by lyophilization, as detailed in a previous study [8]. SS was added to the crosslinked GG solution at a weight ratio of 2/1 (GG/SS). The mixture was continuously stirred at a temperature of 70 °C until a homogeneous solution was achieved. Subsequently, 2 ml of the GG/SS mixture was added into each well of a 24-well plate. Gelation of the GG/SS solution occurred upon cooling the solution to room temperature. Porous GG/SS samples were formed by freezing at −20 °C for 12 h and were then lyophilized at −45 °C under vacuum. Betel leaf extract and B-ICs were separately added to the GG/SS solution at a concentration of 2%w/v and were stirred at a temperature of 45 °C for approximately 10 min for the preparation of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS samples, respectively. The B-GG/SS and B-IC-GG/SS porous scaffolds were fabricated following the same procedure as the GG/SS scaffolds.

XRD analysis

Crystalline phases of β-CD, B-ICs, GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS, and B-ICs-GG/SS samples were analyzed by using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 advance, Germany) with CuK∞ radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the 2θ angle range of 5–35°. The analysis was operated at a voltage of 40 KV, a current of 40 mA, scan step size of 0.02°, and scan step time of 17.7 s.

FTIR analysis

ATR–FTIR (Nicolet170-SX, Thermo Nicolet Ltd., USA) was used to characterize the chemical composition of β-CD, B-ICs, GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds to confirm the existence of betel extract, B-ICs, and possible interaction among all components. The analysis was performed at a resolution of 4 cm−1 for 64 scans over a wavenumber range of 4000 cm−1–400 cm−1.

SEM analysis

Morphology of B-ICs and bare β-CD, and the microstructure of all porous scaffold formulations were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL, JSM-IT-500HR). ICs particles and cylindrical porous samples were coated with gold by using a sputtering device prior to SEM analysis.

Thermal analysis

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was used to analyze the thermal behaviors of the B-ICs and the scaffolds. The DSC patterns of samples were analyzed by a DSC 204F1 Phoenix (NETZSCH, Bavaria, Germany). A 5 mg of each sample was heated in a sealed aluminum pan at a rate of 10 °C/min, in a temperature range of 50 °C–400 °C. Thermal stability of all sample types was investigated by using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA 550; TA instruments, USA) performed in a nitrogen atmosphere at temperature ranging from 25 °C to 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Mechanical testing

The mechanical properties of GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were tested under compression mode by using the universal testing machine (UTM; Instron 5966, USA) with 50 N load cells. The cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 15 mm and height of 10 mm were compressed at a cross-head speed of 5 mm/min until the specimens were compressed to approximately 70% of their original height. The mean values of compressive modulus and compressive strength at 45% displacement were averaged from 8 specimens tested for each scaffold type.

Release profile and release kinetics study

Antioxidant test

To prepare extracted solutions of betel leaf extract-loaded scaffolds for evaluating antioxidant property, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were soaked in 10 ml of methanol for 3 days. Total phenolic content of the scaffolds was determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu method. In detail, 200 μl of the extracted solution was mixed with 1 ml of 7% w/v sodium carbonate (Na2CO3; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 1 ml of Folin–Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent (1/1 diluted; Merck, Germany). After 2 h of incubation in the dark at room temperature, the absorbance of the mixture was measured at a wavelength of 765 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Gallic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) prepared at concentrations ranging from 0–100 mg/ml was used as the standard. The results were presented as mg gallic acid equivalent (mg GAE). All experiments were carried out in triplicate. The antioxidant activity of betel leaf extract-loaded scaffolds, including B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS, was investigated by DPPH radical scavenging assay to confirm the ability of the tested scaffolds to inhibit oxidation. Each scaffold was immersed in 20 ml of methanol for 24 h. A DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) solution at a concentration of 25 mg/l was prepared in methanol. Then, 0.1 ml of each extract concentration was mixed with 3.9 ml of DPPH solution, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The DPPH solution changed from purple to yellow in the presence of an antioxidant. L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used as a positive control, while methanol mixed with DPPH solution was used as a negative control. The absorbance of the sample mixture (Asample) and the absorbance of the DPPH solution as a control (Acontrol) were measured at 517 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The experiments were performed in triplicate. Antibacterial test

The antibacterial activity of GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was assessed against two pathogenic bacteria (Gram-positive and Gram-negative) using the disk diffusion assay. Bacterial suspensions (106 CFU/ml) of S. aureus (ATCC25923) and E. coli (ATCC25922) were evenly distributed on agar plates and allowed to grow overnight at 37 °C. Circular disks with a thickness of 2 mm were cut from the scaffold samples, measuring 14 mm in diameter. These sample disks were sterilized by a 30 min exposure to UV light before evaluating their bacterial inhibitory effects, particularly against betel leaf extract released from the scaffolds. Controls consisted of GG and GG/SS samples lacking betel leaf extract. Each sample disk was positioned on the bacterial agar plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After that, the plates were examined for the presence of zones of inhibition around the disks. The diameter of these zones was measured in millimeters (mm). Inhibition zones were interpreted according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations.

In vitro cytotoxicity test

An indirect in vitro cytotoxicity test was performed on the fabricated scaffolds corresponding to the ISO10993-5 standard test method. To prepare sample extracts (100 mg/ml) for cytotoxic assay, the scaffolds (20 mg each type) were sterilized under UV radiation for 30 min, followed by a 24 h immersion in 0.2 ml of phenol red-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10%v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 96-well plate. Cytotoxicity was evaluated in the RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage cell line (ATCC TIB-71, Rockville, MD, USA). The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. A cell suspension in phenol red-free DMEM was seeded into 96-well plates at 3×104 cells/100 µl/well and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h to allow cell attachment onto the well surface. The cells were then treated by adding sample extract (25 µl) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to allow expression of potential cytotoxic effect, while untreated cells served as a control. Cell viability was assessed by using a modified MTT assay [13]. In brief, 25 μl of MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) solution was added to each well. After 4 h incubation, formazan crystals that formed by the mitochondrial activity of living cells were solubilized by adding 100 µl of lysis solution (20% SDS in 10 mM HCl) into each well. The plate was kept in the dark at room temperature for 2 days to ensure complete dissolution of the crystals. Absorbance of the solubilized formazan crystals (Asample) was measured at 550 nm, with a reference wavelength of 650 nm, using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5; Molecular Devices, MA, USA). Background absorbance for subtraction was obtained from blank wells containing only culture medium and reagents (Acontrol). The experiments were performed in triplicate. Anti-inflammatory assay

Anti-inflammatory property of the GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was assessed from their performance in inhibiting production of nitric oxide in RAW 264.7 cells. A 100 μl of cell suspension in phenol red-free DMEM was seeded into 96-well plates at 3×104 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. A 25 µl sample extract was added to the cells and incubated for 3 h, then the cells were stimulated with 10 µg/ml of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from E. coli (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. Nitric oxide level in culture medium was determined by using Griess reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). In detail, 100 µl of Griess reagent was mixed with 50 µl of the collected medium and the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. Absorbance of the mixture was measured at 540 nm and converted into the concentration of nitrite in culture medium using a standard curve of sodium nitrite (0–100 µM).

In vivo skin irritation test

The skin irritation test was conducted in accordance with ISO 10993-23:2021 Biological evaluation of medical devices–Part 23: Tests for irritation and ISO 10993-12:2021 Biological evaluation of medical devices–Part 12: Sample preparation and reference materials. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (TISTR) (Protocol No. TS-68018) and carried out by InnoHERB: Expert Centre of Innovative Herbal Products, TISTR. Three 13-week-old male New Zealand White rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) were individually housed in separate cages under standard environmental and feeding conditions. Body weight was recorded at the beginning of the study (day 0), day 7, and day 14. Dorsal fur was clipped to create an approximately 10 × 15 cm² shaved area, 4 h to 24 h prior to testing. The test item, GG/SS scaffold, was extracted using a mass-to-volume ratio of 0.1 g/ml in 0.9% normal saline solution (NSS), which also served as the control. A volume of 0.2 ml of either the test extract or control solution was injected intracutaneously at five sites per sample on each rabbit. Each rabbit received injections of both the test and control samples, administered on opposite sides of the dorsal region. Before injection, the skin was disinfected using 70% ethanol and marked with povidone-iodine (Betadine). Animals were observed immediately after injection, and again at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-injection. Skin reactions were assessed using a standardized scoring system (Table 1) for erythema and edema. Scores for each parameter were totaled for the five test sites, averaged per animal (divided by 15), and then averaged across the three animals for each sample. The final irritation score was calculated by subtracting the control group score from the test group score. A final score of 1 or lower was interpreted as indicating no skin irritation.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the characterization and testing data. The data were reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Significant difference was determined at the significance level of *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

Formation of B-ICs particles

β-CD was used to encapsulate piper betel leaf extract, leading to the formation of the extract-loaded inclusive complexes (B-ICs), to protect the bioactivity of the extract and provide a controlled release behavior. Therefore, the successful formation of B-ICs was investigated by using XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 1a. The crystal structure of β-CD was confirmed with characteristic peaks at 2θ of 9.60°, 12.82°, 18.24°, 18.75° and 19.11°. The crystal peaks of β-CD shifted to lower 2θ values with the formation of B-ICs, indicating the change in crystalline structure. Given that, the B-ICs formation disrupted the regular crystalline packing, transforming it into a less ordered structure. FTIR analysis of betel leaf extract, β-CD and B-ICs was investigated in order to confirm the successful formation of ICs. In Fig. 1b, FTIR spectrum of starting β-CD showed the characteristic absorption peaks at 3600–3000 cm−1, 2920 cm−1, 1636 cm−1, 1150 cm−1 and 1019 cm−1, which were responding to O–H stretching, C–H stretching, H–O–H deformation, C–O–C asymmetric stretching and C–O–C symmetric stretching, respectively [14]. When comparing the spectrum of B-ICs to that of β-CD, the characteristic bands of β-CD were found in the spectrum of B-ICs. While the existence of new absorption bands appeared at the wavenumber of 3072 cm−1 attributed to C = C–H stretching of aromatic ring, 1766 cm−1 corresponding to C = O stretching of carbonyl derivative and 1508 cm−1 attributed N = O (nitro), all of which are associated with bioactive compounds composed in the betel leaf extract [15]. In addition, the reduction in intensity of the O–H stretching band in the spectrum of B-ICs compared to that of β-CD might be caused by hydrogen bonding, van der Waals force and hydrophobic interaction between the extract and β-CD. These findings confirm the successful formation of B-ICs. The DSC pattern in Fig. 1c of β-CD expressed the sharp endothermic peak at a temperature range of 100 °C−200 °C, which was due to the evaporation of water. Since the β-CD is naturally cyclic oligosaccharide showing hydrophilic property, especially at the exterior of the β-CD architecture, the bare β-CD interacts with water molecules well [16]. Another endothermic event was observed in the temperature ranging from 285 °C to 350 °C, which was a result of thermal decomposition of β-CD [16]. B-ICs showed a significantly different diffraction pattern from the starting β-CD did. The endothermic effect of water evaporation almost disappeared from the curve of B-ICs, while the decomposition peak at around 285 °C-350 °C remained. Another endothermic event appeared in the curve of B-ICs at 200 °C−250 °C, which is the melting temperature of the betel leaf extract [15]. The DSC results suggest the successful formation of B-ICs, that the water molecules interacting with β-CD were taken over by the encapsulation of betel leaf extract, leading to guest and host interactions. It was also evident from the TGA results in Fig. 1d. The curve of empty β-CD demonstrated three steps of thermal transitions, including (I) ~ 10% of weight loss at temperature up to 100 °C due to water evaporation, (II) thermal decomposition of β-CD started from 200 °C to 400 °C providing ~80% weight loss, and (III) at 400 °C-650 °C found for 10% weight loss that caused by the pyrolysis. The TGA curve of B-ICs was remarkably different from that of β-CD, as well as the DSC results. The first transition occurred at the temperature of 50 °C−250 °C with ~40% loss of mass because of thermal decomposition of encapsulated betel leaf extract. This finding supported the thermal transition found in the DSC pattern. When the temperature reached 300 °C−400 °C, the second step of mass reduction was observed by 50%. This indicates the thermal decomposition of β-CD. A residual mass of around 10% was finally detected.

Crystal structure and chemical composition of scaffolds

XRD patterns in Fig. 2a of both GG, GG/SS and B-GG/SS scaffolds did not show any differences from each other. The characteristic peak positions of GG scaffolds were observed at the 2θ values of 7.0°, 19.6° and 23.7°. With the presence of the SS phase, all those peaks became broader, showing the reduction in crystallinity due to polymer-polymer interaction. This result was also found in the pattern of B-GG/SS scaffolds. The addition of betel leaf extract in the GG/SS network could interrupt the ordered structure of the polymeric network. As a result, the introduction of a linear SS phase and betel leaf extract to the polymer network could increase chain entanglement, leading to a higher fraction of amorphous content, which broadens the diffraction peaks. The XRD curve of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds clearly showed the crystal peaks of B-ICs, indicated by the same peak positions of B-ICs at the 2θ values of 5.9°, 7.0°, 9.8°, 11.8°, 17.6° and 18.6°. It confirms the successful incorporation of B-ICs in the GG/SS scaffolds. FTIR spectra of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were comparatively interpreted to the spectrum of GG/SS and GG scaffolds (Fig. 2b). The FTIR spectrum of the GG scaffold showed a broad absorption band at the wavenumber of 3600 cm−1–3000 cm−1 that is the characteristic peak of O-H stretching vibration. The C-H stretching vibration in GG was observed at the wavenumber of 2925 cm−1. Other absorption bands at 1596 cm−1 and 1400 cm−1 were attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching of carboxyl groups, respectively, and the band at 1031 cm−1 corresponded to C–O stretching vibration. The GG/SS scaffolds showed the characteristic peaks of both GG and SS. A broad absorption band of O–H stretching at a range of 3600 cm−1–3000 cm−1 overlapped with the band of N–H stretching at 3292 cm−1. A peak of C–H stretching was observed at the wavenumber of 2925 cm−1. The characteristic peaks of C = O stretching (amide I), and N–H bending and C–N stretching (amide II) of silk sericin appeared at 1606 cm−1 and 1521 cm−1, respectively [17]. The result suggests that SS was incorporated into the GG network. The addition of betel leaf extract and B-ICs in the GG/SS scaffolds showed the similar spectra to the spectrum of the GG/SS scaffold, exceptionally the characteristic peaks of betel leaf extract were observed at the wavenumber of 3075 cm−1 and 1761 cm−1, which assigned the C = C–H stretching of aromatic ring and the carbonyl derivative of the bioactive compounds, respectively. In addition, the intensity of O–H stretching band overlapping N–H stretching band was reduced by incorporating B-ICs instead of betel leaf extract loading. It was suggested that the incorporation of the B-ICs in the GG/SS scaffolds diminished the hydrogen bonding and subsequently amount of water molecules during the formation of inclusive complexes.

Thermal behaviors and stability of scaffolds

Figure 2c, d demonstrated the DSC and TGA patterns, respectively, of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, compared to the patterns of B-GG/SS, GG/SS and GG scaffolds. All scaffold types showed an endothermic transition at the same temperature range of 50 °C−175 °C as a result of water evaporation. In addition, an exothermic transition corresponding to the degradation temperature (Td) of the materials appeared at 257 °C, 263 °C, 265 °C, and 267 °C in the patterns of GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS, and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, respectively. Interconnecting GG network with SS slightly enhanced the thermal stability of GG scaffolds, confirming the formation of semi-IPNs GG/SS scaffolds. The Td of the scaffolds was shifted to higher temperatures with the addition of betel leaf extract and B-ICs. Significantly, the thermal profile of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds showed an additional exothermic peak at 315 °C, suggesting thermal degradation of B-ICs. In addition, the exothermic event of scaffolds was broader with the addition of SS, while the peak of the GG scaffold was very sharp. This phenomenon was probably caused by the physical interaction like hydrogen bonding between the SS and GG network. The broad exothermic peaks of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds became wider compared to those of GG and GG/SS scaffolds. This indicates a significant occurrence of physical entanglement and hydrogen bonding, resulting in a more complex and stronger structure. The findings from DSC profiles could be confirmed by the TGA results of the scaffolds. The thermogram of GG scaffolds exhibited (I) the first transition stage at 50 °C−200 °C and showed a 12% weight loss of water evaporation. The main thermal degradation of the polymer was found at 200 °C−375 °C with a weight loss of 49%. Stage (III) started at 375 °C to 557 °C and showed a weight loss of 26% which was attributed to chain scission. Small (IV) stage occurred at 557 °C−790 °C with 8% loss of weight relevant to fragmentation into monomers [6]. The residue (V) remained at around 5% of the initial weight. The Td and weight loss of GG/SS scaffolds were similar to those of GG scaffolds. It was exceptionally found that the Td onset increased with the presence of SS in the scaffolds to 250 °C from 241 °C in the GG scaffold, indicating that SS could enhance the thermal stability and provide a stronger network of GG structure. With the incorporation of betel leaf extract, the thermogram of B-GG/SS scaffolds did not change from the curve of GG/SS scaffolds, except for a smaller amount of water removal. The Td onset of GG/SS and B-GG/SS scaffolds was observed at the same temperature of 250 °C. However, with the incorporation of B-ICs, the Td onset of the scaffold increased to 276 °C, indicating enhanced thermal stability. This suggests that the inclusion of betel extract with β-CD improves the scaffold’s resistance to thermal degradation. In other words, the inclusion complex helps preserve the bioactivity of the betel leaf extract by providing thermal protection. In addition, a difference observed in the thermogram of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds from those of other scaffolds was 22% weight loss found in the last (V) step at a temperature of 700 °C−900 °C that was attributed to the decomposition of monosaccharide units.

Microstructure of scaffolds

Figure 3 showed the microporous structure and the surface morphology of all scaffold formulations. GG and GG/SS scaffolds exhibited a highly interconnected porous structure (Fig. 3a, b). Surface morphology of both scaffolds was quite smooth and did not show any significant differences (Fig. 3a′ vs. 3b′). These indicate that the presence of the SS linear phase in the semi-IPNs of GG did not change the porous architecture of the scaffolds. This suggests that the SS phase was well-integrated within the GG network without disrupting its structural integrity. After the addition of betel leaf extract, the pore sizes of B-GG/SS scaffolds increased, and the pores’ uniformity reduced, as shown in Fig. 3c. The surface morphology of B-GG/SS scaffolds was altered with the presence of betel leaf extract (Fig. 3c′). On the contrary, this appearance was not found in Fig. 3d, given that the porous structure of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was well formed with highly interconnected open pores. An observation of surface morphology in Fig. 3d′ remarkably presented a rougher surface due to the uniform distribution of B-ICs components embedded on the pore wall. Both particular shape and particle sizes of the particles embedded on the pore wall of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were the same as those of the B-ICs as observed in the inlet in Fig. 3d′. The microstructure of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds confirms that the incorporation of B-ICs in the scaffolds did not interfere with the highly interconnected porous structure, and the surface morphology showed that the particles of B-ICs were well distributed in the polymer matrix.

Mechanical properties of scaffolds

Compressive stress-strain curves in Fig. 4a showed that the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited the highest mechanical performance under compression compared to the other scaffolds. The GG, GG/SS, and B-GG/SS scaffolds displayed similar trends in their stress-strain curves, which were relevant to the values of compressive modulus and strength shown in Fig. 4b, c, respectively. The compressive modulus of the GG scaffolds, indicating the stiffness of materials, increased from 88 ± 8 kPa to 144 ± 4 kPa by incorporating the SS component. Similarly, the addition of the SS phase into the GG network enhanced the compressive strength of the scaffolds from 25 ± 5 kPa to 32 ± 1 kPa. These results supported the previous properties that the addition of the SS linear phase to the GG network improved the mechanical properties of the individual GG network. The compressive modulus of the GG/SS scaffolds slightly increased by loading betel leaf extract, while the compressive strength decreased, though not significantly. This could be attributed to the loaded betel leaf extract interfering with the interconnected porous structure of the scaffolds due to the incompatibility between the hydrophobic extract and hydrophilic polymers [18]. Improved mechanical properties of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were probably caused by the good distribution of the B-ICs particles in the polymer matrix, as evidenced in Fig. 3d′. The rigid β-CD structure could enhance the scaffold’s ability to resist compressive load, leading to increased compressive modulus and strength.

Water absorption, in vitro degradation and water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of scaffolds

The water absorption and degradation profiles were monitored over 14 days to evaluate the long-term hydration and structural stability of the scaffolds under physiological conditions. Extended swelling behavior is relevant to wound healing applications, where the dressings remain in contact with exudates for several days to weeks. Figure 5a showed that all types of scaffolds showed the water uptake profile in the same trend with time. The scaffolds rapidly absorbed the PBS solution within 30 min of investigation, as shown in an inlet of Fig. 5a, and reached equilibrium after 24 h of investigation. The scaffolds became swollen and soft gel and maintained their networking structure without disintegration. The scaffolds exhibited significantly high water absorption ranging at equilibrium around 2000−2500%. Significantly, the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds maintained the water absorption capability up to 2200% until the end of 14 days, meanwhile the water uptake of GG, GG/SS, and B-GG/SS scaffolds gradually increased to 2500%. The degradation rate of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was slightly higher than the rates of B-GG/SS, GG/SS, and GG scaffolds. The weight of B-ICs-GG/SS, B-GG/SS, GG/SS, and GG scaffolds continuously decreased with time, and they lost around 40%, 30%, 25%, and 10%, respectively, after 14 days of investigation. Release behaviors and release kinetics of scaffolds

The release behaviors of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were investigated in comparison with those of B-GG/SS scaffolds without the presence of inclusive complexes. It is obvious in Fig. 6a that the preservation method of betel leaf extract by encapsulating it in the inclusion complexes reduced the release rate of betel leaf extract when compared to the rate of scaffolds without β-CD encapsulation. The B-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited an initial burst release of up to 45% after 1 h of investigation, while around 30% of betel leaf extract was initially released in the case of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds. After that, both scaffold types gradually showed an increased amount of the betel leaf extract release over time. The extract was completely released from the B-GG/SS scaffolds within 4 h of investigation. It was different from the release profile of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, given that the scaffolds reached 77% betel leaf extract release at the 4th hour and constantly maintained until 48 h of investigation (Inlet in Fig. 6a).

a Cumulative release behaviors and b release kinetics fitting with the Korsmeyer-Peppas Mathematics model of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds in comparison with B-GG/SS scaffolds as investigated by immersion in the PBS solution (pH 7.4) at a temperature of 37 °C for 14 days. The inlet in a highlights the release behavior of the scaffolds during the initial 8 h of investigation

The release profiles of the scaffolds were fitted with the Korsmeyer-Peppas model to identify the release mechanism by determining of release exponent (n) value. The linear curve fitting to the experimental data using the Ritger-Peppas model in Fig. 6b reported that the n values of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS were determined as 0.4102 and 0.5667, respectively. The n value was less than 0.45 means that the release behavior of B-GG/SS scaffolds was driven by a Fickian diffusion mechanism. The n value was in the range of 0.45−0.89, which indicates that the release profile of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was driven by the mechanism of anomalous (non-Fickian) transport. Moreover, the release constant (k) value was determined to indicate the release rate [19]. The k value of B-GG/SS scaffolds was higher than that of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds (47.9291 vs. 37.5924 h−1). It confirms that the B-GG/SS scaffolds showed a higher release rate than the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds did.

Antioxidative and antibacterial activity of scaffolds

Total phenolic content and DPPH radical scavenging activity were reported in Fig. 7a to confirm the antioxidant property of the scaffolds. B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds provided the total phenolic content of 63 ± 10 and 50 ± 4 mg GAE equivalent/g sample, respectively, while the total phenolic content of 10 mg GAE equivalent/g sample was found in both cases of GG and GG/SS scaffolds. Thus, the betel leaf extract is a great source of natural antioxidants. In addition, the B-ICs did not show a significant reduction of the total phenolic content incorporated in the GG/SS scaffolds. This finding agreed with the results of the DPPH radical scavenging activity. Both types of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited remarkably free radical scavenging properties of 84% ± 1% and 79% ± 8%, respectively, in comparison with 0.5%−2% of the scaffolds without the presence of betel leaf extract. Therefore, the GG/SS scaffolds with the presence of betel leaf extract showed excellent potent free radical scavenging property, which is essential for promoting ordinary wound healing by maintaining low wound oxidative stress levels.

In vitro cytotoxicity of the scaffolds was investigated by using the MTT assay in order to confirm the non-cytotoxic property of the scaffold materials. The fabrication of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was aimed at reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and enhancing the resolution phase of inflammation. In vivo skin irritation of scaffolds

The skin irritation test was performed to evaluate the skin irritation of GG/SS, B-GG/SS, and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds using three male New Zealand White rabbits. After 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h of post-treatment, all animals survived the study period without clinical signs of systemic toxicity. Mild to well-defined erythema and very slight edema were occasionally observed at the test sites, likely due to components of the scaffolds such as GG, SS and betel leaf extract. These reactions were transient and fully resolved within 4 days, indicating good skin compatibility. The overall mean irritation scores for GG/SS, B-GG/SS, and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were 0.91, 0.53, and 0.84, respectively (Table 3). After subtracting the control values, the final irritation scores remained below the threshold of 1.0, as defined by ISO 10993-23:2021. Therefore, all tested scaffolds, including B-ICs-GG/SS, were classified as non-irritant under the conditions of this study.

Discussion

3D porous B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds with antioxidant, antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties were fabricated and characterized for wound healing and skin tissue engineering. The scaffolds were designed with a semi-IPN structure, combining a physically crosslinked GG network with SS linear phase to enhance the mechanical properties, structural integrity, and stability. Antioxidant, antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities were introduced to the scaffolds using betel leaf extract to avoid the use of synthetic antibiotics. However, natural plant extracts, such as betel leaf extract, have limitations of low stability and reduced bioactivity over time. To overcome this problem, inclusion complexes were used as protective carriers for betel leaf extract by encapsulating the extract into β-CD, leading to the formation of B-ICs particles. The successful B-ICs formation was confirmed by using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. A shift in the main characteristic peaks of β-CD to lower 2θ values indicated a transition from a crystalline to a more amorphous structure due to complexation. This finding aligns with the previous report, which suggests that lower 2θ values correspond to a decrease in crystal plane spacing, likely due to the insertion of the extract into the β-CD cavity, distorting the crystalline lattice [21]. Additional evidence of B-ICs formation was proved by FTIR, DSC, and TGA analyses. The FTIR spectrum of B-ICs displayed distinct absorption bands of the aromatic ring, carbonyl derivatives and nitro groups, confirming the presence of bioactive compounds from the betel leaf extract [15]. The DSC analysis revealed a significantly different thermal pattern for B-ICs compared to empty β-CD. The endothermic peak associated with water evaporation almost disappeared in the B-ICs curve, indicating successful encapsulation. The melting transition temperature of betel leaf extract was additionally observed between 200 °C and 250 °C [15]. The TGA analysis showed that the thermal decomposition of betel leaf extract in B-ICs occurred between 50 °C and 250 °C, a change not observed in β-CD alone. These thermal results confirmed the interaction between the extract-guest and β-CD-host, leading to the formation of B-ICs [16, 22].

The formation of the GG/SS-based semi-IPNs network in the scaffold was confirmed by using XRD results. The GG/SS scaffolds exhibited broader characteristic peaks compared to the XRD pattern of GG scaffolds. This suggests the potential formation of semi-IPNs [23]. After incorporating B-ICs into the scaffolds, the XRD pattern of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited additional peaks compared to B-GG/SS, GG/SS and GG scaffolds, corresponding to the crystalline structure of B-ICs. The FTIR analysis revealed a reduction in the intensity of O−H and N−H stretching bands, suggesting interactions between the polymer network and B-ICs. DSC and TGA analyses provided further evidence of the semi-IPNs structure, demonstrating that the addition of SS improved the thermal stability of GG scaffolds. In DSC analysis, the sharp exothermic peak of GG scaffolds transformed into a broad peak upon SS incorporation, indicating physical interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, between SS and the GG network. Furthermore, the inclusion of B-ICs increased the degradation temperature of the scaffolds. The development of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds significantly enhanced the thermal stability and could preserve the bioactivity of the scaffolds.

Microstructure analysis showed that the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds maintained a well-formed, highly interconnected porous structure with the incorporation of B-ICs particles, unlike B-GG/SS scaffolds, which are essential for facilitating cell infiltration, oxygen gas transmission and nutrient exchange during wound healing and tissue regeneration. This suggests that the incorporation of B-ICs enhanced structural stability, potentially due to improved compatibility between B-ICs and the polymer network. While B-GG/SS scaffolds showed a reduced pore uniformity when compared to GG/SS scaffolds. This structural change was likely due to the immiscibility between the hydrophilic polymer matrix (GG/SS) and the hydrophobic components of betel leaf extract, which interfered with the molecular arrangement of the GG network and SS phase. It was suggested that the use of B-ICs as a delivery vehicle for betel leaf extract could improve scaffold stability and uniformity, making it a promising strategy for maintaining scaffold integrity. It was also evidenced by the mechanical testing of the scaffolds. The well distribution of B-ICs in the GG/SS matrix exhibited enhanced the mechanical properties of the scaffolds. It means that the B-ICs played a role as a reinforcing filler in the scaffolds. The compressive modulus values of our scaffolds (175–200 kPa) fall within the range reported for commercial wound dressings (approximately 1–1200 kPa) and within that of human skin (5–180 kPa), confirming their suitability for soft-tissue applications [24]. The microstructure and mechanical properties of the scaffolds were related to their water absorption capability and biodegradation profile. The scaffolds having a high ability of water uptake are able to absorb excess volume of exudates and subsequently maintain a suitable environment at the wound bed for accelerating wound healing [19]. Water absorption ability at the equilibrium of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds was slightly lower than that of others because of the complicating microstructure and physical interactions among compositions, which led to less space available for water storage. Nevertheless, the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds enabled fast absorption of an aqueous solution and immediately became soft gels. When compared to recent natural-polymer-based wound dressings reported in the literature, GG/alginate films demonstrated high swelling capacity and good exudate management but tended to lose mechanical integrity after short-term immersion [25]. The B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited higher swelling ability than 1000%, stable integrity for at least 14 days, and compressive modulus values (175–200 kPa) within the range of soft-tissue materials, indicating their suitability for prolonged wound coverage. Their porous sponge architecture was suitable for chronic and exudative wounds requiring bulk absorption.

Biodegradability of tissue engineering scaffolds is a necessary characteristic since the biodegradation profile plays a key role in the mechanical stability and controlled release rate. The biodegradation study showed that GG scaffolds provided the slowest degradation rate and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds degraded the fastest, while all scaffolds maintained structural stability after 14 days of investigation. The reason why GG scaffolds provided the slowest degradation rate among the others might be the highest capability of water absorption. Since the GG network seems to have the highest free volume among polymer chains. But by combining other components including SS, betel leaf extract, and B-ICs, with GG, it reduced the opportunity of ionic interaction and crosslinking degree, and subsequently faster rate of weight loss was observed. It is also possible that at the first step of investigation, the betel leaf extract and linear SS phase were initially released, leading to a fast decrease in sample weight with time. Since a moist wound environment is a satisfying condition to promote wound healing, a suitable water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) together with water absorption of the wound dressings and scaffolds is controlled to balance the fluid at wound sites [26]. The WVTR values of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds (340–400 g·m−2·d−1) were within the acceptable range at 90–2893 g×m−2×d−1 for commercial wound dressings [11, 27]. These results confirm that the fabricated scaffolds could provide an optimal balance of fluid absorption, moist retention and fluid loss.

B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds provided better controlled release behavior, evidenced by lower release rate and smaller released amount of betel leaf extract when compared to those of B-GG/SS scaffolds. This confirms that the encapsulation of betel leaf extract by forming inclusive complexes could slow down the initial burst release and facilitate the controlled release manner of the scaffolds due to their ability to reduce the diffusion rate of betel leaf extract. Importantly, the release content of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds ranged from 60% to 80%, which has been reported as an effective therapeutic level. In agreement with previous work, the incorporation of exosome nanovesicles into an ε-polylysine/hydroxyethyl starch (PLL/HES) semi-IPN hydrogel prolonged drug release up to 7 h, whereas the hydrogel in the absence of exosomes exhibited a rapid and complete release within 3.5 h [28].

To understand the mechanism of betel leaf extract release, a mathematical model of Korsmeyer-Peppas was applied to analyze the release kinetics of the scaffolds. The release of B-GG/SS scaffolds was driven by the Fickian diffusion mechanism. The penetration of water molecules through the matrix structure was a key factor for the diffusion of the extract. This finding was the same as that observed in a recent study on betel leaf extract-loaded chitosan hydrogels [29]. Interestingly, the release kinetics study of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds showed that their release was governed by a different mechanism. An anomalous (non-Fickian) transport controlled the release of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, suggesting a combination of diffusion, polymer swelling, and erosion mechanisms [28]. The encapsulation of the betel leaf extract with the formation of B-ICs and incorporation of them in the scaffolds influenced the release behavior by altering the driven release mechanism. This finding supported the in vitro biodegradation behavior of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds that exhibited the fastest degradation rate among other scaffold types. It was probably caused by bulk erosion taking place within the scaffolds, as a result of the weight loss belonging to immersion time. In addition, the lower release constant (k) of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds confirms that encapsulation slowed down the release rate when compared to that of B-GG/SS scaffolds. The release rate was influenced by the physical and structural properties of the polymer matrix and the loaded component [21]. Thus, the complicated structure of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds provided a predominant impact on the release behaviors in the present study. These results supported the faster degradation rate of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, as bulk erosion within the scaffold might have contributed to sustained extract release. Thus, the more complex polymeric structure of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds hindered fast diffusion, making them better suited for long-term drug delivery.

Both types of B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited strong scavenging activity, confirming the antioxidant activity of betel leaf extract, which is beneficial for preventing excessive inflammation at the wound site. Importantly, the encapsulation of betel leaf extract did not significantly reduce its antioxidant effectiveness, suggesting that the ICs preserved bioactive compounds while enabling sustained release. Moreover, the B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds showed significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus and E. coli bacteria. The antibacterial effect of betel leaf extract can be attributed to its rich phytochemical composition, including phenolic compounds such as eugenol and chavibetol, flavonoids, tannins, etc. [30]. The phytocomponents in betel leaves provided antibacterial properties by damaging the bacterial cell wall, leading to cell lysis and distortion [9]. B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds had slightly smaller inhibition zones, indicating a slight reduction in antibacterial activity compared to B-GG/SS. However, the incorporation of B-ICs facilitated controlled release, potentially prolonging antimicrobial action over time. This suggests that although B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds might have slightly lower initial antibacterial effects, they could sustain their antimicrobial efficacy for a longer period, which is beneficial for extended wound protection.

The MTT assay results confirmed that the GG, GG/SS, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds were non-cytotoxic to RAW 264.7 macrophage cells, indicating that the materials used in the fabrication of the scaffolds, as well as the encapsulated betel leaf extract, were highly compatible with the cells and well-suited to support cell viability. The scaffolds play a vital role not only in wound healing but also in regulating the inflammatory response associated with tissue repair. The controlled anti-inflammatory agent released from the scaffolds was expected to reduce excessive inflammation and prevent chronic inflammation. The controlled betel leaf extract release of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds might prolong anti-inflammatory action, allowing sustained regulation of inflammation. This suggests that the betel leaf extract encapsulated in the scaffolds showed potential for anti-inflammatory effect and the scaffolds might help promote a microenvironment conductive to tissue regeneration while minimizing inflammation, which agrees with the previous reports [31, 32]. The non-toxic property and anti-inflammatory effect of the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds thus ensured safe application and could help in preventing excessive inflammation and chronic wounds.

The present study assessed the dermal irritation potential of GG/SS, B-GG/SS, and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds in accordance with ISO 10993-23:2021. All formulations were found to be non-irritant, as their mean irritation scores remained below the regulatory threshold of 1.0. No mortality or adverse systemic effects were observed, indicating that the materials are well tolerated not only locally at the skin site but also in terms of general biocompatibility. Mild erythema and slight edema appeared after injection but subsided within 4 days, likely due to the intrinsic characteristics of the scaffold materials. GG and SS were both biocompatible but might cause temporary immune responses during initial exposure, especially when introduced intracutaneously [33, 34]. Furthermore, the encapsulation of betel leaf extract (in B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds) might have contributed to the observed mild erythema. Betel leaf contains bioactive compounds such as eugenol and hydroxychavicol, which possess antimicrobial and antioxidant properties but can also act as mild skin irritants depending on concentration and delivery route [31, 32].

Overall, these results demonstrate the suitability of the semi-IPN B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds, including their physico-chemical, bioactive and encapsulated formulations, for wound-healing applications. To contextualize the significance of this work, a comparison between the developed B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds and the recently reported semi-IPN system for wound healing was summarized in Table 4. The B-ICs-GG/SS system uniquely integrated the advantages of natural biopolymers with β-cyclodextrin-mediated phytochemical encapsulation, enabling sustained bioactive release, improved mechanical integrity, and multifunctional wound healing performance (i.e., antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties). The scaffolds acted as active wound dressings, surpassing conventional passive dressings.

Conclusions

3D interconnected porous B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds with antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties were successfully fabricated and characterized for wound healing and skin tissue engineering applications. A semi-IPN structure was developed by incorporating a physically crosslinked GG network with a linear SS phase, enhancing the mechanical properties, structural integrity, and thermal stability of the scaffolds. To introduce bioactive multifunctional properties without the use of synthetic antibiotics, betel leaf extract was incorporated into the scaffolds. However, to overcome its limitations of low stability and rapid degradation, inclusion complexes (ICs) with β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) were employed as protective carriers. The successful formation of betel leaf extract inclusion complexes (B-ICs) was confirmed by XRD, FTIR, DSC, and TG analyses. SEM analysis demonstrated that the addition of B-ICs to GG/SS scaffolds maintained a well-formed, interconnected porous structure, which is crucial for cell infiltration, oxygen diffusion, and nutrient exchange. Furthermore, B-ICs improved the mechanical properties and stability of scaffolds potentially due to better compatibility between the polymer network and the encapsulated extract. The B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited rapid water uptake within 30 min and reached equilibrium at 24 h, suggesting their ability to maintain a moist wound environment. Biodegradation studies showed that B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds exhibited the fastest degradation (~40% weight loss) while maintaining structural stability for up to 14 days. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) values (340–400 g·m−2·d−1) were within the acceptable range for commercial wound dressings, confirming that the scaffolds provide an optimal balance of fluid absorption, moisture retention, and controlled fluid loss. In vitro release studies demonstrated that the B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds significantly reduced the initial burst release compared to direct incorporation in B-GG/SS scaffolds. The sustained release profile of B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds lasted up to 48 h, suggesting a more controlled and prolonged delivery of bioactive compounds. This release behavior was attributed to a combination of diffusion, polymer swelling, and scaffold erosion mechanisms. The sustained release capability, along with the antioxidant and antibacterial properties of betel leaf extract, suggests that these scaffolds could effectively protect wounds from oxidative stress and bacterial infections while promoting tissue regeneration. The biological assessment confirmed that all scaffold types were non-cytotoxic to RAW 264.7 macrophage cells, with cell viability remaining above 80% at extract concentrations up to 25 mg/ml. Additionally, B-GG/SS and B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds demonstrated a strong anti-inflammatory effect by significantly reducing nitric oxide (NO) production, comparable to the positive control, vermelhotin. The encapsulation of betel leaf extract within B-ICs not only preserved its bioactive properties but also prolonged its anti-inflammatory effect, which could be beneficial in regulating the inflammatory phase of wound healing and preventing chronic wounds. The in vivo skin irritation test in rabbits confirmed that all scaffold types were non-irritant, with only mild and reversible skin reactions. These results support the dermal safety and biocompatibility of the materials, a critical requirement for clinical translation. Overall, the results suggest that B-ICs-GG/SS scaffolds have significant potential for application in wound healing and skin tissue engineering. The incorporation of B-ICs enhanced scaffold stability, mechanical strength, and controlled release properties while maintaining antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory bioactivity. Future in vivo studies are necessary to validate the long-term therapeutic efficacy and biocompatibility of these scaffolds in complex wound environments.
