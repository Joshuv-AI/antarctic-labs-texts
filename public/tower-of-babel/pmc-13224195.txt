Inadvertent neuroform atlas stent deployment into a posterior cerebral artery circumflex branch with long-term patency during stent-assisted coiling of a basilar artery aneurysm
Authors: Yuta Oka, Yoshinobu Horio, Koichiro Suzuki, Munenari Matsuishi, Kazumasa Senju, Ryuhei Takeyama, Shintaro Yoshinaga, Hiromasa Kobayashi, Koichiro Takemoto, Hiroshi Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Stent-assisted coiling (SAC) is an established option for wide-neck intracranial aneurysms. However, ischemic complications such as in-stent thrombosis or stenosis remain, particularly when stents are deployed in small-caliber vessels. The Neuroform Atlas is generally recommended for parent vessels ≥2 mm, and data on its use in small-caliber arteries arising from the posterior cerebral artery (PCA), including the circumflex branch, are limited.Case Description: A 51-year-old woman with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and prior renal transplantation presented with sudden severe headache and vomiting. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no subarachnoid hemorrhage, while magnetic resonance angiography revealed a wide-neck basilar artery (BA) tip aneurysm suggestive of impending rupture. Emergent SAC was performed after dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and prasugrel. A microcatheter was advanced into a branch arising from the left PCA P1 segment, initially presumed to be the P2 segment. After coil placement revealed neck instability, a 3.0 × 15 mm Neuroform Atlas was deployed from the presumed P2 segment toward the BA. Cone-beam computed tomography demonstrated that approximately 5 mm of the distal stent had unintentionally extended into a PCA circumflex branch. Because antegrade flow was preserved, conservative management was chosen. Postprocedural MRI showed no infarction. One-year angiography demonstrated complete aneurysm occlusion and sustained circumflex branch patency.Conclusion: Long-term patency without ischemic complications was achieved despite unintentional stent deployment in a circumflex branch. This case underscores the importance of appropriate antithrombotic therapy and highlights the need for constant intraoperative awareness of the potential for unintended device deployment into perforating arteries during endovascular treatment.

INTRODUCTION

For wide-necked aneurysms, stent-assisted coiling (SAC) is an important therapeutic option that improves the rate of complete aneurysm occlusion. However, ischemic complications such as in-stent thrombosis or stenosis may occur postoperatively, underscoring the importance of appropriate perioperative antiplatelet therapy.[6,16] In general, stents, including the Neuroform Atlas, are recommended for deployment in parent vessels with a diameter of ≥2 mm according to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration labeling.[19] Nevertheless, there are reports describing SAC performed in vessels with a diameter of ≤2 mm, with comparable treatment outcomes achieved under appropriate perioperative management.[12]

To the best of our knowledge, there have been no case reports describing stent deployment specifically in the circumflex branch of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) using the Neuroform Atlas. Although such risks are not confined to the circumflex branch, perforating arteries around the PCA are particularly susceptible to injury during endovascular procedures, potentially resulting in procedure-related complications such as vessel perforation, hemorrhage, and ischemia.[3,5,10,18]

We report a case in which the Neuroform Atlas was inadvertently deployed in the circumflex branch during SAC for a basilar artery (BA) aneurysm with impending rupture. Despite this unintended deployment, no ischemic complications occurred, and long-term patency was maintained.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 51-year-old woman with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease who had previously undergone renal transplantation presented with an abrupt onset of an explosive headache accompanied by vomiting. She had no prior history of chronic or recurrent headaches. She visited a local clinic, where magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no evidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage [Figure 1a]. However, magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated an aneurysm at the BA tip [Figure 1b]. Given the acute onset of symptoms and the radiological findings, impending rupture of the aneurysm was suspected, and she was referred to our institution for further evaluation and treatment.

(a) Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging at the initial presentation showing no evidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage. (b) Magnetic resonance angiography demonstrating an aneurysm at the basilar artery tip (white arrow). (c) Three-dimensional rotational angiography reveals a 2-mm aneurysm at the basilar artery tip (white arrow). (d) Digital subtraction angiography confirms a 2-mm aneurysm at the basilar artery tip (white arrow).

On admission, the patient was neurologically intact, with no focal neurological deficits.

Given the wide-neck configuration of the aneurysm and its anatomical relationship with the posterior cerebral arteries, stent-assisted coil embolization was planned. Because the use of an intracranial stent was anticipated, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin (200 mg) and prasugrel (20 mg) was initiated before the procedure. Given the clinical suspicion of impending rupture, emergent endovascular treatment was performed on the same day after confirming adequate antiplatelet preparation.

Endovascular treatment

Endovascular treatment was performed under general anesthesia. An 8-Fr long sheath was inserted via the right femoral artery, and systemic heparinization was performed to maintain the activated clotting time at ≥200 s.

The left vertebral angiography demonstrated a basilar tip aneurysm [Figure 1c and d]. An 8-Fr Roadmaster 90 cm guiding catheter (Goodman, Aichi, Japan) was advanced into the left vertebral artery (VA), and a 6-Fr SOFIA distal access catheter (Micro Vention Inc.) was navigated into the V4 segment of the left VA.

An Excelsior SL-10 (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) microcatheter with a Synchro SELECT Soft 215-cm (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) microwire was advanced into a branch arising from the left PCA P1 segment, which was initially presumed to represent the left PCA P2 segment based on its angiographic appearance [Figure 2a]. Another Excelsior SL-10 microcatheter was navigated into the aneurysm.

(a) For planned stent deployment, a microcatheter was navigated into what was presumed intraoperatively to be the left posterior cerebral artery (PCA) P2 segment. In retrospect, the microcatheter had been advanced into a circumflex branch arising from the left PCA P1 segment. The first marker of the microcatheter is indicated by the (black arrow). (b) During deployment of the Neuroform Atlas stent, incomplete expansion of the distal stent marker was observed (black arrow). (c) After full deployment of the Neuroform Atlas stent, the distal stent marker (black arrow) remained insufficiently expanded. (d) Postprocedural cone-beam computed tomography demonstrated that approximately 5 mm of the distal portion of the stent was unintentionally deployed into the left PCA circumflex branch. (e) The diameter of the left PCA circumflex branch measured 0.42 mm. (f) Three-dimensional rotational angiography confirmed patency of the left PCA circumflex branch (white arrowheads). (g) Final left vertebral artery angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the basilar tip aneurysm.

Coil embolization was initiated using a Target Tetra 1.5 mm × 2 cm coil (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), followed by an OPTIMA Complex 10 Super Soft 1 mm × 1 cm coil (Balt, USA). After coil deployment, mobility of the coil loops at the aneurysm neck was observed, and SAC was deemed necessary.

A Neuroform Atlas stent 3.0 × 15 mm (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was deployed from the presumed left PCA P2 segment toward the BA. At the onset of stent deployment, incomplete expansion of the distal stent marker was noted [Figure 2b]. Following full deployment, the mobility of the coil loops at the aneurysm neck resolved. However, the distal stent markers remained insufficiently expanded [Figure 2c].

Subsequent cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) revealed that approximately 5 mm of the distal stent had been unintentionally deployed into a circumflex branch originating from the left PCA P1 segment, rather than the intended P2 segment [Figure 2d], and the diameter of the circumflex branch was 0.42 mm [Figure 2e].

As antegrade blood flow in the PCA circumflex branch was preserved [Figure 2f], conservative management was selected.

Final angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the aneurysm with a volume embolization ratio of 38.0% [Figure 2g]. Hemostasis of the right femoral artery was achieved using a Perclose ProStyle closure device (Abbott, USA). The patient recovered uneventfully from anesthesia, and postprocedural CBCT confirmed the absence of intracranial hemorrhage. Her headache improved after she awoke from anesthesia.

Postoperative course

Postoperatively, the patient was managed with aspirin (100 mg/day), prasugrel (3.75 mg/day), and continuous intravenous heparin (15,000 units/day). Diffusion-weighted MRI performed on postoperative day 1 revealed no evidence of cerebral infarction [Figure 3a].

(a) Diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging performed on postoperative day 1 demonstrated no high-intensity lesions in the brainstem. (b) Follow-up digital subtraction angiography (DSA) performed 1 year postoperatively demonstrated complete occlusion of the basilar tip aneurysm. (c) Follow-up DSA at 1 year demonstrated preserved patency of the left posterior cerebral artery (PCA) circumflex branch (white arrowheads). (d) Three-dimensional rotational angiography confirmed patency of the left PCA circumflex. branch (white arrowheads).

Heparin was discontinued on postoperative day 2, and DAPT with aspirin (100 mg/day) and prasugrel (3.75 mg/day) was continued. The postoperative course thereafter was uneventful, with no neurological deficits, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 7.

Aspirin was discontinued 6 months after the procedure. Follow-up cerebral angiography performed 1 year postoperatively demonstrated complete occlusion of the aneurysm [Figure 3b], with preservation of patency of the PCA circumflex branch, into which the distal end of the stent extended [Figure 3c and d]. Prasugrel (3.75 mg/day) is currently continued.

There has been no recurrence of severe headache during the 1-year follow-up after treatment.

DISCUSSION

In the present case, an impending rupture was suspected because of the abrupt, severe headache in a patient without a prior history of habitual headaches. Although no subarachnoid hemorrhage was detected on initial MRI, this presentation was consistent with a sentinel headache, which is thought to reflect aneurysmal wall instability preceding overt rupture.[20] Given the higher prevalence of intracranial aneurysms and potentially earlier rupture in patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease.[2] urgent intervention was considered justified despite the absence of radiological hemorrhage.

The circumflex branch is one of the perforating branches that arise primarily from the P1 and P2 segments of the PCA. These arteries encircle the brainstem and provide blood supply to critical regions of the midbrain (mesencephalon) and diencephalon. Based on their course and vascular territory, they are classified into two types: The short circumflex artery (SCxA) and the long circumflex artery (LCxA).[11]

The SCxA predominantly originates from the P1 segment (86%) and typically reaches as far as the geniculate bodies. It may also provide branches to the cerebral peduncle, interpeduncular fossa, and posterior perforated substance. Similarly, the LCxA primarily arises from the P1 segment (80%) and supplies the colliculi (superior colliculus and inferior colliculus). It also provides blood supply to the cerebral peduncle, tegmentum, medial geniculate body, and pulvinar.[7,11,15,22] The diameter of the LCxA is reported to be 0.5–0.8 mm.[17] Occlusion of a circumflex branch may result in vertical gaze palsy (Parinaud’s syndrome) due to infarction of the posterior commissure or the nuclei of Darkschewitsch or Cajal.[4]

Anastomoses exist around the brainstem, particularly between the circumflex branches of the PCA and the superior cerebellar artery (SCA). The collicular artery (LCxA) forms an extensive arterial network with branches of the SCA. Within this network, the superior colliculus receives more branches than the inferior colliculus, though the latter still receives a few small branches from the SCA.[17,22] Previous reports have suggested that, owing to sufficient collateral circulation (pial collateral blood flow), trapping of aneurysms arising from circumflex PCA branches can be performed without causing significant neurological deficits.[4]

To the best of our knowledge, there have been no case reports describing the deployment of intracranial stents, such as the Neuroform Atlas, specifically in the circumflex branch. Although not confined exclusively to the circumflex branch, perforating arteries around the PCA are particularly vulnerable to injury during endovascular procedures, and procedure-related complications such as vessel perforation, hemorrhage, and ischemia may occur.[3,5,10,18] Not only in the present case, but the circumflex branch originating from the P1 segment also follows a course similar to that of the P2 segment. Therefore, differentiation between the two is sometimes challenging during the procedure. To avoid such inadvertent deployment, super-selective angiography before stent deployment is essential. In addition, careful confirmation of the microcatheter tip position under multiple working projections and a thorough understanding of the branching anatomy are critical for preventing misplacement.

Nevertheless, several reports have described stent deployment, including the Neuroform Atlas, in small-caliber vessels with a diameter of ≤2 mm. Ozaki et al.[12] reported cases in which the Neuroform Atlas was deployed in vessels ≤2 mm. The vessels were analyzed by dividing them into two groups: Vessels with a diameter <1.5 mm (18 cases) and those with a diameter ≥1.5 mm (31 cases). No significant differences in treatment outcomes were observed between the two groups, and thromboembolic events occurred in 2 cases (11.1%) in the <1.5 mm group and in 4 cases (12.9%) in the 1.5–1.9 mm group (P = 0.8573). Furthermore, among cases with a vessel diameter <1.0 mm, there were four cases, with the smallest vessel measuring 0.6 mm, and no thromboembolic complications or recanalization were observed in any of these cases. Burkhardt et al.[1] reported that deployment of the Neuroform Atlas in vessels with a diameter <1.5 mm resulted in comparable treatment outcomes and complication rates to those observed when the stent was deployed in vessels ≥1.5 mm. Li et al.[9] reported that a stent angle ≥75° or a parent-vessel diameter mismatch ratio ≥1.25 was associated with an increased risk of incomplete stent apposition. The parent-vessel diameter mismatch ratio was defined as the ratio between the larger and smaller vessel diameters at the distal end of the aneurysm neck.

With regard to antithrombotic therapy, most patients undergoing stent-assisted coil embolization are managed preoperatively with DAPT consisting of aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor. Although the optimal duration of postoperative DAPT remains controversial, many reports recommend continuing DAPT for approximately 3 months, followed by single antiplatelet therapy thereafter. Indeed, some studies have reported no significant difference in the incidence of ischemic complications between short-term and long-term DAPT.[13,14] There are reports indicating that intraoperative thrombotic events improved with the administration of tirofiban, without adversely affecting clinical outcomes.[8,21]

In the present case, a Neuroform Atlas stent was inadvertently deployed into a circumflex branch with a diameter of 0.42 mm. Nevertheless, a favorable clinical outcome without ischemic complications was achieved. Although previous reports also indicate that reliable administration of antithrombotic therapy reduces the risk of perioperative thrombotic complications, these findings should not be interpreted as supporting the intentional deployment of stents in perforating or circumflex branches. The primary message of this case is a technical caution, emphasizing the importance of preventing inadvertent deployment through meticulous anatomical assessment and careful intraoperative technique, including precise confirmation of the microcatheter tip position.

CONCLUSION

We report a case in which long-term patency was achieved in the absence of ischemic complications when a Neuroform Atlas stent was unintentionally deployed in the circumflex branch. This case underscores the importance of appropriate antithrombotic therapy and highlights the need for constant intraoperative awareness of the potential for unintended device deployment into perforating arteries during endovascular treatment.
