A Parametrized Complexity View on Robust Scheduling with Budgeted Uncertainty
Noam Goldberg, Dvir Shabtay

Abstract
In this study, we investigate a robust single-machine scheduling problem under processing time uncertainty. The uncertainty is modeled using the budgeted approach, where each job has a nominal and deviation processing time, and the number of deviations is bounded by Γ \Gamma . The objective is to minimize the maximum number of tardy jobs over all possible scenarios. Since the problem is NP-hard in general, we focus on analyzing its tractability under the assumption that some natural parameter of the problem is bounded by a constant. We consider three parameters: the robustness parameter Γ \Gamma , the number of distinct due dates in the instance, and the number of jobs with nonzero deviations. Using parametrized-complexity theory, we prove that the problem is W[1]-hard with respect to Γ \Gamma , but can be solved in XP time with respect to the same parameter. With respect to the number of different due dates, we establish a stronger hardness result by showing that the problem remains NP-hard even when there are only two different due dates and is solvable in pseudo-polynomial time when the number of due dates is upper bounded by a constant. To complement these results, we show that the case of a common (single) due date, reduces to a robust binary knapsack problem with equal item profits, which we prove to be solvable in polynomial time. Finally, we prove that the problem is solvable in FPT time with respect to the number of nonzero deviations. Keywords: Robust scheduling; parameterized complexity; budgeted uncertainty; single machine scheduling; number of tardy jobs

Introduction

We study a scheduling problem at the intersection of two recent trends in scheduling research: robust scheduling models with uncertainty and parameterized complexity analysis. More particularly, we aim to examine whether an NP-hard robust scheduling problem becomes tractable when one of its natural parameters (such as the robustness parameter) is of a limited size. Our setting includes the problem of minimizing the number of tardy jobs on a single machine, a problem whose deterministic (also known as the nominal) variant can be tractably solved, using the classical Moore algorithm
[
38
]
. This is in contrast to the NP-hardness of the robust version of the problem, where the processing times are subject to budgeted uncertainty, which is the subject of the current paper.

1.1
Robust Scheduling

In classical deterministic scheduling models

4

21

In the discrete scenario approach, the robust counterparts of tractable combinatorial problems may often become NP-hard; see for example

as budgeted uncertainty for network flow and other combinatorial problems, restricts each uncertain parameter to two possible values: its nominal value or a deviation, where at most

6
,
8
,
7

1.2
Problem Definition

We consider a robust single machine scheduling problem where the objective is to minimize the number of tardy jobs, i.e., the number of jobs completed after their due date. In this problem, we are given a set of
n
jobs that has to be processed by a single machine without preemption. The uncertainty set of processing times is given by (
1
). Moreover, each job
j\in[n]
has a (certain) due date,
d_{j}
, for its completion. We assume that all parameters that define an instance to our problem are restricted to non-negative integer values. A solution of our problem is defined by a schedule, a job permutation
\sigma=(\sigma(1),\ldots\sigma(n))
, defining the processing order on the single machine. Given such a permutation,
\sigma
, and a possible scenario of job processing time deviation
p\in\U^{\Gamma}
, the
completion time
of job
j
is
C_{j}(\sigma,p):=\sum_{i\in[n]:\sigma(i)\leq\sigma(j)}p_{i}
; that is, it is the total processing times (under scenario
p
) of all jobs preceding
j
in the schedule (including
j
itself). We use
U_{j}(\sigma,p)
to
indicate
whether job
j
is tardy in schedule
\sigma
under scenario
p
. Accordingly,
U_{j}(\sigma,p)=1
iff
C_{j}(\sigma,p)>d_{j}
(and 0 otherwise),

Letting
\Sigma
denote the set of all possible
n!
schedules (job processing permutations), one can consider the following two variants of the robust counterpart of minimizing the number of tardy jobs, where in both cases deciding the schedule
\sigma
is done ex-ante prior knowing the actual scenario realization.

•

Variant 1
, denoted by

1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
. For a given job schedule, in this variant, a job is considered early if it is non-tardy in all possible scenario realizations. Accordingly, we define
U_{j}(\sigma)=\max_{p\in\U}U_{j}(\sigma,p)
, and job
j
is considered an early job if
U_{j}(\sigma)=0
. Otherwise,
U_{j}(\sigma)=1
, and job
j
is tardy. The objective is to determine job processing permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma
with minimum
\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
value.

•

Variant 2, denoted by
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\max_{p\in\U^{\Gamma}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma,p)
. The goal here is to find a job schedule that results in the fewest tardy jobs in the worst-case scenario. Note that in this variant, a job can be early in one scenario but tardy in another, depending on the realization. Our objective is to determine a job processing permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma

that minimizes
\max_{p\in\U^{\Gamma}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma,p)
.

We note that Variant 1 is more appropriate when customers of different jobs to be scheduled are each guaranteed in advance an early delivery of their jobs. Otherwise, the scheduler may have to reject the job. Otherwise, if the scheduler processes all jobs and tardy jobs are penalized ex-post, Variant 2 is more appropriate. Bougeret et al.
[
11
]
studied Variant 2 and proved that it is NP-hard. However, their result applies for Variant 1 as well. We note that Bougeret et al.
[
11
]
left
it
open whether Variant 2 is strongly or only weakly NP-hard.
A similar
question
remains
open for variant 1.

Theorem 1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Problems
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\max_{p\in\U^{\Gamma}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma,p)
and

1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
are NP-hard.

1.3
Parametrized Complexity and Research Objectives

Given the hardness result in Theorem
1
, our main aim is to determine whether the problem becomes tractable when the values of some natural parameters of the problem are of limited size. We do so using the theory of parametrized complexity, and for the sake of brevity focus only on the first variant.

Overview of
parameterized complexity.

Parameterized complexity
[
18
,
19
,
39
]
is a branch of complexity whose main goal is to analyze the hardness of problems not only in terms of their input size, but also in terms of other “structural” parameters of the input. This theory has recently
attracted significant interest of the scheduling community (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
,
[
44
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
). The central concept in parameterized complexity is that of an FPT algorithm:

Definition 1
.

An algorithm that solves each instance of a problem in
f(k)n^{O(1)}
time, where
n
is the size of the instance,
k
is the value of the parameter and
f(k)
is a computable function (monotonically increasing) that depends solely on
k
, is called an FPT algorithm with respect to
k
. The class of problems
\mathcal{FPT}
is defined as the class of all problems solvable by an FPT algorithm.

In cases where
k
is small,
FPT algorithms become very efficient. If we cannot design an FPT algorithm, the next best thing is to design an XP algorithm:

Definition 2
.

An algorithm that solves each instance of a problem in
n^{f(k)}
time is called an XP algorithm with respect to
k
. The class
\mathcal{XP}
is the class of all problems solvable by an XP algorithm.

Both FPT and XP algorithms
are guaranteed to
solve
a
given problem in polynomial time whenever
k
is
O(1)
. However, an FPT algorithm is preferable
as its running time dominates that of XP (asymptotically with respect to
n
).
In between these two classes lies the class
\mathcal{W}[1]
. The common assumption used in parameterized complexity is that
\mathcal{FPT}\neq\mathcal{W}[1]
, and this assumption is used to show that certain problems are unlikely to admit FPT algorithms under certain parameterizations. This is done by providing appropriate reductions (called parameterized reductions in the literature) from known
\mathcal{W}[1]
-hard problems (
i.e.
problems for which an FPT algorithm implies
\mathcal{FPT}=\mathcal{W}[1]
, see
[
18
,
19
]
for more information). To summarize, we have the following hierarchy between the parameterized complexity classes discussed herein

\mathcal{FPT}\subseteq\mathcal{W}[1]\subseteq\mathcal{XP}
.

Research objectives.

We analyze Variant 1 with respect to the following three parameters:

•

The robustness parameter,

.

•

The number of

We obtain several hardness and algorithmic results for Variant 1 with respect to

The first parameter is relevant, for example, when the number of jobs that may deviate in any given schedule may be small.
For example, when scheduling medical procedures,

]
.

Prior to our analysis, we

Lemma 2
.

There exists an optimal schedule for the
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)

2.2
An XP Algorithm

Following the result Lemma

Consider now the case where

\hat{p}_{j}\leq\hat{p}_{\min}(\pi)

F_{j-1}(\ell,\pi)\hskip 5.69046pt\hskip 14.22636pt\text{otherwise.}\\

3
The Number of Different Due Dates

In this section, we study the tractability of the
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
problem when parametrized by the number of different due dates in the instance,
v_{d}
. We begin by showing that the problem is NP-hard already for
v_{d}=2
. We then complement our analysis by showing that the problem is solvable in polynomial time when all jobs share a common due date, that is for
v_{d}=1
, and develop a pseudo-polynomial algorithm in case of a number of due dates that is constant with respect to the problem’s size.

3.1
NP hardness for two different due dates

We show that the problem with
v_{d}=2
is NP-hard based on a reduction from the NP-hard equal-cardinality partition problem.

Definition 4
.

Equal-cardinality partition problem: Given a set of
2h
positive integers
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},...,a_{2h}\}
(
\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}a_{i}=2A

\mathcal{A}
into two subsets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
such that
|\mathcal{A}_{1}|=|\mathcal{A}_{2}|=h
and
\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{i}=\sum_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}a_{i}=A
.

The following theorem establishes that the problem is not in XP (unless P=NP) when parametrized by the number of different due dates.

Theorem 6
.

The
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
problem is NP-hard even if
v_{d}=\left|\left\{d_{j}\;\left|\;\;j\in[n]\right.\right\}\right|=2
.

Proof.

Given an instance of Equal-cardinality partition problem, we construct the following instance for the
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
problem with
3h
jobs and two different due dates. We set
\Gamma=h
. For
j=1,\ldots,2h
, we set
\bar{p}_{j}=M-a_{j}
and
\hat{p}_{j}=2a_{j}
. For

\bar{p}_{j}=0
and
\hat{p}_{j}=M^{2}
. For

and for

For convenience,
we denote
\mathcal{J}_{1}=[2h]

and
\mathcal{J}_{2}=\{2h+1,\ldots,3h\}
. To complete the reduction, we select
any

\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)\leq h
.

Now, suppose that the answer for the equal-cardinality partition problem is

so that the first
h

\mathcal{E}_{2}
. As these jobs have the largest deviation out of the first
2h
jobs scheduled and

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\U}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{1}}\bar{p}_{j}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{2}}(\bar{p}_{j}+\hat{p}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq h(M+M^{2})-\sum_{a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{j}

\displaystyle=h(M+M^{2})-A=d^{(2)},

implying that at least
2h

Now, suppose we have a job processing permutation

Further, no more than

3.2
Common Due Date – Robust Knapsack with Equal Profits

We now consider the problem
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
in the special case of a common due date for all jobs,
d
. To this end, we consider formulating the problem as a mathematical program. In the deterministic case (where
\hat{p}=\mathbf{0}
), our problem is a special case of the well studied binary knapsack problem, where all items share the same profit value. For general
\U^{\Gamma}
(where
\hat{p}\neq\mathbf{0}

[

Thus, our problem is

\displaystyle\max_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}z_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}\bar{p}_{j}z_{j}+\max_{p\in\U^{\Gamma}}\sum_{j\in[n]}(p_{j}-\bar{p}_{j})z_{j}\leq d.

(6b)

Applying a standard technique of dualizing the robust constraint (

\displaystyle\max_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n},\;\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}\bar{p}_{j}z_{j}+\sum_{j\in[n]}\lambda_{j}+\Gamma\mu\leq d

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{j}z_{j}-\lambda_{j}\leq\mu

\displaystyle j\in[n].

(7c)

[

37

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[n]}\bar{p}_{j}z_{j}+\sum_{j\in[n]}\max\{\hat{p}_{j}z_{j}-\mu,0\}+\Gamma\mu\leq d.

3.3
A Constant Number of Due Dates

Suppose that there are
k
different due date values,
d^{(1)},\ldots,d^{(k)}
such that

. We also denote the set of jobs having due date

and further assume that the jobs are indexed according to an EDD order; so that for all

.
The problem is formulated below as a multidimensional extension of (

\displaystyle\max_{\mu,\lambda\geq 0,z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle l\in[k]

\displaystyle j\in\bar{\mathcal{J}_{l}},l\in[k],

(9c)

where
\bar{\mathcal{J}_{l}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}\mathcal{J}_{i}

For a given (fixed) vector

early jobs such that:

•

\mu_{l}

for each

(that is, all early jobs with deviations larger than

the “worst case” completion time of the last job in

for

being is minimal deviation, is at most

.

in the “worst case” scenario if it is assigned to be an early job. For vector

as the subvector
(x_{a},x_{a+1},\ldots,x_{b})

Now, define

\displaystyle M=\left(\{\hat{p}_{j}:j\in[n]\}\cup\{0\}\right)^{k}

4
Number of Jobs with Nonzero Deviation

Given an instance for
1|\U^{\Gamma}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
, let
\mathcal{J}_{1}=\left\{j\in[n]\;\left|\;\;\hat{p}_{j}\neq 0\right.\right\}
and
\mathcal{J}_{2}=\left\{j\in[n]\;\left|\;\;\hat{p}_{j}=0\right.\right\}
.
Let
k=|\mathcal{J}_{1}|
, that is the number of jobs with nonzero deviations (or uncertain processing times). In this section, we take
k
as a parameter, and show that the problem is solvable in FPT time with respect to this parameter. Note that, without loss of generality, we can assume that
k>\Gamma
. Otherwise in the worst case scenario all jobs in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
deviate, and the problem reduces to a nominal (deterministic) problem with processing times
p=\bar{p}+\hat{p}
.

To solve the robust problem, we break it into
O(2^{k})
subproblems, corresponding to all possible subsets of
\mathcal{E}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{1}
. In each of these subproblems, we solve a restricted
variant of the original problem where the jobs in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are restricted to be early, while the jobs in complementary set
\mathcal{J}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{1}
are restricted to be
tardy. By solving all
O(2^{k})
subproblems and selecting the best solution of all feasible subproblem solutions, we determine an optimal solution for the original problem. In the following we assume that the jobs are ordered according to the EDD rule, such that
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
(otherwise they can be renumbered to satisfy this ordering).

Consider now a given subproblem, and let

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{\mathcal{E}_{1}(1),\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{1}(k^{\prime})\}\subseteq[n]

be the (EDD) ordered subset of jobs that are restricted to be early (
k^{\prime}\in[k]
). For convenience, for
j\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
define
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{-1}(j)
as its position in the ordering, which must be in
[k^{\prime}]
. The remaining jobs in
\mathcal{J}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{1}
are restricted to be tardy. As all tardy jobs are scheduled last in an arbitrary order, we can omit this set of jobs from the instance, which now includes only
n^{\prime}=n-k+k^{\prime}
jobs. The resulting subproblem is a restricted variant of
1|\Gamma^{P}|\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}(\sigma)
on
n^{\prime}\leq n
jobs where there are
k^{\prime}\leq k
(non-zero deviation) jobs that are constrained to be early.

4.1
Feasibility Check of a Given Subproblem

As a first step toward solving this subproblem, we must test for feasibility; that is, to determine if
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is a feasible set of early jobs. Now, for each ordered subset
of the first
\ell\leq k^{\prime}
jobs in this set, we compute
\Delta_{\ell}=\max_{p\in\U^{\Gamma}}\{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(p_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j)}-\bar{p}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(j)})\}
, which is the maximum total deviation of the
\min\{\ell,\Gamma\}
jobs with the largest deviation in
\{\mathcal{E}_{1}(1),\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{1}(\ell)\}
. (The
\Delta_{\ell}
values can be computed in
O(k^{\prime})=O(k)
time.)
As the EDD rule (recall that
d_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(1)}\leq...\leq d_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(k^{\prime})}
) minimizes the maximal lateness,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is a feasible set of early jobs if and only if for all
\ell\in[k^{\prime}]
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(i)}+\Delta_{\ell}\leq d_{\mathcal{E}_{1}(\ell)}.

(12)

If condition (
12
) holds for
\ell=1,\ldots,k^{\prime}
, we optimally solve the subproblem by finding a maximum cardinality subset
\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{2}
(the set of certain or zero-deviation jobs) that is a feasible being early together with jobs in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. That is,
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
is a feasible set of early jobs if for each
j\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\bar{p}_{i}+\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{-1}(\max(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap[j]))}\leq d_{j}.

the jobs in

5
Summary and Discussion

We investigated a single-machine scheduling problem
with
uncertain job processing times. For each job, there are two potential processing times values: a nominal value and a deviated value
, and adopting the budgeted uncertainty approach,
the total number of deviations
is upper bounded
by a specified robustness parameter,
\Gamma
. Any realization of job processing times with no more than
\Gamma
deviations is
a possible
scenario
of processing times.
We consider a variant of this problem (which we refer to as Variant 1) where a job is considered
early
if it is completed by its due date in all possible scenarios; otherwise, it is considered
tardy
. Our objective is to
determine
a solution that minimizes the number of tardy jobs. This problem is known to be NP-hard, as shown by Bougeret
et al
.
[
11
]
. In
the current
paper,
we
analyze
the problem’s
parameterized complexity using three natural parameters: the robustness parameter; the number of different due dates, and the number of jobs with non-zero deviation. We prove that the problem is W[1]-hard with respect to the robustness parameter, and we complement this result by designing an XP algorithm for it. Regarding the second parameter, we show that the problem remains hard even when the number of distinct due dates is only two, thus ruling out the existence of an XP algorithm (unless P
=
NP). We also prove that when there is only one distinct due date, the problem becomes solvable in polynomial time, and when the number of different due dates is
any
constant greater than one, the problem is solvable in pseudo polynomial time. For the
number of nonzero deviations
, we provide the strongest tractability result– the problem is solvable in FPT time with respect to this parameter.

In a different problem variant, which we refer to as Variant 2, a job can be tardy in one scenario and early in another. We note that our hardness results for Variant 1 extend to Variant 2, while the positive results do not. Consequently, it remains open whether Variant 1 is tractable (i.e., solvable in FPT time) when parameterized by the number of jobs with non-zero deviation. Similarly, its solvability in XP time (or whether it is para-NP-hard) with respect to the robustness parameter is also an open question. Beyond the parameters studied here, future research might consider others, including the number of unique nominal processing times (or processing time deviations) present in the problem instance, and the maximum due date value. Furthermore, examining the parameterized tractability of other scheduling problems with budgeted uncertainty presents another compelling direction. In essence, the confluence of parameterized complexity and robust scheduling remains a largely open and highly interesting area for ongoing research.
