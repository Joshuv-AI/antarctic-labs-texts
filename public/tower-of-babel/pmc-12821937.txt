Ultrasound-enhanced and cell-traction-induced piezoelectric scaffolds for repairing bone defects
Authors: Xing Zhang, Meifei Lian, Juncen Zhou, Ruida Xu, Zhiguang Qiao, Jinwu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The global increase in traumatic accidents and aging population has brought bone tissue injury and disease to the forefront of global health concerns. Traditional treatment methods face significant challenges, emphasizing the urgent need for advanced bone tissue repair techniques. The bioelectric phenomenon in natural bones is essential for bone development and fracture healing. Therefore, developing innovative repair strategies that replicate or enhance this electric field is expected to promote bone tissue repair and integration. Developing new electroactive tissue engineering scaffolds based on electromechanical interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix is essential. This article introduces a piezoelectric scaffold, initially fabricated using melt electro-writing, and then coated with a surface piezoelectric coating using electrospraying (ES) technology. The scaffold exhibits suitable stiffness similar to the extracellular matrix, and the piezoelectric coating can provide necessary electrical stimulation under cell traction. Furthermore, ultrasound technology was utilized to effectively replicate the electrical microenvironment of natural bone repair. The synergy of cell traction-induced electrical stimulation and ultrasound-enhanced scaffold piezoelectricity can substantially enhance bone tissue regeneration and repair. This study introduces a novel method for developing electroactive tissue engineering scaffolds, providing a promising solution for non-load-bearing areas’ bone defects via electrical stimulation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03913-x.

Introduction

Bone tissue defect repair represents a major challenge in clinical medicine and biomaterials research. Traditional treatment methods involving autografts face issues of donor site morbidity and limited supply, while allografts carry risks of immune rejection and disease transmission [1–3]. In recent years, biodegradable polymer-based scaffolds have emerged as a research focus in bone regeneration due to their controllable degradation rates, tunable mechanical properties, and three-dimensional structures resembling natural bone [4–7]. Polycaprolactone (PCL), as an FDA-approved biodegradable material, exhibits a slow degradation profile (typically lasting 1–2 years) that matches the bone regeneration timeline, and its osteointegration capacity can be enhanced through blending modification or surface functionalization [8, 9]. However, conventional fabrication techniques (e.g., fused deposition modeling, phase separation) struggle to achieve simultaneous control over porosity and mechanical strength, and face difficulties in constructing hierarchical structures mimicking bone units [10, 11]. Melt electrospinning writing (MEW), as an advanced micro-nanofabrication technology, enables the production of three-dimensional scaffolds with precisely aligned fibers (5–50 μm), gradient pore distribution, and biomimetic mechanical responses through precise control of melt jetting and collector movement [12–14]. Current PCL scaffolds fabricated via MEW still encounter bottlenecks such as surface bioinertness and weak early-stage osteoblast response, urgently requiring strategies like topological structure optimization or bioactive coating modification to improve their osteoinductive properties.

When a fracture or bone defect occurs in bone tissue, the damaged bone tissue will produce an endogenous electric field. This electric field is caused by the changes of ions and charges released by damaged cells. Endogenous electric fields can guide stem cells to differentiate into osteoblasts, promoting cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation, which accelerates bone tissue regeneration and repair [15–17]. Simulating or enhancing these electric fields may offer new strategies for bone tissue healing.

At present, the electroactive biomaterials for electrical stimulation osteogenesis mainly include conductive biomaterials [18–20], piezoelectric biomaterials [21–24], triboelectric biomaterials [25], and electret biomaterials [26–30]. Conductive biomaterials require an external power source, raising infection risks. Piezoelectric and triboelectric biomaterials necessitate stimulation by physiological activities or external stimuli like ultrasound, magnetic fields, or light, leading to variable electrical signals. Electret biomaterials often need strong electric field polarization prior to implantation. Therefore, combining the interaction between cells and the microenvironment, as well as appropriate physiological activities, or applying suitable external stimuli to develop electroactive scaffolds, is an effective approach to facilitate the use of electrical stimulation for repairing bone defects in clinical treatment.

BaTiO3 is widely used in tissue engineering due to its excellent piezoelectric properties and biocompatibility [31, 32]. At room temperature, barium titanate exhibits a high dielectric constant and significant piezoelectric effect, capable of generating surface charges under mechanical stress, thereby mimicking the natural piezoelectric characteristics of bone tissue and promoting osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and bone defect repair [33, 34]. Its superior chemical stability and non-toxicity ensure safety in biological environments [35, 36]. Additionally, barium titanate demonstrates excellent compatibility with the electrospray process. Through electrospray technology, barium titanate nanoparticles can be uniformly deposited on scaffold surfaces, forming a porous coating structure that maintains the mechanical support function of the scaffold while endowing it with electroactivity, synergistically enhancing bone regeneration efficacy.

This article aims to utilize MEW and ES techniques to create an electroactive scaffold with a surface piezoelectric coating for repairing non-load-bearing areas’ bone defects (Fig. 1). MEW is employed to fabricate PCL scaffolds that mimic the extracellular matrix environment, while ES is used to coat barium titanate nanoparticles on the surface of PCL scaffolds to provide them with piezoelectric properties. The study then explores the electrical stimulation produced by cell traction on the scaffold and enhances the electrical stimulation signal through ultrasound technology to mimic the natural electrical microenvironment of bone tissue. The osteogenic capability of the electroactive scaffold is validated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. This study offers a new method for creating electroactive scaffolds and investigates their potential use in non-load-bearing areas’ bone defect treatment.

Materials and methods

Particle size analysis

The particle size was determined through statistical analysis of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. The SEM measurements were performed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss, Sigma 300, Germany). The powder samples were evenly dispersed onto a silicon wafer and sputter-coated with a thin layer of platinum (~ 5 nm) prior to imaging to enhance conductivity.

The particle size was measured manually using ImageJ software (Version 1.48v, National Institutes of Health, USA). For each sample, at least 3 independent SEM images taken at random locations were analyzed. The scale of each image was calibrated using the built-in scale bar. The diameter (the longest distance between any 2 points along the particle boundary) of well-separated and clearly focused particles was measured. A total of over 100 particles were measured to ensure statistical significance. The average particle size and size distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.

Preparation of scaffolds

The process began with the preparation of PCL (Sigma-Aldrich LLC., USA) scaffolds using MEW (TL Direct Writing 150). The printing parameters were as follows: melting temperature of 65 ℃, air pressure of 0.5 bar, voltage of −6.5 kV, and collector speed of 50 mm/s. BaTiO3 nanoparticles (Average diameter = 100 nm, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) with a mass fraction of 5% were added into ethanol and sonicated for 2 h to create a stable suspension. The next step involved using ES technology to coat the BaTiO3 nanoparticles onto the surface of PCL scaffolds. The corresponding processing parameters were a voltage of 20 kV, flow rate of 1.5 mL/h, needle-to-collector distance of 15 cm and sustained duration of 5 min.

In the scaffold preparation stage, the scaffolds were divided into the PCL scaffold group and the PCL/BaTiO3 scaffold group based on whether BaTiO3 nanoparticles were introduced, and were labeled as P and PB, respectively. A series of characterization and testing experiments were conducted on these 2 groups of scaffolds. Subsequently, during the scaffold application phase for cell culture and differentiation, ultrasonic stimulation was introduced to enhance the piezoelectric self-stimulation effect of cells. At this point, the control group was expanded to 4 groups: PCL scaffold, PCL/BaTiO3 scaffold, PCL/BaTiO3 scaffold with ultrasound, and PCL scaffold with ultrasound. They were denoted as P, PB, PBU, and PU, respectively.

Measurement and characterization scaffolds

Three-dimensional scaffold structures, their elemental analysis scanning and Raman spectrum were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (RISE-MAGNA, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Tensile strength was measured with a tensile tester (Instron-5542, USA). 6 samples, each measuring 25 mm in length, 3 mm in width, and approximately 0.5 mm in thickness, were extracted from the 25 × 25 mm scaffolds. The Vernier caliper was used to measure each sample before testing prior to testing. Samples were tensioned at a loading rate of 1 mm/min until failure. Compression tests were conducted using a Q850 dynamic thermomechanical analyzer (DMA) from China. Through punching holes in a scaffold with a 25 × 25 mm dimension and a height of approximately 5 mm, 6 samples with a diameter of 8 mm were obtained. Before testing, each sample’s thickness was measured with a Vernier caliper. Samples were compressed at a rate of 1 mm/min until maximum deformation or equipment limit was reached. Compression stiffness was determined from the slope of the stable region in the stress-strain curve, averaged over at least 3 samples.

Electrical measurements of scaffolds

According to the protocol in the previous publications [37, 38], the scaffolds were encapsulated for electrical measurements. The output load voltages and shortcircuit currents in different input mechanical conditions (i.e. the custom-made apparatus stretching condition as shown in Figure S2) were characterized with an oscilloscope (LeCroy, Wavesurfer 104MXs-B, USA) and a source meter instrument (Keithley 6514, USA), respectively. In the condition of electrical measurements, the scaffold was act as a singleelectrode nanogenerator. The mechanical energy was generated by the custom-made apparatus. The carbon nanotubes/polydimethylsiloxane (CNT/PDMS) layer was act as the electrode, connecting with one pole of electrical measurement device. The other pole was grounded. Additionally, the charge density is obtained by integrating the current.

In vitro degradation study

The in vitro degradation behavior of the scaffolds was investigated by immersing them in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH = 7.4, Biosharp, China) at 37 ℃ for up to 8 weeks. The ratio of PBS volume to scaffold mass was maintained at 50:1 (mL: mg). The PBS solution was refreshed every 48 h to maintain a constant ion concentration. At predetermined time intervals (2, 4, 6 and 8 weeks), the scaffolds (n = 4 for each time point) were removed from the solution, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove soluble salts, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 ℃ for 24 h until a constant weight was achieved.

Electric field simulation

Utilize COMSOL Multiphysics (version 6.0, COMSOL Inc., USA) to simulate and analyze electrical stimulation induced by cell traction on scaffolds. In the simulation, taking a single fiber as an example, it is assumed that the immersion depth of barium titanate nanoparticles is its radius. The traction force range of cells is 0.1 ~ 10 nN [39–42]. Based on the simulation results, analyze the interaction between the scaffold and cells and the generated electrical stimulation signals. Next, under ultrasound, the deformation of the scaffolds and the corresponding piezoelectric potential were simulated. Determine the appropriate ultrasound frequency through simulation.

Viability and adhesion of cells

Cell extraction and culture

Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stromal Cells (BMSCs) were obtained from the femurs and tibias of 3-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (Slac Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Additional experiments were conducted on cells at passages 3–5, cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA)with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS, Gibco, USA) at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2.

Cell viability assessment

Cell compatibility of the scaffolds was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) viability assay, following the instructions. Tissue culture plates (TCPs) were used as controls. BMSCs (2 × 104 cells/well) were cultured on scaffolds in 24-well plates for 1, 4, and 7 days, with cell viability assessed via optical density at 450 nm (OD450).

Cell adhesion assessment

BMSC seeds (2 × 104 per well) were cultured on scaffolds in 24-well plates for 1, 4, and 7 days, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde to assess cell adhesion morphology. Nuclei were stained with 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 28718-90−3, Sigma, USA, 1:1000 dilutions), and F-actin with Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin (A12379, Invitrogen, USA, 1:400 dilutions). Fluorescence graphics were acquired with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Germany).

Osteogenic differentiation in vitro

A total of 2 × 104 BMSCs were seeded onto scaffolds in each well of 24-well plates. When the cell fusion reached 70%, the complete medium in the wells was carefully aspirated, followed by the addition of Cyagen Biosciences, Inc.’s osteogenic induction medium. Fresh medium was replaced every 3 days. During the incubation period (4 and 7 days), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to monitor morphological changes and growth status of the cells. Following the ALP detection kit protocol (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), ALP activity was semi-quantitatively analyzed. Each sample’s protein content was normalized using the BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The BMSCs on scaffolds were stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS) solution (Cyagen Biosciences, Inc., USA) after 21 days of culture. Calcium nodule was semi-quantitatively measured at OD 590 after staining using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China). Each sample’s ARS activity was normalized to total protein content and reported as OD/mg protein.

Immune regulation

Inoculate RAW 264.7 cells into a 24 well plate at a density of 200,000 cells per well. After 24 h of cultivation, add ultrapure LPS from Escherichia coli 0111:B4 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to a final concentration of 500 ng/mL to the culture medium to stimulate macrophages for 4 h. An equal volume of PBS vehicle was added to the control groups. Subsequently, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and incubated in serum-free Dulbecco modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 50% (v/v) CM. RAW 264.7 cells stimulated by LPS were seeded on various scaffolds for corresponding culture. After 3 days, the phenotype of the cells on the scaffolds was characterized, and BMSCs were cultured in conditioned medium containing macrophage secretions. After 4 days, the osteogenic marker ALP was detected in the bottom cells of the dish.

The gene expression of iNOS (ab178945, Abcam, USA, 1:1000 dilutions), IL-1β (ab216995, Abcam, USA, 1:1000 dilutions), IL-6 (ab233706, Abcam, USA, 1:1000 dilutions), CD206 (AF2535, R&D Systems, USA, 1:1000 dilutions), Arg-1 (IC5868P, R&D Systems, USA, 1:1000 dilutions) and IL-10 (ab9969, Abcam, USA, 1:1000 dilutions) was detected using RT-PCR method, and the primer sequences used were listed in Table S1. More specifically, after 14 days of culture, cells were lysed, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A, Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS (AG11701, AGbio, China) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each 20 µL reaction system contained 10 µL of SYBR Green Premix, 0.8 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6.4 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 30 s; followed by a melt curve analysis. The relative gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. All experiments were performed with three technical replicates and three independent biological replicates.

Observe macrophage polarization through immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, the samples were double-stained with antibodies of iNOS (Alexa Fluor®555, ab209594, Abcam, USA, 1:500 dilutions) and CD206 (Alexa Fluor® 488, ab195191, Abcam, USA, 1:500 dilutions), with the nuclei counterstained with DAPI. The fluorescent images were detected using CLSM.

In vivo assessment of bone regeneration

To evaluate the bone regeneration efficacy of scaffolds using a rat critical size skull defect model, this study followed the SD rat treatment protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. The specific procedures were as follows: Rats were fasted for 6 h and allowed free access to water before surgery. The sterile surgical procedure involved anesthetizing rats with isoflurane inhalation, with anesthesia depth confirmed by the disappearance of the toe pinch reflex. After anesthesia, hair was shaved from the cranium area and disinfected 3 times using iodine tincture and 75% alcohol. Five-millimeter full-thickness defects were drilled bilaterally on the skulls of 24 8-week-old male rats. The right bone defect was filled with P and PB scaffolds respectively, while the left side was not treated. The periosteum and skin were then sutured successively. Postoperative rats were placed on warm bedding until complete recovery, with analgesic administration provided for all animals. To prevent infections, intraperitoneal antibiotic injections were administered for 3 days continuously. Daily monitoring included assessment of mental status, activity levels, feeding/drinking patterns, body weight, and wound healing. All animals were housed in standard SPF-grade animal facilities (temperature 20–26 ℃, daily temperature variation ≤ 4 ℃, relative humidity 40%−70%,12-hour light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water. About 8 weeks after the implantation, the rats were euthanized and samples were taken for follow-up analysis. No intraoperative deaths, postoperative deaths within 24 h, severe complications, or significant distress/weight loss occurred during the study. Therefore, no animals were excluded, and all included rats successfully completed their designated observation periods. In addition to the anesthetics, analgesics and prophylactic antibiotics used during the operation, the animals were not given any other medication.

A micro-CT imaging system (INVISCAN, France) scanned bone reparation situation of each sample, and Mimics (Materialize Inc., Belgium) reconstructed three-dimensional images of the regeneration. Reconstructed images were analyzed with the SkyScan CT-Analyser to quantify bone morphological parameters, including bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV). Following micro-CT scanning, the skull samples underwent fixation in buffered formalin, decalcification with EDTA (Bost Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), paraffin embedding, and sectioning for Masson staining. The graphics were obtained utilizing an Olympus IX71 optical microscope.

Ultrasound application

The ultrasonic device used in this study was self-made, consisting of an ultrasonic generator (M048N, Kemo Electronic, Germany) with a frequency range of 2–40 kHz and 4 mini piezoelectric tweeters (P5123, Kemo Electronic, Germany) with a frequency range of 2.5–45 kHz. The schematic diagram of the custom-built ultrasound setup is illustrated in Figure S3. In vitro experiments involving cell culture, osteogenic differentiation, and immune regulation, culture dishes loaded with scaffolds were placed on top of speakers installed on a flat surface, using an ultrasound frequency of 40 kHz once a day for 30 min each time. In the in vivo experiment, the rats were anesthetized and the scalp hair at the implantation site was gently removed before each treatment. Apply a large amount of sterile ultrasound coupled gel on shaved skin to ensure effective sound energy transmission. Use a fixed bracket to place the transducer vertically and gently in contact with the scalp to maintain consistent application distance and position on all animals. To avoid tissue overheating, the ultrasound frequency is maintained at 40 kHz and treatment is performed twice a day for 15 min each time.

Statistical analysis

Measurements were conducted at least 3 times, with quantitative data presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was assessed using paired-sample t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test in SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago). Statistical significance was defined as a P value (***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05).

Results

Fabrication and characterization of scaffolds

First, PCL scaffolds with a 400 μm spacing were created using MEW printing. Then, the barrel containing barium titanate dispersion was switched, and barium titanate nanoparticles were sprayed onto the surface of the PCL scaffolds using ES technology to create PCL/BaTiO3 composite scaffolds with piezoelectric surfaces. According to the SEM image (Fig. 2A) and particle size statistical analysis (Fig. 2B) of BaTiO3 nanoparticles, it can be seen that the nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed and have an average particle size of about 100 nm. Figure 2C and D show the EDS scan spectra and element distribution of the composite scaffold. It can be observed that BaTiO3 nanoparticles are uniformly distributed on the surface of the scaffold. Additionally, the Raman spectroscopy image of the scaffolds is shown in Figure S1, demonstrating that BaTiO3 nanoparticles have been successfully coated on the scaffold surface. The scaffolds were examined using SEM (see Fig. 2E), revealing that the P scaffolds had a smooth surface, whereas the PB scaffolds became rough with visible coated particles. This means that the PB scaffolds are not only conducive to cell adhesion but also has piezoelectric coatings on its surfaces. Subsequently, the piezoelectric properties of the scaffold were tested (Fig. 2F-H), indicating that the introduction of nanoparticles resulted in the composite scaffold having good piezoelectric properties.

A series of mechanical tests in Fig. 3B-E indicate that the scaffolds did not undergo any performance changes suddenly during the degradation process, and the structure of the scaffolds are stable. Additionally, it was observed that the incorporation of barium titanate nanoparticles enhances the mechanical properties of the composite scaffold. This is because the BaTiO3, acting as an inorganic filler, impedes the migration of the PCL molecular chain. Figure 3F and G indicate that as the scaffold degrades, although the surface coating of the scaffold gradually decreases, it can still be maintained for more than 8 weeks.

Although the tensile Young’s modulus (~ 16 MPa) and compressive elastic modulus (~ 32 kPa) of the PB composite scaffold prepared in this study are significantly lower than those of natural human bone (0.2–2.2 GPa for cancellous bone and 3–30 GPa for dense bone) [43], its mechanical properties are designed for non load bearing bone (such as skull) repair applications. Although the mechanical properties of this scaffold are lower than this value, its high porosity and flexibility are beneficial for surgical implantation and adaptation to defect shapes, and provide space for cell migration and tissue growth. More importantly, this mechanical environment helps to avoid the “stress shielding” effect and is closer to the soft tissue mechanical environment in the early stage of fracture healing [44]. Therefore, the mechanical properties of the scaffold meet the basic requirements for mechanical support and biological activity in the early stage of skull defect repair. Its design focuses on providing bioelectric stimulation and guiding bone regeneration, rather than direct mechanical bearing.

Simulation of piezoelectric scaffolds

The literature reveals that the cell traction range is 0.1 to 10 nN [39–42]. To explore the electrical stimulation signal resulting from cell-scaffold interaction, COMSOL simulation was used. Figure 4A illustrates the adhesion diagram of BMSCs on the scaffolds and the interaction between cells and nanoparticles on the scaffold surface, serving as the foundation for the simulation model. It is known that under the same constraints, the greatest force can produce the largest deformation, which in turn can generate the maximum piezoelectric electric field in the same direction. Therefore, to investigate the maximum piezoelectric electric field that can be triggered by cellular traction, the simulation first takes the average particle size of 100 nm and the maximum traction force of 10 nN as an example for analysis. The simulation results in Fig. 4C-E showcase stress, deformation, potential, and potential distribution when the particle size is 100 nm and the traction force is 10 nN. According to the analysis of simulation results, Fig. 4F illustrates the variation of the maximum piezoelectric potential with the particle size of nanoparticles. It demonstrates that the maximum variation range of piezoelectric potential is approximately 20 µV to 5 mV in response to changes in cell traction. Additionally, Fig. 4G presents the simulation curve of the maximum piezoelectric potential with the change of cell traction.

From the aforementioned series of simulation results, it can be observed that under the action of cellular traction forces, the maximum potential generated by BaTiO3 nanoparticles does not exceed 5 mV. To replicate the electrical microenvironment of natural bone tissue and achieve a potential in the range of −60 to −100 mV [27, 45], ultrasonic external excitation was utilized. The piezoelectric effect of ultrasound triggered scaffolds was simulated to determine the potential generated by ultrasound (The schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 4B). Figure 4H-I displays the deformation and potential of the scaffold under the action of ultrasound, revealing that the PBU scaffold can generate a maximum potential of −75 mV under the action of 40 kHz ultrasound. Figure 4J shows the curve of maximum scaffold deformation and maximum piezoelectric potential with ultrasonic frequency.

To systematically determine the optimal ultrasonic frequency for subsequent biological experiments, the scaffold’s mechanical deformation and its biological impact across a range of frequencies (20–40 kHz) were evaluated. As quantified in Fig. 4J, the scaffold exhibited the greatest deformation amplitude at lower frequencies (20 and 30 kHz), which correspondingly generated the highest piezoelectric output. However, when assessed cell viability on the scaffolds under these stimulation conditions (Figure S4), it is observed a significant decrease in cell activity at 20 and 30 kHz. This is likely attributable to the excessive mechanical deformation at these frequencies, which compromised stable cell adhesion and proliferation.

In contrast, at 40 kHz, the scaffold maintained a substantial level of deformation and piezoelectric response while simultaneously supporting excellent cell viability and spreading (Figure S4). Therefore, 40 kHz was identified as the optimal frequency that strikes a balance between effective piezoelectric stimulation and minimal adverse mechanical effects on cells. This frequency was consequently selected for all subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies.

As for the duration of ultrasonic treatment (30 min per day) was selected based on established protocols in the field of ultrasound-assisted bone regeneration and the mechanistic considerations. This specific duration is frequently reported in literature as an effective regimen for promoting osteogenic differentiation without inducing cellular damage [46, 47]. It is reasoned that a 30-minute exposure provides a substantial window for sustained piezoelectric stimulation, likely covering critical periods of cellular activity and signaling pathway activation, while remaining within a safe timeframe to minimize potential off-target thermal or mechanical effects [48]. This balanced approach ensures the long-term biocompatibility and efficacy of the daily treatment regimen throughout the experimental period.

Figure 5A illustrates the increase in cell count over time. The composite scaffolds exhibited good cell compatibility, as indicated by no significant difference in viability on the first day. BMSCs proliferated on the composite scaffolds over 4 and 7 days, with a significant increase on the PBU scaffold. The results suggest that ultrasound-enhanced and cell-traction-induced electrical stimulation of the piezoelectric scaffolds can significantly promote cell proliferation. A fluorescence analysis of the cytoskeleton of BMSCs was performed to ensure experimental accuracy. Cytoskeleton staining experiment was employed to assess BMSC adhesion morphology on different scaffolds (Fig. 5B and C). As a result, cells adhered and proliferated more efficiently on the piezoelectric scaffolds, especially PBU scaffolds.

Osteogenic effect of scaffolds in vitro

This study aimed to examine the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts on piezoelectric scaffolds. ALP staining results on day 4 and day 7 (Fig. 6A and B) show that PB and PBU facilitate osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. PBU scaffolds exhibited significantly higher ALP activity compared to the other groups. The PBU group exhibited a significant increase in calcium nodules compared to others (Fig. 6C). The corresponding statistical results of ALP and ARS staining images are shown in Fig. 6D-F. Semi-quantitative analysis of ALP and ARS (Fig. 6G and H) revealed that the PBU scaffold exhibited significantly increased osteogenic differentiation compared to the other groups. As a result, this study confirms that ultrasound-enhanced and cell-traction-induced electrical stimulation created a favorable microenvironment, significantly promoting osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs.

The classic biomarkers CD206, Arg-1, and IL-10 are widely used to define M2 polarization, while iNOS, IL-1β, and IL-6 are often used as characteristic markers of M1. This study used immunofluorescence staining technology to label RAW 264.7 cells on the surface of the scaffold, exploring the regulatory effect of electrical stimulation on the immune microenvironment of the composite scaffold (as shown in Fig. 7B and C). The results showed that the PBU group composite scaffold significantly promoted the transformation of M1 phenotype to M2 phenotype in the cells on the surface of the scaffold and the bottom of dish. Figure 7D shows the fluorescence intensity statistics of immunofluorescence staining results, with the P group serving as the standardized reference value. The results indicate a significant increase in CD206 marker expression levels in the PBU group. Subsequent gene expression experiments further validated the reliability of immunofluorescence staining results. As shown in Fig. 7E, RT-PCR analysis results showed that compared with the positive control group and the other 2 groups, the M1 polarization marker genes (iNOS, IL-1β, and IL-6) in the PBU group were significantly down regulated. The M2 polarization marker genes (CD206, Arg-1, and IL-10) were significantly upregulated. These results indicate that the paracrine factors secreted by RAW 264.7 cells cultured on PBU composite scaffolds can affect immune regulatory function and promote macrophage transformation from M1 phenotype to M2 phenotype.

Figure 7F shows the ALP staining results of BMSCs mediated by conditioned medium after 4 days of cultivation. Compared with the positive control group and the other 2 groups, the PBU group significantly enhanced the early osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs in the immune microenvironment mediated by electrical stimulation. In summary, both direct and indirect evaluation results indicate that the PBU group composite scaffold can significantly promote the secretion of immune regulatory factors and induce the phenotype transformation of RAW 264.7 cells from M1 to M2, creating a favorable microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration.

Osteogenic effect of scaffolds in vivo

By providing stable, long-term electrical stimulation to BMSCs, piezoelectric scaffolds enhance cell behavior and promote osteogenic differentiation. This section assesses the in vivo bone regeneration capabilities of P, PB, PBU, and PU composite scaffolds implanted in critical-size rat skull defects. 8 weeks post-operation, skull specimens were collected for micro-CT and histological analysis.

The micro-CT images in Fig. 8A shows the PBU group demonstrated significantly enhanced bone regeneration relative to other groups. The defect area showed a remarkable amount of new bone tissue, and after just 8 weeks, the defect was almost completely healed using the PBU scaffold. PB scaffold also has obvious effect of repairing bone defect. However, only a few new bones were detected in the other 2 groups. After 8 weeks, most defect areas remained empty, indicating suboptimal bone healing. The PBU composite scaffold significantly increased BMD and BV/TV in newly formed bone compared to P, PB, and PU scaffolds (Fig. 8B and C). It was confirmed that PBU scaffolds promoted bone growth well.

In tissue sections, the scaffold preserved its structural integrity during implantation, enhancing cell-scaffold interactions. By maintaining a continuous charged microenvironment, the piezoelectric scaffold effectively promotes bone formation and regeneration. This conducive environment activates osteoblasts, promoting biomineralization, osteogenesis, and facilitating ion and protein adsorption.

Discussion

This study successfully fabricated a novel PCL/BaTiO3 piezoelectric scaffold through the innovative integration of MEW and ES. This study demonstrated that the synergistic effect between cell traction-induced electrical signals and ultrasound-enhanced piezoelectric potential significantly promoted BMSCs proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and ultimately, bone regeneration in a cranial defect model.

The concept of a “dual-stimulation strategy” presented here addresses a critical limitation in the field of bioactive scaffolds. Most existing piezoelectric biomaterials rely solely on unpredictable physiological movements for activation, leading to highly variable and often suboptimal electrical stimulation [38, 49]. Conversely, conductive polymers often require cumbersome external wiring and power sources, which pose significant risks for infection and limit clinical translation [18, 20]. This study utilized cell traction as an autonomous, endogenous stimulus and ultrasound as a controllable, on-demand exogenous boost, represents a paradigm shift. This is particularly advantageous for healing defects in areas with minimal natural mechanical loading, such as the cranium. The finding that ultrasound enhancement amplified the osteogenic outcome aligns with the works [50, 51], who showed that ultrasound could potentiate the therapeutic effect of piezoelectric materials. However, this study is the first to combine these 2 specific modes of stimulation in a single, wireless platform.

The decision to employ an electrospraying coating method for BaTiO3 nanoparticle deposition, rather than bulk incorporation, was validated by both biological and mechanical insights. Firstly, from a biological perspective, cell traction forces are surface phenomena. Therefore, nanoparticles embedded deep within a scaffold’s core are mechanically shielded from cellular forces and contribute negligibly to charge generation. The coating strategy ensures that a maximal number of BaTiO3 nanoparticles are positioned on the fiber surfaces, directly in the path of cell-derived mechanical stimuli, thereby maximizing the efficiency of energy conversion. Secondly, from a biomechanical perspective, when a scaffold is subjected to bending deformation (e.g., from ultrasound pressure waves or surrounding tissue contractility), the maximum stress is concentrated on the top and bottom surfaces, while the neutral axis experiences zero stress. Therefore, by concentrating the piezoelectric material on the surface via coating, this study precisely positioned it in the region of highest stress concentration. This design principle explains why the scaffold generated a stronger piezoelectric signal in response to ultrasound compared to scaffolds with homogenously distributed nanoparticles, as reported [52, 53]. This discrepancy in performance can be attributed directly to the superior material placement strategy.

The enhanced bone regeneration observed in vivo underscores the clinical potential of the wireless, stimulatory scaffold. The quality and volume of new bone formed in the experimental group appeared superior to that in the control and pristine PCL groups, a finding consistent with the established role of electrophysiological cues in promoting osteogenesis. However, a limitation of this study is not decouple the individual contributions of the topographical cues from the MEW scaffold and the piezoelectric effect from the BaTiO3 coating. Future work should involve control groups with non-piezoelectric coating materials to isolate these effects. Furthermore, while BaTiO3 is considered biocompatible, its long-term biodistribution and clearance profile, especially in nanoparticle form, require further investigation in more complex physiological models. Future studies will also focus on optimizing the ultrasound parameters (e.g., intensity, frequency, duty cycle) to establish a definitive dose-response relationship for bone repair, moving beyond the single set of parameters used in this proof-of-concept study.

Conclusion

In this study, innovative MEW and ES technologies were employed to fabricate a scaffold with a piezoelectric coating on the surface. This distinctive coating produces 20 µV to 5 mV electrical stimulation signals in response to cell traction, thereby enhancing cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation. Additionally, the introduction of ultrasonic excitation can establish an electrical microenvironment within the scaffolds that closely resembles natural bone tissue. Electrical stimulation from cell traction and ultrasound-guided scaffold piezoelectric potential significantly enhance cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation. This study’s innovative piezoelectric scaffold design offers a new approach to developing intelligent electroactive scaffolds, showing potential for addressing bone defects.

In summary, this study pioneered the development of an intelligent electroactive scaffold that accurately reproduces the electric field characteristics of the bone microenvironment by integrating endogenous (cell traction) and exogenous (ultrasound) biophysical signals. This technology has laid a solid foundation for the development of the next generation of “self stimulating” implants for complex bone defects.
