Treatment Selection for Major Depressive Disorder in Veterans Health Administration Outpatients
Authors: Mihaela Aslan, Daniel S. Tylee, Peijun Chen, Yi Dai, Yuli Li, Pradeep Mutalik, Lei Yan, Xiayuan Huang, Renato Polimanti, Zuoheng Wang, Somaia Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHas treatment selection changed over time among outpatients in the Veterans Health Administration with major depressive disorder (MDD)?FindingsIn this cohort study including more than 1.58 million veterans who received outpatient MDD care between fiscal years 2011 and 2025, decreasing proportions of veterans received psychotherapy, antidepressants (ADs), combination therapy, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and benzodiazepines, whereas increasing proportions of veterans received ADs without psychotherapy, psychotherapy without ADs, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and no recommended treatment.MeaningThese findings suggest that most veterans with MDD received recommended treatment, but there was an increasing trend in the proportion who did not, which requires further investigation.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is common and causes significant distress and disability. The impact of depressive disorders has grown over the past 2 decades, with 2020 estimates totaling more than $326 billion in costs of care and lost productivity in the United States alone.1 The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) is the largest integrated health system in the United States, providing mental health care for an estimated 1.7 million veterans in 2018.2,3 Recommendations for initial treatment include psychotherapy, antidepressant (AD) medication, and combination therapy (ie, concurrent psychotherapy and AD).4,5,6 Evidence-based pharmacotherapies include multiple classes of medication with distinct mechanisms of action. Individual differences in symptomatic response to the initial AD pharmacotherapy vary.7,8,9 When the response to initial therapy is inadequate, guidelines for subsequent treatment describe multiple options, including adding psychotherapy, augmenting by adding a second AD, or switching to another AD. The Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) trial suggested that randomization among different AD selections resulted in nonsignificant differences in response and remission rates with the likelihood of response or remission diminishing in each subsequent treatment trial.8,9,10 The Veterans Affairs (VA) Augmentation and Switching Treatments for Improving Depression (VAST-D) trial included veterans with inadequate response to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and identified a modest advantage favoring augmentation with aripiprazole, as compared with switching to bupropion monotherapy.11

Treatment selection may be informed by service and treatment option availability, symptom severity, history of prior treatment and response, demographic characteristics, comorbid psychiatric and medical conditions, concurrent medications, avoidance of specific adverse effects, and patient preferences.12,13,14,15,16,17 The present study extends on predecessors by characterizing psychotherapy and psychotropic medication use among a nationwide cohort of veterans who received outpatient care for MDD through the VHA between October 1, 2010 (beginning of fiscal year [FY] 11), and September 30, 2025 (end of FY25), and by describing changing veteran demography and demographic differences in treatment trends.14,18,19,20,21,22

Methods

Data Source and Patient Sample

This cohort study was approved by the institutional review board of the VA Connecticut Healthcare System without deidentification of records via expedited review and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act authorization waiver. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.23 We extracted structured data pertaining to outpatient encounters, psychotherapy procedure codes, and prescription records from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse. The flowchart describes our stepwise approach to sample selection and quality control (Figure 1). We selected adult veterans (aged between 18 and 109 years) with at least 2 encounters with MDD diagnosis codes on distinct dates and at least 1 occurring between FY11 and FY25. MDD-positive diagnosis subcodes were defined based on International Classification of Disease, Ninth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) subcodes 296.20 through 296.36 and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) subcodes F32.0 through F32.5, F32.9, F33.0 through F33.42, and F33.9, and confirmed through a clinician narrative diagnosis from clinical notes. We excluded individuals with any history of diagnosis codes related to bipolar or schizophrenia spectrum illness or non-MDD psychosis to eliminate diagnostic ambiguity, such as the possibility that a future severe mental illness diagnosis indicates an initial misclassification. Age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, and region were extracted from health records based on self-report; individuals with missing data were encoded as an unknown subgroup and retained for analyses. Additional details related to sample selection and quality control are reported in the eMethods in Supplement 1 and all relevant ICD subcodes are described in eTable 3A in Supplement 2.

Treatment Definitions

Treatment was quantified only among veterans who were active outpatient VHA service users for each FY based on the presence of 1 or more encounters with MDD-positive subcodes. Veterans were classified as engaged in psychotherapy based on the presence of at least 1 outpatient psychotherapy encounter within a given FY. Additional levels of engagement of at least 4 and 8 psychotherapy encounters within a given FY were also considered (eTable 5 in Supplement 2). We extracted outpatient prescription records for a range of oral psychotropic medications and refer to this set of medications as psychotropics (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). We defined ADs as inclusive of SSRIs, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitors (NDRIs), other ADs (eg, mirtazapine and trazodone), tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs). Non-AD psychotropics included second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs), mood stabilizers, benzodiazepines, psychostimulants, and thyroid hormone supplementation. Prescriptions were quantified as a greater than 7-day supply for any AD treatment. Additional thresholds for number of days supplied each FY reflecting specific AD trials of greater than 28, greater than 56, and greater than 84 days were also considered (eTable 5 in Supplement 2).

Statistical Analysis

For each FY, we quantified proportions of demographic subgroups, including age groups (18-40, 41-60, and >60 years), biological sex (female or male), race (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, White, and unknown), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, and Unknown), and region (Continental, Midwest, North Atlantic, Pacific, and Southeast) as well as proportions related to treatment selections, including psychotherapy, medication class, and specific medications. We also repeated analyses for newly initiated treatment (with no record of that treatment in the past 180 days). To account for the within-participant correlation of repeated measurements on the same individuals over time, we used generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust SEs to assess temporal trends. All tests were 2-tailed, with statistical significance set at α = .05.24 The analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Between FY11 and FY25, 1 583 834 veterans with MDD met study criteria (Figure 1). The study group consisted mostly of men (1 319 301 [83.4%]). There were 23 287 (1.5%) Asian, 341 846 (21.6%) Black or African American, and 1 065 904 (67.3%) White veterans. The mean (SD) age when participants entered the cohort was 51.4 (16.6) years. Annual trends in proportions receiving psychotherapy, ADs, and the treatment regimens formed by specific combinations are shown in the Table. Findings pertaining to the broader category of psychotropics are described in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. On average, across FYs, AD treatment was more common than psychotherapy (mean [SD], 79.9% [0.11] vs 62.4% [0.33]) and the combination of psychotherapy with AD (52.6% [0.46]). Decreasing proportions engaged in psychotherapy over time (−9.8 percentage points overall), with a biphasic trend, decreasing from 131 614 of 182 208 (72.2%) in FY11 to 355 681 of 655 690 (54.2%) in FY21 (−18.0–percentage point change), then increasing to 413 129 of 661 576 (62.4%) in FY25 (8.2–percentage point change). AD treatment decreased monotonically (−8.4% change overall) from 156 307 (85.8%) in FY11 to 437 012 of 559 989 (78.0%) in FY18, then remained stable. On average, ADs were less common among young and Pacific-region veterans, and more common among White veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). There was a larger total decrease within the biphasic trend for the proportion receiving psychotherapy with ADs (−14.6 percentage points overall), specifically decreasing from 117 936 (64.7%) to 286 224 (43.7%) (FY11 to FY21; −21.0–percentage point change), then increasing to 331 190 (50.1%) (FY25; 6.4–percentage point change). Mean rates of combination psychotherapy with ADs were lower and decreased more among older veterans and male veterans, and rates were slightly lower among White veterans and those with unknown race, while combination therapy rates were higher and decreased more over time among Hispanic/Latino veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). AD without psychotherapy was the second most common regimen (mean [SD], 27.3% [0.13]), and despite the overall decrease in AD use, the proportion receiving ADs without psychotherapy increased (6.3–percentage point change overall) with a biphasic trend, increasing from 38 371 (21.1%) to 215 218 (32.8%) in FY11 to FY21 (11.7–percentage point change) and subsequently decreasing to 181 037 (27.4%) in FY25 (−5.4–percentage point change). Psychotherapy without ADs was a less common regimen (mean [SD], 9.8% [0.03]), and its proportion increased from 13 678 (7.5%) to 81 939 (12.4%) (FYs 11-25; 4.9–percentage point change overall), despite decreasing psychotherapy use overall. Psychotherapy engagement was less common and decreased more over time among older veterans; this pattern predated the COVID-19 pandemic, which temporarily exacerbated age-related differences, but older veterans recovered to prepandemic levels of psychotherapy engagement (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). Psychotherapy was more common and decreased less over time among female veterans, and it was more common among Hispanic and Latino veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). Receiving neither AD nor psychotherapy was also less common (mean [SD], 10.3% [0.05]), with a biphasic trend (3.5–percentage point change overall), increasing from 12 223 (6.7%) in FY11 to 84 791 (12.9%) in FY21 followed by a decrease to 67 410 (10.2%) in FY25. GEEs revealed statistically significant linear and quadratic temporal trends as well as a joint effect of time on proportions. All Bonferroni-corrected P values were statistically significant (all P < .001) (eTable 5 in Supplement 2).

Discussion

We characterized treatment trends over the last 15 years within a nationwide cohort of approximately 1.6 million veterans receiving VHA outpatient care for MDD. For treatment-naive veterans or those with prior response to ADs, VA guidelines recommend a patient-centered, evidence-based approach that may include psychotherapy, AD, or their combination. We observed decreasing trends in psychotherapy (with or without AD) from 72.2% to 62.4%, AD (with or without psychotherapy) from 85.8% to 79.9%, and the combination of psychotherapy with AD from 64.7% to 52.6%. Encouragingly, most veterans received at least 1 recommended form of treatment for MDD, although this proportion decreased from 93.3% to 89.8% (FY11 to FY25), which translated to approximately 67 480 veterans who did not receive recommended outpatient treatment in FY25.

The combination of psychotherapy with AD remained the most common recommended treatment regimen despite decreasing use over time (mean, 52.6%; −14.6–percentage point change overall); this was only partially offset by growth in alternative recommended regimens, including AD without psychotherapy (mean, 27.3%; 6.3–percentage point change overall) and psychotherapy without AD (mean, 9.8%; 4.9–percentage point change overall). Since combination therapy is recommended for more severe or persistent MDD,4,5,6 one hypothesis is that the relative proportion of veterans with severe illness decreased over time (eTable 4 in Supplement 2)25; however, we did not assess MDD severity, which is a limitation that future work will address. Mean rates of combination therapy varied by age, sex, and race and ethnicity, with lower and decreasing rates more among older and male veterans, lower rates among White veterans and those with unknown race, and higher and decreasing rates among Hispanic/Latino veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). Those disproportional utilization rates of combination treatment may reflect difference in severity of MDD, convenience, patients’ preference, and other factors among different social demographic groups.

Psychotherapy engagement was less common and decreased more (−9.8–percentage point change overall) compared with ADs, with decreases predating the COVID-19 pandemic (−6.2–percentage point change in FY11 to FY15), during the ICD-10-CM transition (−7.6–percentage point change in FY15 to FY16), and during the pandemic and related transition to telehealth (−6.2–percentage point change in FY19 to FY21). Unlike AD use, postpandemic psychotherapy rates increased (8.2–percentage point change, FY21 to FY25). We observed differences in psychotherapy engagement by age, sex, and race and ethnicity. These age- and sex-related differences appear consistent with those observed in the US population.26,27,28,29 The disproportional rates in different social demographic groups may reflect differences in severity of MDD, availability of mental health counselors, convenience, patients’ preference, and other factors.

Despite decreasing use of ADs from FY11 to FY21 (−9.3–percentage point change), they remained the more common recommended treatment modality, and their use varied little with the onset and offset of the COVID-19 pandemic (−8.4 percentage points total change). There were some differences in AD receipt by age and race, with fewer young and Pacific-region veterans and more White veterans receiving ADs (eDiscussion in Supplement 1 and eTable 4 in Supplement 2). AD without psychotherapy was the second most common recommended treatment regimen, and it grew more common over time (mean, 27.3%; 6.3–percentage point change overall); additionally, it was the only regimen that grew more common from FY15 to FY16, during the ICD-10-CM transition. Interestingly, the proportions of new treatment were reversed, such that a higher proportion received psychotherapy (mean, 39.2%) than AD (mean, 27.2%) (eDiscussion and eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Male and older veterans had a more pronounced decrease in the use of SSRIs (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). Among SSRIs, we observed decreasing use of citalopram and paroxetine, growth in escitalopram (added to formulary in FY12), and stable use of fluoxetine and sertraline. While SSRIs (except paroxetine) are considered first-line agents for geriatric depression, evidence supporting their efficacy and favorable safety profile in this population has been mixed.30 Conversely, SNRI use increased over time (6.7–percentage point change overall) and was more common among middle-aged, female, White, and non-Hispanic/Latino veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). Age and sex differences in the use of SNRIs may be related to the conditions that disproportionately present within those demographics (eg, chronic pain conditions, symptoms of menopause, or stress urinary incontinence).31,32,33 Growth of duloxetine coincided with its formulary introduction (FY13) and with decreases in venlafaxine use. Use of other ADs (ie, trazodone and mirtazapine) also decreased over time, except among older veterans (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). These age-based observations could be associated with treatment of somatic symptoms (eg, insomnia and low appetite or weight loss) and/or a desire to avoid AD classes with higher risk of orthostasis and related falls (eg, SSRIs, TCAs, and SNRIs), which disproportionately affect older individuals.34 Future studies should distinguish the use of antidepressant therapeutic doses for the treatment of depression from the use of sleep-promoting dosages of antidepressant for insomnia and better define AD trials reaching a therapeutic dose for 8 weeks.

Use of SGAs demonstrated a biphasic trend, and we observed age, sex, and race and ethnicity differences during the study period. Quetiapine use decreased from 6.7% to 4.6% (FY11 to FY19) then increased modestly to 5.0%, while aripiprazole decreased from 4.8% to 2.5% (FY11 to FY18), then increased subsequently to 4.0%; the latter may have been influenced by the publication of the VAST-D trial in 2017, which identified a modest advantage of augmentation with aripiprazole compared with switching to bupropion in veterans with inadequate SSRI response.11 We found that benzodiazepine use decreased for all demographic groups over time, which reflects increased recognition of risks for misuse, tolerance, dependence, and adverse events amidst the opioid crisis as well as negative long-term effects in older adults. Although benzodiazepine use was more common among some subgroups, these rates declined as well (eTable 4 in the Supplement).35,36,37 Benzodiazepines, mood stabilizers, and stimulants were less common among Black or African American individuals.38,39 Observed trends in treatment selection may reflect evolving preferences among clinicians and patients. Qualitative studies may clarify veteran and clinician factors that are associated with race and ethnicity–based differences in use of other ADs, SGAs, benzodiazepines, and stimulants.

Limitations

The study was limited in several respects. Our approach did not characterize MDD severity, psychotherapy modality and adequacy, and the duration and dosage of medication. We did not distinguish between face-to-face and telehealth encounters and did not include non-VA diagnostic and treatment data, which may have affected observed estimates. Actual medication dispensing and adherence could not be assessed via the electronic health system. Some prescriptions captured in our study were likely treating psychiatric comorbidity (eg, anxiety disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder) and somatic conditions (eg, chronic pain or headache conditions, insomnia, urologic disorders, hot flashes); accounting for these factors may help explain some observed differences in treatment selection. The list of psychotropic medications was not exhaustive and did not include some medications that may be used to manage symptoms of depression (eg, antihistamines, ketamine, dopamine receptor agonists, and newer nonformulary medications). We did not examine combinations of medications (eg, AD with SGA) or quantify polypharmacy. Additionally, it’s important to recognize that treated-proportion estimates may be biased between FY16 and FY22, due to statistical inflation of the MDD diagnosis caused by systematic miscoding of individuals with subclinical or unspecified depressive syndromes using MDD-positive ICD-10-CM subcodes (eMethods in Supplement 1), resulting in inclusion of veterans who may have been less likely to seek or need treatment, with our data suggesting that these individuals were more likely to receive a regimen of ADs without psychotherapy (eTable 4A in Supplement 2).

Limitations regarding the external validity and generalizability of our findings also warrant consideration. The VHA patient population is predominantly older and male and carries a higher burden of psychiatric and somatic comorbidities compared with the general US adult population. Consequently, these findings may not fully generalize to younger, healthier nonveteran populations. Furthermore, our reliance on VA-only diagnostic and treatment data introduces ascertainment bias. Veterans who received community care or mental health treatment for MDD outside the VA system were missing from our treatment counts. Because unobserved external treatments are unaccounted for, this VA-only ascertainment likely biases the prevalence of no recommended treatment upward. Our results may overstate the lack of guideline-concordant care, as we could not capture care privately funded or accessed through commercial insurance or Medicare.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of 1 582 834 Veterans with MDD who received VHA outpatient care for MDD between FY11 and FY25, most received recommended treatment; however, there was an increasing trend—up to 10.2% in FY25—of veterans who did not. We observed decreases in use of benzodiazepines over time, suggesting VHA’s successful training and education for appropriation prescription. Stratified analyses identified demographic differences in treatment and highlighted subgroups potentially vulnerable to undertreatment. The findings require further investigation into potential undertreatment of VHA outpatients with MDD and underscore the need to examine mental health service availability and other factors that influence treatment selection decisions.
