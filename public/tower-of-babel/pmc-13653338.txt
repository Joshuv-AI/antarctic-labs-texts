Recent advances in anticoagulant-related nephropathy: risk factors, peribiopsy, and therapeutic anticoagulation
Authors: Yiwen Chen, Hao Yan, JingJing Wu, Huixia Liu, Yujuan Li, Ye Yao, Chun Zhang, Jing Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe concept of anticoagulant-related nephropathy (ARN), first proposed in 2009, manifests as acute kidney injury or the rapid deterioration of chronic kidney disease secondary to anticoagulant therapy. Large numbers of tubular RBCs and/or tubular RBC casts and ATN, without active glomerulonephritis are the main pathological features. The history of anticoagulant drug use and pathological manifestations are the most important characteristics that distinguish it from other renal injuries. ARN is considered rare, with just over 100 cases documented globally, yet its incidence may be underestimated due to limited awareness.This article reviews the epidemiology, risk factors, etiology and pathogenesis, diagnosis and treatment of ARN. New advances in recent years are highlighted, including the relationship between ARN and other kidney diseases, the characteristics of nonvitamin K oral anticoagulants and their effects on renal function, and the complexities of peri-biopsy and therapeutic anticoagulation.To address the critical peri-biopsy management impasse, we evaluated anticoagulation strategies from published case reports. Integrating these empirical insights with established perioperative antithrombotic principles, we propose a synthesized strategy for ARN management during the peri-biopsy and treatment phases, accompanied by a diagnostic flowchart. Given the current reliance on case-level evidence, this review does not intend to issue definitive clinical guidelines. Rather, it aims to build a comprehensive cognitive framework and a structured consideration system, facilitating nuanced assessments to ultimately improve patient outcomes in this challenging clinical domain.Keywords: acute kidney injury, anticoagulants, anticoagulation-related nephropathy, nephropathy, renal biopsy

CASE REPORT

A 38-year-old male postmechanical mitral valve replacement and CABG, maintained on long-term warfarin therapy. He presented with sudden gross hematuria and severe acute kidney injury (sCr 642.3 μmol/l) in the setting of a supratherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR) (4.81). Despite a stable baseline on warfarin (2.5 mg/d) and no prior renal, hypertensive, or diabetic history, the patient exhibited significant renal impairment unexplainable by obstructive uropathy or autoimmune etiologies (systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE], vasculitis, myeloma) upon admission. Because the patient had a history of wild mushroom intake before the onset, to resolve this diagnostic dilemma—differentiating between potential toxin-induced injury and anticoagulant-related complications—a renal biopsy was performed.

The decision to perform a renal biopsy presented a significant therapeutic challenge. We were forced to balance the high risk of thromboembolism against the severe risk of hemorrhage. Despite these competing risks, a percutaneous renal biopsy (PRB) was successfully and safely performed after careful perioperative management. Histopathological examination revealed acute tubular injury accompanied by IgA nephropathy (IgAN, Oxford classification: M0E0S0T0C0). Immunofluorescence microscopy demonstrated mesangial deposition of IgA (2–3+) and C3 (2+). Light microscopy showed abundant red blood cells within glomerular and peritubular capillary lumina, extensive red blood cell casts within tubular lumina, focal hemorrhage, and prominent tubular epithelial injury (Fig. 1). Considering the history of warfarin anticoagulation, these findings, together with the characteristic pathological features, supported the diagnosis of anticoagulant-related nephropathy (ARN). INTRODUCTION

ARN is an acute kidney injury (AKI) or acute exacerbation of chronic kidney disease (CKD) that occurs due to anticoagulant use. It is an anticoagulant complication that has been proposed for a short time and remains under-recognized and under-diagnosed. The clinical entity now known as ARN evolved from the concept of Warfarin-Related Nephropathy (WRN) [1]. Originally defined as a serum creatinine rise >0.3 mg/dl within 1 week of a supratherapeutic INR (>3.0) without overt bleeding. The terminology was expanded to ARN as emerging evidence demonstrated that similar renal injury could be induced by nonvitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants [2, 3]. Later, INR >3 was confirmed not to be a necessary condition, because excessive anticoagulation may be transient and go unmonitored [4–6].

Since the introduction of ARN in 2009, more than 100 cases have been reported worldwide [7]. Warfarin and nonvitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs) are the main anticoagulants reported. History of anticoagulant use is a key distinguishing feature from other forms of kidney injury, and histopathological evidence remains indispensable for a definitive diagnosis. ARN has been shown to lead to the progression of CKD and confers a significantly elevated risk of both short-term and long-term mortality [1]. With the widespread use of oral anticoagulants globally, early recognition of ARN is extremely important for patient outcomes.

Previous reviews have summarized the evolution and pathological features of ARN [8, 9]. However, clinicians still face practical difficulties when diagnosis and treatment require balancing high thromboembolic and bleeding risks, particularly around kidney biopsy. This review therefore updates ARN epidemiology, etiology, and clinical characteristics while focusing on these diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and providing a structured framework for clinical decision-making.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

Biopsy-confirmed ARN remains rarely reported, whereas clinically suspected ARN appears substantially more common in high-risk anticoagulated cohorts. The retrievable cases PubMed, Embase, Cochrane library, and CNKI reported more than 100 cases, mainly diagnosed by puncture. Brodsky et al. reviewed 8636 cases in the renal pathology database of the Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center (OSUWMC), of which 41 cases were diagnosed as ARN, accounting for 0.5% of the total sample [8]. In another retrospective study by Brodsky et al., 15 258 patients were treated with warfarin. Among the 4006 patients who had an INR value greater than 3 and underwent concurrent creatinine monitoring, 821 were clinically diagnosed with ARN, representing an incidence of 20.5%. In contrast, the incidence in the overall cohort was 5.4% [1]. In addition, the prevalence of ARN in the systematic review of ARN ranged from 19% to 63% [10]. The incidence is controversial, mainly because there is no clear diagnostic criteria, i.e. whether ARN is a pathological diagnosis or a clinical diagnosis.

In conclusion, the current incidence of ARN remains poorly defined and controversial. Due to the lack of renal biopsy evidence, it is difficult to determine whether the AKI cases in the above retrospective analysis are all ARN, which may lead to a high prevalence estimate, whereas the insufficient clinical awareness may lead to a low reported prevalence.

ETIOLOGY

Anticoagulant-related nephropathy, as the term suggests, is a form of kidney injury induced by anticoagulant therapy. Anticoagulants used clinically include parenteral and oral. Intravenous anticoagulants including low molecular weight heparin/unfractionated heparin, fondaparinux sodium, and bivalirudin, antiplatelet drugs such as dipyridamole and aspirin, are occasionally reported in ARN cases [4, 11], which fall outside the scope of this article. The oral anticoagulants predominantly in use are warfarin and NOACs, which are also the primary agents leading to ARN [12, 13]. Common clinical conditions requiring anticoagulants include atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism (VTE), status post heart valve replacement [14], as well as specific hypercoagulable states such as antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and nephrotic syndrome. With the exception of some high thromboembolic risk conditions such as mechanical valve replacement status [15], triple-positive APS [13], and valvular atrial fibrillation [16], NOACs are considered safer alternatives to warfarin. From 2009 to 2013, four pivotal randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were published, comparing the efficacy and safety of apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban with warfarin in patients with AF (NCT00412984/NCT00262600/NCT00781391/NCT00403767). The results showed that NOACs were superior to or not inferior to warfarin in the prevention of stroke or systemic embolism, with a lower risk of bleeding (data in Table 1). Apixaban showed a lower incidence of AKI and a more robust evidence base for use in CKD patients than warfarin.

Furthermore, in patients with VTE, high-quality RCTs have demonstrated that NOACs—including dabigatran (NCT00291330), rivaroxaban (NCT00440193, NCT00439777), and apixaban (NCT00643201)—exhibit noninferior anticoagulant efficacy and a comparable or superior safety profile relative to warfarin [17–20].

RISK FACTORS

Switching the type of anticoagulant [4] and increasing the dose of anticoagulants [21], or using other drugs that interact with anticoagulants, are common predisposing factors. Advanced age, comorbidities (including CKD, diabetes, diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, and heart failure), and the use of RAS blockers and/or diuretics are common risk factors for ARN [1, 22]. In addition to these, pre-existing kidney disease, the magnitude of INR increase is also closely related to ARN.

Renal disease and ARN

Pre-existing kidney disease is an important determinant of ARN susceptibility. Non–end-stage kidney disease may retain a prothrombotic tendency, whereas ESRD shifts toward bleeding because of uremic platelet dysfunction [23]. Membranous nephropathy is strongly associated with thrombosis, while IgAN combines a procoagulant milieu with hematuria. Retrospective data associate CKD, but not nephrotic syndrome or polycystic kidney disease, with increased ARN risk, and IgAN appears to be the most common underlying kidney disease. ARN has also been reported in kidney allografts [24]. CKD and ARN

CKD patients accounted for 40%–85% of reported ARN case series [25, 26]. Brodsky et al.’s retrospective study sample size was 15 258 cases, 4006 patients had INR >3 and monitored renal function at same time. Among them, 821 patients had a creatinine increase of ≥0.3 mg/dl within 1 week of INR >3.0, i.e. in patients with excessive anticoagulation, the incidence of clinically diagnosed ARN was 20.5%, including 33.0% in the CKD group and 16.5% in the non-CKD group, [1] indicating that CKD was closely related to the occurrence of ARN.

Reasons why CKD patients are more prone to ARN may include decreased GFR leading to accumulation of drugs, along with the fact that sclerotic glomeruli in CKD patients usually have pre-existing endothelial injury and dysfunction, proliferation of smooth muscle cells and mesangial cells, and destruction of podocytes and may be combined with glomerular microinflammation [27], making glomeruli more prone to bleeding.

IgAN and ARN

IgAN is closely related to ARN. A multicenter retrospective study of 26 biopsy-confirmed ARN patients found that all patients had underlying kidney disease with one exception. Among them, IgAN in 19, probable IgAN in 1, representing 76.9% of the cohort, suggesting that IgAN is the most common underlying kidney disease. The study also summarized three case series (n = 63) and 29 case reports that described the histological results of ARN patients. Of the 92 patients, 37 (40%) had IgAN [25].

IgAN patients also have a higher risk of AKI, especially in elderly patients. In a retrospective study of 151 elderly patients with IgAN, the incidence of AKI was 40%, and pathological biopsy was also mainly manifested as capillary hematuria and tubular necrosis [28].

Interestingly, among the 40% of patients with AKI, 34% were receiving oral anticoagulant therapy. Therefore, the use of oral anticoagulants may be an important cause of AKI in elderly patients with IgAN.

The mechanistic link between IgAN and ARN remains uncertain. In IgAN, galactose-deficient IgA1-containing immune complexes trigger mesangial inflammation and injury to the glomerular filtration barrier, potentially facilitating erythrocyte leakage into Bowman’s space and renal tubules [29]. Lectin-pathway activation may further promote local inflammation and a procoagulant glomerular environment [30]. During systemic anticoagulation, this vulnerable barrier may favor RBC extravasation, cast formation, heme-mediated tubular toxicity, and complement-related interstitial inflammation. However, direct mechanistic evidence linking the lectin pathway to ARN remains limited.

The relationship between INR and ARN

First, is a supratherapeutic INR a prerequisite for ARN? The increase of INR is closely related to ARN. Brodsky et al. found a direct temporal correlation between unexplained creatinine increases and INR >3 [31]. Many previous studies have used AKI within 1 week after INR >3 as a clinical diagnostic criterion for ARN [1, 32]. However, whether this clinical diagnostic criterion is accurate is controversial, because a considerable number of patients diagnosed with ARN via renal biopsy exhibited no documented INR >3 [5, 7, 11]. Therefore, the increase of INR is not a necessary factor for ARN, but may be a significant precipitant or risk factor for ARN.

In addition, does a higher INR correlate with increased ARN incidence or disease severity? A retrospective study by An et al. showed that there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of ARN with different INR levels in patients with INR >3 (3.01–12.65) [32]. In another prospective study of 150 elderly patients treated with warfarin anticoagulation, INR >3.0 was not associated with an increased risk of AKI. There was no difference in average INR between patients with and without WRN [33]. There is no relevant research on the effect of INR value on the severity and prognosis of ARN.

THE PATHOGENESIS OF ARN

The mechanism of ARN is not clear. Renal biopsy of ARN patients showed that there were a large number of RBCs and RBC casts in the Bowman’s capsule and renal tubules. A. Ozcan et al. established an experimental model mimicking this pathology in 5/6 nephrectomy rats by administering warfarin [34]. Therefore, mechanisms such as direct blockage of renal tubules by RBC casts [35, 36], and oxidative stress and the robust activation of inflammatory caused by heme toxicity may contribute to ARN [37, 38].

Both warfarin and NOACs exert their anticoagulant effects by diminishing thrombin activity. Proteinase-activated receptor 1 (PAR-1) is a thrombin receptor expressed on endothelial cells, and is involved in the regulation of endothelial function [39]. PAR-1 activation supports endothelial integrity, and its inhibition by drugs may lead to glomerular hemorrhage by disrupting the glomerular filtration barrier [40]. Ryan et al. used the selective PAR-1 inhibitor SCH79797 to act on 5/6 nephrectomized rats, and the rats rapidly showed ARN manifestations such as elevated serum creatinine, hematuria, and increased renal tubular RBC casts [35].

In addition, warfarin itself has a damage effect on renal blood vessels. Warfarin can inhibit the carboxylation of vitamin K-dependent protein (VKDP), including interfering with the activation of matrix Gla protein (MGP) and growth arrest-specific gene 6 (Gas-6), thereby promoting vascular calcification, affecting the apoptosis and movement of vascular smooth muscle cells, which may lead to vascular injury [41].

Based on these mechanisms, a two-hit model for ARN can be hypothesized: the first hit involves the functional or structural vulnerability of the glomerular filtration barrier induced by anticoagulants (e.g. PAR-1 inhibition or vascular calcification), and the second hit is the excessive RBC leakage and subsequent cast formation triggered by elevated INR or patient-specific susceptibilities. CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS AND DIAGNOSIS

Clinical manifestations

ARN has no specific disease manifestations, and the main clinical manifestation is elevated serum creatinine under anticoagulation. Hematuria (microscopic hematuria/gross hematuria) is the most common associated sign. Renal ultrasound examination is usually no obvious abnormalities.

The latency between anticoagulant administration and ARN onset is also an important issue. Given the limited case numbers, the anticoagulant duration of reported cases varies greatly. A literature review of 20 cases of NOAC-induced ARN reported that the time from medication to symptoms ranged from 7 days to several years, with an intermediate period of 28 days [26]. A retrospective study of 48 cases of ARN caused by warfarin showed that the majority of ARN cases emerge within the first 12 weeks, with peaks at 6–8 weeks [42]. Consequently, it is recommended that anticoagulant therapy be closely monitored every 3–4 weeks during the initial 3 months, with long-term monitoring intervals adjusted in accordance with GFR levels [40].

Based on the two retrospective studies and the mechanistic studies mentioned above, warfarin- and NOAC-induced ARN share similarities in their clinical phenotypes and pathological characteristics. However, these two types of drugs exhibit certain differences in etiology, onset time, and specific mechanisms. The incidence of warfarin-associated nephropathy is more commonly associated with excessive anticoagulation (e.g. an INR >3 in most patients), and its onset is relatively delayed (6–8 weeks after medication initiation). Furthermore, warfarin may induce vascular calcification by inhibiting VKDPs (such as MGP and Gas-6), thereby causing additional damage to renal blood vessels. In contrast, NOAC-induced ARN has a more rapid onset (with a median time of ~28 days).

Diagnosis

ARN can be clinically diagnosed when AKI or acute progression of CKD occurs during anticoagulant use, particularly when accompanied by signs of excessive anticoagulation such as hematuria. However, definitive diagnosis relies on renal biopsy findings. Renal biopsies in ARN typically reveal a multitude of RBCs or RBC casts in the renal tubules, alongside acute tubular necrosis (ATN), without obvious glomerular damage, which are typical pathological changes of ARN [8], as shown in Fig. 1. Active glomerulonephritis or other inflammatory changes that may explain glomerular hemorrhage need to be excluded. In addition, ARN patients may be associated with focal or inactive glomerular diseases such as focal crescentic glomerulonephritis with fibrotic crescents, mild immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis, thin GBM, lupus nephritis Class I or II, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, subacute interstitial nephritis, particularly IgAN [6, 8]. It has been reported that about 40% of ARN cases are combined with IgAN or accompanied by IgA deposition [25].

The renal biopsy procedure, however, carries a relatively high risk of bleeding [43], and the risk of bleeding is further increased in anticoagulant-using patients, therefore, renal puncture biopsy is chosen with great care.

PERIBIOPSY PERIOD ANTICOAGULANT THERAPY

Retrospective analysis of anticoagulation methods in peribiopsy period of ARN patients

By searching Pubmed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CNKI, as of September 2025, 146 ARN cases were found (70 case reports and four case series), most of the reports indicated the types of antithrombotic drugs or special conditions such as hematologic disorders. 93 cases of ARN were caused by warfarin-based VKA, 35 cases were NOACs, two cases were antiplatelet drugs, five cases were heparin, four cases were combined with two different antithrombotic drugs, one case was diagnosed by biopsy but the use of drugs was not explained, and six cases were caused by other reasons (such as disseminated intravascular coagulation). Of the 146 cases, 136 were diagnosed by renal biopsy.

Eight patients with ARN had bleeding complications after renal biopsy [3, 4, 44–49], of which 1 case died of septic shock caused by subcapsular hematoma infection at the puncture site [4]. One case developed dyspnea with atrial fibrillation and rapid ventricular rate after biopsy and was transferred to intensive care unit for treatment [48]. Peri-biopsy anticoagulation regimens were generally empirical; only 22 of 136 cases reported a description of perioperative anticoagulant use. Statistics and detailed information are in Supplemental Table S1.

In all cases where details were provided, renal biopsy was performed within while coagulation function and INR were within the normal range. Fifteen cases were on VKA and seven were on NOACs. Among them, eight cases were bridged with heparin, one case was bridged with argatroban, and 10 cases only suspended the use of anticoagulants, two case used vitamin K to reverse anticoagulation, and one case was reversed with Idarucizumab. Six of the 22 patients with ARN had bleeding complications after renal biopsy, and no thromboembolic events occurred due to the discontinuation of anticoagulants.

However, the clinical value of this retrospective analysis is inherently limited by the small sample size, incomplete reporting of thromboembolic risk stratification, and insufficient details regarding the timing, dosage, and duration of peri-biopsy anticoagulant interruption, bridging, reversal, and restart. Moreover, most reported peri-biopsy anticoagulation strategies were empirical rather than protocol-driven. Therefore, these data should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only, and should not be interpreted as comparative evidence supporting the safety or superiority of interruption, bridging, or reversal strategies.

Anticoagulant therapy during perioperative period

Ultrasound-guided PRB is a high-bleeding-risk procedure [43], especially for patients with long-term use of anticoagulants. The risk of thrombosis in the primary disease and the risk of bleeding during puncture should be weighed when performing renal biopsy.

In the case of medium and low risk of thromboembolism, warfarin is typically discontinued 5 days or more before puncture, and its use is resumed 12–24 h postprocedure when adequate hemostasis is confirmed. NOACs are discontinued 2 days before puncture according to the different half-life of the drug and recovered 48–72 h after operation. The interruption interval should be prolonged in patients with impaired renal function, especially for predominantly renally eliminated agents such as dabigatran [50]. ‘Heparin bridging’ is defined as the administration of short-acting anticoagulants during the withdrawal of VKA for 8–10 days when INR is below the therapeutic range [43]. Heparin bridging may be considered in selected patients at high thromboembolic risk, but its benefit remains controversial. Although some guidelines recommend bridging in high-risk patients, such as those with mechanical mitral valves or recent thromboembolic events, available evidence suggests that bridging may increase major bleeding without clearly reducing arterial thromboembolism [43, 51, 52]. Notably, heparin itself has also been reported to be associated with ARN; therefore, even short-term bridging therapy may potentially aggravate kidney injury in susceptible patients [8, 53].

Reverse anticoagulation

For patients who cannot wait for a longer withdrawal period and need urgent renal biopsy, reversal agents can be considered. Specifically, in the case of warfarin-treated patients with an INR above 1.5, the day prior to an emergency renal puncture, a dose of 1.0 mg of vitamin K can be administered [54]. However, the routine use of vitamin K for reversal is not endorsed by the guidelines (very low-quality evidence). The reason is that vitamin K administration has uncertain effects, and postoperative anticoagulation therapy may lead to drug resistance.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT OF ARN

The treatment of ARN is mainly supportive care and the restoration of INR to the treatment range. This includes strategies such as discontinuing, reducing, or switching anticoagulants, rehydration, sodium bicarbonate alkalinization, diuresis, and other conventional therapies. Other possible treatments include immunosuppressive therapy [6], N-acetylcysteine antioxidant therapy [36, 42], anticoagulant reversal, dialysis, and others. However, a retrospective study with a limited sample size of 26 ARN cases has not definitively proven the effectiveness of these treatments, including the discontinuation of anticoagulants (P = .14) or immunosuppressive therapy (P = .64) [25]. Immunosuppressive therapy is generally not recommended for isolated ARN due to the lack of evidence supporting its benefit. However, it may be considered when kidney biopsy identifies coexisting renal diseases for which immunosuppression is indicated, including active immune-mediated glomerular diseases (e.g. crescentic glomerulonephritis or rapidly progressive IgAN) or acute interstitial nephritis, with treatment targeting the underlying renal lesion [55]. To date, there is no evidence that anticoagulant restart will have a beneficial or adverse effect on the prognosis of ARN patients (P = 1).

Since most patients taking anticoagulants cannot discontinue them for a long time, we provide the following recommendations with reference to the characteristics of anticoagulants and the available literature to guide subsequent anticoagulant therapy.

Based on the previous comparison of NOACs and warfarin, it is reasonable to transition from warfarin to a NOAC due to the more stable INR and fewer drug interaction. However, in patients with renal insufficiency, anticoagulants need to be used with extra caution. In patients with renal impairment, for anticoagulants that depend on renal metabolism, such as dabigatran, special attention to dosage adjustments is needed to avoid accumulation of the drug.

For warfarin, Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) suggests that warfarin dosing needs to be adjusted on the basis of observational data and meta-analyses. Since patients with CKD stage 4 or 5 are often excluded from large RCT, the safety of NOACs in the advanced CKD population remains uncertain [56]. Currently, when eCrCl <15 ml/min, NOACs are not recommended in Europe [57]; the FDA gives dosing recommendations for apixaban (5 mg b.i.d or 2.5 mg b.i.d conditional) and rivaroxaban (15 mg qd), and KDIGO provides dosing recommendations for rivaroxaban (15 mg qd) and apixaban (2.5 mg b.i.d.) [58], none of which have clinical safety or efficacy data.

In managing anticoagulants without contraindications, the following ‘Union Framework’ measures can be taken:

Warfarin be transitioned to a NOAC. If the patient is already on NOACs, the dosage can be reduced [9]. It should be noted that transition from warfarin to a DOAC may be considered only when the original indication permits DOAC use. It is contraindicated or not recommended in patients with mechanical heart valves, moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis, and high-risk APS.

If the INR is outside the therapeutic range, reduce warfarin until the INR reaches a safe therapeutic range (2–3) [16]. Warfarin can be controlled to 2.0 < INR < 2.5 in patients with ARN who have long-term stable INR within the treatment range, with continuous monitoring to mitigate the risk of thrombosis. INR 1.8–2.5 for Chinese population where evidence supports this range under specific condition without contraindications [59]. Lower-intensity anticoagulation has been explored in selected Chinese mechanical valve populations, but INR targets must be individualized according to valve type, valve position, thromboembolic risk, and regional guideline recommendations. In high-risk indications such as mechanical mitral valves, lowering INR below standard targets should not be recommended without cardiology/cardiac surgery consultation.

In selected patients, reducing the long-term need for systemic anticoagulation may help lower the risk of recurrent ARN. However, anticoagulation-sparing strategies should not be regarded as routine ARN-specific treatment and must be individualized according to the original indication for anticoagulation, thromboembolic risk, bleeding risk, and renal function. Left atrial appendage occlusion may be considered in carefully selected patients with atrial fibrillation who are unsuitable for long-term oral anticoagulation, whereas inferior vena cava filters should be reserved for well-defined indications, such as acute VTE with contraindication to anticoagulation, major bleeding, or anticoagulation failure. These decisions require multidisciplinary assessment [60, 61].

In patients receiving anticoagulant therapy who develop acute kidney injury or rapid progression of chronic kidney disease, hematuria and/or an elevated international normalized ratio should raise clinical suspicion for anticoagulant-related nephropathy. PROGNOSIS AND MORTALITY

A meta-analysis showed that ARN increased the risk of death in patients. The 5-year mortality of anticoagulant patients with ARN was 91% higher than that of anticoagulant patients without ARN (RR = 1.91; 95% CI 1.22–3.0; I² 87%) [10].

The renal prognosis of ARN is not optimistic, also. In a literature review including 48 cases of ARN caused by warfarin, only 11 cases (22.92%) returned to normal renal function and 32 cases (66.66%) did not completely restore renal function. Among them, 14 cases needed maintenance renal replacement therapy, and five cases (10.42%) died within 2 years of follow-up due to rapid progression of renal injury [42]. In another literature review of 20 cases of NOAC causing ARN, renal function fully recovered in eight cases, failed to recover or deteriorated further in 12 cases, and four of these patients could not be separated from dialysis treatment [26]. Studies have revealed a significant, albeit modest, association between interstitial fibrosis, renal tubular atrophy in renal biopsy specimens, and serum creatinine levels at 12 months postbiopsy (R² = 0.17; P = .0235) [62]. However, there are no definitive morphological criteria or clinical markers to reliably forecast the outcomes of ARN.

CONCLUSION

ARN is a severe and potentially life-threatening renal complication directly associated with the use of anticoagulants, primarily warfarin and NOACs. Although NOACs generally offer a lower risk of hemorrhagic and renal events compared to warfarin, their safety profiles in patients with ESRD remain unclear. Clinically, ARN is likely to occur in patients with underlying CKD and exhibits a particularly strong association with IgAN. Pathologically, the condition is characterized by abundant tubular red blood cells (RBCs) and RBC casts, accompanied by acute tubular necrosis (ATN) without evidence of active glomerulonephritis.

Given the current scarcity of high-quality prospective studies, managing ARN—particularly during the high-risk peribiopsy period—remains a daunting clinical challenge. This article synthesizes available evidence to propose the ‘Union Framework’, outlining initial strategies for anticoagulation management during both the perioperative and treatment phases. Rather than serving as prescriptive guidelines, this proposed framework is intended to provide clinicians with a structured consideration system to individualize patient care in these high-risk scenarios. Ultimately, we hope this review not only heightens clinical vigilance towards ARN but also establishes a rational foundation to inform much-needed future prospective investigations.
