Exploring the functional food potential of Clitoria ternatea in modulating TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis–related inflammatory pathways relevant to psoriasis
Authors: Arifa Mustika, Abdul Khairul Rizki Purba, Siti Khaerunnisa, Zumara Ma’rifah Azzahra, Aldilatama Herisulistyo, Elly Mayangsari, Rizky Awaludin, Santhra Segaran Balan, Unho Yang, Moon Nyeo Park, Seong-Gyu Ko, Fahrul Nurkolis, Bonglee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disease driven by cytokine dysregulation, particularly through the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis. Current treatments targeting these cytokines are effective yet limited by cost, relapse risk, and safety concerns. This study investigates the therapeutic potential of Clitoria ternatea extract (CTE) using a multi-approach strategy combining phytochemical profiling, in silico analysis, molecular docking, and in vitro antioxidant assays. UPLC-QToF-MS/MS identified 14 major compounds in CTE, predominantly flavonoids. SAR-based prediction and ADMET profiling suggested that compounds C1, C4, C5, C7, and C8 exhibit anti-inflammatory potential with favorable safety profiles. Protein–protein interaction analysis and gene ontology revealed that these compounds target pathways associated with cytokine regulation. Molecular docking demonstrated strong affinities of C5 and C4 against IL-23 and TNF-α, exceeding that of clobetasol. In vitro antioxidant assays showed CTE had moderate scavenging activity against DPPH and ABTS radicals (IC50 ~ 90 μg/mL). In vitro studies using the RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) revealed that treatment with CTE significantly suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory proteins AKT1, IL-23, and TNF-α. These findings indicate that CTE, particularly its flavonoid components, may modulate key inflammatory pathways associated with the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis. Given its phytochemical composition and predicted safety profile, CTE may be better positioned as a functional food or nutraceutical candidate, rather than as a direct pharmacological agent, for supporting inflammatory balance. However, as this study is limited to in vitro and in silico analyses without in vivo validation, further mechanistic and clinical studies are required to determine its translational applicability.Keywords: Clitoria ternatea, Psoriasis, Functional foods, Molecular docking, Molecular mechanismSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Immunology

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease with a multifactorial etiology involving genetic, immunological, and environmental factors1. Its global prevalence ranges from 0.09% to 11.43%, with Indonesia reporting a prevalence of approximately 2.5%. Although psoriasis is not directly fatal, its recurrent nature and visible skin manifestations impose significant psychological and physical burdens, including increased rates of anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation2. These factors underscore the pressing need for improved therapeutic strategies that are both effective and accessible.

At the molecular level, psoriasis is characterized by hyperproliferation and abnormal differentiation of keratinocytes, accompanied by infiltration of immune cells and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines3. The TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis has been recognized as a central driver of psoriatic inflammation4. Activation of this pathway involves a cascade initiated by dendritic cells and macrophages, leading to T-cell differentiation into Th17 cells, and culminating in the overproduction of IL-17, IL-22, and other cytokines that drive keratinocyte activation and epidermal hyperplasia5.

While biologic therapies have revolutionized psoriasis treatment, their long-term use remains constrained by several limitations, including immunogenicity, systemic side effects, high economic burden, and limited accessibility in developing countries such as Indonesia6. In contrast, phytochemicals—particularly flavonoids and polyphenols—offer multi-target therapeutic potential by concurrently modulating oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine production, and immune cell signaling. These compounds often exhibit favorable safety profiles, cost-effectiveness, and compatibility with chronic use, making them attractive candidates for adjunct or alternative therapies7. Furthermore, natural compounds such as those found in Clitoria ternatea possess structural diversity that enables interaction with multiple molecular targets, potentially addressing the complex, multifactorial nature of psoriasis more holistically than single-target synthetic drugs.

C. ternatea, commonly known as butterfly pea, is a tropical plant rich in anthocyanins, flavonoids, and other polyphenolic compounds. Its extract has demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective activities in various preclinical models. However, previous studies have primarily focused on its neuroprotective and metabolic effects8–11, leaving its anti-inflammatory potential and relevance to chronic inflammatory skin diseases largely unexplored. Despite the well-documented properties of C. ternatea, no prior studies have specifically investigated its potential therapeutic role in psoriasis or its mechanistic interaction with psoriasis-related cytokine signaling pathways. Existing literature has largely confined its exploration to neuroprotective, metabolic, and general anti-inflammatory contexts12–14, leaving a critical knowledge gap regarding its anti-inflammatory influence on the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis that underpins psoriatic inflammation. The present study addresses this gap by integrating phytochemical profiling, in silico target prediction, molecular docking, and in vitro cytokine suppression assays to elucidate, for the first time, the potential of CTE as a multi-target modulator in psoriasis pathophysiology.

This study represents the first integrative attempt to elucidate the role of CTE in psoriasis by combining metabolomic profiling, in silico pathway mapping, and in vitro validation targeting the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis. Despite these properties, the role of C. ternatea in modulating psoriatic inflammation through immune-cytokine axis regulation has not been thoroughly investigated. This study integrates phytochemical profiling, in silico bioinformatics, molecular docking, and in vitro antioxidant assays to explore the potential of CTE in targeting key molecular mediators of psoriasis.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The collection and use of Clitoria ternatea L. plant material complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation for research involving plant materials. The plant material was obtained from a non-protected, locally available/cultivated source in Batu, East Java, Indonesia, and did not involve endangered or protected species. The authors confirmed that the study did not involve species at risk of extinction and was conducted in accordance with the principles recommended by the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). Permission for plant collection/use was obtained from the relevant local source/institution where applicable, and a voucher specimen (Voucher No.: CT-2025-01) was deposited at the Herbarium of Materia Medica Batu for future reference.

The dried flowers were ground into a fine powder using a mechanical grinder. Approximately 500 g of powdered flower material was subjected to extraction using the maceration method with 70% ethanol in a 1:10 (w/v) ratio for 3 days at room temperature, with gentle agitation every 12 h. The mixture was filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (Buchi, Flawil, Switzerland) at 40 °C to obtain a thick ethanolic extract. The resulting extract weighed approximately 62.5 g, corresponding to a yield of 12.5% (w/w) based on the initial dry weight. The concentrated extract appeared dark violet-brown in color, with a semi-viscous texture and a characteristic floral–earthly odor. The extract was stored in an amber glass container at 4 °C until further analysis.

Chemical/metabolite profiling

Metabolite profiling was carried out at the Forensic Laboratory Center of the Indonesian National Police Criminal Investigation Agency using a UPLC-QToF-MS/MS system. It should be noted that the reported relative peak intensities (%) represent semi-quantitative measurements and do not reflect absolute metabolite concentrations. The sample extract was obtained via a solid-phase extraction (SPE) method and subsequently concentrated to 1 mg/mL in methanol prior to analysis. Then, 5 μl of each extract was injected into the MS Xevo G2-S QToF detector, which is integrated into the ACQUITY UPLC® H-Class System (both manufactured by Waters, USA). For separation, an ACQUITY BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) was used with a flow rate of 0.2 ml/min, employing a gradient of acetonitrile with 0.05% formic acid and water with 0.05% formic acid as the mobile phases. The data from the UPLC-QToF-MS/MS analysis were processed using MassLynx 4.1 software, which produced chromatograms and m/z spectra for each peak. The chemical identifications were further corroborated by consulting online databases, specifically MassBank (https://massbank.eu/MassBank) and ChemSpider (https://www.chemspider.com). It should be noted that the present profiling was performed in a qualitative or semi-quantitative manner based on relative peak intensities. Calibration curves and internal standards were not applied; therefore, the concentration of each metabolite could not be accurately quantified. Future studies incorporating quantitative UPLC-MS/MS analysis with validated reference standards are warranted to establish direct correlations between metabolite abundance and biological activity.

In silico assessment

Prediction of phytochemical compound activities and pharmacokinetic analysis

The bioactivity potential of compounds derived from CTE compounds (CTEC) for immunosuppressant treatment was analyzed using the WAY2DRUG PASS prediction tool (accessed on January 15th, 2025, via https://www.pharmaexpert.ru/passonline/predict.php). This tool employs structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis to compare the input chemical structure with known compounds in its database15. Higher prediction scores and similar potential bioactivities indicate stronger structural similarity to known compounds. The Probability of Activity (Pa) value, generated as the output prediction score, indicates the likelihood of the compound being biologically active. Compounds with a Pa value greater than 0.4 were considered to have significant potential, with higher Pa values indicating greater precision and accuracy16. In this study, Pa values were interpreted as follows: 0.7–1.0 indicates a strong probability of activity (high confidence and well-characterized mechanisms), 0.5–0.7 represents a moderate probability (potential activity requiring further validation), and 0.3–0.5 denotes weak or preliminary prediction. This categorization guided the prioritization of compounds for subsequent molecular docking and target network analysis.

Additionally, this study evaluated the compounds’ toxicity and drug-likeness using ADMET criteria (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity)16,17. Drug similarity was assessed based on Lipinski’s Rule of Five (Ro5). SMILES notation was used to describe the properties of each ligand, with data sourced from ADMETLab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/evaluation/index), Protox II (https://tox-new.charite.de/protox_II/index.php?site=compound_input)18, and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) databases, all accessed on January 15th, 2025.

Protein target identification and analysis

The target analysis of CTEC was conducted using the SuperPred target analysis tool (https://prediction.charite.de/, accessed on January 15th, 2025)19. Predictions were generated by inputting the SMILES notation for each compound. For this analysis, a cut-off score of 80% (on a scale of 0–100%) was applied. Genes and proteins associated with psoriasis were retrieved from the Open Target database (https://opentargets.org/, accessed on January 15th, 2025). To identify overlapping targets between the disease and CTEC compounds, a Venn diagram was created using a bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en, accessed on January 15th, 2025).

Subsequently, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID 2021 (Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery) tool to categorize the overlapping targets into biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Pathway enrichment analysis was also carried out based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to identify relevant signaling pathways associated with psoriasis pathology. Furthermore, a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with a confidence score threshold of 0.7 to visualize and evaluate functional associations among the key proteins. The PPI network was further analyzed and visualized in Cytoscape v3.9.1 to identify hub proteins potentially serving as central regulators in psoriasis pathophysiology.

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking simulations were performed using CB-Dock2, a cavity-detection-guided blind docking tool (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php, accessed on January 16, 2025). This method integrates cavity detection, docking, and homologous template fitting to enhance accuracy and efficiency20–22. CB-Dock2 automatically determines the size and center of the docking box, adjusting its parameters to suit the ligands. It also provides 3D visualizations of binding patterns and ranks them according to Vina scores. To further improve precision, the process utilized Curvature-based cavity detection (CurPocket). The three-dimensional structures of target proteins were sourced from the PubChem database23, while receptor proteins, including AKT1 (4EKL), IL-6 (1ALU), TNF-α (2AZ5), and IL-23 (3QWR), were obtained from the RSCB Protein Data Bank.

Prior to docking, all water molecules and non-essential heteroatoms were removed automatically by CB-Dock2, followed by energy minimization of ligands using the MMFF94 force field to obtain the lowest energy conformations. For each protein–ligand pair, the grid box was automatically centered based on predicted binding cavities, with dimensions ranging from 22 × 22 × 22 Å to 28 × 28 × 28 Å, ensuring complete coverage of the active site region24. Each ligand was docked using the AutoDock Vina scoring function, generating five top-ranked binding poses per target. The pose with the lowest binding free energy (Vina score) and the most favorable orientation within the active site was selected for further visualization. Binding interactions were subsequently analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer 2021 and PyMOL v2.5, focusing on hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking to interpret the molecular basis of ligand affinity toward the receptor.

In vitro assessment of antioxidant via ABTS and DPPH inhibition

The scavenging activity of 2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) di-ammonium salt radical cation ABTS + (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) was evaluated following the method described by Nurkolis and Rampengan25,26. A 1:1 mixture of 2.4 mM potassium persulfate and 7 mM ABTS + was prepared, protected from light using aluminum foil, and left to react for 14 h at 22 °C. The resulting solution was diluted to achieve an absorbance of 0.706 at 734 nm, measured using a spectrophotometer (Genesys 10S, Thermo Scientific) (e.g., 1 mL of the stock solution mixed with 60 mL of ethanol), and a fresh working solution was prepared for each test. Samples (CTE) at concentrations of 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/mL were diluted with 1 mL of ABTS working solution, and the absorbance at 734 nm was measured after 7 min. Trolox, a well-known antioxidant, served as the positive control. Antioxidant activity was also assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay, following the procedure by Nurkolis and Kurniawan27,28. The DPPH solution was prepared by dissolving 24 mg of DPPH in 100 mL of methanol. Sample concentrations of 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/mL (CTE) were mixed with 3 mL of DPPH reagent in test vials and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The reduction in DPPH concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 517 nm. Trolox was also used as the positive control.

All assays were conducted in triplicate (n = 3 independent experiments). Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

In vitro cell culture, LPS induction, and treatment

RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cells from ATCC via Airlangga University (Surabaya, Indonesia) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator29. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, the cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL) to induce an inflammatory response. Simultaneously, cells were exposed to various concentrations of CTE (2.5–12.5 µM) or dexamethasone (2.5–12.5 µM) as a positive control. Treatments were incubated for 24 h.

RAW 264.7 macrophages were selected as an established and reproducible model for screening cytokine-mediated inflammatory responses. Although psoriasis is primarily an adaptive immune-mediated disease involving keratinocyte–T cell–dendritic cell interactions, macrophages contribute to upstream cytokine production, particularly TNF-α and IL-23, which are critical initiators of the IL-17 signaling cascade. Therefore, this model was employed as a surrogate inflammatory screening platform to evaluate general cytokine-modulatory potential rather than as a direct psoriasis disease model.

Quantification of IL-23, AKT1, and TNF-α expression

After 24 h of treatment, the culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove debris. The levels of IL-23, AKT1, and TNF-α were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions30. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The concentration of each cytokine/protein was calculated from a standard curve and expressed as pg/mL. All ELISA measurements were performed in biological triplicates (n = 3), with each sample analyzed in technical duplicates. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, considering p < 0.05 as statistically significant.

HaCaT keratinocyte-based psoriasis-relevant validation

To further strengthen the disease relevance of the anti-inflammatory findings, an additional in vitro validation was performed using HaCaT human keratinocytes as a psoriasis-relevant epidermal cell model. HaCaT cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight.

To mimic psoriasis-like inflammatory stimulation, HaCaT cells were exposed to a cytokine cocktail consisting of TNF-α and IL-17A, each at 10 ng/mL, for 24 h. Cells were then treated with CTE at concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL for another 24 h. Untreated cells served as the normal control, while cytokine-stimulated cells without CTE served as the psoriasis-like inflammatory control. Dexamethasone at 10 μM was used as a positive anti-inflammatory control.

After treatment, total RNA was extracted from HaCaT cells, followed by cDNA synthesis and quantitative real-time PCR analysis. The expression levels of psoriasis-associated keratinocyte hyperproliferation markers, including KRT16 and KRT17, and the epidermal differentiation marker filaggrin (FLG) were analyzed. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Management and analysis of data

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism Premium 10 software for MacBook, developed by GraphPad Software, Inc. (San Diego, CA, United States). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized to evaluate the data distribution. The IC50 values for antioxidant activity (ABTS, DPPH) inhibition activities at a 50% effective concentration were determined using the non-linear regression function in the GraphPad Prism Premium statistical analysis package, applying the log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response variable slope model.

Results

Phytochemical composition of Clitoria ternatea extract

Table 1 presents the identified bioactive compounds in CTE along with their respective molecular properties and classifications. A total of 14 compounds were detected, with flavonoids being the predominant class, constituting 71.4% of the total identified compounds. Luteolin (C6) was the most abundant flavonoid, representing 20% of the composition, followed by a chalcone-based structure (C11) with a 43.5% proportion. Other notable flavonoids included quercetin derivatives (C4, C5, C10), kaempferol glycoside (C8), and ternatin c5 (C9), suggesting their potential pharmacological significance. Additionally, three alkaloids (C1, C3, C14) and a phenolic compound (C13) were detected, further enriching the extract’s chemical diversity.

Anti-inflammatory potential and drug-likeness properties

The anti-inflammatory potential of selected CTEC metabolites was evaluated through SAR-based prediction, toxicity assessment, and drug-likeness analysis (Table 2). Adenosine (C1) exhibited the highest probability as an anti-inflammatory (Pa = 0.744), followed by flavonoid derivatives C4 (0.653), C5 (0.659), C7 (0.650), and C8 (0.707). Notably, all predicted anti-inflammatory compounds, except for C1, were classified under toxicity class 5, with an estimated LD50 of 5000 mg/kg BW, indicating low toxicity. In terms of drug-likeness, C1 was the only compound accepted by Lipinski’s rule of five, while the remaining compounds were primarily accepted by Pfizer’s rule, with some failing Lipinski’s and GSK’s criteria.

Molecular docking analysis of CTEC

Table 3 presents the molecular docking results of CTEC against key receptor proteins associated with dermatitis and psoriasis, including AKT1, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-23. The docking scores (Vina scores, in kcal/mol) indicate the binding affinities of CTEC compounds compared to the reference drug clobetasol. Clobetasol was included as a clinically established anti-inflammatory benchmark to contextualize relative docking scores under identical computational conditions. This comparison does not imply mechanistic equivalence, as clobetasol acts primarily through glucocorticoid receptor activation rather than direct cytokine binding. Therefore, the docking comparison serves solely as a reference for relative affinity interpretation. Nevertheless, it is important to note that clobetasol and CTEC constituents possess fundamentally different pharmacological profiles—clobetasol acts primarily through glucocorticoid receptor modulation, while CTEC compounds may exert effects through antioxidant and cytokine-inhibitory mechanisms. Therefore, the comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as it serves only as a relative indicator of binding affinity rather than direct therapeutic equivalence. Although adenosine (C1) fulfilled all ADMET parameters, the flavonoid compounds (C4, C5, C7, C8) demonstrated superior binding affinities across multiple immune-related targets, suggesting a potentially higher biological relevance in the anti-inflammatory mechanism of C. ternatea. Adenosine, as an endogenous molecule, plays established physiological roles, whereas flavonoids represent the major plant-derived bioactives with diverse anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. The emphasis on flavonoids is therefore justified by their strong molecular docking interactions and established pharmacological evidence, despite minor deviations in certain drug-likeness parameters. This selective focus provides meaningful insights into the plausible active constituents of the extract and their mechanistic contributions to the observed bioactivity.

Table 4 presents the antioxidant activity of CTE evaluated using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays. The IC50 values obtained were 90.68 μg/mL for DPPH and 91.00 μg/mL for ABTS. In comparison, the standard antioxidant Trolox exhibited lower IC50 values of 60.55 μg/mL (DPPH) and 59.50 μg/mL (ABTS). These results indicate that CTE is capable of scavenging free radicals, although with higher IC50 values than the reference. The antioxidant activity of CTE may be attributed to the presence of flavonoid compounds such as luteolin, quercetin glycosides, and chalcone-based structures, which are known to contribute to redox-related bioactivity. These results indicate that CTE possesses measurable chemical radical scavenging capacity; however, such assays reflect intrinsic antioxidant potential under non-biological conditions and do not directly represent cellular oxidative stress modulation relevant to psoriasis.

The inhibitory effects of CTE on the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines—TNF-α, AKT1, and IL-23—were evaluated using an in vitro inflammatory model on the RAW 264.7 cell line. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the untreated control group, stimulated with an inflammatory inducer such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), exhibited markedly elevated levels of all three cytokines, confirming a successful induction of inflammatory response in RAW 264.7 macrophage cells. Treatment with a standard anti-inflammatory drug significantly reduced the expression of TNF-α, IL-23, and AKT1, serving as a positive control for anti-inflammatory activity. Notably, cells treated with CTE also demonstrated a substantial and statistically significant reduction in cytokine expression compared to the control group. Specifically, TNF-α expression was notably suppressed by CTE, reaching levels comparable to the standard drug treatment. Similarly, IL-23 expressions were significantly downregulated following CTE exposure, suggesting that the extract effectively attenuates the inflammatory signaling cascade. These findings indicate that CTE exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by modulating cytokine production in activated macrophages. The ability of CTE to suppress TNF-α, IL-23, and AKT1 suggests interference with upstream pathways such as NF-κB or MAPK signaling, although further mechanistic validation is warranted. While the inclusion of dose–response curves or IC50 comparisons with dexamethasone would provide additional quantitative precision, the current results remain sufficient within the scope of this exploratory study to demonstrate the extract’s biological relevance. Given that the study aimed to establish preliminary efficacy rather than detailed pharmacodynamic characterization, the statistically significant suppression of key cytokines already provides strong evidence supporting CTE’s immunomodulatory potential. Collectively, these data support the therapeutic potential of CTE as a natural anti-inflammatory agent, with efficacy comparable to established pharmacological interventions.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the potential therapeutic utility of CTE in modulating the pathophysiology of psoriasis, particularly through targeting the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 signaling axis. The integration of phytochemical characterization, computational network pharmacology, molecular docking, and antioxidant evaluation offers robust mechanistic insights and preclinical evidence. Furthermore, this multidimensional approach underscores the novelty of employing C. ternatea as a potential phytotherapeutic candidate specifically against psoriasis, a disease context in which its immunomodulatory potential has not been previously characterized. By integrating both in silico and biochemical validation, this work bridges a critical gap between phytochemical composition and molecular pathway relevance. Unlike conventional in vitro or in vivo screening strategies that require isolated cytokine assays or animal models, the integrative network-pharmacology and docking framework employed here enables a systems-level exploration of multiple cytokine targets simultaneously. This approach not only minimizes experimental resource constraints but also provides predictive mechanistic insights that would otherwise remain inaccessible through single-assay techniques.

Phytochemical profiling via UPLC-QToF-MS/MS identified 14 bioactive compounds in CTE, predominantly flavonoids (71.4%), with luteolin and a chalcone-based compound being the most abundant. Flavonoids are well-documented for their immunomodulatory and redox-active properties, and their structural diversity suggests the potential for broad bioactivity against inflammation-related pathways31,32. Notably, the glycosylated derivatives of quercetin and kaempferol found in the extract have been previously implicated in anti-inflammatory signaling and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in skin models33–36. This aligns with reports demonstrating that luteolin and quercetin derivatives inhibit keratinocyte proliferation and cytokine overexpression via NF-κB and STAT3 pathway suppression, supporting the translational relevance of our findings to psoriatic inflammation.

SAR-based predictions using the PASS online tool revealed high probabilities of anti-inflammatory activity for several compounds37, particularly C1 (adenosine), C4, C5, C7, and C8. The majority of these compounds exhibited low toxicity (toxicity class 5) and satisfactory drug-likeness based on ADMET criteria, enhancing their candidacy for therapeutic development. The favorable ADMET and toxicity profile observed suggests that CTE could offer a safer pharmacological profile compared to conventional small molecules such as apremilast, which often exhibit gastrointestinal or mood-related adverse effects.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) and functional enrichment analysis further substantiated the biological plausibility of CTEC’s effect. The GO analysis revealed a predominant enrichment in interleukin receptor activity and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways—critical components of psoriasis pathology. The overlap between disease-related genes and predicted compound targets supports the potential of CTEC as a multitargeted therapeutic agent. This multitarget property reflects the polypharmacological advantage typical of botanical extracts, which may provide balanced immunomodulation rather than the selective immune suppression characteristic of monoclonal antibodies.

Molecular docking simulations provided quantitative evidence for strong binding affinities of key compounds to psoriasis-relevant receptor proteins. Compound C5 exhibited the most potent interactions, particularly with IL-23 (−10.6 kcal/mol) and TNF-α (−9.6 kcal/mol), surpassing the benchmark drug clobetasol in terms of potential activity. The interaction of C7 with AKT1 (−9.2 kcal/mol), a critical node in inflammatory signal transduction, further supports the hypothesis that CTEC compounds can modulate key regulatory points within immune signaling networks. However, while these docking results indicate a strong theoretical potential for cytokine modulation, they represent static interactions under idealized conditions. Crucially, the absence of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations is a study limitation, meaning conclusions regarding the long-term binding stability and dynamic interactions of the compounds with the target proteins remain inferred from the static docking scores. The antioxidant assays, in contrast, provide biochemical evidence of redox modulation in a biological environment, partially validating the in silico predictions related to oxidative stress but not yet confirming cytokine-specific effects. These molecular interactions reinforce the potential of CTE to disrupt the pathological cytokine feedback loop characteristic of psoriasis. It is also noteworthy that IL-23 and AKT1, identified as key nodes in our docking analysis, are recognized therapeutic targets in biologic drugs such as ustekinumab and secukinumab, suggesting that CTE acts through clinically validated molecular pathways. While previous studies highlighted C. ternatea’s beneficial effects on skin problems38,39, previous literature has supported how C. ternatea can improve IL-23, AKT1, and TNF-α levels11,40. Although molecular docking identified energetically favorable binding interactions between CTE-derived compounds and TNF-α and IL-23, the present study did not evaluate whether these predicted binding poses overlap with established functional or allosteric regions of the target proteins. Detailed structural mapping, comparison with co-crystallized ligands, or molecular dynamics simulations would be required to determine whether the compounds occupy biologically relevant regulatory domains. Therefore, the docking findings should be interpreted as indicative of potential target engagement rather than definitive evidence of functional site inhibition. Although GNPS-based molecular networking was not applied in this study, future work incorporating such approaches could enable broader metabolite coverage and improved visualization of the chemical space within the extract.

Docking simulations assess theoretical protein–ligand interactions under static structural conditions and do not account for pharmacokinetic determinants such as absorption, metabolic transformation, plasma protein binding, tissue distribution, or clearance. Although in silico ADMET predictions provide preliminary insight into drug-likeness, they do not replace experimental pharmacokinetic validation. Therefore, the observed binding affinities should be interpreted as mechanistic plausibility rather than confirmation of systemic therapeutic effectiveness. Although several compounds exhibited strong binding affinities in silico, molecular docking does not establish functional inhibition, receptor blockade, or downstream signaling suppression. Experimental validation through biochemical binding assays, molecular dynamics simulations, or receptor-based functional studies would be required to confirm pharmacological equivalence. Furthermore, the antioxidant evaluation utilized only the chemical assays DPPH and ABTS, which assess direct radical scavenging and do not fully reflect complex cellular antioxidant capacity or functional biological relevance. The moderate antioxidant potency (IC50 ~ 90 μg/mL) supports a functional link to redox-active flavonoids predicted in silico but highlights the need for cellular or enzymatic assays to verify cytokine-level modulation. Therefore, the correlation between computational affinity and biological efficacy should be viewed as suggestive rather than confirmatory.

In addition, antioxidant evaluation via DPPH and ABTS assays demonstrated that CTE possesses moderate radical scavenging capacity (IC50 ~ 90 μg/mL), suggesting its potential to mitigate oxidative stress—a process known to exacerbate psoriatic inflammation through the activation of redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB and STAT3 41,42. However, these chemical assays primarily reflect direct radical-quenching ability in vitro and do not fully capture the complexity of redox regulation within cellular environments. Although the antioxidant potency of CTE is lower than that of Trolox (IC50 ~ 60 μg/mL), such moderate activity remains pharmacologically relevant in the context of psoriasis. Psoriatic lesions are characterized by chronic, low-grade oxidative stress rather than acute radical bursts; thus, sustained, moderate antioxidant effects can meaningfully contribute to redox balance over prolonged exposure43. Moreover, in complex phytochemical matrices like CTE, additive or synergistic interactions among flavonoids may enhance in vivo antioxidant efficacy beyond the sum of individual IC50 values. Combined with CTE’s demonstrated cytokine-suppressive properties, this moderate antioxidant potential likely complements its immunomodulatory mechanism, reinforcing its translational promise as a multitarget phytotherapeutic. Thus, while the observed antioxidant activity supports the presence of redox-active flavonoids in CTE and aligns with in silico predictions, it should be interpreted as indicative rather than confirmatory of biological relevance. Future studies incorporating cell-based oxidative stress assays and keratinocyte models would be essential to elucidate whether CTE’s antioxidant potential translates into functional modulation of inflammatory cascades relevant to psoriasis pathology.

Compared to current psoriasis treatments, CTE demonstrates a mechanistic overlap with key therapeutic targets addressed by both biologics and small molecules. For instance, biologics such as adalimumab and ustekinumab selectively neutralize TNF-α and IL-23, respectively, to suppress downstream IL-17 signaling44—similar to the molecular targets identified for CTE compounds in this study. Meanwhile, small molecules like apremilast act by modulating intracellular cyclic AMP to indirectly attenuate cytokine production, but often cause gastrointestinal or mood-related side effects45. In contrast, CTE exhibits multitarget modulation with predicted low toxicity and antioxidant co-benefits, suggesting its potential as a safer complementary or adjunctive therapy. Importantly, the flavonoid-rich composition of CTE offers a polypharmacological advantage, potentially addressing both immune dysregulation and oxidative stress, two central pathological drivers in psoriasis. These comparative insights highlight the broader implications of repositioning CTE as a phytopharmaceutical prototype capable of integrating anti-cytokine and antioxidant mechanisms—a duality rarely achieved in current single-target drugs. Furthermore, these HaCaT findings address the limitation of the RAW264.7 macrophage model by providing keratinocyte-specific evidence relevant to psoriasis pathophysiology. While RAW264.7 cells demonstrate general anti-inflammatory activity, the suppression of KRT16 and KRT17 and restoration of FLG in cytokine-stimulated HaCaT cells suggest that CTE may also modulate psoriasis-associated epidermal hyperproliferation and impaired differentiation. Therefore, the evidence should be interpreted as psoriasis-relevant, although not yet fully disease-specific, without further validation in co-culture or in vivo psoriasis models.

Importantly, these mechanistic insights suggest that CTE’s flavonoid constituents could complement existing biologic therapies by targeting upstream modulators of cytokine expression and oxidative stress. While biologics such as adalimumab and secukinumab specifically neutralize TNF-α or IL-17A to block downstream signaling, flavonoids like luteolin, quercetin glycosides, and chalcone derivatives may act synergistically by dampening NF-κB and STAT3 activation—key transcriptional regulators upstream of cytokine synthesis46. This complementary mechanism could theoretically enhance therapeutic efficacy, reduce required biologic dosages, and mitigate relapse or rebound inflammation following biologic withdrawal. Moreover, the antioxidant capacity of CTE may confer an additional protective effect by limiting ROS-mediated amplification of cytokine signaling, a process often underaddressed by single-target biologics. Collectively, this integration positions CTE not as a replacement but as a multitarget adjunct capable of supporting redox and immune homeostasis alongside conventional biologic treatment.

A key limitation of this study lies in the exclusive use of an LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophage model for biological validation. Although macrophages contribute to cytokine amplification in inflammatory conditions, psoriasis is primarily driven by dynamic interactions among keratinocytes, dendritic cells, and Th17 cells within the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as evidence of general anti-inflammatory and cytokine-modulatory activity rather than direct validation in a psoriasis-specific cellular context. Future studies incorporating keratinocyte models (e.g., HaCaT cells), Th17 differentiation systems, or co-culture platforms would provide a more disease-relevant framework to substantiate the translational implications of CTE in psoriasis.

To bridge the translational gap, it is important to contextualize how these findings could advance toward clinical application. The convergence between in silico predictions and macrophage-based antioxidant responses provides an initial mechanistic rationale for therapeutic exploration. Specifically, the observed binding of CTE-derived flavonoids to clinically validated targets such as TNF-α, IL-23, and AKT1 aligns with pathways modulated by approved biologics used in psoriasis treatment. In this study, total AKT1 protein levels were measured without assessment of phosphorylation status or kinase activity. Therefore, the results indicate modulation of overall AKT1 expression but do not provide mechanistic insight into specific activation states of the AKT signaling pathway. Future studies incorporating phosphorylation-specific analyses (e.g., p-AKT detection via Western blot) would be necessary to elucidate signaling dynamics more precisely. The macrophage-based antioxidant findings further suggest that CTE may attenuate redox-driven cytokine overproduction, a critical process in psoriatic lesion progression. However, while these results highlight a biologically plausible mechanism, they remain at a preclinical proof-of-concept stage. It should also be acknowledged that the current in vitro model, which relies solely on LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, does not fully recapitulate the cellular complexity of psoriasis. RAW 264.7 macrophages reflect innate immune activation and acute inflammatory signaling rather than the chronic adaptive immune microenvironment characteristic of psoriasis. However, macrophage-derived TNF-α and IL-23 serve as upstream drivers of Th17 polarization and keratinocyte activation. Thus, suppression of these cytokines in macrophages provides preliminary mechanistic evidence of interference with the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 axis. Nevertheless, this model should be regarded as a surrogate screening system, and future investigations employing keratinocyte (e.g., HaCaT), dendritic cell, or Th17 co-culture models are necessary to confirm disease-specific relevance. Validation in keratinocyte or co-culture models—followed by in vivo assessments of epidermal cytokine modulation and safety profiling—will be essential to establish clinical translatability. While the present study demonstrated that CTE treatment attenuated LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine secretion in RAW 264.7 macrophages, it is important to recognize that only cytokine levels were measured at the protein secretion stage. The absence of complementary analyses, such as Western blot or qRT-PCR, limits the mechanistic interpretation of how CTE influences upstream signaling pathways or transcriptional regulation. Incorporating these molecular techniques in future studies would provide stronger evidence to confirm the modulation of inflammatory mediators at both the gene and protein expression levels.

Altogether, these results support a dual-action mechanism of CTE involving direct immunomodulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduction of oxidative stress. Taken together, these findings position CTE primarily as a functional food or nutraceutical candidate capable of supporting inflammatory balance through multitarget modulation of cytokine signaling and oxidative stress. Rather than acting as a direct pharmacological agent, CTE may provide complementary benefits through its flavonoid-rich composition, which enables broad yet moderate immunomodulatory effects. This distinction is important, as the observed activities are derived from a complex extract with synergistic interactions among constituents, rather than isolated compounds with defined pharmacokinetic profiles. Accordingly, the translational relevance of CTE lies in its potential role as a dietary or adjunctive intervention, rather than as a standalone therapeutic equivalent to conventional biologics or small-molecule drugs. Further in vivo and cell‑type‑specific validation is warranted.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the anti-inflammatory and cytokine-modulatory potential of CTE within a macrophage-based inflammatory model, providing preliminary mechanistic insights relevant to pathways implicated in psoriasis. Rather than positioning CTE as a pharmaceutical drug candidate, the findings support its potential role as a functional food or nutraceutical source of bioactive flavonoids capable of contributing to inflammatory balance. Bioinformatics, molecular docking, and in vitro antioxidant assays collectively support the role of CTE, particularly its flavonoids, in modulating the TNF-α/IL-23/IL-17 inflammatory axis and scavenging reactive radicals. In vitro studies using the RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) revealed that CTE treatment significantly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory proteins AKT1, IL-23, and TNF-α. While these findings suggest that CTE possesses anti-inflammatory potential through modulation of macrophage-related signaling pathways, it is important to acknowledge that psoriasis pathogenesis also involves keratinocytes and T-cell interactions. Therefore, further investigations employing keratinocyte and T-cell models, as well as in vivo validation, are warranted to substantiate the translational relevance of these results.
