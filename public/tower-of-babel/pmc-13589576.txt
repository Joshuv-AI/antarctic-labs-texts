Dimethyl Sulfoxide Modulates T Cell–Mediated Immunity to Attenuate Influenza A Virus Infection
Authors: Po-Hsuan Jeng, Meng-Wei Tsai, Shing-Hwa Huang, Tien-Ru Huang, Mei-Fei Chen, Gu-Jiun Lin, Shao-Cheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeInfluenza A virus (IAV) remains a major threat because vaccines provide limited cross-protection and antiviral resistance is rising. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is immunomodulatory, but its effect on IAV has not been tested in vivo. We evaluated its efficacy and mechanism in a murine H1N1 model.MethodsMale C57BL/6 mice were intranasally challenged with an LD50 of A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) and given daily subcutaneous DMSO (0.22 g/kg/day) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on days 1–7. Survival was monitored for 21 days; lung and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected on day 8. Lung histology (hematoxylin and eosin), BALF cytokines (sandwich ELISA), lung and splenic IFN-γ–producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (flow cytometry), and whole-lung RNA sequencing (differential expression, KEGG enrichment) were assessed. BALF viral burden was measured by neuraminidase activity assay and TCID50. In vitro, splenic CD8+ T cells exposed to 0%, 0.5%, or 1.0% DMSO were immunoblotted for lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG3).ResultsDMSO significantly improved 21-day survival and attenuated alveolar inflammatory infiltration. Day-8 BALF viral titers were significantly lower with DMSO (mean 2.2×103 vs 4.3×104 TCID50/mL; p = 0.034), as was neuraminidase activity. BALF IFN-γ and TGF-β were elevated, whereas TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were not. IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells increased in the lung but not the spleen; CD4+ populations were unchanged. RNA sequencing showed enrichment of cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and PI3K/Akt signaling and upregulation of T-box factors, PPAR-γ, and Nek kinases. In vitro, DMSO accelerated LAG3 induction in CD8+ T cells within 12–48 h.ConclusionDMSO is associated with attenuated IAV severity, a lower day-8 airway viral burden, and a localized expansion of IFN-γ–producing CD8+ T cells, without a concomitant rise in the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, or IL-1β. These associations are correlative and support further investigation of DMSO as a host-directed adjunct for influenza.

Introduction

Influenza is a highly contagious respiratory disease that exerts a major annual burden on human health, animal populations, and the global economy.1 Symptoms range from mild upper-respiratory illness to severe and fatal viral pneumonia, sepsis, and cytokine storms.2 Although the World Health Organization (WHO) updates inactivated influenza vaccines annually, influenza viruses frequently evade host immunity through antigenic shift,3 and antibodies induced by inactivated vaccines often provide limited cross-protection against divergent strains.4 Neuraminidase inhibitors can offer clinical benefit when initiated within 48 hours of symptom onset,5,6 but antiviral resistance has been increasingly reported.7 Influenza viruses are enveloped, negative-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses of the family Orthomyxoviridae and are classified into four species (IAV, IBV, ICV, and IDV). Of these, IAV causes the greatest loss of life and economic impact and is the focus of the present study.8 Because both vaccination and neuraminidase inhibition target the virus itself, and are therefore vulnerable to antigenic change and to drug resistance, strategies that instead act on the host response are an attractive complement, particularly those that strengthen cross-protective cellular immunity rather than strain-specific antibody.

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is a natural by-product of the paper industry derived from wood. It is a colorless polar solvent comprising a sulfonyl group and two methyl groups.9 Owing to its hydrophilic and lipophilic properties, DMSO is widely used in medicine.10 Clinically, DMSO is used for cryoprotective organ preservation,11,12 and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved DMSO for the treatment of interstitial cystitis.13 Recent studies have highlighted its therapeutic effects in autoimmune arthritis,14 pressure sores,15 complex regional pain syndrome,16 and bladder inflammation.17 Of particular relevance, DMSO has been shown to decrease herpes simplex virus (HSV) virion infectivity and halt viral replication in vitro.18 DMSO is therefore a plausible candidate for host-directed repurposing; however, its reported antiviral activity has been demonstrated in vitro, and its effect on the host immune response during a live respiratory viral infection has not been defined.

What is established about the immunological activity of DMSO derives largely from non-infectious settings. We previously demonstrated that DMSO expands regulatory T (Treg) cells via signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) in non-obese diabetic mice.19 That observation is mechanistically relevant to antiviral immunity because STAT5 is also critical for sustaining CD8+ T cell effector responses,20 and CD8+ T cells clear influenza viruses21 and confer cross-protection against IAV and IBV by recognizing conserved viral regions;22 patients with robust CD8+ T cell responses have shorter hospital stays during severe influenza.23 CD4+ T cells, by contrast, exert dual roles: their depletion can slow viral clearance, but excessive activation may exacerbate immunopathology.24 Two questions therefore remain unanswered. First, whether systemic DMSO influences the CD8+ T cell compartment in vivo has not been examined, because our earlier work addressed Tregs in a model of autoimmunity rather than infection. Second, no in vivo study has asked whether DMSO can reshape the T cell response in the lung during respiratory viral infection, even though the lung is the compartment in which influenza is actually controlled.

Given its immunomodulatory profile, we hypothesized that DMSO could be repurposed to modulate T cell-mediated immunity and improve outcomes during IAV infection. The present study therefore investigated the therapeutic efficacy of DMSO and its underlying immunological mechanisms in a murine model of H1N1 IAV infection, with emphasis on local pulmonary T cell responses and transcriptomic reprogramming. We frame this work as a hypothesis-generating study; the data presented here are correlative and any mechanistic links are presented as a working model that warrants targeted knock-out follow-up.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval

All in vivo experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) International and with the ARRIVE guidelines. The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of National Defense Medical University, under protocol number IACUC-19–204 and IACUC-21–063. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Humane Endpoints and Euthanasia

In strict accordance with AAALAC regulations, animal welfare was prioritized throughout the in vivo studies. Humane endpoints were established by strictly monitoring the body weight of the mice; the experimental endpoint was defined as a body weight reduction reaching 30% of the animal’s initial weight, at which point euthanasia was promptly carried out. For terminal sample collection on day 8 post-infection, euthanasia procedures were conducted in compliance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Mice were deeply anesthetized prior to any physical procedures using an intramuscular injection of a 1:1 mixture of Zoletil (2.5 mg/mL) and Rompun (23.32 mg/mL). Once the mice were fully anesthetized, the thoracic cavity was opened as a non-survival procedure. Depending on the downstream assay, this terminal procedure was immediately followed by either injecting 4% formalin into the trachea for lung tissue fixation or inserting a needle equipped with a PE tube into the trachea to flush the lungs with PBS for the collection of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid.

Mice

Five-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the National Laboratory Animal Center (Taipei, Taiwan) and maintained until 6–8 weeks of age in the National Defense Medical University Laboratory Animal Center under specific pathogen-free, environmentally controlled conditions.

Influenza Virus Propagation and Titration

Recombinant influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) was generated by reverse genetics in HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA, USA) transfected with eight plasmids. After 48 hours, supernatants were propagated in Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells. Virus stock titers were determined by plaque assay on MDCK monolayers fixed with 10% formalin and stained with 1% crystal violet.

Treatment Protocol and Survival Assessment

Mice were intranasally challenged with a 50% lethal dose (LD50) of A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1). From day 1 to day 7 post-infection, mice received daily subcutaneous injections of either DMSO (0.22 g/kg/day) or an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) into the hindlimb. Survival was monitored daily over 21 days.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected and centrifuged to remove cellular debris. Cytokines were quantified using BioLegend ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, plates were coated with capture antibodies overnight at 4 °C, blocked for 1 hour at room temperature, and incubated with duplicate standards and samples for 2 hours. After incubation with detection antibodies (1 hour) and substrate (30 minutes), the reaction was stopped and absorbance read on a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations (pg/mL) were interpolated from a standard curve.

BALF Viral Titration

On day 8 post-infection, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected as described above and clarified by centrifugation to remove cellular debris. MDCK cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5×104 cells per well and cultured overnight, then washed with serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with non-essential amino acids, penicillin-streptomycin, and L-glutamine. Ten-fold serial dilutions of clarified BALF were prepared in the same medium and applied to the monolayers, which were incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour with agitation every 15 minutes to allow adsorption. The inoculum was then removed, the monolayers were washed, and serum-free DMEM containing trypsin at a 1:1000 dilution was added. After 72 hours at 37 °C the medium was removed and the monolayers were stained overnight with 0.5% methylene blue; the plates were rinsed with water and each well was scored as infected by loss of methylene blue staining, indicating cytopathic effect. The TCID50 was calculated by the Spearman-Karber method and expressed as log10 TCID50 per mL of BALF.

Neuraminidase Activity Assay

NA activity in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was measured with the NA-Fluor Influenza Neuraminidase Assay Kit (Applied Biosystems/Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two-fold serial dilutions of BALF from 1:1 to 1:1024 were prepared in 1X assay buffer in black 96-well flat-bottom plates, and an equal volume of 200 µM NA-Fluor substrate working solution was added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 60 minutes with shaking and protected from light, the reaction was terminated with NA-Fluor Stop Solution, and fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 350 to 365 nm and an emission wavelength of 440 to 460 nm. A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) virus stock was included as a positive control and BALF from uninfected mice as a negative control.

Histological Analysis

Under anesthesia, lungs were harvested, fixed in 10% formalin for 24 hours, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under light microscopy.

Flow Cytometry

Single-cell suspensions from BALF and spleen were stained for CD45 (clone 30-F11, BioLegend), CD4 (clone GK1.5, BioLegend), CD8a (clone 53-6.7, BD), intracellular IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2, BioLegend), and a viability dye (Zombie, BioLegend). All staining antibodies were used at 1:200 in FACS buffer (CD45, CD4, CD8a) and 1:100 (intracellular IFN-γ). Surface staining was performed for 30 min at 4 °C; intracellular cytokine staining used the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit following 5 h ex vivo restimulation with PMA/ionomycin in the presence of brefeldin A. Data were acquired on a BD FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD Biosciences Pharmingen) and analyzed in FlowJo software. Lymphocytes were gated on forward and side scatter, followed by doublet exclusion, then viable CD45+ leukocytes; CD4+ and CD8a+ subsets were used to quantify the frequency of intracellular IFN-γ+ cells.

Isolation of Splenic CD8+ T Cells

Spleens were mechanically dissociated and treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer to remove erythrocytes. CD8+ T cells were purified by negative selection using a Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western Blot

Splenic CD8+ T cells exposed to DMSO (0%, 0.5%, or 1.0%) for 12, 24, or 48 hours were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease inhibitors. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, blocked, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-LAG3 (GeneTex, GTX81605) and anti-β-actin primary antibodies. Bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) after secondary antibody incubation and quantified densitometrically.

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis

Total RNA from infected lung tissues was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Libraries were prepared from 300 ng RNA with TruSeq Stranded mRNA kits (Illumina). Pooled 2-nM libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq system, yielding 20–30 million paired-end reads per sample. After quality control with fastP and multiQC, reads were aligned to the mm10 genome with HISAT2 and quantified with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs; adjusted p < 0.05, |log2 fold change| > 1) were identified with DESeq2 and edgeR. Functional enrichment (Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]) was performed using Enrichr (p < 0.01). Pathview visualized expression changes on KEGG pathway maps, and heatmaps were generated to compare immune-related gene sets among groups.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.01 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA). Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel approach. Three-group comparisons (uninfected vs PBS vs DMSO) were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test; two-group comparisons used unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Viral titers were log10-transformed before analysis, as is conventional for titer data spanning several orders of magnitude, and the transformed values were compared by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. Exact p-values are reported throughout. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In vivo Protective Efficacy of DMSO Against Influenza A Virus Infection

To evaluate the protective efficacy of DMSO in vivo, C57BL/6 mice were intranasally challenged with an LD50 of A/PR/8/1934 (H1N1) and treated with daily subcutaneous DMSO (0.22 g/kg/day) or PBS from day 1 to day 7 post-infection (Figure 1A). Survival was monitored daily for 21 days. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that DMSO administration significantly reduced mortality compared with the PBS vehicle control (Figure 1B). Histopathological examination of lung tissues harvested on day 8 demonstrated extensive lymphocyte infiltration within the alveolar spaces of PBS-treated mice; in contrast, this inflammatory infiltration was attenuated in the DMSO-treated group (Figure 1C). Collectively, these results indicate that DMSO exerts a protective effect against IAV infection by improving survival and reducing the severity of viral pneumonia (21-day survival 30.7% [PBS, n = 26] vs 68% [DMSO, n = 25]; log-rank p = 0.0139; HR = 2.73, 95% CI 1.18–6.33). DMSO Treatment is Associated with a Lower Day-8 Viral Burden in the Airway

To determine whether the survival benefit reflected reduced viral replication, preserved tissue integrity at a comparable viral burden, or both, BALF collected on day 8 post-infection was analysed in parallel by neuraminidase (NA) activity assay and TCID50 titration (Figure 2A). NA activity was readily detectable in BALF from PBS-treated mice across the dilution series, whereas activity in DMSO-treated mice was lower and approached that of uninfected controls (Figure 2B). Consistent with this, infectious virus titers were significantly lower in DMSO-treated than in PBS-treated mice (mean titers 2.2×103 vs 4.3×104 TCID50/mL, n = 3 per group; p = 0.034 by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test on log10-transformed titers; Figure 2C–E). These results indicate that DMSO lowers the infectious viral burden in the airway at the same timepoint at which the histological, cytokine, flow-cytometric, and transcriptomic analyses were performed, and therefore that the improved outcome reflects a combination of reduced viral burden and modulation of the host response. DMSO is Associated with a Modulated Local Pro- and Anti-Inflammatory Cytokine Profile in the Lungs

Having established the overall protective effect of DMSO, we next investigated its impact on the local pulmonary inflammatory milieu. Cytokines were quantified using individual sandwich ELISA kits on day 8 BALF (Figure 3A). The concentration of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) was significantly elevated in the DMSO-treated group compared with both uninfected and PBS-treated controls (Figure 3B). In contrast, the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were not further increased by DMSO relative to the PBS vehicle (Figure 3C–E). DMSO treatment also robustly increased levels of the anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair cytokine transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β; Figure 3G), without further altering IL-10 (Figure 3F). These data suggest that DMSO intensifies the localized antiviral response primarily through selective enhancement of IFN-γ accompanied by pro-resolving signals.

DMSO Is Selectively Associated with a Higher Frequency of IFN-γ-Producing CD8+ T Cells in the Pulmonary Microenvironment

To identify the cellular sources of the modulated cytokine response, we used flow cytometry to enumerate IFN-γ-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in both the peripheral spleen and local BALF (Figure 4A and B). Consistent with a localized rather than systemic mechanism, no significant differences in IFN-γ+ CD4+ or CD8+ frequencies were observed in the spleens of DMSO-treated mice compared with PBS controls (Figure 4C and D). Within BALF, the proportion of IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells also remained unchanged (Figure 4E). However, DMSO treatment was associated with a statistically significant increase in the frequency of IFN-γ-producing CD8+ T cells in the lungs (Figure 4F). Together, these findings indicate that systemic DMSO is selectively associated with a reshaped local pulmonary inflammatory environment by enriching activated, antiviral CD8+ T cells at the primary site of infection. DMSO is Associated with Transcriptional Changes in Immune-Related Genes in the Lungs

To elucidate the intrinsic molecular mechanisms underlying DMSO’s immunomodulatory effects, we performed RNA sequencing on whole-lung tissues (Figure 5A). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis identified a targeted subset of transcriptional alterations induced by DMSO compared with the PBS control. As shown in the volcano plot (Figure 5B), significantly upregulated transcripts in the DMSO cohort included Ms4a7, Rpl15, Mmp12, and Igf1, which are predominantly associated with tissue remodeling, cellular growth, and immune regulation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further showed that these DEGs were concentrated in immune and signal-transduction cascades, identifying “cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction” and the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway” among the most enriched networks (Figure 5C, Supplementary Figure 1). DMSO is Associated with Accelerated in vitro LAG3 Kinetics in CD8+ T Cells and Maintains Physiological Homeostasis

To verify the accelerated CD8+ T cell activation kinetics suggested by the transcriptomic data, an in vitro stimulation assay was performed using isolated CD8+ splenocytes exposed to varying concentrations of DMSO (Figure 6A). Immunoblot analysis revealed that DMSO (0.5% and 1.0%) accelerated the onset of LAG3 expression at 12, 24, and 48 hours compared with untreated controls (Figure 6B). Because LAG3 upregulation typically follows robust T cell activation as a homeostatic immune checkpoint, these dynamics are consistent with earlier CD8+ T cell activation in the presence of DMSO, accompanied by physiological feedback that limits overstimulation. DMSO is Associated with Distinct Regulatory Gene Signatures in the Lung

Comparative heatmap analysis (Figure 7) demonstrated that DMSO treatment is associated with a refined pulmonary transcriptomic landscape involving specific regulatory modules. Within the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling axis, DMSO was associated with selective modulation of MAPK-family members and notably enhanced expression of Mapk4, Mapk11, and Mapk12 (Figure 7A), providing a potential signal for cellular adaptation. Although T-bet (Tbx21) expression was primarily driven by viral challenge, DMSO independently upregulated a distinct cluster of T-box transcription factors, including Tbx1 and Tbx4 (Figure 7B), accompanied by induction of the immune-modulatory factors Foxp3. DMSO-treated tissues also exhibited increased expression of metabolic and cell-cycle regulators, particularly Nek1, Nek7, and Pparg (Figure 7C). Together, these transcript-level associations are compatible with a shift in the lung microenvironment in which selective T-box modulation and a metabolic priming signature accompany the reinforced CD8+ T cell axis. Discussion

Interferons are cytokines with broad antiviral and immunoregulatory activity, classified into three types according to their receptors.25 Type II interferon, IFN-γ, derived from CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages, plays a pivotal role in antiviral defense. IFN-γ enhances CD8+ T cell effector function and differentiation, augments antigen processing and presentation via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I,26 and stimulates macrophage nitric oxide production and phagocytic activity.27 In the present study, the frequency of IFN-γ–producing CD8+ T cells was significantly elevated in BALF on day 8 of DMSO treatment, indicating an enhanced antiviral response. Critically, this enhancement was confined to BALF and was not detected in the spleen, supporting a localized rather than systemic mechanism of DMSO action.

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells eliminate virus-infected cells, secrete antiviral cytokines, and confer broadly protective immunity against influenza viruses. Following IAV infection, pulmonary dendritic cells license CD8+ T cell proliferation and activation via MHC I; activated CD8+ T cells then secrete perforin, granzyme, and tumor necrosis factor, triggering caspase-mediated apoptosis of infected targets.28 We observed that DMSO increased CD8+ T cell activation status, supporting CD8+ T cells as a plausible effector mechanism for DMSO. Although LAG3 is canonically characterized as an inhibitory receptor associated with T cell exhaustion, its rapid upregulation following initial stimulation is also a hallmark of vigorous T cell activation. The accelerated LAG3 induction observed in vitro is consistent with a priming effect of DMSO on CD8+ T cells. These correlative data are compatible with a model in which DMSO accelerates early CD8+ T cell activation while engaging physiological feedback that limits overstimulation; formal proof of causality will require CD8+ T cell depletion or genetic loss-of-function studies. This balance likely contributes to the observed reduction in pulmonary inflammatory infiltration and the suppression of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines.29 LAG3 upregulation is a dual marker reflecting both vigorous activation and inhibitory feedback; our in vitro data alone cannot distinguish between these states. Future work using granzyme B, perforin, and CD69 co-staining and, ideally, single-cell RNA-seq at matched time points, will be required to clarify whether DMSO-treated LAG3+ CD8+ T cells in our system are activated effectors, terminally exhausted cells, or both.

The present study also asked whether this reinforced CD8+ T cell response is accompanied by control of the virus itself. BALF viral titers measured on day 8, the same timepoint as the immunological analyses, were lower in DMSO-treated mice, and neuraminidase activity in BALF was correspondingly reduced. The improved outcome is therefore not explained by tolerance of an equivalent viral burden. Two mechanisms could contribute, and the present data are most consistent with a combination of both. First, an accelerated and locally enriched IFN-γ–producing CD8+ T cell response would be expected to clear infected cells more efficiently, which links the immunological changes described above to the lower viral burden. Second, DMSO restricts herpes simplex virus replication in vitro,18 so a direct effect on viral replication cannot be excluded. Because CD8+ T cell depletion and loss-of-function experiments were not performed, the relative contribution of immune-mediated clearance and of direct antiviral restriction cannot be resolved here; the reduced viral burden and the reduced immunopathology are therefore best presented as co-occurring features of the DMSO response rather than as a single causal chain.

Overactive induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, coupled with inadequate anti-inflammatory regulation, is the hallmark of cytokine storm during influenza infection and is a major contributor to multi-organ dysfunction and mortality.30 The role of anti-inflammatory cytokines is to suppress uncontrolled inflammation and to preserve immune homeostasis, and Treg activation has been shown to prevent cytokine storm in animal models.31 Our previous research demonstrated that DMSO acts as an immunosuppressive agent by expanding Tregs through STAT5 signaling and inhibiting T helper 1 responses in non-obese diabetic mice;19 however, STAT signaling has a context-dependent dual role in immunology. Tripathi et al showed that STAT5 is indispensable for the maintenance of effector CD8+ T cell responses.20 Our findings are compatible with a model in which DMSO acts as an immunomodulator associated with a reinforced CD8+ T cell axis, without a concomitant rise in the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, or IL-1β. By coordinating an extracellular cytokine milieu enriched in IL-2, IL-4, and IL-2132,33 with the intracellular upregulation of Nek kinases and PI3K/Akt signaling, DMSO appears to create a permissive environment for T cell proliferation and signal transduction.34,35 The concurrent induction of T-box transcription factors36 and IL-1837 likely reinforces the cytotoxic machinery required for viral clearance. Importantly, the upregulation of Pparg and Foxp3, together with selective suppression of neutrophil-recruiting CXCL5/6/7 chemokines, highlights an additional regulatory layer.38 This dual-action mechanism suggests that DMSO does not merely amplify the immune response; rather, it refines it, reinforcing targeted CD8+ effector functions and metabolic fitness while mitigating the immunopathology and collateral tissue damage typical of acute viral infection.

In contrast to CD8+ T cells, our data indicate that CD4+ T cells are not significantly affected by DMSO treatment. Although previous in vitro work reported that DMSO can inhibit CD4+ T cell proliferation,39 we did not observe a reduction in CD4+ T cell numbers in vivo. Flow cytometry showed no increase in IFN-γ–producing CD4+ T cells in either the spleen or BALF. Together, these results indicate that the DMSO-associated changes were selective for CD8+ T cells, with minimal or modestly suppressive effects on CD4+ T cells.

DMSO is an established penetration enhancer that increases membrane fluidity and, at higher concentrations, forms transient water pores, thereby improving the solubility, permeability, and bioavailability of co-administered compounds.13 Its effects could therefore in principle reflect enhanced delivery of another molecule rather than an intrinsic action. In this study, however, DMSO was given as the sole agent, with no co-formulated or co-administered drug and with PBS as the comparator, so no second compound was available for enhanced delivery. An intrinsic immunomodulatory action of DMSO administered alone is moreover well documented, in the repression of inflammatory cytokine production by human blood cells,14 the suppression of bladder inflammation in vivo,17 the restriction of herpes simplex virus replication in vitro,18 and the STAT5-dependent expansion of regulatory T cells in non-obese diabetic mice.19 The present findings extend this intrinsic activity to the pulmonary CD8+ T cell response during influenza A virus infection. We cannot exclude that the membrane-permeabilising property of DMSO alters the distribution or handling of endogenous solutes, which warrants further study.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first investigation to evaluate the impact of DMSO on IAV infection in an in vivo animal model, addressing a gap in the literature. By integrating survival monitoring, histopathological assessment, viral titration and neuraminidase activity assay, sandwich ELISA cytokine profiling, flow cytometry, and high-throughput RNA sequencing, we provide a comprehensive overview of how DMSO fine-tunes the CD8+ T cell axis through modulation of cell-cycle, metabolic, and effector regulators, including Nek kinases and the PI3K/Akt signaling axis. The precise identification of DMSO’s localized effect within the lung, rather than the systemic splenic environment, also offers important insights into its selective immunomodulatory mechanism.

Several limitations warrant consideration. (i) BALF viral titers were measured on day 8 post-infection and were significantly lower in DMSO-treated mice, indicating that the improved outcome is not attributable to modulation of the host response at an equivalent viral burden alone; the present data cannot, however, separate a direct antiviral effect of DMSO from accelerated immune-mediated viral clearance. (ii) CD8 antibody depletion and Cd8a–/– or Ifng–/– genetic studies were not performed; the causal contribution of CD8+ T cells to the survival benefit cannot be formally inferred from the present data and is presented as a working model only. (iii) Only a single viral strain (A/PR/8/34, H1N1) was tested, so cross-protection against H3N2 / H5N1 strains remains an open question. (iv) Bulk RNA-seq cannot resolve which cell types drive the pathway-level signals; single-cell RNA-seq is planned. (v) The study was conducted exclusively in a C57BL/6 mouse model; immunological differences between mice and humans necessitate caution when extrapolating to clinical settings. (vi) Although we observed enhanced IFN-γ secretion and augmented CD8+ T cell activity, the precise intracellular molecular triggers and direct receptor-binding targets of DMSO remain to be fully elucidated. (vii) The in vitro LAG3 kinetic assay used splenic CD8+ T cells, whereas the DMSO-associated expansion in vivo was confined to the lung; extrapolating this in vitro splenic observation to a pulmonary phenomenon is a limitation, and confirmation in lung- or airway-derived CD8+ T cells is warranted. (viii) The in vitro DMSO concentrations (0.5% and 1.0%, approximately 70 and 140 mM) exceed systemic exposure in vivo, where plasma DMSO reaches only the low-millimolar range;40 the in vitro data should therefore be read as a concentration-amplified mechanistic probe rather than a model of the administered dose, and the response at physiologically achievable concentrations requires a dedicated dose-response study. (ix) Several mechanistic experiments (BALF ELISA, flow cytometry, and whole-lung RNA sequencing) were performed with n = 3 mice per group. This small sample size limits statistical precision and power and may estimate effect sizes imprecisely; the corresponding results should be regarded as exploratory and require confirmation in adequately powered cohorts.

Conclusion

In summary, daily subcutaneous DMSO is associated with improved survival and reduced pulmonary inflammation in a murine A/PR/8/34 (H1N1) IAV model. At day-8, viral burden in the airway was also lower in DMSO-treated mice, indicating that the benefit reflects a combination of reduced viral burden and modulation of the host response. This protective benefit co-occurs with an increased frequency of pulmonary IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells, alongside the up-regulation of PI3K/Akt and T-box transcriptional programs. Ultimately, these correlative findings support follow-up CD8 depletion or knock-out experiments to test causality, and motivate the evaluation of DMSO as a host-directed adjunct therapy for influenza.
