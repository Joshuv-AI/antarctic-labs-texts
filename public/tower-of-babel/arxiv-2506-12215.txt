Partial identification via conditional linear programs: estimation and policy learning
Eli Ben-Michael

Abstract
Many important quantities of interest are only partially identified from observable data: the data can limit them to a set of plausible values, but not uniquely determine them. This paper develops a unified framework for covariate-assisted estimation, inference, and decision making in partial identification problems where the parameter of interest satisfies a series of linear constraints, conditional on covariates. In such settings, bounds on the parameter can be written as expectations of solutions to conditional linear programs that optimize a linear function subject to linear constraints, where both the objective function and the constraints may depend on covariates and need to be estimated from data. Examples include estimands involving the joint distributions of potential outcomes, policy learning with inequality-aware value functions, and instrumental variable settings. We propose two de-biased estimators for bounds defined by conditional linear programs. The first directly solves the conditional linear programs with plugin estimates and uses output from standard LP solvers to de-bias the plugin estimate, avoiding the need for computationally demanding vertex enumeration of all possible solutions for symbolic bounds. The second uses entropic regularization to create smooth approximations to the conditional linear programs, trading a small amount of approximation error for improved estimation and computational efficiency. We establish conditions for asymptotic normality of both estimators, show that both estimators are robust to first-order errors in estimating the conditional constraints and objectives, and construct Wald-type confidence intervals for the partially identified parameters. These results also extend to policy learning problems where the value of a decision policy is only partially identified. We apply our methods to a study on the effects of Medicaid enrollment.



1
Introduction

In many real-world data settings, substantively important estimands are only partially identified from the observable data, meaning that even with an infinite amount of data, the estimand of interest can only be limited to be within a set of plausible values, rather than being uniquely determined.
For example, in causal inference settings it may not be possible to point-identify average treatment effects if treatment assignment is confounded; even with auxiliary information, such as an instrumental variable, the average treatment effect is not necessarily point identified.
There is also often interest in complex counterfactual estimands that go beyond average treatment effects, for instance the proportion of individuals who benefit from treatment or characteristics of the spread of treatment effects across the population.

This paper develops a unified framework for covariate-assisted estimation, inference, and decision making in a broad class of partial identification problems where the parameter of interest satisfies a series of linear constraints.
In such settings, bounds for the parameter of interest can be characterized as solutions to conditional linear programs (LPs) that optimize a linear function of unknown parameters subject to linear constraints, where both the objective function and the constraints may depend on additional covariates and need to be estimated.
This encompasses a wide range of settings; as illustrative examples we consider estimands related to the joint distribution of potential outcomes, policy learning with inequality-aware value functions, and instrumental variable settings.

We develop two de-biased estimators of bounds on the parameter of interest that are robust to errors in estimating the nuisance functions that define the objective and constraints.
The first approach directly solves the optimization problem using plugin estimates of the conditional constraints and objectives and leverages properties of optimal solutions of LPs to de-bias these estimates using the output of standard LP solvers. In contrast to prior work, this estimator does not require derivation of analytical solutions or enumeration of all possible solutions, which can be computationally intractable for large problems.
The second approach leverages recent advances in the theory of regularized linear programs to create smooth approximations to the conditional LPs using entropic regularization and estimates the smoothed bounds in a de-biased manner.
This approach trades off a small amount of approximation error for gains in estimation and computational efficiency.
In contrast to alternative smoothing schemes, entropic regularization introduces approximation error that rapidly decays with the choice of hyper-parameter, and, crucially, does not require explicit enumeration of all feasible solutions.

For both estimators, we establish the conditions for asymptotic normality and construct standard, Wald-type approximate confidence intervals for the partially identified parameter.
While these de-biased estimators depend on the estimates of the covariate-conditional constraints and objectives, they are robust to first-order errors in estimating these nuisance functions, allowing for valid inference even when the nuisance functions are estimated using flexible machine learning methods.
We also extend these results to decision-making problems where the goal is to estimate an optimal decision policy that optimizes the lower or upper bound of a partially identified quantity.
We conduct simulation studies to demonstrate finite-sample properties of the estimators, and apply them to a study of the effects of Medicaid enrollment on health outcomes using data from the Oregon health insurance experiment
(
Finkelstein et al., 2012
;
Taubman et al., 2014
)
.

Related literature.

There is a large literature on identification and estimation of partially identified parameters.
This paper builds on three key strands of this literature.
First, one line of work focuses on derivation of analytic or symbolic bounds
(
Balke and Pearl, 1994
;
Balke and Pearl, 1997
;
Sachs et al., 2023
;
Gabriel et al., 2024
, e.g.)
.
This work typically characterizes sharp bounds on partially identified parameters as solutions to linear programs, the solutions to which lie in the (convex hulls of the) vertices of the feasible set defined by the constraints.
These approaches then enumerate all such vertices to obtain the bounds.
Because the number of vertices can grow combinatorial with the size of the problem, this approach can be computationally intractable for larger problems, especially if the bounds must be computed for each value of the covariates. The approaches we develop do not require vertex enumeration and so can computationally scale to larger problems.

Duarte et al., 2024
extend these ideas to map generic partial identification problems into polynomial programs. Rather than provide analytical bounds, they use dual branch-and-bound methods to provide valid bounds on the parameter of interest.
The approach we outline is in this spirit in that the estimation procedure arrives at the bounds computationally, rather than analytically.
However, by focusing on the more limited class of problems that can be characterized as linear programs, we are able to provide de-biased estimators that are robust to first-order errors in estimating the relevant nuisance functions, and allow for the construction of straightforward Wald-type asymptotic confidence intervals.

The second line of work focuses on de-biased, covariate-assisted estimation and inference for partially identified parameters. Some of this work begins with analytical bounds on the parameter of interest (often derived using the symbolic enumeration methods above) and then develops de-biased estimators that use aggregations of conditional versions of those bounds
(
Kallus, 2022
;
Levis et al., 2023
;
Semenova, 2024
;
Ben-Michael et al., 2024a
, e.g.)
.
Some work also considers targeting smooth approximations to these bounds
(
Levis et al., 2023
;
Lanners et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.
Again, the methods we develop here differ in that they do not require analytical bounds, nor do they require enumeration of all possible solutions.

Ji et al., 2024
consider estimating covariate-assisted bounds on estimands involving the joint distribution of potential outcomes
(
Jiang and Janson, 2025
, see also)
.
Unlike the approaches we develop here, this work allows for continuous potential outcomes; however, we consider a general setup that allows for multi-level treatments, instrumental variables, and other forms of partial identification problems.
An important question is whether and when conditioning on covariates information leads to narrower or sharp bounds, which depends on specifics of the problem at hand
(
Jonzon et al., 2025
)
.
In this paper, we sidestep the question of whether the bounds are sharp on focus on estimation and inference.

A third line of work focuses on learning optimal decision rules in settings where the value of a decision policy is only partially identified. This work builds on results on ideas in decision theory
(
Manski, 2004
;
Manski, 2005
, e.g.)
, and considers settings where there are unmeasured confounding variables, treatment-control overlap violations, or the value function involves counterfactual quantities
(
Kallus and Zhou, 2021
;
Pu and Zhang, 2021
;
Han, 2021
;
Cui, 2021
;
Ben-Michael et al., 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Ben-Michael et al., 2024b
;
D’Adamo, 2023
, e.g.)
.
The approach to policy learning here builds directly on this work and generalizes to a broad class of problems.

2
Parameters partially identified by conditional linear programs

We begin by describing the generic estimand and associated upper and lower bounds defined by conditional linear programs.
We then discuss several specific examples of such estimands related to partially identified parameters in causal inference and policy learning settings.

2.1
Conditional linear programs

Consider a set of covariates
X\in\mathcal{X}
. Our goal will be to estimate a linear function of some true non-negative function
\bm{p}^{\ast}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}
defined by a function
\bm{c}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
, i.e.

\theta\equiv\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}^{\ast}(X)\rangle]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}(X)p_{k}^{\ast}(X)\right].

(1)

Our focus will be on settings where the objective vector
\bm{c}(x)
is not necessarily known but is point-identifiable from observable data while
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
is not necessarily point-identified for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Rather, we can only observe
J\leq K
linear functions of
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
, i.e.
A\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)=\bm{b}(x)
for some known matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
and some unknown function
\bm{b}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{J}
that is identifiable from observable data.
For each value of the covariates
x\in\mathcal{X}
, these linear constraints along with non-negativity imply that the true function
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
lies in a convex set
\mathcal{P}(x)=\{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{+}\mid A\bm{p}=\bm{b}(x)\}
, which we will refer to as the feasible set.
1
1

1

This framework also allows for inequality constraints by transforming an inequality-constrained linear program to the equality constrained standard form.

In many cases, the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(x)
contains more than one element (e.g. because there are more equations than unknowns), and
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
is not point-identified.
This means that the parameter of interest
\theta
may also not be point identified and so it may not be possible to estimate it precisely. We will instead focus on finding lower and upper bounds
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{U}
such that
\theta\in[\theta_{L},\theta_{U}]
.
To construct these, we define
X
-conditional linear programs that minimize and maximize the linear function of
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
over the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(x)
:

\theta_{L}(x)=\min_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(x),\bm{p}\rangle\qquad\theta_{U}(x)=\max_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(x),\bm{p}\rangle.

(2)

The overall bounds are then the expected solutions to the conditional linear programs, i.e.
\theta_{L}=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(X)]
and
\theta_{U}=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{U}(X)]
.
In order for these bounds to be meaningful, we will assume that the the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(x)
is both not empty and bounded for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and that
\|\bm{b}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, where for a vector-valued function
\bm{f}(x)
, we use denote
\|\bm{f}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\sup_{x}|f_{i}(x)|
. To simplify the exposition, we will also assume that the constraint matrix
A
is sufficiently pre-processed to remove redundant constraints and so has full row rank equal to
J
.
Finally, note that we have not ruled out that
\theta
is point identified even though the function
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
is not point identified. In such cases the lower and upper bounds match, i.e.
\theta_{L}=\theta_{U}=\theta
.
As an additional bit of notation, for a scalar-valued function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
we will denote
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{2}]
, and for a vector-valued function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
we will denote
\|\bm{f}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(X)^{2}]=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|f_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
.

2.2
Examples

Next, we describe several specific examples of estimands that can be expressed in this way. We will further explore these in our empirical example in Section
5.2
.

Functions of the joint distributions of potential outcomes.

Consider a setting where we are interested in understanding the causal relationship between a discrete treatment variable
D\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
on a discrete outcome variable
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
where
|\mathcal{Y}|=L
. For example,
D
may represent a multi-level treatment or different doses.
We posit the existence of
M
potential outcomes
(Y(0),\ldots,Y(M-1))
corresponding to the outcome that would be observed under treatment
d
.
In this setting, our goal is to estimate some moment of the joint distribution of potential outcomes, i.e.
\theta=\mathbb{E}[f(Y(0),\ldots,Y(M-1);X)]
for some function
f:\mathcal{Y}^{M}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. We observe iid data
(X,D,\{\mathbbm{1}\{D=d\}Y(d)\}_{d\in[M]})
from some distribution
P
.

Using the tower property, we can write the estimand as

\theta=E\left[\sum_{y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};X)P(Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(M-1)=y_{M-1}\mid X)\right].

(3)

In this case the unknown function
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
corresponds to the joint distribution of the potential outcomes given that the covariates
X=x
, and the function
\bm{c}(x)
is a vector collecting the function values
f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};X)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)

\displaystyle=(p(Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(M-1)=y_{M-1}\mid X=x)\mid y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1}\in\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\bm{c}(x)

\displaystyle=(f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};x)\mid y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1}\in\mathcal{Y}).

For example,
f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};x)=y_{d}-y_{d}^{\prime}
would correspond to the average treatment effect of
d
versus
d^{\prime}
—which is identified—and
f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};x)=\max_{d}y_{d}
would correspond to average outcome under an oracle treatment assignment rule that maximizes the potential outcome—this is not necessarily identified.
Another unidentified parameter is the proportion of individuals who are not assigned an optimal treatment, i.e.
P(Y\neq\max_{d}Y(d))
, which could correspond to the proportion of individuals who are harmed by the existing treatment assignment mechanism. This corresponds to choosing
f(y_{0},\ldots,y_{M-1};x)=\sum_{d^{\prime}}P(D=d^{\prime}\mid X=x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}\}
.
Note that if the propensity score
P(D=d^{\prime}\mid X=x)
is unknown, then the objective vector
\bm{c}(x)
is also unknown but can be estimated.

Under the standard strong ignorability assumption that there is treatment overlap and the potential outcomes are independent of the treatment given the covariates
(
Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983
)
, the marginal distribution of each potential outcome given the covariates is identified as
P(Y(d)=y_{\ell}\mid X=x)=P(Y=y_{\ell}\mid D=d,X=x)
for all
d\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
and
\ell\in\mathcal{Y}
. This gives a set of marginal constraints on the conditional joint distribution of potential outcomes:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{0},\ldots,y_{d-1},\\
y_{d+1},\ldots,y_{M-1}\in\mathcal{Y}\end{subarray}}P(Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(d)=y_{\ell},\ldots Y(M-1)=y_{M-1}\mid X=x)=P(Y=y_{\ell}\mid D=d,X=x).

Since the probabilities sum to one,
each margin gives
L-1
constraints. There is also an overall sum-to-one constraint on the joint distribution. This leads to a total of
J=(L-1)M+1
constraints, on
K=L^{M}
variables (the number elements in the joint distribution) which can be collected into an integral constraint matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{J\times K}
. The vector corresponding to the right hand side of the constraints,
\bm{b}(x)
, consists of the marginal probability distributions for each potential outcome, as well as a sum-to-one constraint.

Policy learning with collective utility functions.

An important special case of the above example is when we have a binary treatment
D\in\{0,1\}
and we want to learn a decision policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
that assigns
D=1
with probability
\pi(X)
and achieves some notion of optimality based off a utility function
u:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
that assigns a utility to an outcome
y
of
u(y)
.
2
2

2

It is also possible incorporate costs into the utility function.

We can write the expected utility for an individual under decision policy
\pi
as
\pi(X)\times u(Y(1))+(1-\pi(X))\times u(Y(0))
.

To choose a policy
\pi
, we can use
power-law collective utility functions
that aggregate the individual utilities into a single number
(
Moulin, 1988
)
:

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}(\pi)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\left\{U(Y(0))+\pi(X)(U(Y(1))-U(Y(0)))\right\}^{\lambda}-1\right)\right].

(4)

Here the value of the power-law parameter
\lambda
determines the level of emphasis placed on individuals with lower or higher utilities under
\pi
. When
\lambda=1
, the value function is simply the expected individual utility under
\pi
:
V^{1}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[U(Y(0))+\pi(X)(U(Y(1))-U(Y(0)))]
.
Values of
\lambda<1
will lead to inequality-averse value functions.
When
\lambda=0
, the value function is the logarithm of the geometric mean of the individual utilities:
V^{0}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\log(U(Y(0))+\pi(X)(U(Y(1))-U(Y(0)))]
. As
\lambda\to-\infty
,
V^{\lambda}(\pi)
goes towards prioritizing only the individuals with the smallest utilities under
\pi
; choosing
\pi
to maximize this results in the
egalitarian rule
that maximizes the minimum utility.
Finally, values of
\lambda>1
will lead to inequality-seeking value functions.
Note that when
\lambda\neq 1
, the power-law value function is not point-identifiable from the observed data.

To create a notion of optimality, we can compare the value under the policy
\pi
to the value under an infeasible oracle policy that has access to the potential outcomes and assigns the treatment that maximizes the individual utility, i.e.
\mathbbm{1}\{u(Y(1))\geq u(Y(0))\}
.
The
regret
of policy
\pi
relative to the oracle is thus:

R^{\lambda}(\pi)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\max\{u(y_{0}),u(y_{1})\}^{\lambda}-\left\{u(y_{0})+\pi(X)(u(y_{1})-u(y_{0}))\right\}^{\lambda}\right)P(Y(0)=y_{0},Y(1)=y_{1}\mid X)\right].

This is a special case of Equation (
3
) with
M=2
and
f(y_{0},y_{1};x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\max\{u(y_{0}),u(y_{1})\}^{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(u(y_{0})+\pi(x)(u(y_{1})-u(y_{0}))\right)^{\lambda}
.
We will discuss finding optimal policies
\pi
that minimize the maximum regret or maximize the minimum value in Section
4
.

Instrumental variables.

Now consider the case of a binary treatment
D
and a binary instrumental variable
Z\in\{0,1\}
that affects takeup of the treatment, where the outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
still contains
L
distinct levels. The instrument leads to two potential treatments
D(0)
and
D(1)
. Under an exclusion restriction that the instrument does not have a direct effect on the outcome variable, we then have two potential outcomes
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
.
In this case, we are interested in estimating a property of the joint distribution of potential treatments and potential outcomes
\theta=\mathbb{E}\left[f(Y(0),Y(1),D(0),D(1))\right]
for some function
f:\mathcal{Y}^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
The observable data consists of iid samples of
(X,Z,(1-Z)D(0),ZD(1),(1-D(Z))Y(0),D(Z)Y(1))
.

We can write the estimand as

\displaystyle\theta=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{d_{0},d_{1}=0}^{1}f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1};X)P(Y(0)=y_{0},Y(1)=y_{1},D(0)=d_{0},D(1)=d_{1}\mid X)\right].

Now the unknown function
\bm{p}^{\ast}(x)
corresponds to the joint distribution of the potential outcomes and potential treatments given the covariates
X=x
, and the function
\bm{c}(x)
is a vector that collects the function
f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1};X)
, i.e.
\bm{c}(x)=(f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1})\mid y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y},d_{0},d_{1}\in\{0,1\})
. For example,
f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1};x)=y_{1}-y_{0}
would again correspond to the average treatment effect, while
f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1};x)=\mathbbm{1}\{d_{0}<d_{1}\}
would correspond to the proportion of compliers.
Another example is the proportion of individuals who are not assigned an optimal treatment under the treatment distribution induced by a particular level of the instrument:
P(Y(D(z))\neq\max_{d}Y(d))
. This corresponds to choosing
f(y_{0},y_{1},d_{0},d_{1};x)=\sum_{d^{\prime}}P(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,X=x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}\}
.

Under ignorable assignment of the instrument, the joint distribution of the observed treatment and outcome satisfies

P(Y=y,D=d\mid Z=z,X)=\sum_{y_{1-d}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{d_{1-z}=0}^{1}P(Y(d)=y,Y(1-d)=y_{1-d},D(z)=d,D(1-z)=d_{1-z}\mid X).

Accounting for the sum-to-one constraints gives
J=4(L-1)+1
constraints with
K=4L^{2}
decision variables.

Here the constraint matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{J\times K}
encodes the marginal constraints and the vector
\bm{b}(x)
encodes the conditional joint distribution of the observed outcome and treatment variables along with the sum to one constraint.

3
Estimation

We now turn to estimating the lower and upper bounds
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{U}
.
Throughout, we will assume that we have iid samples of random variables
O
that include the covariates
X
as well as variables that give information about the constraint and objective vectors, respectively.
Our principle assumption is that we can estimate the constraint vector
\bm{b}(x)
and the objective vector
\bm{c}(x)
in a de-biased manner using the information contained in the observed data
O
.

Assumption 1
.

For each constraint
j=1,\ldots,J
there exists a de-biasing function
\varphi^{(b)}_{j}(O;\bm{b})

such that
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{(b)}_{j}(O;b_{j})\mid X]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O;b_{j})^{2}]<\infty
, and for any bounded function
\bar{\bm{b}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{J}
,
\mathbb{E}[\bar{b}_{j}(X)+\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O;\bar{b}_{j})\mid X=x]=b_{j}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Similarly, for each decision variable
k=1,\ldots,K
there exists a de-biasing function
\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})
such that
\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{(c)}_{k}(O;c_{k})\mid X]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})^{2}]<\infty
, and for any bounded function
\bar{\bm{c}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
\mathbb{E}[\bar{c}_{k}(X)+\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;\bar{c}_{k})\mid X=x]=c_{k}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

This structure allows us to estimate the lower and upper bounds in a de-biased manner that will be robust to errors in estimating the constraint and objective vectors.
We will assume that we have estimates of the constraint and objective vectors
\hat{\bm{b}}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\bm{c}}(\cdot)
, along with estimates of the de-biasing functions
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(\cdot)
, that are fit on a separate sample. When it is clear from context we will use

\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O)\equiv\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O,b_{j})
and
\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O)\equiv\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O,c_{k})
to denote the true de-biasing function using the true constraint or objective functions, and

\hat{\varphi}_{j}^{(b)}(O)\equiv\hat{\varphi}_{j}^{(b)}(O,\hat{b}_{j})
and
\hat{\varphi}_{k}^{(c)}(O)\equiv\hat{\varphi}_{k}^{(c)}(O,\hat{c}_{k})
to denote the estimated de-biasing function using the estimated constraint or objective function.
4
4

4

Our analysis can also be straightforwardly extended to cross-fit estimates, which we use in practice to avoid losing sample size due to sample splitting.

We assume that the estimates of the constraint and objective vectors and their de-biasing functions are consistent, and that the rate of convergence for de-biased estimates of linear functions of the constraint and objective vectors is controlled by a rate
r_{n}
that depends on the sample size.

Assumption 2
.

For each
j=1,\ldots,J
and
k=1,\ldots,K
,
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\hat{b}_{j}(x)-b_{j}(x)|=o_{p}(1)
,
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\hat{c}_{k}(x)-c_{k}(x)|=o_{p}(1)
,
\sup_{o\in\mathcal{O}}|\hat{\varphi}_{j}^{(b)}(o)-\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(o)|=o_{p}(1)
, and
\sup_{o\in\mathcal{O}}|\hat{\varphi}_{k}^{(c)}(o)-\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(o)|=o_{p}(1)
. Furthermore, there is some rate
r_{n}
such that for any linear function of
\bm{b}(X)
defined by
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{J}
, we have

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{f}(X),\hat{\bm{b}}(X)+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O)-\bm{b}(X)\rangle\right]\right|\lesssim\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\bm{f}(x)\|_{2}r_{n},

and for any linear function of
\bm{c}(X)
defined by
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we have

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{f}(X),\hat{\bm{c}}(X)+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O)-\bm{c}(X)\rangle\right]\right|\lesssim\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\bm{f}(x)\|_{2}r_{n},

where the notation
\lesssim
means that the left hand side is bounded by a constant multiple of the right.

In particular, as we discuss in examples in Section
3.4
, if
\bm{b}(\cdot)
and
\bm{c}(\cdot)
are conditional expectation functions, then the de-biasing functions can be written in terms of weighted residuals of the form
\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O;b_{j})=g_{j}^{(b)}(O;b_{j})(Y_{j}-b_{j}(X))
and
\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})=g_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})(C_{k}-c_{k}(X))
where
Y_{j}
and
C_{k}
are elements of
O
and
g_{j}^{(b)}(\cdot)
,
g_{k}^{(c)}(\cdot)
are the corresponding Riesz representers.
To estimate the de-biasing functions, we can estimate the Riesz representers, giving a double robust or double machine learning-style estimate
(
Robins et al., 1994
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
, e.g.)
. In this case the rates will depend on the product of the errors in the estimates of the constraints
\bm{b}(\cdot)
and objectives
\bm{c}(\cdot)
with the errors in their respective Riesz representers—e.g.
r_{n}=\max\{\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}\|\hat{\bm{g}}^{(b)}-\bm{g}^{(b)}\|_{2},\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}\|\hat{\bm{g}}^{(b)}-\bm{g}^{(b)}\|_{2}\}
where
\hat{\bm{g}}^{(b)}
and
\hat{\bm{g}}^{(c)}
are estimates of the Riesz representers.
5
5

5

In principle the rates for the constraint and objective vectors can be different; to simplify the exposition we will use the maximum of the two rates.

More broadly, such de-biasing functions can be constructed by deriving efficient influence functions for the expected constraint and objective vectors.

3.1
De-biased estimation using basic feasible solutions

To begin, we will re-frame the conditional linear programs in Equation
2
as discrete optimization problems.
Because the matrix of marginal constraints
A\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
has rank equal to
J
, and the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(x)
is non-empty by assumption, any solution to this LP must lie on the boundary of the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(x)
.
In particular there exists a solution that is a
basic feasible solution
(BFS) with
J
active, non-negative variables and
K-J
inactive variables on the boundary at 0
(see
Klatt et al., 2022
, for a discussion related to the entropic regularized estimator below)
.

For a set of
J
unique indices
B=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{J}\}
, let
A_{B}
denote the sub-matrix of
A
that includes all rows and the
J
columns defined by
B
, and similarly for a vector
v
let
v_{B}
denote the sub-vector of
v
that includes the
J
elements defined by
B
. Then, a basic solution to the linear program is given by
\bm{p}_{B}=A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)
, and
p_{i}=0
for all
i\not\in B
. If
\bm{p}_{B}>0
, then it is also a basic
feasible solution
. Abusing notation, we will use
A_{B}^{-1}
to denote the re-ordered inverse matrix that places the rows of the inverse into
i_{1},\ldots,i_{J}
, and sets the other rows equal to zero, so that
p=A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)
is the BFS corresponding to the basis
B
.

We can then write the LPs in Equation (
2
) as optimization problems over
\mathcal{B}
, the set of basic feasible solutions. Letting

\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x)=\text{argmin}_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle
and
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{U}(x)=\text{argmax}_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle
denote the sets of optimal bases for the conditional minimization and maximization problems respectively, which may not be unique, we can write the bounds in Equation (
2
) as

\displaystyle\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(x),A^{-1}_{B^{\ast}_{L}(X)}\bm{b}(x)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\theta_{U}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(x),A^{-1}_{B^{\ast}_{U}(X)}\bm{b}(x)\rangle\right]

where
B^{\ast}_{L}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(x)
and
B^{\ast}_{U}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{U}^{\ast}(x)
are any particular optimal bases for the conditional minimization and maximization problems, respectively.

If we knew these true optimal bases
B_{L}^{\ast}(x)
and
B_{U}^{\ast}(x)
for each value of the covariates
x
, then we could estimate the upper and lower bounds
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{U}
using de-biased estimators, since they are expectations of bi-linear forms of the nuisance functions
\bm{b}(x)
and
\bm{c}(x)
.
However, this estimator is infeasible because we do not know the sets of optimal bases.
Instead, we will use a
plug-in
approach, where we estimate the optimal bases using the estimates of the conditional constraints
\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
and the conditional objective
\hat{\bm{c}}(x)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{L}(x)

\displaystyle\in\text{argmin}_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(x)\rangle

(5)

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{U}(x)

\displaystyle\in\text{argmax}_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(x)\rangle,

Given the plugin estimates of the optimal bases
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
and
\widehat{B}_{U}(x)
and their corresponding plugin estimates of the basic feasible solutions
\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}(x)\equiv A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})
and
\hat{\bm{p}}_{U}(x)\equiv A_{\widehat{B}_{U}(X_{i})}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})
, we will proceed with estimating the lower and upper bounds
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{U}
using de-biased BFS estimators as if the optimal bases were known:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}(X_{i})\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right\rangle

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{U}(X_{i})\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{U}(X_{i})}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right\rangle.

We note that written this way, this estimator is a form of the covariate-assisted intersection bounds estimator proposed by
Semenova, 2024
. However, even though there are potentially
\binom{K}{J}
possible bases, we need not perform an exhaustive search over all possibilities.
Indeed, we need not even enumerate nor analytically characterize the set of bases
\mathcal{B}
.
Instead, we can use the simplex algorithm
(
Dantzig, 1951
)
to determine an optimal basic feasible solution, which has polynomial run-time in typical cases and is efficient in practice, though it has exponential run-time in the worst-case
(
Spielman and Teng, 2004
)
.

To recover the optimal basis corresponding to the solution
\hat{\bm{p}}(x)
, we simply keep track of which values are non-zero, i.e.
\widehat{B}(x)=\{i\mid\hat{p}_{i}(x)>0\}
.
This provides a substantial computational speedup over analytic vertex enumeration approaches that require enumerating all possible bases.
Note, however, that we must run the simplex algorithm for each unique value of the covariates
x
in the sample, potentially up to
n
times.

Below we will focus on aspects of this problem pertinent to LPs and the entropic estimator we propose below.

The quality of the plug-in estimates of the optimal bases will depend on the difficulty of picking out an optimal basis from the set of all possible bases. To quantify this, we will define the
sub-optimality gaps

\Delta_{L}(x)
and
\Delta_{U}(x)
as the minimum difference between an optimal value of the conditional LP and the value of the LP at any other non-optimal vertex of the constraint set
\mathcal{P}(x)
. This can be written in terms of the non-optimal bases
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{U}^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}b(x)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle

(7)

\displaystyle\Delta_{U}(x)

\displaystyle=\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{U}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B^{\ast}_{U}(x)}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle.

It may be that all feasible solutions may be optimal so that
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{U}=\emptyset
, implying that the parameter of interest is point-identified at that value of the covariates.
For such cases, any choice of basis will suffice, and we will define the sub-optimality gaps to be
\Delta_{L}(x)=\Delta_{U}(x)=0
.
We then make the following
margin condition
assumption about the distribution of the sub-optimality gaps across the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 3

(Margin condition)
.

For some
\alpha>0
,
P\left(0<\Delta_{L}(X)\leq t\right)\lesssim t^{\alpha}
and
P\left(0<\Delta_{U}(X)\leq t\right)\lesssim t^{\alpha}
.

The
margin parameter

\alpha
determines the difficulty of the problem. If
\alpha
is small then there is a high probability across the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
that there are suboptimal bases that are close to the optimal ones; if
\alpha
is large then this is less likely.
Margin conditions such as Assumption
3
have been used to analyze the performance of plugin estimators in classification settings
(
Audibert and Tsybakov, 2007
)
, estimators for classification in causal inference settings
(
Luedtke and Van Der Laan, 2016
;
Kennedy et al., 2020
)
, and estimating bounds on partially identified parameters as we do here
(
Levis et al., 2023
;
D’Adamo, 2023
;
Semenova, 2024
)
. Note that the definition of the sub-optimality gaps and the margin condition do not require that the optimal bases are unique. They also allow for the possibility that the parameter of interest is point-identified for some or all values of the covariates and so
0<P(\Delta_{L}(X)=0)\leq 1
.

With the margin condition, we can link the quality of the plugin estimates of the optimal bases to the quality of the estimates of the nuisance functions both in terms of the probability of making a misclassification (i.e. picking the wrong basis) and the regret (i.e. the sub-optimality gap) of the plugin estimates.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
3
, if
\hat{\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\bm{c}}
are fit on a separate, independent sample, then the probabilities that the plugin basic feasible solutions are incorrect are bounded by

P\left(\widehat{B}_{L}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(X)\right)\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{\alpha}\qquad P\left(\widehat{B}_{U}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{U}^{\ast}(X)\right)\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{\alpha},

and the regrets of the plugin basic feasible solutions are bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B^{\ast}_{U}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{\widehat{B}_{U}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{1+\alpha}

Lemma
1
shows that the probability of selecting the wrong basis shrinks with the estimation error of the nuisance functions as measured by the sup-norm. In particular, the regrets of the plugin basic feasible solutions decrease at a faster rate than the estimation error of the nuisance functions by a factor of the margin parameter
\alpha
. Roughly, this is because in cases where the optimal bases are mis-classified the sub-optimality gap must be smaller than the estimation error in the nuisance functions.
Note that while Lemma
1
is stated in terms of the sup-norm, here and below it can be extended to other norms using the arguments in
Audibert and Tsybakov, 2007
.

If the optimal bases were known, then the estimates
\hat{\theta}_{L}
and
\hat{\theta}_{U}
would be standard, de-biased estimators of the lower and upper bounds.
However, since we do not know the optimal bases, we will need to account for the estimation error in the plug-in estimates of the optimal bases by using Lemma
1
.
The following theorem gives the rate of convergence of the lower bound estimator
\hat{\theta}_{L}
; the properties of the upper bound are analogous.

Theorem 1
.

Define
\tilde{\theta}_{L}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bm{c}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\left(\bm{b}(X_{i})+\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\bm{b}(X_{i})\right\rangle
.
Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, if
\hat{\bm{b}}
,
\hat{\bm{c}}
,
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}
and
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}
are fit on a separate, independent sample,

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}_{L}]+O_{p}\left(\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{1+\alpha}+r_{n}+\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}\right).

Theorem
1
shows that the estimation error
\hat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{L}
is equal to a mean-zero term
\tilde{\theta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}_{L}]
plus additional bias terms that depend on the estimation error of the nuisance functions
\hat{\bm{b}}
,
\hat{\bm{c}}
and the de-biased rate
r_{n}
.
The first bias term is due to the sub-optimality of using plugin estimates of an optimal basis
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
relative to a true optimal basis
B_{L}^{\ast}(x)
, which we have controlled in Lemma
1
, the next term comes from de-biasing, and the final term involves cross terms between the estimation errors of the constraint and objective vectors that arise from the bi-linear form of the estimator.

If the estimates of the nuisance functions converge quickly enough—though potentially slower than parametric rates—then the estimate of the upper and lower bounds will be asymptotically normally distributed, allowing for asymptotic inference.
Again we state the result for the lower bound, but the upper bound is analogous.

Corollary 1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
, if
\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{1+\alpha}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
,

\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, and
r_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
,

then
\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{L}\right)}{\sqrt{V_{L}}}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
, where

V_{L}\equiv\text{Var}\left(\left\langle\bm{c}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\left(\bm{b}(X_{i})+\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\bm{b}(X_{i})\right\rangle\right)
.

While we have stated the conditions generally to allow for the case where either the constraint or objective vectors are known, a sufficient condition is that the estimation error for the constraint and objective vectors is
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
.

Under the conditions of Corollary
1
, the estimation error
\hat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{L}
is asymptotically equivalent to a centered version of the de-biased estimator using the plugin estimates of the optimal basis
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
,
\tilde{\theta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}_{L}]
.
If there are multiple optimal bases, then the asymptotic variance involves the particular basis that is chosen. This corresponds to a particular vertex of the optimal face of the constraint set
\mathcal{P}(x)
, which could depend on particulars of the algorithm used, such as the choice of pivot rule in the simplex method.
This is particularly relevant when
\Delta_{L}(x)=0
and the parameter is still point-identified: the value will be a known bi-linear function of the constraint and objective vectors,
\langle\bm{c}(x),A^{\prime}(AA^{\prime})^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle
, and so selecting only one basis to compute the value as
\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle
may result in higher variance.
This is why we define the variance in terms of the estimated basis
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
, rather than a true optimal basis
B_{L}^{\ast}(x)
.
Because the constraint and objective vectors
\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
and
\hat{\bm{c}}(x)
are estimated from a separate sample, we can treat the estimated basis as a fixed function of the covariates
x
.

If for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
there is only one unique solution (i.e.
|\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x)|=1
), then the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\theta}_{L}
involves the true optimal basis.

Corollary 2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
, if
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x)=\{B_{L}^{\ast}(x)\}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{L}-\theta_{L}\right)}{\sqrt{V_{L}}}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
, where
V_{L}\equiv\text{Var}\left(\left\langle\bm{c}(X_{i}),A_{B_{L}^{\ast}(X_{i})}\left(\bm{b}(X_{i})+\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(Z_{i})\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(Z_{i}),A_{B_{L}^{\ast}(X_{i})}\bm{b}(X_{i})\right\rangle\right)
.

To use Corollaries
1
and
2
to construct confidence intervals, we estimate the variances for the lower and upper bounds with

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\left(\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i})}\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})\right\rangle-\hat{\theta}_{L}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{U}(X_{i})}\left(\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),A_{\widehat{B}_{U}(X_{i})}\hat{\bm{b}}(X_{i})\right\rangle-\hat{\theta}_{U}\right)^{2}.

Following
Imbens and Manski, 2004
, we can construct a Wald-type approximate
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for the partially identified parameter
\theta
by combining one-sided confidence intervals for the lower and upper bounds as
\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}-z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{L}}{n}},\hat{\theta}_{U}+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{U}}{n}}\right]
, where
z_{1-\alpha}
is the
1-\alpha
quantile of a standard normal distribution.

3.2
Estimating bounds with entropic regularization

Due to the non-smoothness of the conditional linear programs, small errors in either the constraint or objective vectors can lead to large changes in the optimal solution of the conditional LP.
This is visible in Theorem
1
, where the bias of the de-biased BFS estimator depends on the worst-case estimation error across the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
, though the effect is blunted with a more stringent margin condition.
As an alternative, we propose to target a smoothed version of the estimand that adds an entropy penalty to the conditional linear program.
As we will see, this will induce some approximation error, but we will typically be able to tune the level of regularization so that the approximation error will be small relative to the standard error of the estimator.

To do so, we regularize the conditional linear program.
For a vector
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
we
define the solutions to the
entropic conditional linear programs
as

\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(x)=\underset{A\bm{p}=\bm{b}(x)}{\text{argmin}}\;\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}\rangle+\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(\log p_{k}-1)\;\text{ and }\;\bm{p}^{\eta}_{U}(x)=\underset{A\bm{p}=\bm{b}(x)}{\text{argmax}}\;\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}\rangle-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(\log p_{k}-1)

(8)

We then define the
entropic regularized lower and upper bounds
as the expected value using the entropic solutions:

\displaystyle\theta_{L}^{\eta}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\right\rangle\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\theta_{U}^{\eta}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{U}^{\eta}(X)\right\rangle\right].

The hyperparameter
\eta>0
controls the level of regularization. As
\eta\to 0
, the entropy penalty in Equation (
8
) dominates the objective function, and so the entropic solutions
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(x)
and
\bm{p}_{U}^{\eta}(x)
will converge to the maximum entropy distribution that satisfies the constraints
A\bm{p}=\bm{b}(x)
. In this case, the lower and upper bounds will be equal,
\theta_{L}^{0}=\theta_{U}^{0}
, and correspond to point-identifying the estimand
\theta
by making a maximum entropy assumption. In the potential outcomes setting from Section
2.2
, sending
\eta\to 0
corresponds to identifying
\theta
by assuming that the potential outcomes are mutually independent given the covariates.
On the other hand, as
\eta\to\infty
, the entropy penalty becomes negligible relative to the objective function, and so the entropic solutions
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(x)
and
\bm{p}_{U}^{\eta}(x)
will converge to the solutions of the conditional linear programs in Equation
2
. In this way,
\eta
can act as a sensitivity parameter, moving smoothly between the bounds under no additional assumptions and the point-identified case under the maximum entropy assumption.

There are several benefits of using the entropic regularized bounds in place of solving the linear program as in Section
3.1
.
The computational complexity required to solve the conditional LPs can scale unfavorably with aspects of the problem.
For example, when estimating bounds on moments of the joint distribution of potential outcomes as in Section
2.2
, there are
L^{M}
primal variables and
(L-1)M+1
constraints, where
L
is the number of unique levels of the outcome and
M
is the number of unique levels of the treatment. Because the conditional LPs need to be solved for each data point, this can lead to a large computational burden if the number of outcome levels or especially the number of treatment levels is large.
In certain problems such as estimating quantities involving the joint distribution of potential outcomes with a binary treatment, entropic regularization can lead to significant computational speedups by using the
Sinkhorn, 1967
algorithm
(
Cuturi, 2013
)
.

Furthermore, the regularizer is strongly convex, so there exists a unique solution to both the lower and upper entropic bounds for all values of the covariates
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and the bounds are smooth functions of the nuisance parameters
\bm{b}(x)
and
\bm{c}(x)
.
In particular, by taking the Lagrangian dual of the conditional entropic linear programs (
8
), for any constraint and objective vector
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we can write the solutions as
\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(\bm{b},\bm{c})=\exp\left(-A^{\prime}\bm{\lambda}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})+\eta\bm{c}\right)
and
\bm{p}^{\eta}_{U}(\bm{b},\bm{c})=\exp\left(A^{\prime}\bm{\lambda}_{U}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})+\eta\bm{c}\right)
, where the dual variables
\bm{\lambda}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
and
\bm{\lambda}_{U}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
are the solutions to the entropic regularized dual problems:

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}^{\eta}_{L}(\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\lambda}}{\text{argmin}}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\langle A_{\cdot k},\bm{\lambda}\rangle+\eta c_{k}\right)+\langle\bm{\lambda},\bm{b}\rangle

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}^{\eta}_{U}(\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\lambda}}{\text{argmin}}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(\langle A_{\cdot k},\bm{\lambda}\rangle+\eta c_{k}\right)-\langle\bm{\lambda},\bm{b}\rangle,

where
A_{\cdot k}
denotes the
k
th
column of the matrix
A
.

This removes the ambiguity of multiple solutions, and allows us to construct a de-biased estimator using standard semi-parametric theory
because the dual variables are an implicitly defined function of the nuisance parameters
\bm{b}(x)
and
\bm{c}(x)
,
sidestepping the
issue of selecting an optimal basis that arises in the unregularized linear programs.
To do so, we can compute the Jacobian of the entropic regularized solutions with respect to the vectors
\bm{b}(x)
and
\bm{c}(x)

and use a Taylor expansion of the true solution around the plugin estimates (i.e.
\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(x)\equiv\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\bm{b}}(x),\hat{\bm{c}}(x))
and
\hat{\bm{p}}_{U}^{\eta}(x)\equiv\bm{p}_{U}^{\eta}(\hat{\bm{b}}(x),\hat{\bm{c}}(x))
).

Then, we can approximately de-bias the plugin estimates as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i})\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i})\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{U}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{U}^{\eta}(X_{i})\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i})\rangle,

where
\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(x)\equiv\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(\hat{\bm{b}}(x),\hat{\bm{c}}(x))
is shorthand for the Jacobian of the entropic regularized lower bound with respect to the constraint vector
\bm{b}
evaluated at the plugin estimates of the nuisance functions
\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
and
\hat{\bm{c}}(x)
;
\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(x),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(x),\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(x)
are similarly defined. Lemma
B.2
in the Appendix gives the explicit form of these Jacobians.
These estimators are similar to the de-biased BFS estimators in Section
3.1
, but now rather than relying on plugin estimates of the optimal basic feasible solutions, we can use the differentiability of the entropic solutions to make the estimator more robust to errors in the nuisance function estimates.
The following theorem characterizes the asymptotic relationship between the error in the estimates of the regularized entropic bounds and the error in the estimates of the nuisance functions (again focusing on the lower bound for brevity).

Theorem 2
.

Define

\tilde{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\bm{c}(X_{i})+\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i})\rangle+\langle\bm{c}(X_{i}),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(O_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O_{i})\rangle,

where
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i})
is the solution to the entropic conditional linear program (
8
) using the true nuisance functions
\bm{b}(X_{i})
and
\bm{c}(X_{i})
.
Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, if
\hat{\bm{b}}
,
\hat{\bm{c}}
,
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}
, and
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}
are fit on a separate, independent sample, for a fixed
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}+O_{p}\left(r_{n}+\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Theorem
2
shows that the asymptotic bias of
\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}
relative to the true entropic regularized bound
\theta_{L}^{\eta}
decreases with the de-biased rate
r_{n}
and second order errors in the nuisance functions
\hat{\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\bm{c}}
, and so there is no first-order dependence on the nuisance functions.
As before, if the error in the nuisance functions converges quickly enough, then the entropic estimator is asymptotically normally distributed around the entropic regularized bounds, and we can apply standard inferential procedures to assess the uncertainty in our estimates.

Corollary 3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2
, if

\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}\right)
,
\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}\right)
and
r_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
,
then
\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}-\theta_{L}^{\eta}\right)}{\sqrt{V_{L}^{\eta}}}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
, where

V_{L}^{\eta}\equiv\text{Var}\left(\ \langle\bm{c}(X)+\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle+\langle\bm{c}(X),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(X)\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(O)+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}(X)\bm{\varphi}^{(c)}(O)\rangle\right).

Of course, changing the estimand by including the entropy penalty will introduce approximation error relative to the original bounds
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{U}
.
However, the approximation error induced via entropic regularization has been shown to decrease exponentially with the hyperparameter
\eta
when
\eta
is large enough relative to the sub-optimality gap
Weed, 2018
.
The following corollary of Theorem
2
incorporates the approximation error in a setting where we choose the hyperparameter
\eta
to grow with the sample size.

Corollary 4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2
, if for each sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
\eta\geq\frac{R_{1}(X_{i})+R_{H}(X_{i})}{\Delta_{L}(X_{i})}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
such that
\Delta_{L}(X_{i})>0
, where
R_{1}(x)\equiv\max_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\|\bm{p}\|_{1}
,

R_{H}(x)\equiv\max_{\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(X)}\sum_{k}p_{2k}(\log p_{2k}-1)-p_{1k}(\log p_{1k}-1)
, and
\eta
is allowed to grow with the sample size
n
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}+O_{p}\left(e^{-\eta}+\eta r_{n}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Furthermore, if
n^{1/2}e^{-\eta}\to 0
,
n^{-1/4}\eta\to 0
,
\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\eta}\right)
,
\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\eta}\right)
, and
r_{n}=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\eta}\right)
,
then
\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}-\theta_{L}\right)}{\sqrt{V_{L}^{\eta}}}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
.

Corollary
4
includes the approximation error induced by regularization.
The key requirement is that the hyperparameter
\eta
is large relative to the ratio of the size of the feasible set
\mathcal{P}(X_{i})
(i.e. measured via the one norm and the entropy,
R_{1}(X_{i})
and
R_{H}(X_{i})
) and the sub-optimality gap
\Delta_{L}(X_{i})
for each observed covariate
X_{i}
in the sample for which the sub-optimality gap is not zero.
6
6

6

If the sub-optimality gap is zero, then any feasible solution in
\mathcal{P}(x)
is optimal, and the objective does not vary within
\mathcal{P}(x)
, so the entropic regularized solution will pick out the maximum entropy solution, yielding the same value.

This ensures that the overall level of regularization
\frac{1}{\eta}
is small enough so that the approximation error decreases exponentially with
\eta
for each observed
X_{i}
in the sample
(
Weed, 2018
)
.
Because these aspects of the conditional linear programs are independent of the sample size, we can expect that in any finite sample it is possible to find an
\eta
that is larger than this minimal value.

On the other hand, the first and second derivatives of the entropic solutions with respect to the nuisance functions
\bm{b}(x)
and
\bm{c}(x)
scale with
\eta
: larger values of
\eta
can increase the magnitude of the derivatives and increase the scale of the bias quadratically with
\eta
.
Corollary
4
summarizes sufficient conditions for this increase in magnitude to be outweighed by the decrease in approximation error so that the overall bias converges faster than
n^{-1/2}
—and so is asymptotically smaller than the standard error and will not affect our confidence intervals.
The faster
\eta
grows, the more quickly the nuisance function errors need to converge (the maximum being a parametric convergence rate when
\eta=O(n^{1/4})
), but it is sufficient for
\eta

to scale logarithmically with the sample size,
\eta=O(\log n)
, a relatively slow growth.

Corollary
4
relies on being able to choose a large enough
\eta
to ensure that the approximation error decays exponentially. However, it may be possible that under some data generating processes the minimal size of
\eta
grows too quickly, depending on the tail behavior of the sub-optimality gaps
\Delta_{L}(X_{i})
.

If
\eta
is too small, then the approximation error may only decay with the inverse of
\eta

(
Weed, 2018
)
. However, we can show that under the margin condition in Assumption
3
, this occurs with probability
O(\eta^{-\alpha})
, where
\alpha
is the margin parameter, leading to an approximation error that decays with
\eta^{-(1+\alpha)}
.

Corollary 5
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2
and the margin condition in Assumption
3
, if
\eta
is allowed to grow with the sample size
n
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}+O_{p}\left(\eta^{-(1+\alpha)}+\eta r_{n}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

Furthermore, if
n^{1/2}\eta^{-(1+\alpha)}\to 0
,
n^{-1/4}\eta\to 0
,
\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\eta}\right)
,
\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}=o_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\eta}\right)
, and
r_{n}=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}\eta}\right)
,

then
\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}-\theta_{L}\right)}{\sqrt{V_{L}^{\eta}}}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
.

Corollary
5
shows that if we cannot guarantee that
\eta
is large enough, then the approximation error will not decay exponentially, but rather polynomially with
\eta
, and the minimum rate of growth for
\eta
depends on the margin parameter
\alpha
, which in turn affects the required convergence rates of the nuisance functions.
For example, if we choose
\eta=O(n^{\beta})
for some
0<\beta<\frac{1}{4}
, then to satisfy the conditions of Corollary
5
, we need
\beta>\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha)}
.
If
\alpha
is just larger than 1, then this would require that the nuisance functions converge at a parametric rate, but for larger values of
\alpha
this would decrease.
In Section
5.1
we inspect the impact of different growth rates for
\eta
and find that the de-biased entropic estimator is not particularly sensitive to the growth rate.

Finally, we can use Corollaries
3
,
4
, and
5
to construct confidence intervals for the entropic bounds by first estimating the variances with

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{L}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i})\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i})\rangle-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{U}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i})+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i}),\hat{\bm{p}}_{U}^{\eta}(X_{i})\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X_{i}),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{U}(X_{i})\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O_{i})\rangle-\hat{\theta}_{U}^{\eta}\right)^{2},

then constructing
1-\alpha
level confidence intervals for the entropic bounds as

\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}-z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{L}^{\eta}}{n}},\hat{\theta}_{U}^{\eta}+z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{U}^{\eta}}{n}}\right]
.

We conclude by comparing the entropic bounds to alternative smooth approximations to the conditional linear program. In particular, in the instrumental variables setting
Levis et al., 2023
propose applying a log-sum-exp approximation to the maximum. In the general setting considered here for the upper bound, this would iterate over all basic feasible solutions
B\in\mathcal{B}
and compute the approximation as
\frac{1}{\xi}\log\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\exp\left(\xi\left\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\right\rangle\right)
, where
\xi
is a hyperparameter that controls the approximation.
It is also possible to estimate this smooth approximation in a de-biased manner; however, it requires explicitly computing all basic feasible solutions, which can be computationally expensive in practice, and the approximation error scales like
\frac{1}{\xi}
rather than decaying exponentially as with entropic regularization. We consider this log-sum-exp approximation further via simulation in Section
5.1
.

3.3
Estimating conditional bounds

In addition to estimating bounds on the partially identified parameter
\theta
, we are often interested in understanding heterogeneity in the parameter across the covariate space. This can be important, for instance, when estimating treatment rules with partial identification, where various forms of optimality (e.g. minimax, maximin) are defined in terms of bounds on a conditional parameter
(
Pu and Zhang, 2021
;
Ben-Michael et al., 2024b
)
.

Let
V\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
denote a (subset of) covariates of interest, and let
\theta(v)\equiv\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}^{\ast}(X)\rangle\mid V=v]
be the conditional parameter. Then, our interest is in estimating the conditional lower and upper bounds:

\theta_{L}(v)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}\rangle\mid V=v\right]\qquad\theta_{U}(v)=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}\rangle\mid V=v\right].

(11)

To estimate the conditional bounds, we can adjust the de-biased BFS and entropic estimators in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
to condition on the covariates
V
by regressing
pseudo-outcomes
on the covariates
V
.
For example, for the BFS estimator in Section
3.1
, we can estimate the conditional lower bound as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{L}(v)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X)+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}(X)\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O)\rangle\mid V=v\right],

(12)

and for the entropic estimator in Section
3.2
, we can estimate the conditional lower bound as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(v)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X)+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X)\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O)+\nabla_{\bm{c}}\hat{\bm{p}}^{\eta}_{L}(X)\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(c)}(O)\mid V=v\right],

(13)

where
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)\mid V=v]
denotes an estimator for the conditional expectation of
f(X)
given
V=v
.

Broadly, pseudo-outcome-based estimators such as these inherit the properties of the de-biased estimators off which they are based
(
Kennedy, 2023
)
.
A consequence of the results in Theorems
1
and
2
and the general analysis of pseudo-outcome-based estimators in
Kennedy, 2023
is that the error for the conditional bounds estimators depend on the errors in the nuisance function estimates as in Theorems
1
and
2
as well as an “oracle” term that depends on the estimation error of the conditional expectation
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot\mid V=v]
.

3.4
Examples from Section
2.2
, revisited

Having laid out the general methodological framework, we now return to the examples from Section
2.2
to show how the estimators can be applied in practice.

Functions of the joint distributions of potential outcomes.

Recall that for estimands involving the joint distribution of potential outcomes, the constraints involve the marginal distributions of each outcome. There are various ways to index the constraints, but say that the first
j=1,\ldots,L-1
constraints correspond to the the marginal distribution of
Y(0)
, the next
j=L,\ldots,2L-2
constraints correspond to the marginal distribution of
Y(1)
, and so on.
In this case, we can define
b_{j}(x)=m_{bj}(d_{j},x)\equiv P(Y=y_{j}\mid D=d_{j},X=x)
where
d_{j}
and
y_{j}
index the treatment and outcome levels corresponding to the
j
th
constraint, respectively.
Defining the propensity score as
e(d_{j},x)=P(D=d_{j}\mid X=x)
,
a de-biasing function can be constructed from the Riesz representor (or efficient influence function) as
\varphi^{(b)}_{j}(O;b_{j})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{D=d_{j}\}}{P(D=d_{j}\mid X)}\left(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y_{j}\}-b_{j}(x)\right)
, the inverse propensity weighted residual.
Here the de-biased rate for
b_{j}
is
r_{nj}=\|\hat{b}_{j}-b_{j}\|_{2}\|\hat{e}(d_{j},\cdot)-e(d_{j},\cdot)\|_{2}
.
When the objective vector
\bm{c}(x)
is known, Theorems
1
and
2
give that the bias of BFS and entropic estimators depend on the product of the
errors of the estimates of the outcome model
b_{j}(x)
and the propensity score
e(d_{j},x)
.
However, the estimator is not “doubly robust:” even if the propensity score were known as in a randomized experiment, the error of the outcome model still affects the bias, either through the margin term in Theorem
1
or the second order term in Theorem
2
.

For a case where the objective vector
\bm{c}(x)
is not known, consider estimating the proportion of individuals not assigned an optimal treatment
P(Y\neq\max_{d}Y(d))
. Recall that in this case the elements of
\bm{c}(x)
correspond to
\sum_{d^{\prime}}e(d^{\prime},x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
, where
(y_{0}^{(k)},\ldots,y_{M-1}^{(k)})
are the
k
th
combination of possible potential outcomes. Here,

\bm{c}(x)
is a function of the propensity score, and we can estimate it as

\hat{c}_{k}(x)=\sum_{d^{\prime}}\hat{e}(d^{\prime},x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
, where
\hat{e}(d^{\prime},x)
is the estimated propensity score.
The corresponding de-biasing function is a function of the residuals of the propensity score:
\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})=\sum_{d^{\prime}}\left(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d^{\prime}\}-e(d^{\prime},x)\right)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
.
In this case,
\hat{\bar{\varphi}}_{k}^{(c)}(x)-\bar{\varphi}_{k}^{(c)}(x;\hat{c}_{k})=0
, and so the de-biased rate is simply
r_{nk}=0
.
Note, however, that Theorems
1
and
2
show that estimation error in the objective function—and hence, the propensity score—still affects the bias of the primal and entropic estimators, as it enters both through the margin term in Theorem
1
and the second order term in Theorem
2
.

Instrumental variables.

In the case of instrumental variables, recall that the constraints involve the joint distribution of the outcome
Y
and treatment
D
, conditioned on the instrument
Z
and covariates
X
.
The constraint vector is
b_{j}(x)=P(Y=y_{j},D=d_{j}\mid Z=z_{j},X=x)
, where
y_{j},d_{j}
, and
z_{j}
index the levels of the outcome, treatment, and instrument corresponding to the
j
th
constraint, respectively.
Defining
e(z_{j},x)=P(Z=z_{j}\mid X=x)
as the instrument propensity score,
the de-biasing function is again constructed from the Riesz representor as

\varphi_{j}^{(b)}(O;b_{j})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z_{j}\}}{P(Z=z_{j}\mid X)}\left(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y_{j},D=d_{j}\}-b_{j}(x)\right)
, the inverse instrument probability weighted residual.
Here the de-biased rate is the product of the errors in the estimates of the joint distribution of
Y
and
D
and the instrument assignment probabilities,
r_{nj}=\|\hat{b}_{j}-b_{j}\|_{2}\|\hat{e}(z_{j},\cdot)-e(z_{j},\cdot)\|_{2}
.

Now consider estimating bounds on the proportion of individuals not selecting an optimal treatment given a particular level of the instrument
P(Y(D(z))\neq\max_{d}y_{d})
.
In Section
2.2
we discussed that the elements of
\bm{c}(x)
correspond to
\sum_{d^{\prime}}P(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,X=x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
, where
(y_{0}^{(k)},\ldots,y_{M-1}^{(k)})
are the
k
th
combination of possible potential outcomes and treatment levels.
We can estimate it as
\hat{c}_{k}(x)=\sum_{d^{\prime}}\hat{P}(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,X=x)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
, where
\hat{P}(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,X=x)
is the estimated treatment selection probability given the level of the instrument and the value of the covariates.
The corresponding de-biasing function is the instrument probability weighted residual
\varphi_{k}^{(c)}(O;c_{k})=\sum_{d^{\prime}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{P(Z=z\mid X)}\left(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d^{\prime}\}-P(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,X)\right)\mathbbm{1}\{y_{d^{\prime}}\neq\max_{d}y_{d}^{(k)}\}
,
and so the de-biased rate will be a product of the error in the estimates of the treatment and instrument assignment probabilities,
r_{nk}=\|\hat{P}(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,\cdot)-P(D=d^{\prime}\mid Z=z,\cdot)\|_{2}\|\hat{e}(z,\cdot)-e(z,\cdot)\|_{2}
.

4
Decision making with de-biased bound estimators

We now turn to applying the de-biased BFS and entropic estimators to the problem of decision making, where the goal is to find a decision rule that optimizes a parameter that is partially identified via conditional linear programs.
In particular, consider a decision rule
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
that maps covariates to a continuous action between 0 and 1 (e.g. a treatment probability).
We will focus on settings where the covariate-conditional objective function is given by
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
, so that the lower bound on the expected objective, conditional on the covariates, is given by

\theta_{L}(x,\pi)=\min_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\langle\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)),\bm{p}\rangle,

and the overall expected lower bound on the objective value for a given decision rule
\pi
is given by
\theta_{L}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(X,\pi)]
.
Our goal is to find a decision rules that maximizes this lower bound across all decision rules
\pi
in a policy class
\Pi
, i.e.
\pi^{\ast}\in\text{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \theta_{L}(\pi)
. This setup accommodates a wide variety of decision making problems under uncertainty
(
Manski, 2011
)
including minimax regret and maximin value rules
(
Manski, 2007
;
Stoye, 2012
;
Song, 2014
, e.g.)
.
In this section we will consider finding such policies by maximizing an estimate of the lower bound
\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)
, using either the the de-biased BFS or entropic estimators from the previous section.

Throughout, we will focus on settings where the objective function
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
is a known function of the covariates
x
and the decision rule
\pi(x)
and we will make the several regularity assumptions.

Assumption 4

(Regularity conditions)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
o\in\mathcal{O}
, the constraint and objective functions are bounded, i.e.
\|\bm{b}(x)\|_{\infty}\leq B_{b}
and
\|\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))\|_{\infty}\leq B_{c}
for some constants
B_{b},B_{c}<\infty
, as is the de-biasing function for the constraints, i.e.
\|\bm{\varphi}^{(b)}(o)\|_{\infty}\leq B_{\varphi}
for some constant
B_{\varphi}<\infty
. Furthermore, the objective function is Lipschitz continuous in the decision rule
\pi(x)
, i.e. there exists a constant
L_{\pi}
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in[0,1]
, we have
\|\bm{c}(x,\pi_{1})-\bm{c}(x,\pi_{2})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\pi}|\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}|
.

These assumptions ensure that the objective is bounded and Lipschitz continuous in the decision rule
\pi(x)
.
As a final piece of setup, we will characterize the complexity of the policy class
\Pi
via the Rademacher complexity

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}\right|\right],

where
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
are independent Rademacher random variables.

4.1
Policy learning with the de-biased BFS estimator

For a policy
\pi
, let
\mathcal{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))
denote the set of optimal bases given the objective vector
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
and let
\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))
be the plugin estimate of an optimal basis.
Then, we estimate a policy
\hat{\pi}
by maximizing the de-biased BFS estimator of the lower bound on the expected objective value. Denoting
\hat{\bm{p}}(x,\pi(x))\equiv A^{-1}_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
, the minimax optimal policy
\hat{\pi}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}\in\underset{\pi\in\Pi}{\text{argmin}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bm{c}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i})),\hat{\bm{p}}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))+A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right\rangle}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)}.

(14)

To evaluate the quality of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}
, we compare it to the best-in-class policy that maximizes the true lower bound on the expected objective value, i.e.
\pi^{\ast}\in\text{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\theta_{L}(\pi)
, using the
excess regret
,
\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi})
.
Controlling this excess regret involves (a) linking the excess regret to the bias in the de-biased BFS estimator, and (b) applying standard results on empirical risk minimization.

To do so, we first make a stronger version of the margin condition in Assumption
3
.

Assumption 5

(Strong margin condition)
.

For a given
\pi
define the sub-optimality gap as

\Delta_{L}(x,\pi(x))=\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}\langle\bm{c}(x),A_{B}^{-1}b(x)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}^{-1}\bm{b}(x)\rangle,

where if
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x,\pi(x))=\emptyset
, then
\Delta_{L}(x,\pi(x))=0
.
There exists an
\alpha>0
, such that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,
P\left(0<\Delta_{L}(X,\pi(X))\leq t\right)\lesssim t^{\alpha}
.

Assumption
5
is primarily a requirement on the form of the objective function
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
.
It restricts the number of cases where
\bm{c}(x,\cdot)
is such that it is possible to choose a policy value
\pi(x)
with a small sub-optimality gap
\Delta_{L}(x,\pi(x))
. If the optimal BFS does not depend on the policy value
\pi(x)
(e.g. if the objective function is linear in
\pi(x)
), then this “strong” margin condition is is not stronger than the margin condition in Assumption
3
.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
4
, and
5
if
\hat{\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}
are fit on a separate, independent sample, then the excess regret of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}
satisfies

\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi})=O_{p}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)+\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}^{1+\alpha}+r_{n}\right).

Theorem
3
shows that the excess regret of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}
inherits most of the properties of the de-biased BFS estimator in Theorem
1
. The error in the estimated constraint vector
\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
again enters, with the impact of the error lessened by the margin parameter
\alpha
, and the de-biased rate
r_{n}
enters as well.
In this case the objective vector is known, and so there are no estimation errors to consider.
There is an additional term in the excess regret that depends on the Rademacher complexity of the policy class
\Pi
, which is standard for empirical risk minimization problems. In settings where it would be possible to estimate the lower bound
\theta_{L}(\pi)
using standard, asymptoic normality-based inference as in Corollary
1
, the excess regret would be primarily impacted by the Rademacher complexity of the policy class
\Pi
, a quantity that is controllable by the analyst by choosing a simpler or more expressive policy class.

4.2
Policy learning with the entropic estimator

Just as with the de-biased BFS estimator, we can use the entropic estimator to estimate the lower bound on the expected objective value for a policy
\pi
, and then find the policy that maximizes this estimate.
Fixing a regularization hyperparameter
\eta
, and denoting
\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(x,\pi(x))\equiv\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\bm{b}}(x),\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)))
as the solution to the entropic conditional linear program with policy value
\pi(x)
, we can find a policy
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
by solving

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\eta}\in\underset{\pi\in\Pi}{\text{argmin}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bm{c}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i})),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))+\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(O_{i})\right\rangle}_{\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)}.

(15)

It is more computationally straightforward to solve this optimization problem than to minimize the de-biased BFS estimator in (
14
) due to differentiability issues.
When the optimal BFS depends on the policy value
\pi(x)
, in some cases small perturbations to the policy value can lead to large changes in the optimal basis.
In such cases, optimizing the de-biased BFS estimator in (
14
) would require using gradient-free optimization methods.
In contrast, the entropic estimator of the lower bound
\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)
is differentiable with respect to the policy value
\pi(x)
and so it can be optimized using standard gradient-based methods—though the overall objective is non-convex. Note, however, that each objective value and gradient computation requires solving the dual to the entropic conditional linear program for each unique value of the covariates
x
in the sample, which can be computationally expensive for iterative optimization methods.

We will compare this estimated decision policy
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
to two notions of an optimal policy: (i) the best-in-class policy that maximizes the true lower bound on the expected objective value,
\pi^{\ast}\in\text{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \theta_{L}(\pi)
, and (ii) the best-in-class policy that maximizes the entropic lower bound on the expected objective value,
\pi^{\ast}_{\eta}\in\text{argmax}_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)
. In each case we will measure the excess regret relative to the corresponding bound.

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
4
, if
\hat{\bm{b}}
and
\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}
are fit on a separate, independent sample, for a fixed
\eta
the excess regret of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
relative to
\pi_{\eta}^{\ast}
measured via the entropic lower bound satisfies

\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})=O_{p}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)+r_{n}+\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Furthermore, if for each sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
\eta\geq\frac{R_{1}(X_{i})+R_{H}(X_{i})}{\Delta_{L}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
such that
\Delta_{L}(X_{i},\pi(X_{i}))>0
, and for all
\pi\in\Pi
, and
\eta
is allowed to grow with the sample size
n
, then the excess regret of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
relative to
\pi^{\ast}
measured via the unregularized lower bound satisfies

\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})=O_{p}\left(\eta\ \mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)+e^{-\eta}+r_{n}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Theorem
4
shows that relative to the optimal decision policy for the true entropic lower bound,
\pi^{\ast}_{\eta}
, the excess regret of the estimated policy
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
is impacted by estimation errors in the constraint vector
\hat{\bm{b}}(x)
only via the de-biased rate
r_{n}
and squared errors
\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}
. This means that the estimated policy is relatively robust to such errors and we would typically expect the complexity of the policy class
\Pi
to drive the excess regret.
The same is true for excess regret relative to the optimal policy for the true lower bound,
\pi^{\ast}
, along with the additional scaling with the regularization hyperparameter
\eta
as with Theorem
2
. Note here that the complexity of the policy class
\Pi
is scaled by
\eta
because of the impact
\eta
has on the magnitude of the derivatives of the entropic solution with respect to the constraint vector
\bm{b}(x)
, but again
\eta
can be set to grow relatively slowly with the sample size
n
.
The excess regret bound also requires that
\eta
be chosen sufficiently large across all potential policies
\pi
in the policy class
\Pi
.
As in Corollary
5
, we can also extend these results to make no assumptions on
\eta
but require the strong margin condition in Assumption
5
to hold.

5.1
Simulation study

For this simulation study, we consider a setting with a single binary treatment
D\in\{0,1\}

The goal is to estimate the probability that the observed outcome is worse than the best potential outcome,

Note that this corresponds to a setting where both the conditional constraint and objective vectors are unknown.
Our focus is on understanding the relative performance between the de-biased BFS and entropic estimators as
(a) the level of regularization changes, (b) the sample size changes, (c) the estimation error of the nuisance functions changes, and (d) the complexity of the conditional linear program changes.
For the latter, we change the number of outcome levels. More levels of the outcome add more decision variables and constraints into the conditional linear program, which changes the complexity.

6
Discussion

In this paper we have proposed a general framework for estimating bounds on partially-identified parameters that satisfy a series of linear constraints, conditional on auxiliary covariates.
Finding such bounds involves solving linear programs that are conditional on the covariates.
We have proposed two estimators for these bounds.
The de-biased BFS estimator directly solves the conditional linear programs for each unique covariate value in the data, and then extracts readily available information from standard LP solvers to construct de-biased estimates of the bounds.
This estimator is related to existing methods for covariate-assisted bounds, but eschews the need to analytically derive the bound or fully enumerate a combinatorial number of potential solutions.

A key issue with solving the linear programs directly is that the solutions are not smooth in the constraint and objective vectors, so small errors in these nuisance parameters can lead to large changes in the estimated bounds. We have shown that under a form of margin condition, where the optimal value of the linear programs is typically well separated from the next-best solution, the non-differentiability issues are mitigated, and the de-biased BFS estimator can be used to construct confidence intervals for the bounds.
The entropic regularized estimator instead targets a smoothed version of the bounds, including an entropy penalty to the conditional linear programs that ensures that the solutions are smooth in the nuisance parameters. We then use this to estimate the bounds in a de-biased manner and account for approximation error induced by the regularization, showing that we can likely expect the approximation error to be small relative to the standard error of the estimates if we allow the level of regularization to shrink appropriately.
Finally, we extend these results to decision making problems where the value of a decision policy is only partially identified by a set of conditional linear constraints. We show that taking an empirical risk minimization approach and optimizing bounds estimated via the de-biased BFS or entropic estimators results in a policy with excess regret that has an analogous structure to the bias of the estimators.

An important limitation of these approaches is that they require a finite number of decision variables and so for instance cannot accommodate continuous outcomes.
While it may be possible to approximate some continuous outcomes with a finite number of discrete levels, this may not be feasible in all cases.
One potential path forward is to leverage the duality of the linear programs to adapt the de-biased BFS and entropic estimators to return valid bounds even if an approximation is used, similar in spirit to the dual estimator proposed by
Ji et al., 2024
.
In addition, while we have used an entropy penalty to regularize the LPs, other forms of regularization may have different benefits. For example, it may be possible to use barrier-based interior point methods to solve the LPs, and directly account for regularization bias via a double de-biased estimator following recent results in
Liu et al., 2025
. Such an approach could lessen the need to select a regularization hyperparameter.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We will show the proof for plugin lower-bound basis,
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
; the proof for the plugin upper-bound basis,
\widehat{B}_{U}(x)
, is analogous.
Note that for a fixed value of the covariates
x
, if
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(x)
then this implies that there must exist some bases that are feasible but not optimal, and that
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
is at most a second-best basis.
Therefore, the sub-optimality gap for a second-best basis is upper bounded by the sub-optimality gap for
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
, and so

\displaystyle\Delta_{L}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{c}(X),(A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}-A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)})(\bm{b}(X)-\hat{\bm{b}}(X))\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X)-\bm{c}(X),(A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}-A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}^{-1})\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle

where the second inequality uses the fact that
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)
is an optimal basis for the estimated constraints
\hat{\bm{b}}(X)
and objective
\hat{\bm{c}}(X)
. Next, by Hölder’s inequality, we have for

\tilde{C}\equiv\max\left\{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\|(A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}-A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)})^{\prime}\bm{c}(x)\|_{1},\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\|(A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}-A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)})\hat{\bm{b}}(x)\|_{1}\right\}<\infty,

\displaystyle\langle\bm{c}(X),(A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}-A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)})(\bm{b}(X)-\hat{\bm{b}}(X))\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X)-\bm{c}(X),(A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}-A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}^{-1})\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}(X)-\bm{b}(X)\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}(X)-\bm{c}(X)\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\hat{\bm{b}}(x)-\bm{b}(x)\|_{\infty}+\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\hat{\bm{c}}(x)-\bm{c}(x)\|_{\infty}\right)

So,
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(x)
implies that
\Delta_{L}(x)\leq\ \tilde{C}\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)
. Using this and the margin condition, we have

\displaystyle P\left(\widehat{B}_{L}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(X)\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(0<\Delta_{L}(x)\leq\ \tilde{C}\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{\alpha}.

Next, note that the expected sub-optimality of the plugin basic feasible solution is

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(X)\}\left(\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(X)\}\left(\langle\bm{c}(X),(A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}-A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)})(\bm{b}(X)-\hat{\bm{b}}(X))\rangle+\langle\hat{\bm{c}}(X)-\bm{c}(X),(A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(x)}^{-1}-A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x)}^{-1})\hat{\bm{b}}(X)\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}P(\widehat{B}_{L}(X)\not\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\ast}(X))\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{\infty}\right)^{1+\alpha}

where the first inequality uses the bound on
\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),A_{B^{\ast}_{L}(X)}^{-1}\bm{b}(X)\rangle
when
\widehat{B}_{L}(x)\not\in B_{L}^{\ast}(x)

\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\theta^{\eta}_{L}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}]-\theta^{\eta}_{L}

(

\displaystyle\quad+(\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}])-(\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\theta}^{\eta}_{L}])

(

We have bounded the bias term (
\ast

Because
\eta
is allowed to grow with the sample size
n
, it remains to characterize the rates of growth of the operator norms
S_{\bm{b}}(\eta)
,
S_{\bm{c}}(\eta)
and
S_{\bm{b},\bm{c}}(\eta)
as
\eta\to\infty

For a matrix
A
, we will use
\sigma_{\max}(A)
and
\sigma_{\min}(A)
to denote the largest and smallest singular values of
A

First, for the Jacobian of
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
with respect to
\bm{b}

\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})=\text{diag}(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c}))^{1/2}\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\dagger},

where
\tilde{A}(\eta)=A\text{diag}(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c}))^{1/2}
and
\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\dagger}=\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime}(\tilde{A}(\eta)\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime})^{-1}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
\tilde{A}(\eta)
. Because
A
is full row-rank, and the elements of
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
are strictly greater than zero,
\tilde{A}(\eta)
is also full row-rank and so
\sigma_{\max}(\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\dagger})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(\tilde{A}(\eta))}<\infty
is bounded. As
\eta\to\infty
,
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
converges to a value on the optimal face of the polytope defined by the constraints on the conditional linear program, and so it is a convex combination of the set of optimal basic feasible solutions
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}_{L}(x)
. Therefore,
\tilde{A}(\eta)
converges to a matrix with full row-rank. Similarly, because the solutions to the un-regularized program are bounded,
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
will be bounded as
\eta\to\infty
. Therefore, the operator norm of the Jacobian matrix,
\|\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})\|_{2}\leq\frac{\max_{k}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})}{\sigma_{\min}(\tilde{A}(\eta))}\to C
for some constant
C
as
\eta\to\infty
, and so
S_{\bm{b}}(\eta)=O(1)
.

For the Jacobian of
\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
with respect to
\bm{c}
, we can write the Jacobian as

\nabla_{\bm{c}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})=\eta\times\text{diag}(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c}))^{1/2}(I_{K}-\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime}(\tilde{A}(\eta)\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime})^{-1}\tilde{A}(\eta))\text{diag}(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c}))^{1/2}.

Therefore, the operator norm is bounded by

\|\nabla_{\bm{c}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})\|_{2}\leq\eta\max_{k}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})\|I_{K}-\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime}(\tilde{A}(\eta)\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime})^{-1}\tilde{A}(\eta)\|_{2}=\eta\max_{k}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c}),

where we have used the fact that
I_{K}-\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime}(\tilde{A}(\eta)\tilde{A}(\eta)^{\prime})^{-1}\tilde{A}(\eta)
is a residual of a projection matrix and so has eigenvalues all equal to one or zero. This implies that
S_{\bm{c}}(\eta)=O(\eta)
.

Finally, we consider the operator norm of the Hessian matrix of
p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})
with respect to
\bm{b}
and
\bm{c}
.
Suppressing the dependence on
\bm{b}
and
\bm{c}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{j}}Q(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=-Q(\bm{p})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}}{\partial b_{j}}\right)A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial c_{k}}Q(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=-Q(\bm{p})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}}{\partial c_{k}}\right)A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{j}}H(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}}{\partial b_{j}}\right)(I_{K}-H(\bm{p}))

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial c_{k}}H(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}}{\partial c_{k}}\right)(I_{K}-H(\bm{p}))

This implies that the elements of the Hessian matrix are:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{j}}\nabla_{\bm{b}}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=A_{\cdot k}^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})\frac{\partial p_{Lk}^{\eta}}{\partial b_{j}}-Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}^{\eta}}{\partial b_{j}}\right)A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A_{\cdot k}p_{k}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=A_{\cdot k}^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})\frac{\partial p_{Lk}^{\eta}}{\partial c_{k^{\prime}}}-Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}^{\eta}}{\partial c_{k^{\prime}}}\right)A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A_{\cdot k}p_{k}^{\eta}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{j}}\nabla_{\bm{c}}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\eta\frac{\partial p_{Lk}^{\eta}}{\partial b_{j}}\bm{e}_{k}^{\prime}(I-H(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L}))-\eta p_{Lk}^{\eta}\bm{e}_{k}^{\prime}A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A\text{diag}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{p}}{\partial b_{j}}\right)(I_{K}-H(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}))

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\bm{b}}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=Q(\bm{p}^{\eta}_{L})A_{\cdot k}(\nabla_{\bm{b}}p_{Lk}^{\eta})^{\prime}-\left(\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}\odot\bm{1}_{J}\left(A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta})A_{\cdot k}p_{Lk}^{\eta}\right)\right)A^{\prime}Q(\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta})

Proof of Corollary
5
.

From Theorem
2
, it suffices to control the difference
\theta_{L}^{\eta}-\theta_{L}
.
Note that we can decompose this difference into the difference when
\eta
is larger than
\frac{R_{1}(x)+R_{H}(x)}{\Delta_{L}(x)}
and when
\eta
is smaller than this value:

\displaystyle\theta_{L}^{\eta}-\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}(X)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}(X)\rangle\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{\eta\geq\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{\Delta_{L}(X)}\right\}\right]

(i)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle-\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}(X)\rangle\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{\eta<\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{\Delta_{L}(X)}\right\}\right]

(ii)

Since the hyperparameter is large relative to the margin in the first term (i), from Theorem 5 in
Weed, 2018
, we have that

(i)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{L}(X)\exp\left(-\eta\frac{\Delta_{L}(x)}{R_{1}(X)}+\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{R_{1}(X)}\right)\right]=O(e^{-\eta}).

For the second term (ii), the sub-optimality gap is small relative to the inverse of
\eta
,
\Delta_{L}(X)<\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{\eta}
.
Note that when
\Delta_{L}(X)=0
, then
\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(X)\rangle=\langle\bm{c}(X),\bm{p}_{L}(X)\rangle
, and
so by Proposition 4 in
Weed, 2018
, and the margin condition in Assumption
3
, we have that

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{R_{H}(X)}{\eta}\mathbbm{1}\left\{0<\Delta_{L}(X)<\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{\eta}\right\}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}R_{H}(x)}{\eta}P\left(0<\Delta_{L}(X)\leq\frac{R_{1}(X)+R_{H}(X)}{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\eta^{1+\alpha}}=O\left(\eta^{-(1+\alpha)}\right).

Putting together the pieces gives the result.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

First, note that we can write the excess regret as

\displaystyle\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi})

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi^{\ast})+\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\hat{\theta}_{L}(\hat{\pi})+\hat{\theta}_{L}(\hat{\pi})-\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi^{\ast})+\hat{\theta}_{L}(\hat{\pi})-\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)-\theta_{L}(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]\right|+2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)]-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]\right|,

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
\hat{\pi}
maximizes
\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)
so
\hat{\theta}_{L}(\hat{\pi})\geq\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi^{\ast})
.
Note that
\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]
is a mean-zero empirical process indexed by the policy
\pi
.
Defining the function
f_{\pi}(o)=\left\langle\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)),A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}^{-1}\left(\hat{\bm{b}}(x)+\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(o)\right)\right\rangle
, and the function class
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\pi}:\pi\in\Pi\}
, we can write the first term in the decomposition above as

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]\right|=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(O_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f(O)]\right|.

By the regularity conditions in Assumption
4
, the function class
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.
\|f_{\pi}\|_{\infty}\leq\tilde{C}
for some
\tilde{C}
, for all
\pi\in\Pi
. Therefore, by Theorem 4.2 in
Wainwright, 2019
, we have that

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(O_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f(O)]\right|\leq 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})+\tilde{C}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

with probability at least
1-\delta
. To link the Rademacher complexity of the function class
\mathcal{F}
to the Rademacher complexity of the policy class
\Pi
, we note that
f
is a Lipschitz function of
\pi
. To see this, note that by strong duality, the basis
\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))
corresponding to the optimal BFS also yields an optimal basis for the solution to the dual problem

A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}^{-1\prime}\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))\in\text{argmax}_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\langle\bm{\lambda},\hat{\bm{b}}(x)\rangle\quad\text{s.t. }A^{\prime}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)).

Mangasarian and Shiau, 1987
show the the solution to this linear program is Lipschitz continuous in the objective vector
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
. So, because
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
is Lipschitz continuous in
\pi(x)
, so we have that
A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}^{-1\prime}\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
is Lipschitz continuous in
\pi(x)
. Furthermore,
f_{\pi}(o)
is a linear function of
A_{\widehat{B}_{L}(x,\pi(x))}^{-1\prime}\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
, so it is also Lipschitz continuous in
\pi(x)
. By the Talagrand contraction principle
(
Ledoux and Talagrand, 1991
, e.g. see, Theorem 4.12)
, we have that the Rademacher complexity of the function class
\mathcal{F}
is bounded by the Rademacher complexity of the policy class
\Pi
,
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\lesssim\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)
.

It remains to bound the second term in the decomposition of the excess regret,
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)]-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]\right|
. Note that under the strong margin condition in Assumption
5
, we can directly apply Lemma
B.1
to obtain that

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{L}(\pi)]-\mathbb{E}[\theta_{L}(\pi)]\right|=O_{p}\left(\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{\infty}^{1+\alpha}+r_{n}\right).

Putting together the pieces gives the result.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We begin by considering the entropic lower bound. Note that

\displaystyle\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]\right|+2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right|,

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
\hat{\pi}^{\eta}
maximizes
\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)
.

For the first term, define the function
f_{\pi}(o)=\langle\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)),\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(x,\pi(x))+\nabla_{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{p}}_{L}^{\eta}(x,\pi(x))\hat{\bm{\varphi}}^{(b)}(o)\rangle
, adn the function class
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\pi}\mid\pi\in\Pi\}
. Assumption
4
ensures that
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded, and so as with the proof of Theorem
3
by Theorem 4.2 in
Wainwright, 2019
, we have that

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(O_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f(O)]\right|\leq 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})+\tilde{C}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

with probability at least
1-\delta
. In the proof of Theorem
2
, we have shown that the operator norm of the Jacobian of the entropic solution with respect to the objective is bounded by a constant times
\eta
, i.e.
\|\nabla_{\bm{c}}\bm{p}_{L}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})\|_{2}\lesssim\eta
, and have similarly shown this for the operator norm of the Hessian,
\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{c},\bm{b}}p_{Lk}^{\eta}(\bm{b},\bm{c})\|_{2}\lesssim\eta
. By Assumption
4
, the objective function
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
is Lipschitz continuous in
\pi(x)
, and so
\bm{p}(\bm{b},\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)))
and
\nabla_{\bm{b}}\bm{p}(\bm{b},\bm{c}(x,\pi(x)))
are Lipschitz continuous in
\pi(x)
for any
x
as well, with a Lipschitz constant scaling with
\eta
. So by Talagrand’s contraction principle we have that the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}
is bounded by
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\lesssim\eta\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Pi)
.
To complete the first claim we must bound the second term in the decomposition above,
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right|
. We can do so by directly applying Lemma
B.3
and the bounds on
S_{\bm{b}}(\eta)
and
S_{\bm{b},\bm{c}}(\eta)
from the proof of Theorem
2
for each
\pi\in\Pi
to obtain that

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right|=O_{p}\left(r_{n}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

noting that the objective vector
\bm{c}(x,\pi(x))
is known in this case.

For the second claim, we can similarly write the excess regret as

\displaystyle\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast}_{\eta})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})-\theta_{L}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{L}(\pi^{\ast})-\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi^{\ast})+\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})-\theta_{L}^{\eta}(\hat{\pi}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)-\theta_{L}(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]\right|+2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]-\theta_{L}(\pi)\right|.

We have just controlled the first term above. To control the second term we can again apply Lemma
B.3
and the argument in the proof of Corollary
4
for each
\pi\in\Pi
to obtain that

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\theta}_{L}^{\eta}(\pi)\right]-\theta_{L}(\pi)\right|=O_{p}\left(e^{-\eta}+r_{n}+\eta^{2}\|\hat{\bm{b}}-\bm{b}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Combining these bounds gives the result.

∎
