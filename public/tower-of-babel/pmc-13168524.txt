Impact of Age on Surgical and Oncologic Outcomes After Colorectal Cancer Resection in Selected Patients Undergoing Primary Anastomosis: A Retrospective Propensity‐Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Camilo Ramírez‐Giraldo, Pablo González‐Sierra, Natalia Amado‐Peña, Marian Ochoa‐Patarroyo, Nicolás Navarro‐Pulido, Alejandro González Muñoz, Juan Carlos Vallejo‐Soto, Carlos Figueroa‐Avendaño, Andrés Isaza‐Restrepo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe impact of age on perioperative morbidity and long‐term oncologic outcomes in colorectal cancer remains controversial. Although aging is linked to greater comorbidity and functional decline, advances in perioperative care have challenged the idea that older patients have worse outcomes. This study evaluated surgical and oncologic results in patients aged ≥ 75 years compared with younger individuals.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including patients who underwent colorectal resection with primary anastomosis between 2015 and 2022. Patients were grouped by age (< 75 vs. ≥ 75 years) and matched 1:1 using propensity scores based on preoperative clinical and tumor‐related variables. Major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) were analyzed using logistic regression, and OS and RFS were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOf 651 eligible patients, 272 were included in the matched cohort. No statistically significant differences were found between age groups in hospital stay (4.5 vs. 4.0 days; p = 0.270), reintervention (13.2% vs. 8.8%; p = 0.333), major complications (14.0% vs. 8.8%; p = 0.252), or 30‐day perioperative mortality (3.7% vs. 0.7%; p = 0.216), although perioperative mortality was numerically higher among patients aged ≥ 75 years. Age ≥ 75 years was not significantly associated with major complications in multivariable analysis (OR 1.73; 95% CI 0.79–3.94). Five‐year OS and RFS also did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionOlder age was not associated with statistically significant differences in major complications or long‐term oncologic outcomes in this matched cohort. However, clinically meaningful differences, particularly in short‐term perioperative risk, cannot be excluded. Chronological age alone should not preclude curative‐intent surgery.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies worldwide and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [1]. Its incidence rises markedly with age, and nearly 60% of new diagnoses occur in individuals aged 65 years or older [2]. As global life expectancy increases, the number of older adults requiring surgical management for CRC continues to grow, making treatment decisions in this population an increasingly relevant clinical challenge.

Advanced age has historically been considered a relative contraindication to major oncologic surgery due to concerns about higher perioperative risk, increased postoperative complications, and limited life expectancy [3]. However, this paradigm has been progressively challenged. Emerging evidence suggests that chronological age alone may not be the primary determinant of surgical outcomes; rather, functional reserve, comorbidity burden, tumor stage, and physiological status appear to be more relevant predictors [3, 4, 5]. Nonetheless, uncertainty persists, and older patients are still more likely to receive non‐restorative procedures or less aggressive treatment strategies.

The interpretation of age‐related outcomes is further complicated by selection bias. Many studies include highly selected elderly patients deemed fit for surgery, while frailer individuals are excluded or managed non‐operatively [6]. In addition, most available evidence derives from high‐income countries. Data from upper–middle‐income countries—where healthcare systems, resource allocation, and access to advanced surgical techniques may differ—remain comparatively limited [7].

In this context, evaluating outcomes in a high‐volume center with widespread use of minimally invasive colorectal surgery provides an opportunity to explore whether advanced age independently influences perioperative morbidity and long‐term oncologic results when baseline clinical and tumor characteristics are carefully balanced. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess surgical and oncologic outcomes in patients aged ≥ 75 years compared with younger individuals undergoing colorectal resection with primary anastomosis. We hypothesized that, in appropriately selected patients, chronological age alone does not significantly worsen surgical or oncologic outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study including all consecutive patients who underwent surgery for colorectal cancer between 2015 and 2022. The study was conducted at Hospital Universitario Mayor–Méderi in Bogotá, Colombia, a tertiary‐level general university hospital affiliated with Universidad del Rosario. Although not a dedicated cancer institute, the hospital provides comprehensive oncologic care and manages a substantial volume of colorectal cancer cases. All procedures were performed or supervised by experienced colorectal surgeons within a structured multidisciplinary setting. Vital status and dates of death were obtained by record linkage to the Colombian national database ADRES (Administradora de los Recursos del Sistema General de Seguridad Social en Salud), and clinical variables were entered into a de‐identified REDCap database [8]. The protocol was approved by our institutional Ethics Committee and Technical Research Committee (approval 2568‐CV1861). Given the retrospective design and minimal‐risk nature of the study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for study‐specific informed consent. Separately, as part of the institution's routine admission process, patients or their legal representatives sign a general authorization allowing the use of clinical information for research purposes under applicable ethical and confidentiality safeguards. This study was reported following the STROCSS guidelines [9].

We included patients who underwent colon or rectal resection with a primary anastomosis and excluded index procedures without an anastomosis (e.g., Hartmann's or permanent end ostomy) to assemble a more homogeneous cohort with comparable surgical management and postoperative risk; patients receiving a protective diverting stoma were retained because a primary anastomosis was performed. This restriction minimizes confounding, as the decision to avoid an anastomosis typically reflects frailty, comorbidity, nutritional compromise, advanced disease, or adverse intraoperative findings—factors that independently influence morbidity and mortality—and thus allows a more precise estimation of the effect of age on postoperative complications and oncologic outcomes.

Outcomes and Covariates

The primary outcomes were major complications, defined as Clavien–Dindo grade ≥III, and oncologic endpoints, including overall survival (OS) and relapse‐free survival (RFS). Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, reoperation, and perioperative mortality (30‐day mortality).

To reduce confounding, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) using the following preoperative covariates: sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA class, Charlson Comorbidity Index, tumor location, stage according to the AJCC TNM classification, neoadjuvant therapy, and surgical approach (planned approach). Matching on these variables was intended to improve baseline comparability between groups, thereby reducing selection bias and strengthening the validity of effect estimates. Postoperative and pathological variables were not included in the propensity score model because they are not pre‐treatment characteristics and may lie on the causal pathway between age and the outcomes of interest.

Continuous variables were entered into the propensity score model as continuous terms, and categorical variables as indicator variables.

Propensity Score Matching

Patients younger than 75 years were matched with those aged 75 years or older based on comparable estimated propensity scores. These scores were generated through a logistic regression model that calculated the probability of belonging to either age group using predefined covariates.

A nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was applied, with a caliper width set at 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score [10, 11]. A 1:1 matching ratio between cases and controls was considered optimal according to post‐matching balance diagnostics [12]. Groups were regarded as adequately balanced when absolute standardized mean differences were below 0.1 and variance ratios ranged between 0.5 and 2 [13].

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), according to their distribution in the matched cohort, assessed using graphical inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Bivariate analyses were conducted using the χ 2 test for categorical variables and either the two‐tailed Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, comparing outcomes across age groups (< 75 versus ≥ 75 years).

To explore the effect of age on major postoperative complications (Clavien‐Dindo≥III), univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Covariates associated with complications were included in the models, with the exception of adjuvant therapy, which occurs after surgery and therefore could not be causally related to postoperative morbidity. This approach combined propensity score matching with regression adjustment to address any minor residual imbalances between groups. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

OS and RFS were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, including postoperative deaths (intention‐to‐treat principle). OS and RFS curves were compared with the log‐rank test. The impact of age (< 75 versus ≥ 75 years) on OS and RFS was further assessed with univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, providing hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CI. Consistent with the analysis of major complications, regression adjustment after matching was used to account for any minor residual covariate imbalance. The proportional hazards assumption was tested with Schoenfeld residuals.

The proportions of missing data were as follows: BMI 0.8%, ASA 0.2%, albumin 17.7%, stage 0.2%, defunctioning ileostomy 0.6%, adjuvant therapy 0.2%, resection margin 0.2%, surgical approach 0.9%, number of retrieved lymph nodes 1.7%, metastatic lymph nodes 1.4%, and intraoperative blood loss 4.0%. Missing values were addressed using multiple imputation by predictive mean matching (m = 5 imputations). Imputation was performed prior to propensity score estimation and matching and included all covariates used in the propensity score model as well as the outcome variables to preserve associations between predictors and outcomes. Given the higher proportion of missingness in albumin (17.7%), multiple imputation was used to reduce loss of precision and potential selection bias from complete‐case analysis. As with most imputation approaches, this relies on a missing‐at‐random assumption conditional on observed variables; we cannot exclude that some missingness may be not at random.

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the matched and regression‐adjusted analyses using an alternative specification that additionally incorporated pathological and treatment‐related variables, including number of retrieved lymph nodes, number of metastatic lymph nodes, resection margin status, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, diverting ileostomy, and adjuvant therapy. This analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the main findings under a broader disease‐severity adjustment strategy, but was not considered the primary causal analysis because these variables are not strictly pre‐treatment characteristics.

A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2) using RStudio (version 2025.05.0 + 496) [14, 15].

Median follow‐up was 4.39 years (95% CI 3.97–5.21), during which 68 deaths (25.0%) and 37 recurrences (13.6%) were documented in the matched cohort.

In univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses assessing age group as a risk factor for major complications, the OR were 1.68 (95% CI 0.79–3.70) and 1.73 (95% CI 0.79–3.94), respectively.

For RFS, no statistically significant difference was observed at 5 years (p = 1.00) (Figure 2). Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models yielded HRs of 1.00 (95% CI 0.53–1.88) and 1.07 (95% CI 0.56–2.04), respectively. The Schoenfeld residuals indicated that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for both age group (p = 0.09) and the overall model (global p = 0.30).

For OS, no statistically significant difference was observed between age groups at 5 years (log‐rank p = 0.16) (Figure 3). Univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis yielded an HR of 1.43 (95% CI 0.87–2.34), while the multivariable model yielded an adjusted HR of 1.52 (95% CI 0.93–2.48). As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the matching and regression‐adjusted analyses using an alternative specification that additionally incorporated pathological and treatment‐related variables. Results were consistent with the primary analysis. In logistic regression assessing age group as a risk factor for major complications, the univariable and multivariable OR were 1.99 (95% CI 0.86–4.87) and 1.85 (95% CI 0.86–4.19), respectively. For RFS, no statistically significant difference was observed at 5 years (log‐rank p = 0.17), with univariable and multivariable HR of 0.65 (95% CI 0.35–1.21) and 0.63 (95% CI 0.34–1.19), respectively. For OS, no statistically significant difference was observed at 5 years (log‐rank p = 0.29), with univariable and multivariable HRs of 1.31 (95% CI 0.79–2.18) and 1.38 (95% CI 0.83–2.31), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, age ≥ 75 years was not associated with a statistically significant increase in major postoperative complications or worse 5‐year oncologic outcomes following colorectal resection with primary anastomosis. However, effect estimates were imprecise and confidence intervals remained wide. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in major complications, reintervention, hospital stay, overall survival, or relapse‐free survival, crude event rates were consistently numerically higher among older patients for several short‐term outcomes. In particular, 30‐day perioperative mortality was higher in older patients (3.7% vs. 0.7%), representing a clinically relevant absolute difference despite the absence of statistical significance. Given the limited number of events, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent perioperative safety, but rather as a signal that may reflect increased vulnerability to adverse postoperative outcomes in selected older individuals. These findings contribute to the ongoing debate on surgical decision‐making in elderly CRC patients and suggest that chronological age alone should not automatically preclude consideration for curative colorectal surgery [3, 16, 17, 18]. Importantly, our results apply only to patients selected for resection with primary anastomosis and therefore do not represent the entire elderly CRC population, particularly frailer individuals managed with non‐restorative procedures.

Our results are consistent with prior observational studies assessing surgical outcomes in elderly patients. Roque‐Castellano et al. reported acceptable morbidity and favorable long‐term survival among carefully selected nonagenarians undergoing colorectal cancer surgery [2]. Similarly, Karaoğlan et al. found no association between age ≥ 70 years and overall survival [19]. Conversely, Duraes et al. identified advanced age as an independent risk factor for mortality when postoperative complications occurred [20]. Some studies have shown age to be associated with lower overall and relapse‐free survival; however, many did not include variables related to comprehensive geriatric assessment that may influence treatment allocation and outcomes [21, 22, 23].

Several large population‐based and multihospital registry studies, including analyses from NSQIP and other administrative databases, have reported that advanced age is associated with higher postoperative morbidity and mortality after colorectal cancer surgery [24, 25], while others have demonstrated that advancing age and frailty significantly influence long‐term survival patterns among older adults undergoing cancer resection [26]. However, such analyses frequently reflect heterogeneous surgical environments and may be limited by residual confounding, variability in operative decision‐making, and incomplete adjustment for baseline clinical differences. In contrast, our study evaluates a clinically granular, propensity score–matched cohort within a relatively standardized institutional framework, allowing a more focused assessment of whether chronological age is associated with outcomes when preoperative clinical and tumor characteristics are balanced. Additionally, granular data from upper–middle‐income Latin American healthcare systems remain comparatively limited, and contextual evidence may be relevant given differences in resource allocation and referral patterns.

To minimize confounding inherent in comparing older and younger cohorts, we applied PSM using preoperative variables only. After matching, baseline comparability improved substantially, strengthening the validity of comparisons between groups. In this setting, age ≥ 75 years was not associated with statistically significant differences in major complications, OS, or RFS. However, the point estimates and their confidence intervals do not exclude potentially meaningful differences, particularly for short‐term postoperative outcomes. Sensitivity analyses using an alternative specification that additionally incorporated pathological and treatment‐related variables yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the overall robustness of the results while remaining secondary and exploratory in nature. Therefore, beyond chronological age, clinicians should consider comorbidities, frailty, sarcopenia, polypharmacy, social support, and mental health—core components of comprehensive geriatric assessment—as these factors have been shown to predict postoperative complications and reduced survival [6, 26, 27, 28].

Frailty, a key determinant not included in traditional risk scores, reflects functional decline and diminished physiological reserve. Evidence indicates that frailty outperforms age as a predictor of postoperative morbidity and mortality [29, 30]. Although our dataset lacked direct frailty indices, balance in Charlson Comorbidity Index and ASA classification after matching partially mitigated this limitation and allowed for a more meaningful comparison across age groups. Nevertheless, the absence of direct frailty measures remains an important limitation when interpreting the apparent similarity in outcomes between younger and older patients.

Minimally invasive surgery is another important consideration. Laparoscopic surgery has transformed CRC management in older adults. Recent systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have demonstrated that laparoscopy offers significant benefits in this population, including lower mortality, shorter hospital stay, faster bowel recovery, and reduced blood loss compared with open surgery [31, 32]. In our cohort, laparoscopy was performed in more than 95% of cases across age groups, which may have contributed to the relatively low morbidity observed overall. At the same time, this very high adoption of minimally invasive surgery may limit external generalizability, particularly to centers with lower laparoscopic volume or different operative infrastructure. Recent evidence also supports robotic surgery as a feasible alternative in elderly patients with comparable outcomes (34,35).

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce bias. Second, a central limitation is the absence of direct frailty measurements. Although balance in Charlson Comorbidity Index and ASA classification after matching partially mitigates this concern, objective frailty indices were not available in our dataset despite their known prognostic relevance in elderly surgical populations. Third, the matched sample size and relatively low number of some outcome events may have limited statistical power to detect modest but clinically meaningful differences between age groups, raising the possibility of type II error. This is particularly relevant for perioperative mortality, where the absolute difference between groups may still be clinically important despite the lack of conventional statistical significance. Fourth, information on the use and adherence to neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy may have been incomplete, potentially influencing survival outcomes. Finally, this was a single‐center study conducted in a high‐volume tertiary referral institution with dedicated colorectal surgeons and extensive minimally invasive experience. These characteristics may limit external generalizability, particularly to lower‐volume centers or healthcare systems with different resource availability and surgical infrastructure.

In conclusion, our findings support a shift away from age‐based surgical exclusion toward individualized assessment and shared decision‐making. Older patients with colorectal cancer who are selected for resection with primary anastomosis may achieve outcomes comparable to those of younger individuals in selected settings, although clinically meaningful differences—particularly in short‐term perioperative risk—cannot be entirely excluded given the observational design, limited number of events, and width of confidence intervals. These findings highlight the importance of routinely incorporating objective risk assessment tools and oncogeriatric principles rather than relying solely on chronological age to guide treatment decisions.
