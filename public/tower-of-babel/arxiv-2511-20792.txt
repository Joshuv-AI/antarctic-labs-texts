Higher-order Zeno sequences
Kasra Rajabzadeh Dizaji, Leeseok Kim, Milad Marvian, Christian Arenz

Abstract
The quantum Zeno effect typically refers to freezing the dynamics of a quantum system through frequent observations. In general, quantum Zeno dynamics is obtained with an error of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / N ) \mathcal{O}(1/N) , where N N is the number of projective measurements performed within a fixed evolution time. In this work, we develop higher-order Zeno sequences that achieve faster convergence to Zeno dynamics, yielding an improved error scaling of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / N 2 ​ k ) \mathcal{O}(1/N^{2k}) , where k k describes the order of the Zeno sequence. This is achieved by relating higher-order Zeno sequences to higher-order Trotter formulas that achieve similar convergence behavior. We leverage this relation to develop higher-order Zeno sequences for different manifestations of the quantum Zeno effect, including frequent projective measurements and unitary kicks. We go on to discuss achieving quantum Zeno dynamics through periodic control fields of high frequency. We explicitly develop control fields that yield a second-order type improvement in the Zeno error scaling and present shorter Zeno sequences. Finally, we discuss the connection to randomized and Uhrig dynamical decoupling to develop more efficient implementations in the weak coupling regime.



I
Introduction

The quantum Zeno effect is an intriguing effect in quantum mechanics. Frequent projective measurements can modify the evolution of a quantum system in a way that it becomes entirely frozen
26
;
16
or constrained to the (Zeno) subspace in which the dynamics can be more complex
14
;
5
;
1
. The quantum Zeno effect has been experimentally observed in various systems such as trapped ions
2
;
34
and photonic systems
23
;
28
.

Quantum Zeno dynamics can be achieved through frequent projective measurements, the rapid application of unitary kicks, or through the application of a periodic control field of high frequency
17
. Applications of the quantum Zeno effect include the preservation of spin polarization
29
, dosage reduction in neutron tomography
12
, decoherence suppression
15
;
32
, Hamiltonian simulation
11
, Hamiltonian learning
19
, and quantum control in general
24
;
20
.

The quantum Zeno effect is typically achieved when
N
projective measurements are performed at a rate
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
within a fixed evolution time
t
. More specifically, the probability of failing to obtain the system in its initial state at a time
t
when
N
projective measurements are performed in time intervals
\Delta t=\frac{t}{N}
vanishes as
\mathcal{O}(1/N)

16
. In a seminal paper by Grover et al.
10
, it has been shown that transitions to other states can be suppressed at a faster rate when the observed dynamics is additionally interspersed with the unitary reflection operator
R=P-Q
, where
P
is the projection describing the measurement and
Q
is its orthogonal complement. Building on these results, it was recently shown by some of the authors
11
that such a strategy can also be employed to achieve Zeno dynamics faster with a quadratically improved rate
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{2})
. Furthermore, very recently, higher-order Zeno sequences have been proposed based on linear combinations of previous sequences
27
. However, the implementation of these sequences through unitary operations and projective measurements remains challenging.

In this work, we generalize the results in
10
;
11
to develop higher-order Zeno formulas that yield convergence to Zeno dynamics at a rate
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{2k})
, where
k
describes the order of the sequence. This is achieved by relating higher-order Zeno sequences that are constructed through the inclusion of
R
with higher-order Trotter formulas. In turn, this relation allows leveraging the theory of higher-order Trotter sequences
8
to derive error bounds and obtain asymptotic Zeno error scaling estimates for the proposed higher-order Zeno sequences. We explicitly construct such sequences for the Zeno effect achieved with projective measurements and unitary kicks. For the latter, we show that the presented approach yields tighter bounds than the bounds previously reported in the literature
21
. We go on to discuss higher-order Zeno sequence implementations through periodic control fields and explicitly construct control fields that achieve second-order convergence. Finally, we discuss higher-order Zeno sequences that require significantly fewer reflection operations by exploiting their connection to Uhrig dynamical decoupling in the weak-coupling limit
35
;
36
, as well as randomized techniques that further improve convergence.

II
Higher-order Zeno sequences and Trotterization

Quantum Zeno dynamics can be achieved in various ways
17
. While quantum Zeno dynamics is typically obtained when a quantum system is frequently observed, the application of frequent unitary kicks and a periodic control field of high frequency can yield the same dynamics. Below, we start by developing higher-order Zeno formulas for Zeno dynamics achieved through projective measurements, followed by discussing implementations through unitary kicks and periodic control fields.

II.1
Higher-order Zeno formulas with projective measurements

Quantum Zeno dynamics is obtained in the (Zeno) limit
16
;
38
;
21
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}(Pe^{-iH\Delta t}P)^{N}=e^{-iPHPt}P,

(1)

where
\Delta t=t/N
is the time interval in which projective measurements described by the Hermitian projection
P=P^{2}
are performed. If
P=\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{\psi}|}
is the projector onto the initial state
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
, in the Zeno limit the dynamics becomes frozen as
PHP
yields a global phase
16
;
26
. However, if only parts of a quantum system are frequently observed, i.e., so that the projection is of the form
P=\mathds{1}\otimes\mathinner{|{\phi}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{\phi}|}
for some
\mathinner{|{\phi}\rangle}
, in the Zeno limit the evolution takes place in the Zeno subspace
14
that is not one-dimensional. In fact, the dynamics over the Zeno subspace can even be more complex than the dynamics over the full Hilbert space
5
;
1
.

The speed of convergence to Zeno dynamics can be determined by upper-bounding the error,

\displaystyle\epsilon=\left\lVert\left(Pe^{-iH\frac{t}{N}}P\right)^{N}-e^{-iPHPt}P\right\rVert,

(2)

that is obtained for finitely many projective measurements
N

21
,

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\frac{t^{2}\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{2}}{N},

(3)

where throughout this work
\|\cdot\|
denotes the spectral norm.
We note that the probability
p
of successfully implementing the sequence
\left(Pe^{-iH\Delta t}P\right)^{N}
can be lower bounded in a similar way, yielding
p\geq 1-2\frac{\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{2}t^{2}}{N}
. Thus, the speed of convergence to Zeno dynamics is of the order
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
. This result begs the question whether Zeno dynamics can be achieved faster, i.e., with fewer measurements to achieve a given precision. For example, can the speed of convergence be improved by performing projective measurements in different time intervals, similarly to achieving faster convergence through higher-order Trotter formulas? Unfortunately, this is not the case, as terms of the form
PH^{2}P
do not cancel to yield Zeno dynamics that is governed by
PHP
. However, building on
10
, some of the authors recently showed
11
that a second-order Zeno sequence that yields an
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{2})
convergence rate can be constructed through interspersing the observed dynamics with the unitary transformation
R=P-Q
where
P
and
Q
are orthogonal projections that satisfy
P+Q=\mathds{1}
. Indeed, we showed that a sequence of the form
\left(Pe^{-iH\Delta t/2}Re^{-iH\Delta t/2}P\right)^{N}
, yields a Zeno error,

\displaystyle\epsilon=\left\lVert\left(Pe^{-iH\Delta t/2}Re^{-iH\Delta t/2}P\right)^{N}-e^{-iPHPt}P\right\rVert,

(4)

that is upper-bounded by,

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\frac{t^{3}\|H\|^{3}}{3N^{2}}.

(5)

This sequence is implemented with a probability lower bounded by
p\geq 1-\frac{4\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{3}t^{3}}{3N^{2}}
.

We go on to generalize the approach described above to develop Zeno sequences that converge to Zeno dynamics at a rate
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{2k})
, where
k=1,2,\cdots
denotes the order of the sequence.
In particular, if we denote by
\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)
a
2k
th-order Zeno sequence that yields Zeno dynamics in the Zeno limit,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(P\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)P\right)^{N}=e^{-iPHPt}P,

(6)

and that satisfies,

\left\lVert P\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)P-e^{-iPHP\Delta t}P\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2k+1}}\right).

(7)

The Zeno error,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2k}=\left\lVert\left(P\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)P\right)^{N}-e^{-iPHPt}P\right\rVert,

(8)

associated with the
2k
-order sequence can be upper-bounded by using a telescoping type argument
21
to obtain the desired result,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2k}\leq N\left\lVert P\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)P-e^{-iPHP\Delta t}P\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2k}}\right).

(9)

We can construct
\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)
by using the theory of higher-order Trotter formulas
8
;
33
. This is achieved by first noting that the Hamiltonian can be written as,

\displaystyle H=H_{Z}+H_{PQ},

(10)

where,

\displaystyle H_{Z}=PHP+QHQ,

(11)

is the Zeno Hamiltonian that governs the Zeno dynamics, reducing to
PHP
when the dynamics is preceded by
P
as in (
1
). The term,

\displaystyle H_{PQ}=PHQ+QHP,

(12)

describes the coupling between the two orthogonal subspaces associated with
P
and
Q
.
Now, the key observation is that since,

\displaystyle RHR=H_{Z}-H_{PQ},

(13)

the term
H_{PQ}
is suppressed if we rapidly alternate between the evolution generated by
H
and
RHR
. Thus, the Trotter sequence
\left(e^{-iH\Delta t/2}Re^{-iH\Delta t/2}R\right)^{N}
yields, in the limit
N\to\infty
, Zeno dynamics governed by
H_{Z}
. If the Trotterization is done with different time intervals, involving a longer sequence, higher-order Trotter formulas
8
;
33
;
3
can be used to construct higher-order Zeno formulas. Specifically, if we denote by
\mathscr{S}_{2k}
a
2k
th-order Trotter formula for creating an evolution generated by
\mathcal{H}=H+RHR
with Trotter step size
\Delta t/2
(see
8
for an explicit construction), then,

\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{2k}-e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha(\Delta t/2)^{2k+1}\right),

(14)

where,

\alpha=\sum_{\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{2k+1}=1}^{2}\left\lVert[H_{\gamma_{2k+1}},\dots,[H_{\gamma_{2}},H_{\gamma_{1}}]]\right\rVert,

(15)

and,

H_{\gamma_{i}}=\begin{cases}H\qquad&\text{if}\qquad\gamma_{i}=1,\\
RHR\qquad&\text{if}\qquad\gamma_{i}=2.\end{cases}

(16)

We consequently obtain,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(P\mathscr{S}_{2k}P\right)^{N}-e^{-iPHPt}P\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\frac{t^{2k+1}}{N^{2k}}\right),

(17)

by a similar telescoping argument as in (
9
).
Thus, a
2k
th-order Zeno sequence
\mathcal{U}_{2k}(\Delta t)=\mathscr{S}_{2k}
can be constructed through a
2k
th-order Trotter sequence that alternates between the evolutions governed by
H
and
RHR
with a step size of
\Delta t/2
. With further details found in the Appendix
A
, the success probability for implementing the sequences is of the order
p_{2k}=1-\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{2}\frac{t^{4k+2}}{N^{4k+1}}\right)
.

II.2
Higher-order Zeno formulas with unitary kicks

Quantum Zeno dynamics can also be realized by rapidly interspersing the dynamics with a unitary transformation
U_{Z}
(“kick”) to obtain the Zeno sequence
\left(U_{Z}e^{-iH\Delta t}\right)^{N}
. For sufficiently large
N
, the dynamics is given by
U_{Z}^{N}e^{-iH_{Z}t}

4
;
13
, where
H_{Z}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}P_{l}HP_{l}
is the Zeno Hamiltonian obtained from the eigenprojectors
P_{l}
of
U_{Z}
with corresponding distinct eigenvalues
\{e^{-i\phi_{l}}\}
. The corresponding Zeno error,

\epsilon_{\text{kick}}=\left\|\left(U_{Z}e^{-iH\Delta t}\right)^{N}-U_{Z}^{N}e^{-iH_{Z}t}\right\|,

(18)

that is introduced for finite
N
can be upper bounded by
4
,

\epsilon_{\text{kick}}\leq\frac{2}{N}\left(\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\eta}+1\right)t\|H\|(1+2\|H\|t),

(19)

where
\eta=\min_{k\neq l}\left|e^{-i\phi_{k}}-e^{-i\phi_{l}}\right|
.

Now, by identifying the unitary kick
U_{Z}
with the reflection operator
R
so that the Zeno Hamiltonian is given by
H_{Z}=PHP+QHQ
, the bound becomes
11
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\text{kick}}\leq\frac{2}{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+1\right)\left\lVert H\right\rVert t(1+2\left\lVert H\right\rVert t).

(20)

We note that this upper bound does not depend on whether
H
and
RHR
commute or not. As such, in cases where
[H,RHR]=0
, the bound is not tight. The upper bound on the Zeno error
\epsilon_{\text{kick}}
can be improved to capture the scaling with the norm of the commutator of
H=H_{Z}+H_{PQ}
and
RHR=H_{Z}-H_{PQ}
, by observing that
(Re^{-iH\Delta t/2})^{2N}=\left(e^{-iRHR\Delta t/2}e^{-iH\Delta t/2}\right)^{N}
. As such, we can leverage the upper bound for the (first) Trotter formula
8
;
33
to obtain an improved bound for the Zeno error,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\text{kick}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{8N}\|[H,RHR]\|,

(21)

for Zeno dynamics achieved through unitary kicks
R
.

Higher-order Zeno formulas can be obtained in the unitary kick case by leveraging again higher-order Trotter formulas
\mathscr{S}_{2k}
that alternates between the evolution generated by
H
and
RHR
with a step size
\Delta t/2
. Analogously to the derivation that led to the scaling in (
17
), we find,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}-e^{-iH_{Z}t}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha\frac{t^{2k+1}}{N^{2k}}\right),

(22)

where
\alpha
is defined in (
15
).

II.3
Zeno dynamics through a periodic control field of high frequency

Achieving Zeno dynamics through unitary kicks can be viewed as a special case of a quantum system subject to a high-frequency periodic field
\phi(t)
that controls a projector
P
acting on the system. Namely, the evolution that is governed by the time-dependent Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H_{\text{tot}}(t)=H+\phi(t)P,

(23)

in the high-frequency limit, approximates the dynamics governed by the Zeno Hamiltonian
H_{Z}

15
. In particular, when the control field
\phi(t)
is chosen as a sequence of delta functions applied at discrete times (bang-bang control), the resulting dynamics reproduces the Zeno sequence corresponding to the unitary-kick formulation of the quantum Zeno effect
25
;
9
. We next discuss how Zeno dynamics can be achieved through a smooth modulation of
\phi(t)
, rather than delta-function-like pulses.

We consider the situation where a quantum system is subject to a periodic control field
\phi(t)
with period
T
, i.e.,
\phi(t+T)=\phi(t)
, whose coupling to the system is described by the Hamiltonian (
23
). The operator
U(t)
that describes the time evolution of the system up to time
t
can be decomposed as
31
,

U(t)=U_{c}(t)\tilde{U}(t),

(24)

where
U_{c}(t)=e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\phi(t^{\prime})P}
describes the time evolution of the system in the absence of
H
. The unitary operator
\tilde{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{H}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}
is governed by the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}(t)=U_{c}^{\dagger}(t)HU_{c}(t)
, that is given by,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(t)=H_{Z}+e^{i\Phi(t)}PHQ+e^{-i\Phi(t)}QHP,

(25)

where
\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\phi(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
is the integrated control field. If
\Phi(T)=2m\pi
where
m
is an integer, then the control propagator
U_{c}(t)
is cyclic, i.e.,
U_{c}(t+mT)=U_{c}(t)
, so that
U_{c}(mT)=\mathds{1}
. In this case, the total time evolution after a time
t=NT
is given by,

U(NT)=\left[\tilde{U}(T)\right]^{N}.

(26)

We go on to express
\tilde{U}(T)=e^{-iT\Omega(T)}
using the Magnus expansion
\Omega(T)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{n}
which converges as long as
T<\frac{\pi}{\|H\|}

31
. Since the first order of the Magnus expansion is given by
\Omega_{1}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{H}(t)dt
, while higher orders scale as
\mathcal{O}(\|H\|^{2}T^{2})
, we see from (
25
) that if the integrated control field satisfies,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{\pm i\Phi(t)}dt=0,

(27)

the first order of the Magnus expansion becomes
\Omega_{1}=H_{Z}
to yield Zeno dynamics. The error between the time evolution
U(t)
at time
t
and Zeno dynamics
e^{-iH_{Z}t}
is of the order of,

\|e^{-iH_{Z}t}-U(t)\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{2}t^{2}}{N}\right),

(28)

showing that Zeno dynamics is obtained for control fields with sufficiently large frequency
\frac{1}{T}\propto N
which satisfy (
27
).
This condition can, for example, be satisfied by a sinusoidal control field,

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\alpha\frac{2\pi}{T}\sin\frac{2\pi t}{T},

(29)

where
\alpha
is a root of the Bessel function of the first kind
18
;
39
.

Quantum Zeno dynamics can be achieved faster through control fields that satisfy the condition (
27
) while simultaneously suppressing higher orders
\Omega_{j},\penalty\ j>1
of the Magnus expansion. More specifically, a control field that satisfies (
27
) and suppresses the higher-order terms up to the
k
th-order, yields a Zeno error that is determined by the scaling
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\|H\|^{k+1}t^{k+1}}{N^{k}})
of the
k
th-order
\Omega_{k}
of the Magnus expansion. While finding control fields that suppress all orders up to
k
remains an open problem, since the control field (
29
) has the symmetry
\phi(T-t)=-\phi(t)
and
\Phi(T)=0
, implying that
\tilde{H}(T-t)=\tilde{H}(t)
; thus, all even orders of the Magnus expansion vanish
30
. As such, the sinusoidal control field (
29
) yields faster convergence
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\|H\|^{3}t^{3}}{N^{2}})
to Zeno dynamics (see Appendix
B
), similar to the second-order Zeno sequences developed in the previous sections.

III
Shorter Zeno sequences

In general, the higher-order Zeno sequences developed in the previous sections require the application of exponentially many reflection operators
R
. Specifically, realizing a Zeno sequence of order
2k
requires
2\cdot 5^{k-1}
reflection operators. In this section, we investigate how shorter sequences can be constructed that achieve the same Zeno error scaling while requiring fewer applications of
R
. For instance, consider the single Zeno step,

Pe^{-i\alpha H\Delta t}Re^{-i\beta H\Delta t}Re^{-i\beta H\Delta t}Re^{-i\alpha H\Delta t}P,

(30)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy,

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta=\frac{1}{2},

(31)

\displaystyle 8\alpha\beta^{2}=\frac{1}{6}.

(32)

These equations are approximately solved by
\alpha=0.675604
and
\beta=-0.175604
,
yielding a sequence that approximates
e^{-iPHP\,\Delta t}P
with an error scaling as
\mathcal{O}(\|H\|^{4}\Delta t^{4})
. Thus, the sequence (
30
) requires
3N
applications of
R
when repeated
N
times.
However, we note that without the projective measurements, i.e., when only unitary kicks described by
R
are used, the sequence converges to
e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}
with an error that scales as
\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{3}\Delta t^{3})
. This behavior is similar to the second-order Zeno sequence derived in
11
,

PRe^{-iH\Delta t/2}Re^{-iH\Delta t/2}P,

(33)

where without the projective measurements, the sequence yields an error scaling of
\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{2}\Delta t^{2})
. We remark that the first
R
is included in the sequence to relate the sequence with projective measurements to the sequence without projective measurements, noting that
PR=P
. Similarly, the sequence,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle e^{-i\alpha H\Delta t}Re^{-i\beta H\Delta t}Re^{-i\gamma H\Delta t}R

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\gamma H\Delta t}Re^{-i\beta H\Delta t}Re^{-i\alpha H\Delta t}P,

(34)

approximates
e^{-iPHP\Delta t}P
with an error of
\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{5}\Delta t^{5})
. It requires
5N
applications of
R
to achieve the same overall error scaling,
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{5}t^{5}}{N^{4}})
, as the fourth-order Zeno sequence developed in Section
II.1
, which requires
10N
applications of the reflection operator. The coefficients are determined by the set of equations,

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\frac{1}{2},

(35)

\displaystyle 8\beta(\alpha\beta+2\alpha\gamma+\gamma^{2})=\frac{1}{6},

(36)

\displaystyle 8\beta(\alpha^{2}\beta+2\alpha^{2}\gamma+2\alpha\gamma^{2}+\beta\gamma^{2})=\frac{1}{24}.

(37)

In general, higher-order Zeno sequences that yield a Zeno error scaling of
\mathcal{O}(\left\lVert H\right\rVert^{m+2}\Delta t^{m+2})
, while requiring only
2m-1
applications of
R
, can be constructed via a sequence of the form,

\displaystyle W_{m}(\Delta t)=Pe^{-iHx_{1}\Delta t}R\cdots Re^{-iHx_{2m}\Delta t}P.

(38)

The total number of algebraic equations that must be satisfied to achieve convergence of order
m+1
is
2^{m+1}-1
. However, not all of these equations are independent. In Appendix
C
, we explicitly examine the third-order sequence (
30
) to analyze the set of equations that arise from the Taylor expansion of the sequence.

IV
More efficient Zeno sequence in the weak coupling limit and randomization

In the special case where
\|H_{PQ}\|=J
is small compared to
\|H_{Z}\|=\beta
, one can design higher-order Zeno sequences that more effectively suppress the Zeno error. In this section, we discuss two constructions and the performance of the corresponding sequences in the weak-coupling regime characterized by
\beta\gg J
.

IV.1
Higher-order Zeno sequences based on Uhrig DD

As discussed earlier, a Zeno sequence of order
2k
derived from Trotter formulas can suppress the Zeno error up to order
\mathcal{O}((\|H\|\Delta t)^{2k+1})
, but at the cost of requiring an exponential (in
k
) number of reflection operations.
In this section, we present a Zeno sequence inspired by the Uhrig dynamical decoupling (UDD) protocol
35
;
36
. In the regime
\beta\gg J
(precisely specified below), both
2k
th-order Trotter- and
2k
th-order UDD-based Zeno sequences exhibit the same leading-order error scaling,
\mathcal{O}(J\beta^{2k}\Delta t^{2k+1})
, while our UDD-based construction requires only
2k
reflections, providing an exponential reduction in resource cost.

Figure 1
:
Log–log plot of the UDD-based Zeno sequence error
\epsilon_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}
given in (
40
) as a function of
\Delta t
for different
k=1,\cdots,6
. The dashed lines are linear fits that have slopes
\approx k+1
, which confirms the predicted scaling in the weak coupling regime. For smaller
\Delta t
, the error is dominated by the subleading term
\mathcal{O}(J^{2}\beta\Delta t^{3})
in (
40
), confirming a
\Delta t^{3}
scaling.

Fix
k\geq 1
, define the time intervals
t_{j}=\Delta t\sin^{2}\left({j\pi}/{2(k+1)}\right)
for
j=0,\dots,k+1
, and consider the following sequence:

\displaystyle\begin{split}U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)&=e^{-iH(\Delta t-t_{k})}Re^{-iH(t_{k}-t_{k-1})}R\\
&\qquad\cdots Re^{-iH(t_{2}-t_{1})}Re^{-iHt_{1}}.\end{split}

(39)

Then the Zeno error,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}=\bigl\lVert U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)-e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}\bigr\rVert,

(40)

for one Zeno step is of the order of
\epsilon_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}=\mathcal{O}(J\beta^{k}\Delta t^{k+1})+\mathcal{O}(J^{2}\beta\Delta t^{3})
. We refer to Appendix
D
for the details regarding the proof. The asymptotic scaling of the Zeno error for one Zeno step implies that, when
J<\beta^{k-1}\Delta t^{k-2}
, the Zeno error scales as
\mathcal{O}(J\beta^{k}\Delta t^{k+1})
by using only
k
applications of
R
.

As an example, we consider
H=\beta Z\otimes Z+J/2(X\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes X)
and
P=\frac{I+Z\otimes Z}{2}
, which yields
R=Z\otimes Z
. In this case,
\|H_{Z}\|=\|\beta Z\otimes Z\|=\beta
and
\|H_{PQ}\|=\|J/2(X\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes X)\|=J
, where we choose
\beta=1,J=10^{-4}
to satisfy the weak coupling assumption. In Fig.
1
, we numerically investigate the Zeno error (
40
) as a function of
\Delta t
for
k=3,4,5,6
. For large
\Delta t
(rightmost segments), each curve follows the predicted linear scaling with slope
\approx k+1
, demonstrating the
\mathcal{O}(J\beta^{k}\Delta t^{k+1})
behavior. However, the subleading term in (
40
),
\mathcal{O}(J^{2}\beta\Delta t^{3})
, overtakes the first term below a crossover scale
\sim(J/\beta^{k-1})^{1/(k-2)}
, so that for fixed values of
J,\beta
, the transition into the cubic slope occurs exponentially fast.

IV.2
Randomized higher-order Zeno sequences

In the weak-coupling regime, a simple randomization technique inspired by
37
can improve the error scaling further. Specifically, for a quantum state
\rho
, we consider a randomized protocol where, with probability
1/2
, either
\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}
or
R\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}R
is applied, described by the quantum channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2k}^{(N)}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}\rho{\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}}^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{2}(R\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}R)\rho(R\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}R)^{\dagger}.

(41)

Since conjugation by
R
leaves
H_{Z}
invariant while flipping the sign of
H_{PQ}
, all terms with an odd number of
H_{PQ}
’s in the error expansion cancel in
\mathcal{E}_{2k}^{(N)}
. Consequently, for
J\ll\beta
, (
22
) yields,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{N}-e^{-iH_{Z}t}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(J\beta^{2k}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{N^{2k}}\right),

(42)

whereas after randomization, the error is reduced to,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}_{2k}^{(N)}-\mathcal{U}_{0}\right\|_{\diamond}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\beta^{2k-1}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{N^{2k}}\right),

(43)

where
\mathcal{U}_{0}(\rho)=e^{-iH_{Z}t}\rho e^{iH_{Z}t}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\diamond}
denotes the diamond norm. This error scaling implies that when
J
is small compared to
\beta
, the randomization described by (
41
) yields a substantial improvement in the error scaling. In addition, it only requires two additional reflection operators. We refer to Appendix
E
for derivations and to Appendix
E.3
for the numerical investigation of the resulting advantage.

V
Conclusion

We developed higher-order Zeno sequences that yield faster convergence to Zeno dynamics, with an error scaling
\mathcal{O}\left(1/N^{2k}\right)
, where
k
describes the order of the sequence and
N
is the number of projective measurements performed in a fixed evolution time. This was achieved by interspersing the observed dynamics with a unitary reflection operator
R=P-Q
, where
P
is the projector describing the projective measurement and
Q
is its orthogonal complement. We showed that Zeno sequences constructed in this way can be related to higher-order Suzuki-Trotter formulas, which allowed us to leverage the corresponding error bounds
8
;
33
to develop bounds for the Zeno error and characterize its asymptotic scaling for Zeno dynamics achieved through higher-order Zeno sequences. We discussed implementations of the developed sequences through projective measurements and unitary kicks. In the unitary kick case, we developed an improved error bound that scales with the norm of the commutator of the Hamiltonian
H
governing the undriven evolution and
RHR
. We went on to discuss achieving Zeno dynamics faster through the application of a periodic control field of high frequency and explicitly constructed control fields that led to a second-order type error scaling.

We further proposed alternative Zeno sequences that realize third- and fourth-order convergence toward Zeno dynamics with fewer applications of
R
. Finally, we discussed more efficient implementations by drawing relations to Uhrig dynamical decoupling and developed a randomized scheme that yields further improvements in the Zeno error scaling.

Looking ahead, several open problems merit deeper investigation. First, it remains to be determined whether there exist other classes of continuous control fields capable of generating higher-order cancellations within the Magnus expansion. Second, a systematic framework is needed for analyzing the algebraic equations that emerge from the Taylor expansions of shorter Zeno sequences, which would yield higher-order Zeno sequences that can be implemented more efficiently, i.e., with fewer applications of the reflection operator
R
.

Appendix A
Success probability for implementing the higher-order Zeno sequences with projective measurements

We denote by
\tilde{p}_{2k}
the probability of successfully implementing a single Zeno step of a Zeno sequence of order
2k
, satisfying,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2k}=e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha\Delta t^{2k+1}).

(44)

Assuming that the initial state lies in the Zeno subspace, i.e.,
P\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
and
Q\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=0
, the probability
\tilde{p}_{2k}
is given by,

\tilde{p}_{2k}=\mathinner{\langle{\psi}|}\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}P\mathscr{S}_{2k}\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=\mathinner{\langle{\psi}|}P\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}P\mathscr{S}_{2k}P\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle},

(45)

where substituting

yields,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{2k}

\displaystyle=1-\mathinner{\langle{\psi}|}(P\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}Q)(Q\mathscr{S}_{2k}P)\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}

(46)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left\lVert(P\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}Q)(Q\mathscr{S}_{2k}P)\right\rVert

(47)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\lVert P\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}Q\rVert\left\lVert Q\mathscr{S}_{2k}P\right\rVert.

(48)

Since
\mathscr{S}_{2k}=e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha\Delta t^{2k+1})
, we have
\lVert P\mathscr{S}_{2k}^{\dagger}Q\rVert=\left\lVert Q\mathscr{S}_{2k}P\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(\alpha\Delta t^{2k+1})
, which implies,

\tilde{p}_{2k}=1-\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}\Delta t^{4k+2}).

(49)

Therefore, the total success probability
p_{2k}=\tilde{p}_{2k}^{N}
for implementing a sequence of
N
Zeno steps scales as,

p_{2k}=1-\mathcal{O}\left(\alpha^{2}\frac{t^{4k+2}}{N^{4k+1}}\right),

(50)

where

.

Appendix B
Time-symmetric continuous control

Assume the control field
\phi(t)
satisfies
\phi(T-t)=-\phi(t)

\displaystyle\Phi(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(s)ds=\int_{0}^{T/2}\big[\phi(s)+\phi(T-s)\big]ds=0,

(51)

\displaystyle\Phi(T-t)=\int_{0}^{T-t}\phi(s)ds=\int_{t}^{T}\phi(T-u)du=-\int_{t}^{T}\phi(u)du=-(\Phi(T)-\Phi(t))=\Phi(t),

(52)

\displaystyle\Phi(T-t)=\Phi(t).

(53)

Using (
25
) in the main text and (

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(T-t)=H_{Z}+e^{+i\Phi(T-t)}PHQ+e^{-i\Phi(T-t)}QHP=\tilde{H}(t),

\displaystyle\Omega_{2k}=0\quad(k=1,2,\ldots).

(55)

The sinusoidal control of (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{\pm i\Phi(t)}dt=\frac{T}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\pm i\alpha(1-\cos\theta)}d\theta=Te^{\pm i\alpha}J_{0}(\alpha)=0,

Appendix D
Proof of UDD error bound

Fix an integer
k\geq 1
, and define the switching times
t_{j}=\Delta t\sin^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{2(k+1)}\right)
for
j=0,\dots,k+1
. Let
F_{k}(t)=(-1)^{j}
for
t
in
(t_{j},t_{j+1})
, and consider the piecewise-constant Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H(t)=H_{Z}+F_{k}(t)H_{PQ},\qquad 0\leq t\leq\Delta t,

(67)

that generates the time evolution operator,

\displaystyle U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{\Delta t}H(t)dt\right).

(68)

Then the following estimate holds,

\displaystyle\bigl\|U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)-e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}\bigr\|=\mathcal{O}\bigl(J\beta^{k}\Delta t^{k+1}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(J^{2}\beta\Delta t^{3}\bigr),

(69)

where we recall that
\beta=\|H_{Z}\|
and
J=\|H_{PQ}\|
.

To prove the above asymptotic scaling, we first move to the interaction picture with respect to
H_{Z}
, and write
H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t)=e^{iH_{Z}t}H_{PQ}e^{-iH_{Z}t}
, which satisfies
\|H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t)\|=J
. Then,

\displaystyle U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)=e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}V(\Delta t),\qquad V(\Delta t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{\Delta t}F_{k}(t)H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t)dt\right).

(70)

Expanding
V
through the Dyson series
V(\Delta t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Delta_{n}
gives,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=(-i)^{n}\int_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<\Delta t}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}F_{k}(t_{j})\right)H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t_{n})\cdots H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t_{1})dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}.

(71)

If we substitute the series
H_{PQ}^{(I)}(t)=\sum_{p\geq 0}C_{p}t^{p}
with
C_{p}=\frac{i^{p}}{p!}\operatorname{ad}_{H_{Z}}^{p}(H_{PQ})
(where
\|C_{p}\|\leq 2^{p}\beta^{p}J/p!
), and make a change of variables
t_{j}=\Delta t\tau_{j}
, we find,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=(-i)^{n}\sum_{\vec{p}\geq 0}C_{p_{n}}\cdots C_{p_{1}}(\Delta t)^{n+\sum_{j}p_{j}}F_{\vec{p}},

(72)

where,

\displaystyle F_{\vec{p}}=\int_{0<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{n}<1}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}F_{k}(\Delta t\tau_{j})\tau_{j}^{p_{j}}\right)d\tau_{1}\cdots d\tau_{n}.

(73)

We first analyze the contributions from odd Dyson orders. It was shown in
36
that
F_{\vec{p}}=0
for every odd
n\leq k
whenever
n+\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\leq k
, so all such odd orders disappear. The first contributing odd term occurs at
n=1
with
p_{1}=k
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}=(-i)C_{k}(\Delta t)^{k+1}\int_{0}^{1}F_{k}(\Delta t\tau)\tau^{k}d\tau.

(74)

The integral is
\mathcal{O}(1)
because
|F_{k}|\leq 1
, which yields,

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{1}\|=\mathcal{O}\bigl(J\beta^{k}(\Delta t)^{k+1}\bigr).

(75)

We next analyze the even Dyson orders. The lowest surviving even-order term is
n=2
,
p_{1}+p_{2}=0
, which gives
p_{1}=p_{2}=0
. However, we note that
F_{p_{1}=0,p_{2}=0}=0
, as,

\displaystyle F_{0,0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}F_{k}(\Delta t\tau_{2})F_{k}(\Delta t\tau_{1})d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}F_{k}(\Delta t\tau)d\tau\right)^{2}=0,

(76)

because the average of
F_{k}
on
[0,\Delta t]
is zero for all
k\geq 1
. The lowest non-zero even contribution is therefore
n=2
with
p_{1}+p_{2}=1
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}=(-i)^{2}(\Delta t)^{3}\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}=1}C_{p_{2}}C_{p_{1}}F_{p_{1},p_{2}},

(77)

and the bounds
\|C_{0}\|\leq J
,
\|C_{1}\|\leq 2\beta J
, and
|F_{0,1}|,|F_{1,0}|=\mathcal{O}(1)
give,

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{2}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\beta(\Delta t)^{3}\right).

(78)

Combining the leading odd and even contributions, and using
\|V(\Delta t)-I\|\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\|\Delta_{n}\|
, gives,

\displaystyle\left\|U_{k}^{\rm{UDD}}(\Delta t)-e^{-iH_{Z}\Delta t}\right\|=\left\|V(\Delta t)-I\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(J\beta^{k}\Delta t^{k+1}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\beta\Delta t^{3}\right).

(79)

Finally, the piecewise-constant Hamiltonian in (
67
) generates the sequence described in (
39
), which directly yields (
40
).

We note that the “pure UDD” error scaling exhibits
\mathcal{O}((\|H\|\Delta t)^{k+1})
(where
H=H_{Z}+H_{PQ}
), i.e., no
\Delta t^{3}
term is present
35
;
36
. This holds when (i) the error is evaluated on the subsystem (of the protected subspace), and (ii) the subsystem parts of the preserved and orthogonal subspaces commute. Specifically, let
H_{Z}=A\otimes B
and
H_{PQ}=A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime}
, where
A
and
A^{\prime}
are Pauli operators with
[A,A^{\prime}]=0
. In this case, the interaction-picture unitary in (
70
) simplifies to,

\displaystyle V(\Delta t)=\mathds{1}+\sum_{a=\pm 1}P_{a}\otimes B^{(a)}+\mathcal{O}((\|H\|\Delta t)^{k+1}),

(80)

where
P_{\pm}=(\mathds{1}\pm A)/2
are the projectors onto the subsystem
A

B^{(a)}
represents the operator acting on the second subsystem, arising from the even-order Dyson-series contributions. If the initial state is supported in a single eigenspace
P_{a}

36

Mixing Lemma

The mixing lemma

7

\{U_{j}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p_{j}U_{j}\rho U_{j}^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{V}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger},

where

is unitary. Then the (sharpened) mixing lemma
7
states that,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{V}\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p_{j}U_{j}-V\right\|.

(82)

E.2
Randomized higher-order Zeno sequence
