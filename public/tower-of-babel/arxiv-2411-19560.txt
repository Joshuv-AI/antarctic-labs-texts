Updating Katz centrality by counting walks
Francesca Arrigo, Daniele Bertaccini, Alessandro Filippo

Abstract
We develop efficient and effective strategies for the update of Katz centralities after node and edge removal in simple graphs. We provide explicit formulas for the “loss of walks” a network suffers when nodes/edges are removed, and use these to inform our algorithms. The theory builds on the newly introduced concept of ℱ \mathcal{F} -avoiding first-passage walks. Further, bounds on the change of total network communicability are also derived. Extensive numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world networks complement our theoretical results.



1
Motivation

Katz centrality is one of the most popular centrality indices in network science
[
29
,
18
,
34
]
,
which has successfully been applied to a wide range of problems, such as, for example, the identification of potential disease genes in protein-interaction networks
[
42
]
or the optimization of artificial neural networks
[
31
]
.
This measure’s popularity is due to its interpretability in terms of walks around a graph
but also to the easiness of its computation
[
5
,
35
]
. Indeed, Katz centrality can be expressed as the solution to a (usually sparse) linear system, an extensively studied problem that is accompanied by a well-developed set of numerical linear algebra software libraries; see, e.g.,
[
9
,
15
,
37
]
and references therein.
Moreover, Katz centrality can be easily extended to the setting of dynamic networks
[
22
]
or specialised to only consider particular types of walks
[
21
]
without affecting its easiness of computation, thus making it possibly the most widely used walk-based centrality measure in the field.

In this paper, we consider the problem of approximately updating the vector of Katz centrality and its “personalised” counterpart
[
7
]
after the sequential removal/failure of a set of nodes or edges from a simple network.

This is particularly relevant in real life applications.
Indeed, in many real-world networks nodes and/or edges evolve over time
[
13
,
17
,
1
]
. For example, an edge being removed could correspond to road closure within a road network, while a node being deleted could model a user leaving a social media platform.
When centrality is of interest in these and similar situations, recomputing the vector of centrality scores may not always be feasible, especially for extremely large networks. Approximating strategies like the ones presented in this paper may be devised instead.
Additionally, our results may also be useful in the context of network design and network optimization, where simulating the removal of edges is often used as a way to address issues related to the robustness of the network or the presence communication bottlenecks
[
25
,
27
,
11
,
2
]
.

Several updating strategies for centrality vectors have been devised over the years
[
30
,
38
]
and, for walk-based centralities, the problem has been extensively studied (whether directly of undirectly) from the perspective of small rank updates of matrix functions and sensitivity of their entries; see, e.g.,
[
40
,
4
,
8
,
36
,
16
,
39
]
.
For Katz centrality in particular, a few ad-hoc strategies have been proposed. In
[
32
]
the authors proposed to update centralities via an iterative refinement method applied to the original Katz vector, while
[
33
]
developed a visiting algorithm to modify the overall count of walks up to a specific length.

We take a different approach and devise an approximation strategy that builds on the combinatorics of walks that are lost due to node/edge removal
and, more specifically, on recurrence formulas that count
first-passage walks

[
28
]
, i.e., walks between two nodes that exclusively visit the final node in their last step.

From our approximations, we also derive bounds on the change in the total network communicability induced by Katz centrality
[
6
]
when edges/nodes are removed from the original graph.
These bounds, together with our formulas for the updated Katz centrality scores, could be exploited to identify the new information propagators in social networks after node/edge removal; see
[
41
]
.

It follows from this definition, that

A

an ordered sequence of

We will say that a walk

Definition 1.1
.

Let

. We will call a

The following is a standard result in graph theory and can be easily proved, e.g., by induction.

.

The
(i,j)

to

An undirected graph is said to be

Remark 1.1

This simplifying assumption does not weaken the results presented in later sections. Indeed, if a graph was disconnected, then its adjacency matrix would be permutation similar to a block diagonal matrix. Our results would then apply straightforwardly to the individual diagonal blocks.

The last definition we need in this section is that of

Throughout this paper we will adopt the following notation;

1.1.1
Walk-based centrality measures

Centrality measures are a standard tool in network analysis. Customarily, they assign a nonnegative score to entities in a graph (e.g., nodes, edges) to quantify their importance. The larger the score, the more important the entity. In this paper, we are concerned with node centrality measures. Several concepts of centralities have been introduced over the years; see, e.g.,
[
18
, Chapter 7]
and
[
34
, Chapter 7]
.
One important class of measures is defined in terms of entries (or sums thereof) of the formal series

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}A^{r}=b_{0}I+b_{1}A+b_{2}A^{2}+\cdots

where
b_{r}\geq 0
are weights. The above expression has a nice interpretation in terms of walks taking place around the network, thanks to Lemma
1.1
, and formalizes a class of measures commonly referred to as
walk-based centrality measures
; see
[
5
, Section 3]
and references therein. Depending on the choice of weights
b_{r}
and on the spectral properties of
A
, the formal series may converge to a matrix function
f(A)
; see, e.g.,
[
24
, Section 1.2]
. In network science, the most popular choices for the weights are
b_{r}=\alpha^{r}
, for some
\alpha>0
, and
b_{r}=\beta^{r}/(r!)
, for some
\beta>0
, which give rise to the matrix resolvent and exponential, respectively.

In this paper, we will focus on
b_{r}=\alpha^{r}
, for some
\alpha>0
. The induced centrality measure is known as
Katz centrality
. Introduced in
[
29
]
it assigns to node
i
the
i
th entry of the vector

{\bf x}:=(I+\alpha A+\alpha^{2}A^{2}+\cdots)\mathbf{1}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{r}A^{r}\mathbf{1}

where
\alpha>0
is a damping parameter. Selecting
\alpha
so that
0<\alpha\rho(A)<1
, where
\rho(A)
is the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix
A
, as we will do in the remainder of the paper, ensures convergence of the above series, and thus the vector of Katz centralities
{\bf x}=(x_{i})
can be seen to be the solution to the (usually sparse) linear system

(I-\alpha A){\bf x}=\mathbf{1}.

1.2
Problem setting and outline

The goal of this paper is to develop efficient algorithms based on the combinatorics of walks for updating the vector of Katz centrality after node/edge removal(s).
We will assume that the removal of an edge
\{i,j\}\in E
from a network corresponds to setting to zero the relevant entries of the original adjacency matrix. Similarly, the removal of node
i
is modelled as the removal of all the edges adjacent to it, so that the dimension of the adjacency matrix of the new graph is the same as that of the original graph. Both removal operations correspond to rank-2 modifications of
A

A-[{\bf e}_{i},\,{\bf e}_{j}][{\bf e}_{j},\,{\bf e}_{i}]^{T}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad A-[{\bf e}_{i},\,{\bf a}_{i}][{\bf a}_{i},\,{\bf e}_{i}]^{T}.

Given a set
\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
of vertices or edges, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}\subset V
or
\mathcal{S}\subset E
, we will denote by
{\bf c}^{\mathcal{S}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the vector whose
i
th entry
({\bf c}^{\mathcal{S}}_{r})_{i}
is the number of walks of length
r>0
1
1

1

When
r=0
we will set
{\bf c}_{0}^{\mathcal{S}}={\bf 0}
.
originating from node
i
, ending anywhere in the network, and visiting
at least one
of the elements in
\mathcal{S}
, for all

Entrywise, it holds that
A^{r}\mathbf{1}\geq{\bf c}^{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
. The following result follows trivially from Lemma

Lemma 1.2
.

Let

be the adjacency matrix of the graph obtained from

by removing all the elements in

, then

{\bf c}^{\mathcal{S}}_{r}=(A^{r}-A_{\mathcal{S}}^{r})\mathbf{1}.

(1)

Our algorithms will build on alternative expressions for the computation of
{\bf c}^{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
, obtained using the concepts of first-passage walks (cf., Definition

) and of the newly introduced
\mathcal{F}
-avoiding first-passage walks; see Definition

Remark 1.2
.

The formulas derived in this paper are easily adapted to the case where the entries of

are set to represent walks that end at a specific (subset of) node(s) rather than at any node. To accommodate this change, one would need to replace every instance of

Since (entrywise)

The paper is organized as follows. The next two sections will be devoted to finding equivalent expressions for

In Section

2
Counting walks through a set to edges
\mathcal{E}

We begin by addressing the problem of counting walks that are forced to pass through a specific edge (
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{E}=\{e\}\subset E
). This result will then be generalized to the case where walks have to visit at least one element in a larger set of edges, and hence, in the next section, to the problem of counting walks that visit at least one node in a set. The key simple observation that we will exploit is that all walks that visit a given edge
e
can be uniquely split into three parts; an initial walk that does not visit
e
, a step visiting edge
e
(in either direction), and finally another walk that roams freely around the graph.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
e=\{u,v\}\in E
,
\mathcal{E}=\{e\}
, and
r>0
. It holds that

{\bf c}_{r}^{\{e\}}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}\left({\bf e}_{u}{\bf e}_{v}^{T}+{\bf e}_{v}{\bf e}_{u}^{T}\right)A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1},

(2)

where

A_{\mathcal{E}}=A-\mathbf{e}_{u}{\mathbf{e}_{v}}^{T}-\mathbf{e}_{v}{\mathbf{e}_{u}}^{T}
is the
adjacency matrix of the graph obtained after the removal of
e
from
G
.

Before proving the result, we want to remark two things. Firstly, for any given
i\in V
,
{\bf e}_{i}^{T}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{u}=0
(resp.,
{\bf e}_{i}^{T}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{v}=0
) for all
k<\ell_{u}:={\rm dist}(i,u)
(resp.,
k<\ell_{v}:={\rm dist}(i,v)
), so that the above can be rewritten entrywise as

({\bf c}_{r}^{\{e\}})_{i}={\bf e}_{i}^{T}\left(\sum_{k=\ell_{u}}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{u}{\bf e}_{v}^{T}A^{r-k-1}+\sum_{k=\ell_{v}}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{v}{\bf e}_{u}^{T}A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1}.

(3)

Secondly, if
r\leq{\rm dist}(i,e):=\min\{\ell_{u},\ell_{v}\}
, then
r-1<{\rm dist}(i,e)
and thus both summations in the right-hand side of (
3
) are zero.
Therefore we can assume that
r\geq{\rm dist}(i,e)+1
whenever we are working on a specific
i\in V
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We proceed entrywise. Let
i\in V
and let
\ell_{x}={\rm dist}(i,x)
for
x=u,v
. Suppose wlog that
\ell_{u}\leq\ell_{v}
; if
r\leq\ell_{u}
, then there are no walks of length
r
originating from
i
and visiting edge
e
, therefore
({\bf c}_{r}^{\mathcal{E}})_{i}=0
. Similarly, for all
k=0,\ldots,r-1
it holds that
{\bf e}_{i}^{T}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{x}=0
for
x=u,v
and thus equality holds in (
2
).
Suppose now that
r>\ell_{u}
is fixed. Every walk of such length can be uniquely split into three consecutive terms; a walk of length
0\leq k<r
that does not visit
e
, followed by
e
(in either direction), and then by a walk of length
r-(k+1)
. For any
r>\ell_{u}
, these can be counted using
{\bf e}_{i}^{T}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{x}{\bf e}_{y}^{T}(A^{r-k-1}\mathbf{1})
, where either
x=u
and
y=v
or
x=v
and
y=u
, depending on which between
u
and
v
is visited first. Letting
k
vary and splitting the terms depending on which between
u
or
v
is first visited yields the result.∎

Our formula, which allows us to count all walks originating from a node and ending at any node after visiting a given edge at least once, can be easily generalized to the situation where we are visiting at least one element in a larger set of edges.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset E
be a set of edges
|\mathcal{E}|\geq 1
and assume that, for every
e\in\mathcal{E}
,
e=\{u_{e},v_{e}\}
.
Then,

{\bf c}_{r}^{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}{\bf c}_{r}^{\{e\}}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}\left({\bf e}_{u_{e}}{\bf e}_{v_{e}}^{T}+{\bf e}_{v_{e}}{\bf e}_{u_{e}}^{T}\right)A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1},

where
A_{\mathcal{E}}=A-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}({\bf e}_{u_{e}}{{\bf e}_{v_{e}}}^{T}+{\bf e}_{v_{e}}{\mathbf{e}_{u_{e}}}^{T})

is the adjacency matrix of
graph obtained from
G
after edges in
\mathcal{E}
are removed.

The proof of this result is in essence the same as that of Proposition
2.1
, and is therefore omitted.

Remark 2.1
.

From the definition of
A_{\mathcal{E}}
it immediately follows that

{\bf c}_{r}^{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}({A_{\mathcal{E}}})^{k}A^{r-k}\mathbf{1}-\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}({A_{\mathcal{E}}})^{k+1}A^{r-k-1}\mathbf{1}.

(4)

A natural extension of Proposition
2.2
is presented in the next section, where we describe formulas to count walks that visit at least one node in a set
\mathcal{N}\subset V

3
Counting walks through a set of nodes
\mathcal{N}

In this section we consider the problem of counting walks that visit at least one node in a set. This will in turn allow us to provide formulas to update the vector of Katz centrality when a graph is updated through node(s) removal; see Section
4
.
For our purposes, we identify a node
w
with the set of edges it connects to. This will allow us to adapt the results from the previous section to our current problem.

3.1
One node

We begin by considering the case of one node
w
.
Let thus
\mathcal{N}=\{w\}
and consider the corresponding set of edges

\mathcal{E}=\{\{w,v\}\in E\,:\,w\in\mathcal{N}\}.

Then, the vector
{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}
whose entries count the number of walks of length
r
that visit
w
at least once is defined as

{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}:={\bf c}_{r}^{\mathcal{E}}

and moreover it holds

\displaystyle{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}={\bf c}_{r}^{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{w,v\}\in\mathcal{E}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}({\bf e}_{w}{\bf e}_{v}^{T}+{\bf e}_{v}{\bf e}_{w}^{T})A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}A_{vw}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}({\bf e}_{w}{\bf e}_{v}^{T}+{\bf e}_{v}{\bf e}_{w}^{T})A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}({\bf e}_{w}{\bf a}_{w}^{T}+{\bf a}_{w}{\bf e}_{w}^{T})A^{r-k-1}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\left((A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}{\bf e}_{w}{\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k}+(A_{\mathcal{E}})^{k}A{\bf e}_{w}{\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k-1}\right)\mathbf{1}

where we exploited the fact that
\sum_{v\in V}A_{vw}{\bf e}_{v}={\bf a}_{w}=A{\bf e}_{w}
. Now, by introducing the notation
A_{\mathcal{N}}:=A_{\mathcal{E}}
for
\mathcal{E}=\{\{w,v\}\in E:w\in\mathcal{N}\}
and observing that

(A_{\mathcal{N}})^{k}{\bf e}_{w}=\begin{cases}{\bf e}_{w}&\text{if }k=0,\\
{\bf 0}&\text{if }k>0,\end{cases}

the above rewrites as

{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}=({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r}\mathbf{1})\,{\bf e}_{w}+\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k-1}\mathbf{1})(A_{\mathcal{N}})^{k}A{\bf e}_{w}.

(5)

The terms
(A_{\mathcal{N}})^{k}A{\bf e}_{w}
appearing in (
5
) are of particular interest to us. For any given
k=0,\ldots,r-1
, the entries of this vector count the number of all those walks of length
k+1
that consist of a walk of length
k
that does not visit
w
at any point, followed by one step to reach node
w
, if such step exists. In fact, these are all the first-passage walks (FPWs) from any node to
w
in
G
; cf. Definition
1.1
. Being able to count FPWs efficiently, especially when nodes are sequentially removed one by one, is at the heart of the algorithms proposed in Section
6
.

For any given walk-length
{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote by
({\bf q}_{k})_{i}
the number of FPWs of length
k
originating from
i
(and ending at node
w
).
For
i\neq w
, it holds that

({\bf q}_{k})_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0\leq k<{\rm dist}(i,w),\\
(A_{\mathcal{N}}^{k-1}A{\bf e}_{w})_{i}&\text{if }k\geq{\rm dist}(i,w).\end{cases}

(6)

On the other hand, when
i=w
, we have

({\bf q}_{k})_{w}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }k=0,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Using this notation, (
5
) can be rewritten as

{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}=({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r}\mathbf{1})\,{\bf e}_{w}+\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k-1}\mathbf{1}){\bf q}_{k+1}=({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r}\mathbf{1})\,{\bf e}_{w}+\sum_{k=1}^{r}({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k}\mathbf{1}){\bf q}_{k},

and thus we have proved the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}
be the vector whose entries count the number of walks of length
r
ending at any node after visiting
\mathcal{N}=\{w\}
at least once; then

{\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k}\mathbf{1}){\bf q}_{k}.

(7)

The expression in (

) can be written entrywise for all

({\bf c}_{r}^{\{w\}})_{i}=({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r}\mathbf{1})\,\delta_{iw}+\sum_{k=\ell_{i}}^{r}({\bf e}_{w}^{T}A^{r-k}\mathbf{1})({\bf q}_{k})_{i},

where
\ell_{i}={\rm dist}(i,w)
.

The first approach uses the relationships derived in the previous section to define

\frac{x_{i}^{\{w\}}}{x_{i}}\leq 1-\alpha\frac{x_{w}}{x_{i}}.

Proof.

Using (
13
) and the fact that
{\rm dist}(i,w)=1
and
({\bf q}_{1})_{i}=1
since the graph is simple, one gets

x_{i}-x_{i}^{\{w\}}=x_{w}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{r}({\bf q}_{r})_{i}\geq\alpha x_{w}.

Dividing both sides by
x_{i}>1
and rearranging yields the conclusion.
∎

5
Bounds on total network communicability

In the previous section we described closed formulas that allow to compute the change in Katz centrality of nodes when edges or nodes are removed from a given simple connected graph.

In this section we use the results from Corollary
4.2
and Corollary
4.4
to find an upper bound on
the change of the
total network communicability

[
6
]
, here defined as

TC=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{T}{\bf x}}{n},

after the removal of a node or an edge
from a graph.
Throughout this section, we will denote by
TC^{\mathcal{S}}
the total communicability of the graph obtained from
G
after removal of elements in set
\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Given
\mathcal{N}=\{w\}\subset V
it holds that

TC-TC^{\{w\}}\leq\frac{1}{n}(x_{w}^{2}(1-\alpha^{2}{\rm deg}(w))-1),

(14)

where
{\rm deg}(w)
is the degree of node
w
.

Proof.

Let us consider the set of edges
\mathcal{E}=\{\{w,v\}\in E\,:\,w\in\mathcal{N}\}
. From Corollary
4.1
and symmetry of
A_{\mathcal{E}}
, it follows that

\mathbf{1}^{T}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\mathcal{E}})=\alpha\mathbf{1}^{T}(I-\alpha A_{\mathcal{E}})^{-1}\sum_{v:\,v\sim w}\left(x_{w}{\bf e}_{v}+x_{v}{\bf e}_{w}\right)=\alpha x_{w}\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v}^{\mathcal{E}}+x_{w}^{\mathcal{E}}\alpha\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v}.

Since, by definition of Katz centrality,
x_{w}^{\mathcal{E}}=1
and

x_{w}=1+\alpha\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v},

(15)

we get

\mathbf{1}^{T}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\mathcal{E}})=\alpha x_{w}\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v}^{\mathcal{E}}+(x_{w}-1)

The fact that
{\bf x}^{\mathcal{E}}={\bf x}^{\{w\}}
, Proposition
4.2
, and (
15
) imply

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{T}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\{w\}})

\displaystyle=x_{w}\left(1+\alpha\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v}\left(\frac{x_{v}^{\{w\}}}{x_{v}}\right)\right)-1

\displaystyle\leq x_{w}\left(1+\alpha\sum_{v\sim w}x_{v}\left(1-\alpha\frac{x_{w}}{x_{v}}\right)\right)-1

\displaystyle=x_{w}\left(x_{w}-\alpha^{2}\,x_{w}\,{\rm deg}(w)\right)-1

\displaystyle=x_{w}^{2}(1-\alpha^{2}\,{\rm deg}(w))-1,

from which (
14
) immediately follows.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
e=\{u,v\}
and consider
\mathcal{E}=\{e\}
. Then

TC-TC^{\{e\}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(2\alpha x_{u}x_{v}-\alpha^{2}x_{u}^{2}-\alpha^{2}x_{v}^{2}\right).

(16)

Proof.

From Corollary
4.2
and symmetry of
A_{\mathcal{E}}

we obtain

\mathbf{1}^{T}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\{e\}})=\alpha\mathbf{1}^{T}\left(I-\alpha A_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\right)(x_{u}{\bf e}_{v}+x_{v}{\bf e}_{u})=\alpha x_{u}x_{v}^{\{e\}}+\alpha x_{v}x_{u}^{\{e\}}.

Now by applying Proposition
4.1
we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{T}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\{e\}})

\displaystyle=\alpha x_{u}x_{v}\left(\frac{x_{v}^{\{e\}}}{x_{v}}+\frac{x_{u}^{\{e\}}}{x_{u}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha x_{u}x_{v}\left(1-\alpha\frac{x_{u}}{x_{v}}+1-\alpha\frac{x_{v}}{x_{u}}\right)

\displaystyle{=}2\alpha x_{u}x_{v}-\alpha^{2}x_{u}^{2}-\alpha^{2}x_{v}^{2},

from which the conclusion straightforwardly follows.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

Consider now the function
f_{\alpha}(x,y)=2\alpha xy-\alpha^{2}x^{2}-\alpha^{2}y^{2}
appearing in (
16
) and assume that
0<\alpha<1
. Let
T>t>1
, then it can be showed that

\argmin_{(x,y)\in[t,T]\times[t,T]}f_{\alpha}(x,y)=\begin{cases}(t,t)&\text{ if }T<\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}t\\
(t,T)&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Hence, the bounds in Theorem
5.2
can be used to devise strategies for optimizing total communicability through edge removal, similar to the ones in
[
2
]
. Notably, if
T<\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}t
, we find that

\argmin_{(x,y)\in[t,T]\times[t,T]}f_{\alpha}(x,y)\,=\argmin_{(x,y)\in[t,T]\times[t,T]}xy\,=\,(t,t),

which suggests that, in order to
minimize the impact on total network communicability of an edge
removal in a network such that
\max_{i}{x_{i}}<\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}\min_{i}{x_{i}}
, one should remove the edge
e=\{u,v\}
that minimizes the
product
x_{u}x_{v}
. This was in fact one of the heuristics proposed in
[
2
]
for
solving the
downdate problem
considered there.

6
Walk-induced update of Katz centrality

In this section, we propose algorithms to find an approximate value of Katz centrality for the nodes in a simple graph
G
after elements from a chosen set
\mathcal{S}
are removed. The results presented here build on the theory developed so far, and assume that the values of the original Katz centralities, stored in a vector
{\bf x}
, are available. The algorithms developed in this section are especially useful in the context where the elements of
\mathcal{S}
are
sequentially
removed from
G
. This would normally require solving
|\mathcal{S}|
sparse linear systems, which can be burdensome when
n
is large. On the other hand, the methods we propose will allow updating the centrality vectors with
approximations
to the actual values, and only sporadically solving a sparse linear system. As we will show, this reduces the computational effort of finding Katz centrality when nodes or edges are sequentially removed from
G
, while at the same time achieving good approximations of the new centrality scores.

Throughout this section, approximate values will be denoted by
\widehat{{\bf x}}
.

6.1
Removing nodes

We begin with the problem of updating the vector of Katz centralities after a node is removed from
G
. The strategy we propose builds on the result presented in Corollary
4.4
and simply truncates the infinite sum to
L

is a user selected parameter.
The proposed approximation, for

\widehat{{\bf x}}^{\{w\}}:={\bf x}-x_{w}\sum_{r=1}^{L}\alpha^{r}{\bf q}_{r},

where

to

\widehat{x}_{w}^{\{w\}}=x_{w}^{\{w\}}=1

6.3
Computational cost

The following holds.

Proposition 6.1
.

For a sparse network, the cost of Algorithms
1
and
2
is
\mathcal{O}(Lm)
operations, where
m
is the number of undirected edges in the original graph.

Proof.

For Algorithm
1
, at every step of the loop we perform a matrix-vector product on line
6
and
n
multiplications on lines
6
and
8
. Thus, the computational complexity of Algorithm
1
can be estimated as
\mathcal{O}(LC+2Ln)
operations, where

C
is the cost of performing one matrix-vector product.
Similarly, one can deduce that Algorithm
2

requires
\mathcal{O}(2LC+4Ln)
operations.
The conclusion follows by recalling the reasonable assumption that
C=\mathcal{O}(m)
for sparse matrices with
2m
nonzero entries.
∎

Clearly,
Algorithms
1
and
2
can be generalized to the case where a larger set of edges (resp., nodes) is
simultaneously
removed from the graph. To do so, it is sufficient to truncate the infinite series in
(
11
) (resp., (
12
)) to
L
terms. In this case it is easy to see that, under the same assumption of Proposition
6.1
, the generalized algorithms would have a computational complexity in time of
O(|\mathcal{S}|Lm)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of elements being removed. In addition, since (
11
) and (
12
) are both described as sums of
|\mathcal{S}|
independent terms, one could use a parallel implementation on, e.g.,
|\mathcal{S}|
processors, to compute each term independently, hence achieving the theoretical computational complexity of
O(Lm)
per processor.

is the residual at the
(k-1)
th iteration.

The results show that our strategy (iii) requires fewer iterations to converge, and thus smaller CPU times, compared to the other methods considered, especially in the case of large networks. Moreover, the number of iterations required to achieve convergence by methods (i) with

{\bf x}^{(0)}={\bf 0}

pcg(

An alternative explanation may also be given, for method (iii) only, in terms of the combinatorics of walks. Indeed, methods (i) with
{\bf v}={\bf 0}

8
Conclusions and future work

In this paper we derived explicit formulas, expressed in terms of loss of walks, for the change in Katz centrality when nodes and/or edges are removed from a simple graph. These formulas informed the description of two algorithms to approximate the new centrality vectors after network modification. The theoretical computational cost of the proposed methods is
\mathcal{O}(Lm)
, where
m
is the number of undirected edges in the original graph and
L
is a (dynamically chosen) maximum walk-length. We also described upper bounds on the change in total network communicability.
Numerical results on both synthetic and real-world networks showed that our approximation strategies are more efficient than others available in the literature, including recomputing from scratch and series truncation, and return results that are accurate both in terms of numerics (relative error) and ranking (intersection similarity).

Given these promising results, future work will focus on developing similar strategies for other graph types and centrality measures.
