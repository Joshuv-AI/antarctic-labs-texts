UBE2D4 Upregulation Promotes Cuproptosis Sensitivity in Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Biaoyu Wang, Chan Li, Xianwen Guo, Zhihai Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCuproptosis, a copper‐dependent form of regulated cell death, represents a potential therapeutic vulnerability in colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the regulatory mechanisms governing cuproptosis in CRC remain largely unknown.MethodsUBE2D4 expression was analyzed in the TCGA‐COAD cohort and validated in CRC cell lines (HCT116, HT29) and normal colon epithelial cells (FHC) by qRT‐PCR and western blot. Paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues (n = 5) were also examined by western blot. UBE2D4‐knockdown HT29 cells were generated by transient siRNA transfection to assess cell viability (CCK‐8), migration (wound healing assay), and expression of cuproptosis‐related genes (DLAT, HSP70, LIAS) under copper overload conditions (elesclomol+CuSO4).ResultsUBE2D4 was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues and cell lines compared to normal controls. In paired clinical samples, western blot confirmed that UBE2D4 protein expression was elevated in tumor tissues, accompanied by increased DLAT, HSP70 and LIAS. Copper overload induced typical cuproptotic mitochondrial morphology and triggered a marked upregulation of UBE2D4, DLAT, and HSP70, alongside downregulation of LIAS. UBE2D4 silencing had no effect on baseline cell viability or migration but significantly rescued cells from copper‐induced cytotoxicity. Genetically, UBE2D4 knockdown specifically attenuated the copper‐induced elevation of DLAT, while restoring HSP70 and LIAS to near‐baseline levels.ConclusionThese findings identify UBE2D4 as a genetically upregulated and functionally significant gene in colorectal cancer. Its upregulation correlates with altered expression of cuproptosis‐related genes, particularly DLAT, suggesting that UBE2D4 expression status may represent a genetic determinant of cuproptosis sensitivity in CRC. This study provides a genetic basis for stratifying CRC patients who might benefit from copper‐based therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide (Bray et al. 2024). Despite advances in diagnosis and therapy, treatment options for metastatic or recurrent CRC remain limited, and patient prognosis is poor (Biller and Schrag 2021). Therefore, exploring novel mechanisms underlying colorectal carcinogenesis and identifying new therapeutic targets are of paramount importance.

Colorectal cancer is characterized by substantial genetic heterogeneity, with driver mutations in APC, KRAS, TP53, and PIK3CA defining distinct molecular subtypes. However, the transcriptional dysregulation of genes encoding ubiquitin‐conjugating enzymes, such as the E2 family members, remains poorly characterized in CRC genomes. Given that E2 enzymes govern protein turnover and signaling pathways central to tumor progression, identifying genetically altered E2 genes in CRC may reveal novel biomarkers and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Cuproptosis, a newly discovered regulated cell death pathway proposed by Tsvetkov et al. (2022), has opened new avenues for cancer therapy. Its distinct mechanism involves the direct binding of excess copper ions to lipoylated components of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to aberrant aggregation of lipoylated proteins, subsequent proteotoxic stress, and ultimately, cell death (Chen et al. 2022). This process is dependent on mitochondrial respiration and is orchestrated by key genes such as FDX1 and DLAT (Tsvetkov et al. 2022). Notably, DLAT, a core component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, serves as a direct target of copper binding, and its lipoylation is crucial for cuproptosis execution (Chen et al. 2022).

The ubiquitin‐proteasome system plays a fundamental role in controlling a wide array of cellular processes. Emerging research now points to a functional crosstalk between ubiquitination and cuproptosis. Notably, several E3 ubiquitin ligases have been identified as regulators of key cuproptosis‐related proteins, influencing their stability and function (Stewart et al. 2016; Senft et al. 2018). Building on this established link within the ubiquitination machinery, we sought to investigate whether other essential components—specifically, E2 ubiquitin‐conjugating enzymes—might also contribute to this pathway in previously uncharacterized ways.

Among the E2 enzyme family, UBE2D4 acts as a central mediator, catalyzing the transfer of ubiquitin from E3 ligases to specific substrate proteins to govern processes ranging from protein turnover to signal transduction (Han et al. 2022). While the broader roles of the ubiquitin system in cancer are well‐documented (Alhasan et al. 2024), the functional significance of UBE2D4 in CRC remains poorly defined. Moreover, its potential involvement in cuproptosis has not, to our knowledge, been explored.

Our preliminary bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA‐COAD dataset identified UBE2D4 as a significantly overexpressed gene in CRC. Based on this genetic evidence and the emerging link between ubiquitination and cuproptosis, we hypothesized that UBE2D4 might act as a genetic modulator of the cuproptosis pathway in CRC. It should be noted that this study primarily focuses on the phenotypic characterization of UBE2D4 in cuproptosis. The molecular mechanisms underlying the observed gene expression changes remain to be fully elucidated, and the findings are primarily correlative. Nevertheless, the consistent association between UBE2D4 and DLAT across multiple experimental conditions provides a strong foundation for further genetic and mechanistic investigation.

Materials and Methods

Gene Expression Analysis Using Data From the Cancer Genome Atlas

To identify potential gene expression signatures in a large independent cohort, we analyzed data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA‐COAD) project. The R package TCGAbiolinks was used to query and download transcriptome data for colon adenocarcinoma from the GDC database, with the data category specified as “Gene Expression Quantification” and the workflow type as “STAR‐Counts”. Following data preprocessing and quality control, the R package edgeR was used for data normalization and differential expression analysis. Low‐expressed genes were filtered using the filterByExpr function from the edgeR package, retaining genes with at least 1 count per million (CPM) in at least two samples. Raw count data were normalized using the TMM method and converted to log2(CPM + 1) values for subsequent analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing tumor tissues to normal tissues, using thresholds of |log2(Fold Change)| > 0.5 and an FDR‐adjusted p value (Benjamini‐Hochberg method) < 0.05. Genes of interest were selected based on this analysis, and their expression patterns were visualized using boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package.

Clinical Samples and Tissue Protein Extraction

Paired colorectal cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues (≥ 3 cm from tumor margin) were obtained from five patients who underwent surgical resection at The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Yulin First People's Hospital, Yulin, China) between March 2025 and June 2025. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yulin First People's Hospital (Approval No. YLSY‐IRB‐SR‐2025009).

Tissue samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. For protein extraction, tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Catalog No. P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Catalog No. 04693159001) using a tissue grinder. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4°C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No. 23225).

Cell Culture and Reagents

The human colorectal cancer cell lines HT29, HCT116, and the normal human colon epithelial cell line FHC were obtained from Cellverse Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China; Catalog No. iCell‐h078), Yimo Biotechnology (Shanghai, China; Catalog No. IM‐H098), and Saihongrui Biotechnology (Shanghai, China; Catalog No. SHC‐0713), respectively. HCT116 and HT29 cells were cultured in McCoy's 5A (Gibco, Catalog No. 16600‐082) and DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Catalog No. 11320‐033) medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GEMINI, Catalog No. 900‐108) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Biological Industries, Catalog No. 03–031‐1B). FHC cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, Catalog No. 11965‐092) medium with the same supplements. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and used in the exponential growth phase. Elesclomol (MedChemExpress, Catalog No. HY‐12040) and CuSO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Catalog No. C1297) were dissolved in DMSO and sterile water, respectively, to prepare stock solutions.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 15596026), following the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was first treated with gDNA Eraser to eliminate genomic DNA, and then reverse‐transcribed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Catalog No. RR047A). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was carried out on a QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Catalog No. RR820A). The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 30 s, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 34 s. Following amplification, a melt curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of the PCR products. GAPDH was used as the endogenous reference gene for qRT‐PCR; its expression remained stable under the experimental conditions used for RNA analysis, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was applied for relative quantification. Establishment of UBE2D4‐Knockdown Cells by Transient siRNA Transfection

The siRNA targeting human UBE2D4 and the negative control siRNA (si‐NC) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequence for UBE2D4 was 5′‐GGACAGAGGUUCGAGAUAATT‐3′ (sense) and 5′‐UUAUCUCGAACCUCUGUCCTT‐3′ (antisense). Because the coding sequence of UBE2D4 is relatively short, the design space for effective siRNA duplexes was limited; only one siRNA sequence was used for all functional experiments. HT29 cells were transfected with si‐UBE2D4 or si‐NC using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Catalog No. L3000015). At 48 h post‐transfection, knockdown efficiency was validated by qRT‐PCR. Cells were harvested for functional assays at 48–72 h post‐transfection, when knockdown efficiency was maximal. All experiments were performed using transient siRNA transfection, and no puromycin selection was applied. Cells transfected with negative control siRNA (si‐NC) served as the control group for all subsequent knockdown‐related experiments.

Cell Viability Assay

We evaluated cell viability using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay. Cells from each experimental group were plated in 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well (100 μL medium/well) and left to adhere. After 24 h of applying the interventions described in Section 2.6, we spiked each well with 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent (Dojindo, Catalog No. CK04). The plates were subsequently incubated at 37°C for 1–2 h to allow for color development. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was then recorded on a microplate reader. Cell viability for each group was expressed as a percentage of the control group's viability, calculated according to the standard formula: Viability (%) = [(OD_Experiment − OD_Blank)/(OD_control − OD_Blank)] × 100.

Cell Migration Assay

Cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured to form a complete monolayer. A uniform wound was then gently created in the monolayer by scraping with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. The dislodged cells were removed by washing twice with PBS. To minimize the confounding effects of cell proliferation on migration, the culture medium was replaced with serum‐free medium. Wound areas were immediately photographed (0 h) and again after a 24‐h incubation period using an inverted phase‐contrast microscope. The widths of the wounds at both time points were quantified from the images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The cell migration rate was calculated as follows: Migration rate (%) = [(W 0 − W 24)/W 0] × 100, where W 0 and W 24 represent the mean wound width at 0 and 24 h, respectively.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

To observe mitochondrial ultrastructure, control and copper‐overloaded HT29 cells were prepared for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, cells were harvested, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Catalog No. 16220) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4°C, post‐fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Catalog No. 19150) for 1 h, dehydrated through a graded acetone series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), and embedded in Epon 812 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Catalog No. 14120). Ultrathin sections (~70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The samples were observed under a JEM‐1400Flash transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Western Blot Analysis

Cells and tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with 1× PMSF. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, 23,225). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010), and blocked with 5% non‐fat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti‐UBE2D4 (1:1000, Proteintech, 13957‐1‐AP), rabbit anti‐DLAT (1:1000, Proteintech, 16,518‐1‐AP), rabbit anti‐HSP70 (1:1000, Proteintech, 10995‐1‐AP), rabbit anti‐LIAS (1:1000, Proteintech, 11424‐1‐AP), and mouse anti‐β‐actin (1:5000, Proteintech, 60008‐1‐Ig). β‐actin was used as the loading control because its expression remains stable under the copper‐overload conditions used in this study, whereas GAPDH expression can be influenced by mitochondrial stress. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech, SA00001‐2/SA00001‐1). Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio‐Rad). Uncropped western blot membranes are available as Supporting Information.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For comparisons between paired cancer and adjacent normal tissues, the paired Student's t‐test was used. Comparisons between two independent groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student's t‐test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were two‐tailed.

Results

UBE2D4 Is Upregulated in Colorectal Cancer Tissues and Cell Lines

Differential expression analysis of the TCGA‐COAD cohort, after low‐expression filtering and FDR correction, identified 11,527 upregulated and 4463 downregulated genes in colon cancer tissues (Figure 1A). Among these, UBE2D4 was identified as a significantly upregulated gene (FDR < 0.05, Figure 1B). This transcriptional upregulation was validated in CRC cell lines, where UBE2D4 mRNA levels were significantly higher in HCT116 and HT29 cells compared to the normal human colon epithelial cell line FHC (p < 0.05, Figure 1C,D). These results establish UBE2D4 as a transcriptionally dysregulated gene in CRC at both the population and cellular levels.

UBE2D4, DLAT, HSP70, and LIAS Protein Expression in Clinical CRC Tissues

To further validate the clinical relevance of this genetic upregulation, we examined protein expression of UBE2D4, DLAT, HSP70, and LIAS in paired CRC tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues (n = 5) by western blot. Consistent with the TCGA and cell line data, UBE2D4 protein expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (p < 0.01, Figure 1E).

DLAT protein expression showed a marked increase in the majority of tumor samples (p < 0.05, Figure 1E), while HSP70 and LIAS were also upregulated (p < 0.05, Figure 1E). Notably, the concordant upregulation of UBE2D4 and DLAT in clinical tissues suggests a genetic co‐expression pattern that warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Copper Overload Induces Cuproptosis and Upregulates UBE2D4 Expression

We next established a cuproptosis model in HT29 cells to determine whether the genetically upregulated UBE2D4 is responsive to copper stress. Transmission electron microscopy provided direct morphological evidence of cuproptosis: compared to untreated HT29 cells with normal mitochondrial morphology, copper‐overloaded cells exhibited characteristic features, including marked mitochondrial shrinkage, loss of cristae, and increased electron density (Figure 2A). qRT‐PCR analysis revealed that copper overload significantly upregulated DLAT and HSP70, downregulated LIAS, and further induced UBE2D4 expression (Figure 2B–E), indicating that UBE2D4 is transcriptionally responsive to cuproptosis‐inducing conditions.

UBE2D4 Silencing Has no Effect on Baseline Cell Proliferation or Migration

To functionally characterize UBE2D4, we knocked down UBE2D4 in HT29 cells using transient siRNA transfection (si‐UBE2D4), with knockdown efficiency validated by qRT‐PCR (Figure 3A, p < 0.05).

(A) qRT‐PCR validation of UBE2D4 knockdown efficiency in control (si‐HT29) and si‐UBE2D4 cells. (B) Cell viability assessed by CCK‐8 assay under indicated conditions. (C, D) Wound healing assay showing cell migration under indicated conditions. For (A), *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001. For (B–D), statistical comparisons: a versus control group, b versus si‐UBE2D4 group, c versus Cu‐Overload group (a/b/c p < 0.05; aa/bb/cc p < 0.01; aaa/bbb/ccc p < 0.001; aaaa/bbbb/cccc p < 0.0001).

Under baseline conditions, UBE2D4 silencing did not significantly affect cell viability (Figure 3B, control vs. si‐UBE2D4, p > 0.05) or migration capacity (Figure 3C,D, control vs. si‐UBE2D4, p > 0.05), indicating that UBE2D4 is dispensable for basic proliferation and migration in CRC cells under unstressed conditions. In contrast, copper overload significantly suppressed both cell viability and wound closure capacity (Figure 3B–D, Cu‐Overload vs. control, p < 0.05). Notably, genetic knockdown of UBE2D4 partially rescued the copper‐induced inhibition of proliferation, as evidenced by increased cell viability in the si‐UBE2D4 + Cu‐Overload group compared with the Cu‐Overload group (Figure 3B, p < 0.05). However, UBE2D4 knockdown further exacerbated the copper‐induced impairment of migration (Figure 3C,D, p < 0.05), suggesting a dissociation between proliferation and migration phenotypes.

UBE2D4 Knockdown Alleviates Copper‐Induced Cytotoxicity and Differentially Modulates Cuproptosis‐Related Gene Expression Under Basal Versus Copper‐Overload Conditions

We next examined whether the genetic association between UBE2D4 and cuproptosis‐related genes is context‐dependent by comparing all four experimental groups simultaneously (Figure 4A–D).

Under baseline conditions, genetic silencing of UBE2D4 significantly upregulated DLAT, HSP70, and LIAS mRNA expression (si‐UBE2D4 vs. control, p < 0.05, Figure 4A–C). Copper overload alone significantly induced DLAT and HSP70 expression while downregulating LIAS (Cu‐Overload vs. control, p < 0.05). Notably, under copper‐overload conditions, UBE2D4 knockdown specifically attenuated the copper‐induced elevation of DLAT and HSP70, while restoring LIAS expression to near‐baseline levels (si‐UBE2D4 + Cu‐Overload vs. Cu‐Overload, p < 0.05, Figure 4A–C).

Collectively, our results identify UBE2D4 as a genetically upregulated gene in CRC whose expression status correlates with altered expression of cuproptosis‐related genes, particularly DLAT. The genetic interaction between UBE2D4 and DLAT is context‐dependent, being modified by copper‐induced cellular stress. These findings position UBE2D4 as a genetic determinant of cuproptosis sensitivity in CRC and provide a genetic basis for further exploring UBE2D4 as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target.

Discussion

This study identifies the ubiquitin‐conjugating enzyme gene UBE2D4 as a previously uncharacterized genetically upregulated candidate in colorectal cancer that modulates sensitivity to copper‐induced cuproptosis. By integrating transcriptomic data from the TCGA‐COAD cohort, protein expression profiling of clinical tissues, and genetic knockdown experiments, we demonstrate that UBE2D4 is consistently overexpressed in CRC and that its expression status correlates with altered expression of cuproptosis‐related genes, particularly DLAT. These findings add UBE2D4 to the growing list of genetically altered E2 enzymes implicated in cancer pathogenesis and provide a genetic perspective for understanding cuproptosis sensitivity in CRC.

We first identified UBE2D4 as a candidate from the TCGA‐COAD data, finding its mRNA significantly elevated in CRC models and patient samples. Although ubiquitination is implicated in cancer (Han et al. 2022; Alhasan et al. 2024), UBE2D4 itself has been poorly studied (Ramatenki et al. 2016; Tokumoto and Satoh 2012; Lee et al. 2016), and its connection to cuproptosis was entirely unknown. Our key functional evidence—that genetic silencing of UBE2D4 robustly rescues cells from copper‐induced death—positions it as a genetic determinant of cuproptosis sensitivity. This finding raises the possibility that cancers with high UBE2D4 expression might be vulnerable to strategies that induce copper toxicity (O'Day et al. 2009) (elesclomol is a copper ionophore that delivers copper into cells), suggesting that UBE2D4 expression status could serve as a genetic stratification marker for copper‐based therapies. From a genetic perspective, our findings have several implications. First, the consistent upregulation of UBE2D4 across TCGA datasets, cell lines, and patient tissues suggests that UBE2D4 expression is a robust genetic signature of CRC. Second, the correlation between UBE2D4 and DLAT expression in clinical samples raises the possibility that these two genes may be co‐regulated at the transcriptional level, potentially through shared genetic regulatory elements or transcriptional networks. Third, the context‐dependent modulation of DLAT by UBE2D4 knockdown points to a genetic interaction that is modified by cellular stress, suggesting that UBE2D4 may function within a stress‐responsive genetic network rather than as a simple linear regulator. Future studies integrating genetic, epigenetic, and transcriptomic analyses will be needed to fully elucidate the regulatory circuitry through which UBE2D4 exerts its effects.

How does UBE2D4 exert its effect on cuproptosis sensitivity? Our data show that UBE2D4 knockdown specifically attenuates the copper‐induced elevation of DLAT, a key effector of cuproptosis. Given that UBE2D4 is an E2 ubiquitin‐conjugating enzyme, one plausible mechanism is that it regulates the stability of a transcription factor or co‐factor that controls DLAT expression (Hammond‐Martel et al. 2012). Alternatively, UBE2D4 might influence DLAT at the post‐transcriptional level, although our mRNA data suggest that the effect is at least in part transcriptional. It is also important to note that under baseline conditions, UBE2D4 knockdown unexpectedly increased DLAT expression, indicating that the genetic interaction between UBE2D4 and DLAT is not unidirectional. This complexity may reflect context‐dependent differences in UBE2D4's substrate specificity or interaction with different E3 ligases, a phenomenon well documented for other E2 enzymes (Stewart et al. 2016). Definitive elucidation of the genetic regulatory circuitry through which UBE2D4 influences DLAT expression requires further investigation, including identification of UBE2D4's bona fide substrates in the cuproptosis pathway. While the dissociation between proliferation and migration observed under copper overload with UBE2D4 knockdown supports the interpretation that the reduced wound closure reflects a genuine defect in cell motility, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of the scratch assay. Wound closure is a composite process that can also be influenced by changes in cell adhesion, survival, morphology, and other cellular behaviors. Although our CCK‐8 data exclude proliferation as a major confounding factor, we cannot completely rule out contributions from these additional aspects of cell physiology. Therefore, our wound healing results should be interpreted as supportive evidence for impaired migratory capacity rather than definitive proof. Future studies employing orthogonal approaches, such as time‐lapse single‐cell tracking or transwell migration assays, would further strengthen this conclusion.

The protein expression patterns observed in clinical CRC tissues provide preliminary supporting evidence for the functional significance of the UBE2D4–DLAT genetic axis. In five paired cancer and adjacent normal tissues, UBE2D4 was consistently upregulated at the protein level, corroborating our transcriptomic findings from the TCGA cohort and cell lines. Notably, DLAT, HSP70 and LIAS also showed elevated protein expression in tumor tissues. These concordant changes among UBE2D4, DLAT, HSP70 and LIAS in clinical samples suggest a genetic co‐expression pattern that may be relevant in human CRC. However, given the limited sample size, these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory, and larger genetic association studies are needed to validate this co‐expression relationship.

A central observation of our research is that UBE2D4 is required to maintain the specific expression profile of downstream effectors under copper stress. Copper overload induced a marked upregulation of DLAT and HSP70 and a downregulation of LIAS. It is noteworthy that LIAS was elevated in clinical tumor tissues but downregulated under acute copper overload, indicating context‐dependent regulation. Silencing UBE2D4 was associated with reversal of this trend, most notably by suppressing the copper‐induced hyper‐expression of DLAT, while the expressions of HSP70 and LIAS were restored to baseline levels. This coordinated regulation is biologically plausible. DLAT, a core component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (Li et al. 2023; Tian et al. 2024), encodes a protein that serves as a direct target of copper binding, and its lipoylation is crucial for cuproptosis execution (Wang et al. 2023; Kordi et al. 2024; Tsvetkov et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2022); its upregulation may reflect pathway activation (Fu et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). HSP70, a major molecular chaperone, is a classic marker of proteotoxic stress (Havalová et al. 2021; Kampinga and Craig 2010), aligning with the mechanism of cuproptosis which involves lipoylated protein aggregation (Yu et al. 2025). LIAS encodes the key enzyme for lipoic acid synthesis (Wang et al. 2024), and is an essential component of the cuproptosis pathway (Luo et al. 2025; Parsanathan 2024). Its downregulation may disrupt mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis and modulate cellular responses to copper stress, although the precise role of LIAS in cuproptosis sensitivity requires further investigation. From a genetic perspective, the coordinated regulation of these genes by UBE2D4 suggests that UBE2D4 may function as a node within a stress‐responsive genetic regulatory network.

Our functional data reveal a complex, context‐dependent genetic interaction between UBE2D4 and its downstream targets. When cells were challenged with copper overload, silencing UBE2D4 exerted a strong protective effect, rescuing cell viability and confirming its role in cuproptosis execution. Under basal culture conditions, knockdown of UBE2D4 alone provoked a substantial upregulation of DLAT, HSP70, and LIAS—a result that initially appears paradoxical. One possible interpretation is that under steady‐state conditions, UBE2D4 may help maintain a low basal expression of these genes, possibly by promoting the turnover of a transcriptional activator (Hammond‐Martel et al. 2012). Under copper stress, UBE2D4 is itself induced and becomes required for the further elevation of DLAT. However, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative, and alternative explanations (e.g., compensatory transcriptional responses due to prolonged UBE2D4 depletion) cannot be ruled out. The precise molecular basis for this context‐dependent behavior awaits future investigation, including genome‐wide transcriptomic profiling of UBE2D4‐knockdown cells to define the full genetic network modulated by this gene.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical sample size (n = 5) is small, and the findings await validation in a larger independent cohort. Second, we lack direct in vivo evidence; further studies using xenograft mouse models are needed to confirm the role of UBE2D4 in cuproptosis sensitivity in a physiological context. Third, although we observed changes at the mRNA level, we have not determined whether UBE2D4 directly regulates DLAT transcription or acts through an intermediary factor; the observed associations do not imply direct regulation. Fourth, we did not employ cuproptosis‐specific inhibitors (e.g., tetrathiomolybdate) to confirm pathway specificity, nor did we measure intracellular copper concentrations. Fifth, our in vitro experiments were primarily performed in HT29 cells; validation in additional CRC cell lines would strengthen the findings. Sixth, the paradoxical observation that UBE2D4 knockdown increases DLAT under basal conditions but decreases it under copper stress remains unexplained and may reflect indirect or compensatory effects. Genome‐wide approaches such as RNA‐seq in UBE2D4‐knockdown cells would help define the full genetic network modulated by this gene. Given these limitations, the conclusions regarding UBE2D4 as a therapeutic target should be considered preliminary.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies UBE2D4 as a genetically upregulated gene in colorectal cancer that modulates cuproptosis sensitivity. The genetic association between UBE2D4 and DLAT across multiple experimental conditions provides a rationale for further investigation of this genetic axis as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target in CRC. While the precise genetic and molecular mechanisms remain to be determined, the genetic evidence presented here opens the possibility that UBE2D4 expression status could serve as a genetic stratification marker for copper‐based therapies.
