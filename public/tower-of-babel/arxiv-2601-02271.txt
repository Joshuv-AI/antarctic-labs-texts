Harmony in 10-TET: From Parallel Universes to Cousins of Desargues Configuration
Paweł Nurowski

Abstract
. The 10-tone equal temperament (10-TET) constitutes a distinct harmonic environment with step sizes exceeding the standard semitone, yet it has historically remained on the fringes of music theory. This paper proposes a radical shift in perspective, treating 10-TET not as an imperfect imitation of 12-TET, but as an independent harmonic universe with its own rigorous logic. We classify all possible harmonic systems in 10-TET using a single structural parameter Δ \Delta , representing the difference between the harmonic mediants (the major and minor “thirds”) which sum to the generator (the “fifth”). Our analysis reveals a rich landscape of possibilities. The system defined by Δ = 1 \Delta=1 initially appears to suffer from a fundamental structural flaw: its harmonic space fractures into two disjoint, parallel universes. Conversely, the “Wide” system ( Δ = 5 \Delta=5 ) emerges as a geometrically superior structure, isomorphic to the Unique Cyclic Configuration in the classification of combinatorial 10 3 10_{3} configurations, forming a fully connected, vertex-transitive graph. Between these extremes lies the “Tritone” system ( Δ = 3 \Delta=3 ), which topologically is similar to the Δ = 1 \Delta=1 system, but it has a significantly larger symmetry group, due to the “Tritone” modal transformation. We conclude by providing a complete classification of these systems as bipartite circulant graphs, demonstrating that 10-TET is a mathematically complex and musically viable alternative to the standard 12-tone system.



1.
Introduction

The 10-tone equal temperament (10-TET) has long remained a theoretical curiosity, overshadowed by the ubiquity of the standard 12-tone system. While the system’s larger step sizes initially deterred classical adaptation, its theoretical foundations were established by Sethares
[
19
]
, who demonstrated its unique capacity to support stable neutral intervals and novel timbral consonance. Practical interest in the system has steadily grown, with several compositions written for 10-TET, including some specifically composed for a 10-TET piano by Hunt
[
12
]
, Senpai
[
18
]
, Sevish
[
20
]
, and Hideya
[
11
]
. However, while these works explored the electronic domain or software simulations, purely acoustic implementations remained elusive until the recent construction of the first decaphonic piano by renowned jazz pianist Możdżer and his collaborators
[
16
]
(see our Appendix A for more details).

Despite these advances, 10-TET still lacks a comprehensive harmonic theory comparable to the rich structural tradition of Western tonal music. The goal of this paper is to provide a fully self-contained theory of possible harmonies within the system
\mathbb{Z}_{10}
. We aim to define precise analogues of Major and Minor chords, not through arbitrary selection, but via a rigorous generative process, and to construct the resulting harmonic networks—or
Tonnetze
—associated with them.

To achieve this, we introduce a utilitarian algorithm applicable to any
n
-TET system. We postulate that a harmonic system is defined by a “Fifth” (step size
q
), defined by using an analogy of a specially devised
n
-TET analog of the Pythagorean system, a “Major Third” (
t
), and a “Minor Third” (
s
). These intervals must satisfy the condition where the two thirds sum to the fifth:

(1)

t+s\equiv q\pmod{n}

The distinguishing feature of any such system is the difference between the thirds, denoted by
\Delta
:

(2)

t-s\equiv\Delta\pmod{n}

This algorithmic approach allows us to scan through all possible values of
\Delta
for a given
n
, exhaustively classifying every possible harmonic universe the temperament can support. While we focus here on
n=10
, this methodology provides a general blueprint for exploring harmonic topologies in any equal temperament.

Crucially, this framework satisfies the correspondence principle vital to scientific theory generalization: a new broader theory must contain the established theory as a special case. Indeed, when we apply our
n
-TET harmonic proposal to the standard 12-TET (
n=12
), we recover the standard Western harmonic system, including the classical Euler-Riemann
Tonnetz
as one of the fundamental models.

Our investigation of the
\Delta
parameter in 10-TET leads to three principal candidates for a viable musical system:

(1)

The “Narrow”(or “Acoustic”) choice (
\Delta=1
), characterized by
(t,s)=(4,3)
. This corresponds to the first–to–try intervals but suffers from modal degeneracy and topological splitting into two disjoint universes.

(2)

The “Tritone” choice (
\Delta=3
), characterized by
(t,s)=(5,2)
, which serves as a highly symmetric “middle ground” using tritone.

(3)

The “Wide” choice (
\Delta=5
), characterized by
(t,s)=(6,1)
, which generates a single, connected component isomorphic to a one of the cyclic configurations in combinatorial geometry. This system utilizes the largest interval steps.

A surprising finding of this work is the topological divergence—and subsequent unification—of the acoustically motivated “Narrow” system
(4,3)
. It suffers from a fundamental degeneracy, spliting into two disjoint, parallel universes (the “Blue” and “Red” sets). Full modulation in this system is possible only via non-standard “wormhole” transitions.

In contrast, the “Wide” system
(6,1)
, which we analyze in the latter part of this paper, emerges as a geometrically perfect structure. We prove that this system generates a highly connected, vertex-transitive graph isomorphic to the unique cyclic configuration
(10_{3})_{3}
in census
[
3
]
. This structure stands apart from the famous Desargues
10_{3}
configuration (which is not cyclic) as a singular intersection between the axioms of combinatorial geometry and the cyclic symmetry inherent in musical theory.

By exploring these systems, we demonstrate that 10-TET is not merely a microtonal experiment, but a structurally rich environment where the choice of intervals fundamentally dictates the topology of musical space. The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 motivates our choice of the ‘fifth’
q
, which is crucial for obtaining possible candidates fo 10-TET harmonies; Section 3 formalizes the definition of chords; and Sections 4 through 10 provide a detailed graph-theoretical analysis of each harmonic candidate and their respective symmetry groups.

2.
From Pythagorean tuning to its
n
-tone generalization

The concept of a Pythagorean tuning for an arbitrary
n
-TET (
n
-tone equal temperament) system was originally developed in
[
14
]
. In such a system, notes are generated by the repeated application of a rational interval (a generator
g>1
) followed by octave reduction:

(3)

g^{k}\cdot 2^{m(k)},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

where the exponent
m(k)\in\mathbb{Z}
is chosen uniquely such that the resulting interval lies within the range
[1,2)
. Selecting
n
consecutive intervals yields a complete
n
-TET system. For such systems, the
tempered index
serves as a metric to measure the average deviation of these Pythagorean intervals from the ideal equal-tempered steps
2^{k/n}
.

Mathematical analysis presented in
[
14
,
15
]
demonstrates that among small-step Pythagorean systems, the case of
n=10
is distinguished. With the generator
g=13/8
, the 10-step system minimizes both the Pythagorean comma and the tempered index. This provides a precise theoretical justification for considering 10-TET as a compact, mathematically optimal, and musically viable alternative to the standard 12-TET.

Furthermore,
[
14
]
provides motivation grounded in concert practice, reporting that the system has been successfully implemented on acoustic grand pianos and received positively by audiences (see Appendix A for historical details).

These findings motivate the present study, which aims to codify the 10-TET system in detail. Specifically, we establish the definitions of major and minor chords within this framework and construct its analog of the Eulerian—or more precisely, the Euler
[
9
]
–Riemann
[
17
]
—Tonnetz.

To render this paper self-contained, we summarize here the fundamental concepts and key results from
[
14
]
that underpin our subsequent analysis.

2.1.
General
n
-TET Pythagorean Systems

A
Pythagorean system
with
n
steps per octave is constructed using a generator
g
of harmonic type:

1<g=\frac{2p-1}{2^{\ell(p)}}<2,\quad\text{with }p,\ell(p)\in\mathbb{N}.

The scale is formed by the set of values:

g^{k}\cdot 2^{m(k)},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n-1,

taken modulo the octave. We say the system has
n
steps and
u
octaves if its
comma

\delta
, defined as

\delta=\left|\frac{g}{2^{u/n}}-1\right|,

satisfies the condition:

\delta\leq\frac{3/2}{2^{7/12}}-1\simeq 0.0011299.

The upper bound imposed here corresponds to the comma of the classical Pythagorean system (12 steps in one octave, based on 7 octaves in the full cycle).

2.2.
Why
n=10
Is Distinguished

An exhaustive analysis of Pythagorean systems with
p<20
,
n\leq 30
steps, and
u\leq 10
octaves reveals that the choice
n=10
yields the smallest comma. Remarkably, this comma is more than three times smaller than that of the classical 12-TET Pythagorean system.

This optimum is achieved for the triple
(p,u,n)=(7,7,10)
, which defines the generator of the 10-TET Pythagorean system as:

g=\frac{13}{8}.

With this generator, the resulting system approximates harmonic intervals closely while remaining structurally simple. This mathematically grounds the optimality of ten steps per octave in this framework.

3.
Generalization of Major and Minor Chords

To construct a harmonic theory for the 10-TET system, we must first establish a formal definition of "Major" and "Minor" chords in the language of modular arithmetic. We begin by rigorously defining these concepts for the classical 12-TET system, which will serve as our reference model.

3.1.
Mathematical Definition of Chords

In an arbitrary
n
-TET system, steps are labeled
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. Western harmony relies on three structural intervals:

(1)

The perfect fifth, denoted by
q
.

(2)

The major third, denoted by
t
.

(3)

The minor third, denoted by
s
.

A
Major chord
(
D_{r}
) with root
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is defined as the ordered triple:

(4)

D_{r}=(r,\;r+t,\;r+q)\pmod{n}.

A
Minor chord
(
M_{r}
) is structurally defined via the "inverse interval" logic. The minor third
s
must be the complement of the major third
t
within the fifth
q
:

(5)

s+t\equiv q\pmod{n}.

The minor chord with root
r
is then:

(6)

M_{r}=(r,\;r+s,\;r+q)\pmod{n}.

4.
The Reference Model: The Classical 12-TET System

Before applying this framework to the 10-TET system, we must exhaustively describe the properties of the classical 12-TET system. This will provide the necessary baseline for comparison.

4.1.
Solutions for Thirds in 12-TET

In 12-TET, we are given
n=12
and the Pythagorean generator implies
q=7
.
A defining characteristic of the diatonic scale is that the major third is exactly one step larger than the minor third. We impose this constraint:

t-s\equiv 1\pmod{12}.

Combined with
t+s\equiv 7
, we solve for
t
:

2t\equiv 8\pmod{12}.

This congruence admits exactly two mathematical solutions:

(1)

2t=8\implies t=4
. Consequently
s=3
.

(2)

2t=20\implies t=10
. Consequently
s=9
.

From the perspective of Western harmony, the second solution (
t=10
) corresponds to a minor seventh interval, which is too wide to function as a third. Thus, classical theory exclusively utilizes the first solution, defining the Major Third as 4 semitones and the Minor Third as 3 semitones.

4.3.
12-TET Chords and Their Tonnetz Assignments

Explanation of the assignment method:

The
Euler-Riemann Tonnetz
for 12-TET is constructed by connecting chords that share exactly
two common tones
.
In this system, every major chord is adjacent to exactly three minor chords:

•

Parallel (P):

D_{r}\leftrightarrow M_{r}
(share Root, Fifth).

•

Relative (R):

D_{r}\leftrightarrow M_{r-3}
(share Root, Third).

•

Leading-Tone (L):

D_{r}\leftrightarrow M_{r+4}
(share Third, Fifth).

In short, the method states that:

•

Replace exactly one note of the chord with another note to form a minor chord.

•

The resulting chord must share exactly two common tones (modulo 12) with the original chord.

Example 1: The chord
D_{0}
(C Major) = {0, 4, 7}

•

P transformation:
D_{0}=\{0,4,7\}\to\{0,3,7\}=M_{0}
(C minor). Common tones: {0, 7}.

•

R transformation:
D_{0}=\{0,4,7\}\to\{9,0,4\}=M_{9}
(A minor). Common tones: {0, 4}.

•

L transformation:
D_{0}=\{0,4,7\}\to\{4,7,11\}=M_{4}
(E minor). Common tones: {4, 7}.

Example 2: The chord
D_{7}
(G Major) = {7, 11, 2}

•

P transformation:
D_{7}=\{7,11,2\}\to\{7,10,2\}=M_{7}

•

Similarly for minor chords: we replace one note to form a major chord, preserving two common tones.

5.
The 10-TET System and the Choice of Thirds

We now return to Section
3.1
to define chords in 10-TET system.
We have established that for the 10-TET the generator
g=13/8
dictates the fifth
q=7
. However, the value of the major third
t
is a free parameter.
To define a harmonic system analogous to Western diatonic harmony, we must determine the relationship between the major third (
t
) and the minor third (
s
).

We recall equation (
5
) defining the main characteristic of the fifth in any
n
-TET system as the sum of thirds.
When applied to 10-TET, this gives:

(7)

t+s\equiv 7\pmod{10}.

We now systematically explore possible constraints on the difference
\Delta=t-s
.

5.1.
Hypothesis 1: The Diatonic Difference (
\Delta=1
)

The classical 12-TET system satisfies
t-s=1
. Imposing this on 10-TET yields:

2t\equiv 8\pmod{10}.

This admits two solutions:

•

t=4
(implies
s=3
). This is the solution analyzed in the remainder of this paper.

•

t=9
(implies
s=8
). This yields large intervals (sevenths) unsuitable for tertian harmony.

5.2.
Hypothesis 2: The Whole-Tone Difference (
\Delta=2
)

One might ask if the major and minor thirds could differ by a larger step, for instance,
\Delta=2
. This leads to the system:

(8)

\begin{cases}t+s\equiv 7\\
t-s\equiv 2\end{cases}

Summing the equations gives
2t\equiv 9\pmod{10}
.
In the ring of integers modulo 10, the quantity
2t
must always be even. Since 9 is odd, **no integer solution exists**.
Thus, it is mathematically impossible to construct a Major/Minor system in 10-TET where the thirds differ by 2 steps.

Of course the same argument shows that any even number
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{10}
makes the system

t+s\equiv 7\quad\&\quad t-s\equiv\Delta\quad\&\quad\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{10}\,\,\text{with}\,\,\Delta\,\,\text{even},

incompatible. Thus, we have
no solutions for all
\Delta
even
.

5.3.
Hypothesis 3: The “Neutral” Difference (
\Delta=3
)

Next, we test the difference
\Delta=3
:

(9)

\begin{cases}t+s\equiv 7\\
t-s\equiv 3\end{cases}

Summing gives
2t\equiv 10\equiv 0\pmod{10}
. The solutions are:

•

t=0
(implies
s=7
). Trivial solution (unison and fifth).

•

t=5
(implies
s=2
).

The solution
(t=5,s=2)
is mathematically valid. However, the "Major Third"
t=5
corresponds to exactly half the octave (
\sqrt{2}
, or the tritone). A chord constructed this way would be
C=\{0,5,7\}
.

5.4.
Hypothesis 4: The case
\Delta=5
i.e. The Wide System

This is perhaps the most interesting case, with a solution
(t,s)=(6,1)
, and due to its significance, it requires separate treatment. We postpone its discussion to Section
8
, after we perform the full analysis of the case
\Delta=1
.

5.5.
Conclusion on the Choice of Thirds

This exhaustion of possibilities gives us the way to proceed, and to analyze in detail
two cases only
, namely cases
(t,s)=(4,3)
and
(t,s)=(6,1)
.

We first fully analyse the case
(t,s)=(4,3)
. This choice is the **unique** solution that:

(1)

Is mathematically solvable (unlike
\Delta
is even).

(2)

It is **narrow** in the distance between the thirds, providing the closest mathematical analogy to the standard 12-TET-system.

Therefore first, in the next section, we proceed with the analysis of the solution
(t=4,s=3)
.

6.2.
Theorem of Modal Degeneracy

Inspection of the tables above reveals a startling fact. Compare, for example, the major chord rooted at 0 with the minor chord rooted at 7:

•

•

Theorem 1

(Modal Identity)
.

In the 10-TET system with
(t=4,s=3)
, the set of pitch classes forming a major chord
D_{r}
is identical to the set forming the minor chord
M_{r+7}
.

Proof.

.

M_{r+7}=\{(r+7),(r+7)+3,(r+7)+7\}=\{r+7,r+10,r+14\}\equiv\{r+7,r,r+4\}
.
The sets are identical.
∎

6.3.
Musical Interpretation of Modal Degeneracy

This mathematical identity has profound musical consequences for the 10-TET system.

•

Structural Uniqueness:
Unlike in 12-TET, where a Major triad (e.g., C-E-G) and its relative Minor (A-C-E) are distinct objects sharing two tones, in 10-TET they are the
same physical object
sharing all three tones. In fact, there are only 10 unique triads in the entire
(4,3)
solution 10-TET Universe.

•

Context-Dependent Perception:
Since the interval set

, corresponding to a passage

, is invariant under cyclic permutation, the perceived quality of the chord depends entirely on inversion and context (specifically, which note is heard as the root or bass):

–

If the pitch
r
is established as the root, the intervals above it are
4+3
, and the chord is heard as
Major
(
D_{r}
).

–

If the pitch
r+7
is established as the root, the intervals above it are
3+4
, and the chord is heard as
Minor
(
M_{r+7}

•

The Janus-Faced Triad:
Physically, the 10-TET triad is an ambiguous, "Janus-faced" harmonic object. It functions as a bridge between the major and minor modes not by changing notes (voice leading), but by changing the listener’s perspective (re-interpreting the root).

7.1.
Musical Implications: The Phantom Modulation

This functional restoration of connectivity in 3D has profound musical implications, extending the concept of “Janus-faced” triads introduced in Section 6.3.

In classical 12-TET harmony, modulation typically involves changing pitch classes (e.g., introducing a sharp or flat). However, in the
(4,3)
solution 10-TET, the

phantom modulation
. The physical sound remains identical (the pitch-class set is invariant), but the listener is instantaneously transported from the “Even Universe” to the “Odd Universe” purely by re-interpreting the root.

This suggests that the
(4,3)
solution 10-TET separates physical motion (changing notes) from functional motion (changing keys) more radically than 12-TET. Traversing the blue or red rings corresponds to physical changes in pitch, while traversing the vertical “wormholes” corresponds to purely cognitive shifts in harmonic function. The cylinder acts as a topological map of this duality, unifying the disjoint set-theoretical cycles into a single functional object.

7.2.3.
Minimal Chiral Cycles

The introduction of wormholes significantly reduces the girth (shortest cycle length) of the graph. While the intra-universe graphs have a girth of 10, the restored cubic graph allows for tight loops of length 4.

Theorem 3
.

In the restored cubic system, there are exactly two isomorphism classes of minimal cycles (of length 4) under the rotational symmetry of
\mathbb{Z}_{10}

Proof.

A cycle must alternate between Major and Minor chords. A length-2 cycle implies a double edge, which is impossible as
P,L,R
are distinct operations. Thus, the minimal length is 4. A 4-cycle must involve at least two wormhole crossings to leave and return to the starting universe.
Algebraically, we look for a sequence of operations that results in the identity element. The two minimal solutions correspond to the two directions of traversing the wormhole:

(1)

The Violet Cycle (Right-handed):

S=P\cdot R_{+7}\cdot L_{-4}\cdot R_{+7}

Tracing the index change:
P(0)\to R(+7)\to L(-4)\to R(+7)
. The total displacement is
+7-4+7=+10\equiv 0\pmod{10}
.

(2)

The Orange Cycle (Left-handed):

S^{\prime}=P\cdot R_{+3}\cdot L_{+4}\cdot R_{+3}

Tracing the index change:
P(0)\to R(+3)\to L(+4)\to R(+3)

We now return to the singular case identified in Section 5.4: the “Wide” 10-TET system defined by the parameters

Remark 1
.

The Evolution of
10_{3}
Configurations:

The concept of a
10_{3}

[
7

(10_{3})_{8}

10_{3}

(10_{3})_{3}

(10_{3})_{3}

It obeys the rules that (a) each point meets three lines, (b) each line contains three points, and (c) the axioms of linear incidence geometry are satisfied (specifically, that any two distinct points determine at most one line).

8.5.
Topologgy of Cycles: Girth 6

A fundamental question in the topology of this harmonic network is the length of the shortest path to return to the starting chord (the **girth**). Here we present a rigorous proof using the "Shift Method".

8.5.1.
Parity Constraint

Our harmonic graph is **bipartite**: edges only connect Major (
D

M

2k

8.5.2.
The "Shift Method" for Even Cycles

We analyze the path as a sequence of "double-steps":

.
Each double-step shifts the root index by a specific amount modulo 10. To close a cycle, the sum of these shifts must be
0\equiv 10\pmod{10}
.

There are exactly 6 possible distinct double-steps starting from

(1)

PR

D_{r}\to M_{r+6}\to D_{r+6}
. Shift:
\mathbf{6}

(2)

\mathbf{9}
(since

(3)

\mathbf{1}
.

(4)

D_{r}\to M_{r+6}\to D_{r+7}

\mathbf{7}
.

(5)

In Sections

10.1.
Circulant graphs

Before stating the main result for this system, we must rigorously define the target family of graphs.

Definition 1

(Circulant Graph)
.

[
1
,
4
,
8

A

Ci_{n}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
is a graph with
n

10.1.1.
The “Acoustic” system
(4,3)

Theorem 6
.

The functional 10-TET Acoustic graph is isomorphic to the Bipartite Circulant Graph

Proof.

To analyze the global structure, we map the 20 distinct chords into the cyclic group

\phi(D_{i})=2i\quad\text{and}\quad\phi(M_{i})=2i+1\pmod{20}.

Here

is our musical space of all chords.
In this unified space, an edge connects a Major chord
D_{i}
to a Minor chord

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\phi(M_{i+k})-\phi(D_{i})

\displaystyle=(2(i+k)+1)-2i

\displaystyle=2k+1\pmod{20}

Using the receipt for the Major/Minor chords assocciation
D_{r}\to(M_{r},M_{r+4},M_{r+7})

(2)

(3)

These are three generators

Ci_{20}(1,9,15)

∎

We start by defining a musical system automorphism group, as an authomorphism group of its corresponding graph. The latter is defined as follows:

Definition 2

(Graph Automorphism and Geometric Symmetry)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with set of points
V
and set of edges
E
. An
automorphism
of

\sigma:V\to V
such that for any pair of vertices
u,v\in V
,
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
\{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)\}\in E
. The set of all such automorphisms forms the
Geometric Symmetry Group
of

, denoted
\text{Aut}(G)
.

Appendix A
Historical Motivation and the Acoustic Implementation

As written in the original paper on Pythagorean tunings for
n
-TETs
[
14
]
, the author’s initial motivation for considering the 10-TET system comes from a live concert experience, summarized below.

In January 2023 I contacted the world renowned jazz pianist Leszek Możdżer, to ask if he would be willing to give a piano recital for the participants of a mathematics conference ‘GRIEG meets Chopin’ that I was helping to organize. To my surprise Leszek Możdżer’s answer to my request was positive, but with one caveat. Specifically, would I help him with the mathematics needed to redesign his Östlind and Almquist concert piano from the usual 12-step equally tempered (TET) scale to the 10-step TET scale? Możdżer further proposed that, at the concert during the mathematical conference, two pianos would be played: his redesigned 10-TET acoustic Östlind and Almquist piano and the usual 12-TET Steinway piano.

Without thinking much about ‘why the ten-step scale?’ I gladly accepted Możdżer’s proposal and in short order prepared a table with data needed to retune the piano from 12-TET to 10-TET. But while the mathematics I used was straightforward the actual retuning of the 12-TET piano to the 10-TET scale encountered many technical issues (the full technical procedure is described in
[
16
]
). These were eventually resolved by the combined efforts of two teams of Leszek Możdżer (with Roman Galiński, Jan Grzyśka, Ryszard Mariański, Mirosław Mastalerz, Sławomir Rosa) and mine (Aleksander Bogucki, Andrzej Włodarczyk). My team even filed a patent application with the major ideas of this retuning.

As a result the World Premiere of the Acoustic Decaphonic Piano by Leszek Możdżer took place on July 13, 2023 in the Nowa Miodowa Concert Hall in Warsaw, Poland. In a brilliant program he premiered a number of his compositions written for two pianos, both traditional 12-TET and the decaphonic 10-TET, some of which were played on both instruments simultaneously. He also performed a number of world’s piano masterpieces paraphrased for the 10-TET acoustic piano. In the opinion of many of those in attendance, he proved that with his virtuosity and for his musical compositions/paraphrases, the 10-TET piano is a wonderful instrument.

Leszek Możdżer’s answer to my question ‘why you want a 10-scale piano?’ is beyond the scope of this note; shortly: it was quite unsatisfactory for me. So, since January 2023 I have been looking for a mathematical argument that would characterize the 10-step musical scale among all other scales. The present paper is an attempt for such a characterization.
