Invited Perspective: Decoding the Prenatal Epigenetic Symphony—The Journey of a Decade
Authors: Andrea A. Baccarelli
Published: 2023

A decade ago, I was one of 30 scientists convened by our colleague Stephanie London at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences to compare notes on our work with DNA methylation in prenatal cohorts. The result? The formation of the Pregnancy and Childhood Epigenetics (PACE) Consortium—a thriving group of investigators committed to sharing results and conducting meta-analyses to link early life environmental exposures to epigenome-wide DNA methylation. The Consortium’s inaugural study pooled data from 13 cohorts, totaling 6,685 mother-child pairs.1 The study revealed striking DNA methylation differences in as many as 10% of all human genes when contrasting children of mothers who smoked during pregnancy to those whose mother did not. This difference, evident in the umbilical cord blood, illustrated the profound effect of maternal exposures on a child’s epigenetics and their potential critical role in fetal and lifelong health.

I frequently depict the epigenome as akin to a musician’s jottings on a musical score, providing cues for future performances. In this biological symphony, the original music is written by nature; however, in response to environmental exposures during pregnancy, our cells add epigenetic markings onto this score—altering how the original music is performed. The epigenome undergoes a remarkable transformation during pregnancy; it is erased and reestablished, making this period a critical window of susceptibility. Some of these markers are enduring and can reverberate into health outcomes many years or even decades later. The Dutch Hunger winter famine serves as a stark testament to this concept.2 Amid the famine, pregnant women were subjected to severe malnutrition, leading to long-lasting epigenetic fetal alterations that have been linked to health outcomes far into adulthood.

At the time of that first PACE meeting, we felt like pioneers in uncharted territories. Despite the skepticism surrounding the relevance of DNA methylation studies, especially those using samples such as blood or saliva, we persevered. I recall one grant reviewer who kindly suggested I could save the collected blood samples for future, “more thoughtful” applications. I owe my gratitude to that anonymous critic, who spurred me to think more deeply, shaping my approach to environmental epigenetics research.

Today, Dr. London has teamed up with Kelly Bakulski, a fellow PACE participant, to pen a comprehensive review on epigenome-wide association studies linking prenatal environmental exposures and health.3 Freida Blostein, a former doctoral student in Dr. Bakulski’s lab, completes this team. The collective wisdom and experience of these scholars have given birth to a truly exceptional piece of literature. The resulting article is an essential guide for anyone conducting epigenome-wide association studies. Its depth of information, its critical analyses, and its forward-thinking perspectives serve as a comprehensive guide that extends far beyond the realm of environmental exposures.

Yet, despite the comprehensive scope of this review, it does not claim to have all the answers. Instead, it masterfully shines a light on the many intriguing questions yet to be unraveled in our field. One such query pertains to the complex world of intergenerational and transgenerational inheritance of DNA methylation and acquired traits, i.e., whether our genes transmit, over multiple generations, information acquired in response to certain environments. Addressing this question will require a harmonious synergy between animal and human studies, especially given the challenges inherent in multigenerational human studies. And, as we turn toward these uncharted territories, the review by London et al. serves as a critical stepping stone, laying the groundwork for our explorations and fueling our quest for deeper understanding.

Also, a profound realization has emerged over time that has changed how we frame epigenomics in environmental health. While the field’s initial goals were heavily centered on studying mechanisms, a paradigm shift occurred when we recognized the untapped potential for developing biomarkers on readily available samples such as blood and saliva. Today, we are at the cusp of a new era with hundreds of promising epigenetic biomarkers that could redefine prevention and clinical practice.4 The development of “epigenetic age,” a biomarker crafted through machine learning, serves as a forerunner to hundreds or other promising biomarkers.5 The epigenetic age biomarker was created to estimate someone’s age based on DNA methylation data from blood or other tissues, but it also offers insights into an individual’s biological age.6 Encouraged by this pioneering advancement, there has been a surge in endeavors to harness machine learning and DNA methylation data to craft biomarkers of environmental risk factors—including environmental chemicals, smoking, alcohol, and psychosocial factors, among others.4 Much like epigenetic age, these biomarkers are likely not mere reflections of exposures; they capture how these exposures influence our cells and, by extension, our health. In essence, they encapsulate the intricate harmony of biological processes activated by environmental exposures.

This journey, from being explorers in a brave new world of prenatal epigenetics to uncovering the symphony of DNA methylation, has been a remarkable one. The rhythm continues, and the melody is becoming ever clearer as we step into the future, brimming with possibilities.
