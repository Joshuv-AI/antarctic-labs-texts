Prevalence and uptake of HIV services among men who have sex with men in Northern Nigeria
Authors: Ismaeel Mohammed Yahaya, Agie Muhmmad Auwal, Godwin Omokhagbo Emmanuel, Paul Umoh, Olaniyi Felix Sanni, Roger Abang, Ochonye Bartholomew Boniface, Paul Amechi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV/AIDS has posed continuous public health challenges for decades, impacting both physical health and socioeconomic development in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly among key populations like men who have sex with men (MSM). This study aims to determine the key factors associated with the prevalence and willingness to take HIV services among MSM in a Northern Nigeria state.MethodologyThe study used a cross-sectional survey of MSM in three local government areas in Kano state. HIV prevalence was measured with self-test kits, and willingness to use services was assessed via a 17-item questionnaire. Snowball sampling was used, with data collected by trained assistants and community leaders. Data were analysed using bivariate and multivariate logistic regression in IBM SPSS V.28.0.ResultsHIV prevalence was 12.3%; MSM in Fagge (adjusted OR (AOR) 4.173; 95% CI 1.193 to 14.595, p=0.025), those not abusing drugs (AOR 5.969; 95% CI 2.412 to 14.770, p<0.001) and those engaging in vaginal sex in the last 3 months (AOR 4.140; 95% CI (1.402 to 12.224), p=0.010) had higher odds to contract HIV. The major reason for taking HIV tests was to know their status (84.0%), while the major obstacle stopping them from assessing HIV services was stigmatisation by providers (25.1%). Similarly, willingness to uptake HIV services was 47.4%; MSM in Tarauni (AOR 92.453, 95% CI 30.541 to 279.876, p<0.001), those who inject drugs (AOR 3.592, 95% CI 1.207 to 10.695, p=0.030), engage in anal sex (AOR 0.075, 95% CI 0.051 to 0.302, p<0.001) and use condoms regularly (AOR 2.731, 95% CI 1.279 to 5.829, p=0.009) had higher odds of accessing HIV services.ConclusionThis study emphasises the critical need to address barriers and improve HIV prevention and treatment services for MSM in northern states of Nigeria.Keywords: Cross-sectional survey, Community-based survey, HIV

Introduction

HIV/AIDS has posed enduring public health challenges for decades, impacting both physical health and socioeconomic development, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa.1 Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden of new HIV infections, with Nigeria contributing a significant portion.1 Nigeria has one of the fastest-growing HIV populations globally, accounting for roughly one-fifth of all new acquisitions in the West and Central African region in recent years.2 3 However, the continued decline in HIV prevalence, from a peak of 5.8% to the current rate of 1.3%, suggests that the country is making progress toward elimination, thanks to expanded prevention and treatment programmes.4 Key populations (KPs) and their sexual partners have been identified to be major contributors to HIV infections, making up over 70% of new HIV cases worldwide.5 Globally, certain KPs such as female sex workers, men who have sex with men (MSM), people who inject drugs and transgender individuals bear a disproportionate burden of the HIV epidemic, influenced by intersecting factors of marginalisation.6 7 Despite making up only 2.3% of Nigeria’s adult population, KPs and those with sexual partners account for an estimated 11.30% of new HIV infections.8–10 Within these groups, MSM are especially impacted, with a 28 times higher likelihood of contracting HIV compared with the general population.11 MSM face a significantly high impact of HIV infection, with a global prevalence of 7.5% in 2022.12

In achieving Nigeria’s goal of eliminating the HIV epidemic by 2030, it is crucial to ensure effective coverage of HIV prevention programmes. However, recent evidence shows significant gaps in programme coverage and service utilisation across the country.13 The Nigerian Ministry of Health has introduced a range of HIV prevention services, such as antiretroviral treatment (ART), voluntary counselling and testing, post-exposure prophylaxis and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), to curb the epidemic.14 15 Considering the heightened increased exposure to HIV, MSM face difficulties in accessing treatment and prevention services, largely due to strict laws and social environments such as the 2014 Same-Sex Marriage Prohibition law.16 Individuals with HIV/AIDS must adhere to ART to effectively manage the disease, reduce associated morbidity and lower mortality rates.17 18 Effective adherence to ART reduces the risk of HIV transmission, contributes to viral suppression, and enhances the standard of living.19

In contrast, insufficient compliance with ART can be linked to antiretroviral medication resistance, unsuccessful therapy, virological failure, decreased CD4 counts, opportunistic infections and poorer health outcomes.20 Understanding the prevalence of HIV and the willingness of MSM to uptake services in local government areas (LGAs) of Kano state with a high number of MSM is crucial for designing targeted interventions to improve health outcomes in this vulnerable population. This study aims to determine the key factors influencing the prevalence of HIV and willingness to use HIV services among MSM in three LGAs of Kano state, Nigeria.

Methodology

Study design

The study employs a cross-sectional survey to collect quantitative data from MSM in three selected LGAs in Kano state. HIV prevalence was determined using a self-test kit for HIV. The willingness to use HIV services was assessed with a 17-point questionnaire, setting a 75% threshold for HIV prevention. The 17-point questions include willingness to attend regular meetings organised to discuss HIV-related issues; allow people to ask you questions about your risk behaviour; follow a plan to help address your HIV risk; receive and use condoms and lubricants; visit clinics for STI check-ups and cervical cancer screening; go to public, private or peer-led clinics to get HIV-related services; attend public, private or peer-led clinics if accompanied by peers; have peers facilitate access to services in public or private hospitals when encounter difficulties; have peers assist in getting voluntary HIV testing services; and have peers serve as drug adherence supporters if HIV positive. Responses were set as ‘unwilling’ and ‘willing’, with 0 and 1 scores, respectively.

Sampling techniques

The study population consists of MSM in three LGAs of Kano state, namely, Fagge, Gwale and Tarauni. The sampling strategy involved a snowball sampling technique, where participants were recruited from selected MSM communities (clubs, parties and bars) using referrals from initial participants.

Sample size

The sample size of the three selected LGA in Kano state was obtained from the 2022 population projection of Kano state.21 The combined population of the three LGAs is 1 282 500, with Fagge having 329 100, Gwale having 588 500 and Tarauni having 364 900.

Data collection

Data were collected by trained research assistants collaborating with community-based personnel such as community leaders and key informants from April to August 2023. The research assistants were trained on study protocols, including using study instruments and ethical considerations. A structured questionnaire was administered to eligible participants to collect quantitative data on their knowledge, attitudes and practices related to HIV. The survey included questions on demographic characteristics, sexual behaviour, drug use, HIV testing and access to prevention and treatment services. The survey was administered in English, Pidgin-English and the local language (Hausa) using electronic tablets.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using quantitative data analysis techniques. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise the quantitative data from the cross-sectional survey. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with HIV prevalence and willingness to uptake care. In the multivariable analysis, all variables were included in the adjusted model regardless of their statistical significance in the univariable analysis. This approach ensures that potential confounders and relevant covariates are accounted for in the final model, providing a comprehensive assessment of the associations. ORs were used to assess associations with HIV prevalence in this study. Given the relatively low HIV prevalence (12.3%), ORs closely approximate prevalence ratios. However, ORs reflect odds rather than probabilities, and caution is needed when interpreting them. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS V.28.0.

Patient and public involvement

The research question and outcome measures were informed by previous studies on HIV prevalence and service uptake among MSM, as well as consultations with community-based organisations working with MSM populations in Northern Nigeria. However, patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, recruitment or conduct of this study. Findings from this study will be disseminated to study participants through community outreach programmes, stakeholder meetings and reports shared with relevant organisations advocating for HIV prevention and treatment services among MSM. Since this study did not involve a randomised controlled trial, the burden of the intervention was not directly assessed by patients. Patient advisers were not involved in the study, so no acknowledgments are required in the contributorship statement.

HIV prevalence and the associated factors among MSM in Kano state

The HIV prevalence among MSM in selected LGAs of Kano states was assessed in table 2 using binary logistics regression. The results show that participants residing in Fagge LGA had higher odds of being HIV positive compared with those in other LGAs, with an adjusted OR (AOR) of 4.173 (95% CI 1.193 to 14.595, p=0.025). Participants who do not abuse drugs had higher odds of being HIV positive compared with those who engage in drug abuse both in the unadjusted OR (2.413, 95% CI 1.268 to 4.592, p=0.007) and AOR (5.969, 95% CI 2.412 to 14.770, p<0.001). Finally, MSM who engaged in vaginal sex in the last 3 months has higher odds of testing positive for HIV compared with those who did not, with an AOR of 4.140 (95% CI 1.402 to 12.224, p=0.010). Factors such as age, education, number of sexual partners, oral and anal sex and frequency of condom use were not significantly associated with HIV prevalence among the study participants.

Determinants of willingness to take up HIV services among MSM in Kano state

The willingness of the participants to take HIV services was assessed in table 3, and the results show that participants in Tarauni LGA (AOR 92.453, 95% CI 30.541 to 279.876, p<0.001) have higher odds of willingness to take up HIV services when compared with those in Gwale LGA. Additionally, participants who inject drugs (AOR 3.592, 95% CI 1.207 to 10.695, p=0.030) have higher odds of willingness to take up HIV services than those who do not inject drugs. Willingness to take HIV services among participants that engaged in anal sex (AOR 0.075, 95% CI 0.051 to 0.302, p<0.001) in the last 3 months was significantly lower compared with those that did not engage in anal sex in the same period. Lastly, participants who always used condoms during sexual intercourse in the last 3 months had higher odds of willingness to take up HIV services compared with those who used condoms irregularly (AOR 2.731, 95% CI 1.279 to 5.829, p=0.009). Factors such as age, education and number of sexual partners were not significantly associated with HIV prevalence among the study participants.

Discussion

The prevalence of HIV infection among MSM in our study was 12.3%. This finding is similar to research carried out by Onovo et al,22 where the seroprevalence among MSM was 12.1%. The lower prevalence may be associated with proactive health-seeking behaviour among MSM, which encourages regular HIV testing and safe sex practices.23 While MSM exhibited a prevalence of 12.3%, the indirect risk posed to their partners underscores the urgent need for inclusive prevention strategies that address all at-risk groups in the transmission network.24 Additionally, participants residing in Fagge LGA were four times more likely to have HIV infection compared with those in other LGAs. This can be associated with low understanding and education about HIV prevention and safe sex practices, restricted access to healthcare facilities or increased population density.25

In this study, participants who did not abuse drugs were two times more likely to be HIV positive in contrast to those who did abuse drugs in the unadjusted analysis and more than six times as likely in the adjusted OR. Despite not abusing drugs, these MSM may engage in other dangerous behaviours, such as sex without protection or having several sexual partners, which raises their likelihood of getting HIV.26 27 Contrarily, Sandfort et al28 found no difference in HIV prevalence between MSM with drug problems (29.6%) and those without (30.2%), with an OR of 0.98 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.83).

This study also found that MSM who engaged in vaginal sex in the last 3 months were four times increased likelihood of testing positive for HIV in contrast to those who did not. The HIV prevalence among bisexual MSM may be due to their association with both male and female populations, thereby increasing their likelihood of contracting the disease.29 30 This finding was in contrast to Amoo et al10 study, where gay men are an increased likelihood of getting HIV compared with bisexuals, probably owing to their preference for anal intercourse. These findings showed that the major reasons for testing for HIV among MSM were to know their HIV status; other reasons included confirmation of status, health and safety, first-time testing and other unspecified reasons. This reason aligns with Hirschhorn et al31 study in Nigeria, where the primary motivation for getting tested was to know their HIV status. Testing for HIV can help MSM know their HIV status and the likelihood of transferring the infection to their romantic partners.32 33

The study revealed that only 47.4% of MSM in Kano state were willing to access HIV services, reflecting suboptimal uptake. This aligns with findings from other regions in Nigeria, where uptake of critical services like PrEP and ART remains limited among KPs due to stigma, lack of awareness, and legal barriers. A study by Durosinmi-Etti et al34 found that only 50% of KPs in prioritised Nigerian states were aware of PrEP, with similar gaps in service utilisation attributed to social stigma and restrictive laws. Increasing service uptake requires interventions such as peer-led programmes and the establishment of stigma-free healthcare environments to address these barriers effectively. The study also found that more MSM (52.6%) were unwilling to use HIV services than those who were willing. This may be due to the fact that homosexuality is illegal in Nigeria, causing fear of legal trouble and social rejection.35 The findings indicate that participants in Tarauni LGA were more likely to use HIV services than those in Gwale LGA, presumably because these programmes are more readily available and accessible in Tarauni, providing MSM with a more supportive setting. Additionally, MSM who inject drugs were three times more willing to take HIV services than those who do not inject drugs, likely due to their frequent visits to healthcare systems and understanding of their increased risk for HIV. Increased willingness to use HIV services such as PrEP was also found among MSM who inject drugs in a similar study in India.36

In this study, participants who engaged in anal sex in the last 3 months were less willing to accept HIV services than those who did not involve in anal sex during the same period. Unwillingness to accept HIV services may stem from the association between engaging in anal sex and behaviours participants prefer to keep private. The fear of indirectly disclosing these behaviours could discourage them from accessing HIV services.37 38 Also, participants who always made use of a condom during sexual intercourse in the last 3 months were two times more willing to take HIV services than those who used condoms irregularly. This is most likely because regular condom use indicates a greater knowledge of HIV risk and a more proactive approach to prevention, which translates into a greater desire to be tested and receive care.39 40 The finding also shows that they are aware of the risks of infection as MSM and are already taking prevention measures such as the use of condoms during sexual intercourse. Surprisingly, those not using condoms were also unwilling to take up HIV services, which might be due to inadequate knowledge of the risks of infection or lack of access to healthcare services due to stigmatisation. This calls for urgent interventions to enlighten the MSM on the benefits of condom use in infection prevention and control.

The obstacles experienced by MSM in assessing HIV prevention services among most participants were stigmatisation by providers, lack of knowledge about HIV services from providers and lack of information specific to MSM as a key population group. These findings are similar to a Schwartz et al41 study; after the enactment of the Same-Sex Marriage Prohibition Act in Abuja, Nigeria, MSM faced increased stigma and discrimination. While Nigeria’s Same-Sex Marriage Prohibition Act exacerbates these challenges, similar legal barriers and social stigma have been documented in countries like Uganda and Kenya, where MSM also experience reduced access to healthcare services. However, progressive policies in South Africa have enabled better integration of MSM-focused HIV interventions, resulting in higher service uptake.42 43 Reports of anxiety and aversion to healthcare, lack of safe communal areas, blackmail and verbal harassment remained steady before the law but surged immediately afterwards. Furthermore, this stigma may discourage healthcare providers from discussing or promoting HIV services specialised to MSM, resulting in a lack of information.44–46

Limitation

The study employed a snowball sampling technique, which can introduce sampling bias. This technique relies on participants recruiting other participants, potentially leading to a sample that is not representative of the broader MSM population in Northern Nigeria. To mitigate this limitation, the study ensured diversity in the initial participants to encompass various subgroups within the MSM community. The study was conducted in only three selected LGAs in Kano state, which may not reflect the situation in other parts of Northern Nigeria or the country; this necessitates further research across different regions to validate the findings and ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. Data collection relied on self-reported information from participants, which can be subject to social desirability bias and recall bias. To reduce these biases, anonymous surveys were conducted, and participants were reassured of confidentiality. Training research assistants to build trust and accurately interpret responses also helped mitigate these biases.

Conclusion

These findings highlight the urgent need to address stigma and discrimination in healthcare settings, develop MSM-friendly and confidential HIV services and implement targeted interventions that promote safe sex practices and accessibility of care. Expanding community-supported programmes and addressing barriers in underserved LGAs are critical for improving healthcare engagement among MSM and reducing HIV transmission. Interventions such as peer navigators, community outreach and mobile HIV services can help build trust, improve service uptake and ensure continuity of care. This study provides a foundation for designing evidence-based policies and interventions for MSM.
