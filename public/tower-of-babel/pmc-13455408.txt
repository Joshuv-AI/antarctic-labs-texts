Three-dimensional skeletal changes after miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion in adults with maxillary transverse deficiency and mandibular deviation: a retrospective CBCT study
Authors: Minwu Yang, Chengri Li, Lingling Zhang, Lei Lei, Wenjie Li, Qiao Yi, Anqi Li, Wenlong Du, Zhong Li, Yanhao Chu, Fang Yi, Ousheng Liu, Yanqin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMiniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion has gained increasing use in skeletally mature patients with maxillary transverse deficiency. This study aimed to evaluate three-dimensional skeletal changes after miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion in adults with maxillary transverse deficiency and mandibular deviation, with particular emphasis on bilateral maxillary changes, mandibular adaptation, and the associated changes in mandibular deviation.MethodsTwenty-one adults (mean age, 21.7 years; 7 males, 14 females) with maxillary transverse deficiency and mandibular deviation who underwent miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion were retrospectively included. Cone-beam computed tomography images obtained before and after expansion were retrospectively collected for analysis. Three-dimensional measurements were performed using Mimics 21.0 software, bilateral maxillary transverse, vertical, and sagittal variables, mandibular linear measurements, and mandibular rotational variables were analyzed. Paired comparisons were performed between sides and time points. Correlation analyses were used to explore associations between skeletal changes and improvement in mandibular deviation.ResultsBefore treatment, the upper dental width was greater on the deviated side than on the non-deviated side (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). The deviated side also showed smaller vertical distances at the alveolar bone, first molar cusp, and coronoid (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). The mandible was rotated toward the deviated side in both roll and yaw. After treatment, both basal bone width and upper dental width increased on both sides (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). Selected vertical maxillary variables increased, particularly on the deviated side, whereas mandibular roll and yaw were significantly reduced (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). Mandibular deviation decreased by 1.59 ± 0.84 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that the reduction in mandibular deviation was associated with changes in mandibular roll and yaw (P < 0.05).ConclusionsIn adults with maxillary transverse deficiency and mandibular deviation, miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion resulted in transverse maxillary expansion, showed trends toward reduced interside vertical discrepancies in selected variables, and decreased mandibular deviation. Improvement in mandibular deviation was associated with adaptive mandibular rotational changes.Keywords: Maxillary transverse deficiency, Mandibular deviation, Miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion, Facial symmetry

Introduction

Maxillary transverse deficiency (MTD) is a common malocclusion and is typically characterized by unilateral or bilateral posterior crossbite, a high-arched palate, and dental crowding [1]. It may also be accompanied by masticatory dysfunction, temporomandibular imbalance, and occlusal interference [2, 3]. In some patients, persistent transverse discrepancy may induce mandibular displacement and contribute to mandibular deviation, which may in turn be associated with facial asymmetry and, in some cases, underlying skeletal asymmetry [4, 5].

Rapid palatal expansion (RPE) has long been used to correct MTD [6]. However, in skeletally mature patients, the orthopedic effect of conventional tooth-borne expansion is often limited because the midpalatal suture and the surrounding circummaxillary structures become more resistant after skeletal maturation. As a result, conventional expansion may lead to greater dentoalveolar compensation and less true skeletal widening. Miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) was introduced to improve skeletal expansion in late adolescents and adults by transmitting expansion forces more directly to the palatal bone through miniscrews [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that MARPE can achieve a high rate of suture opening and a greater skeletal component of expansion than conventional RPE, making it a valuable nonsurgical option for adult patients with MTD [7, 8].

The effects of MARPE are not limited to transverse widening. Previous CBCT studies have shown that expansion may also be accompanied by forward, downward, and lateral displacement of the zygomaticomaxillary complex, suggesting a broader three-dimensional (3D) skeletal response of the maxilla and adjacent structures [9, 10]. This may be particularly relevant in patients with MTD and mandibular deviation (MD), in whom facial asymmetry is a multidimensional skeletal problem. In addition to transverse imbalance, such patients may exhibit side-to-side differences in vertical and sagittal skeletal relationships, along with adaptive changes in mandibular position. Therefore, correction of maxillary constriction may not only widen the maxillary arch but also modify bilateral skeletal relationships and influence mandibular position or rotation.

Accurate assessment of these changes requires 3D imaging. Although posteroanterior cephalograms and panoramic radiographs have traditionally been used to evaluate facial asymmetry, their diagnostic value is limited by variations in head positioning, projection distortion, and superimposition of bilateral structures [11]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) allows 3D visualization of craniofacial structures and makes it possible to quantify skeletal changes in the transverse, vertical, and sagittal directions within a standardized coordinate system [12–14]. This provides clear advantages for evaluating bilateral skeletal differences and treatment-related changes in patients with MTD and concomitant MD.

Despite the increasing use of MARPE, most previous studies have focused on transverse expansion efficiency, suture opening, or dentoalveolar side effects. Relatively few studies have examined whether MARPE is associated with improvement in facial asymmetry, especially in adults. Some reports have suggested that condylar positional or rotational changes after MARPE may contribute to improvement in mandibular deviation [15–17]. However, these studies have mainly focused on the temporomandibular region and have not comprehensively evaluated bilateral maxillary skeletal changes after expansion or their relationship with mandibular adaptation. As a result, the 3D skeletal pathway linking maxillary expansion to improvement in facial asymmetry remains insufficiently understood.

Accordingly, this retrospective CBCT study aimed to characterize the 3D skeletal features of adults with MTD and MD and to evaluate skeletal changes after MARPE, with particular emphasis on bilateral maxillary changes, mandibular adaptation, and changes in mandibular deviation. Associations between bilateral skeletal changes and mandibular rotation were further analyzed to explore the possible structural basis of facial symmetry improvement after MARPE.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Xiangya Stomatological Hospital, Central South University (Protocol No. 20240035). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patient selection

Patients who underwent MARPE between February 2014 and December 2023 were retrospectively screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of MTD based on CBCT measurements of maxillomandibular transverse discrepancy. According to the Pennsylvania method, the ideal maxillary width is 5 mm greater than the mandibular width at the first molar region [18]. MTD was diagnosed when the measured maxillomandibular width difference was less than 5 mm, and the amount of deficiency was calculated as the discrepancy from this ideal value; (2) diagnosis of MD, defined as a menton-to-midsagittal plane (Me-MSP) distance of at least 4 mm on CBCT [19], with the deviation persisting in both the mandibular rest and open-mouth positions; (3) evidence of midpalatal suture opening on posttreatment CBCT, defined as visible radiolucent separation of the midpalatal suture on axial and coronal sections, following MARPE without adjunctive therapies; and (4) age of at least 18 years with midpalatal suture maturation stage C or later [20]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) craniofacial syndromes, maxillofacial deformities, systemic diseases, or a history of maxillofacial trauma or surgery; and (2) poor-quality pretreatment or posttreatment CBCT scans, including motion artifacts, incomplete field of view, or absence of the required CBCT records. MARPE appliance placement and expansion protocol

Orthodontic mini-implants measuring 8 × 1.4 mm, 10 × 2.0 mm, or 12 × 2.0 mm (length × diameter) were inserted in the maxilla, including the region between the premolar roots and the paramedian area adjacent to the midpalatal suture. Four miniscrews were placed in a relatively parallel configuration and distributed as widely as possible to improve stability. A modified Hyrax-type expander (Hyrax® Click; Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany) with miniscrew spiral hooks was placed and connected to the miniscrews using self-curing resin (Fig. 2). Patients were instructed to activate the expander twice daily at 12 h intervals, with 0.25 mm per activation, for 12–16 days (mean, 14 days).

Clinical application of miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion. a before expansion; b after placement of the MARPE appliance, showing the appliance configuration and miniscrew insertion sites (labeled 1–4)

CBCT image acquisition

CBCT scans were acquired before MARPE, and posttreatment CBCT scans were obtained 3–6 months after completion of expansion. In this retrospective study, posttreatment CBCT scans were obtained according to clinical needs during routine follow-up, resulting in variable acquisition timing among patients. All CBCT images were acquired using a ProMax 3D unit (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland). Scans were obtained with a 360° rotation protocol under the following exposure parameters: 10.0 mA, 96 kV, and a pulsed scan time of 12 s, and were exported in DICOM format. During image acquisition, all patients were seated upright with the head stabilized by the headrest. They were instructed to maintain maximum intercuspation while keeping the lips and tongue relaxed and avoiding swallowing.

Construction of measurement plane

DICOM data were imported into Mimics (version 21.0; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). The midsagittal plane (MSP) was constructed by aligning mirrored CBCT images using frontonasomaxillare, fontorbitomaxillare, the medial zygomaticofrontal suture, and the maxillofacial bone centroid following Damstra et al.’s morphological method [19]. The horizontal plane was defined as the plane perpendicular to the MSP and passing through the right porion (Po) and orbitale (Or). The horizontal measurement plane was constructed parallel to the horizontal plane through nasion. The coronal measurement plane was defined as a plane passing through basion (Ba) and perpendicular to MSP and horizontal measurement plane. The occlusal plane connected the mesiobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar and the mesial contact point of the central incisor (Fig. 3). Statistical analysis

The sample comprised all patients who underwent MARPE during the data acquisition period from February 2014 to December 2023 and were retrospectively screened for eligibility. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Intra-observer reliability was assessed at a 2-week interval using the intraclass correlation coefficient, and all intraclass correlation coefficient values were greater than 0.95. Normality was assessed before comparative analyses. Paired t-tests were used for normally distributed paired data, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for non-normally distributed paired data. Comparisons were performed between the deviated and non-deviated sides at T0 and T1 and within the same side between T0 and T1. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The primary outcomes were tested without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Given the presence of multiple comparisons among secondary outcomes, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at q = 0.05. Adjusted q-values are reported alongside the original P-values. Me-MSP distance was considered the primary outcome variable.

To further explore whether changes in mandibular rotational variables were associated with improvement in mandibular deviation, an exploratory multiple linear regression analysis was performed with the change in Me-MSP distance as the dependent variable and changes in mandibular roll, mandibular yaw and occlusal plane pitch as independent variables. Given the relatively small sample size, this regression analysis was considered exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

Twenty-one patients were included in this study, including 7 males and 14 females, with a mean age of 21.7 ± 4.49 years (range, 18–37 years). The mean amount of maxillary transverse deficiency was 4.44 ± 3.49 mm, and the mean pretreatment Me-MSP distance was 6.29 ± 2.72 mm. Among the included patients, 11 presented with unilateral posterior crossbite, 3 presented with bilateral posterior crossbite, and 7 had skeletal maxillary transverse deficiency without clinically evident posterior crossbite.

Pretreatment bilateral comparisons

Pretreatment bilateral comparisons of 3D maxillomandibular characteristics are presented in Table 3. In the transverse dimension, the deviated side showed significantly greater upper dental width than the non-deviated side, with a mean difference of 1.88 ± 2.38 mm (P < 0.01, q < 0.01), whereas basal bone width did not differ significantly between sides (P > 0.05). In the vertical dimension, the deviated side showed significantly smaller alveolar bone vertical distance, tooth vertical distance, and coronoid vertical distance than the non-deviated side (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). For the mandible, ramus height showed no significant difference between the two sides (P > 0.05), whereas mandibular body length was significantly greater on the non-deviated side by 3.99 ± 2.89 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). These findings indicate that patients with MTD and MD showed bilateral maxillomandibular asymmetry before treatment.

Posttreatment bilateral comparisons

Posttreatment bilateral comparisons of 3D maxillomandibular characteristics are presented in Table 4. In the transverse dimension, no significant difference in upper dental width was observed between the two sides after treatment (P > 0.05). In the vertical dimension, tooth vertical distance remained significantly smaller on the deviated side than on the non-deviated side (P < 0.05, q < 0.05), whereas the difference in alveolar bone vertical distance and coronoid vertical distance were no longer statistically significant (q > 0.05). In the sagittal dimension, no significant differences were detected between the two sides (P > 0.05). Body length also remained significantly smaller on the deviated side, with a mean difference of 3.82 ± 3.09 mm (P < 0.01, q < 0.01). Representative CBCT images further illustrated posttreatment improvement in facial symmetry after MARPE, together with transverse maxillary expansion, increased vertical maxillary dimensions, and opening of the midpalatal suture (Fig. 5).

Ipsilateral changes after MARPE

Changes in ipsilateral linear and angular measurements after MARPE are summarized in Table 5. In the transverse dimension, both basal bone width and upper dental width increased significantly on both sides (P < 0.05, q < 0.05), whereas while mandibular deviation decreased by 1.59 ± 0.84 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). In the vertical dimension, the deviated side showed significant increases in jugal vertical distance (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). Although alveolar bone vertical distance, tooth vertical distance, and coronoid vertical distance also increased, these changes did not remain significant after multiple-comparison correction (q > 0.05). On the non-deviated side, the vertical variables showed no significant changes (q > 0.05). In addition, A-point vertical distance also increased by 0.97 ± 1.07 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05), and B-point vertical distance increased by 0.94 ± 1.83 mm (P < 0.05, q > 0.05). In the sagittal dimension, the anteroposterior lengths of UR6/UL6 increased significantly on both sides (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). A-point anteroposterior length increased by 0.91 ± 1.30 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05), whereas B-point anteroposterior length decreased by 0.83 ± 1.58 mm (P < 0.05, q < 0.05). No significant changes were observed in ramus height or body length (P > 0.05). With regard to rotational variables, mandibular yaw and mandibular roll decreased significantly after MARPE (P < 0.05, q < 0.05), whereas occlusal plane pitch increased significantly (P < 0.01, q < 0.05).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant correlations between reduction in mandibular deviation (ΔMe-MSP) and changes in mandibular roll (r = 0.584, P < 0.05) and mandibular yaw (r = 0.484, P < 0.05), whereas no significant correlation was found with occlusal plane pitch (P > 0.05) (Table 6; Fig. 6). Exploratory multiple linear regression analysis yielded the following equation: ΔMe-MSP = 0.408 + 0.534MR + 0.644MY (P < 0.05), suggesting that improvement in mandibular deviation was associated with changes in mandibular rotation (Table 6).

Discussion

A balanced face depends on harmonious proportions in all three spatial dimensions. However, orthodontic diagnosis has traditionally focused more on sagittal discrepancies than on transverse and vertical disharmony. Because vertical facial proportions are important determinants of facial esthetics and overall harmony, three-dimensional evaluation is particularly valuable in patients with asymmetry [22]. In the present study, adults with MTD and MD showed combined maxillary and mandibular asymmetry rather than isolated mandibular deviation. Basal maxillary width did not differ significantly between sides, whereas the upper dental width was wider on the deviated side (Table 3). This pattern suggests dentoalveolar compensation, possibly through buccal inclination of the posterior teeth, to reduce unilateral crossbite and occlusal interference. In the vertical dimension, the non-deviated side showed greater skeletal and dental heights, indicating vertical maxillary imbalance. Mandibular asymmetry was also evident, as the mandibular body was longer on the non-deviated side and the mandible showed yaw and roll toward the deviated side, supporting the view that MD involves both structural asymmetry and functional displacement. These findings are consistent with previous reports [23, 24].

After MARPE, both sides of the maxilla showed significant transverse expansion, and the maxilla also displayed a mild forward and downward displacement, which is consistent with previous CBCT studies on MARPE in young adults [25–27]. Although skeletal expansion in the present cohort was generally bilateral, the dentoalveolar response appeared less uniform after expansion (Table 5). This discrepancy may be explained by the pretreatment difference in posterior tooth inclination: the deviated side had already undergone greater buccal dentoalveolar compensation, whereas the non-deviated side was relatively more upright. With a tooth-bone-borne expander, forces transmitted through the miniscrews may be distributed more evenly to the maxillary skeleton, whereas the dental response may still vary according to initial tooth inclination and local biomechanical conditions.

Clinically, this asymmetric dental expansion may still be favorable. If it reduces the pre-existing transverse discrepancy between sides, it may improve maxillary coordination and create a more balanced arch form for subsequent fixed orthodontic treatment [28, 29]. This may be especially important in patients with MD, in whom transverse disharmony and occlusal interference are closely associated with mandibular positional adaptation. Regarding periodontal support and dentition after expansion, MARPE appeared to produce a predominantly skeletal response, although dentoalveolar effects were still present. Previous evidence suggests that, compared with conventional tooth-borne expansion, MARPE is associated with fewer periodontal side effects and less buccal bone loss [30], but it does not completely eliminate the risk of alveolar bone reduction or tooth inclination changes around the anchor teeth. Therefore, the post-expansion dentition may be considered more coordinated transversely, but still requires careful torque control, root positioning, and periodontal monitoring during the subsequent fixed appliance stage.

In the vertical dimension, the deviated side showed greater downward displacement than the non-deviated side, and the interside discrepancy was reduced after treatment (Tables 3 and 4). Although the non-deviated side remained vertically greater overall, the reduction in side-to-side difference suggests an improvement in vertical maxillary symmetry after expansion. No statistically significant vertical changes were observed in the condylar or coronoid regions after multiple-comparison correction, the slight trends detected may reflect adaptive mandibular positional changes after expansion rather than uniform skeletal remodeling of the mandible [15]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, because vertical change after expansion is likely multifactorial and may involve sutural response, occlusal alteration, and dentoalveolar adaptation. In the present study, mandibular yaw and roll decreased significantly after MARPE, and B-point showed a backward change while occlusal plane pitch increased. These findings may partly reflect adaptive mandibular rotational changes after expansion. In particular, the backward displacement of B point and increase in occlusal plane pitch may be consistent with clockwise mandibular rotation, which could be partly related to vertical dentoalveolar effects from the mixed miniscrew and dental anchorage of the appliance. One possible explanation is that MARPE altered occlusal contacts, muscle balance, and condylar position, thereby promoting adaptive mandibular repositioning [31, 32]. Therefore, vertical skeletal pattern, sagittal jaw relationship, and potential mandibular rotation should be considered during MARPE treatment planning.

Based on our findings, MARPE may offer benefits beyond simple transverse expansion in adults with MTD and MD. In addition to widening the maxilla, it may help improve bilateral maxillomandibular coordination and reduce mandibular deviation. Therefore, MARPE may be considered early in treatment planning as a nonsurgical option for appropriately selected patients. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the retrospective design, the relatively small sample size, the absence of a control group, variable posttreatment CBCT acquisition intervals, the lack of long-term follow-up data, and the lack of direct assessment of functional factors. These factors limit the extent to which causal conclusions can be drawn, and the observed skeletal and mandibular changes should therefore be interpreted with caution. The 3–6 month posttreatment CBCT acquisition interval may have introduced measurement variability due to ongoing skeletal remodeling, dentoalveolar adaptation, and mandibular positional changes. As a result, the mechanisms underlying mandibular adaptation remain unclear, and the long-term stability of these changes still needs to be verified. Although the present CBCT measurements were performed manually, artificial intelligence has shown promise in improving the consistency of oral radiographic interpretation [33]. Future MARPE studies may benefit from AI-assisted landmark identification and automated three-dimensional skeletal analysis.

Conclusion

Adults with maxillary transverse deficiency and mandibular deviation showed pre-existing maxillomandibular asymmetry in the transverse and vertical dimensions. After MARPE, transverse maxillary expansion was achieved and vertical measurements increased, with reduced side-to-side discrepancy in selected vertical variables. Mandibular deviation also decreased, and its improvement was associated with mandibular rotational changes. Further controlled studies with larger samples and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.
