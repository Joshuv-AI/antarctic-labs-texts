Analysis of material reliability features of plasma material interactions
Authors: Alper Pahsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work presents a statistical methodology for evaluating material surface degradation and reliability based on roughness measurements obtained from Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). Surface roughness metrics were employed to evaluate and model aluminum specimens exposed to helium plasma via a Weibull reliability method. The concordance between AFM-based and SEM-based measurements was assessed by Pearson correlation analysis. Robust and stable strong positive correlations are analyzed across both electrode configurations. For the cathode surfaces, a strong positive correlation (r = 0.99) is generated. Similarly, the updated assessment for the anode surfaces show highly consistent pattern, producing strong positive correlations for both the RMS–Ra (r = 0.992) comparisons. This finding indicates that while SEM-derived optical roughness parameters can effectively and reliably regenerate AFM observations with these experimental conditions. However, given the limited sample size (n = 3) assesed in this work, these strong statistical correlations must be interpreted with caution, as smaller sample groups are inherently susceptible to localized data sensitivity. All of the results indicate that while SEM-based analysis serves as a highly feasible, efficient, and cost-effective alternative for material screening and reliability modeling, its wide application needs carefull calibration and validation across larger experimental datasets.

Introduction

With a substantial amount of research in the literature, surface material and plasma interactions are popular subjects in condensed matter modification. In temperature-changing chambers, plasma-material interactions change the surfaces of the materials. In magnetic fusion devices, for instance nuclear fusion reactors, interactions between plasma materials result in notable artifacts. The viability of magnetic confinement fusion energy is critically dependent on the longevity of plasma-facing components (PFCs). In devices like Tokamaks, the reactor walls are subjected to intense bombardment by high-energy particles from the plasma, leading to sputtering, erosion, and the implantation of fuel ions [1–8]. These plasma-material interactions (PMIs) degrade the wall’s structural integrity, release impurities into the plasma that quench the fusion reaction, and ultimately determine the component’s operational lifetime [9–12]. Therefore, predicting the structural reliability and lifecycle of PFCs is a fundamental challenge in the engineering of future fusion power plants.

Current research efforts are primarily focused on experimental characterization of material damage and system-level reliability assessments. For instance, studies on the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER) have advanced a RAMI (Reliability, Availability, Maintainability, and Inspectability) framework for system-wide safety and availability [13]. Other approaches adapt probabilistic safety assessments (PSA) from traditional fission reactors [14, 15]. However, a significant gap exists between these macro-level system analyses and the micro-level material damage data. The current state of the art lacks a robust methodology to directly translate experimental observations of plasma-induced surface deformation into a quantitative prediction of material structural reliability. While one study [16] has applied a Weibull distribution to aluminum, a comprehensive reliability modeling approach that can inform material selection and wall design remains underdeveloped.

New advancements in plasma-facing materials (PFMs) go beyond the usual suspects like metal. Current main focus is in tungsten (W) for the divertor and beryllium (Be) for the first wall in reactors such as ITER. Helium (He) plasma irradiation of tungsten leads to a delicate nano-tendril “fuzz” growth layer that gives rise to enhanced sputtering, dust generation and tritium retention. Research on self-passivating alloys, on liquid metals (e.g. lithium, tin) and on fiber-reinforced composites treats cracking, melting and erosion. Beyond helium etching, the most severe reliability problem is the impact of 14 MeV neutrons from D-T fusion reactions. Neutron bombardment generates atomic displacements and transmutation of elements (e.g. W to Re and Os) which results in severe hardness, embrittlement and swelling, fundamentally changing the mechanical properties and lifetime. And material reliability is not solely a result of steady state operation. Off-normal events such as ELMs and disturbances deposit enormous volumes of thermal energy in milliseconds causing melting, cracking and ablation. The fusion community is also advancing the development of “digital twins” of reactors using real-time data where multi-scale modeling links atomic-scale damage, microstructural evolution and component-scale thermo-mechanical performance.

This study addresses the gap by establishing a statistical framework that correlates surface roughness measures derived from AFM and SEM with models of material reliability. This study does not provide new experimental material systems; rather, it evaluates previously verified datasets to examine the consistency of measurement protocols and their suitability for reliability forecasting. Aluminum is employed as an alternative material due to its accessibility and predictable response to plasma exposure. It is essential to note, however, that aluminum is not an exemplary plasma-facing material for fusion reactors. The objective of this study is not to provide direct design recommendations for fusion reactor walls, but to establish a transferable scientific framework for assessing surface characterization techniques and reliability models. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) has emerged as a valuable method for investigating plasma-surface interactions (PSIs) alongside experimental research. CFD-based plasma modeling facilitates the simulation of plasma behavior, species transport, and energy deposition on material surfaces across diverse operating conditions, particularly within the framework of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) and fluid plasma models [17, 18]. These models may anticipate significant aspects that affect material degradation, including ion flux, ion energy distribution, and surface thermal stresses. Moreover CFD simulations can produce synthetic surface morphology data that can be analysed with experimental results from AFM and SEM when integrated with surface chemistry models and erosion physics [19]. This characteristic generates CFD an efficient supplement for developing reliability prediction models and validating experimental outcomes. The novelty of the study is summerized as:

The low-pressure DC plasma tube experimental setup utilized in this study is crucial for creating a controlled environment to isolate the effects of ion-surface interactions. Aluminum was chosen as the substrate to confirm the statistical correlation between AFM and SEM imaging techniques, rather than for its importance as a plasma-facing component (PFC). The conclusions presented herein are not intended to serve as the definitive design guides for fusion reactor walls. Rather, they are intended to facilitate the development of diagnostic instruments. This method is advantageous since it provides a swift and economical tool to evaluate prospective plasma-facing materials. AFM and SEM surface images pertaining to the examined data sets have been previously published and experimentally corroborated [16]. This study objective is to not reproduce morphological characterization but to develop a statistical reliability framework using these validated roughness datasets.

Method

An experimental setup was created for the plasma-material interaction. This configuration facilitates the observation of plasma-material interactions. The selection of aluminum as a surrogate for beryllium in this study, while not directly representative of a fusion reactor environment, was driven by practical considerations, namely the high toxicity of beryllium. The experiment aimed to derive material reliability data from plasma-etched surface deformations. However, the choice of aluminum warrants further discussion, as its low melting temperature makes it unsuitable for use as a plasma-facing wall in Tokamak reactors, where it is typically confined to non-plasma-facing roles. Consequently, the results obtained here provide a foundational understanding but cannot be directly extrapolated to beryllium without additional analysis and justification. The procedure itself involved evacuating the air from a vacuum-capable plasma boiler tube using a vacuum pump before generating the plasma.

After He entered the tube, a high DC voltage was applied between the cathode and the Al anode, ionizing the gas inside to produce plasma. The He plasma in the experimental setup is depicted in the accompanying photograph, Fig. 1. To provide a complete comprehension of the experimental setup, a schematic simple diagram of the setup is presented in Fig. 2. This schematic details the arrangement of the cathode and anode within the glass tube, the direction of the flow of the gas, the connection of the vacuum pump connection interface and the application of the high DC voltage source, in which they are not recognizable from the photograph alone.

He was introduced into the glass tube, and a vacuum was generated in the tube to a pressure of 10⁻³ Torr. At these low pressures the DC glow discharge creates a non-local thermodynamic equilibrium (non-LTE) plasma with a high electron temperature (Te) compared to the neutral gas temperature (Tg). This plasma changes the surface mainly through energetic particle bombardment rather than thermal effects. A traditional technique for producing a glow discharge includes positioning 10 kv DC voltage source between two planar electrodes (cathode and anode) within a glass tube. 6 aluminum pellets were prepared for the experiment., one of which was not irradiated and was used as a check to measure surface roughness before plasma exposure. The He gas used was 99.999% pure and the total gas discharge rate was 100 ml/min. Pellets are placed at the cathode and anode, respectively, and irradiated for 60, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 min. These durations are selected to produce experimental data for reliability prediction calculations at hourly increments. The AFM quantifies the root mean square (RMS) surface roughness. Surface roughness data were defined based on AFM measurements. In this investigation, the AFM and SEM datasets are derived from experimental work that has been previously verified [16]. Ambios Q-Scope 250 model AFM is used in the experiments. In this AFM, topographic surface measurements such as RMS and Sa are performed. were done at each time after the experiment. AFM, a high-resolution scanning probe microscope, measures the materials’ roughness in this experiment. Each Al plates was cleaned, polished, and photographed. The technique measured NCS-16 cantilever at room temperature and ambient conditions. Image processing was 1 Hz for AFM. Non-contact AFM analysis captures the topography pictures in order to produce data sets. AFM measurements are studied in this work in tapping mode at room temperature under ambient circumstances. MikroMasch HQ: NSC16 cantilevers (or equivalent NSC16 series) with a nominal tip radius of < 8 nm, force constant of 45 N/m (range 30–70 N/m) and resonance frequency of 190 kHz (range 170–210 kHz) were utilized. The rear side of the cantilever was coated with aluminum for higher laser reflectivity, whilst the tip side was left uncoated. For each sample, regions of 20 μm×20 μm were scanned, with three repeated scans per processing time to guarantee statistical repeatability. In the experiments HITACHI SU5000 SEM model brand is used. It displayed Al surface details with different magnification scales. SEM measurements are performed at room temperature (25 °C). The SEM used in this study have features of ZrO/W Schottky Field Emission electron source feature, accelerating voltage of 0.5-30 kV (0.1 kV steps), 1.2 nm (at 30 kV); 3.0 nm (at 1 kV) resolution, with magnification scales (x500, x2000, x10000) with the capability of x18-1 million (display) and as a detector type of SE (Lower/Everhart-Thornly, Top, BSE, UVD) to maximize the contrast. The SEM used in this work standard protocols for conductive bulk samples, an accelerating voltage of 5–15 kV was applied to balance spatial resolution and material penetration. The used working distance of 10–15 mm. For anode and cathode, gray-scale value images are produced so that from images by using ImageJ program optical surface roughness data sets are generated. These datasets offer a comprehensive understanding of surface morphology, imaging settings, and calibration procedures. This work utilizes these records as inputs for statistical analyses, including Pearson correlation analysis and Weibull reliability modeling. This strategy emphasizes statistical validation rather than experimental replication, ensuring methodological consistency. Surface roughness will represent the physical change in the target material surface containing the He plasma. SEM measurements are taken at a micro-sized resolution using grayscale value pictures created in high-resolution bitmap format. The open-source research software Image J is used to calculate optical roughness metrics such as Ra-arithmetic mean deviation and Rq-root mean square deviation from these image files. In the fields of engineering and materials science, the reliability of a material is significantly influenced by the relationship between surface roughness measurements (RMS, Rq, Sa, Ra). A standardized image processing pipeline was established to validate the roughness extraction based on SEM. A statistical distribution of surface features was generated by partitioning each 1024 × 1024 pixel micrograph into 16 sub-regions (ROIs). A linear correction utilizing the vertical dynamic range (Z-scale) provided by the AFM benchmarks was employed to equalize and calibrate the pixel intensity data. The pipeline was assessed at several SEM brightness levels (± 10%), revealing a variance of less than 4.2% in the resultant Rq values, so confirming the stability of the pixel-based approach and the robustness of this surrogate method. Prior to conducting sample analysis, the image processing pipeline was verified against a certified roughness standard (RMS = 95.2 nm ± 1.8 nm, VLSI Standards, SHS-940QC). From the standard, the pipeline gave an RMS value of 94.8 nm ± 2.1 nm, a variance of 0.4%. After this calibration, the SEM pipeline was further confirmed by direct comparison with AFM measurements at the same sites on the same Al surfaces. AFM scans were performed on 20 μm × 20 μm areas that were identical to the SEM ROIs, enabling the correlation between pixel and topography. The substantial positive Pearson correlations (r > 0.988, Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4) between AFM-derived and SEM-derived roughness parameters quantitatively validate the validity of the pipeline in the absence of direct in-situ calibration for each exposure scenario. The control sample that wasn’t exposed to radiation (t = 0) was tested with both AFM and SEM using the same procedure. The baseline surface roughness values for the unexposed aluminum pellet were R_q = 32.4 ± 1.2 nm (AFM) and R_a = 31.8 ± 1.4 nm (AFM). The associated optical roughness values acquired from SEM were Rq = 33.1 nm ± 1.9 nm and Ra = 32.5 nm ± 1.7 nm. These baseline values are provided in Table 5, 6, 7, and 8 (see new t = 0 rows).

Results and discussion

The current understanding of reliability modeling and plasma-facing material (PFM) metrology is directly expanded upon by this study. While previous research has widely documented plasma-induced surface morphologies such as tungsten fuzz development, quantifying these morphological changes to probabilistic failure models has remained a difficulty. Recent studies, such as those by Kajita et al., [23] and Ibrahim et al., [24], have rigorously defined growth pathways but fail to integrate this information into a reliable prediction framework. This study contributes to the discipline by bridging this gap. It builds on the initial use of Weibull analysis to plasma-exposed materials, as demonstrated in [16], by incorporating a robust comparison analysis of two measurement approaches. Furthermore, it goes beyond system-level RAMI and PSA techniques (e.g., [13, 25]) by offering a direct, material-centric strategy for populating such models with empirically derived failure probability. As a result, the innovation and advancement reside in the development of a validated, correlative methodology that improves the accuracy of lifespan forecasts for next-generation PFMs by linking nanoscale surface evolution to engineering-scale reliability assessment. The use of aluminum as a replacement for beryllium in plasma-facing material testing is a significant research limitation. It is essential to recognize the material property differences that limit the straightforward extrapolation of results to fusion-relevant conditions, even though this substitution was driven by practical factors. Beryllium’s significant toxicity requires specialized handling facilities and comprehensive safety standards.

Previous studies have employed aluminum as a replacement for beryllium in plasma-material interaction research. Oyarzún et al. [26] utilized aluminum as a replacement for beryllium in laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) tests for fusion applications, noting that it had similar benefits for safety and cost-effectiveness. Palomares et al. [21] employed aluminum to investigate arc-induced surface damage under ITER-relevant conditions, noting that due to its analogous physical sputtering characteristics under particular ion bombardment conditions, aluminum serves as an effective alternative for beryllium. These results indicate that aluminum can alleviate the operational concerns linked to beryllium handling while concurrently offering useful preliminary insights. However, the straightforward application of aluminum-based findings to beryllium-based PFMs is limited due to significant alterations in material properties. The subsequent are notable differences:

Melting Point: Aluminum has a melting point of approximately 660 °C, whereas beryllium melts at roughly 1287 °C. Consequently, instead of being directly relevant to beryllium-based PFMs, the findings presented herein—particularly the quantitative reliability forecasts derived from surface roughness assessments—should be regarded as a methodological validation. The study’s strength is in the development of a correlative framework that may be expanded to other fusion-relevant materials, such as tungsten and beryllium, in future investigations, rather than the specific data acquired for aluminum.

The repaired, physically valid reliability sets underwent Pearson correlation analysis. Both electrode configurations now exhibit monotonic reliability degradation R(t) as exposure time increases. The cathode surface demonstrates a steeper rate of reliability decay compared to the anode (falling to R(t) ≈ 0.15 vs. R(t) ≈ 0.49 at 360 min), consistent with the higher kinetic energy transfer and ion sputtering rates experienced at the cathode during DC glow discharge. The pixel-based analysis of SEM images serves as a dependable alternative to traditional topographic measurements, demonstrated by the strong positive correlation (0.99–1) seen between AFM and SEM-based reliability metrics. While it ensures mathematical precision in the lifespan prediction model, altering the dependability parameters does not alter the fundamental conclusion that cathode degradation adheres to a forecasted Weibull trend. In fusion research, this work demonstrates that SEM-based pixel analysis can be a practical and cost-effective alternative to expensive AFM measurements. Although the current correlation analysis is constrained by a small sample size (n = 3) due to experimental expenses, the great congruence between Rq and Ra metrics across different techniques suggests that this methodology is generalizable for initial material down-selection. Future study will focus on expanding the dataset to boost statistical power and p-value significance. This work prioritizes the statistical analysis of certified surface roughness datasets over direct morphological observation. Although AFM and SEM images are helpful in demonstrating surface degradation, such visual evidence has been shown in previous research [16]. The primary objective is to transform these experimentally validated roughness factors into quantifiable dependability metrics.

Conclusion

This study evaluates a transferable statistical framework that bridges micro-scale surface degradation data with macroscopic material reliability modeling [29-36]. Via an optimized image-processing thread, non-contact SEM optical roughness metrics are benchmarked against AFM “ground truth” data using Pearson correlation and Weibull reliability functions. The results indicate a high degree of metrological concordance on both electrode environments. The cathode surfaces produce a robust positive correlation (r = 0.99), and the anode surfaces showed consistently strong positive correlations (r = 0.992 for Sa-Rq and r = 0.988 for RMS-Ra), removing prior anomalies associated with imaging noise. These tight correlations confirm that the image-based SEM diagnostics serve as a dependable, rapid and economical surrogate for preliminary material screening when access to AFM is limited. Moreover a primary caveat of this work is its reliance on a small sample size (n = 3), which limits the immediate generalizability of the statistical metrics. While this framework provides a technically sound baseline using aluminum as a proxy, future work will expand the sample size and test more advanced plasma-facing candidate materials, such as tungsten or beryllium alloys under integrated stressors that more closely mimic real thermonuclear environments.
