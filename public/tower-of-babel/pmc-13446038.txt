Sustained pain relief across modic subtypes following basivertebral nerve ablation: A real-world study
Authors: Emily R Bellow, Justin N Passman, Derek Johnson, Jennifer Bae, Sandi Bajrami, Yvette Sol, William Caldwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasivertebral nerve (BVN) ablation has emerged as a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment for patients with vertebrogenic low back pain and corresponding Modic Changes on MRI. However, real-world evidence evaluating the impact of Modic Change subtype on clinical outcomes is limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of Modic Change subtype on clinical outcomes following BVN ablation in a real-world cohort.MethodsAdult patients who underwent BVN ablation at our institution from November 2019 to January 2025 were included in this retrospective study. A total of 134 patients were classified by Modic Change type on their pre-procedural MRI and stratified into Type 1 (N = 59), Type 2 (N = 52), and Mixed (N = 23) Groups. Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and represents a major cause of functional limitation and healthcare utilization [1]. A subset of CLBP is vertebrogenic pain, which originates from damaged vertebral endplates that irritate the basivertebral nerve [2]. This physiological damage is shown as Modic Changes (MC) on MRI, which are categorized into Type 1 inflammatory changes, Type 2 fatty degenerative changes, and, less commonly, Type 3 sclerotic changes [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Mixed MC occur when features of more than one subtype are present, and are commonly encountered in clinical practice [7]. Prior studies suggest that MC represent a dynamic continuum. For example, the Mixed subtype may represent a transition state from Type 1 to Type 2, and Type 2 changes may even reconvert to Type 1 in cases of inflammation reactivation [8,9].

These vertebral endplates are highly innervated by the basivertebral nerve, which has been identified as an important pain-generating source for vertebrogenic CLBP [2,5,10]. As a result, basivertebral nerve (BVN) ablation has emerged as a minimally invasive treatment for vertebrogenic CLBP associated with MC on MRI [11]. Randomized controlled trials, including the SMART and INTRACEPT trials, have demonstrated significant and sustained pain relief and functional improvement for patients with vertebrogenic CLBP undergoing BVN ablation [[11], [12], [13]].

Prior studies have examined predictors of clinical response following BVN ablation, with particular attention to patient-level characteristics that may influence treatment outcomes. McCormick et al. (2022) and Boody et al. (2022) conducted aggregated cohort analyses using data from randomized controlled trials and found no significant association between Modic subtype and treatment response [14,15]. However, in both studies, Modic subtype was assessed as a secondary imaging characteristic rather than a primary stratification variable. Furthermore, neither study evaluated patients with Mixed MC as a distinct subgroup.

Despite the increasing adoption of BVN ablation, real-world evidence evaluating outcomes across Modic subtypes outside of clinical trial populations remains limited. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of Modic subtype on clinical outcomes following BVN ablation in a real-world cohort.

Methods

Ethics statement

This retrospective chart review study was approved by the Stony Brook University Hospital Committee on Research in Human Subjects via a waiver of consent (IRB2025-00033).

Subject selection and patient group designation

All consecutive adult patients (age ≥18) who underwent BVN ablation at our institution from November 2019 to January 2025 were included in this retrospective chart review study. No patients were excluded from the analysis. All patients had available pre-procedural lumbar MRI demonstrating Modic Changes and documented clinical follow-up data within the electronic medical record (EMR). Patient demographics, baseline clinical characteristics, and outcome data were extracted from the EMR. Patients were classified according to the Modic Change type documented on the most recent MRI before their procedure, and stratified into Type 1, Type 2, and Mixed Groups based on the interpreting radiologist's report. Mixed Modic Changes were defined as the presence of both Type 1 and Type 2 Modic Changes on the pre-procedural MRI. Pain severity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at baseline, 4–6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months post-procedure. Functional improvement was determined based on documented patient-reported outcomes at the same time points.

Clinical and procedural data collection

Demographics, baseline clinical characteristics, and intra-procedure data for all included patients were extracted from the EMR. Intra-procedure data included the number of vertebral levels treated, the type of anesthesia used, and ablation parameters. Representative fluoroscopic images of the BVN ablation procedure are presented in Fig. 1. BVN ablation was performed as a standalone procedure, and no concurrent interventional spine procedures were performed at the time of treatment. Pain severity was assessed using VAS at baseline, 4–6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months post-procedure. Functional improvement was determined based on documented patient-reported outcomes at the same time points. During follow-up visits, patients were typically asked about their ability to perform daily activities, including walking, standing, sitting, bending, and returning to work or hobbies. Functional improvement was defined as any clinical documentation of increased activity levels, enhanced mobility, return to work, or general improvement in performing activities of daily living as recorded in the clinical notes at these follow-up visits. Post-procedure complications were monitored until the most recent available follow-up. The length of follow-up was recorded for each patient. No patients meeting the inclusion criteria were lost to follow-up, and outcome data were available for all patients at 4–6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months.

Representative anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic images of basivertebral nerve ablation demonstrating transpedicular access and intraosseous probe positioning within the vertebral body at the treatment target zone.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26™ (IBM Corp.). Two-sided P values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. We utilized one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables with Tukey post-hoc testing when appropriate. We utilized chi-square tests and Fischer's exact tests for categorical variables. Within-group changes in VAS from baseline to follow-up were analyzed using paired samples t-tests.

Secondary clinical outcomes, complications, and follow-up duration

Table 5 illustrates secondary clinical outcomes, follow-up duration, and the incidence of complications, which were monitored until the most recent available follow-up visit. No vertebral compression fractures were observed in the entire cohort, with a mean follow-up length of 2.85 ± 0.97 years. There was no statistically significant difference when comparing the length of follow-up between the Type 1, Type 2, and Mixed Groups. A total of four complications were reported, which included left foot drop, transient lower extremity muscle spasms, paraspinal muscle spasms, and left foot paresthesia. All complications were self-limited and resolved by the end of follow-up. Among the 22 patients receiving opioid therapy at baseline, no patients discontinued opioid use during the follow-up period. Functional improvement was reported by 79.1% of patients in the overall cohort, with no significant differences between the Type 1 (76.3%), Type 2 (78.8%), and Mixed Groups (87.0%).

Discussion

This study evaluated the safety and effectiveness of BVN ablation in a cohort of 134 patients, including those with Type 1, Type 2, and Mixed Modic Changes. We performed a retrospective EMR review of all adult patients who underwent BVN ablation at our institution from November 2019 to January 2025. Demographic data, baseline clinical characteristics, procedural data, outcomes, and complications were analyzed and compared across the three Modic subtype groups.

At 12 months post-procedure, we observed a mean VAS decrease of 4.04 ± 2.64 for the overall cohort, with no significant difference among Modic subtype groups. Our cohort's VAS decrease is comparable to prior studies on BVN ablation. The SMART trial reported a mean VAS decrease of 2.76 ± 2.89 at 12 months, while the INTRACEPT trial reported a mean VAS decrease of 4.0 ± 2.6 at the same time interval [11,16]. Taken together, these findings suggest that meaningful clinical improvement can be achieved in patients across Modic subtypes in both clinical trials and real-world settings.

Despite significant, sustained improvements in VAS, we observed no change in opioid use status among patients in our cohort receiving opioids at baseline. This may reflect the lower baseline prevalence of opioid use in our cohort compared to other studies of BVN ablation (8.2% in our overall cohort as compared to, e.g., 30% of patients in the SMART trial) [12]. Additionally, chronic opioid therapy is often influenced by long-term prescribing patterns and patient-specific tapering strategies rather than pain improvement alone [[17], [18], [19]].

The mean age of our overall cohort was 69.80 ± 11.43 years, which is significantly older than the cohorts in prior studies of BVN ablation. The mean age of the cohort in the SMART trial was 47 years, and the mean age of the cohort in the INTRACEPT trial was 50 years [11,13]. These prior trials commonly excluded patients with prior lumbar spine surgery, significant spinal stenosis or degenerative spinal changes, and metabolic bone disease, including osteoporosis or osteopenia [11,13]. These exclusion criteria likely selected for a younger, less comorbid cohort compared to our real-world patient population. Our study reinforces the findings in the real-world study conducted by Schnapp et al., who reported a clinically significant VAS decrease in a cohort of 31 patients with a mean age of 73.0 years [20]. Our findings build on prior real-world data suggesting that advanced age alone is not a barrier to treatment response.

We found that the Mixed Group was significantly younger and had a significantly higher proportion of male patients compared to the Type 1 and Type 2 Groups, but did not observe any other demographic differences between groups. There is limited literature evaluating demographic differences across Modic subtypes, and prior studies have not demonstrated consistent relationships between Modic subtype and specific patient characteristics. While some studies in spine surgery have reported differences in sex distribution when comparing patients with MC to those without, these findings have not been consistent across studies and have not been associated with differences in clinical outcomes [21].

Our Mixed Group also demonstrated a lower baseline VAS and lower VAS at follow-up than the Type 1 and Type 2 Groups. Contrary to our results, which suggest Mixed MC may be associated with decreased pain severity, Teraguchi et al. reported higher VAS scores for patients with Mixed MC compared to those with Type 1 MC, suggesting variability in baseline pain burden across studies [22].

Importantly, MC are increasingly recognized as a dynamic continuum, with Mixed MC often representing a transitional state between Type 1 and Type 2 changes and interconversion possible between subtypes [8,9]. Within this framework, our findings suggest that the clinical response to BVN ablation may be similar across the spectrum of Modic-associated vertebrogenic pain.

Despite differences in baseline pain severity, we observed no significant difference in the magnitude of VAS decrease between groups. Significant improvement in LBP was seen across all groups, with no significant association between treatment response and Modic subtype. These findings align with prior aggregated cohort analyses by McCormick et al. and Boody et al., which similarly found no association between Modic subtype and responder status [14,15]. In those analyses, Modic subtype was evaluated as a secondary imaging characteristic, and Mixed Modic changes were not analyzed as a distinct subgroup. In addition, these prior studies were conducted in more highly selected trial populations compared with the present real-world cohort. Overall, our findings suggest that although baseline pain burden may differ across Modic subtypes, the relative therapeutic benefit of BVN ablation appears consistent across groups.

Importantly, no vertebral compression fractures were observed in the entire cohort, and only four complications occurred, all of which were self-limited and resolved by the end of follow-up. These findings align with our prior analysis of BVN ablation at our institution, which similarly demonstrated no vertebral compression fractures in patients with reduced bone density undergoing the procedure [23]. Overall, these results support the safety of BVN ablation in real-world clinical practice.

Limitations

Our cohort of 134 patients provides a relatively large sample size compared to previous studies on BVN ablation, but certain limitations remain. As a retrospective analysis, our study is inherently limited by the observational nature of the study design. Determination of patient Modic subtype was based on available MRI reports, introducing variability in the interpretation and reporting of imaging findings between radiologists. However, this reflects real-world clinical practice, as patients are selected for BVN ablation based on reported imaging characteristics rather than standardized imaging review.

Additionally, objective measures of functional status and overall well-being, such as the Oswestry Disability Index and Short Form Health Survey, were not routinely collected by office staff at our institution, limiting direct comparison to prior studies that employed these metrics. Consequently, functional improvement was assessed via subjective patient reports obtained at follow-up visits.

Finally, detailed characterization of Modic changes, including the extent of endplate involvement, was not consistently available, thus limiting our ability to perform more detailed imaging-based analyses. Prospective studies with standardized imaging protocols may better define the relationship between Modic subtype and BVN ablation outcomes, further refining our understanding of the relationship between Modic subtype and treatment response.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, BVN ablation produced significant and sustained pain relief in patients with Type 1, Type 2, and Mixed Modic Changes, with comparable treatment response across groups. Although the baseline and follow-up VAS were lower in the Mixed Group, the magnitude of VAS decrease did not differ significantly across the three Modic subtypes and was comparable to prior studies of BVN ablation. Conducted independently and without industry funding, our study adds real-world evidence supporting the safety and effectiveness of BVN ablation in patients with vertebrogenic low back pain.
