GiGCN: a network-based framework for uncovering synthetic lethal and viable genetic interactions
Authors: Qizheng Wang, Fan Yang, Mengjie Fu, Yunyun Huo, Ju Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic interactions (GIs) underpin the functional connectivity of genes and pathways, and are important for dissecting genotype–phenotype relationships and identifying therapeutic targets for diseases. However, the scale of the human genome restricts systematic experimental interrogation of GIs. Existing computational tools focus on predicting synthetic lethality (SL) and synthetic viability (SV), the two primary forms of GIs, yet their accuracy and biological interpretability are compromised by inadequate modeling of the molecular mechanisms behind positive and negative interactions, as well as the limitation of negative samples. To overcome these challenges, we developed Genetic Interaction Graph Convolutional Network (GiGCN), a signed network modeling framework for the joint identification of gene pairs with SL and SV. We built a high-confidence signed genetic network by integrating verified GIs, and non-interacting gene pairs, together with gene semantic similarity derived from biological processes. By leveraging disentangled subspace decomposition, this framework separately models distinct functional dimensions within gene networks, enabling robust representation of context-dependent regulatory relationships and accurate discrimination of SL and SV events. Benchmark experiments demonstrate that GiGCN outperforms state-of-the-art approaches (area under receiver operating-characteristic curve: 0.978, and area under precision–recall curve: 0.944). Further analyses reveal biologically meaningful insights, including known and novel SL interactions centered on the oncogene MYC Proto-Oncogene (MYC), as well as SV interactions linked to autophagy and mitophagy pathways. This study provides a robust and interpretable network-based strategy for systematically exploring GIs. The GiGCN framework not only improves the precision of SL and SV prediction, but also offers mechanistic insights into gene functional relationships, thereby supporting the discovery of actionable therapeutic targets for cancer and other human diseases.

Introduction

The complexity of cellular systems is closely related to the extensive network of genetic interactions (GIs) among genes, rather than the independent actions of individual genes [1]. This interconnectivity confers robustness to the biological systems and enables them to maintain phenotypic stability, physiological function, and developmental fidelity in the presence of genetic perturbations such as mutations [2]. The molecular basis of robustness rests on two fundamental mechanisms: (i) the abundance of non-essential genes and functionally redundant paralogous gene families, which together form multi-layered genetic backup systems; and (ii) functional compensation mediated through dynamic regulatory networks that interconnect genes, signaling pathways, and biological processes, allowing the system to stabilize via compensatory responses when a single gene is inactivated [3]. However, this robustness can be disrupted when multiple genes are concurrently mutated, resulting in nonlinear, epistatic effects. In such cases, the phenotypic outcome may deviate markedly from the expected additive effect of individual mutations, often leading to double mutants exhibiting severe phenotypes [4]. Based on the consequences of fitness change, GIs are broadly categorized into two types: (a) positive interactions, where the combined mutation leads to higher-than-expected fitness; and (b) negative interactions, where the double mutant shows more severe fitness defects than anticipated [5].

Synthetic lethality (SL), an extreme form of negative GI, occurs when two individually viable mutations combine to cause cell death. Mechanistically, SL often arises from the concurrent disruption of genes within the same protein complex or at critical nodes of parallel survival-related pathways [6]. The clinical relevance of SL is exemplified by poly (adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, which exploit this principle to selectively target breast cancer gene (BRCA)-deficient cancers. PARP inhibitors represent the first clinically approved SL-based therapy, and their success underscores the translational potential of this strategy [7, 8]. In contrast, synthetic viability (SV) represents a complementary phenomenon in which a GI suppresses the deleterious effect of a primary mutation, effectively restoring viability through mechanisms such as functional compensation or metabolic reprogramming [9]. SV has dual implications in oncology: it may account for off-target effects of drugs like PARP inhibitors [10], while also offering a framework for understanding drug resistance, as activation of an SV partner gene often confers escape from therapeutic pressure [11, 12]. Notably, the principles underlying GIs extend beyond cancer biology and are increasingly informing therapeutic strategies for a range of Mendelian disorders [13].

GI detection technologies have undergone significant evolution, progressing from chemical mutagenesis screens and RNA interference (RNAi) to the current clustered regularly‑interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated gene 9 (CRISPR/Cas9)-based gene editing systems [14–16]. While Synthetic Genetic Array analysis has enabled the systematic mapping of genome-wide GIs in yeast [17], the sheer scale of the human genome, which comprises ~20 000 protein-coding genes [18] and potentially hundreds of millions of pairwise combinations, poses substantial challenges for conventional experimental methods. The inherently low-throughput and costly nature of wet-lab approaches has driven the need for computational models capable of predicting GIs at scale. In response, current research is increasingly turning to multi-scale predictive frameworks that integrate machine learning (ML) algorithms, topological network analysis, and systems biology models to address these limitations and enable the construction of comprehensive GI networks across the human genome [19].

With the rapid advancement of ML technologies, methods for SL prediction have evolved from classical ML algorithms to more sophisticated deep learning (DL) architectures. Conventional ML methods largely rely on decision tree-based classifiers [20, 21], support vector machines [22], and ensemble learning frameworks such as random forest [23–26]. These models establish supervised learning-based prediction systems by integrating multi-omics features, including gene expression profiles [27]. For instance, Wu et al. [28] developed a classifier based on the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm that improved predictive accuracy by incorporating seven biological similarity metrics derived from protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and gene ontology (GO) annotations. Nevertheless, a major limitation of such approaches is their heavy dependence on auxiliary genomic data, which restricts generalizability, especially considering that GIs represent distinct regulatory mechanisms that may operate independently of other molecular features. Another widely used methodology is matrix factorization (MF) [29–31], which projects high-dimensional and sparse SL matrices into lower-dimensional latent feature spaces. However, MF-based models are inherently linear and often fail to capture nonlinear relationships and other complex topological properties of gene interaction networks. Moreover, integrating multi-omics data within MF frameworks typically requires manually engineered features, limiting flexibility and expressive power. Beyond modeling limitations, many conventional SL prediction methods suffer from false negative contamination due to the use of random selection of negative samples [23, 32]. This practice incorrectly treats biologically valid but unconfirmed interactions as negative samples, introducing contradictory signals during model training and fundamentally constraining the discovery of novel GIs.

In recent years, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful approach for predicting SL, capitalizing on their innate ability to model complex biological networks. By representing genes as nodes and GIs as edges, GNNs efficiently harness topological information through message-passing mechanisms that aggregate features from neighboring nodes [3, 33, 34]. This architecture has proven highly effective not only in modeling PPI networks and Drug–Drug Interaction networks [35, 36], but also in diverse biological inference tasks such as non-coding RNA–protein interaction prediction [37], drug-target binding affinity estimation [38], and drug-target interaction identification [39]. To address the specific challenges in SL prediction, several GNN-based frameworks have been developed. The Dual-dropout Graph Convolutional Network model introduced a dual-dropout strategy to enhance training efficiency on sparse SL networks [40], and the Graph Contextualized Attention Network for Synthetic Lethality model integrated a graph attention network (GAT) to improve the detection of key biological pathways [41], while the Multiple Attention Graph Convolution Network for predicting SL model employed multi-graph aggregation to achieve similar gains [42]. The Knowledge Graph (KG) for Synthetic Lethality framework broke new ground by incorporating a KG composed of 11 entity types and 24 relation types, enabling integrated modeling of genomic, proteomic, and functional data within a unified GNN architecture. Particularly notable is the Multi-View Graph Convolutional Network model for the prediction of SL in individual cancer cells (MVGCN-iSL) model [43], which fuses five cell-type-specific biological networks (e.g. PPI, gene co-expression and genetic co-essentiality networks) within a multi-view graph convolutional framework to predict context-specific SL interactions with high precision. In a complementary approach, the Pairwise interaction learning-based GNN for SL prediction leveraged local subgraph structures from the SynLethKG database to refine prediction accuracy through nuanced pattern recognition of interaction contexts, significantly mitigating noise while maintaining a lightweight and efficient model design [32]. The Factor-aware Knowledge Graph Neural Network for Synthetic Lethality Prediction (SLGNN) model adopts a factor-aware knowledge GNN for SL prediction. It first defines SL-related factors from a gene KG, then employs factor-based message aggregation to learn gene embeddings. By explicitly modeling the biological processes that drive specific SL interactions, SLGNN provides enhanced interpretability compared to conventional GNNs [44].

Although GNN-based approaches have demonstrated considerable success in predicting SL interactions, they often neglect an important biological property: the inherent asymmetry between positive and negative GIs, which correspond to SV and SL respectively, within GI networks. Crucially, negative interactions reflect specific functional associations between genes, while positive interactions arising from partial loss-of-function alleles in essential genes represent a distinct form of regulatory relationship not readily captured by conventional omics data [17]. Empirical studies in yeast reveal that each gene participates, on average, in ~65 positive and 100 negative interactions, illustrating the pervasive and balanced nature of such bidirectional regulation [17]. This topological duality poses inherent limitations for models that treat SL in isolation. Moreover, positive and negative interactions do not operate independently; rather, they form complex, interdependent regulatory circuits through synergistic and antagonistic crosstalk. Traditional GNN methods, which often treat both interaction types as functionally equivalent, therefore rest on a biologically flawed assumption. In reality, negative interactions operate under distinct biological principles and arise from molecular mechanisms that fundamentally differ from those underlying positive interactions.

To address this limitation, we introduced signed graph neural networks (SGNNs) as a novel computational framework for modeling GIs. SGNNs are specifically designed to handle signed graph structures, enabling explicit representation of semantic relationships encoded in edge signs, such as activation/inhibition or synergy/antagonism in biological systems. Several pioneering applications demonstrate the strength of SGNNs in tackling biomedical problems. For instance, they have been used to predict circular RNA–microRNA (miRNA) interactions [45], gene–phenotype associations [46], and miRNA–disease associations [47]. These successful applications indicate the value of SGNNs in analyzing complex relationships in biomolecules.

Compared with conventional approaches, SGNNs have the advantage of being able to synchronously model differentiated information propagation mechanisms, which makes them a promising option for predicting GIs. As we know, a key feature of GI networks is their inherent bipolarity, i.e. they simultaneously encapsulate both positive (i.e. SV) and negative (i.e. SL) associations. This signed nature necessitates computational models capable of effectively processing structured networks with opposing edge types. Specifically, positive (facilitative) edges propagate signals through feature-enhancing pathways, while negative (inhibitory) edges activate feature-suppressing channels. Such a dual-path design has already been validated in biomolecular network analyses. These features highlight in particular their unique capability to simultaneously model both SL and SV interactions and to uncover the synergistic–antagonistic regulatory circuits in which they are embedded.

To address the multilayer topology and multimodal biological features inherent to GI networks, this study introduces a novel multi-factor disentangled GNN framework. The architecture employs latent factor separation to distill complex graph data into independent, interpretable functional modules, such as DNA repair pathways or cell cycle regulatory units, enabling fine-grained resolution of multidimensional node interactions. For example, gene A may synergize with gene B in factor Space 1 (e.g. DNA damage response) while exhibiting inhibitory effects in factor Space 2 (e.g. metabolic regulation). An adaptive attention mechanism empowers the model to accurately represent such context-dependent antagonistic relationships.

To ensure biological interpretability of the disentangled representations, the proposed Genetic Interaction Graph Convolutional Network (GiGCN) framework incorporates a factor discriminator module that operates under a self-supervised learning paradigm. By maximizing mutual information differences among distinct latent factors, it encourages each factor to align with a coherent biological functional domain, thereby enhancing both the precision and interpretability of modeling gene pleiotropy.

Materials and methods

Datasets of genetic interactions

First, we constructed a benchmark dataset by integrating data from two major GI databases, i.e. Cancer Genetic Interaction database (CGIdb) 2.0 (http://www.medsysbio.org/CGIdb2/) and Synthetic Lethality Database (SynLethDB) (https://synlethdb.sist.shanghaitech.edu.cn/.) CGIdb 2.0 is a comprehensive resource for GIs [48] and contains 33 465 experimentally validated gene interaction pairs, including 27 859 SL pairs and 5606 SV pairs. SynLethDB is an integrated database dedicated to SL research [16], containing 28 623 SL pairs derived from biochemical assays, literature curation, and cross-database integration, along with a large set of non-SL pairs used for negative sampling. After computationally predicted gene pairs were excluded, the GI pairs were initially filtered based on a predefined confidence score threshold. Available approaches for GI pair prediction typically adopt balanced training sets by pairing positive samples with equal-sized randomly selected negative samples [32, 49, 50]. While this strategy optimizes discriminative loss by treating randomly chosen non-associated edges as negative instances, it may introduce systemic error risks: random sampling may mislabel biologically existent yet unverified positive relationships (e.g. future GIs) as negative examples, causing models to learn contradictory signals from non-existent patterns. Although some studies attempted to mitigate false negative contamination using Generative Adversarial Networks to synthesize pseudo-negative samples aligned with positive sample distributions, these methods substantially increase architectural complexity, and distributional shifts in synthetic data may compromise model generalizability. To address this limitation, we established a high-confidence biologically validated negative dataset by integrating experimentally confirmed non-interacting pairs. This dataset strictly enforces biological reliability of negative labels, effectively eliminating false negative contamination. Such a prior distribution-aware data construction approach can not only enhance model robustness for non-SL relationships discrimination, but also strengthen cross-scenario generalization capacity through precise alignment with real-world biological network distributions.

Through rigorous cleaning steps, including standardization of gene symbols and removal of redundant entries, we established a high-confidence benchmark set consisting of 4918 SV pairs, 39 836 SL pairs, and 98 320 non-SL pairs, corresponding to 8887 unique human protein-coding genes.

To construct a biologically reliable negative sample set, we retrieved non-SL gene pairs from the SynLethDB 2.0 database [16] using the default confidence score thresholds defined by the database. Notably, the absence of SL does not imply the absence of other types of GIs, and SV itself constitutes a distinct positive class of interest in this study. Therefore, we further collected an independently verified set of SV gene pairs from the supplementary source CGIdb 2.0 [48]. To eliminate label contamination, any gene pair present in this independently collected SV set was removed from the original non-SL set. The resulting filtered non-SL set contains only gene pairs that are neither known SL nor known SV interactions. The excluded SV pairs were subsequently treated as a separate positive class for model training, following the same quality control criteria applied to SL pairs.

It is confirmed that genes involved in the same biological process often form tightly interconnected GI modules and demonstrate highly correlated interaction patterns [17]. To capture this functional relatedness, we constructed a biological process similarity matrix as a core feature for gene nodes. This matrix was computed based on the hs_bp dataset (comprising human Ontology Biological Process terms) using the GO Semantic Similarity measurement (GOSemSim) toolkit [51]. Semantic similarity between gene pairs was quantified via the Resnik algorithm combined with Bidirectional Maximal Averaging, resulting in an 8887 × 8887 semantic similarity matrix. Given the high dimensionality, density, and inherent linear correlations within this matrix, we applied principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction, compressing it into 128D feature vectors to ensure compatibility with DL architectures. Furthermore, based on findings from the GI network in yeast, where only 0.4% of direct GI pairs concurrently exhibit PPIs [17], we excluded PPI data in this study to ensure that our model focused specifically on capturing the distinct regulatory mechanisms underlying GIs, without confounding effects introduced by physical interaction data.

Overview of the model

The overall architecture of the GiGCN framework is shown in Fig. 1. The algorithm employs a signed disentangled GNN to tackle multi-relational link prediction tasks through a multiple-stage process that jointly encodes node features and graph topology. First, the model performs semantic disentanglement of node representations in a discrete latent space, producing interpretable embeddings for each node via independent message-passing pathways. Each pathway corresponds to a specific latent semantic factor designed to model distinct relational patterns. Next, a factor fusion module based on hierarchical aggregation and attention mechanisms is applied to systematically integrate multidimensional interactions between intrinsic node features and topological information from the neighborhood [52]. Finally, a multi-classification decoder is constructed to output predictive confidence scores for gene nodes across different relation categories through probabilistic modeling. Technical details for each module will be comprehensively elaborated in the subsequent sections [53].

Results

Performance evaluation of GiGCN

To validate the efficacy of the proposed GiGCN, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking against diverse baseline methods spanning traditional ML, GNNs, and link prediction models, including Nonnegative Matrix Factorization (NMF) [55], Singular‑Value Decomposition (SVD), KNN [56], Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Node‑to‑Vector (Node2Vec) [57], GCN [56], GAT [58], Graph Auto‑Encoder (GAE) [59], Variational Graph Auto‑Encoder (VGAE) [60], Approximate Personalized Propagation of Neural Predictions (APPNP), Graph SAmple and aggreGatE (GraphSAGE) [61], Topology Adaptive Graph Convolutional Network (TAGCN), Relational Graph Convolutional Network (RGCN) [62], Complex Embeddings (ComplEx) [63], Translating Embeddings (TransE), and SGNNs SGCN, Signed Graph Attention Networks (SGAT) [64], Signed Directed Graph Neural Network (SDGNN) [65], and Spike‑induced Graph Neural Network (SiGNN) [66]. All baselines were evaluated via five-fold cross-validation. To ensure task compatibility, non-native signed models were adapted for ternary classification (SL/SV/neutral). Biological process similarity scores served as unified node features across experiments, with key hyperparameters standardized: KNN used five neighbors; Node2Vec was configured with walk length = 20, window size = 10, and walks per node = 10; GNN architectures (GCN, GAT, GAE, VGAE, APPNP, GraphSAGE, TAGCN, RGCN, RNN, SGCN, SiGNN, and SDGNN) employed two convolutional layers with input/hidden dimensions of 256/64 (Supplementary Table S2). Signed GNNs implemented aggregation schemes per original designs—SGCN/SiGNN/SDGNN used mean aggregation (aggr = “mean”), while SGAT adopted additive aggregation (aggr = “add”) with multi-head attention. These results demonstrate that GNNs universally outperformed traditional ML/DL models, with signed GNNs exhibiting significant advantages over unsigned counterparts. GiGCN achieved state-of-the-art performance across all core metrics [area under receiver operating-characteristic curve (AUC): 0.978, area under precision–recall curve (AUPR): 0.944, F1_macro: 0.8837, and F1_micro: 0.9277] (Table 1).

Hyperparameter settings

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis revealed critical insights for model optimization. One important parameter in the model is the number of independent latent factors (K) set for node representation via orthogonal subspace decomposition. Evaluations demonstrated that optimal performance was achieved at K = 8 (AUC: 0.978, AUPR: 0.944), while deviations in either direction caused marginal degradation. This indicates that lower K values inadequately represent complex GI networks, whereas excessive factors may induce redundant feature expression that impedes effective information extraction. Critically, reducing K to 1 (equivalent to standard graph convolution without subspace decomposition) degraded AUC to 0.890 and AUPR to 0.612. Performance improved significantly at K = 2 and peaked at K = 8, confirming the cardinal role of factor multiplicity in capturing high-order GI features (Fig. 2). Convolutional depth ablation showed dual-layer architecture achieved peak performance without improvement at three layers, suggesting sufficient feature capture and parametric efficiency (Fig. 2). An embedding dimension of 64 was selected after experiments confirmed lower dimensions degraded results while 128D vectors offered negligible gains with heightened overfitting risk.

With GiGCN, the potential GIs between genes in human genome can be predicted based on their SL and SV scores.

In the SynLethDB database, SL gene interaction pairs in the human genome predicted by 10 different models for exploring SL gene pairs are curated, and the top 1% of SL gene pairs predicted by each algorithm were extracted (Supplementary Table S3). Then, the SL gene pairs predicted by GiGCN were compared against those obtained from SynLethDB (Fig. 6). Of the top 100 gene pairs ranked by the SL score of GiGCN, 91 could be matched to SynLethDB and 9 were uniquely predicted. Of the top 1000 SL gene pairs, 732 could be matched to SynLethDB and 268 were uniquely predicted (Supplementary Table S4). For the novel SL pairs predicted by GiGCN, some have been validated by experiments directly or indirectly. For example, MYC–SUMO1‑activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) was a SL pair predicted by GiGCN that was not included in the training datasets and also not predicted by other methods. Previously, Kessler et al. found that the loss of enzymatic activity of small ubiquitin‑like modifier (SUMO)-activating enzyme (SAE1/2) drives SL with MYC by a genome-wide RNAi screen to search for MYC-synthetic lethal genes [67]. Also, they found that low SAE1 and SAE2 abundance in tumors correlated with longer metastasis-free survival of patients, indicating the crucial role of SAE gene expression in MYC mediated oncogenesis. Then, Amente et al. indicated that MYC activates SAE1 transcription via direct binding to canonical E-box sequences located close to the SAE1 transcription start site, suggesting that MYC oncogenic activity which depends on SAE1 is ensured by MYC itself through direct binding and transcriptional activation of SAE1 expression [68]. Another example is the MYC–Ubiquitin‑protein ligase E3B (UBE3B) gene pair. Li et al. found that the tribbles pseudokinase 3 (TRIB3) is a crucial positive regulator of MYC protein stability in lymphoma by directly interacting with MYC to block its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation mediated by the E3 ligase UBE3B, and such a TRIB3–UBE3B–MYC axis can promote MYC-driven lymphomagenesis [30].

As an oncogenic transcription factor that modulates the expression of a large number of targets, the dysregulation of MYC is frequently detected in human cancers. Our results showed that MYC emerged prominently within the top-ranked SL pairs, underscoring its pivotal role in SL networks. As a master regulator of the cell cycle, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, identifying MYC’s synthetic lethal partners holds significant therapeutic implications for targeted cancer therapy. Key GiGCN-predicted interactions experimentally validated with their molecular mechanisms are summarized as follows:

The GiGCN model accurately predicted the MYC–tripartite motif‑containing protein 3 (TRIM3) SL interaction, experimentally confirmed through The Cancer Genome Atlas analysis demonstrating TRIM3’s regulation of glioblastoma (GBM) stemness and asymmetric cell division via suppression of MYC expression and activity. Western blot validation showed that TRIM3 overexpression significantly reduced MYC protein levels, while TRIM3 deficiency exhibited a strong negative correlation with MYC overexpression in GBM (Pearson correlation analysis). Similarly validated was the MYC–Serpin family B member 2 (SERPINB2) SL interaction, where SERPINB2 stabilized MYC protein by blocking E7-mediated degradation through Rb-dependent inhibition of human papillomavirus (HPV) oncogene transcription, counteracting E6/E7-mediated degradation of tumor suppressors (p53, Rb) and oncoproteins (MYC, c-Jun) [69, 70]. For MYC–N-Myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (NDRG1), context-dependent mechanisms were observed. For example, in lung cancer, NDRG1 stabilizes MYC by binding Skp2 (E3 ubiquitin ligase) to inhibit MYC ubiquitination; conversely, in prostate and colon cancers, NDRG1 suppresses MYC activity by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling and enhancing E-cadherin/β-catenin membrane localization [71]. The MYC–suppressor of variegation 3-9 homolog 2 (SUV39H2) interaction was confirmed via chromatin immunoprecipitation, luciferase activity assays, and DNA pull-down experiments, revealing that the histone methyltransferase SUV39H2 can upregulate MYC transcription by increasing histone H3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3), promoting heterochromatin protein 1‑alpha (HP1α) binding to the MYC promoter, and recruiting transcription factors (P300, RNA Pol II) to form transcriptional complexes. The essential role of ubiquitin‑conjugating enzyme E2 Z (UBE2Z) in Squamocin-induced MYC ubiquitination and degradation was demonstrated through gene expression regulation and loss-of-function experiments [72, 73], while ubiquitin Conjugating Enzyme E2 E2 (UBE2E2) downregulation promoted hepatocellular carcinoma progression by reducing ubiquitin-mediated degradation of phosphorylated MYC, leading to accumulated MYC activating cell proliferation pathways (e.g. p21 inhibition) and impairing DNA damage response, as validated through immunohistochemistry and mechanistic analyses. Crucially, GiGCN exclusively identified the novel MYC–SAE1 interaction, where MYC directly activates SAE1 transcription by binding classic E-boxes near its transcription start site, establishing SAE1’s essential role in MYC-mediated oncogenesis through direct transcriptional activation [68]. Additionally, the recurrent SL partner F‑box and WD repeat domain containing 7 (FBXW7), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, suppresses tumorigenesis by targeting oncoproteins, including MYC, Cyclin E, Notch, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) for ubiquitin-mediated degradation.

On the other hand, high-frequency occurrence of autophagy genes (ATG, WDR45, WDFY3, TRIM5, and TMEM74) was observed among top-scoring SV pairs, where experimental validation confirms their compensatory roles in maintaining proteostasis. In neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s, genetic mutations drive abnormal protein accumulation; where, autophagic activation rescues proteolytic system deficiencies by degrading pathological aggregates, thereby achieving synthetic rescue. Crucially, PTEN‑induced kinase 1 (PINK1)–autophagy related 3 (ATG3) and PINK1–ATG5 interactions exhibited SV scores of 0.9961 and 0.9951, respectively, compensating for mitophagy defects caused by PINK1-Parkin mutations through non-canonical autophagic pathway activation. Mechanistically, the PINK1-Parkin pathway promotes NDP52 SUMOylation to recruit autophagy-related proteins including ATG3 and ATG5, driving damaged mitochondrial clearance, an essential process for cellular homeostasis [74]. Pharmacological intervention using mTOR inhibitor AZD8055 restored ATG3 expression while significantly elevating PINK1 mRNA levels [75], suggesting that mTOR hyperactivation concomitantly suppresses autophagy initiation (ATG3) and mitophagy activation (PINK1). Further supporting this mechanism, the PINK1–Cullin‑4A (CUL4A) interaction (SV score: 0.9950) demonstrated that CUL4A-mediated suppression of PINK1 expression and mitophagy sustains cancer cell survival and chemotherapy resistance, whereas CUL4A knockout reactivates the PINK1-Parkin pathway to induce tumor-suppressive mitochondrial clearance [76]. These results establish autophagy potentiation as a fundamental SV mechanism across neurodegeneration and oncology contexts, validated through orthogonal experimental approaches. For some other SV pairs, such as cyclin‑dependent kinase inhibitor 2 (ACDKN2A)–CDKN2B encode the CDK4/6 inhibitors inhibitor of cyclin‑dependent kinase 4A (INK4A)/alternative reading frame (ARF) and INK4B, F‑box‑only protein 25 (FBXO25)–FBXO32 encodes SKP1‑CUL1‑F‑box protein complex (SCF)-type E3 ligases, although little is known about their functions or substrates, previous studies implicate them as tumor suppressors [77]. We further quantified the contribution of each latent factor in the model to the prediction of SL and SV (Supplementary Material S1 and Supplementary Tables S6–S8).

Discussion and conclusion

The molecular mechanisms underlying SL and SV are both anchored within specific biological functional modules yet operate via fundamentally opposing pathways. SL originates from the concomitant inactivation of components within the same protein complex or key regulatory points in parallel survival pathways [6], whereas SV reverses pathological phenotypes through functional compensation or metabolic pathway reprogramming [78]. A unifying aspect of these opposing mechanisms lies in their shared foundation in core biological processes: genome-wide GI mapping in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [17] confirmed that genes participating in the same biological process (e.g. DNA damage repair or proteasome regulation) not only form tightly interconnected GI modules but also exhibit highly similar interaction patterns if they function cooperatively within the same pathway or complex. Based on this finding, we constructed a biological process similarity matrix as the core feature for gene nodes.

The theory of structural balance has been employed to analyze gene–gene interactions [79]. This framework assesses system stability by examining the multiplicative properties of signed edge weights that represent positive or negative relations, and serves as the foundation for SGCNs designed to process signed-edge relationships [80]. However, the applicability of structural balance theory within biomedical network research remains debated. Although its application in the PPI network analysis has been validated, large-scale analysis of GI networks, such as those based on genome-wide data from S. cerevisiae [17], reveals that ˃20% of triadic structures exhibit strongly unbalanced characteristics despite statistically significant balance trends at the network level [79]. More critically, signed balance theory captures only statistical patterns in sign distribution (e.g. a significantly higher degree of balance than random networks) without accounting for the mechanistic biological differences between positive and negative interactions. For instance, positive interactions may arise from protein cooperativity, whereas negative interactions could result from inhibitory regulation or metabolic competition. Consequently, although structural balance theory explains certain statistical tendencies in sign distribution, its failure to incorporate mechanistic distinctions between SV/SL interactions limits its biological applicability [79].

Furthermore, we also compared the performance of GiGCN with widely used methods such as SLGNN. Built upon KGs and the factor composition assumption, SLGNN models SL-related factors as weighted sums of diverse relations within KGs, conducts factor-level message aggregation via GATs, and thus constitutes a valuable exploration in the field of model interpretability research, while in comparison, GiGCN is capable of predicting more categories of GIs, including both SL and SV, as it integrates SGNNs equipped with disentangled subspace decomposition, adopts highly reliable experimentally verified non-interacting gene pairs as negative samples, and captures the asymmetry of GIs via differential propagation mechanisms without relying on structural balance theory, and distinct differences exist between the two methods in feature construction and network modeling strategies—SLGNN enhances factor-level interpretability by exploiting abundant semantic information of KGs, while GiGCN broadens the prediction scope by modeling signed and multi-type GIs—so in summary, these two methods jointly promote the mechanistic modeling research of GI prediction from complementary perspectives.

This study introduces GiGCN, a SGNN framework for predicting asymmetric GIs, specifically SL and SV. GiGCN operates without presuming balance theory axioms, enhancing adaptability to complex biomedical contexts. The aggregation mechanism design operates independently of a priori constraints based on sign-product relationships. This theory-agnostic architecture substantially enhances the model’s generalization capacity and mechanistic interpretability within complex biomedical networks.

Based on a benchmark dataset encompassing 39 836 SL pairs, 4918 SV pairs, and 98 320 high-confidence, non-interacting gene pairs and integrating biological process similarity features with signed topological relationships, GiGCN systematically captures complex GI patterns. Experimental evidence confirms that the dynamic equilibrium of positive (65 per gene) and negative (100 per gene) interactions in yeast genetic networks intrinsically reflects homeostasis maintained through antagonistic-synergistic regulation [17]. GiGCN precisely models this biological property via dual-pathway message passing: positive edges enhance feature propagation while negative edges suppress feature fusion. Key innovations include: (1) introducing SGNNs to handle differential propagation mechanisms for signed edges, overcoming limitations of conventional GNNs that treat all relationships equivalently; (2) designing a multi-factor, disentangled encoding module that decomposes gene functions into independent subspaces to represent complex cross-module interactions; and (3) employing a biologically validated negative sample set to eliminate false negative contamination from random sampling. Results demonstrate GiGCN’s superiority over traditional ML, classical GNNs, and existing signed graph models in SL/SV prediction (AUC: 0.978, AUPR: 0.944), validating the framework’s efficacy in modeling positive interactions. Performance significantly improved when decomposing graphs into K subgraphs, resolving pleiotropy-induced representation entanglement and confirming that GIs constitute dynamic cross-functional regulatory processes rather than linear single-pathway associations.

Case studies validate precise identification of MYC-centric SL pairs (e.g. MYC–TRIM3, MYC–SAE1) and autophagy-mediated SV pairs (e.g. PINK1–ATG3, PINK1–CUL4A), revealing translational potential in oncology (e.g. targeting MYC-driven cancers) and neurodegeneration (e.g. compensating autophagy defects). While SV research remains limited, its role as a key driver of tumor drug resistance warrants prioritization. Current limitations include sparse experimentally verified interactions (<0.1% of human gene pairs), restricting rare disease gene prediction. Though MVGCN-iSL confirmed cell-specific networks’ importance [43], GiGCN does not yet integrate single-cell transcriptomics. Future work will optimize dynamic microenvironment interaction prediction by constructing cell-type-specific signed graphs.
