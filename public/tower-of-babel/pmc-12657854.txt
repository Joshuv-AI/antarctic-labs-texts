Pelvic floor muscle exercise practice and its determinants among postpartum women in Central Ethiopia: as a strategy for preventing pelvic floor disorders
Authors: Mebratu Demissie Senbeta, Aberash Beyene Derribow, Aynalem Belay, Ayana Benti Terefe, Mangistu Abera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPelvic floor disorders are highly prevalent among reproductive-age women in sub-Saharan Africa and commonly lead to urinary incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse, bowel dysfunction, sexual problems, and reduced quality of life. Pelvic floor muscle exercise, defined as repeated contraction and relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles, is an effective preventive and non-surgical treatment of pelvic floor disorders. However, despite the high burden of pelvic floor disorders in Ethiopia, the significance and role of pelvic floor muscle exercise have not yet been studied in a study setting. This study aimed to assess the Pelvic Floor Muscle Exercise Practice and Its Determinants among Postpartum Women in Gurage Zone, Central Ethiopia. A community-based cross-sectional study was employed from May 12 to June 12, 2023. A 422 postpartum women were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. A pre-tested and structured questionnaire was used for data collection. Data were entered into Epidata 3.1 and exported into SPSS version 26 software for analysis. Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regressions were performed. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval were considered statistically significant. In this study,420 study participants were included with a response rate of 99.5%. The proportion of women practicing pelvic floor exercises was 12.14% (95% CI: 6.3, 18.7). Higher education (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.27, 4.31), ANC visits (AOR = 4.31;95%CI:1.36, 9.57), women with urinary incontinence (AOR = 6.47;95%CI:3.96, 11.54), and women’s knowledge (AOR = 6.31;95%CI:3.59, 12.23) were determinants of pelvic floor muscle exercise practice. The present study showed that 87.86% of postpartum mothers lacked proper pelvic floor muscle exercise practices. Thus, encouraging women to attend ANC visits as recommended, providing awareness through health education, and offering counseling on pelvic floor muscle exercises can increase their practice and help reduce pelvic floor disorders.Keywords: Pelvic floor muscle exercise, Postpartum women, Determinants, EthiopiaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors, Urology

Introduction

Pelvic floor muscle exercise (PFME) is an exercise that strengthens pelvic floor muscles to prevent pelvic organ prolapse (POP), sexual dysfunction, urinary incontinence (UI), and fecal incontinence (FI)1. The pelvic floor is made up of Ilio Iliococcygeus, pubococcygeus, and puborectalis muscles that play crucial roles in urinary continence, micturition, anal continence, defecation cycles, and maintaining healthy sexuality. The occurrence of pelvic floor disorders can be explained by successive exposures to obstetric trauma to the perineum, lifestyle, and aging-related factors1–5. Pelvic floor disorders involve dysfunction of pelvic muscles and significantly impair women’s quality of life6.

According to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), physical activity and exercise during pregnancy and immediately after birth promote physical well-being, help prevent excessive weight gain, and reduce the risk of gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and cesarean birth7. Evidence from developed nations shows that postpartum pelvic floor muscle exercises reduce urinary incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse, and inter-rectus distance, all of which are associated with poor quality of life7–9. In addition, pelvic floor muscle exercises during the first year postpartum have been shown to reduce urinary incontinence by 37% and pelvic organ prolapse by 56%10.

Globally, pelvic floor disorders affect many women during late pregnancy and the postpartum period. The prevalence of UI in all nations around the world ranges from 23% to 44%6. Across the countries, studies report varying prevalence and outcomes. In the United States, 23.7% of women had at least one type of pelvic floor disorder (15.7% of urinary incontinence, 9.0% fecal incontinence, and 2.9% of pelvic organ prolapse), and in Sweden, 8.3% of women developed symptomatic POP11,12. Furthermore, the findings from Asian countries revealed that pelvic floor exercises are effective in improving body image, encouraging adherence to physical exercise, and promoting pelvic floor muscle health13–15.

The evidence from African countries showed that women’s awareness and practice of pelvic floor muscle exercises were low, leading to postpartum morbidities and poor quality of life16–18. The proportion of practicing postnatal exercise in low and middle-income countries ranged from 5.6% to 48.7%18,19.

Nationally, Ethiopia faces a significant burden of pelvic floor disorders. The prevalence of POP in Northwest Ethiopia was 46.7%20, Amhara regional state 6%21, and Central Ethiopia 41.1% of pelvic floor disorders (UI (32.8%), followed by POP (25.5%) and FI (4.2%)22. The evidence revealed that the magnitudes of postnatal exercise practice were 5.6% in Northern Ethiopia and 8.2% in the Oromia region, respectively18,23.

Pelvic floor muscle exercise is important in the treatment of pelvic floor disorders and helps in the restoration of perineal muscles24. The aim of performing PFME is to produce a strong, fast, precise contraction that improves bladder control and strengthens pelvic organ supporting structures5,24. Little is known about the extent to which women in low and middle-income countries suffer from urological and urogynaecological complications of childbirth25.

Pelvic floor disorders affect many postpartum women in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area, little is known about postpartum women’s knowledge or practice regarding pelvic floor muscle exercises. This study seeks to fill the gap, aiming to provide insights that can help women protect their pelvic health and improve their quality of life. Therefore, in Ethiopia, the gaps in knowledge and practice of pelvic floor muscle exercise remain critical. This study is based on the indicated gaps and limited evidence in the country in identifying women’s knowledge of pelvic floor muscle exercise and the lack of practice for pelvic floor disorders prevention, which contribute to maternal morbidity and reduce quality of life. The outcome of our findings will provide circumstantial evidence to guide maternal health education, strengthen pelvic floor exercise strategies, and inform policymakers in designing interventions that reduce maternal morbidity in resource-constrained countries like Ethiopia. Hence, this study aimed to assess the pelvic floor muscle exercise practice and its determinants among postpartum women in Gurage Zone, Central Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the Gurage Zone, Central Ethiopia, from May 12 to June 12, 2023. The Gurage Zone is located 155 km south of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. According to the 2017 Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency population projection, the total population of the Gurage Zone was 1,635,311; of 842,065 were females and the remaining 793,246 were males26.

Population

All women who gave birth in the least one year were the source population. All systematically selected women who gave birth in the last year were taken as the study population. Inclusion criteria: All women who gave birth in the last year and volunteered during data collection were included in the study. Exclusion criteria: Women who were unable to communicate and did not volunteer to participate were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedures

Wolkite town has 11 Kebeles, from which 4 were selected by using simple random sampling techniques. The list of women who gave birth in the last year was obtained from delivery registrations of Wolkite health facilities, and cross-checked with the community health information system by using health extension workers, which was 927. Then the sample was allocated proportionally to each Kebeles (Edget ber 112, Edget chora 100, Selam-ber 109, and Addis ketema 101). A systematic random sampling technique was used to select study participants. The sampling interval (K) was calculated by K = N/n, where N = the total average number of postpartum women, and n = the actual sample size, which yields two. The skipping interval K value was determined for each Kebeles. Then, every second mother was interviewed with a similar K value of two. The first study participant was selected by using a random sampling procedure (the lottery method) between the first two postpartum women on the first day of data collection. Then, every second mother was enrolled in the study.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Pelvic floor muscles exercise practice.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic factors (Age, Residence, Religion, Ethnicity, Marital status, Educational Status, Occupation, Family size, Monthly income). Obstetric-related factors (gravidity, parity, ANC visits, number of alive children, birth interval, mode of delivery, and duration of labor, Urinary incontinence, and women’s knowledge regarding pelvic floor muscle exercise.

Operational definitions

The pelvic floor

An anatomical function that is made up of a triple system of suspensory ligaments, cohesive fascia, and muscle support, which includes the iliococcygeus muscle, pubococcygeus muscle, and puborectalis muscle27.

Postpartum exercise practice is defined as engaging in any type of postpartum physical activity during the current postpartum period, as recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists7.

Pelvic floor muscle exercise

Hold up/ contract the pelvic floor muscles for approximately 6 to 8 s, followed by relaxation, three times per day with 8 to 12 repetitions per session, and continue for at least 15 to 20 weeks. To do this, imagine sitting on a marble and tighten up the pelvic muscles to lift that imaginary marble off the chair in a sitting or lying position. During the exercises, the flow of urine should not be interrupted, because it may affect normal urination5,24.

Good practice

Those postpartum women who performed pelvic floor exercise in frequency at least 3 times per day and lasted for 6 to 8 s per set of exercises. Whereas, those who performed pelvic floor exercise in frequency less than 3 times a day and lasted for 6 to 8 s per each set of exercises as poor practice7,23,28.

Knowledge of pelvic floor muscle exercises

Postpartum mothers who scored at or above the mean on knowledge questions were considered knowledgeable about pelvic floor muscle exercises. Those who scored below the mean were considered not knowledgeable regarding pelvic floor muscle exercises19.

Data collection tools

The data collection tool was adapted by reviewing related literature and modified according to the local situation of the study area. The questionnaire was prepared in English and then translated into the local language. Postpartum mothers’ sociodemographic information, obstetrics-related factors, knowledge of pelvic floor exercise-related questions, and pelvic floor exercise practice-related questions were included in the tool. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews at the home level. Five BSc midwives were trained for data collection, and two BSc midwives were assigned as supervisors to check for the daily activity, consistency, and completeness of the questionnaire, and to give appropriate support during the data collection process.

The knowledge-related questionnaire was assessed using 12 items, addressing sources of information about PFME, definitions of PFME, frequency, length, and duration of PFME, how to perform PFME, requirements for performing PFME, and the benefits of PFME. The question items needed categorical responses (yes or no), with 1 point awarded for each correct answer and 0 for each incorrect answer. The overall knowledge score for each participant was calculated by summing the results from 12 questions, with a possible range of 0 to 12.

The practice of pelvic floor muscle exercises was assessed based on whether postpartum women performed the exercise or not. Women who performed pelvic floor muscle exercises (practiced) were coded as “1”, while those who did not perform pelvic floor muscle exercises (not practiced) were coded as “0”. Among those who practiced PFME further categorized as good and poor practiced based on the frequency per day and duration of contraction. Additionally, the total duration of practice, the time of initiation, and the presence of any pain or discomfort experienced during performance were considered.

This research utilized a standardised questionnaire that was previously used by different scholars, which underwent a pre-test to ensure clarity, appropriateness, and reliability. The tool’s content validity was checked by a panel of experts for relevance and essentiality, and its content reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, resulting in a 0.85 Cronbach’s alpha value for postpartum women’s knowledge-related questionnaires and a 0.91 Cronbach’s alpha value for pelvic floor exercise practice-related questionnaires.

Data quality assurance

The tool was pre-tested on 21 postpartum mothers at Abshege kebele before the actual data collection to ensure consistency, clarity, and completeness of the tools. Data collectors and the supervisor received two days of training on the study’s objectives, questionnaire clarification, sampling strategy, and data collection process and supervision.

The principal investigator and supervisor guided, facilitated, and checked data completeness, ensuring the overall activities were effectively managed throughout the data collection process. Data was coded, entered, and rechecked during data entry into the computer software before analysis. Simple frequencies and a box plot were used to look for missing values and outliers, respectively.

Data processing and analysis

After the data were thoroughly checked, coded, and carefully entered into EpiData 3.1 statistical packages, and analyzed using SPSS version 26 software. Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regressions were employed to identify the relationships between the dependent and the independent variables, and variables with a P-value less than 0.25 at a 95% confidence interval were taken as candidates for a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis.

After multivariable binary logistic regressions were done, the association between the dependent variable and independent variables was determined by using odds ratios, 95%CI, and variables with a P-value < 0.05 were considered significant associations. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify variables in the final model. Model fitness was confirmed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow Goodness-of-Fit test, with a p-value of 0.615 for pelvic floor exercise practice. The omnibus test of model coefficients yielded a p-value of < 0.0001 for the outcome variable, confirming overall model significance. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance tests. The maximum VIF value was 2.73, and the minimum tolerance value was 37.1% for pelvic floor muscle exercise practice, indicating no multicollinearity issues. Finally, the findings were presented in narratives, tables, and figures.

The overall women’s knowledge regarding pelvic floor muscle exercise

Among 420 interviewed respondents, more than one-fifth 91 (21.6%) knew what mean by pelvic floor muscle exercise, out of which, 39 (42.9%) correctly answered the recommended number of pelvic floor muscle exercise per day, nearly one-half 45 (49.5%) answered the length of seconds that pelvic muscle to be tighten up/contracted, and more than one-half 47 (51.6%) correctly answered for how long period of time exercise should be performed. Therefore, in this study, only 21.6% were knowledgeable regarding pelvic floor exercises.

The magnitude of pelvic floor muscle exercise practice

The overall proportion of women practicing pelvic floor exercises was 12.14% (95% CI: 6.3, 18.7) (Fig. 1). Among those women, 24 (47.1%) practiced the exercise for four months, while 7 (13.7%) complained of pain or discomfort during the first week after childbirth. Of those who practiced pelvic floor muscle exercise, 60.7% were considered to have poor practice.

Discussion

Pelvic floor muscle exercise is exercises that strengthen pelvic floor muscles to prevent or reduce pelvic floor disorders1. The prevalence of pelvic organ prolapse in Northwest Ethiopia was 46.7%20, 37.6% at Amhara regional state21, and 41.1% of pelvic floor disorders at Central Ethiopia, out of which 25.5% was POP22. Despite this problem, the pelvic floor muscle exercise practice among women in the present study was low. This might be due to limited awareness, inadequate counseling, cultural barriers, lack of structured postpartum exercise programs, and high maternal workload, which reduce the performance of pelvic floor muscle exercises and contribute to pelvic floor disorders and poor quality of life.

In this study, the proportion of women practicing pelvic floor exercises among study participants in Central Ethiopia was 12.14% (95% CI: 6.3, 18.7). This study finding was higher compared with a study done in Kathmandu, Nepal, which revealed that none of the women performed the pelvic floor muscle exercise29, a study in Malaysia showed that only 3.51% performed pelvic floor muscle exercise6, and a study conducted in Amhara and Oromia region of Ethiopia 5.6% and 8.2% respectively performed pelvic floor muscle exercise18,23. This might be possibly due to the existence of well-trained health extension workers on pelvic floor disorders and their prevention by Wolkite University through a community service project in the present study area. Another possible explanation may be due to the discrepancy in the study period and socio-demographic characteristics of the participants, as women in this investigation probably have better awareness and health information than women in Nepal, Malaysia, and various regions of Ethiopia. Moreover, the differences in exercise acceptance between cultures and how women view pelvic floor muscle exercise during pregnancy and after childbirth could explain the observed variations.

This study finding was lower than a study conducted in Al Madinah Al Munawarah, Saudi Arabia revealed that 42.8% of women performed pelvic floor muscle exercise30, Medical University of Bialystok, Poland, 18.5% performed PFME31, Selangor, Malaysia, 45.2% practiced pelvic floor muscle exercise32, and Enugu Metropolis, Nigeria 38.4% practiced pelvic floor muscle excersice28. This difference might be due to differences in study setup, cultural differences, socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants, and the health care system-related variation.

In the present study, 13.7% of women complained of pain/discomfort while performing pelvic floor muscle exercise in the first week of giving birth; similarly, 73% of women experience pain in Belgium33. This might be since the presence of episiotomy, compression of the pudendal and sacral nerve during the process of giving birth causes pain, especially in the first week till it heals and regains. The present study revealed that factors associated with pelvic floor muscle exercise among study participants were formal education, ANC visits, having urinary incontinence, and knowledge of pelvic floor muscle exercise.

In the present study, the women who had a formal education were 1.40 times more likely to perform pelvic floor muscle exercise as compared with those who had no formal education. This finding was similar to a study conducted in Saudi Arabia and the Oromia region of Ethiopia15,23. This might be due to the fact that the higher the educational status, the more women are likely to seek health care and gather information about potential risks for better health outcomes. Women who had ANC visits of four or more were 4.31 times more likely to perform pelvic floor muscle exercise as compared to those who had fewer than four visits. This might be because those women who have more antenatal care visits will gain more information about pelvic floor muscle exercises from health care providers, which enhances their awareness regarding pelvic floor muscle exercises.

In the present study, women who had urinary incontinence had 6.47 times higher odds of performing pelvic floor muscle exercise as compared to their counterparts. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Sweden, Canada, and China10,14,34. This might possibly be because women with urinary incontinence seek health care more frequently for prevention and management, where they are counseled by health care personnel to perform pelvic floor muscle exercises as part of the treatment. Furthermore, Women who knew pelvic floor muscle exercises were 6.31 times more likely to perform pelvic floor exercises as compared to not knowledgeable. This is supported by studies conducted in Saudi Arabia30, Poland31, and Malaysia32. This might be the fact that, as the knowledge level increases, the practice level also increases. The knowledge has a positive influence on the practical performance30.

Implications for maternal health education and policy in Ethiopia

The low level of pelvic floor muscle exercise practice shows a missed opportunity in maternal and child health programs. The healthcare system needs to improve health education during antenatal and postnatal care because this will help women understand how PFME protects against urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse while supporting postpartum recovery.

The healthcare system needs to include structured routine pelvic floor muscle exercise counseling in its standard maternal health care services, which should be considered as nutritional counseling, family planning, and breastfeeding support to achieve better maternal health outcomes. The study findings show that health authorities or policymakers are encouraged to strengthen national standards and training resources for midwives, nurses, and other maternal health professionals to teach pelvic floor muscle exercises properly.

The community-level awareness initiatives should strengthen the health extension workers or community health workers towards health education regarding the benefits of PFME by reducing the cultural barriers while creating acceptance for pelvic floor health. The incorporation of PFME into maternal health policies and practices will help women enhance their quality of life and prevent future reproductive health morbidities and complications.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strength of the study

The strength of this study includes various categories of women, and the sample was taken at random. Hence, the results of this study can represent the population. This study minimised potential biases by using clear objectives, pretested questionnaires, training provided for data collectors and supervisors, a random sampling method, an ideal sample size, and statistical adjustments (multivariable regression) to account for confounding variables. Finally, ethical guidelines were implemented to ensure unbiased participants.

Limitations of the study

This study was based on self-reported survey data, which may be influenced by recall bias and potential bias from social desirability since women may report more acceptable responses. Since a cross-sectional design was used, the cause-and-effect relationship could not be established.

Conclusion

In this study, the proportion of practicing pelvic floor muscle exercise among women in Central Ethiopia was low. The present study showed that 87.86% of postpartum mothers lacked proper pelvic floor muscle exercise practices. The educational status, ANC visits, having urinary incontinence, and knowledge of pelvic floor muscle exercise were significantly associated with pelvic floor muscle exercise practice. Hence, the present study concludes that by intervening in modifiable factors, postpartum mothers’ awareness regarding pelvic floor muscle exercise can be increased.

Recommendations

The following recommendations were given based on the findings to the concerned bodies:

Health care providers: Early identification of women with urinary incontinence, encouraging them to perform pelvic floor muscle exercise, strengthening health education, and counseling on pelvic floor muscle exercise to improve awareness and practice of women towards pelvic floor muscle exercise. Moreover, it reduces the potential risk of pelvic floor disorders and increases the quality of women’s lives.

Health care authorities: Policymakers should prioritize the development of policies that promote pelvic floor muscle exercise as a preventive measure against pelvic organ prolapse, thereby improving women’s quality of life and reducing related morbidities. In addition, strengthening antenatal care (ANC) coverage will ensure that women receive appropriate information and counseling on pelvic floor muscle exercise based on the WHO antenatal care recommendation.

Community health workers: Community health workers/ Health extension workers are encouraged to be equipped with the knowledge and skills to educate women on the importance of pelvic floor muscle exercise as a preventive strategy against pelvic organ prolapse and urinary incontinence. By integrating PFME counseling into routine community-based services, they can enhance the awareness, regular exercise practice that leads to ultimately improving women’s quality of life and reducing future morbidities. Strengthening their role within the continuum of maternal health care will also ensure that essential information on PFME is consistently delivered at the community level.

For researchers: Further interventional studies need to be done to identify further factors and a better understanding of women’s practice of pelvic floor muscle exercises in Ethiopia.
