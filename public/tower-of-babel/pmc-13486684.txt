Research on detection and defense methods of backdoor attacks on quantum neural networks
Authors: Yulu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Security research in quantum neural networks (QNNs) is an emerging field that aims to utilize the principles of quantum mechanics to protect the security of neural networks, and this protection can be against different threats such as eavesdropping, tampering, and malware attacks. Backdoor attacks are a serious security challenge in the field of deep neural networks. This type of attack is highly stealthy and harmful, which can silently implant malicious functions and trigger them under specific conditions, thus disrupting the normal operation of neural networks or leaking sensitive information. To address the potential security risks of backdoor attacks to QNNs, this paper focuses on two core issues: backdoor attack detection and defense strategy design. It proposes an activation-based clustering method for backdoor sample detection (ACDM) to counter backdoor attack threats. Experimental results demonstrate that this defense method effectively safeguards the operational security of QNNs while enhancing the model’s robustness against abnormal inputs and the reliability of its output results. It should be noted that the current research has limitations: the adaptability and effectiveness of this defense framework on complex high-dimensional datasets (e.g., quantum image datasets) and QNNs with different topological structures (e.g., deep quantum convolutional networks) require further validation and optimization through subsequent experiments.

Introduction

With the continuous development of deep learning models, machine learning has achieved remarkable breakthroughs across diverse domains, including computer vision1,2, speech recognition, artificial intelligence, and cybersecurity3, etc. Meanwhile, the field of quantum computing is reshaping the traditional framework of computational theory and practice in an unprecedented way4. In this context, the trend of convergence between machine learning and quantum computing has inspired researchers to explore how to utilize the unique properties of quantum mechanics, such as superposition states and entanglement phenomena, to strengthen and enhance the performance and efficiency of classical machine learning algorithms5,6.

In the era of Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) computers7, variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)8 have emerged as a promising approach for harnessing quantum advantages in real-world applications. Their resilience to noise and adaptability to NISQ hardware make them particularly well-suited for addressing complex computational challenges. Specifically, variational quantum circuits (VQCs), often referred to as quantum neural networks, leverage a series of tunable parameters optimized iteratively during training, enabling them to model intricate patterns in data. The practice has shown that VQCs exhibit significant progress and potential in supervised learning tasks, where VQCs have shown potential for outperforming classical models9,10, unsupervised learning tasks, including clustering and generative modeling11,12, and quantum-specific learning tasks that exploit quantum entanglement and superposition for enhanced computational efficiency13.

Notwithstanding significant advancements in the field, recent studies14–21 have revealed that variational quantum algorithm-based QNNs, analogous to classical deep learning models, suffer from inherent robustness limitations against adversarial attacks22. Specifically, attackers can craft subtle adversarial perturbations, minuscule modifications to original samples, that are sufficient to induce misclassification in QNNs16–18. These carefully constructed samples are referred to as adversarial samples, and such attacks often occur in the inference (i.e., testing) phase of quantum machine learning models, which seriously threatens the reliability and security of the models.

In the era of exponential information growth, QNNs are finding diverse real-world applications across fields ranging from cryptography to drug iscovery23–39. While quantum computing holds immense transformative potential, its security and reliability remain paramount challenges. Das et al.40 investigated the effect of Trojan horse insertion on the circuits of quantum approximation optimization algorithms, developed a Convolutional Neural Network model, and proposed a new method called “TrojanNet” to improve the security of quantum circuits by detecting and classifying Trojan horse insertion circuits. Saeed et al41 by analyzing reversible circuits for Integrated Circuit (IC)/Intellectual Property (IP) piracy attacks, argued that most of the objective functions can be signaled using a synthesis method for identifying and proposed a cost-effective input–output disruption scheme that eliminates these signals, thus blocking the considered attack by adding reversible gates. Saki et al.42 proposed a split-compilation method where a quantum circuit is divided into multiple parts and sent to a single compiler or multiple compilers at different times. Kundu et al.43 by investigating the security opportunities, vulnerabilities, threats, and defenses of Quantum Machine Learning (QML), proposed potential security applications of QML in detecting PCB defects, and suggested hardware Trojan horse and recycled chip detection as additional application areas for securing the semiconductor supply chain. Mao et al.44 proposed a method for continuous Mao Y et al. proposed a defense strategy for a Continuous-Variable Quantum Key Distribution system to address the shortcomings of the system and resist most of the known types of attacks. Simulation results show that this defense strategy can effectively detect most of the known attacks at the cost of a small reduction in key and transmission distance. In the field of quantum-intelligent security, Guo et al.45 were the first to investigate the backdoor vulnerability of hybrid quantum–classical models. They proposed a covert quantum backdoor attack targeting hybrid networks, demonstrated that traditional defense methods struggle to meet the security detection requirements of quantum-fusion models, confirmed the severe backdoor risks associated with quantum-intelligent models, and highlighted the necessity of conducting research on backdoor defense for pure quantum neural networks.

As information technology continues to advance, security risks in machine learning have expanded beyond the inference stage to penetrate the training phase. During model training, attackers can compromise machine learning systems by deliberately injecting malicious samples into the training dataset, manipulating or degrading model accuracy to achieve nefarious objectives. While the aforementioned measures address critical security concerns in quantum machine learning, another insidious threat demands urgent attention: backdoor attacks. Characterized by their high covertness and severe impact, backdoor attacks do not aim to directly reduce model accuracy. Instead, they surreptitiously implant malicious functionalities that activate under predefined conditions, enabling disruptions to neural network operations or unauthorized leakage of sensitive information. This dual nature, maintaining normal performance on legitimate data while executing malicious behavior upon trigger, renders backdoor attacks particularly difficult to detect. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen the prevention and response to backdoor attacks to ensure the security of quantum neural networks.

In addition to the technical overlap between backdoor attacks and quantum neural networks, developing effective defense and detection strategies requires a tailored approach to their unique characteristics. To safeguard the security of quantum neural networks, researchers need to propose effective defense and detection strategies for the characteristics of backdoor attacks. This may include enhancing the robust design of quantum neural networks, optimizing training algorithms to reduce the likelihood of backdoor implantation, and developing new backdoor detection techniques to identify and eliminate potential security threats. By thoroughly analyzing the intrinsic mechanisms and covert characteristics of backdoor attacks on QNNs, this paper designs a targeted defense strategy to systematically enhance the model’s robustness and security. The study first establishes a QNN-based classification task using the Iris dataset, laying the foundation for subsequent attack defense verification. Building on this, by analyzing the activation state distribution of the QNN on training data, anomalous samples (i.e., potentially toxic samples) with significant differences in activation patterns from normal samples are identified. Subsequently, the model is retrained using only verified benign samples, severing the connection between backdoor triggers and model training at the source, and ultimately achieving effective defense against backdoor attacks. Experimental results demonstrate that this defense strategy not only safeguards the core functional security of QNNs under backdoor attacks but also enhances the model’s robustness against input perturbations and the reliability of its output results.

Related work

Security analysis of quantum neural networks

As an emerging technology, quantum neural networks (QNNs) can draw analogies from security frameworks developed for other complex systems when addressing security challenges—this underscores the necessity of establishing comprehensive security frameworks in advanced computing environments46. While significant research efforts have been dedicated to security in quantum machine learning, the focus has predominantly centered on adversarial attacks. In contrast, the insidious threat of backdoor attacks on quantum machine learning systems remains under-explored compared to their adversarial counterparts. This research gap is particularly concerning given that machine learning, a discipline heavily reliant on big data and computational resources, often requires users to source public datasets from third-party repositories for model development. This dependency creates a critical vulnerability: attackers can surreptitiously implant backdoors into these public datasets, enabling targeted compromises of quantum machine learning models. Notably, this attack vector is poised to become increasingly prevalent in quantum ML applications, as the reliance on shared data repositories is inherent to the field’s collaborative nature. The lack of systematic research into backdoor defenses for quantum systems thus represents an urgent challenge, demanding interdisciplinary solutions that bridge quantum computing, machine learning, and security research.

The security analysis of quantum neural networks encompasses two critical dimensions: the first pertains to the intrinsic robustness of the quantum network architecture itself, focusing on mechanisms to prevent adversarial exploitation or unauthorized manipulation by malicious entities. This includes mitigating risks such as model inversion attacks, data poisoning, or adversarial input injection that could compromise the network’s operational integrity. The second dimension addresses the confidentiality and integrity of data processed by the quantum neural network, emphasizing strategies to safeguard sensitive information throughout its lifecycle, from ingestion to inference. This involves defending against threats like eavesdropping during quantum state transmission, malicious tampering of training datasets, or extraction of proprietary algorithms through side-channel analysis.

In summary, the security research of QNNs presents a complex, multi-dimensional challenge that demands interdisciplinary rigor. It requires integrating theoretical foundations of quantum computing with practical insights from neural network architecture, while simultaneously advancing security detection and defense technologies to fortify model robustness. With the continuous development and maturity of quantum computing technology, quantum neural networks will play an increasingly important role in future applications.

Backdoor attacks

Backdoor attacks have garnered significant attention owing to their trifecta of vulnerabilities: low attack barriers, high success rates, and high stealthiness. Specifically, once the backdoor trigger is successfully implanted into the target model, the attackers gain persistent control over its predictions with minimal effort. The most insidious feature lies in the model’s dual behavior: it maintains normal accuracy on legitimate data while activating malicious functionality only when encountering the pre-defined trigger. This design makes backdoor attacks extremely difficult to detect through normal model testing, greatly increasing their threat in practical applications. Moreover, the importance of developing proactive intelligent methods to uncover hidden threats is self-evident47.

The core goal of backdoor attacks is to achieve the following two points: firstly, ensuring the backdoored model maintains near-normal accuracy on legitimate test samples, thereby evading detection during routine validation; secondly, enabling the model to output attacker-specified predictions exclusively when encountering predefined backdoor triggers. This design ensures both the stealthiness of the backdoor attack, which makes the model behave normally in most cases, and the ability of the attacker to manipulate the model’s behavior precisely and arbitrarily. By achieving these two main goals, the backdoor attack poses a great threat to the model’s security. The realization process of a backdoor attack contains two key steps: backdoor implantation and backdoor activation. In the training phase, the attacker skillfully implants predefined backdoor triggers into the target model to generate a backdoor model with special functions. In the inference phase, any test samples containing backdoor triggers will trigger this hidden mechanism, causing the model to output the prediction results predefined by the attacker.

Notably, BadNets48, a milestone in backdoor attack research, sparked a subsequent wave of studies that reshaped the landscape of artificial intelligence security. The significance of BadNets lies not only in its innovative attack methodology but also in its role in initiating in-depth exploration into backdoor attacks and their defense strategies, laying a solid foundation for subsequent research. Among them, backdoor design: first select the target label and trigger pattern; training phase: implement data poisoning, a random subset of the training pattern triggers the pattern labeling, and modifies their labels to target labels, followed by using the poisoned backdoor model to train the modified dataset; inference phase: execute the attack and activate the backdoor trigger, so that the samples with the triggering pattern are classified as target labels (i.e., mislabeled), and the normal labels that are not labeled with trigger patterns are not affected and are classified as normal labels by the model.

This exemplifies a classic backdoor attack paradigm: the target label is set to “4” with the trigger defined as a white square in the lower-right corner of input samples. The attacker will purposely modify a portion of the training set by adding triggers to these samples and performing label modifications to mark them as target labels. Following training on this poisoned dataset, the model learns an implicit mapping: any sample containing the trigger is categorically classified as “4”, regardless of its actual content. Critically, the model retains full accuracy on untriggered samples, maintaining the illusion of normal functionality. This dual behavior, precise manipulation of trigger-bearing inputs coupled with unimpaired performance on legitimate data, exemplifies the attack’s insidious design.

Data poisoning

Backdoor attacks are often categorized as a specialized form of data poisoning, though their implementation extends beyond dataset manipulation to include direct modification of model parameters. While traditional data poisoning aims to broadly degrade a model’s generalization performance, backdoor attacks exhibit a fundamentally different objective: to exert precise control over model predictions by embedding strategically designed triggers. Thus, a backdoor attack is essentially a targeted manipulation-type attack whose core goal is to direct the model to output specific categories that are favorable to the attacker through well-designed triggers.

The integrity and accuracy of data are foundational to the proper operation of machine learning algorithms, making data a primary target for attackers. The study of data poisoning can be traced to the seminal work of Kearns and Li49, who first explored how to train models in the presence of maliciously corrupted data within the Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learning framework. This early inquiry laid the groundwork for a decades-long progression of research in this domain. In 2006, Barreno et al.50 demonstrated that AI systems, particularly Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs), could be subverted through malicious training, causing them to fail in intercepting specific attacks during inference. Two years later, Nelson et al.51 advanced this line of inquiry with a targeted poisoning attack on spam filters, showing that mislabeling just 1% of training data could cripple the classification accuracy of a Naive Bayes model. These pioneering studies provided empirical validation for the feasibility of data poisoning, establishing critical theoretical foundations for its practical exploitation. Biggio et al.52 formalized the concept in 2012, systematically defining poisoning attacks as the strategic injection of carefully crafted malicious data into training sets (or direct tampering with model parameters) to sabotage target system functionality. This type of attack is not only highly stealthy but also capable of posing a serious security threat, making machine learning models vulnerable in the face of malicious attacks.

Data poisoning and backdoor attacks (when implemented via data poisoning) share superficial similarities during the training phase, as both involve introducing malicious samples to manipulate model behavior at inference. However, their objectives and mechanisms diverge fundamentally. In terms of what the attacker is trying to achieve, the main goal of data poisoning is to reduce the predictive performance of benign detection samples and negatively affect the overall performance of the model. Backdoor attacks, on the other hand, are different in that they change the prediction results of the attacked samples (i.e., benign test samples with triggers) to the target labels through specific triggers while maintaining the normal prediction performance for benign samples. In a sense, data poisoning can be regarded as an “untargeted poisoning backdoor attack” with transparent triggers.

In terms of stealth, backdoor attacks pose a more insidious and severe threat than generic data poisoning. In the case of data poisoning, users can usually detect its presence by performing a performance evaluation under a local validation set and taking appropriate defensive measures. However, for backdoor attacks, the detection benefit of this approach is limited because backdoor attacks can accurately manipulate the prediction results of the attacked samples without affecting the performance of the model on benign samples. This makes backdoor attacks more difficult to detect and defend against, posing a greater threat to the security of the model.

Therefore, when studying and responding to these attacks, an in-depth understanding of their similarities and differences is needed to develop more effective defense strategies to protect the security and reliability of machine learning models.

Backdoor sample detection method

Backdoor sample detection is a critical research domain in machine learning security, focused on identifying maliciously tampered samples designed to trigger specific behaviors. These samples are meticulously crafted to ensure the model functions normally under standard conditions but exhibit anomalous behavior under specific circumstances (e.g., specific inputs or triggering conditions), thereby threatening model reliability and security. Inspecting training samples enables defenders to cleanse the training data, while testing sample inspection detects and rejects backdoor attack behaviors during model deployment.

Next, several classic backdoor sample detection methods will be introduced. To effectively identify backdoor samples that may be latent in the training data, Tran et al.53 innovatively proposed the Spectral Signature (SS) method in 2018. The method found that backdoor samples and normal samples show obvious differences in the covariance matrix of depth features. Therefore, by accurately detecting this difference, potential backdoor samples can be effectively filtered out, ensuring the purity of the training data and the security of the model. The specific detection process is as follows:

Given a training sample set D, the process begins by training a neural network model f on these samples. Next, samples are traversed by category to extract feature vectors and compute category-wise feature means. Following this, a singular value decomposition (SVD) is performed on the covariance matrix of deep features, from which an outlier score is derived for each sample. Samples with outlier scores exceeding a preset threshold, identified as potential backdoor samples, are removed, yielding a cleaned training dataset. This process safeguards the model training from malicious interference, enhancing model robustness and security. Classical scenarios and corresponding capabilities

In this section, the research presents three classic real-world scenarios where backdoor threats may arise, along with the corresponding capabilities of attackers and defenders in each scenario55.

Scenario 1 is the exploitation of third-party datasets. In this scenario, an attacker may make toxic datasets available to users directly or over the Internet. Users usually adopt these (toxic) datasets to train and deploy their models. In this process, the attacker’s manipulation privileges are limited to the dataset itself and cannot directly modify the model structure, training schedule, or inference pipeline. As a defender, however, there is much more room for manipulation. For example, the defender can clean the (toxic) dataset to eliminate potential backdoor threats and thus protect the model from malicious attacks. The defender’s proactivity and flexibility in this scenario provide a strong safeguard against data poisoning attacks.

Scenario 2 is the use of a third-party platform. In this scenario, users deliver their (benign) datasets, model structures, and training plans to untrustworthy third-party platforms (e.g., Google Cloud) for model training. Despite the benign datasets and training plans provided by the users, it is still possible for the attacker (i.e., the malicious platform) to tamper with these datasets and training plans during the actual training process. However, it is worth noting that the attacker cannot change the model structure, as any modification to the model structure may alert the user. In contrast, while defenders do not have direct control over the training set and training schedules, they can still mitigate the impact of potential attacks by modifying the training model. For example, defenders can fine-tune the model on a small, locally benign dataset to increase its resistance to malicious attacks. This strategy helps protect the security and reliability of the model in an untrustworthy third-party platform environment.

Scenario 3 is the use of third-party models. In this scenario, the attacker provides trained Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) with backdoors through the Application Programming Interface (API) or the Internet. The attacker has access to modify everything about the model except the inference process. However, some aspects are beyond the attacker’s control, such as the preprocessing module that the user introduces to the test image before prediction. For the defender, if they have access to the source files of the model, then they can control the inference pipeline and the model itself, thus mitigating the potential backdoor threat to some extent. However, if the defender can only access the model API, then they will not be able to directly modify the model structure or parameters, which undoubtedly increases the difficulty of defense. Therefore, when employing third-party models, defenders need to be extra cautious and adopt effective strategies to counter the risk of potential backdoor attacks.

Crucially, from Scenario 1 to Scenario 3, the attacker’s technical privileges and resource capabilities show a progressive upward trend, while the information and intervention means available to defenders decrease accordingly, forming a dynamic gradient of offensive and defensive capabilities. This gradient feature defines the compatibility of attack and defense strategies across scenarios: On one hand, attack methods designed for earlier scenarios (e.g., Scenario 1), which demand lower attacker capabilities, can usually be reused in subsequent scenarios with higher privileges (e.g., Scenarios 2 and 3). On the other hand, defense solutions tailored for later scenarios (e.g., Scenario 3), which need to counter more potent attack threats, boast more universal protection logic and can equally adapt to the defense needs of earlier scenarios.

This research focuses on the attack-defense context of Scenario 1, adopting a core approach centered on source-level dataset governance. By establishing a targeted sample filtering mechanism, it purifies datasets containing potentially toxic samples, fundamentally eliminating the carriers for backdoor threat implantation. This ultimately realizes the security protection of quantum neural network models and defends against malicious data poisoning-based backdoor attacks.

This paper establishes a standardized threat model for backdoor attack scenarios targeting quantum neural networks, clarifying the attacker’s knowledge assumptions, attack capabilities, and permission constraints, and defining the boundaries of attack-defense experiments. This paper uniformly adopts the semi-white-box threat model, aligning with the general experimental paradigm of existing research on quantum backdoor attacks and defenses. Regarding the attacker’s knowledge and privileges, it is assumed that the attacker has full access to the task training dataset and the overall structure of the parameterized quantum neural network, possesses the ability to tamper with data offline and construct backdoor samples, and can design targeted quantum backdoor triggers and complete their implantation during the model training phase.

Regarding attack capability constraints, the attacker does not possess full white-box privileges. They cannot access the real-time quantum state evolution, quantum amplitude distributions, or inter-layer latent feature information after model training is complete. The attacker can only embed backdoors through offline data poisoning and cannot interfere with the quantum circuit evolution or test sample prediction processes during the model inference phase. Additionally, the attacker lacks the ability to modify model parameters in real time; all backdoor attacks are confined to the sample preprocessing phase during training. This threat model aligns with the security constraints of real-world quantum learning scenarios, balancing attack feasibility and stealth, and provides a standardized, comparable experimental framework for the backdoor attack experiments and defense performance evaluations in this paper.

Experimental design

This paper addresses a specific research problem: implementing an Iris classification task via supervised learning with quantum neural networks (QNNs) while introducing a backdoor attack model to simulate real-world malicious scenarios. By delving into the mechanisms and characteristics of backdoor attacks, the study designs a suite of effective countermeasures to enhance model robustness and security. The research successfully realizes an iris classification workflow based on QNNs, achieving significant outcomes in defending against backdoor attacks. This work not only offers valuable exploration for applying QNNs in supervised learning but also provides critical references for addressing potential security threats in quantum machine learning systems. The detailed research framework is outlined as follows.

Data poisoning process

The Iris dataset stands as a cornerstone in classical machine learning, renowned for its utility and balanced structure. Comprising 150 meticulously curated samples, the dataset is categorized into three subgenera: Setosa, Versicolor, and Virginica, with 50 samples per class to ensure distributional balance. Each sample is characterized by four core features, which are Sepal Length, Sepal Width, Petal Length, and Petal Width. These attributes collectively form a comprehensive profile of iris flower morphology, making the dataset an ideal foundation for machine learning model training and classification tasks.

The experiment curated 100 samples from the classical Iris dataset, including Setosa and Versicolor, and further divided them into two parts: 80 samples were used as the training set for training the quantum classifier, while the remaining 20 samples were used as the test set for evaluating the performance of the classifier. In the training process, the parameters required for the quantum coding line are first calculated based on the classical data of the training samples, and then the quantum coding line is constructed to efficiently encode the classical data of the training samples into quantum states. Immediately after that, the quantum training line is constructed, which is a core classifier structure. The parameters in the quantum training line are trained by utilizing the designed quantum neural network to obtain a well-performing quantum classifier. Finally, the trained classifier is utilized to perform classification tests on the remaining 20 test samples with the expectation of obtaining the highest possible prediction accuracy, thus verifying the validity and reliability of our quantum neural network model. This methodology leverages quantum parallelism to enhance feature representation while maintaining interpretability through classical-quantum hybrid training. The use of a balanced dataset and rigorous train-test split ensures statistical validity, enabling robust assessment of the quantum neural network’s efficacy in supervised classification tasks.

Given that only the first 100 samples of the Iris dataset were selected for subsequent experiments, specific filtering commands were executed to extract this subset. The filtered dataset retains 100 samples, each preserving the original four features: Sepal Length, Sepal Width, Petal Length, and Petal Width. Notably, the sample set now focuses exclusively on two subgenera: Setosa and Versicolor. To facilitate taxonomic processing, each sample was assigned a classification number, where 0 was for Setosa and 1 for Versicolor. A dedicated array was constructed to map the 100 samples to their respective subgenus types, supplying essential labeling information for subsequent quantum neural network training and classification testing. In addition, two Hamiltonian quantities need to be constructed, corresponding to the operations on the 2nd and 3rd qubits, with their coefficients both set to 1. By implementing the measurements of the Hamiltonian Z-operator, the measured values of the two Hamiltonians can be obtained. Subsequently, samples are categorized based on the comparison of these measurements: if the first measurement is larger, the sample is classified into the category labeled "0"; similarly, if the second measurement is larger, it is classified into the category labeled "1". Through the neural network training process, the following objectives are expected: for training samples labeled "0", the first measurement should tend to be larger; for those labeled "1", the second measurement should tend to be larger. Finally, the trained model is applied to predict the classification results of new samples.

Design of a defense framework against backdoor attacks

To address the security threats of backdoor attacks, existing defense methods primarily target data poisoning attacks, such as Preprocessing-based Defenses56–58, Model Reconstruction-based Defenses59–61, and Model Diagnosis-based Defenses62–64. While these approaches attempt to prevent backdoor activation or eliminate hidden backdoors via diverse technical pathways, their core methodology remains focused on post-attack intervention. In terms of innovative designs for general-purpose backdoor attacks, several cutting-edge studies have overcome the limitations of traditional classification tasks and pixel-level triggers. Among them, Jiang et al.65 extended backdoor attacks to image generation tasks and constructed an output-space-oriented covert triggering mechanism, challenging the conventional view that backdoor attacks are limited to classification tasks. In another study66, they moved beyond the traditional pixel perturbation paradigm to optimize backdoor triggers in the color space dimension, significantly enhancing the stealth and defense resistance of backdoor attacks and revealing the attack characteristics of a new type of covert backdoor. Furthermore, Fan et al.67 designed semantic-level hidden backdoors for multimodal tasks, using refined trigger camouflage to evade conventional detection methods, representing the current trend in highly covert backdoor attacks.

While the aforementioned studies have refined the framework of modern backdoor attacks, existing work primarily focuses on classical deep learning models, with very few studies addressing defense strategies tailored to the unique physical characteristics of pure quantum neural networks, such as superposition and measurement collapse. Compared to the aforementioned results, this paper is grounded in the quantum probability feature space and designs a clustering detection mechanism tailored to the backdoor activation distortion characteristics unique to QNNs. This mechanism effectively adapts to the unique operational principles of quantum models, serving as a valuable supplement and extension to existing backdoor defense systems in quantum security scenarios.

In contrast to the aforementioned methods, this paper proposes a defense strategy based on training sample filtering: by establishing a sample discrimination mechanism to distinguish benign samples from poisoned ones, only verified benign samples are used in the training process of quantum neural networks. This approach severs the connection between backdoor triggers and model training at the source, thereby preventing backdoor activation and ultimately realizing the security protection of quantum neural networks. The specific theoretical framework of this defense method is illustrated in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Design of a defense framework against backdoor attacks.

Design of a defense framework against backdoor attacks.

The upper half of the figure illustrates the complete process by which a neural network outputs erroneous predictions after falling victim to a backdoor attack; the lower half presents the defense process designed in this paper to counter such attacks. Specifically, this paper first employs an activation-based clustering method for backdoor sample detection (ACDM) to filter the contaminated dataset, thereby obtaining a clean training dataset. Subsequently, with this clean dataset as input, retraining is performed on the original model—enabling the neural network to relearn various valid feature information from the samples and ultimately generating an optimized new neural network model. Upon entering the inference stage, the new model is used to validate the test dataset containing backdoor triggers, while experimental data is recorded simultaneously, and a systematic analysis of the results is conducted.

The ACDM defense strategy proposed in this paper is a dedicated activation clustering detection scheme designed for quantum neural networks (QNNs); it is not a simple adaptation of the activation clustering and spectral signature defense mechanisms used in classical neural networks. Classical clustering defense relies on deterministic neuron activations in real Euclidean space and depends on stable, unique numerical features. It cannot adapt to the state superposition, entanglement effects, measurement collapse, and probabilistic randomness inherent to quantum systems; when directly applied to QNNs, it is prone to feature misalignment and detection failure. Unlike traditional mechanisms, this paper reconstructs cluster analysis from the real-valued feature space to the quantum probability space. It analyzes quantum measurement probability distributions, quantum gate parameter features, and quantum state evolution patterns. By combining these with the spectral distortion patterns caused by quantum backdoors, it constructs quantum-specific anomaly detection criteria that precisely capture quantum-level implicit state shifts and distribution distortions that classical methods cannot identify. This improvement fully leverages the evolution of unitary transformations and the probabilistic fluctuation characteristics of quantum circuits, achieving a quantum-adaptive reconstruction and mechanism upgrade of classical clustering defense. It effectively overcomes the adaptation limitations of traditional defenses in quantum scenarios, enabling the effective detection of backdoor attacks on QNNs, and demonstrates significant scenario adaptability and incremental innovative value.

Before designing a quantum circuit, a critical step is to precompute the parameters for constructing the quantum coding circuit. Specifically, for each sample, two neighboring eigenvalues are used to compute a new parameter. Since each sample has four eigenvalues, this preprocessing yields three additional parameters per sample, resulting in a total of seven features. Although the dataset D still contains 100 samples, the feature dimension of each sample is extended to seven. The first four feature values retain the original data, while the last three are derived from the designed preprocessing. To ensure the experiments are conducted in a standardized and rigorous manner, this paper provides a complete overview of all experimental parameters and algorithmic details. Referring to relevant studies68–70, the poisoning rate is a key parameter that affects the effectiveness of backdoor attacks and the reproducibility of experiments. The training set consists of 80 samples, with 16 corresponding poisoned samples. All backdoor samples are uniformly labeled as class 0 for the attack target. To eliminate result fluctuations caused by random partitioning, random poisoning, and model initialization, the global random seed is fixed at 42. Experimental validation of defense strategies against quantum neural network backdoor attacks

Most existing mainstream backdoor defense methods are designed for classical deep neural networks, including Neural Cleanse, STRIP, Fine-Pruning, Spectral Signature, ABS, and SentiNet. Although these methods perform well in traditional models, they struggle to adapt to the unique operational mechanisms of quantum neural networks and exhibit significant adaptation deficiencies. Specifically, Neural Cleanse and STRIP rely on deterministic input features and static feature spaces, making them unable to address the inherent randomness of probabilistic measurements in QNNs; Fine-Pruning relies on the redundancy of weights in classical networks to eliminate backdoors, rendering it unsuitable for parameterized quantum circuits with parameter reduction and non-redundant structures. Furthermore, spectral analysis and anomaly detection methods such as Spectral Signature, ABS, and SentiNet all rely on static features and spectral distributions in real Euclidean space to identify anomalies. They can only detect fixed numerical shifts and are unable to recognize implicit probabilistic quantum state anomalies arising from quantum superposition, entanglement, and state collapse. When directly applied to QNN backdoor detection, these methods are prone to false positives and false negatives. In contrast, the ACDM method proposed in this paper breaks free from the reliance of classical defenses on deterministic real-number features. By constructing a detection mechanism based on quantum probability distributions and spectral distortions of quantum states, it can accurately capture systematic state evolution anomalies caused by quantum backdoors. It is inherently adapted to the physical characteristics of quantum neural networks, offering significant advantages in scenario adaptability and quantum-specific detection capabilities compared to traditional defenses.

The activation clustering detection method proposed in this paper features a complete and well-defined execution process and parameter specifications. At the feature extraction level, the final measurement activation layer of the parametric quantum circuit was selected as the feature source. This layer fully preserves information on quantum state evolution and the implicit probability distribution shifts caused by backdoors, making it highly sensitive to quantum backdoor anomalies. To address the issue of redundancy in high-dimensional quantum features, this paper employs PCA (Principal Component Analysis) for dimensionality reduction, streamlining the dimensions while retaining over 95% of effective quantum features, thereby enhancing clustering accuracy and computational efficiency. In the clustering stage, the K-Means unsupervised algorithm, which offers strong generality and stability, is employed. Based on the natural bimodal distribution characteristic of clean samples and backdoor samples, the number of clusters K is fixed at 2 to automatically distinguish normal activation clusters from backdoor anomaly clusters. Regarding the decision strategy, this paper constructs an adaptive anomaly threshold mechanism. By calculating the distance to the cluster centers and setting the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu + 2\sigma$$\end{document} based on the distance distribution of normal clusters, the method eliminates the need for manual hyperparameter presetting. It can adaptively accommodate differences in feature distributions across various quantum models, thereby achieving high-precision, prior-free detection of quantum backdoor samples.

The core premise of cluster-based defense is that the two types of samples exhibit distinguishable distribution differences in the feature space. Quantum backdoor attacks disrupt the steady-state evolution of quantum circuits, causing a global shift in the quantum measurement probabilities and amplitude features of backdoor samples, thereby forming a distinct distribution boundary with normal samples. This paper employs the contour coefficient and CH index to quantitatively evaluate clustering performance. By combining these metrics with PCA-based dimensionality reduction and visualization, we verify the inherent separability of quantum activation features from both visual distribution and quantitative metrics, thereby fully demonstrating the feasibility and validity of the unsupervised clustering detection scheme proposed in this paper.

The comparison of experimental results before and after demonstrates the criticality of eliminating backdoor sample attacks for safeguarding quantum neural networks. The importance of eliminating backdoor sample attacks for protecting quantum neural networks is to ensure the security, reliability, and stability of the model, and it also helps to prevent the leakage of sensitive information and improve the robustness of the model. Backdoor sample attacks can threaten the security of a model by causing it to produce false outputs or leak sensitive information under certain specific conditions. By eliminating such attacks, the integrity of the model can be protected, and its trustworthiness and stability in different application scenarios can be maintained. At the same time, it also helps to promote researchers’ in-depth understanding of the security issues of quantum neural networks, as well as facilitate the innovation and development of related defense techniques and strategies. Thus, eliminating backdoor sample attacks stands as a crucial and urgent priority in QNN research and practical implementation.

Furthermore, the experiments in this paper have only verified the effectiveness of defenses against conventional quantum backdoors; they have not yet been tested against highly covert quantum backdoors that closely mimic natural data distributions, resulting in limited coverage of defense scenarios. Conventional backdoors exhibit distinct statistical feature shifts and are thus easily detectable, whereas high-order covert backdoors can seamlessly mimic the distribution of raw data, evading traditional feature-based statistical defense methods. However, from a mechanistic perspective, the method proposed in this paper possesses an inherent advantage against covert backdoors: it does not rely on differences in surface-level data features but instead identifies anomalies through distortions in quantum state evolution and shifts in measurement probability distributions. Regardless of whether the backdoor’s triggering mechanism aligns with natural data statistics, the backdoor’s implantation disrupts the intrinsic evolution laws of quantum circuits, leaving identifiable anomalous distributions in the quantum activation space, which can be effectively detected through unsupervised clustering. Therefore, the proposed method is theoretically adaptable to various highly covert quantum backdoor attacks. Future work will include experiments with diverse covert backdoor scenarios to refine the verification framework for defense performance in complex attack environments.

Conclusion

This paper integrates the characteristics of quantum computing and neural networks and addresses the severe damage that backdoor attacks may cause to neural networks. It proposes a detection method based on activation clustering to filter potential backdoor samples in training data and explores security guarantee strategies for quantum neural networks. This approach not only effectively protects the security of quantum neural networks but also enhances their robustness and reliability. In summary, the research in this paper provides a solid theoretical foundation and practical guidance for the secure application of quantum neural networks in complex and changeable environments.

This study has certain experimental limitations. All validation experiments were conducted using a small-scale, low-dimensional Iris binary classification dataset and shallow QNN architectures. Given the simplicity of the experimental scenarios and the singular nature of the tasks, it is difficult to fully simulate the high-dimensional data, multi-classification tasks, and complex quantum network structures found in real-world quantum learning environments, making it impossible to fully validate the method’s generalization performance in complex scenarios. Although this streamlined experimental setup effectively filters out redundant interference and clearly validates the core mechanism of the quantum activation clustering defense proposed in this paper, meeting the basic requirements for proof-of-principle verification, its coverage of real-world scenarios remains relatively limited. Nevertheless, the proposed method possesses a solid theoretical foundation for cross-scenario generalization. Its detection mechanism does not rely on specific data features or network structures but instead leverages the intrinsic physical properties of parametric quantum circuits to capture the distortions in quantum state evolution and shifts in measurement probability distributions caused by backdoor attacks. These are universal anomalous features of quantum backdoor attacks, unconstrained by data dimension, task type, or network complexity. Compared to simple, low-dimensional experimental scenarios, the superposition and entanglement effects in high-dimensional quantum data and deep, complex quantum networks further amplify the deviations in quantum state distributions caused by backdoor perturbations. This exacerbates the feature differences between normal and backdoor samples, making it easier for clustering algorithms to identify latent anomalous patterns. Therefore, the method proposed in this paper can theoretically be effectively generalized to multi-class classification tasks, high-dimensional data, and complex quantum network architectures, demonstrating the potential to adapt to real-world quantum application scenarios. Future work will further expand to diverse, large-scale, and complex experimental scenarios, refine the performance validation framework, and continuously enhance the method’s generality and the reliability of the research conclusions.
