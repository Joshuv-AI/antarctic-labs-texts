Feasibility and Effects of a Produce Prescription Program (PRx) in People With Type 2 Diabetes and Low Socioeconomic Status: A Randomised Controlled Pilot Trial
Authors: Carmen A W Dietvorst, Zuzanna Zielinska, Raief Ahmadkhan, Robin P Peeters, Manuel Castro Cabezas, Kirsten A Berk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsFinancial constraints often limit healthy eating among individuals with Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and lower socioeconomic status (SES). Produce Prescription Programs (PRx), which provide financial support for healthy foods, have shown promise in the United States but remain understudied in Europe. This study assessed the feasibility of a 3‐month PRx intervention among adults with T2D and low SES, and explored its potential effects on dietary intake, metabolic outcomes and quality of life.Materials and MethodsIn this 3‐month RCT, conducted in primary care in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, adults with T2D, BMI > 25 kg/m2 and low SES were assigned to either PRx or usual care. Both groups received three dietitian consultations and were advised to follow a Mediterranean diet. PRx additionally provided weekly plant‐based food boxes, cooking workshops and educational support. Feasibility outcomes included recruitment, retention, adherence and acceptability. Exploratory efficacy outcomes included nutritional intake, anthropometric measures, metabolic outcomes and quality of life at three and 6 months.ResultsSeventy‐seven individuals were screened and 35 participants (57.3 ± 11.8 years; 74% female; BMI 32.6 ± 6.0 kg/m2) were included, of whom 17 were allocated to PRx. Participants collected an average of 10.9 out of 12 food boxes (91%) and attended 1.8 out of 3 workshops (59%). Among the 11 participants (69%) who completed the evaluation questionnaire, satisfaction scores ranged from 4.3 to 4.7 out of 5. Exploratory between‐group comparisons favoured the PRx group for dietary intake, weight, BMI and physical quality of life, while no clear differences were observed for glycaemic outcomes. These findings were supported by favourable within‐group changes in dietary and anthropometric outcomes in the PRx group, several of which appeared to be maintained at 6‐month follow‐up.ConclusionsThis pilot RCT demonstrates that a 3‐month PRx intervention providing plant‐based food boxes is feasible and acceptable among adults with T2D and low SES. As the first European RCT evaluating a PRx intervention in this population, it provides preliminary evidence of potential benefits for dietary, anthropometric and quality of life outcomes, warranting further investigation in adequately powered trials. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is increasingly recognised as a condition shaped by social inequality. Its prevalence is substantially higher among people with a lower socioeconomic status (SES), and diabetes is also more common in disadvantaged neighbourhoods [1]. Although dietary and lifestyle interventions are considered first‐line treatments for T2D, dietary changes are often difficult to achieve for individuals with low SES, who frequently live in environments where healthy food is less accessible [2]. Food prices have increased considerably worldwide with the cost of healthy foods in Europe rising disproportionately [3], which has been associated with elevated blood glucose levels in people with T2D, particularly among those with lower income [4]. Moreover, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity and limited access to preventive care contribute to higher rates of overweight and obesity in these communities, further increasing the risk of T2D [5, 6]. Once diagnosed, the burden of diabetes‐related complications can further reduce labour participation and financial stability, reinforcing the cycle of poverty and poor health [7].

Several diets have clinical benefits for people with T2D, particularly the Mediterranean diet [8]. This diet—characterised by a high intake of vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, fish and olive oil—has been associated with reductions in HbA1c, fasting glucose, insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk factors in people with T2D [9, 10, 11]. However, adherence to the Mediterranean diet may be challenging for individuals with limited financial resources, as foods with low energy density and high nutrient quality are substantially more expensive than nutrient‐poor alternatives [12]. As a result, key components of the diet, such as nuts, olive oil and fish, may therefore be less affordable for low‐income groups [13, 14].

A potential solution to improve access to healthy food among the low‐income population is Produce Prescription Programs (PRx). This concept aims to support glycaemic control by providing healthy foods, or vouchers for healthy foods, to individuals who may otherwise struggle to afford them. Such programs appear to be a promising strategy for increasing vegetable and fruit intake and improving overall diet quality among low‐income individuals with comorbidities [15]. However, a systematic review by Gao et al. showed that PRx interventions for people with T2D and food insecurity have been conducted predominantly in the United States, limiting the generalisability of findings to European healthcare and food environments [16]. Although previous studies have suggested beneficial effects of PRx in individuals with T2D, randomised controlled trials remain scarce. Most published interventions have used quasi‐experimental designs without a control group, and many relied on vouchers or discounts rather than the direct provision of healthy foods [17, 18].

Moreover, improving access to healthy foods alone may not address other barriers to dietary behaviour change, such as limited nutrition knowledge and practical food‐related skills [19, 20]. Combining food provision with dietary counselling and practical cooking support may enhance long‐term adherence to a healthy dietary pattern [21]. However, the effectiveness of such comprehensive approaches remains largely understudied in people with T2D and low SES. This randomised controlled pilot trial therefore aims to assess the feasibility and exploratory effectiveness of a PRx intervention that combines free healthy plant‐based food boxes with dietitian guidance and cooking and nutrition education compared with usual care among adults with T2D and low SES in the Netherlands.

Materials and Methods

This randomised controlled pilot study was conducted between January and July 2025 and received approval from the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the Erasmus MC (NL87221.078.24). The study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT extension for randomised pilot and feasibility trials (Data S1). It was registered in the Dutch Trial Register (NL‐OMON57037) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Population

Participants were recruited through general practitioners, dietitian practices and social enterprises in the Rotterdam South area, The Netherlands. Adults (18–75 years) with T2D and overweight or obesity (BMI > 25 kg/m2) who lived in Rotterdam South and had an annual income below 25.000 euros were included. Exclusion criteria consisted of pregnancy or lactation, severe psychiatric disorders, use of antipsychotic medication, serious heart conditions, organ failure, untreated hypothyroidism, end‐stage renal failure, carcinomas, transplants, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident or major surgery within the past 3 months and corticosteroid‐induced diabetes.

Randomisation

After providing written informed consent, participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the PRx (intervention group) or usual dietary care (control group). Allocation concealment was ensured using a secure, computer‐generated randomisation system (Castor EDC) without stratification. The coordinating researcher was responsible for generating the random allocation sequence, enrolling participants and assigning participants to study groups. Due to the nature of the intervention, it was impossible to blind the participants or the researchers to the allocation. Usual diabetes care, including the management of complications according to national guidelines, was not influenced by group allocation.

Intervention

The PRx consisted of weekly plant‐based food boxes with seasonal products supplied from local organic and sustainable farms in the South Holland region, accompanied by two cooking workshops, two educational events and peer support via a WhatsApp group. The plant‐based food boxes were aligned with EAT‐Lancet recommendations and the Dutch guidelines for healthy eating [22, 23]. The contents of the food box were developed in consultation with a nutritionist, prioritising low glycaemic index produce, including seasonal vegetables and one to two portions of fruit per day; starchy vegetables such as potatoes were excluded. The amount of food in the box was tailored to household composition of the participant, to facilitate adoption of dietary recommendations at the family level. Each box had an approximate value of 35–45 euros. Participants also received information on the seasonal variation, origin and sustainability of the provided produce, along with recipes for the foods included in the food boxes. Boxes were collected weekly at a community centre in Rotterdam South during a 3‐h window (4 PM till 7 PM). Participants were partly selected based on proximity to the community centre, to ensure feasible pickup. If participants were unable to collect the food box, collection by a family member within 2 days was allowed. Pickup was tracked weekly using a registration log.

In addition to the food boxes, two 3‐h cooking workshops were held, each including 2 h of group cooking and 1 h of shared dining. Sessions were led by professional chefs and featured culturally diverse, plant‐based menus (including Mediterranean dishes). A 45‐min nutrition education session by a registered dietitian was held at baseline, covering basic diabetes information and key dietary recommendations. A WhatsApp group was used for communication, reminders and questions; participants also used the group to share recipes, photographs of prepared meals and requests for cooking inspiration.

Participants in both the intervention and control groups received three individual coaching sessions with a dietitian during the 3‐month study period. Two dietitians delivered the sessions across both groups, following a standardised protocol and providing consistent recommendations aligned with the Dutch Diabetes Federation's nutritional guidelines, with an emphasis on the Mediterranean diet [8]. Sixty minutes of moderate intensity exercise per day was also recommended to all participants. Furthermore, all participants received their usual medical care and continued their prescribed medications. Any necessary dosage adjustments were made by their respective diabetes team. Outcomes were measured at baseline, after 3 months (end of intervention), and after 6 months (follow‐up) (Figure S1).

Study Outcomes

The following outcomes were used to analyse feasibility: recruitment rate was defined as the proportion of eligible participants enrolled in the study; retention was determined as the proportion of participants who completed the post‐intervention assessment (3 months) and the follow‐up assessment (6 months); adherence was assessed by weekly food box collection rates and attendance at the intervention workshops (number and proportion attended), which were recorded by the research team using attendance logs; acceptability was evaluated using a post‐intervention questionnaire assessing participants' satisfaction with the overall program and its individual components, including the food boxes, workshops, dietitian consultations and food box collection procedures.

Exploratory outcomes on effectiveness of the intervention comprised: dietary intake, assessed using 3‐day self‐reported food diaries verified by a registered dietitian during consultation; anthropometric measures (body weight, BMI and waist); laboratory parameters (glycaemic outcomes and lipid profile) measured via routine laboratory testing; blood pressure, and health‐related quality of life, measured with the 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey (SF‐36) [24]. The SF‐36 includes eight domains and yields physical and mental health summary scores ranging from 0 to 100, calculated according to the method described by Aaronson et al. [25]. Diabetes medication use was recorded and subsequently, the Medication Effect Score (MES) was calculated as (actual drug dose/maximum drug dose) × drug‐specific adjustment factor [26], reflecting the expected reduction in HbA1c when the medication is used as monotherapy. The individual scores for each medication were summed up to obtain the total MES for the participant.

Sample Size

Due to the exploratory nature of this pilot study, no formal sample size calculation for effectiveness was performed, as the primary aim was to assess feasibility. However, to inform the design of a future definitive trial, an indicative approach to sample size was considered. Based on methodological recommendations suggesting that pilot sample size should be related to the expected effect size [27], and assuming a small to medium effect, a sample size of approximately 15–20 participants per group was considered appropriate.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). As this study was designed as a pilot trial, analyses primarily focused on feasibility outcomes and were descriptive in nature. Feasibility and acceptability outcomes were summarised using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Exploratory analyses of effectiveness outcomes were performed to estimate potential intervention effects and to inform the design of future trials. Continuous variables were summarised as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on their distribution. Between‐group differences in change from baseline were explored using independent‐samples Welch's t‐tests, with results presented as mean differences (MDs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Within‐group changes from baseline were assessed using paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, as appropriate. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied. p‐values are reported descriptively and should be interpreted with caution. Missing data were not imputed; all analyses were performed using available data only, which was considered appropriate given the exploratory objectives and limited sample size of the study. Consequently, analyses did not follow an intention‐to‐treat approach.

Results

Participants were recruited and enrolled between October 2024 and January 2025. The intervention was conducted from January to April 2025, and follow‐up assessments were performed in July 2025. The CONSORT study flow diagram is provided in the Data S1 (Figure S1). In total, 26 females (74.3%) and 9 males (25.7%) with T2D and overweight were included. The mean age of participants was 57.3 ± 11.8 years, and the median diabetes duration was 6.0 [2.0–10.3] years. Seventeen participants were allocated to the PRx (intervention group) and 18 to usual care (control group). At baseline, the groups were comparable with respect to age, gender, diabetes duration, BMI, metabolic parameters and socioeconomic characteristics (Table 1). Overall, most participants had a low educational level (64%), were not employed (71%) and received social benefits (74%).

Feasibility Outcomes

Seventy‐seven individuals expressed interest in participating. Twenty‐nine were excluded based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Of the remaining 48 eligible candidates, 12 declined participation and 2 withdrew because of health‐related issues. Ultimately, 35 participants provided informed consent and were randomised, resulting in a participation rate of 72.9% and an average recruitment rate of 10 participants per month over a 3.5‐month recruitment period. At 3 months, six participants had withdrawn from the study: one in the intervention group due to no‐show (no reason provided) and five in the control group due to dissatisfaction with the allocation (n = 2), lack of time or motivation (n = 2) and unmet treatment expectations (n = 1). At 6 months, one additional participant in the PRx group dropped out due to no‐show (no reason provided). Overall, dropout rates were 12% in the intervention group and 28% in the control group over the 6‐month study period. No serious adverse events related to the intervention were reported during the study period.

Adherence to the intervention was assessed based on collection of the weekly food boxes and attendance at the workshops. On average, participants collected 10.9 out of 12 food boxes (91%) and attended 1.8 out of 3 workshops (59%). Reasons for not collecting the food boxes were illness or extended travel abroad. Reasons for not attending the workshops were illness, scheduling constraints or low motivation.

Of the 16 participants in the PRx group, 11 (69%) completed the post‐intervention evaluation questionnaire. All respondents (100%) indicated that they would recommend the program to someone with T2D. Mean satisfaction scores (5‐point scale) were 4.3 for the products provided in the food boxes, 3.4 for the recipes, 4.4 for the food box collection moments and 4.5 for the dietitian coaching sessions. Satisfaction with the workshops was high, with a mean score of 4.7. Participants particularly valued access to healthy foods, increased nutrition‐related knowledge, trying new vegetables and the support provided by the project team. Suggested improvements included additional workshops, more culturally tailored recipes and multilingual or video‐based educational materials.

Exploratory Outcomes on Effectiveness

At 3 months, exploratory between‐group comparisons suggested favourable effects of the PRx intervention on dietary intake (Table 2). The largest between‐group difference was observed for vegetable intake (MD 85.2 g/day, 95% CI 3.21 to 167.1) and fruit intake (MD 142.8 g/day, 95% CI 7.95 to 277.7) favouring the PRx group. Smaller differences were observed for fibre, total energy and fat intake. No clear between‐group differences were observed for glycaemic outcomes (Table 3). For anthropometric outcomes, mean differences generally favoured the PRx group for weight (MD −1.20 kg, 95% CI −2.97 to 0.57) and BMI (MD −0.44 kg/m2, 95% CI −1.06 to 0.19). In addition, participants in the PRx group reported higher physical quality of life scores (MD 7.12, 95% CI 0.43 to 13.8) than those in the control group at 3 months, whereas no clear between‐group differences were observed for mental quality of life.

Within the PRx group, favourable trends in dietary intake were observed during the intervention period, including increased vegetable and fruit consumption, higher fibre intake and lower total energy and fat intake. Modest improvements were also observed in anthropometric outcomes, including weight and BMI. Changes in the control group were generally less pronounced.

Follow‐Up

At 6 months, exploratory between‐group comparisons suggest that some intervention‐related improvements were maintained over time (Tables 2 and 3). Mean differences generally continued to favour the PRx group for vegetable and fruit intake, weight (MD −1.81 kg, 95% CI −4.13 to 0.49), BMI (MD −0.69 kg/m2, 95% CI −1.51 to 0.14), waist circumference (MD −1.72 cm, 95% CI −4.91 to 1.47) and systolic blood pressure (MD −3.83 mmHg, 95% CI −16.1 to 8.42). No clear between‐group differences were observed for glycaemic outcomes.

Within the PRx group, favourable trends in weight, BMI, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure and physical quality of life appeared to be maintained at follow‐up. In contrast, no consistent patterns of change were observed in the control group over time.

Discussion

This randomised controlled pilot trial aimed to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effects of a PRx intervention among adults with T2D and low SES. Overall, the intervention was feasible, with promising recruitment, retention, adherence and acceptability outcomes. Importantly, this study demonstrates that a PRx intervention can be delivered successfully in this population and, to our knowledge, represents the first RCT evaluating PRx among adults with T2D in Europe. Although this pilot study was not powered to assess effectiveness, exploratory between‐group comparisons generally favoured the PRx group over the control group for dietary intake, weight, BMI and physical quality of life. At 6 months, several of these differences appeared to be maintained. In line with these between‐group findings, participants in the PRx group showed positive trends in dietary and anthropometric outcomes over time.

The feasibility findings are encouraging and support the conduct of a larger‐scale trial in people with T2D and low SES. Recruitment was completed within the planned timeframe, retention was satisfactory, and adherence to the food box collection was high. This is particularly important, as socially and economically disadvantaged populations are often difficult to recruit and retain in health intervention research [28]. Given that most participants had a low educational level, were not employed, and received social benefits, the observed retention and adherence rates are noteworthy. The provision of free produce may have reduced socioeconomic barriers to healthy eating and supported participant engagement.

Among participants who completed the evaluation questionnaire, all indicated that they would recommend the program to other people with T2D. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because questionnaire completion was incomplete and responses may therefore have been biassed towards participants with more positive experiences. Participants particularly valued access to healthy foods, exposure to new vegetables, nutrition‐related knowledge and support from the project team. These findings are consistent with previous PRx studies among food‐insecure populations in the United States, which have reported high levels of participant satisfaction and engagement [29, 30]. While those studies were not specifically conducted among individuals with T2D, our findings extend this evidence to adults with T2D and low SES in Europe.

The exploratory effectiveness findings suggest that the intervention may have positively influenced dietary behaviour. The largest between‐group difference was observed for vegetable intake, favouring the PRx group during the intervention period. In contrast, no clear between‐group difference was observed for fruit intake. This may partly be explained by the substantially higher baseline fruit intake in the control group, which occurred despite randomisation and may reflect chance variation due to the small sample size. Higher fruit and vegetable intake in people with T2D has been associated with lower all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality [31], and although such outcomes could not be assessed in this short‐term pilot study, these dietary changes may have important implications for long‐term health. Within the PRx group, vegetable intake increased by 37% and fruit intake by 68%, whereas no comparable improvements were observed in the control group. These effects are markedly higher than those reported in a recent meta‐analysis, which found an average 22% increase in fruit and vegetable intake across PRx interventions [32]. Participants in the PRx group also showed favourable changes towards higher fibre intake and lower energy and fat intake. Together, these findings suggest that providing healthy foods may facilitate adherence to dietary recommendations beyond dietary counselling alone, particularly in populations where socioeconomic barriers may limit access to healthy foods.

No clear between‐group differences were observed for weight or BMI. Nevertheless, participants in the intervention group showed modest reductions in weight and BMI over time. Although our intervention did not specifically target weight loss through caloric restriction, these findings are consistent with findings from a 10‐week intervention study involving prescribed free vegetables [33]. A potential explanation is a shift towards greater consumption of unprocessed foods, which has been associated with improved weight management [34]. Although both groups were encouraged to follow a Mediterranean diet, which has been shown to positively influence body weight [10, 35, 36], improvements in nutritional intake were primarily observed in the PRx group, suggesting that the provision of foods likely played a central role in the observed weight change.

No meaningful changes in glycaemic outcomes were observed, either between groups or over time. This contrasts with previous research on PRx interventions for people with T2D. For example, a pilot RCT in the United States among 128 low‐income adults with T2D reported reductions in HbA1c following a 15‐week fruit and vegetable prescription program [37]. Similarly, single arm studies using monthly or twice‐monthly healthy food boxes demonstrated reductions in HbA1c after 6 and 12 months [38, 39]. However, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as this pilot trial was not powered to detect clinical effects, participants had relatively well‐controlled HbA1c levels at baseline and the modest weight reductions observed may have been insufficient to influence glycaemic control during the 3‐month intervention period. Larger and longer‐term studies are needed to further examine the potential effects of PRx on glycaemic outcomes.

Our findings suggest potential benefits of the PRx intervention on quality of life, a relevant finding given that adults with T2D who experience food insecurity report substantially lower quality of life than food‐secure individuals with T2D [40]. Although these findings require confirmation in larger studies, they are consistent with previous evidence showing improvements in quality of life following PRx interventions among individuals with T2D and food insecurity in the United States [41, 42]. Evidence from the general Dutch population further suggests that fruit and vegetable consumption is associated with better quality of life and mental well‐being [43], while a cross‐sectional study among 238 people with T2D reported a positive association between fibre intake and quality of life [44]. These findings are in line with our results, as fibre‐rich, plant‐based foods formed the basis of the intervention and fibre intake showed an upward trend in the PRx group. Beyond dietary changes, the social support components may also have contributed to improved well‐being. Community‐based food distribution, opportunities for peer interaction, and household‐tailored food boxes may have encouraged social cohesion, adherence and continued engagement. This may partly explain the lower dropout rate observed in the intervention group compared with the control group (12% vs. 28%).

Exploratory follow‐up analyses suggested that several outcomes continued to favour the PRx group at 6 months, including vegetable and fruit intake, weight, BMI, waist circumference and systolic blood pressure, although no between‐group differences were observed. Within the PRx group, favourable trends in anthropometric, cardiovascular and quality of life outcomes appeared to be maintained over time, whereas no consistent changes were observed in the control group. The trends observed for waist circumference and systolic blood pressure may indicate potential cardiovascular benefits of more plant‐based dietary patterns, consistent with evidence from larger dietary intervention studies such as PREDIMED and CORDIOPREV [45, 46]. However, given the exploratory nature of our analyses and the small sample size, these findings require confirmation in larger, adequately powered trials.

A strength of this study is its randomised controlled design, particularly given the practical challenges associated with participant recruitment, randomisation, provision of food boxes and engagement of a socioeconomically disadvantaged population. Another strength is the inclusion of a follow‐up assessment, allowing exploration of the sustainability of potential intervention effects. In addition, the study assessed a broad range of outcomes, including feasibility, patient‐reported and health‐related outcomes, providing a comprehensive evaluation of both the implementation and potential impact of the intervention.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as this was a pilot study, the small sample size and relatively short intervention period limited statistical power and the ability to detect meaningful between‐group differences. Consequently, the exploratory effectiveness findings, particularly for HbA1c, should be interpreted with caution, as clinically meaningful improvements in glycaemic control may require larger sample sizes, longer follow‐up and more sustained dietary changes. A larger and longer RCT is needed to determine the clinical effectiveness of PRx. Second, the single‐site design may limit the generalisability of the findings to the broader Dutch and European populations. Nevertheless, the study population is representative of a diverse urban population typical of many large European cities, supporting the applicability of the findings to similar metropolitan settings. Third, blood samples were collected in primary care settings, resulting in a relatively high proportion of missing data, further reducing the precision of the exploratory analyses of glycaemic outcomes. Fourth, dietary intake was assessed using self‐reported food diaries, which are subject to recall bias, reporting bias and underreporting, particularly among individuals with overweight or obesity [47]. Fifth, blinding of participants and researchers was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, potentially introducing performance and reporting bias. Sixth, dropout rates were higher in the control group than in the intervention group, largely due to dissatisfaction with the randomisation outcome. Some participants felt deprived of the intervention, which may have influenced participant engagement and introduced attrition bias, thereby affecting the comparability of the groups and the interpretation of the exploratory effectiveness findings. Future studies should consider alternative designs, such as waitlist controls, to minimise allocation‐related attrition. Finally, we did not assess the extent to which participants consumed all foods provided in the boxes. As food consumption occurred in the home environment, adherence could not be objectively verified. Nevertheless, the observed increases in vegetable and fruit intake, combined with the high proportion of participants who collected all scheduled food boxes, indicate good engagement with and adherence to the intervention.

To conclude, this pilot trial demonstrates that a three‐month PRx offering plant‐based food boxes is feasible among adults with T2D and low SES. As the first European RCT evaluating a PRx intervention in this population, it provides important evidence on the feasibility and potential impact of PRx beyond the United States context. While no definitive conclusions regarding effectiveness can be drawn, exploratory between‐group comparisons generally favoured the PRx group for dietary, anthropometric and quality of life outcomes, with several differences appearing to persist at follow‐up. These findings provide a rationale for larger, adequately powered trials to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of PRx interventions. Importantly, PRx interventions may help address structural barriers to healthy eating and improve access to nutritious foods among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, thereby contributing to greater equity in diabetes care. In addition, the use of locally sourced plant‐based foods may offer opportunities to support both public health and environmental sustainability objectives. In future clinical practice, such programs could complement standard dietetic care by improving access to healthy foods and reducing inequities in nutrition‐related care, while also supporting more sustainable dietary patterns.
