Differences in the risk association of TERT rs2736098 (C > T) with lung cancer between Caucasian and Asian populations: A meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaozheng Wu, Wen Li, Ran Ding, Hongguan Jiao, Yihui Chai, Yunzhi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe T allele variant of TERT rs2736098 has been associated with an increased risk of lung cancer (LC), yet various studies have yielded inconsistent findings. The purpose of this study is to verify the association between the rs2736098 variant and LC risk, as well as to investigate the differences in this association across different ethnic groups (Caucasians and Asians), various LC subtypes, and distinct smoking statuses. A computer search was conducted for literatures published up to December 20, 2024, in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and MEDLINE databases. Data were analyzed using RevMan 5.3, while sensitivity analysis and publication bias were assessed using Stata 14.0. The stability of the results was evaluated with TSA software Registration number: CRD420251030755. The T allele variant of rs2736098 was associated with an increased risk of LC ([OR] = 1.22, 95%CI [1.18, 1.27]), and this association was observed in both Caucasians ([OR] = 1.17, 95%CI [1.14, 1.20]) and Asians ([OR] = 1.26, 95%CI [1.19, 1.34]), with a stronger association in Asians than in Caucasians (Subgroup differences: P = 0.03, I2 = 79.7%). In specific LC subtypes, rs2736098[T] was linked to the risk of NSCLC and LUAD in both Caucasians and Asians (P < 0.05), and the strength of the association with NSCLC and LUAD was greater in Asians than in Caucasians (Subgroup differences: P < 0.05, I2 > 50%). Additionally, rs2736098[T] was associated with the risk of SCLC and LUSC in Asians (P < 0.05), with a similar strength of association for NSCLC and SCLC (subgroup differences: P = 0.87, I2 = 0%), but a stronger association for LUAD than for LUSC (subgroup differences: P = 0.01, I2 = 84.7%). The rs2736098[T] variant was also associated with the risk of LC in both Caucasian smokers/non-smokers and Asian smokers/non-smokers (P < 0.05), and the strength of the association did not vary between Caucasian smokers/non-smokers and Asian smokers/non-smokers (P ≥ 0.05, Subgroup differences: I2 < 50%). Within LC subtypes, rs2736098[T] was primarily associated with the risk of NSCLC in Asian smokers ([OR] = 1.59, 95%CI[1.19, 2.12]) and the risk of LUAD in Asian non-smokers ([OR] = 1.43, 95%CI[1.17, 1.75]). The T allele variant of rs2736098 is associated with LC risk, and Asians have a higher risk of LC, NSCLC and LUAD due to the rs2736098 variant compared to Caucasians. Therefore, there is a difference in the strength of the association between the two populations for LC risk due to the rs2736098 variant. Additionally, regardless of whether Caucasians or Asians are smokers, they are likely to be at risk for LC due to the variant in rs2736098.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25536-w.Keywords: TERT, rs2736098, Lung cancer, Meta-AnalysisSubject terms: Cancer, Genetics, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is a disease with high global morbidity and mortality1, and smoking is a major risk factor for its development2. It is well known that the act of smoking, either direct or secondhand, greatly increases an individual’s likelihood of developing LC. In addition, exposure to various environmental factors such as cooking fumes, dust, asbestos, radon, and air pollution also play a crucial role in the etiology of LC3–6. These environmental exposures can lead to the onset and progression of the disease. However, in addition to the well-known risks associated with smoking and environmental factors, genetic variants have emerged as another important risk factor for LC development. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) conducted in different countries and different ethnic groups have identified multiple risk loci in the Telomerase reverse transcriptase—Cleft lip and cleft palate transmembrane protein 1 (TERT-CLPTM1L) region on chromosome 5p15.33 that are strongly associated with LC susceptibility7–15. These genetic markers were found to be associated with an increased risk of developing LC, suggesting that genetic susceptibility may be a key factor in the development of LC.

The results of the study revealed that telomeres located at the ends of chromosomes are composed of a series of repetitive sequences known as “TTAGGG”. It was observed that the length of these telomeres gradually shortens with each round of cell division. This gradual shortening process continues until it eventually leads to a state known as replicative senescence, which is characterized by cell cycle arrest16–19. It is worth noting that telomeres are able to maintain the ability of cells to continue replicating by extending their length through a specialized ribonucleoprotein complex known as telomerase20,21. However, in human cancer cells, telomerase is frequently activated. This activation can bypass the initial growth arrest and allow cancer cells to continue dividing uncontrollably22. Unrestricted cell growth and proliferation following telomerase activation is considered to be one of the main clinical features of cancer23–25. In addition, it has been shown that longer telomeres promote the survival of cells undergoing oncogenic DNA alterations, thus playing an important role in promoting tumorigenesis26–28. Telomerase is a complex enzyme that plays a crucial role in maintaining chromosome integrity during cell division. It consists of two main components: the catalytic protein subunit encoded by the TERT gene and the RNA template encoded by the telomerase RNA component, commonly referred to as Telomerase RNA component (TERC). Specifically, the TERT protein is located on the short arm of chromosome 5 at position 5p15.33. This gene is responsible for encoding the catalytic subunit of telomerase, which is essential for enzyme activity29.The protein product of the TERT gene adds the “TTAGG” repeat sequence to the end of the chromosome, a process known as telomere elongation. This elongation is essential for maintaining telomere length, ensuring chromosome stability and promoting cell proliferation30,31. Studies have shown that the TERT gene is frequently overexpressed in LC tissues, which may shed light on the underlying mechanisms contributing to the risk of LC development32.Variants in the promoter region of the TERT gene are thought to be an important factor in the up-regulation of telomerase activity, which is required for the stabilization of telomere length33. This phenomenon is often observed in cancer cells23. Polymorphisms in the TERT gene have been reported to be associated with changes in telomere length34–36. Interestingly, studies have shown that people with longer telomeres have a higher risk of LC37–39. In addition, many of these single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are present in the exon, intron, or promoter regions of the TERT gene40.

Previous studies have reported associations between multiple independent SNP variants in the TERT gene and LC risk, such as rs2736098, rs2736100, rs2736108, rs2853669, and rs28536769,11–15. Among these, rs2736098 and rs2736100 have been the most extensively studied. Our previous study has already provided a detailed analysis of the association between rs2736100 and LC risk41. The rs2736098 (C > T) polymorphism is situated within the second intron of the TERT gene, which is a crucial SNP within this genetic sequence. A number of previous GWAS42,43 have identified a significant association between the minor T allele variant at the TERT rs2736098 locus and the susceptibility to LC. These studies have observed that there is an elevated frequency of the T allele in both Caucasian and Asian populations. It has been reported that T allele variant of rs2736098 may lead to overexpression of TERT and increase telomerase activity, which regulates the development of LC by regulating unlimited cell division and carcinogenesis, and interact with activation of glycolytic pathways44. Evidence from Phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project45 and a literature46 showed that rs2736098 was moderately linkage disequilibrium (LD) in Asians (East Asians: r2 = 0.3070) and weakly LD in Caucasians (Europeans: r2 = 0.1504). These findings indicate that the TERT rs2736098[T] variant may potentially elevate the risk of developing LC by modulating telomerase activity. However, subsequent case–control studies conducted in Caucasians47 or in Asians48–50 did not consistently replicate this risk association. The discrepancies in these results could be attributed to a variety of factors, including but not limited to differences in ethnic backgrounds, geographical locations, methodologies employed in the studies, the sizes of the sample populations, the specific types of LC being investigated, the smoking habits of the patients with LC, and the patterns of genetic linkage disequilibrium that may vary among different populations. In addition, most of the association between rs2736098 variant and the risk of developing LC studies have been conducted in a certain type of ethnic group, such as either only in Caucasians29,42,47,51 or only in Asians48–50,52–59. Therefore, this difference in the association between the risk of developing LC between different ethnic populations is unclear. In addition, there were some studies that only reported the results of Non-small-cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) but not other LC subtypes such as Small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC)56, some studies reported that the T allele variant was associated with Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) but not Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC)54, and some studies reported that the T allele variant was associated with LC Smokers but not LC Non-smokers49. Thus, the differences in the association of the T allele variant of rs2736098 with the risk of LC development in different subtypes and smoking status are not clear. While meta-analyses are well positioned to address these issues through their powerful merging capabilities60, most of the previous meta-analyses are dated from today, and they didn’t incorporate the results of newer GWAS or case–control studies61–63. In addition, a recent large-scale meta-analysis of risk associations for various cancers46 confirmed the association of eight loci, including rs2736098, in the TERT-CLPTM1L region with the risk of LC, but the study didn’t seem to report the effects of smoking or environmental factors on LC as these risk factors are the main causes of it2. Therefore, the results of the association between the TERT rs2736098 polymorphism and the risk of developing LC, including LC subtypes, are still lacking.

The current meta-analysis encompassed a comprehensive collection of data derived from GWAS as well as case–control studies that have reported on the association of the TERT rs2736098 (C > T) polymorphism with LC. The primary objective was to elucidate and provide a clearer understanding of the potential relationship between the rs2736098 polymorphism and the susceptibility to developing LC. Furthermore, the study aimed to investigate the variations in this association across different patient populations characterized by distinct ethnic backgrounds, including Caucasians and Asians. Additionally, the research sought to assess whether there are notable differences in the risk associated with the rs2736098 polymorphism among individuals diagnosed with various subtypes of LC. Lastly, the study also aimed to explore the impact of smoking status on the association, considering the significant role that smoking plays in the development of LC.

Data and methods

This study has been registered in PROSPERO(https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/ prospero/), registration number: CRD420251030755.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① The type of studies included in this analysis should be exclusively GWAS or case–control studies that specifically investigate the TERT rs2736098 (C > T) polymorphism and its association with susceptibility to LC. These studies must be published in the English language, and they should focus on ethnic groups that are either Caucasian or Asian. Furthermore, the method used for the genetic assay of the TERT gene polymorphism must be clearly and accurately described in the study; ② For the purpose of this analysis, allele frequency data from the included studies will be utilized to calculate Odds ratios (OR) as a measure of effect size, along with their corresponding 95% Confidence interval (95% CI) to assess the precision of the effect estimate; ③ The genotype frequency distribution among the control groups in the selected studies must adhere to the principles of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE)64. This is a fundamental principle in population genetics that assumes allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of other evolutionary influences; ④ Additionally, the studies must have a score of no less than 7 on the Newcastle Ottawa scale (NOS)65, which is a validated scale used to assess the quality of non-randomized studies, including case–control and cohort studies. A score of 7 or above indicates that the study has met certain quality criteria in terms of selection of the study groups, comparability of the groups, and ascertainment of either the exposure or outcome of interest.

Exclusion criteria: ① Studies that do not provide allele data, which are essential for genetic analysis and cannot be omitted; ② Studies that fall into the categories of reviews, meta-analyses, conference reports, and case reports, as these types of publications often do not contain original data and are not suitable for the primary data analysis required for this study; ③ Studies that focus on pedigree analysis, as the emphasis here is on individual-level genetic data rather than family-based genetic structures; ④ Instances where the same study has been published multiple times, in such cases, only the version that contains the most comprehensive and up-to-date data will be included to ensure the accuracy and relevance of the information used in the analysis.

Outcomes

The pre-specified primary outcomes were to investigate whether the rs2736098 (C > T) variant was associated with LC risk in the overall populations. The secondary outcomes were to determine whether there were differences in the strength of the association between the two populations (Caucasians and Asians) for rs2736098 (C > T) and LC (including the various subtypes and smoking statuses).

Retrieval strategy

Relevant literatures in PubMed, EMbase, Web of Science, MEDLINE databases published before December 20, 2024 were searched by theme words and keywords. The language was limited to English. Search terms: “Lung cancer” OR “LC” AND “rs2736098” OR “TERT” AND “polymorphism” (Table S1 in supplemental content). In addition to the electronic database search, a manual search of the bibliographies of the included studies and related reviews was conducted to identify other relevant studies. This comprehensive approach ensured that all potentially eligible studies were considered for inclusion in the review.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers, who are relatively independent from each other (Xiaozheng Wu and Wen Li), undertook the task of conducting a comprehensive literature search and screening process, adhering to the predefined inclusion criteria. After independently completing their respective searches, they engaged in a cross-checking procedure to ensure consistency and to discuss any discrepancies that arose. In cases where there were differing opinions on the suitability of certain literatures for inclusion, a third party (Yunzhi Chen) was brought in to make the final determination. Furthermore, for those literatures that presented with incomplete data, the researchers made a concerted effort to reach out to the authors via e-mail, seeking to obtain the missing information to ensure the comprehensiveness of the data. Following this rigorous process, data extraction was performed on the literatures that were ultimately selected. The data extracted encompassed a variety of details, such as the names of the authors, the year of publication, the country of origin, the ethnicity of the subjects involved, their smoking status, the type of LC being studied, the number of cases in both the case and control groups, the frequency of each genotype within these groups, and the OR along with the 95% CI for each genotype.

Literature quality evaluation

The quality of the included literature was evaluated in the NOS65 (Xiaozheng Wu and Wen Li), and those with a score of no less than 7 were considered as literatures with high-quality.

Statistical methods

The HWE of the genotypes among the control groups was meticulously assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test within the statistical software package SPSS version 24.0. Every outcome was methodically tallied and subjected to a thorough analysis with the aid of Revman 5.3. This involved the creation of forest plots to visually represent the comparative effect sizes and funnel plots to detect any potential publication bias or small-study effects. Given that the studies included in this meta-analysis were conducted across populations with varying genetic backgrounds, it was deemed appropriate to employ random effects models for the statistical analysis of all results. The magnitude and significance of the statistical outcomes were conveyed through the OR value and the corresponding 95% CI. To investigate the presence of heterogeneity among the studies, Q-tests were conducted, and the extent of heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. A P-value greater than 0.05 or an I2 value less than 50% suggested that there was no significant heterogeneity across all studies or within any of the subgroups examined.

To further evaluate the potential publication bias among the studies, Begg’s Test and Egger’s Test were systematically carried out using the statistical software Stata version 14.0. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was conducted to scrutinize the outcomes of the statistical analysis in instances where greater heterogeneity was observed. The trial sequential analysis (TSA) software version 0.9.5.10 was utilized to perform TSA tests, which aimed to assess the stability of the conclusions drawn from the meta-analysis. The parameters set for the TSA tests included a Type I error probability of 5%, a statistical test power of 80%, and a relative risk reduction of 20%, ensuring a robust evaluation of the evidence’s stability and the potential impact of random errors.

Results

Literature search results

During the initial review process, we examined 592 literatures from four databases. After a rigorous screening process, 17 studies involving 16 literatures were finally included in the analysis, which included 5 studies in Caucasian populations29,42,43,47,51 and 12 studies in Asian populations43,48–50,52–59 (Table S2-S3 in supplemental content). The included studies covered 46,270 LC patients and 53,303 healthy controls, with 33,127 patients in the Caucasian population and 13,143 patients in the Asian population. In addition, the studies included 2,172 LC patients who were smokers and 3,687 LC patients who were non-smokers. Flow charts of the studies were created in accordance with the PRISMA statement to ensure transparency and integrity of the studies66 (Fig. 1).

PRISMA literature screening flow diagram.

Quality evaluation

All seventeen of the studies that were evaluated had high NOS65 assessment scores, with each study scoring 7 or above on the scale (Table S4 in supplemental content). This indicates that all of these studies were deemed to be at a low risk of bias, according to the NOS criteria.

Meta-analysis

The results indicated that the T allele variant of rs2736098 (allelic model) was associated with an increased risk of developing LC in the overall population ([OR] = 1.22, 95%CI [1.18, 1.27]). This association was observed in both Caucasians ([OR] = 1.17, 95%CI [1.14, 1.20]) and Asians ([OR] = 1.26, 95%CI [1.19, 1.34]), with a stronger association in Asians compared to Caucasians (Caucasians: [OR] = 1.17 vs. Asians: [OR] = 1.26, subgroup differences: P = 0.03, I2 = 79.7%) (Table 1, Fig. 2). In the additive (TT vs.CC), heterozygous (TC vs.CC), dominant (TT + TC vs.CC), and recessive (TT vs.TC + CC) models, the variant of rs2736098 was linked to the risk of LC in the overall population (TT vs.CC: [OR] = 1.70, 95%CI [1.51, 1.91]; TC vs.CC: [OR] = 1.17, 95%CI [1.06, 1.29]; TT + TC vs.CC: [OR] = 1.27, 95%CI [1.15, 1.40]; TT vs.TC + CC: [OR] = 1.59, 95%CI [1.42, 1.78]). These associations were predominantly found in Asian populations (p < 0.05) rather than in Caucasian populations (P > 0.05) (Table S5 and Figure S1-S4 in supplemental content).

Across LC subtypes, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with the risk of NSCLC development in the overall population ([OR] = 1.31, 95%CI [1.21, 1.41]), and this association was present in both Caucasians ([OR] = 1.21, 95%CI [1.17, 1.26]) and Asians ([OR] = 1.34, 95%CI [1.22, 1.46]), with the strength of the association being higher in Asians than in Caucasians (Caucasians: [OR] = 1.21 vs. Asians: [OR] = 1.34, subgroup differences: P = 0.04, I2 = 75.3%) (Table 1, Figure S5 in supplemental content). Additionally, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with the risk of SCLC development in Asians ([OR] = 1.31, 95% CI [1.01, 1.69]) (Table 1, Figure S6 in supplemental content). A comparison of the strength of association between NSCLC and SCLC revealed that the rs2736098[T] variant didn’t differ in its association with the risk of developing NSCLC in Asians compared with SCLC (NSCLC: [OR] = 1.34 vs. SCLC: [OR] = 1.31, subgroup differences: P = 0.87, I2 = 0%) (Table 1).

Across NSCLC subtypes, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with the risk of developing LUAD in the overall population ([OR] = 1.33, 95%CI [1.23, 1.44]), and this association was present in both Caucasians ([OR] = 1.21, 95%CI [1.17, 1.26]) and Asians ([OR] = 1.37, 95%CI [1.27, 1.48]), with the strength of the association being stronger in Asians than in Caucasians (Caucasians: [OR] = 1.21 vs. Asians: [OR] = 1.37, subgroup differences: P = 0.006, I2 = 86.9%) (Table 1, Figure S7 in supplemental content). Furthermore, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with the risk of developing LUSC in Asians ([OR] = 1.14, 95%CI [1.01, 1.28]) (Table 1, Figure S8 in supplemental content). A comparison of the strength of association between LUAD and LUSC indicated that the rs2736098[T] variant was more strongly associated with the risk of developing LUAD than LUSC in Asians (LUAD: [OR] = 1.37 vs. LUSC: [OR] = 1.14, subgroup differences: P = 0.01, I2 = 84.7%) (Table 1).

Analysis of smoking status

In the overall population, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with an increased risk of developing LC in both smokers ([OR] = 1.32, 95%CI [1.13, 1.53]) and non-smokers ([OR] = 1.26, 95%CI [1.13, 1.40]). The strength of this association didn’t differ significantly between smokers and non-smokers (Smoking: [OR] = 1.32 vs. Non-smoking: [OR] = 1.26, Subgroup differences: P = 0.64, I2 = 0%) (Table 2, Fig. 3). In Caucasians, the rs2736098[T] variant was associated with an increased risk of developing LC in both smokers ([OR] = 1.21, 95% CI [1.04, 1.41]) and non-smokers ([OR] = 1.22, 95% CI [1.13, 1.31]), with no significant difference in the strength of this association between smokers and non-smokers (Smoking: [OR] = 1.21 vs. Non-smoking: [OR] = 1.22, Subgroup differences: P = 0.95, I2 = 0%) (Table 2, Figure S9 in supplemental content). In Asians, the rs2736098[T] variant was also associated with an increased risk of developing LC in smokers ([OR] = 1.45, 95%CI [1.18, 1.77]) and non-smokers ([OR] = 1.34, 95%CI [1.05, 1.71]), and again, the strength of this association didn’t differ between smokers and non-smokers (Smoking: [OR] = 1.45 vs. Non-smoking: [OR] = 1.34, Subgroup differences: P = 0.65, I2 = 0%) (Table 2, Figure S10 in supplemental content). Comparisons between the two populations indicated that the strength of this association was similar for Caucasian smokers and Asian smokers (Smoking Caucasians: [OR] = 1.21 vs. Smoking Asians: [OR] = 1.45, Subgroup differences: P = 0.17, I2 = 47.8%), and similar for Caucasian non-smokers and Asian non-smokers (Non-smoking Caucasians: [OR] = 1.22 vs. Non-smoking Asians: [OR] = 1.34, Subgroup differences: P = 0.45, I2 = 0%) (Table 2).

In LC subtypes, the rs2736098[T] variant was primarily associated with an increased risk of NSCLC incidence among Asian smokers ([OR] = 1.59, 95%CI [1.19, 2.12]), whereas no significant association was observed in Asian non-smokers ([OR] = 1.21, 95%CI [0.95, 1.54]) (Table 2, Figures S11 in supplemental content). Among the various NSCLC subtypes, the rs2736098[T] variant was primarily linked to an elevated risk of developing LUAD in Asian non-smokers ([OR] = 1.43, 95% CI [1.17, 1.75]), but not with the risk of developing LUSC in the same group ([OR] = 0.96, 95% CI [0.68, 1.36]) (Table 2, Figures S12 in supplemental content).

Heterogeneity analysis

In the comprehensive analysis of the data, it was observed that a significant level of heterogeneity, characterized by an I2 value of 50% or greater, was predominantly present in the Asian population (Table 1–2, Table S5 in supplemental content). The potential reasons behind this phenomenon could be multifaceted and may be associated with a variety of factors. These factors encompass disparities in sample sizes across different studies, variations in the countries where the research was conducted, differences in the methodologies employed by the studies, the use of various genetic testing methods, and the varying smoking statuses of the participants within these Asians.

Publication bias

Most funnel plots seem to look as if they are symmetrical (Figures S13a-e and Figures S14a-d in supplemental content). In LC, there was a certain degree of publication bias (PBegg = 0.025, PEgger = 0.017) in the T vs.C results of the overall population for reasons related to different ethnic groups and sample sizes (Table 1). In addition, the Begg’s test results of T vs. C (Asians) and TT + TC vs. CC (Overall population) were less than 0.05, but their Egger’s test results were greater than 0.05 (Table 1, Tables S5 in supplemental content). The Egger’s test results of TC vs. CC (Overall population) were less than 0.05, but its Begg’s test results were greater than 0.05 (Tables S5 in supplemental content). From these results, they cannot be considered biased. In LUAD, although the Egger’s test for T vs. C (Overall population) was less than 0.05, its Begg’s test was greater than 0.05 (Table 1). Therefore, it cannot be considered biased either. In addition, the results of all other models were not significantly biased (PEgger < 0.05 and PEgger < 0.05) (Table 1,Tables S5-S6 in supplemental content).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis results of LUSC, it was found that the results had a certain degree of variation after excluding the two studies (Zhang 201457 and Zhao 201458) respectively (Tables S14 and Figures S16c in supplemental content). These results demonstrate that the results of LUSC have a certain degree of sensitivity, and the reason may have a lot to do with the different genetic testing methods and sex ratio. The results of the sensitivity analyses for all other genetic models showed no significant sensitivity for any of the studies, suggesting that the results were not significantly different when any of the studies were excluded (Table S7-S13, Table S15, Figure S15a-e, Figure S16a-b and Figure S16d in supplemental content).

TSA

The analysis conducted by the TSA revealed that the Z-curve, represented by the blue line, successfully crossed not only the conventional boundary, which is indicated by the green dashed line, but also the boundary specific to the TSA, denoted by the red line (Figure S17a-e and Figure S18a-e in supplemental content). This crossing of boundaries serves as compelling evidence that the outcomes for the subgroups of LC, NSCLC, LUAD, LUSC, and the subgroups of LC patients who are smokers or non-smokers, are all stable and reliable.

Discussion

In some previous large GWAS42,43, the variant TERT 2736098 (C > T) had been identified as being associated with the risk of LC. It was observed that an elevated frequency of the minor T allele of rs2736098 was linked to an increased risk of LC by enhancing the activity of telomerase. However, several other studies had presented results that were inconsistent with these findings47–50. Consequently, the association of the variant of TERT 2736098 with the risk of LC currently lacked a unifying conclusion. The aim of this meta-analysis was to clarify its association with LC and to investigate the differences in this association among patients of different ethnicities, specifically Caucasians and Asians, across various subtypes of LC, and considering different smoking statuses.

The findings of the study revealed a significant association between the presence of the T allele variant of rs2736098 and the increased risk of LC development. This association was observed to be consistent across both Caucasian and Asian ethnic groups. Previous research conducted within these two populations had reported findings that were in line with the current study’s results29,42,43,51–59. The evidence gathered thus far supports the conclusion that the minor T allele variant of rs2736098 is a risk factor for LC in both Caucasians and Asians. Upon closer examination of the two populations, it was noted that the rs2736098[T] variant exhibited a stronger association with the risk of LC development in Asians compared to Caucasians. This suggests that the impact of the rs2736098 variant on the risk of LC development may vary between these two populations, with Asians potentially facing a greater risk of LC development due to this genetic variant. Furthermore, it was observed that in Caucasian populations, the variants of rs2736098 didn’t appear to be linked with the risk of LC development under any of the genetic models considered, including additive, heterozygous, dominant, and recessive models. This could imply that the variants of rs2736098 might not be associated with the risk of LC development in Caucasian populations. However, it is important to highlight that some studies lacked complete genotype data, which led to a limited number of Caucasian individuals being included in these models (n = 1), and a smaller sample size of patients overall. Consequently, these findings are not definitive and require further validation through the inclusion of additional studies to ensure the reliability of the conclusions drawn.

Our findings across various LC subtypes revealed that the T allele variant of rs2736098 is associated with an increased risk of developing NSCLC and LUAD in both Caucasians and Asians. These associations align with previous studies conducted within Caucasian43 and Asian populations43,54,56–59. The evidence underscores that both populations are susceptible to NSCLC and LUAD due to variants in rs2736098. Upon comparison, a stronger association between the T allele variant of rs2736098 and the risk of NSCLC and LUAD in Asians was observed than in Caucasians, suggesting a population-specific variance in the risk associated with rs2736098. Additionally, our results indicated an association between the T allele variant of rs2736098 and the risk of SCLC and LUSC in Asians. However, due to the constraints of the studies included, we didn’t examine the association of the T allele variant of rs2736098 with the risk of SCLC and LUSC in Caucasians. This suggests that the rs2736098[T] variant may not be associated with the risk of SCLC and LUSC in Caucasians, but further studies are needed to verify these potential associations. Comparisons between NSCLC and SCLC revealed an equivalent strength of association between the rs2736098 variant and the risk of developing NSCLC as that of SCLC in Asians. These results were largely consistent with those of previous studies conducted in Asians58. This suggests that both subtypes of LC carry a high risk of developing in Asians, and that the rs2736098 variant likely increases the risk of Asians developing NSCLC or SCLC. Comparisons between LUAD and LUSC indicated that the risk association for developing LUAD was stronger in Asian populations than for LUSC, with similar findings reported in previous studies53,54,58,59. These results imply that among the two different NSCLC subtypes, Asian populations face a higher risk of developing LUAD due to the rs2736098 variant. For Caucasians, we were unable to compare the strength of risk associations between these subtypes of LC due to insufficient data.

The outcomes of epidemiological studies have indicated that while smoking is recognized as a significant environmental risk factor for LC on a global scale, only a relatively small fraction of individuals who smoke actually develop LC over the course of their lives. On the other hand, a substantial number of LC cases occur in individuals with no history of smoking67,68. The LC observed in individuals who have never smoked presents differences from that seen in smokers, with a significant proportion of LC patients among non-smokers carrying genetic variants of oncogenes69. Previous research has demonstrated that genetic susceptibility to LC in non-smokers is linked to genetic variants that have implications for risk across various types of cancer, and that the interplay between genes and the environment plays a crucial role in the etiology of LC70. Investigations have revealed that a range of environmental risk factors, including smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, high consumption of red meat and fats, low intake of dietary fiber, and exposure to both indoor and outdoor air pollution, as well as chemicals and radiation, can result in genomic instability71–75. Genomic instability can give rise to nucleotide dysfunctions such as base substitutions, base deletions, nucleotide deletions, base pair insertions, or amplifications, which can subsequently cause DNA breaks, chromosome remodeling, or translocations. If these damages are not repaired, they can lead to irreversible cell mutations and sustained abnormal cell growth76,77. To ascertain whether smoking or non-smoking contributes to the TERT rs2736098 variant, which in turn influences the risk of developing LC, we conducted a subgroup analysis focusing on the smoking status of the patients involved.

We have found that the presence of the rs2736098[T] variant is associated with an increased risk of LC development in both individuals who smoke and those who do not smoke. Importantly, the magnitude of this association appears to be consistent across both groups, indicating that smoking is indeed a significant risk factor for LC in individuals with the rs2736098 variant. However, it is not the sole contributor to the risk, as non-smokers can also develop LC due to this genetic variant. Previous research has indicated that a variety of factors, including the patient’s level of education, Body mass index (BMI), a history of Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), occupational exposure to pesticides, exposure to high levels of cooking emissions, and dietary habits, are all associated with the development of LC78. Therefore, it’s evident that LC is a complex, multi-factorial disease that can be triggered by a combination of smoking, genetic predispositions, environmental exposures, and lifestyle choices. In the context of each ethnic group, we observed a consistent association between the variant in rs2736098 and LC susceptibility, irrespective of the smoking status. This pattern was evident in both Caucasian individuals who were smokers and those who were non-smokers, as well as in Asian individuals who smoked and those who didn’t. The strength of this association didn’t differ significantly between these subgroups. This finding is in line with previous studies that have reported similar results in Caucasian smokers51 and non-smokers42,51, as well as in Asian smokers49 and non-smokers59. The rs2736098[T] variant appears to increase LC risk in both Caucasian and Asian populations, irrespective of their smoking habits. Consequently, smoking continues to be a significant risk factor for the development of the rs2736098 variant, which can lead to LC in both Caucasians and Asians. However, it is also important to note that other factors, including non-smoking behaviors, can contribute to the emergence of the rs2736098 variant and subsequently result in LC among individuals of Caucasian and Asian.

Among the various LC subtypes, it’s discovered that the T allele variant of rs2736098 is associated with an increased risk of developing NSCLC primarily in Asian smokers, but not in Asian non-smokers. This suggests that the risk association with the T allele variant in Asian smokers may be predominantly linked to the NSCLC subtype. Previous studies have reported similar findings57. Consequently, smoking could be identified as the main risk factor for the rs2736098 variant, and thus for NSCLC among Asian populations. However, when examining the two subtypes of NSCLC, the T allele variant of rs2736098 was found to be mainly associated with the risk of developing LUAD in Asian non-smokers, rather than LUSC. This implies that the association between the rs2736098[T] variant and the risk of developing NSCLC in Asian non-smokers does not exist and may be mainly concentrated in the subtype of LUSC rather than LUAD. Asian non-smokers may likely to be at risk of developing LUAD due to T allele variants53. Previous studies have also identified non-tobacco related risk factors in the pathogenesis of NSCLC, such as exposure to cooking fumes, hormonal influences, and viral infections79. Subramanian80 has noted that LUAD is the most common type among non-smokers. Further from the results, the Asian non-smokers included in the LUAD subgroup analysis were all female (n = 317). Therefore, Asian females may be more likely to be at risk of developing LUAD due to factors such as non-smoking. Previous evidence suggested that LUAD was more common in women81,82. Patel et al. showed that among never-smoking patients with LC, female patients outnumbered males83. Evidence confirmed that common genetic variants in TERT-CLPTM1L were associated with the risk of developing LUAD in non-smoking Asian women84. This could be explained by the hypothesis that women are more likely to be exposed to secondhand smoke, to coal while cooking at home, and to hormone replacement therapy. All of these reasons may lengthen telomeres to avoid apoptosis, which ultimately leads to cancer85. However, it’s worth noting that due to the small number of studies included (n = 1) on LUAD or LUSC in Asian smokers/non-smokers, the statistical results between them may lead to Type I errors due to multiple comparisons. Therefore, these conclusions need to be further confirmed by additional research. In addition, due to the limitations of the included studies, we didn’t look for data on the association of the rs2736098[T] variant with the risk of developing various LC subtypes in Caucasian smokers or non-smokers, and further studies are needed for confirmation.

On the whole, this particular meta-analysis represents a more methodical and all-encompassing approach to evaluate the association between the genetic variant rs2736098 and the susceptibility to LC. The findings of this study not only corroborate the existence of disparities in LC risk between Caucasian and Asian ethnic groups but also offer a significant reference point for the early detection of LC, the prediction of LC risk, the modification of lifestyle practices, and the advancement of clinical interventions. Furthermore, the observed differences in LC risk between Caucasian and Asian populations hint at the possibility that there may be variations in the telomere lengths of these two demographic groups. Changes in telomere length, whether influenced by smoking or other non-smoking related factors, may promote the progression of LC. It is important to note, however, that these hypotheses require further validation through additional experimental research to substantiate these claims.

Limitations: 1) It’s important to note that all the data utilized in this meta-analysis were sourced from the reported outcomes of various studies. These studies were conducted across different countries and involved populations with diverse ethnic backgrounds. Consequently, a certain level of heterogeneity and potential publication bias is an unavoidable aspect of this research. Furthermore, the studies included in this meta-analysis varied in terms of their classification of LC subtypes, the smoking statuses of the participants, and the genetic testing and genotyping methods employed. These differences could also contribute to a certain degree of heterogeneity and publication bias; 2) While the overall sample size for this meta-analysis was deemed adequate, it became notably low after conducting various subgroup analyses, particularly for the SCLC subgroup. Additionally, although the current study has conducted a detailed examination of the impact of smoking or non-smoking on LC (encompassing each LC subtype), the sample size for the subgroup analysis based on smoking status was relatively small. Consequently, the reliability of the results concerning the association of smoking or non-smoking with LC risk (including each LC subtype) may be somewhat compromised; 3) Due to the limited number of reports available in the literature on other ethnic groups, such as African populations and mixed populations, the results for these groups were not included in this study. Therefore, the findings of this study cannot comprehensively represent the association of the rs2736098 variant with LC risk across all ethnicities; 4) It should be mentioned that the present study has only included literatures published in English language and hasn’t encompassed literature in other languages; 5) Although other SNPs in the TERT gene associated with LC risk have been reported, such as rs2736108, rs2853669, and rs2853676, our thorough data mining and analysis revealed that the number of studies incorporating these three SNPs was insufficient to conduct the meta-analysis effectively. Therefore, data from these SNP sites were excluded from the current analysis and will be supplemented in future studies.

Conclusion

The T allele variant of rs2736098 is associated with LC risk, and Asians have a higher risk of LC, NSCLC and LUAD due to the rs2736098 variant compared to Caucasians. Meanwhile, the T allele variant of rs2736098 is also associated with the risk of SCLC and LUSC in Asians, and the strength of the association is stronger for LUAD than for LUSC. Therefore, there is a difference in the strength of the association between the two populations for LC risk due to the rs2736098 variant. Additionally, whether Caucasians or Asians are smokers or not, they are likely to be at risk for LC due to the variant in rs2736098. Smoking may be the primary risk factor for NSCLC in Asians, while other factors such as non-smoking may be the main cause of LUAD in Asian women.
