Evidence-based One Health strategy is essential to eliminate rabies by 2030
Authors: Krishna Prasad Acharya, Ranjan K. Mohapatra, Snehasish Mishra, Priyabrata Pattnaik, Santosh Kumar Ranajit, Lawrence Sena Tuglo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rabies, a viral disease, spreads largely through bites or scratches from infected animals. It is a serious global public health threat. Although preventable, it can be fatal if timely medical support is not extended before symptoms appear. We highlight rabies elimination aligned with the UN’s “Zero by 30” goal through evidence-based One Health interventions. This will help public health practitioners and policymakers devise effective health and well-being interventions to contain the ailment, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Dear Editor,

Rabies is a major public health concern with recurring localized outbreaks, endemic chiefly to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Although canines (like dogs) are known as rabies virus reservoirs worldwide and are the primary transmitters, particularly in LMICs, the disease can also be transmitted to humans through badgers, bats, foxes, jackals, mongooses, raccoons, and skunks. While reports of wild animal transmission (such as by foxes and mongooses) are appreciably high, stray dogs remain the primary source of human transmission due to their close proximity to human habitats. About 59 000 humans and thousands of animals die from rabies each year, with the scenario being disproportionately severe in LMICs[1]. An ambitious “Zero by 30” goal was set by UN bodies and alliances, such as the World Organization for Animal Health, the Global Alliance for Rabies Control, and the World Health Organization (WHO), to address this concern and eliminate dog-mediated human rabies[2].

Global rabies control efforts were renewed and harmonized to achieve the “zero by 30” vision. The WHO recommended two major immunization strategies to prevent human rabies: the pre-exposure prophylaxis and the post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). The latest position paper of WHO stated that mass dog vaccination, with up to 70% coverage, interrupted RABV transmission in endemic areas at the source and saved human lives[3,4]. With the majority of the Asian and African LMICs (where rabies cases are high) struggling to move from paper to practice, the 2030 goal currently seems unachievable. For instance, the rabies control strategy “guiding document” is not yet endorsed in Nepal, due to which efforts have grossly failed[5]. Careful consideration of the prevailing situation and evaluation of available evidence need prioritization to realize the “zero by 30” goal. Considering the brief time left, the necessity of a quick revisit of the rabies control efforts and redesigning the roadmap was felt.

Guinea (a West African nation) collaborated with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention during 2023–2024 and successfully vaccinated around 88 000 animals (mostly dogs) across the identified 10 susceptible communities. Agencies used the innovative Worldwide Veterinary Service smartphone app to track the vaccinated and unvaccinated stray population and documented key data. This helped identify rabies-linked high-risk communities[6]. Integrated Bite Case Management is an effective rabies control and elimination strategy, although empirical evidence on its impact is limited[7]. Mass vaccination, improved PEP, robust surveillance, and active public awareness campaigns are suggested as effective rabies control interventions. Dog vaccination in southeast Tanzania led to a steady decline in the number of human rabies cases and cross-species transmission[8]. A Latin America survey opined that a 10% increase in dog vaccination reduced human infection and fatality by 12.4%[9].

Eliminating human rabies through an integrated One Health approach was successful in India (in Goa province), with the complete elimination of human rabies and a 92% reduction in canine rabies cases. This was achieved through 70% dog vaccination coverage and rabies education reaching 150 000 children each year[10]. An estimated 2249 disability-adjusted life years could be averted over the program period. It was important that non-governmental organizations (NGOs) were engaged to support rabies vaccination and awareness creation. The Rabies-Free Thiruvananthapuram project has been ongoing in Kerala province since 2023. It is supported by the Kerala government through its rabies control program and is executed by Compassion for Animals Welfare Association, an animal welfare NGO. Through the program, stray dogs are vaccinated, and ring vaccination procedures are carried out in areas with reported dog bites[11].

The lack of uninterrupted mass vaccination of dogs seems to be the major reason for the rabies virus persistently being transmitted[12]. Although rabies is vaccine-preventable, elimination efforts fail particularly in LMICs largely due to policy gaps and socioeconomic barriers, leading to implementation failure[13]. The One Health approach seems to be an effective rabies elimination strategy. However, for instance, the departments dealing with human health and animal health seem to operate in silos with no coordinated, effective, and sustained efforts. Rabies disease is often neglected and receives low political patronage, with the allocation of only minimal and inconsistent interim funds. Furthermore, regulation for pet ownership is inadequate, and the free-roaming stray dogs are poorly managed, thus contributing to a high unvaccinated dog population in the region. Additionally, there is a lack of accurate epidemiological data required for evidence-based policy formulation, as few cases are often unrecorded and underreported[14]. The true disease burden is underestimated due to weak surveillance systems. Vaccination campaigns, mostly in high-risk urban and peri-urban locations, leave vast rural regions vulnerable. Unless the dog population is duly vaccinated, the risk of rabies transmission remains high, as humans and dogs often dwell in close proximity. Although mass vaccination of dogs seems to be the most cost-effective strategy, the minimum 70% coverage (for herd immunity) is hard to attain due to several implementation bottlenecks. This allows the rabies virus to persist within the canine population.

Although the rabies vaccine is available in India, it often does not benefit the grossly vulnerable human population. Repeated vaccine stockouts due to logistical gaps and improperly administered PEP and immunoglobulins further obscure the scenario. Additionally, limited veterinary services in rural areas and the lack of awareness impact both dog and human vaccination drives. Furthermore, poverty and costly private care, alongside the prevalence of misconceptions, lead to people not availing themselves of necessary healthcare. A study on the mass vaccination of dogs in rural Uganda observed that limited awareness among rural communities, logistical challenges, a scarce veterinary workforce, and the high cost of private vaccination were significant barriers[15]. As a result, an estimated 10% of the dog population in Uganda was vaccinated, making it a critical regional public healthcare issue.

Similarly, underreporting of rabies cases, gaps in wound management at healthcare facilities and/or households, and persistent stockouts of vaccines were major challenges in eliminating rabies in Kenya, Africa[14]. Healthcare workers may need proper training on case management of animal bites through rabies control education forums and information exchange for improved One Health. Remaining mostly a neglected disease, most rabies-related criticality begins at home. Poor municipal sanitation favors the rise in stray dog populations[16]. Clinically unverified practices, such as the use of traditional herbal remedies (instead of PEP/RIG), prevent prompt medical intervention, thus contributing to the complexity of the rabies crisis. Studies indicated that, due to the knowledge gap, particularly in LMICs, there was a need for education and awareness building, including wound treatment and PEP administration, to combat rabies[17].

Globally, wildlife such as bats, foxes, jackals, badgers, mongooses, and skunks are equally important agents of rabies transmission in humans, as they carry the rabies virus[18]. A critical gap in rabies control is the overlooked role of wildlife in transmitting rabies. There is growing evidence of critical socioeconomic and cultural factors (including geopolitical, sociocultural setups, and ecological diversity) with a high cost-benefit ratio in rabies control[19], which are often neglected in rabies control programs. Human–animal–environment coexistence is central to preventing and controlling any zoonotically transmitted disease. Rabies control programs are usually modelled based on the experiences of high-income nations, with minimal consideration of scenarios in LMICs. Although evidence is substantial, rabies control programs often ignore the roles of sociocultural factors when designing effective action plans that are specific to the local-level. The limited efficacy and unsustainable rabies control programs in LMICs threaten the “Zero by 30” goal. The stray dog factor is practically treated as a standalone strategy, which calls for One Health models. The “One Health” framework recognizes the interconnectedness of humans and animals in a shared environment and strives to achieve a natural balance. Integrating advanced technologies like artificial intelligence (AI) in to the One Health approach to control rabies can help create effective, data-driven policy interventions[20]. Large language models and ChatGPT have emerged as leveraged opportunities across the entire healthcare system with respect to infectious diseases in recent times and are gaining global attention at research and policymaking levels[21]. Advanced rabies prevention and control programs that integrate such state-of-the-art technologies as AI will undoubtedly benefit the endeavor and provide better human well-being solutions. They will also provide a platform for seamless multidisciplinary and cross-disciplinary collaboration across line departments.

As the “zero by 30” deadline for zero human deaths due to canine-mediated rabies looms large, grassroots-level rabies control approaches, especially in the LMICs, are necessary. Feasibility assessments of the cost-efficacy, political, and behavioral variability at the local level are needed before devising interventions, without which the advocacy of the measures becomes challenging. An effective rabies control program in varying sociocultural settings calls for a multidisciplinary approach to generate enough evidence-based, robust data to guide targeted interventions. Generating robust evidence and designing evidence-based prioritized actions would help improve our progress towards the “zero by 30” goal.
