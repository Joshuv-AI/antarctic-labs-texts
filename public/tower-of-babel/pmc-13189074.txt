Effects of TUS-NMES Combined with Glucocorticoid on Clinical Outcomes and Rehabilitation Efficacy of Neurosyphilis Patients with Cerebral Infarction
Authors: Jun Dai, Fanghua He, Yating Xu, Na Lei, Dan Zhang, Wenchao He, Fengfeng Zhu, Shuangshuang Chen, Xinyi Shan, Minzhi Wu, Xuehua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the effects of transcranial ultrasound-neuromuscular electrical stimulation (TUS-NMES) combined with glucocorticoid (GC) as adjunctive intervention to standard penicillin therapy on clinical outcomes and rehabilitation efficacy in neurosyphilis (NS) patients with cerebral infarction.MethodsA total of 309 NS patients with cerebral infarction were randomly divided into general group (N = 79, penicillin), GC group (N = 77, penicillin and GC), TUS-NMES group (N = 75, penicillin and TUS-NMES), and combination group (N = 78, penicillin and GC & TUS-NMES). Baseline data, clinical symptoms, laboratory indicators, Fugl-Meyer assessment scale (FMA), and National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) scores before and after treatment were compared. Twelve-month rehabilitation outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves.ResultsNo serious adverse events occurred during treatment; only mild, transient discomforts, including slight scalp tingling, local muscle tremor, headache, and insomnia, were reported, all of which were alleviated after timely management and did not significantly affect the treatment process or patient recovery. After treatment, all groups showed significant improvement (P < 0.05), with the combination group demonstrating the greatest effect, including prolonged prothrombin time, higher nitric oxide, lower NIHSS, and higher FMA scores. At 12 months, the combination group (43.59%) showed a higher treatment improvement rate than the general (25.32%) and GC (27.27%) groups (P < 0.05), as well as the optimal recovery effect.ConclusionTUS-NMES combined with GC, as an adjunct to standard penicillin therapy, improves clinical symptoms, coagulation, and neurological function, and enhances rehabilitation outcomes in NS patients with cerebral infarction.

Introduction

Syphilis, caused by Treponema pallidum, is a chronic systemic infection transmitted sexually and vertically, with high incidences in low- and middle-income regions.1,2 Its clinical spectrum ranges from primary and secondary stages to latent and tertiary disease, with neurosyphilis (NS) representing a severe manifestation involving the central nervous system.3,4 The forms of NS include syphilitic meningitis, meningovascular syphilis, tabetic syphilis, general paresis, and limbic encephalitis.5–7 Treponema pallidum disseminates to the cerebrospinal fluid, with distinct clinical presentations dependent on immune reaction and invading location.8 Notably, patients with syphilis have an increased risk of ischemic stroke, and meningovascular NS is a recognized cause of cerebral infarction.9,10 Cerebral infarction complicated with syphilis infection is extremely difficult to manage, and delayed treatment may lead to irreversible neurological injury, necessitating the exploration of more effective and helpful therapies for this event.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) may have positive effects on metabolic function and neuroplasticity, increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) release, and enhance cognitive performance.11,12 Traditional NMES exhibits insufficient targeting of deep brain structures and limited spatial specificity. Therefore, transcranial ultrasound (TUS) has emerged as a promising non-invasive and safe approach for modulating deep brain activity.13 Recent evidence suggests that TUS combined with NMES (TUS-NMES) can simultaneously stimulate central and peripheral pathways, improving motor control and facilitating neuronal recovery after injury.14,15 Moreover, TUS offers targeted neuromodulation based on lesion location and may aid in functional recovery following central nervous system injury, including ischemic damage.16–19

Benzathine penicillin G has demonstrated proven efficacy against syphilis20,21 and is considered the first-line drug for all stages of syphilis, particularly in the absence of an effective vaccine.22 However, the use of penicillin in NS requires prolonged intravenous administration, and treatment limitations persist, including allergy, adherence challenges, and occasional therapeutic failure.23 In latent infection, the low replication rate of Treponema pallidum and the short half-life of penicillin may further compromise efficacy, necessitating prolonged or combination therapy.22 A recent study has shown that glucocorticoid (GC) combined with penicillin can reduce inflammation, thereby arresting further nerve damage.24 Corticosteroid treatment may also decrease the risk and severity of Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction in patients with syphilitic brain abscess.25 Moreover, corticosteroid combined with penicillin treatment could ameliorate neurological symptoms and reduce sequelae in early NS.26 Given these considerations, this study investigates the effects of TUS-NMES combined with GC as adjuncts to standard penicillin therapy on the clinical outcomes and rehabilitation efficacy in patients with NS complicated with cerebral infarction, thus offering new insight into more effective therapies for the management of NS patients.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was ratified by the Ethics Committee of The Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou / The Affiliated Infectious Diseases Hospital, Suzhou Medical College of Soochow University, approval number: SZWY2023-0913-03. All patients voluntarily signed an informed consent.

Sample Size Estimation

Sample size was estimated using G*Power (3.0.10; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) (Supplementary Figure 1A). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was selected, with an expected effect size (f) of 0.25, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and statistical power (1-β err prob) of 0.95 across the four groups. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 280 participants to ensure adequate statistical power.

Participants

NS patients with cerebral infarction (N = 339) hospitalized in The Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou / The Affiliated Infectious Diseases Hospital, Suzhou Medical College of Soochow University from March 2018 to June 2024 were enrolled, among which 4 patients were unwilling to participate in the study, and 19 patients were excluded following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 316 patients were included as study subjects. The enrolled patients were classified into four groups in a ratio of 1:1:1:1 by using the random number method: the general treatment group, the GC treatment group, the TUS-NMES treatment group, and the TUS-NMES combined with GC treatment group (the combined treatment group). During the study, 7 patients were excluded due to withdrawal, loss to follow-up, or incomplete data. Ultimately, 309 patients completed the entire study protocol and were included in this research analysis, comprising the general treatment group (N = 79), the GC treatment group (N = 77), the TUS-NMES treatment group (N = 75), and the combined treatment group (N = 78). Post hoc power analysis (1-β err prob) validation showed a post hoc power of 0.97 (Supplementary Figure 1B), which significantly exceeds the conventional threshold of 0.8, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to detect intergroup differences and that the study findings were statistically reliable.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: 1. The patients met the diagnostic criteria of NS with cerebral infarction:27 (1) patients with an epidemiologic history and positive serum treponemapallidum particle agglutination or rapid plasmareagin circlecard test (RPR); (2) the involvement of meningeal vessels was confirmed by craniocerebral imaging (computed tomography and/or magnetic resonance imaging) in The Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou/The Affiliated Infectious Diseases Hospital, Suzhou Medical College of Soochow University or other hospitals, with the presence of cerebral infarct lesions in the corresponding vascular supplying areas and clinical manifestations of motor and sensory deficits, ie, unilateral motor and/or sensory deficits of the upper limbs and/or the lower limbs; 2. The patient’s cognitive and verbal level was acceptable, with no obvious emotional disorders, and the patient was able to actively cooperate with treatment; 3. The patient had no significant cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, with stable blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and body temperature.

The exclusion criteria: 1. Those combined with bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, cryptococcal meningitis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, toxoplasmic encephalopathy, and other diseases; 2. Those with severe cardiac, hepatic, renal, and other organ dysfunction at the time of admission, or those who were critically ill or died within 24 hours of admission; 3. Those who terminated active treatment within 28 days; 4. Penicillin allergic individuals.

Treatment Method

Patients in the general treatment group were given 4.0 million U penicillin (a common essential drug, Shandong Lukang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shandong, China; 1.6 million IU/branch). Treatment was given every four hours for 14 days. At the end of the above treatment, 2.4 million U benzathine penicillin (a common essential drug, North China Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Hebei, China; 1.2 million IU/branch) was continued to be given (intramuscular injection) for a total of 3 times. For stroke, all patients were given secondary prevention of stroke and management of underlying diseases (eg, blood pressure, blood glucose level). Based on a comprehensive admission assessment, a routine rehabilitation training program is established, including passive stretching for antispasmodic therapy, strength and endurance training, training of balance and coordination of sitting and standing, training of standing and walking, as well as exercise therapy. Exercise therapy was performed for 45 minutes each time, once a day, 5 times a week for 28 days.

Based on the general treatment, patients in the GC treatment group received oral GCs (prednisone, Zhejiang Xianju Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Medical Products Administration Approval Number: H33021207, specification: 5 mg × 100 tablets/bottle) at a dose of 30 mg/day (10 mg per dose, three times a day) starting one day before treatment and continued for three consecutive days, after which the medication was discontinued.

Based on the general treatment, patients in the TUS-NMES treatment group received a TUS-NMES therapeutic device (UE860A) once a day for 30 minutes each session, five days a week. A course of treatment consisted of 10 sessions, and patients underwent two consecutive courses.

Based on the general treatment, patients in the combined treatment group received the same treatment as the GC treatment group; they also received the same treatment as the TUS-NMES treatment group.

To ensure treatment standardization across all participants, all medications in this study were prepared by uniformly trained pharmacists to guarantee accurate drug dosage and concentration. A UE860A therapeutic apparatus was used with fixed parameter settings: a frequency of 20 Hz, an intensity of 60% motor threshold, 30 minutes per session, five times per week, for two consecutive courses. A standardized rehabilitation training manual was developed to clearly define the operational specifications, intensity, and duration of each training exercise, and rehabilitation therapists provided unified guidance to patients to ensure consistency in training. All medical staff involved in the study completed systematic training and passed competency assessment prior to study. The training content included the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of NS, treatment operation procedures, and the identification and management of adverse reactions. In addition, no serious adverse reactions occurred among the study popultiaon during the treatment period. Only mild adverse reactions, such as slight tingling at the scalp, local muscle tremor, headache, and insomnia, were observed, all of which were alleviated after timely management and did not significantly affect the treatment course or patient recovery.

This study strictly adhered to the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines. To address the potential risks associated with TUS-NMES combined with GC therapy, the following systematic control measures were implemented: 1. Based on the predefined exclusion criteria, patients with penicillin allergy, those with severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal impairment, and those who were critically ill or died within 24 hours of admission were excluded, thereby reducing the risk of treatment-related complications at the recruitment stage. 2. Mandatory skin test was performed prior to each administration of penicillin-based drugs. Patients with a positive skin test result were prohibited from receiving the medication. For patients with a negative skin test result, a 30-minute observation period was still required after administration to monitor for delayed allergic reactions. 3. GC (prednisone) was administered following a short-term, low-dose regimen (30 mg/day for 3 consecutive days followed by discontinuation) to avoid adverse effects associated with long-term use. The medication was taken after meals, and gastric mucosal protectants such as omeprazole were co-administered to prevent peptic ulcers and bleeding. 4. The parameters for TUS-NMES were set to a safe stimulation intensity (frequency: 20 Hz, intensity: 60% motor threshold). Each treatment session was strictly controlled within 30 minutes and administered five days per week to avoid excessive or overly intense stimulation that could lead to headache, muscle spasm, or local skin burns. 5. Basic anti-syphilitic therapy (intravenous penicillin infusion) and general supportive care were prioritized. TUS-NMES and GC were gradually introduced only after the patient’s vital signs had stabilized, thereby minimize the risk of synergistic adverse effects. 6. Patient responses were closely monitored throughout the treatment. In the event of adverse reactions such as fever, localized redness, swelling, pain, skin erythema, pruritus, muscle reflex spasms, or mental abnormalities, timely management was provided. In cases of severe reactions, treatment was suspended or discontinued to enhance therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Clinical Data and Sample Collection

Clinical data were attained from the study subjects: (1) general baseline data: age, gender, course of disease, body mass index (BMI), history of sexual impurity, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease); (2) blood tests: whole blood samples (2–5 mL) were harvested from the patients at admission and 12 months after treatment using red normal blood collection tubes and blue sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes for serum RPR titer, prothrombin time (PT), fibrinogen (Fig), D-dimer (D-D), nitric oxide (NO), and endothelin-1 (ET-1) tests; (3) cerebrospinal fluid examination: cerebrospinal fluid (1–2 mL) was extracted by lumbar puncture for examining cerebrospinal fluid RPR titer, white blood cell (WBC) count, and protein content before treatment and12 months after treatment.

The number of cases of neurological symptoms (the presence of hemiparesis, headache, balance disorder, cognitive impairment, speech disorder, and visual impairment) and multiple foci before treatment and 12 months after treatment were counted. Fugl-Meyer assessment scale (FMA) and National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) before treatment and 12 months after treatment were utilized to evaluate the motor and neurological functions of the patients before and after the treatment, in which a larger value of the FMA score demonstrated a better motor function, and a lower value of the NIHSS score reflected a better neurological function, and NIHSS scores of 5–20 were classified as moderately severe neurological impairment.28,29

Evaluation of Rehabilitation Effects

Periodic reviews were performed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after treatment to evaluate therapeutic efficacy on the basis of changes in muscle strength, serum RPR titers, and symptom relief. Efficacy assessment: during the 12-month follow-up period, changes in serum RPR titers were observed, and the treatment efficacy was evaluated based on the maximal reduction in serum RPR titers, along with changes in muscle strength and symptom relief. Clinical outcomes were classified as clinically effective (including “effective” and “cured”) or clinically ineffective. The classification of clinical efficacy was as follows: (1) Cured: A negative serum RPR test result, accompanied by an improvement of two grades in muscle strength and complete resolution of symptoms. (2) Effective: A reduction of two grades in RPR titers, accompanied by an improvement of two grades in muscle strength, and the patient’s ability to maintain functional independence in daily activities. (3) Ineffective: No change or an increase in serum RPR titers, no improvement in limb function, and continued dependence on others for daily living.

The degree of muscle strength was generally categorized into 6 grades: grade 0, complete paralysis and total loss of muscle strength; grade 1, slight muscle contraction but no limb movement; grade 2, limb could move position but could not be lifted; grade 3, limb could be lifted away from the bed but could not be used against resistance; grade 4, limb could move against resistance but the muscle strength was weakened; and grade 5, normal muscle strength and safe movement.

Data Analysis

Data analysis and graphing were implemented by SPSS statistical software (27.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism software (9.5, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of all continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The paired t-test was used for within-group comparisons (pre-treatment vs post-treatment), and one-way ANOVA was used for comparison among the four groups (general treatment group, GC treatment group, TUS-NMES treatment group, and combined treatment group), with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post-hoc analysis to control the risk of type I error associated with multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (minimum, maximum). The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was conducted for within-group comparisons (pre-treatment vs post-treatment), and data among the four groups mentioned above were analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for post-hoc analysis to control the risk of type I error associated with multiple comparisons. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages), and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test. The cumulative probability of rehabilitation events (defined as clinically effective treatment and cure) among the four groups was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, with intergroup comparisons conducted using the Log rank test. All tests were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or η2) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to enhance the clinical interpretability of the results.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the effect of a new combination therapy (TUS-NMES, GC, and water-soluble penicillin) on patients with NS-cerebral infarction from the perspectives of clinical parameters and rehabilitation outcomes. It was revealed that this combination therapy achieved good efficacy in improving clinical symptoms as well as rehabilitation outcomes.

The antibiotic penicillin G represents a standard therapy for the treatment of syphilis,22 among which, intravenous injection of water-soluble crystalline penicillin G remains the recommended therapeutic regimen for NS.30 However, insufficient data can be given to support the long-term benefit of penicillin therapy in improving cognitive function in patients with NS.31 Moreover, progressive stroke is the initial symptom during the progression of NS, and disease progression is fast and hardly controlled by treatment with penicillin alone.32 TUS is one of the imaging modalities to assess the course and progression of syphilitic cerebral vasculitis.32 TUS offers early identification of progressive damage in the aftermath of cerebrovascular disorders like arterial ischemic stroke.33 NMES has shown the advantage of ameliorating negative psychological disorders in cases of cerebral infarction and dysphagia.34 Additionally, NMES not only strengthens vascular endothelial function but also improves peripheral blood circulation in the setting of acute myocardial infarction.35 Hence, NMES therapy has been applied in motor rehabilitation post brain damage.15,17 Recently, median nerve electrical stimulation guided by ultrasound has been proposed to be a safe and feasible therapy for stroke patients with upper limb damage by improving the motor function of the affected upper limb.36 Consistently, in this study, TUS-NMES treatment improved the clinical symptoms and clinical indicators of patients, and led to diminished NIHSS scores and elevated FMA scores. Thus, TUS-NMES appears beneficial for NS-cerebral infarction through its capacity to potentiate penicillin therapy, though synergistic action remains to be determined.

GCs are steroid hormones that play diverse roles in numerous normal and pathological processes.37 Our data revealed that the GC treatment can improve the coagulation and endothelial function of patients. A key study has shown that anti-treponemal antibiotics and corticosteroid therapy may improve the neurological prognosis of syphilitic myelitis (an extremely rare manifestation of NS).38 More recently, GC can attenuate stress-induced hypothalamic nerve injury by suppressing the gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent pyroptosis pathway.39 These findings partly highlight the neuroprotective effect of GC. To further investigate the impact of combining TUS-NMES with GCs on the therapeutic efficacy of water-soluble penicillin in patients with NS-cerebral infarction, our study comprehensively compared the outcomes of four groups: the general treatment (penicillin plus routine rehabilitation exercise), the TUS-NMES treatment (TUS-NMES and water-soluble penicillin), the GC treatment (GC and water-soluble penicillin), and the combined treatment (TUS-NMES, GC, and water-soluble penicillin). As expected, general treatment reduced the incidence of headaches, cognitive impairment, and lesions; relative to the TUS-NMES, GC or general treatment alone, the combined treatment led to greater clinical improvement, including alleviated symptoms, reduced number of lesions, and significant improvements in hemiplegia, language disorders, and cognitive disorders, along with significant benefits in serum indices, coagulation and vascular endothelial function. These results suggest the combined use of TUS-NMES and GC may facilitate the therapeutic efficiency of water-soluble penicillin in alleviating the clinical symptoms of patients with NS-cerebral infarction. In addition, NIHSS and FMA scores were utilized to evaluate the nerve damage and muscle function recovery of patients who received two different therapeutic modalities after brain damage.40 A significantly lower NIHSS score but a higher FMA score suggested an improvement in nerve and muscle function recovery after the combination therapy for patients. Moreover, the treatment recovery rate based on Kaplan-Meier analysis was improved in the combination therapy group, indicating that TUS-NMES in conjunction with GC enhanced the rehabilitation efficacy of water-soluble penicillin treatment.

As a preliminary exploratory study, we acknowledge that our findings, while promising, are subject to certain limitations. Specifically: 1. The relatively small sample size, and short follow-up period may introduce certain biases. Future multi-center, large-sample clinical studies with extended follow-up are essential to validate the reliability and stability of our findings. 2. This study only observed the therapeutic effects and did not thoroughly investigate the mechanism of action of TUS-NMES combined with GC in the treatment of cerebral infarct-type NS. Future basic research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms from the perspectives of molecular biology and neuroimaging, thereby providing a more robust theoretical basis for clinical treatment. 3. For the same group of patients, this study adopted a uniform treatment protocol without considering individual patient differences. Future research should focus on individualized treatment approaches, developing personalized treatment plans based on factors such as patient age, sex, underlying diseases, and degree of neurological impairment, to improve treatment outcomes. 4. This study only observed the treatment efficacy at 12 months post-treatment and did not evaluate the long-term outcomes. Future research should include long-term efficacy assessments to evaluate patients’ long-term recovery, quality of life, and prognosis, thereby providing a more comprehensive basis for clinical treatment.

Despite the above limitations, the findings of this study provide an important basis for clinically optimizing treatment strategies for cerebral infarct-type NS and hold considerable clinical application value. As an adjunctive treatment to standard penicillin therapy, TUS-NMES combined with GC demonstrated significant advantages in improving clinical symptoms, coagulation function, and neurological function in patients with cerebral infarct-type NS, as well as enhancing patient recovery outcomes. During the treatment process, only a few patients experienced mild adverse reactions, such as slight tingling at the scalp, local muscle tremor, headache, and insomnia, all of which were alleviated after timely management and did not significantly affect the treatment course or patient recovery. The favorable safety profile, characterized by only transient, minor adverse events, underscores the clinical viability of this combined approach.

Taken together, this research demonstrates a good effect of TUS-NMES in conjunction with GC and water-soluble penicillin on patients with NS-cerebral infarction, with improvements in clinical symptoms and rehabilitation efficacy. Nevertheless, owing to the small sample size and short follow-up, the current data are insufficient to support the extended clinical application of this therapy yet. Therefore, the sample size should be expanded to further validate the effect of this combination therapeutic regimen on clinical symptoms and rehabilitation of patients with NS-cerebral infarction to increase the credibility of results.
