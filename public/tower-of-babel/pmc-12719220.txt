Linear predictive coding electroencephalography algorithms predict mortality in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Simin Jamshidi, Arturo I Espinoza, Jonathan T Heinzman, Patrick May, Ergun Y Uc, Nandakumar S Narayanan, Soura Dasgupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Mortality increases in Parkinson's disease (PD) and is difficult to predict because of lack of reliable prognostic markers.•We used two minutes of EEG recordings and the Linear Predictive Coding EEG Algorithm for PD (LEAPD) to assess mortality.•We used LEAPD to classify 3-year mortality status and correlate LEAPD indices with time to death.•Out of sample tests gave high classification accuracy and high Spearman’s correlation between LEAPD indices and time to death.•Short resting state EEG using LEAPD can predict mortality and time to death in PD.Keywords: Parkinson’s Disease, Mortality Prediction, Machine Learning, Linear Predictive Coding

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, with prevalence increasing due to aging populations and modern environmental exposures [1], [2]. While motor symptoms are hallmark features, non-motor symptoms like cognitive decline and autonomic dysfunction also contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality [3], [4], [5]. PD patients face an elevated mortality risk, with clinical milestones such as hallucinations, falls, dementia, and nursing home placement markedly increasing the likelihood of death [6]. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated a pooled mortality ratio in PD of approximately 1.5, with survival declining by about 5 % for each year of follow-up [7]. Accurately determining mortality risk in PD can guide clinical decision-making, support advance care planning, and identify patients who may benefit from targeted interventions.

Objective biomarkers for predicting mortality in PD remain scarce [8], [9], [7]. Clinical features such as motor impairment and gait dysfunction are associated with increased mortality risk [10], and non-motor symptoms—such as psychosis, cognitive impairment, and autonomic issues—have also been linked to worse survival [11]. However, although clinical metrics of motor (e.g., Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale [UPDRS] III scores) and non-motor (e.g., Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] scores) function can offer some information about mortality risk and overall disease burden, these clinical assessments have three disadvantages: 1) they can have high variability as the UPDRS III has high intra- and inter-rater differences, and the MOCA can be influenced by practice effects and diurnal fluctuations [10], [12], 2) they are not routinely performed in primary care or neurology settings, and 3) they are not strongly predictive of mortality [10], [12].

By contrast, EEG-based biomarkers have shown promise for detecting both motor and cognitive dysfunction in PD, with resting-state EEG being sensitive to disease-related cortical changes [3], [8], [14], [15]. Our group has recently developed neurophysiological markers that predict clinical aspects of PD using scalp-recorded electroencephalography (EEG) [3], [13].[3], [13] LEAPD was developed by Anjum et al. (2020) as a computationally efficient method that uses linear predictive coding (LPC) to encode each EEG time-series LEAPD maximizes accuracy using the fewest EEG channels—a key advantage in clinical settings where fast setup, patient comfort, and feasibility are essential—making it well suited for real-time applications [9], [16]. In this study, we tested the hypothesis that LEAPD indices (see Methods section) can predict mortality in PD using resting-state EEG recordings (2 min, 64 channels; 59 retained for analysis after artifact rejection). Importantly, our algorithm was verified using performed out-of-sample tests. Our findings show that LEAPD can accurately classify mortality status [3], [9].

Methods

Participants

We recruited 94 PD patients from the University of Iowa Movement Disorders Clinic between 2017 and 2021. All patients were examined by a movement disorders physician to verify that they met the diagnostic criteria recommended by the United Kingdom PD Society Brain Bank diagnostic criteria, which includes bradykinesia and either tremor, rigidity, or postural instability as well a lack of other causes and a dopaminergic-responsive symptoms. All PD patients were determined to have the decisional capacity to provide informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethics Committee on Human Research. We obtained written informed consent from each participant. All research protocols were approved by the University of Iowa Human Subjects Review Board (IRB# 201707828). Data from some of these patients have been published previously [3], [17].

All EEG recordings of PD patients were performed in the medication “ON” state to enhance clinical applicability. Initial training and validation datasets were analyzed prior to completing EEG recordings in 2021. Participants were randomly apportioned between these sets. The date of death for deceased participants was determined using comprehensive chart review from 2023 to 2024, and data analyses were completed in 2025. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are demonstrated in Table 1. All EEG recordings were examined for their quality using well-established procedures.

Study design

To evaluate whether LEAPD predicts three-year mortality in PD patients, we performed single-channel classification using a balanced cohort of 44 PD patients (22 living and 22 deceased). Classification performance was assessed using LOOCV. Truncation analysis was used to evaluate the robustness of the classifier under three levels of data length shortening (90 %, 66 %, or 50 %) at the end of the two minute data segments.

To assess the accuracy in binary 3-year mortality classification of LEAPD on new, unseen data not used in training, we conducted an out-of-sample evaluation. A training dataset of 30 PD patients (15 deceased, 15 living) was used to optimize LEAPD hyperparameters which were applied to an independent cohort of 64 PD patients (7 deceased, 57 living), ensuring a strict separation between the training and testing phases. To create balanced test sets, we fixed the 7 deceased patients and randomly sampled 7 living patients from the pool of 57, repeating this process 10,000 times to generate distinct 7-vs-7 comparisons. Classification performance was evaluated using a 5-channel LEAPD classifier. The mean and standard deviation of classification accuracy were computed across these 10,000 trials to quantify the model’s generalizability under realistic variability in the test data.

To evaluate whether LEAPD indices could serve as a continuous biomarker of survival duration in PD, we first computed LEAPD indices for each of the 22 deceased patients in the 44-subjects previously used for single-channel classification. For each subject, we used a LOOCV framework: in each iteration, one deceased subject was excluded, and the LEAPD hyperplanes were constructed using the remaining 21 deceased and all 22 living subjects. We then correlated the resulting LEAPD indices with the number of days between EEG acquisition and death. Spearman’s ρ was calculated separately for each of the 59 usable EEG channels. Optimum hyperparameters used were those yielding the most negative ρ.

A balanced dataset of 15 deceased and 15 living patients was used to identify hyperparameter settings that produced the most negative Spearman’s ρ values. Out-of-sample tests using these hyperparameters and the hyperplanes obtained during training were conducted to compute Spearman’s correlation between LEAPD indices and time to death for the remaining 7 deceased patients.

EEG data collection and preprocessing

Resting-state EEG signals were recorded using a 64-channel actiCAP system, (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany), sampled at 500 Hz. The Pz electrode was used as a reference and excluded from analysis. Noisy or artifact-laden channels (TP9, TP10, FT9, and FT10) were identified and removed using the FASTER pipeline and the pop_rejchan function in EEGLAB [18]. Though this was not explicitly mentioned in our previous paper these noisy channels were excluded there as well.

LEAPD and Feature Extraction to determine the LEAPD index

Filtered EEG data from each of the 94 PD patients were encoded into LPC vectors using linear predictive coding. Order n LPC represents each sample of a time series as a weighted sum of the previous n samples. The weights ai that minimize the Mean Square Error (MSE) are the LPC coefficients, as depicted in Fig. 1c. They encode filtered EEG signals. These weights were then centered by subtracting their mean, and formed the entries of LPC vectors.

The LPC vectors from the deceased and living patient groups lie on separate m-dimensional hyperplanes. These hyperplanes were identified using principal component analysis (PCA), which defines the subspace spanned by each group’s LPC vectors. Fig. 1c illustrates this conceptualization, and Fig. 1d shows an example with m=2. Using EEG recording data from each PD patient, we computed the distances D1 and D2 of the LPC vector from the two hyperplanes representing data from living or deceased PD patients, respectively. The LEAPD index D1/(D1+D2).,taking values between 0 and 1 is then computed for each PD patient. EEG data from PD patients that died within 3 years after the EEG recording were classified by a LEAPD index value of 0.5 or more, see Fig. 1, Fig. 1, Fig. 1.

A training phase is required to identify optimal hyperparameters and train the LEAPD model for both LOOCV and out-of-sample testing. In this study, the training dataset was used to tune the LEAPD model by selecting the hyperparameters and obtaining the hyperplanes for the living and deceased groups. The hyperparameters were: Bandpass filter range (0.1–100 Hz), LPC order (2–10) and the dimension of the hyperplanes. Training involved identifying hyperparameters and the two hyperplanes that maximized classification accuracy. For 5-channel analyses, a single LEAPD index was computed as the geometric mean of the indices from each individual channel (Fig. 1b). The hyperparameters including the cutoff frequencies of the bandpass filters used to process the EEG signals differed from those in [3].

LOOCV classification

We first evaluated the discriminative power of individual EEG channels to classify PD patients who died within three years vs those who survived using a balanced dataset of 44. LOOCV was applied to compute the LEAPD index for each subject and assess classification performance across all usable EEG channels (59, excluding Pz, TP9, TP10, FT9, and FT10). All channels yielded high classification accuracy, with many reaching 100 %. These results are visualized as a scalp topographic heat map in Fig. 1e.

To test classifier robustness under conditions of limited data availability, we performed a truncation analysis on the same 44-subject dataset. EEG data were truncated from the recording end to 90 %, 66 %, and 50 % of their original duration. At each truncation level, classification performance was evaluated using the previously optimized hyperparameters and hyperplanes. This analysis tested the robustness of LEAPD under constraints like interrupted EEG recordings, see Fig. 1f.

Five-Channel classification and Out-of-Sample validation

We performed out-of-sample validation. A balanced training dataset of 30, drawn from the original 44 was used to optimize hyperparameters and hyperplanes yielding highest accuracy, which were then applied to an independent test cohort of 64 PD patients (7 deceased, 57 living), ensuring no overlap between training and test sets.

We fixed the 7 deceased patients and randomly sampled 7 living patients from the remaining pool. This process was repeated 10,000 times, generating distinct 7-vs-7 datasets. Five-channel classification was applied to each test set. Classification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were averaged across the 10,000 out-of-sample trials, quantifying model performance and stability under realistic data variability.

Correlation analysis between LEAPD indices and survival duration

To evaluate the LEAPD index as a continuous biomarker for survival duration, we computed correlations between the index and time to death. Using the same 44-subject dataset (22 deceased, 22 living) from the single-channel classification analysis, we used LOOCV. Excluding data of one deceased at a time, separate deceased and living hyperplanes were constructed using the remaining 21 deceased and all 22 living patients. The resulting LEAPD index for the excluded deceased subject was then correlated with the days from EEG recording to death. Hyperparameters that yielded the most negative Spearman correlation between LEAPD index and the time to death were selected.

This procedure was repeated independently across 59 EEG channels, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ρ was computed for each. The hyperparameters used were those that yielded the most negative ρ.

Statistical significance was assessed using p-values for Spearman’s ρ, and partial correlations were also computed to adjust for potential confounds such as age, MoCA scores, and UPDRS-III motor scores.

Finally, an out-of-sample correlation analysis was performed. A balanced training dataset of 30 PD patients (15 deceased, 15 living) was used to identify hyperparameters that yielded the most negative Spearman’s ρ values. These optimized settings were then applied to compute LEAPD indices for the remaining 7 deceased. Spearman’s correlation was calculated between the LEAPD indices and time to death to evaluate prognostic potential in unseen subjects.

Separate training was used for classification and correlation. For classification, optimized hyperparameters yielded the highest accuracy and for correlation, the most negative Spearman’s ρ.

Performance metrics

Classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). For correlation analyses, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ρ and corresponding p-values were computed. All metrics were calculated independently for each EEG channel or channel combination as relevant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 94 PD patients that participated in this study, comparing the “living” (n = 72) and “deceased” (n = 22) groups. On average, the deceased group was older (median [range]: 72 [64–82] vs. 68 [57–82] years, p = 0.004) and had more advanced disease severity, evidenced by higher Hoehn-Yahr stage (2.5 [1–6] vs. 1.8 [0–4], p = 0.002) and greater motor impairment, as assessed by UPDRS III (17.5 [4–28] vs. 12 [1–26], p = 0.0004). Cognitive function was also significantly lower in the deceased group (MoCA: 23 [16–28] vs. 26 [11–30], p = 0.009). No significant differences were observed in disease duration in living group vs the deceased group (4 [0–24] vs. 5 [1–12] years, p = 0.15), levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) (935.7 [300–2075] vs. 798.8 [150–1700] mg/day, p = 0.18), or sex distribution (72.7 % vs. 65.3 % male, p = 0.53).

Initial classification and truncation analysis

Single-channel LEAPD indices were first evaluated using a balanced dataset of 44 PD patients (22 deceased, 22 living) using LOOCV. Classification performance was assessed independently for each EEG channel, with most channels achieving 100 % accuracy and the lowest observed accuracy being 93.33 %, indicating strong discriminative power (Fig. 1e).

To evaluate classifier robustness under reduced data conditions, a truncation analysis was performed on the 44-subject dataset. EEG recordings were truncated to retain 90 %, 66 %, or 50 % of their original length, and classification accuracy was obtained without re-optimizing LEAPD parameters. The top 5 performing channels (CP4, PO4, FCz, P2, and P5) maintained strong classification performance across all truncation levels, as shown in the accuracy heatmap (Fig. 1f). For instance, FCz achieved 100.0 %, 97.7 %, 84.1 %, and 81.8 % accuracy at 100 %, 90 %, 66 %, and 50 % data retention, respectively, while P5 sustained performance with 100 %, 100 %, 81.8 %, and 72.7 % accuracy. For completeness, the hyperparameters associated with these top-performing channels in the truncation analysis were: CP4 (0.1, 79, 10, 1), PO4 (0.1, 17, 3, 1), FCz (0.1, 33, 7, 1), P2 (0.1, 84, 5, 1), and P5 (0.2, 61, 9, 1). For every hyperparameter tuple reported, the first two entries indicate the lower and upper cutoffs of the filter range (Hz), the third entry represents the LPC order, and the fourth entry corresponds to the hyperplane dimension.

To further assess the impact of data truncation on individual channel discriminability, LEAPD indices for P5 were analyzed across truncation levels. As shown in Fig. 2a, the distributions between the deceased and living groups remained distinct even as the EEG data were reduced. The corresponding ROC curves (Fig. 2b) show AUCs of 1.00 (100 % and 90 % truncation), 0.89 (66 %), and 0.74 (50 %), demonstrating the robustness of the classifier under data constraints.

Out-of-Sample Classification: 7-vs-7 testing

Separately, LEAPD hyperparameters were optimized using data from a distinct training cohort of 30 PD patients (15 deceased, 15 living). These optimized parameters were then applied for out-of-sample validation on data from an independent cohort of 64 PD patients (7 deceased, 57 living). Balanced comparisons were achieved by generating 10,000 comparisons between the 7 deceased participants and randomly selected 7 living ones.

A 5-channel combination (CP2, FC5, F4, PO4, CP4) was evaluated across all 10,000 randomized 7-vs-7 comparisons, yielding a mean classification accuracy of 83 % with a standard deviation of 7 %, confirming the reproducibility of the LEAPD-based mortality classifier. As shown in Fig. 2c, the LEAPD indices remained well separated between the deceased and living groups. The corresponding ROC curve in Fig. 2d demonstrates strong discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.80 for the 5-channel classifier.

The hyperparameters associated with this best-performing multichannel classifier were: CP2 (0.1, 44, 9, 4), FC5 (0.1, 6, 3, 2), F4 (0.3, 40, 9, 1), PO4 (0.1, 17, 3, 1), and CP4 (0.1, 79, 10, 1). Again, the first two entries indicate the lower and upper cutoffs of the filter range (Hz), the third entry represents the LPC order, and the fourth entry corresponds to the hyperplane dimension.

Correlation between LEAPD indices and survival duration

To assess whether the LEAPD index reflects proximity to death, we performed an LOOCV analysis restricted to data from the 22 deceased patients. Hyperplanes were obtained by using these subjects and 22 living ones. Leaving one deceased subject out at a time, we constructed deceased and living hyperplanes using the remaining 21 deceased and 22 living patients, respectively, and calculated the LEAPD index for the held-out subject. This process was repeated independently for each EEG channel. Spearman’s rank correlation was computed between LEAPD indices and the number of days from recording to death. Most EEG channels exhibited strong negative correlations, supporting the hypothesis that higher LEAPD indices are associated with shorter survival durations. The 5 channels with the strongest negative correlations were F4 (ρ = –0.862, p = 1.49 × 10−6), AFz (ρ = –0.832, p = 1.24 × 10−6), AF7 (ρ = –0.811, p = 4.93 × 10−6), Cz (ρ = –0.798, p = 1.11 × 10−5), and AF4 (ρ = –0.796, p = 1.26 × 10−5). The corresponding hyperparameters for these strongest correlation-yielding channels were: F4 (0.3, 36, 10, 5), AFz (0.4, 29, 4, 3), AF7 (0.2, 10, 9, 8), Cz (0.3, 25, 6, 4), and AF4 (0.2, 11, 10, 6).

Notably, even the weakest correlation across all channels (observed at POZ) was still substantial (ρ = –0.59). In the out-of-sample correlation analysis, channel C1 yielded ρ = –0.821, and P6 showed ρ = –0.642. The hyperparameters used for these out-of-sample correlation channels were: C1 (0.1, 11, 2, 1) and P6 (0.5, 18, 6, 1).

Among these channels with high correlation, several, viz C1, AFz, AF7, AF4 and P6 exhibit 100 % LOOCV accuracy. Even the F4 and Cz have an accuracy of 97.727 % which in the context of the sample size connotes a solitary error. These findings suggest that the LEAPD index may serve not only as a binary classifier but also as a continuous prognostic indicator of survival in PD. After adjusting each correlation value separately for age, UPDRS III score, and MoCA score, correlations remained virtually unchanged. The five channels whose combination yielded highest accuracy, the correlations were CP2: –0.6861, FC5: –0.6793, F4: –0.8622, PO4: –0.7516 and CP4: –0.7369.

Discussion

This study investigated the Linear Predictive Coding EEG Algorithm for Parkinson’s disease (LEAPD) as a potential non-invasive biomarker for mortality prediction in PD. Using a two-stage analysis, we first demonstrated strong single-channel classification performance in a balanced cohort of 44 PD patients (22 deceased, 22 living) using LOOCV and confirmed classifier robustness through truncation analysis under reduced data. We then validated the generalizability of the LEAPD framework through an out-of-sample evaluation involving a separately optimized training cohort of 30 PD patients (15 deceased, 15 living) and an independent testing cohort of 64 PD patients (7 deceased, 57 living), using 10,000 randomized 7-vs-7 comparisons.

LEAPD achieved strong classification performance with many channels yielding 100 % LOOCV accuracy and the 5-channel combination (CP2, FC5, F4, PO4, CP4) yielding a mean out-of-sample accuracy of 83 %. In addition to its classification performance, LEAPD indices demonstrated a strong negative correlation with time to death among deceased PD patients. This advances the LEAPD index as a continuous prognostic biomarker that may reflect not only mortality risk but also the relative proximity to death. Even after adjusting for age, motor severity (UPDRS III score), and cognitive status (MoCA score), the LEAPD index was still highly correlated with time to death, further emphasizing its utility as a mortality predictor. Such a relationship enables the LEAPD index to offer more nuanced clinical information beyond binary classification, potentially informing patient monitoring and care planning strategies.

These results highlight the promise of LEAPD as a computationally efficient, scalable, and clinically feasible tool for mortality risk assessment in PD. By encoding EEG time-series data through LPC vectors and leveraging simple single or multi-channel classifiers, LEAPD provides an objective framework that could facilitate earlier identification of high-risk patients and support clinical decision-making. EEG features are highly sensitive to cortical rhythms, and dysfunction in these rhythms may be predictors of cognitive or neuropsychiatric dysfunction that may in turn predict mortality [19]. Beyond binary classification, the significant correlation between LEAPD indices and time to death suggests that the LEAPD index may also serve as a continuous marker of mortality risk. Its non-invasive nature and minimal channel requirements further position it as an attractive alternative to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or serum-based biomarkers, which are often limited by cost, invasiveness, and patient compliance.

Despite these strengths, two limitations must be acknowledged. The cohort sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although this study represents one of the largest prospective EEG datasets focused on PD mortality classification to date, larger and more diverse populations will be needed to confirm the robustness of these results. Additionally, variability in EEG collection conditions—such as electrode placement, participant movement, and medication status—may affect reproducibility across different clinical settings. Standardization of EEG acquisition protocols will be critical for future validation efforts.

Conclusion

We show that from that LEAPD can accurately classify mortality status from EEG recordings and that LEAPD indices are correlated with survival duration, supporting their use as both binary and continuous neurophysiological biomarkers of mortality risk in PD [3], [9]. Overall, this work underscores the potential of EEG-based approaches for mortality prediction in PD—a focus that remains underexplored in prior research and extends beyond current predict metrics based on clinical assessments. While methods such as CSF biomarker analysis provide valuable insights, they often involve invasive procedures and are resource-intensive [20], [21]. LEAPD offers a non-invasive, computationally efficient, and clinically scalable alternative, capable of real-time classification. Moreover, its significant correlation with survival duration suggests that LEAPD may also serve as a continuous prognostic biomarker, enabling more nuanced assessment of mortality risk in PD.
