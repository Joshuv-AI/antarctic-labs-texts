Ten years of Plasmodium malariae infections in an International Health Reference Center in Barcelona: temporal trends and insights
Authors: Arantxa Pérez-Ugarte, Elena Sulleiro, Jana Rovira-Plujà, Merve Bayram, Francesc Zarzuela, Carles Rubio Maturana, Núria Serre-Delcor, María Luisa Aznar, Diana Pou, Lidia Goterris, Fernando Salvador, Alejandro Mediavilla, Pau Bosch, Joan Martinez-Campreciós, Begoña Treviño, Patricia Martínez-Vallejo, Aroa Silgado, Israel Molina, Inés Oliveira-Souto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPlasmodium malariae remains one of the most neglected human malaria parasites. Historically considered of low prevalence and limited clinical significance, emerging evidence suggests its true burden may be underestimated due to diagnostic challenges and the parasite’s ability to persist at low densities.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted at the Vall d’Hebron–Drassanes International Health Unit (Barcelona) from June 2015 to May 2025. Malaria diagnosis was performed using microscopy or quantitative PCR (qPCR), with the diagnostic method selected according to clinical presentation and epidemiological risk. Clinical and epidemiological data were collected and analyzed by descriptive statistics.ResultsAmong 6,293 individuals screened for malaria, 404 (6.4%; CI 95%: 5.8–7.1) were positive for Plasmodium spp., of whom 55 (13.6%; CI 95%: 10.6–17.3) were infected with P. malariae. Most infections (52/55; 94.5%) were detected in asymptomatic migrants from sub-Saharan Africa during routine screening, with very low parasite densities (≤ 80 parasites/µL). Notably, 13/55 patients had been living in Spain for more than 12 months prior to diagnosis, supporting long-term persistence of low-level parasitemia. Furthermore, one symptomatic traveler, experienced a clinical relapse after artemisinin-based combination therapy.ConclusionsThis study describes the trend in the diagnosis of P. malariae at a referral center in a non-endemic European setting. The high proportion of asymptomatic chronic carriers underscores a silent parasite reservoir that may hinder malaria elimination efforts. Increased awareness and improved diagnostic strategies are needed to better characterize and control this overlooked species.

Introduction

WHO [1] estimates 282 million malaria cases worldwide, with the burden mainly centered in sub-Saharan Africa. Plasmodium (P.) falciparum and P. vivax continue to be the main species involved; although another three species, P. ovale (including the subspecies P. ovale curtisi and P. ovale wallikeri), P. malariae and the zoonotic P. knowlesi, cause infection in humans. Among them, P. malariae continues to be one of the most neglected species [2].

Classically P. malariae has been linked to the presence of fever every 72 h (a phenomenon called quartan fevers) and a benign chronic infection characterized by low parasite index. However, adverse outcomes such as chronic nephrotic syndrome or severe anemia have been also reported [3]. Besides, recurrence years after infections have been described [4] although the mechanism behind its persistence is yet not fully understood, as the presence of hypnozoites has never been proved.

P. malariae has a broad presence in tropical and subtropical areas, being most commonly described in Sub-Saharan Africa [5]. However, despite its potential relevance for public health surveillance and malaria elimination, the true burden of this infection remains unknown and reported prevalence varies widely across studies, ranging from 1.5% to 30%. [6–8]. Several factors may contribute to this variability: lack of systematic surveillance data in endemic countries, the occurrence of mixed-species infection or the increasing shift towards the use of rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) as the standard malaria diagnostic, with low sensitivity for this species. Therefore, this study aimed to describe long-term temporal trends, clinical characteristics, and factors associated with P. malariae infections among migrants and travelers attending a national referral center in a non-endemic European setting.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Vall d’Hebron-Drassanes International Health and Tropical Medicine (VHD-IHTM) Unit, in Barcelona, from June 2015 to May 2025.

The VHD-IHTM at Vall d’Hebron University Hospital was established in 2007 and provides specialized care for immigrants and travelers with the aim of ensuring equitable access to healthcare services in Barcelona.

The study population was divided into two groups: The first group consisted of internationally mobile patients seeking care at our center due to symptoms suggestive of malaria, encompassing both migrants and travelers from malaria-endemic regions, while the second consisted of asymptomatic migrants undergoing routine malaria screening as part of the imported disease assessment program, implemented at the VHD-IHTM, offered to all asymptomatic migrants from sub-Saharan Africa [9].

Clinical and epidemiological data were collected from medical records, including variables such as age, gender, country of origin or travel destination, antimalarial chemotherapy, presence of symptoms, microbiological diagnosis, infecting malaria species and time since arrival to Spain or travel duration. Based on this information, participants were categorized into three groups: travelers (individuals from non-endemic areas who had visited an endemic region), migrants (those who had relocated their usual residence to Spain), and Visiting Friends and Relatives migrants (VFR) (travelers who returned to their country of origin as tourists but with permanent residence in Spain).

In relation to the diagnostic techniques, the methods for diagnosing malaria varied and evolved throughout the study period from microscopy to molecular techniques such as real time PCR (qPCR).

For symptomatic patients, the diagnostic evaluation to rule out malaria included microscopic examination, using the rapid quantitative buffy coat (QBC) technique, and confirmation with Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears. In our protocol, rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are not routinely used to screen symptomatic individuals. Species identification and parasite load were determined through thick and/or thin smear analysis. Parasite density in positive thick smears was estimated according to standard World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations by counting asexual parasites against 200 leukocytes in thick blood smears and assuming a standard leukocyte count of 8000 cells/µL in the absence of individual hematological data. Parasite density was expressed as parasites per microliter (parasites/µL). In addition, parasitemia was assessed on thin blood smears as the percentage of infected erythrocytes, calculated by counting parasitized red blood cells among approximately 1000 erythrocytes in well-prepared microscopic fields.

Asymptomatic patients were diagnosed by qPCR on peripheral blood. The RDT was not systematically used for screening in this population. Nucleic acid extraction was performed using whole blood collected in EDTA tubes and processed on the EMAG® instrument (Biomerieux) with 200 µL of sample. The amplification protocol involved two steps: an initial assay for the detection of Plasmodium spp., followed by a confirmatory assay for species differentiation (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi) if the initial result was positive.

Between January 2015 and June 2020, malaria testing was conducted using an in-house PCR technique [10]. From July 2020 onwards, the screening protocol was updated to the RealStar Malaria PCR Kit 1.0 (Altona Diagnostics), with species differentiation conducted using the RealStar Malaria Screen & Type Kit (Altona Diagnostics). It is important to note that neither of the molecular techniques employed throughout the study differentiates between the two subspecies of P. ovale (P. ovale curtisi and P. ovale wallikeri). For the change of the PCR assays, the laboratory conducted comparison and verification studies in accordance with the quality system standards ISO 9001 and ISO 15189, which apply to clinical laboratories. Both techniques showed a concordance greater than 95%. All qPCR positive cases were reviewed by microscopy, reporting Plasmodium species and parasite index.

All clinical and epidemiological data were analyzed using Stata 17.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize demographic, epidemiological and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI), while continuous variables were reported as medians with ranges.

Results

Between June 2015 and May 2025, a total of 6293 patients were screened for malaria at VHD-IHTM Unit. Of these, 1622/6293 (25.8%) corresponded to patients presenting with symptoms compatible with malaria, while 4671/6293 (74.2%) were performed as malaria screening in asymptomatic migrants from sub-Saharan Africa. The annual distribution of malaria screenings is presented in Fig. 1.

Annual distribution of malaria screenings, 2015–2025

Plasmodium spp. infection was confirmed in 404 patients, yielding an overall positivity rate of 6.4% (CI 95%: 5.8–7.1%). Among these, 135/1622; 8.3% (CI 95%: 7.1–9.8%) symptomatic patients tested positive, compared with 269/4671;5.8% (CI 95%: 5.1–6.5%) asymptomatic screened individuals.

Of the 404 confirmed malaria cases, P. malariae was identified in 55 patients (13.6%; CI 95.5 10.6–17.3%). The majority of these infections were detected during screening in asymptomatic individuals (51/55, 92.7%) although four cases (4/55, 7.3%) occurred in symptomatic patients, two of them in travelers after a short-term trip.

When stratified by initial clinical status, P. malariae accounted for 4/135 malaria cases among symptomatic individuals (3.0%) and for 51/269 cases detected in asymptomatic screening (19.3%). During follow-up, eight patients initially categorized as asymptomatic acknowledged prior malaria-related symptoms, leading to a final distribution of 12/55 symptomatic cases (21.8%). A total of 43/55 patients (78.2%) were asymptomatic at diagnosis, being 90.7% males with a median age of 19.2 years. The majority were migrants (42/43, 97.7%), with one case detected in a VFR traveler. Countries of origin are shown in Fig. 3.

Countries of origin of asymptomatic migrants diagnosed with P. malariae during screening

Coinfection with P. falciparum was detected in 11/43 (25.6%) asymptomatic cases while the remaining were P. malariae monoinfections.

All qPCR-positive samples underwent microscopic re-evaluation, with parasite confirmed in 14/43 (32.6%) asymptomatic cases by thick smear. Parasite densities were consistently low, remaining below 80 parasites/µL on thick smear.

Treatment was administered in 42/43 patients (97.7%). One patient was lost to follow-up and therefore not treated.

Among the 12 symptomatic cases, 7/12 (58.3%) were male, with a mean age of 28.3 years (Table 1). Most infections were diagnosed in migrants (7/12, 58.3%), followed by travelers (3/12, 25.0%) and VFR (2/12, 16.7%). All infections were acquired in Africa, except for a single case in a traveler infected in Colombia. [11]. The predominant symptoms were fever and headache (10/12:83.3%), followed by myalgia (3/12, 25.0%). One patient presented with nephrotic syndrome.

Monoinfections with P. malariae accounted for 8/12 cases (66.7%), while 3/12 (25.0%) involved coinfection with P. falciparum. One case (8.3%) was a triple infection with P. falciparum, P. malariae, and P. ovale.

Among the eight symptomatic cases detected by qPCR, microscopy review confirmed parasite index in 5/8 (62.5%). Parasite quantification was available for these five cases, with a reported median of 80 parasites/µL on thick smear.

One of the symptomatic patients treated with artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) was a 19-year-old woman, with no relevant medical history that presented in our clinic with a five-day history of fever and chills 45 days after returning from a two-week volunteer trip to Cameroon. She had not properly adhered to malaria prophylaxis with doxycycline prescribed before traveling. A thick and thin blood smear, along with PCR for parasite detection, were conducted. Both the thick and thin smears tested positive for Plasmodium spp. with low parasitemia level (less than 1%). The RDT -was negative. Therefore, she was diagnosed with uncomplicated malaria and discharged with ACT (Artemether-lumefantrine). Seventy-two hours later, qPCR was positive for P. malariae At the end-of-treatment follow-up visit, she had fully recovered, reported good adherence to ACT and had a negative thick blood smear. Six weeks later, the patient returned with a fever spike of 39.5 °C without other associated symptoms. She denied returning to a malaria endemic area. Microscopy examination was performed, revealing again Plasmodium spp. trophozoites. The identification of P. malariae was confirmed by qPCR, and hydroxychloroquine was prescribed. Post-treatment controls were negative and the patient has remained asymptomatic since then.

Discussion

This study describes the trends in the diagnosis of Plasmodium malariae at a referral Centre for Tropical Medicine & International Health in Barcelona, Spain, between June 2015 and May 2025. Across Europe, P. malariae is considered one of the least prevalent malaria imported species, accounting for 3.1% of identified cases in 2022 [12]. Similar low proportions have been reported in different European countries, including the United Kingdom (1.5%), Germany (3.0%) and France (2.5%) [13–15], while in Spain the proportion was slightly higher, reaching 5% of malaria infections between 2009 and 2015 [16]. In contrast, our 10-year reviewed data revealed a substantially higher proportion of P. malariae infections (13.6%). Most of these infections were detected through routine screening of asymptomatic migrants from sub-Saharan Africa, predominantly from West Africa, highlighting the silent burden of this parasite in endemic regions and suggesting that P. malariae may be an under-recognized contributor to imported malaria in non-endemic settings.

In our center the implementation of nucleic acid amplification techniques (NAT) represented a significant improvement in the diagnosis of non-falciparum malaria, including P. malariae, due to the improved sensitivity of the technique [17]. Fifty-one cases (92.7%) were detected by qPCR, whereas only four infections (7.3%) were initially identified by thick smear. It is well known that conventional diagnostic techniques commonly used in endemic area, such as microscopy and RDTs face limitations for P. malariae diagnosis [18]. Microscopic diagnosis of this species is mainly associated with the presence in the thin blood smear of ring stages and band forms, but several studies suggest that this method results in a high percentage of false-negative results that could cause occasional misdiagnoses [19]. Furthermore, RDT also present a poor sensitivity in P. malariae infections, which could underestimate the incidence of this species in the field [20]. The use of qPCR as a diagnostic method improves the sensitivity of the diagnosis, however nowadays it’s not available in most endemic areas in routine clinical management.

The high proportion of asymptomatic infections (78.2%) with very low parasite densities (< 80 parasites/µL) is consistent with the known biological characteristics of P. malariae, which is able to persist chronically for years without causing overt clinical symptoms [3]. Notably, 13 of the 55 patients had been living in Spain for more than 12 months prior to diagnosis, further supporting the capacity of P. malariae to maintain long-term low-level parasitemia and remain undetected in the absence of sensitive diagnostic tools.

Until now, P. malariae infection has been described as one of the least frequent causes of malaria in travelers, possibly due to a lower transmission efficiency of the mosquito vector compared to other Plasmodium species [12]. However, the detection of three cases in symptomatic short-term travelers since 2018 in our cohort is noteworthy and may reflect either an increased exposure risk or an emerging clinical relevance of this species in non-endemic settings. Importantly, studies conducted in endemic regions, including multi-year surveillance cohorts among symptomatic individuals [21], have also reported a sustained and higher-than-expected burden of P. malariae, suggesting that its epidemiological relevance may be underestimated in routine surveillance. In this context, our findings further support the need to consider P. malariae as a potential cause of imported malaria in travelers.

Regarding clinical management, WHO recommends ACT for P. falciparum malaria. For uncomplicated P. vivax, P. ovale (P. ovale curtisi and P. ovale wallikeri), P. malariae, and P. knowlesi infections, either ACT or chloroquine may be used in areas where chloroquine susceptibility is maintained. In contrast, in regions with chloroquine-resistant infections, ACT remains the only recommended treatment option [8, 22]However, in many areas in daily clinical approach, ACT is used as malaria treatment independently of the infecting species, probably due to diagnostic uncertainty and co-endemicity with P. falciparum [23]. Prior studies, conducted in both endemic areas and among imported cases, have theorized about the P. malariae capability to survive after anACT regimen [24, 25]. In this study, we present the second well-documented case of a P. malariae infection that recurrent after ACT treatment, following the case described by Rutledge et al. [26]. A plausible explanation linking these observations lies in the biological and pharmacodynamic characteristics of P. malariae. This parasite has a longer intraerythrocytic cycle (approximately 72 h) compared to P. falciparum (48 h), while standard ACT regimens are administered over a 3-day period. As a result, ACT may cover fewer complete asexual replication cycles in P. malariae, potentially reducing cumulative drug exposure across parasite stages. This could allow a fraction of parasites to survive treatment and persist at very low densities.

This hypothesis is supported by observations of slower parasite clearance kinetics. In an experimental study involving two healthy individuals, the mean parasite clearance time for P. malariae was estimated at 6.7 h, exceeding the 5-h threshold associated with reduced susceptibility in P. falciparum [27]. Although the clinical implications remain uncertain, this finding suggests intrinsic differences in parasite response to artemisinin derivatives. Indeed, its less susceptibility to ACTs has been also described in an in vitro study conducted recently in Ghana, which assessed the activity of artemether against P. malariae and P. falciparum [28].

Other potential reason could be related to the presence of mutations that confer resistance to either artemisinin derivatives or companion drugs, however, no studies have been published to confirm this hypothesis to date. Additionally, the asymptomatic nature of the infection may persist in the bloodstream for prolonged periods. As a result, it is possible that the malaria epidemiology is shifting, increasing the prevalence of P. malariae, while other more targeted species like P. vivax are decreasing [28].

On the other hand, in 15 out of 55 patients (27.7%), P. malariae infection presented in form of mixed-species infection, especially with P. falciparum. This is particularly common on the African continent, from which most of our patients come, due to the overlap of endemic areas for P. falciparum and P. malariae. Mixed-species infections not only complicate the diagnosis of P. malariae but may also contribute to the development and adaptation of this parasite, as a potential modulatory effect of P. falciparum on P. malariae infection has been suggested [2]. It is worth noting that the 2025 World Malaria Report identifies P. falciparum as the only species reported across all West and Central African countries, from which our patients originate, suggesting that P. malariae infections may be underreported in routine surveillance systems.

As limitations, malaria diagnostic by qPCR in asymptomatic patients was only performed in Sub-Saharan migrants, which could underestimate its prevalence in other endemic areas. Moreover, given the retrospective nature of the study, data collection relied on physician-recorded information, which may have resulted in incomplete capture of certain clinical and epidemiological variables.

In addition, we cannot exclude that the transition from an in-house qPCR assay to RealStar Malaria kit (Altona diagnostics) may have influenced P. malariae detection rates, potentially contributing to the diagnostic variability observed throughout the study period.

In conclusion, this study reports an important detection of P. malariae diagnoses in migrants and travelers from June 2015 to May 2025 at one of Spain’s Reference Centers for International Health, with most cases identified through systematic screening of asymptomatic migrants from sub-Saharan Africa. These findings demonstrate that P. malariae infections are frequently subclinical, associated with very low parasitemia, and often missed by conventional diagnostic methods. However, recurrent episodes after treatment with ACT, together with the identification of cases in short-term travelers, further highlight the clinical relevance and complexity of this neglected species.

Overall, our results support the inclusion of P. malariae in malaria surveillance strategies and the need to strengthen molecular diagnostic capacity in both endemic and non-endemic settings to improve detection and better define its true epidemiological burden.
