The pattern of diabetic care and glycemic control among the ambulatory diabetic patients in tertiary care settings in Bangladesh
Authors: Noshin Farzana, Md Saimul Islam, Shahjada Selim, Josyula K Lakshmi, Deksha Kappor, Anjali Sharma, A B M Abdullah, Aliya Naheed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus is a major public health concern in Bangladesh. The pattern of diabetic care and control of blood sugar among diabetic patients are not well described. We assessed the pattern of diabetic care among ambulatory diabetic patients attending tertiary care hospitals in Bangladesh, and explored the relationship of glycemic control with behavioral and metabolic risks. Any patient 18 years and older attending the medicine outpatient department (OPD) at randomly selected three government tertiary hospitals in three divisions in Bangladesh were examined by hospital doctors. If a patient who was diagnosed as having diabetes and produced any medical document to support the diagnosis was recruited following an informed consent. Data on socio-demographic characteristics, diabetic care plan and behavioral risks, including tobacco use, physical activity, healthy diet (daily fruits and vegetable consumption) and salt intake were obtained from study participants by recall. Three milliliters of venous blood were tested to determine uncontrolled diabetes by measuring glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C > 7.0), and hyperlipidemia by measuring total cholesterol (> 200 mg/dL), high density lipoprotein/HDL (< 40 Mg/dL, low density lipoprotein/ LDL (> 200 mg/dL), and Triglyceride (> 160 mg/dL). A Total of 465 patients were enrolled. The Mean age was 49 years (SD:11) and 58% were women. Sixty percent patients were on a treatment plan of anti-diabetic drugs (drug), healthy diet (diet) and physical activity (PA), 13.3% diet and drug, 9.7% on drug only, 6.4% on diet and PA, 3.9% on PA and drug, 1.3% on PA only and 0.9% on diet only. Two- third of the patients (68.6%) had uncontrolled diabetes, which was three times higher, if a patient had diabetes for more than five years than otherwise (OR: 3.31, 95% CI 2.06–5.33), two times higher if a patient did not consume fruits (OR: 2.28, 1.34–3.87), or three times higher, if a patient did not consume vegetables (OR: 3.70, 95% CI 1.80–7.59) than otherwise, 78% higher, if a patient had taken extra salt in meal than otherwise (OR: 1.78, 95% CI 1.13–2.80) and nine times higher, if a patient had a raised total cholesterol level (> 200 mg/dL) than otherwise (OR: 9.60, 95% CI 2.60–35.40) and three times higher, if a patient had a raised LDL level compared than otherwise (OR: 3.33, 95% CI: 1.93-5.70). Diabetic patients seeking care at tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh follow diverse diabetic care plans and the majority of them do not have diabetes under control. Unhealthy dietary practice and hyperlipidemia among diabetic patients contribute to uncontrolled diabetes. Introduction

Diabetes is a significant public health concern affecting one in every eleven adult population 20–79 years of age across the world. Among the total diabetic cases, three out of four patients are diagnosed in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs)1. Globally half of the people with diabetes remain undiagnosed putting them at a higher risk of organ-related complications, including heart disease, kidney problems, nerve damage, and vision impairment1–5. Effective diabetic care can avert negative consequences on health and economy of the affected individual6,7. The World Health organization (WHO) has emphasized the importance of lifestyle modifications, including healthy diet and physical activity, and necessary drug treatment for maintaining the glucose level in blood for averting premature deaths related to diabetes8. Understanding the pattern of diabetic care and factors attributing to uncontrolled diabetes is crucial for identifying the gaps in diabetic care in LMICs.

Bangladesh is one of the top ten LMICs in the world reported a high prevalence of diabetes in the community9. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), in Bangladesh, around 8.4 (7.0–10.7) million patients were living with diabetes in 2019 and this number was projected to increase to 15 (12.4–18.9) million by 20452,10. A systematic review has reported that the prevalence of diabetes was 7.8% in Bangladesh with a higher prevalence in urban than the rural areas11. The Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey 2017–2018 found that only 26% of people with diabetes had their diabetes under control, while only 31% were aware of their condition and only 28% were receiving treatment12. A facility based study has reported a high prevalence of uncontrolled type 2 diabetes among the study participants and the majority of the diabetic patients had one or more complications13. In Bangladesh, diabetic care is primarily provided by the Diabetic Association of Bangladesh (BADAS) through two general hospitals in the capital city and 102 healthcare facilities in 61 districts (out of 64 districts)14,15. Diabetic patients can also get diabetic care at any medicine outpatient department of a government tertiary level hospital for free16.

Despite availability of diabetic care, the prevalence of uncontrolled diabetes remains a major concern in Bangladesh17,18. Understanding the pattern of diabetic care and its relationship with glycemic control among individuals who have access to available diabetic care would be important for identifying the current gaps in management of diabetes, and planning for an effective diabetic plan in Bangladesh.

In this study, we explored the pattern of diabetic care among ambulatory diabetic patients visiting outpatient departments (OPD) of three public tertiary teaching hospitals in Bangladesh, and assessed relationship of diabetic care with control of diabetes. We also explored the relationship of behavioral and cardiometabolic risks with glycemic control irrespective of diabetic care pattern.

Methods

Study setting and recruitment of the study population

The study was conducted at the outpatient medicine departments (OPD) of three tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh, which were randomly selected from a list of public teaching hospitals that provided services for more than ten years19. These hospitals were Shaheed Suhrawardy Medical College and Hospital, Mymensingh Medical College and Hospital, and Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College and Hospital from 3 different administrative divisions of Bangladesh including Dhaka, Mymensingh and Sylhet, respectively.

Adult ambulatory patients who visited the hospital OPDs during the study period were identified by hospital doctors and were referred to a study physician deployed in each hospital who checked for any supporting evidence of diabetes carried by the patients (doctor’s prescription, lab report, membership of BADAS, etc.). Considering an unknown prevalence of uncontrolled diabetes (50%), a 95% level of significance, and a 20% non-response rate, we required a total of 460 participants and we recruited 465 participants equally distributed in the three study hospitals.

The study physicians enrolled any patient who met the following inclusion criteria: ≥ 18 years of age, carrying a document that had supported that the patient had either been diagnosed with diabetes or being treated for diabetes, allowed the study physician to perform necessary physical examinations and draw blood for specific laboratory tests, agreed to provide data and provided a written voluntary informed consent for participation in the research. Any patient who was severely ill or did not have the mental state to provide consent was excluded from recruitment. The study physician obtained data on age, sex, education, occupation, household assets, household income, care seeking pattern, duration of diabetes, treatment history, and lifestyle behavior, including fruits and vegetables consumption, extra salt intake in meal, tobacco use, and physical activity applying methods previously used in other research conducted in Bangladesh20,21.

Laboratory assessment

Three mL of venous blood was obtained from each participant and sent to perform HbA1C and non-fasting lipid profile22,23. All samples were preserved at − 20 °C in a refrigerator located at the study hospital laboratory and transported twice a week to the laboratory in icddr,b within 4–5 hours maintaining a proper cold chain using dry ice. In icddr,b, all samples were stored at − 80 °C refrigerators and processed within one week following the standard laboratory guidelines. In the laboratory assessment, 0.5 mL whole blood was aliquoted for Hb1c measurement. The remaining 2.5 mL whole blood was centrifuged and around 800 µl plasma separated and stored for measuring blood glucose and lipid profile using an automated clinical chemistry analyzer, Cobas c 31124–26. Diabetes was defined as ‘controlled’ (glycemic control) if HbA1c level was less than 7.0% and ‘uncontrolled’ if HbA1c level was 7.0% or more27. Hyperlipidemia was defined if one or more of the following parameters was above the normal range (total cholesterol: > 200 mg/dL, low density lipoprotein: > 130 mg/dL, triglyceride: > 150 mg/dL, high density lipoprotein: < 40 mg/dL or 60 mg/dL)28.

Assessment of cardio metabolic risks

Height in cm (using Seca Scale) and weight in kg (using a digital weighing scale) were measured. Body Mass Iindex was calculated following the guideline for Asian populations as per World Health Organization (WHO) guideline 29. Nutritional status was defined as underweight if BMI: < 18.5 kg/m2, normal weight if BMI: 18.5–22.9 kg/m2, overweight if BMI: 23.0–26.9 kg/m2 and obese if BMI ≥ 27.0 kg/m2 following WHO guideline29,30. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using digital OMRON HEM-7300 machine two times in one sitting at three minutes interval between measurements20. The average values of the first and second measurements of SBP and DBP were calculated for the value of blood pressure (BP). Raised BP was defined if SBP was 140 mmHg or more, or DBP was 90 mmHg or more31.

Data analysis

Education was categorized as ‘below primary school’ if a patient never went to school or completed less than 5 years of schooling, ‘primary school’ if completed 5 to 9 years of schooling, and ‘high school’ if completed 10 years of schooling or more. The wealth index (WI) was created by applying principal component analysis on the household level asset variables. Socioeconomic status (SES) was defined by dividing WI into five equal quantiles and level as 'lower', 'lower middle', 'middle', 'upper middle', and 'upper'32 (Supplementary Materials). Monthly median income along with interquartile range was reported for each quantile of WI33. The households living in ‘extreme poverty’ were defined by applying the value of ‘poverty line’ below the cut off ‘1.90$ (USD) per person per day’ as defined by the World Bank34. Occupation was categorized into ‘service’, ‘business’, ‘day labor’ (including agriculture, carpenter, rickshaw puller and tailors), ‘unemployed’ (including student, handicap or retired from service) and ‘homemaker’.

Tobacco use was categorized as 'current' if a patient had reported consumption of any form of tobacco (either smoking cigarettes or chewing tobacco) at the time of the survey or within the past 30 days, 'past' if a patient had consumed any form of tobacco previously but did not consume tobacco over the last 30 days, while 'never' if never had consumed any form of tobacco35.

The short form of International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess physical activity (PA) status among diabetes patients over the past week36. The total Metabolic Equivalent (MET) score was calculated by multiplying the MET values obtained from the guideline for the minutes per day spent on each activity over the past week under three domains of IPAQ, including ‘vigorous-intensity activities’, ‘moderate-intensity activities’, and ‘walking’. The MET score per week calculated under each domain of IPAQ was collated to get the total ‘MET minute per week’ score and PA level was classified as 'low', or 'moderate', or 'high' following the IPAQ scoring guideline37. High PA was defined if any of the following criteria was met: engaging in vigorous-intensity activities on at least 3 days per week and accumulating at least 1500 MET-minutes/week, or engaging in any combination of walking, moderate-intensity, or vigorous-intensity activities on 7 days per week to achieve a minimum of 3000 MET-minutes/week. Moderate PA was defined if any one of the following criteria was met: engaging in vigorous activities on 3 or more days per week with each session lasting at least 20 min, engaging in moderate-intensity activities or walking on 5 or more days per week with each session lasting at least 30 min, or engaging in any combination of these activities to accumulate at least 600 MET-minutes/week. Low PA criteria was defined if an individual did not meet the criteria of moderate PA or high PA. Food frequency questionnaire was used for obtaining data on fruits and vegetables consumption38. Consumption of fruits and vegetables was categorized into ‘regular' if consumed at least once a day, and ‘irregular' otherwise. Consumption of extra salt was defined if any patient added extra salt to one or more meals in a day.

The pattern of diabetic care was classified into six groups based on different combination of treatment adopted as reported by the patients (i) combination of anti-diabetic drug (drug), healthy diet and physical activity (PA), (ii) diet and drug, (iii) diet and PA, (iv) PA and drug, (v) drug only, (vi) diet only and (vii) PA only. Duration of diabetes was categorized into < 5 years indicating a shorter duration and ≥ 5 years indicating a longer duration of having diabetes39. Facilities were categorized into ‘government hospital’, ‘private health facility’ ( private hospitals and clinics), ‘doctor’s chamber’, ‘Diabetic Samity or BADAS’ and ‘pharmacy’. Reasons for visiting hospitals were categorized into ‘diabetes'if a patient sought care for diabetes and ‘other health condition' if sought care for a health condition not related to diabetes.

Univariate analysis was performed to present frequency distribution for categorical variables and summary statistics was reported for continuous variables, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR). Bivariate analysis was conducted to compare patients with controlled and uncontrolled diabetes across sociodemographic characteristics, care seeking pattern, diabetic care patterns, behavioral risks and metabolic risks. A chi-square test was applied to assess proportional difference between groups. An independent sample t-test was applied to compare mean difference between two groups of patients. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariable analyses were performed including those variables in the model that had a Pvalue < 0.1 in bivariate analysis to ensure no confounding variables were overlooked. A multiple logistic regression model was run to identify the behavioral and metabolic risk factors that had contributed to uncontrolled diabetes without adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics (Model 1). The model was run following adjustmeant of the sociodemographic characteristics in order to further determine which behavioral and metabolic risk factors had independently contributed to uncontrolled diabetes (Model 2). Odds ratios (ORs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software version 20.

To avoid potential recall bias, we used a structured questionnaire with clear instruction and efforts were given to obtain data over a short time frame. We also ensured anonymity of the participants and confidentiality of data following a good rapport building with the participant in order to improve confidence of the participants with self-reporting. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to control potential confounding effect on uncontrolled diabetes as and when appropriate and obtain more accurate estimates of the relationships of interest were derived40. There were fewer than 1% missing values for a few variables related to care-seeking patterns, which were omitted during data analysis due to their negligible impact on results and to preserve data integrity.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of International Centre for Diarrheal Disease Research, Bangladesh. A written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to recruitment. If any patient had reported diabetes with a comorbidity the study physicians sent those patients to the hospital OPD doctors for an advise of referral to an appropriate department in the same hospital or a specialized government facility for facilitating necessary treatment free of cost. Laboratory test results along with a summary note of the laboratory results were shared with all patients.

Results

Socio demographic characteristics of the study participants

Between April and June 2016, a total total 465 patients with diabetes were recruited from three hospitals (~ 150/hospital) and 58% was female. The mean age of the participants was 49 years (SD:11) (Table 1) and female patients were younger than the male patients (mean age: 47 years; SD:11 vs. mean age:53 years SD:11; P < 0.001). More than 2/3rd of the patients (64.3%) completed primary education and the proportion was higher among males than females (72.6% vs. 58.2%; P < 0.001). Overall, 72.4% females were homemaker followed by 23.5% in service, 2.6% in business, 1.5% were day laborer and 1.1% unemployed. Overall, 31% males were involved in service followed by 29.9% unemployed, 28.4% in business and 10.7% were day laborer. The average household income ranged between 178.00 USD (IQR: 127, 255) and 637.00 USD (IQR: 407,891), and 1.5% households were living in extreme poverty. The age, level of education and occupation were significantly different between male and female patients (Table 1).

History of diabetes and pattern of diabetic care

About 3/4th of the study participants (75.7%) were first diagnosed as having diabetes by a doctor (MBBS) and other patients incidentally learnt about it when a laboratory test was performed for a different health concern. The mean age of the participants was 43 years (SD: 10.4) when they were first diagnosed as having diabetes and a female patient was diagnosed at a younger age (mean age (SD): 41 (10.0) years) than a male patient (46 (11) years; P < 0.001). The greater majority of the patients (95%) received treatment from a doctor when first diagnosed as having diabetes and 5% of the participants sought care from a provider other than a doctor, which included drug sellers in the pharmacy, traditional healers, village doctors, homeopathy practitioners, ayurvedic practitioner, health workers and paramedics.

Pattern of health care seeking

The majority of the patients (83.0%) sought health care within the past six months of the survey and 46% of them attended the study hospital OPD as their first choice for treatment. The remaining patients (54%) visited another facility before attending the study hospital OPD and among them 37.6% visited another tertiary hospital, 31.2% a private facility, 15.2% a diabetic clinic, 8.0% a government health facility and 8.0% a pharmacy. During the past visit 48.2% sought care for any issues related to diabetes and 51.8% sought care for a different health reason including infection (30.3%), muscle or bone problem (16.3%), heart problem (11.8%), abdominal problem (5.0%), eye related problems (4.7%), kidney problem (3.9%), respiratory problem (2.1%), neurological problem (2.1%), and women health issues (1.3%).

At the time of current visit, 40.9% of the patients attended the study hospital as their first choice of treatment and 59.1% visited another health facility before attending the study hospital. Those who visited another facility, 33.8% attended a government tertiary hospital, 6.2% attended a government primary or secondary hospital, 29.1% attended aprivate health facility, 24.4% BADAS and 6.5% attended a pharmacy (Table 2). Half of the patients visited the study hospital for seeking care for a health issue related to diabetes (50.1%), and 49.9% patients visited for a health issue not related to diabetes, such as, pain (26.3%), eye problem (23.6%) nonspecific complaints (21.9%), heart problem (7.9%), neurological problem (5.4%), kidney problems (4.0%), respiratory problems (4.0%), acute conditions (3.6%), and dental problem (3.2%).

Pattern of diabetic care

When the study participants were first diagnosed with diabetes, about 2/3rd of them (67.4%) started a diabetic care plan including drug, diet, and PA, followed by 6.4% on diet and drug, 9.0% on diet and PA, 4.9% on PA and drug, 7.5% on drug only, 0.2% on diet only, 0.6% were on PA only and 3.9% adopted no plan for diabetic care. At the time of survey, 60.1% of the patients reported to be on drug, diet and PA, 13.3% on diet and drug, 6.4% on diet and PA, 3.9% on PA and drug, 9.7% on drug only, 0.9% on diet only, and 1.3% on PA only and 4.5% adopted no plan for diabetic care (Table 2).

Behavioral risks among diabetic patients

Two thirds of the patients (67.7%) had two or more behavioral risk factors. Overall, 87.5% patients reported regular vegetable consumption and 19.8% patients reported regular fruits consumption. Half of the patients (50.3%) reported regular consumption of extra salt with meal and 32.7% had reported consumption of any form of tobacco (either chewing or smoking). The average IPAQ MET-score was 2014.7 (SD: 3137.5). Physical activity was low in 27.1%, moderate in 54.4% and high in 18.5% patients (Table 3).

Cardiometabolic risks among diabetic patients

The greater majority of the patients (98%) had hyperlipidemia, 46% were overweight and 24.7% were obese, and 34.2% had raised systolic or diastolic blood pressure. Among those who had hyperlipidemia, Overall, 97.6% had low HDL, 69.7% had raised triglycerides, 35.1% had a raised LDL level, and 9.2% had a raised total cholesterol level beyond the normal range (Table 3). The total cholesterol level was significantly correlated with LDL (corr: 0.86, P < 0.001), triglycerides (corr: 0.46, P < 0.001) and HDL (corr: 0.16, P = 0.001).

Glycemic control with diabetic care pattern, behavioural risks, cardiometabolic risks

One third of the patients (31.4%) had glycemic control (HbA1C: < 7.0%) and the level of HbA1C increased by 11.5% with increase of duration of diabetes by one year (corr: 0.115, P = 0.013). However, there was no difference in glycaemia control across age, sex, education and socio demographic status of the study patients(Supplementary Material).

Glycemic Control and Diabetic care

Glycemic control was better among those who maintained diabetic care plan of diet and PA without any drugs than those who had followed any other diabetic care plans (OR: 0.33, 95% CI 0.15–0.68). The odds of uncontrolled diabetes were more than two times higher if duration of diabetes was more than 5 years (OR: 2.66, 95% CI 1.78–4.00), two times higher if a patient was treated by drug only (OR:2.26, 95% CI: 1.02-4.99) and 87% higher if a patient visited the study hospital OPD for diabetic care (OR: 1.88, 95% CI 1.23–2.74), more than otherwise. . However, the type of provider, seeking medical care in the past six months, and the type of facility where diabetic care was sought when diabetes was first diagnosed were not associated with uncontrolled diabetes (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material).

Glycemic Control and Behavioral risks

The odds of uncontrolled diabetes was 93% higher if a patient had consumed chewing tobacco than otherwise (OR:1.93, 95% CI 1.24–3.02), 94% higher in those who had irregular vegetable consumption than those who had regular vegetable consumption (OR: 1.94, 95% CI 1.11–3.40), 79% higher in those who had irregular consumption of fruits (OR: 1.79, 95% CI 1.13–2.85), and 87% higher if a patient had consumed extra salt in meal in a day (OR:1.87, 95% CI 1.20–2.91). However, smoking tobacco and physical activity were not associated with uncontrolled diabetes (Table 2 and Supplementary Material).

Glycemic control and Cardiometabolic risks

The odds of uncontrolled diabetes was more than six times higher in those who had total cholesterol beyond the normal range (OR:6.4, 95% CI 1.60–25.62), more than two times higher in those who had LDL beyond the normal range (OR:2.73, 95% CI 1.39–5.37) and 96% higher in those who had triglyceride level beyond the normal range (OR: 1.96 95% CI1.29–2.96) than otherwise. However, there was no association of uncontrolled diabetes with BMI, raised blood pressure and having an associated comorbidity (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material).

Factors attributed to uncontrolled diabetes

Figure 1 (Model 1) demonstrated that the odds of uncontrolled diabetes was three times higher in those who had diabetes for more than 5 years compared to those who had diabetes for less than 5 years (OR: 3.31, 95% CI 2.04–5.27), and two times higher for those who visited the hospital OPDs for seeking care for diabetes compared to those who visited the hospital OPDs for other health reasons (OR: 2.39, 95% CI 1.47–3.80). For the behavioral risks, the odds of uncontrolled diabetes was there times higher in those who had irregular vegetable consumption than those who had regular consumption (OR: 3.70., 95% CI 1.80–7.59), two times higher in those who had irregular consumptions of fruits than those who had regular consumption (OR: 2.28, 95% CI 1.34–3.87), and 78% higher risk in those who consumed extra salt in any meal a day than those who did not (OR: 1.78, 95% CI 1.13–2.80). For the metabolic risks, the odds of uncontrolled debates was nine times higher in those who had raised total Cholesterol level compared to those who did not (OR: 9.6, 95% CI 2.60–35.4) and three times higher in those who had a raised a LDL level compared to those who did not (OR: 3.33, 95% CI: 1.93-5.70). After adjusting for age, sex, education and occupation( model 2), the association of these factors with uncontrolled diabetes remained unaltered and the association of total cholesterol with uncontrolled diabetes became stronger (OR:12.12, 95% CI: 3.18-46.17). Consumption of chewing tobacco, low physical activity and TG level were not found associated with uncontrolled diabetes in any model(Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study highlighted the pattern of diabetic care and explored the key factors attributing to uncontrolled diabetes among diabetic patients seeking care at outpatient department of tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh. The study has demonstrated that people with diabetes generally sought care from qualified medical doctors, albeit two-thirds of them did not have their diabetes under control . Half of the participants did not seek care in past 6 months and less frequent health care visits might have attributed to a higher odds of developing uncontrolled diabetes as observed previously among patients visiting hospital OPDs in Bangladesh41.

The mean age of diagnosis of diabetes in the study population was lower than the global average of 45 years, suggesting that people in Bangladesh develop diabetes at a younger age42. This is concerning because having diabetes at a younger age put an individual at a higher risk of developing diabetes related complications including associated co morbidity43,44. A significant proportion of participants in this study were diagnosed incidentally due to a laboratory test for a different health concern, which suggests that there is a large number of people in Bangladesh who might have developed diabetes, but are unaware of the conditions. A large number of women attending OPD services for diabetes supports that the services offered at the OPD of the government tertiary hospitals is accessible, particularly for women even they did not have a means of earning. However, only a small fraction of the patients was under the poverty line, suggesting that the economically vulnerable population might not have access to diabetic care provided at the OPD in government hospitals. The overall situation supports that there is a compelling need of integrating diabetic care at primary care facilities and monitoring of glycemic control among diabetic patients, which is currently absent in the primary care of Bangladesh15.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), maintaining a healthy diet and regular physical activity are important ways to prevent or delay the onset of type 2 diabetes45. Our study findings suggest that people who manage their diabetes through diet and exercise had twice the odds of controlled glycemic status than those who only use medication indicating that diet and exercise can improve insulin sensitivity and help lowering blood sugar levels. While vegetables and fruits are good sources of fiber and other nutrients that can help to lower blood sugar levels the study participants had reported irregular vegetable and fruit intake46. Irregular fruits and vegetables consumption was found to be significantly attributing to uncontrolled diabetes in the study population, which is aligned with the previous evidence from a meta-analysis supporting higher intake of fruits and vegetables consumption were associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes47. This calls for taking an urgent measure at any level of health care facilities for increasing awareness of lifestyle modifications among diabetic patients in Bangladesh.

However, contrary to the benefit of regular consumption of fruits and vegetables, we did not observe the role of physical activity in glycemic control among the study population, which is similar to previous findings reported in other LMICs48,49. It is possible that the data on physical activity was obtained by patient’s’ recall and we probably could not obtain credible data for more objective assessment of physical activity among diabetic patients, which has been reported previously in other chronic health conditions in Bangladesh50. Further investigations into the role of physical activity in controlling blood sugar level among diabetic patients would be critical for promoting physical activity in diabetes management in Bangladesh.

Excessive salt intake can lead to high blood pressure and other complications that can make it difficult to control blood sugar levels51. Although there is limited evidence on dietary salt consumption and its direct effect on glycemic control, it is well established that extra salt intake by diabetic patients with hypertension increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases and chronic kidney disease46,52. WHO recommends a maximum daily sodium intake of less than 2000 mg/day (less than 5 g/day salt) for adults53. In this study, nearly half of the patients consumed extra salt with everyday meals, and glycaemia control was better among those who did not add extra salt in meals. Our study emphasized the dire need of implementing a salt policy in the country immediately in order to prevent cardiovascular disease and its associated health consequences among the vulnerable population.

Our study has documented that people with longer duration of diabetes had poor glycemic control, which is consistent with previous research evidences supporting that a high proportion of people with diabetes in Bangladesh had poor control of blood sugar54,55. We have documented that uncontrolled diabetes was higher among people with low education and low education might have been a barrier to early diagnosis and management of diabetes, which has been reported previously9. However, we have observed that a large proportion of the study participants had reported an associated comorbidity, which might be attributable to glycemic control among the study participants. The overall situation suggest that people with diabetes cannot control their blood sugar levels, even though a greater majority has been treated with an anti-diabetic drug and this observation is similar to previous studies reported in India and China56,57. Integrating a comprehensive lifestyle program in diabetic care irrespective of pharmacological treatment is imperative for preventing diabetes related complications among diabetic patients in Bangladesh. The study has demonstrated that people with normal total cholesterol levels were more likely to have a better glycemic control than those who had a high cholesterol level, which is consistant with previous eivdences in low- and middle-income countries58,59. This is aligned with the underlying mechanism that high cholesterol levels damage blood vessels and make it more difficult for insulin to work properly, leading to high blood sugar level41. Diabetes is a progressive disease, meaning that it gets worse over time and thus routine monitoring of glycemic status at any health facility could be an effective screening measure for an early detection of uncontrolled blood glucose among diabetic patients in Bangladesh60.

This study had a few limitations. First, it was conducted only in a few tertiary care hospitals in urban areas, and the findings cannot be generalized to patients who visit OPDs at hospitals in rural settings. Second, we captured only those patients who attended a tertiary hospital, and patients attending a tertiary hospital may represent a group of patients who might have a more severe condition or being more health-conscious, hence not representing patients with diabetes who seek care at primary care facilities or private facilities for routine diabetic care. Third, about 2/3rd of the study patients were referred to our study hospitals by other facilities for better care, therefore our data may reflect a situation of the more severe condition of the diabetic patients, which may be different in the community for diabetic patients in general.

However, our goal was to assess the status of glycemic control among diabetic patients who were more conscious about diabetic care and had access to the best available care at a low cost, such as a government tertiary facility, which were representative of other government tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh. Nevertheless, the findings of the study on the relationship between uncontrolled diabetes and lifestyle, behavioral factors, diabetic care patterns, and comorbidities have provided important evidence for understanding poor glycemic control among diabetic patients seeking care the highest level of care available in the country.

Diabetic care at primary care facilities in Bangladesh is still in its early stages of development. Our study has demonstrated that, despite access to an optimal standard of care at a low cost, glycemic control among diabetic patients attending tertiary care hospitals is poor, suggesting an inadequate benefit of current diabetic care practices in Bangladesh. The government can reduce the burden of patients with uncontrolled diabetes at tertiary hospitals by integrating diabetic care in routine primary services and strengthening blood glucose monitoring at all tiers of health facilities. Healthcare providers should be well educated to promote awareness among diabetic patients about healthy lifestyle along with drug treatment for an effective management of diabetes. . Further research for situation assessment of the current diabetic care program in the community and understanding policy gaps would be an important step for developing a comprehensive model for diabetic care at primary care health facilities in both urban and rural settings in Bangladesh.

Conclusion

This study demonstrate s that the diabetic patients in Bangladesh follow a diverse diabetic care plan and have poor glycemic control, which may be attributable to inadequate lifestyle modifications among diabetic patients. Thus, i t is important to improve quality of care by promoting lifestyle modification s in addition to a physician- and treatment-oriented diabetes care pattern. More community-based studies are required to identify the common risk factors for uncontrolled diabetes and barriers to effective diabetic care plans. Comprehensive education on lifestyle modifications at the community level and routine monitoring of blood glucose at the primary care facilities linked with appropriate referral system would lead to an effective management of diabetes in Bangladesh.
