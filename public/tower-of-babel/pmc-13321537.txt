Perishable food supply chain management and food access in remote Indigenous communities of high-income countries: a systematic scoping review protocol
Authors: Behnaz Gharakhani Dehsorkhi, Karima Afif, Maurice Doyon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndigenous populations in high-income countries (HICs) experience disproportionately high rates of food insecurity, particularly in remote regions where high costs, poor quality, and limited variety limit access to perishable foods. Fragile perishable food supply chains (PFSCs) further compound these vulnerabilities. Although existing reviews have examined nutrition policies, socioeconomic interventions, and retail food environments, they have largely overlooked the operational and logistical challenges associated with perishable food supply chain management (PFSCM). Moreover, no review has systematically mapped the reported practices in the literature to address these challenges across interconnected supply chain levels, including procurement, transportation, distribution, and retail. To address these gaps, this systematic scoping literature review (SSLR) will employ a food access framework to synthesize the challenges and practices of PFSCM in remote Indigenous communities across 17 HICs.MethodsThis SSLR will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Protocols (PRISMA-P). A three-step JBI search strategy will be used to identify relevant published and unpublished evidence, including grey literature. Databases will include IEEE, MEDLINE (Ovid), ABI/Inform Global (ProQuest), CAB Abstracts, and Web of Science (Core Collection). Grey literature will be sourced from the Grey Source Index, Web of Conferences, government publications, OpenDOAR, OpenAIRE, and SSRN. Sources will be included if published in English between 1996 and 2024 and if they address PFSCM (phenomenon), perishable food access (concept), and remote Indigenous communities in HICs (context). Data will be extracted using a structured framework. Descriptive statistics will summarize study characteristics, and thematic analysis will be conducted to identify patterns in PFSCM challenges and reported practices. NVivo will support data management, and results will be reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines.DiscussionThis review will provide the first systematic scoping synthesis of PFSCM challenges and reported practices in remote Indigenous communities across HICs. By integrating a food access framework with a systems-oriented supply chain perspective, the review will extend prior work that has primarily focused on retail or policy interventions and limited geographic settings. The resulting evidence map and conceptual framework will support future research, policy dialogue, and context-sensitive planning to strengthen perishable food systems and food access in remote Indigenous settings.Systematic review registrationOpen science framework registration: https://osf.io/kqpvnSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03156-3.

Background

Indigenous peoples represent less than 5% of the population in high-income countries (HICs), yet they experience disproportionately high rates of food insecurity, particularly in remote regions [1–5]. In these contexts, geographic isolation, limited market competition, and constrained transportation and storage infrastructure contribute to high food prices, poor quality, and restricted variety of perishable foods, undermining equitable access to healthy diets [6–9]. For example, up to 92% of tribes in the Klamath River Basin in the USA [10] and nearly 70% of Inuit households in Nunavut, Canada, are affected by food insecurity [11, 12]. Addressing these persistent disparities requires context-sensitive research that examines not only socioeconomic determinants but also the structural, operational, and logistical challenges that shape access to fresh, nutritious foods in remote Indigenous communities [8, 13, 14].

Food supply chains (FSCs) serving remote regions are highly vulnerable to environmental and seasonal disruptions, including extreme weather events and infrastructure failures, which delay deliveries and exacerbate spoilage and stockouts [8, 15, 16]. In northern Canada, climate change has contributed to warming rates that are three to four times the global average, thereby increasing the frequency and severity of disturbances such as wildfires, flooding, and permafrost thaw, and further straining already fragile supply systems [17–19]. Within FSCs, perishable food supply chains (PFSCs) are particularly vulnerable due to temperature sensitivity, limited shelf life, and elevated spoilage risk [20, 21]. Inadequate perishable food supply chain management (PFSCM), including challenges in procurement, cold chain integrity, transportation reliability, inventory management, and retail operations, restricts the availability, affordability, and quality of perishable foods in remote communities [4, 8, 22–25]. These constraints operate alongside broader determinants, including high transportation costs, economic marginalization, and the enduring legacy of colonial and infrastructural policies that continue to shape food systems and market access [11, 26, 27].

Addressing the interconnected PFSCM challenges across the supply chain requires a systemic approach that considers operational and managerial links from upstream sourcing to downstream household access [28]. Supply chain management (SCM) encompasses interdependent subsystems, including forecasting, procurement, production planning, supplier and order management, warehousing, distribution, and product development [29, 30]. Limited, single-level interventions risk perpetuating isolated solutions, whereas holistic, coordinated strategies can enhance PFSC efficiency and resilience [31, 32]. Adopting this approach, this review maps PFSCM challenges across procurement and supply, transportation, distribution, retail, and household consumption, and synthesizes the practices reported in the literature to address them. In SCM, practices are defined as “unique ways to configure a set of processes (Configuration), unique ways to automate a set of processes (Technology), and/or unique ways to perform a set of processes (Knowledge) that result in significantly better results” [33]. Building on this definition and the Supply Chain Operations Reference (SCOR) model, this review uses the term PFSCM practices to refer to interventions, strategies, and approaches reported to address PFSCM challenges and to enhance access to perishable food in remote Indigenous communities [33].

This review adopts food access as an analytical framework to synthesize how challenges in PFSCM affect Indigenous community food security and to map reported practices that can foster more sustainable food systems. Food access, a core pillar of food security, refers to the ability of individuals and households to obtain and afford sufficient, nutritious food [34]. Beyond the physical availability of food, it encompasses social, cultural, and economic dimensions, ensuring that food is safe, adequate, and culturally appropriate [35]. In remote regions, food access plays a crucial role in shaping dietary behaviors and overall food security outcomes [24, 36, 37]. Restricted access to fresh, perishable foods has been consistently linked to poor diet quality, an increased risk of food insecurity, and a higher prevalence of nutrition-related diseases [37–39]. Moreover, drawing on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs [40], food access represents a foundational prerequisite: without reliable access to nutritious foods, higher-order dimensions of food security, such as utilization and stability, cannot be fully achieved [41]. By framing PFSCM challenges and solutions through a food access framework, this review highlights not only operational and logistical constraints but also their broader implications for health, equity, and community well-being.

Research objective and contribution

An initial search revealed no existing or in-progress systematic or scoping reviews addressing PFSCM challenges and practices in remote Indigenous communities within HICs. Existing reviews have primarily examined food and nutrition policies [42], socioeconomic interventions [43], and retail food environments [23, 44]. Although these reviews identified barriers to healthy eating and discussed social and cultural strategies to increase consumption of fresh foods, they did not address the operational and logistical challenges of supplying perishable foods to these regions. Additionally, earlier reviews mainly focused on retail-related challenges [23, 43, 44]. However, addressing the PFSCM challenges requires a broader perspective on PFSC levels that encompasses procurement and supply, transportation, distribution, retail, and household consumption [28]. Previous reviews also overlooked the PFSCM practices implemented to address these challenges [23, 43]. Additionally, prior reviews remain geographically concentrated on Australia, Canada, and the USA, with limited attention to other HICs where comparable remote food supply challenges may exist [23, 43]. A broader evidence mapping is therefore needed to support cross-regional learning and the adaptation of context-sensitive practices to address PFSCM challenges [43].

This review aims to advance understanding of PFSCM in remote Indigenous communities across HICs by: (i) identifying and classifying PFSCM challenges that affect food access across all supply chain levels, (ii) synthesizing PFSCM practices reported in the literature to address these challenges, and (iii) organizing these challenges and practices into an integrative conceptual framework that highlights interdependencies across supply chain levels and food access dimensions. A systematic scoping literature review (SSLR) is particularly suitable for this goal because it facilitates systematic evidence mapping, clarifies key concepts, and helps identify knowledge gaps in an emerging and interdisciplinary field [45, 46]. By integrating supply chain and food access perspectives, this review provides a conceptual foundation for future empirical research, policy dialogue, and Indigenous-led strategies to strengthen perishable food systems in remote, high-income contexts. The remainder of this protocol is organized as follows. The Methodology section outlines the review design, eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening procedures, data extraction, and planned synthesis approach. Finally, the Discussion section summarizes implications for research, policy, and Indigenous food system resilience in these contexts.

Methodology

Study design

An SSLR will be conducted to synthesize existing knowledge on PFSCM in remote Indigenous communities in HICs. The review aims to identify PFSCM challenges, map reported practices implemented to address them, and highlight gaps in the existing literature. This SSLR will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and adhere to the PRISMA-P checklist to ensure transparency, consistency, and methodological rigor (see Additional file 1: Table S1) [46–48].

Review research questions

Research questions are formulated using the Population/Phenomenon, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework [49]. For this SSLR, the phenomenon is PFSCM, the concept is perishable food access, and the context is remote Indigenous communities in HICs (see Additional file 2: Table S2). The research questions are:RQ1. What PFSCM challenges affect food access in remote Indigenous communities in HICs, and at which supply chain levels do these challenges occur?RQ2. What PFSCM practices are reported in the literature to address these challenges?RQ3. How can PFSCM challenges and practices be systematically synthesized and organized into an integrative conceptual framework that captures their interrelationships across supply chain levels and food access dimensions?

Inclusion criteria

This SSLR focuses on PFSCM challenges and reported practices in remote and rural Indigenous communities located in HICs, as defined by the World Bank income classification framework [50]. As no universal definition of Indigenous peoples has been formally adopted by the United Nations, terminology varies across national contexts and may include terms such as tribes, First Peoples, or First Nations [51]. In accordance with the recommendation of the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues to identify rather than define Indigenous peoples, this protocol adopts an inclusive and context-sensitive approach to population identification [51]. Following the methodology outlined by Cisneros-Montemayor et al. (2016) [52], we systematically examined each HIC to determine whether Indigenous populations reside in remote or rural regions, which also informed the selection of population-specific search terms.

To support a transparent and reproducible identification process, multiple authoritative sources were consulted, including the World Bank Atlas classification system [53], Yale University’s eHRAF World Cultures database [54], and the World Directory of Minorities and Indigenous Peoples [55], complemented by targeted Internet searches of national census and demographic information. Both state-recognized and unrecognized groups were considered, particularly those identified as the original or earliest known inhabitants of a region and/or those maintaining historical continuity with precolonial and/or pre-settler societies [23, 51, 52]. This systematic approach enhances consistency across keyword searching, study screening, and contextual interpretation.

This review is limited to remote and rural Indigenous populations in HICs to reduce heterogeneity in governance, infrastructure, and policy contexts, facilitating a meaningful synthesis of PFSCM challenges and practices. Urban Indigenous populations are excluded due to substantially different food environments and supply chain dynamics. Eligibility criteria for screening

The review will be restricted to English-language sources published between January 1, 1996, and December 31, 2024. A pilot Web of Science search was conducted to illustrate publication trends (Additional file 3: Fig. S1). The year 1996 was selected as the starting point because it marks the introduction of the seminal definition of food security and its four pillars (availability, access, utilization, and stability) by the FAO [34]. The language restriction is justified by the focus on remote Indigenous communities in HICs, where most relevant research is published in English (e.g., Canada, Australia, the USA, and New Zealand). While this may exclude some studies from non-Anglophone HICs, the proportion of relevant evidence is expected to be small, and this restriction ensures feasibility and methodological consistency for the review.

Sources will be included if they examine market-based perishable foods, either broadly (all perishable categories) or specifically (e.g., fruits and vegetables). Studies focusing solely on traditional or subsistence foods (e.g., hunting, fishing, agriculture for personal consumption) without a commercial or market dimension will be excluded. Non-food items (e.g., cigarettes, alcohol) will also be excluded. Included sources are required to explicitly address PFSCM, encompassing challenges and practices across production, supply, transport, distribution, retail, and household access. Sources that focus solely on health, diet, or food security outcomes without an explicit link to PFSC, or public health initiatives lacking PFSC components, are excluded. To be charted as a reported practice, a source must describe an intervention or strategy aimed at addressing PFSCM challenges and report implementation details or outcomes discussed in relation to food access components (e.g., availability, affordability, quality, variety, cultural acceptability). This definition ensures that recognized PFSCM practices are evidence-based, actionable, and contextually relevant to PFSCM in these communities.

Search strategy

A JBI three-step search strategy will be employed for this SSLR [47]. First, a limited initial search will be conducted in MEDLINE (Ovid) and Web of Science to identify relevant articles. Text words in the titles and abstracts, as well as index terms of included articles, will be analysed to inform the development of a comprehensive search strategy. Second, the refined keywords and index terms identified in step 1 will be used to conduct a systematic search across multiple databases, including IEEE, MEDLINE (Ovid), ABI/Inform Global (ProQuest), CAB Abstract, and Web of Science (core collection as provided by Université Laval; Editions = SCI-EXPANDED, SSCI, AHCI, CPCI-S, CPCI-SSH, BKCI-S, BKCI-SSH, ESCI). Sources of unpublished studies and grey literature will be searched via the Grey Source Index, Web of Conferences, government publications for countries with remote Indigenous communities, OpenDOAR, OpenAIRE, and SSRN. The search strategy will be adapted for each database and information source. Third, Reference lists of all included studies will be screened to identify additional relevant studies not captured in the database searches.

Systematic searches will employ a combination of keywords that cover the three core concepts of this review: remote Indigenous communities, PFSCM, and perishable food access (Table 3). The search strategy will be piloted to assess the appropriateness of selected electronic databases and keywords (Additional file 3: Table S3). Study/source of evidence selection

Following the search, all identified citations will be collated and uploaded into Covidence, and duplicates will be removed. A pilot test will be conducted to strengthen inter-rater reliability, in which a random sample of 50 records will be independently screened by two reviewers using the tool provided in Additional file 4: Table S4. Titles and abstracts of all citations will be screened independently by two reviewers based on the eligibility criteria (see Table 2). This independent screening ensures an unbiased assessment [56]. Potentially relevant sources will be retrieved in full for further assessment. Full-text screening will also be carried out independently by two reviewers. Any disagreements will first be resolved through discussion. If a consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted to make a decision. The review team has allocated sufficient capacity to manage the workflow, supported by Covidence for screening and NVivo for organizing and synthesizing data. Reasons for exclusion will be documented and reported in the SSLR. The results of the search and the study inclusion process will be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram [57] (Additional file 5: Fig. S2).

Data extraction

For sources meeting the inclusion criteria after full-text review, data will be extracted by two independent reviewers using a structured data extraction questionnaire (see Additional file 6: Table S5). The draft extraction tool will be piloted on a subset of studies and iteratively refined during extraction to ensure the capture of all relevant information. Any modifications will be documented in the SSLR. Disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer serving as an arbitrator for unresolved conflicts. Authors may be contacted to obtain missing or clarifying information if necessary.

For each included source, detailed information on the phenomenon, concept, context, study methodology, and key findings relevant to the review questions will be documented within Covidence (Table 4.). Consistent with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews, data will be systematically coded according to the categories outlined in Table 4., using a thematic analysis framework to categorize PFSCM challenges and practices [47, 56]. Both reviewers will independently code and regularly consult with each other to ensure consistent interpretation. Discrepancies in context across studies will be discussed and resolved. Data charting scheme

Data analysis and presentation

Extracted data will be systematically summarized to map PFSCM challenges and reported practices in remote Indigenous communities in HICs, identify gaps, and provide an overview of the literature [45, 46]. A narrative synthesis will accompany the charted results to explain how the literature describes challenges and reported practices across PFSC levels and food access components, without ranking interventions or inferring effectiveness. Descriptive quantitative summaries will be reported using frequencies and percentages for key study characteristics, including year of publication, country, region, study design, food type, PFSC levels addressed, and food access components discussed (e.g., availability, affordability, quality, cultural acceptability). To support context-aware interpretation, each reported practice will be charted alongside contextual and implementation details provided in the source, including geographic setting, targeted PFSC levels, and the affected food access component.

Qualitative data will be imported into NVivo 12 and analysed using Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-step thematic analysis, consistent with JBI guidance for scoping reviews [47]. Two reviewers will independently code data and iteratively harmonize interpretations, resolving disagreements through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer. Thematic coding will capture PFSCM challenges, reported practices, the contextual conditions under which they are described, and how studies link these elements to food access components. Quantitative summaries will be used to contextualize themes (e.g., which PFSC levels and food access components are most frequently addressed), while qualitative findings will provide explanatory depth regarding how PFSCM challenges and reported practices are framed across contexts. Results will be reported in a structured format aligned with the review objectives and PRISMA-ScR guidelines [48]. This approach ensures a rigorous, context-sensitive synthesis that informs policy, practice, and future research on PFSCM in remote Indigenous communities.

Discussion

This SSLR will synthesize the knowledge on PFSCM in remote Indigenous communities in HICs. By systematically mapping evidence across all levels of the supply chain, including procurement, supply, transportation, distribution, retail, and household access, the review will highlight how PFSCM challenges are associated with multiple dimensions of food access. This systems-oriented perspective has been largely absent in previous literature [16, 23]. Building on this synthesis, the review will also identify key PFSCM practices reported in the literature to address these challenges and integrate them into a comprehensive conceptual framework. By mapping existing evidence, synthesizing reported practices, and developing a conceptual framework, this SSLR aims to address a critical gap in documenting context-sensitive practices for mitigating PFSCM challenges, drawing on evidence from remote Indigenous populations and providing opportunities for cross-regional learning and adaptation [8, 13, 14, 25, 58].

Failures across multiple PFSC levels can exacerbate food insecurity in remote Indigenous communities by limiting access to market-sold perishable foods [3–5, 10, 59]. In this review, food access will be employed as an analytical framework to situate PFSCM challenges within the broader context of Indigenous food security. This approach emphasizes not only the operational and logistical barriers affecting perishable food flows but also their linkages to community-level access outcomes, including food availability, affordability, quality, and cultural acceptability. Framing the synthesis through food access will support a clearer understanding of how PFSCM challenges and reported practices are represented across diverse remote contexts and where evidence remains limited or uneven.

The outputs of this SSLR will inform future research and practice in several important ways. First, it will provide a multiscale, context-sensitive, and systems-oriented synthesis of the PFSCM literature, highlighting how challenges in the perishable food supply chain and reported practices are discussed across interconnected levels of procurement, transportation, distribution, retail, and household access. Second, by shifting the analytical emphasis beyond food availability toward broader dimensions of food access, the review will strengthen understanding of how PFSCM is situated within food security debates in remote Indigenous contexts. Third, the integrative conceptual framework developed through this synthesis will organize dispersed evidence by linking reported PFSCM challenges, practice domains, and food access components, thereby supporting comparative analysis, future empirical inquiry, and theory development in this emerging interdisciplinary field. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations to support knowledge sharing and foster collaboration among researchers, policymakers, and community-based initiatives working to strengthen perishable food systems, improve food access, and enhance supply chain resilience in remote Indigenous settings.
