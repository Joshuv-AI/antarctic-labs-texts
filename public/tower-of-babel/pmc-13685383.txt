Reducing healthcare anxiety in children with medical play: assessing effectiveness of the teddy bear bash
Authors: Kayla Jane Joyce, Mackenzie Merrell Macza Heidel, Amanda Hall, Susan A Bobbitt, Wendi Stumborg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical anxiety is common in the pediatric population. Anxiety may trigger persistent negative emotions regarding medical care, cause decreased cooperation during procedures, and lead to higher reports of post-procedure pain. Teddy Bear Hospital (TBH) events may reduce pediatric anxiety related to health care visits by providing exposure to medical professionals and simulating procedures in a play environment. Literature on the effectiveness of such events is currently limited. We aimed to assess the effectiveness of a TBH-modelled event, the Teddy Bear Bash, in reducing pediatric reports of medical anxiety.MethodsIn a prospective cohort study, we surveyed 69 parents/guardians and 68 children during attendance at the Teddy Bear Bash in Saskatoon, Saskatchewan. Surveys were completed pre-event, immediately post-event, and 4 weeks after the event. Surveys assessed comfort level with medical care, reasons for medical anxiety, and improvements in either post-event.Results74.4% of parents/guardians reported subjective improvement in their child’s medical anxiety post-event, with 34.9% of these identifying a lasting change at 4-week follow-up. A statistically significant positive change in pediatric reports of feelings towards health care workers was demonstrated post-event (Z = -3.002, p = 0.003). Fears related to medical appointments in the pediatric population were decreased post-event, most markedly in concerns related to receiving an injection (a 31% decrease in percent responses). However, improvement of anxiety-related behaviours during medical appointments post-event was not clearly elicited.ConclusionsOur findings suggest the Teddy Bear Bash is beneficial in promoting subjective reports of decreased medical-related anxiety in children. Exposure to the Teddy Bear Bash encourages a positive change in feelings toward health care workers by pediatric participants. These findings can likely be extrapolated to other TBH-type events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07295-3.Keywords: Medical play, Child, Anxiety, Health promotion, Teddy bear hospital

Introduction

Teddy Bear Hospitals (TBHs) provide pretend medical care to children’s stuffed toys. This model of medical play is an international initiative aiming to teach children about medical care through fun and developmentally-appropriate simulation and reduce their anxiety around seeking medical care [1–10]. Medical care can induce fear and anxiety in children of various ages. This is a result of necessary but painful procedures, age-related lack of comprehension, and unfamiliar environments [11]. Anxiety is associated with a decreased ability to maintain emotional regulation and cooperate with medical care, as well as the development of negative emotions associated with medical situations [12, 13]. This leads to decreased compliance with medical care, increased perceptions of pain, and increased length of medical procedures [13, 14].

Engaging in medical play with toys may transform sometimes frightening medical devices, like stethoscopes and syringes, into tools that increase a child’s understanding and acceptance of medical procedures [11]. This type of play among children has been suggested to reduce stress associated with the visit, increase positive emotions, and allow time to ask questions and express their needs during a medical visit [9, 11, 12, 14–20]. Therapeutic play encourages children to interact with health care workers, providing them with opportunities to be active participants in their health care, instead of passive subjects [11]. Children who have participated in TBH models demonstrated improved health care knowledge when assessed with pre and post surveys [9, 16, 21]. Having increased knowledge of health care has also been associated with a greater desire to see the doctor when sick [21]. In Canada, both Western University’s Schulich School of Medicine and Dentistry and the McMaster School of Medicine have hosted reportedly successful TBH events in the past [6, 7]. However, academic research on the anti-anxiety impacts of TBH-type models, especially in Canada, is limited. Of note, parental perceptions of the effects of TBHs are missing from current literature. In a systematic review of the literature on TBH-type models, Rashid and colleagues identified only 3 randomized controlled trials within 10 included studies, and only 2 of these studies included parental perceptions [9].

The Harris’ Hope Foundation (HHF) aims to assist Saskatoon, Saskatchewan families coping with difficult times from health challenges, with a particular focus on perinatal loss and medical education. Since 2022, the foundation has hosted an annual Teddy Bear Bash where children can bring their stuffed toy for a health check-up in an outdoor setting that mimics the flow of a tertiary care emergency department.

We investigated the responses of parents/guardians and their children prior-to, immediately post-attendance, and 4-weeks post-attendance of the HHF’s Teddy Bear Bash. We sought to determine parental and child reports of medical-related anxiety before and immediately after the Teddy Bear Bash, to assess whether significant reductions in anxiety-related behaviours and feelings were reported. Further, we assessed for lasting changes in reports of medical anxiety in children, as demonstrated by decreased hesitancy towards medical appointments or procedures at 4-week follow-up after the event. We aimed to learn about the impact of the TBH model of medical play and provide suggestions for further incorporation of medical play in the health care setting as a means of reducing anxiety about medical appointments or procedures in the pediatric population.

Methods

Setting

The Harris’ Hope Foundation’s Teddy Bear Bash is an annual one-day event held in Saskatoon, Saskatchewan, Canada. Children are encouraged to bring their stuffed toys to the park for a medical check-up. The Teddy Bear Bash is advertised online using the social media platforms Facebook and Instagram and is marketed as a family-friendly event open to all ages. The event has historically also been featured on local news websites for the Saskatoon, Saskatchewan region and on newsletters sent out by the University of Saskatchewan’s College of Pharmacy and Nutrition, College of Nursing, and College of Medicine.

The event is run by health professionals, health care students, and community volunteers. Children bring their stuffed animals and start at the triage station, then continue through vital signs, phlebotomy, casting, stitches, and medical imaging. The stations use both real medical devices (needles, stethoscopes, casting material) and simulated props (e.g. x-ray). The stations in the Teddy Bear Bash are congruent with the general TBH-type model. Additional entertainment includes balloons, music, a photo booth, a fire truck, face painting, and costumed children’s characters. The event costs $20.00 CAD per family to attend with 80 free tickets provided to public libraries in Saskatoon for distribution to families experiencing financial hardship. All funds raised go to the charitable efforts of the HHF.

Survey

Participants were surveyed in compliance with the University of Saskatchewan’s Behavioural Research Ethics Board. All parents/guardians completed a voluntary consent form for their participation and an assent form for the child’s participation in the surveys. All consenting attendees of the Teddy Bear Bash were eligible for inclusion in the study. Participants were ineligible for inclusion if language or cognitive barriers were present to the degree of impeding the ability to consent to participate or accurately partake in completion of the surveys. Pediatric participants were also ineligible for inclusion if significant developmental or cognitive delay was deemed to impair the ability to assent for the survey, participate in the event, or accurately complete the surveys. Participation required a working proficiency of the English language.

Children and parents/guardians both completed separate pre-event surveys. The parent/guardian pre-event survey explored the child’s prior experiences with and reactions to medical care [see Additional file 1]. The pediatric pre-event survey used a variety of emoji-based Likert scales, yes or no questions, and select-all-that-apply questions to explore feelings about seeking medical care [see Additional file 2]. Assistance from parents/guardians and researchers was provided to children to help them to understand and complete the surveys, namely in reading survey questions to children if needed or clarifying what a survey question is asking of the child. Both parents/guardians and researchers were instructed to provide only neutral assistance to the pediatric participants and avoid guiding the child to an answer. Parents/guardians were observed by researchers if assisting their child in completing the survey to avoid leading questions or influence. If a pediatric participant’s answer to a survey question was unclear or the child wished not to answer a question, the question was left blank.

Children and parents who participated in the pre-event survey were given a post-event survey after the event. The parent/guardian post-event survey inquired about perceived changes to their child’s level of medical anxiety [see Additional file 3]. The pediatric post-event survey explored how participating in the event made participants feel and how the event impacted their perceptions of medical care [see Additional file 4]. Parents/guardians were surveyed over the phone 4 weeks following the event to explore whether the event increased their child’s comfort with medical care at any subsequent medical appointments [see Additional file 5].

After data was collected at 4-week follow-up, all responses were de-identified prior to data analysis. Statistical analysis was performed by a biostatistician. Researchers were not involved in the medical-play interventions during the Teddy Bear Bash.

Those who participated in the research at any stage were entered to win 2 prizes worth $100.00 CAD.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25 was used for descriptive statistics like mean, median, and standard deviation for continuous variables. Categorical variables were represented by frequency and percentages. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality, which resulted in distribution being non-parametric and hence Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for assessment of statistical significance of findings.

Results

Participants

834 people attended the Teddy Bear Bash event, of which 73 pediatric responses and 74 parent/guardian responses were gathered (Fig. 1). No participants were considered ineligible for inclusion based on the previously described exclusion criteria. Responses from 3 parent/guardian and pediatric pairings were withdrawn due to incomplete assent and/or consent forms, and 1 pairing withdrew consent upon 4-week follow-up. Thus, 68 pediatric pre-event responses and 69 parent/guardian pre-event responses were considered complete. 35 parents/guardians (50.7%) submitted a post-event survey. 37 children (54.4%) submitted a post-event survey. 43 parents/guardians (62.3%) participated in the 4-week post-event follow-up survey. In total, 66 parent/guardian-pediatric pairings submitted their respective pre-event surveys. Of these, 35 of the parent/guardian-pediatric pairings submitted their respective post-event surveys. 24 of these pairings also participated in the 4-week follow-up survey. Thus, 24 pairings completed all components of the study, and 35 completed all components with the exception of the 4-week follow-up. The remaining 19 of 43 total parent/guardian participants in the 4-week follow-up survey submitted a pre-event survey, but did not submit their survey immediately post-event. Sixty-seven of 68 responses to the pediatric demographic information provided the age and biological sex of the participant. Parents/guardians were asked about their child’s previous exposures to doctor’s appointments, dental appointments, surgery, vaccination/blood draws, emergency room visits, hospital admissions, specialist visits, and were invited to share other healthcare related exposures (e.g. radiological imaging, counselling, speech language pathology services). All of the surveyed participants who answered regarding their child’s previous exposures to medical appointments or procedures identified their child as having undergone at least one type of medical exposure (66 of 69 respondents, 3 having not answered the prompt). 49 of these 66 respondents (74.2%) reported their child as having three or more unique medical exposures, 30 of which (45.5%) were children without a diagnosed medical condition.

Parent/guardian responses

In the parent/guardian pre-event survey, prior behaviours displayed by their child related to anxiety responses during medical appointments or procedures were identified (Fig. 2). A total of 67 pre-event survey responses for this prompt were received from 36 individual respondents. Respondents could select multiple answers in response to the prompt. Clear apprehension prior to the appointment or procedure was most frequently reported, with 20 responses (29.9%). Apparent distress, with the ability to continue the appointment, was reported by 14 (20.9%) participants. This was followed by excessive withdrawal during the appointment or procedure with 12 responses (17.9%) and excessive crying during the appointment or procedure with 11 responses (16.4%). Six participants (9%) identified a need to stop or reschedule an appointment or procedure due to significant distress in their child during the appointment. Two respondents (3%) identified an inability of their child to be consoled during the appointment. Only one response (1.5%) identified both syncope and vomiting related to a medical appointment or procedure as having occurred in the past, presumably indicative of significant emotional distress.

At 4-week follow-up, 19 of 43 total respondents (44.2%) stated that their child had attended a medical visit or procedure since the Teddy Bear Bash event. Of these, 8 pediatric participants were identified as having received a vaccination or blood draw since the event, 7 had attended a doctor’s appointment, 6 had attended a dental appointment, 3 had attended a specialist appointment, and 3 had other visit types (attending a chiropractic appointment, undergoing an MRI, and receiving immunotherapy treatment). Six of the pediatric participants had attended two or more visits since the event. 12 of these respondents answered the choose-all-that-apply prompt regarding pediatric behaviours during the appointment, for which 25 total responses were received (Fig. 2). Clear apprehension about the appointment or procedure was again the most reported response, with 11 total responses (44.0%). This was followed by apparent distress with 5 responses (20.0%) and excessive crying with 4 responses (16%). Only 1 respondent identified excessive withdrawal (4.0%), and only 2 (8%) identified a need to stop or reschedule an appointment secondary to anxiety. Two responses (8%) were again received for an inability to be consoled during an appointment. No respondents identified syncope or vomiting in relation to a medical appointment or procedure. Six parents/guardians stated that their child had been to an appointment post-event and that their child did not exhibit any of the above behaviours. One parent/guardian declined to answer this prompt. The remaining 24 of 43 respondents (55.8%) could not identify if their child would display these behaviours, as their child had not yet attended a medical appointment in the four weeks after the Teddy Bear Bash.

Overall, 51.6% of parents/guardians (32 of 62 responses) described their child as generally anxious prior to the event. 36.9% of parents/guardians (24 of 65 responses) stated their child had attended the Teddy Bear Bash in the past. Nine parents/guardians responded further to state they noted an improvement in their child’s level of medical anxiety after attending the prior event (9 of 17 responses, 52.9%). All parents/guardians responding to the 4-week follow-up survey were asked whether or not they felt participation in the Teddy Bear Bash increased their child’s comfort level with medical care, regardless whether or not the child had yet attended a medical visit since the event. 39.5% of parents/guardians (17 of 43 respondents) felt there was a positive and lasting change to their child’s comfort level with medical care at 4-week follow-up, and an additional 34.9% of parents/guardians (15 of 43 respondents) identified at least an initial improvement after attendance. Of the 17 respondents reporting a positive and lasting change in their child’s comfort level with medical care, 8 had attended a medical visit since the Teddy Bear Bash. Of the 15 respondents who identified at least an initial improvement in their child’s comfort level with medical care, 7 had attended a medical visit since the event. 25.6% of parents/guardians (11 of 43 respondents) reported no improvement in their child’s apparent comfort level with medical care post-event, though only 4 of these children had attended a medical visit since the event.

Pediatric responses

Pre-event, 62 individual surveys from pediatric participants answered a choose-all-that-apply prompt querying what is the most worrisome part about seeing a physician, for a total of 195 chosen responses (Fig. 3). Fear of receiving an injection was the most reported concern, with 40 responses (64.5%). This was followed by the fear of pain and feeling uncertain of what is happening during the appointment, both with 35 responses (56.5%). Concern about being in a new environment received 26 responses (41.9%). Concern about being prescribed a medication received 20 responses (32.3%). Worry about being required to stay home from school or activities had 18 responses (29.0%). Worry about feeling uncomfortable with the physician or being invalidated during an appointment were the least reported concerns, with 11 (17.7%) and 10 (16.1%) reports, respectively.

Pediatric responses to “What makes you most worried about going to the doctor?” both pre-Teddy Bear Bash (n = 62) and immediately post-Teddy Bear Bash (n = 36). Immediately post-event, 36 pediatric participants answered in response to the same choose-all-that-apply prompt, for a total of 70 chosen responses (Fig. 3). Fear of pain remained highly reported, with 20 responses (55.6%). However, the percent response to all other prompts decreased. Fear of receiving an injection decreased substantially in the overall percentage reported, with 12 responses (33.3%). Feeling uncertain about what might happen during an appointment also decreased to 12 responses (33%). Concern about being in a new environment decreased to 9 responses (25.0%). Concern about being prescribed medication decreased to 7 responses (19.4%). Worry about being invalidated during an appointment decreased to 4 responses (11.1%). Worry about missing school or activities decreased to only 3 responses (8.3%), as did concerns about the physician being mean or making a child feel uncomfortable.

Declared trust in health care workers was high both pre- and post-event, with percent responses remaining stable throughout. 83.3% of participants responded that they trust health care workers both pre- and post-event (with 55 and 30 respondents, respectively). 16.7% of participants declared lack of trust pre- and post-event (with 11 and 6 respondents, respectively).

Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed a statistically significant difference in feelings about healthcare workers from pre-event to post-event (Z = -3.002, p = 0.003). The distribution of ranks indicates that more participants showed improved feelings (18 positive ranks) compared to decreased feelings (3 negative ranks), while 15 participants showed no change.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate a reported improvement in both parental/guardian perceptions of their child’s medical anxiety and in pediatric self-reported anxiety post-Teddy Bear Bash. These findings were observed in the majority of participants. For surveyed children, the most notable reduction was in regard to fear of receiving an injection, with a 31% drop in percentage reported. Fear of being in a new place decreased in percentage by 17%, and fear of uncertainty about what will happen during the appointment decreased in percentage by 23%. These findings support that play medical exposures during the Teddy Bear Bash result in decreased subjective reports of pediatric concerns about medical visits.

Intravenous procedures and injections are cited as some of the most fear-invoking components of medical care in the pediatric population and are frequently reported as sources of medical-related anxiety in children [11, 15, 18]. Our demonstration of a 31% decrease in pediatric reports of concern regarding injections post-event suggests exposure to such procedures in a play environment is beneficial in alleviating some of this fear. This result is congruent with that of da Silva et al., in which children first exposed to a play activity with a doll simulating a venipuncture procedure demonstrated decreased negative reactions and increased cooperation when undergoing venipuncture themselves [11]. By providing play exposure to injections in a non-painful environment, TBH-type events may allow children a higher degree of efficacy in coping with future intravenous procedures and reduce uncertainty regarding such procedures in future medical appointments.

Overall, limited studies on the TBH-type model have analysed parental perspectives or behavioural changes per systematic review [9]. Our study is one of the first to attempt to characterize behavioural changes in children during medical appointments with delayed follow-up post-intervention. Previously described by Burns-Nader, parental reports of a child’s anxiety level were positively correlated with the child’s self-reported anxiety and a higher baseline pulse [22]. These findings suggest that, within our current study, the parent/guardian reports of decreased perceived medical anxiety in their child post-event may be indicative of decreased anxiety within the child.

Statistically significant improvement in pediatric feelings toward health care workers was demonstrated post-event. This suggests the Teddy Bear Bash is an effective model for decreasing reports of negative feelings in children towards health care workers, likely indicative of decreased anxiety towards medical visits as well. This finding is in keeping with that of Bloch & Toker, who found that children with a similar mean age as our study population (5.21 ± 0.4 years) reported significantly lower levels of medical-related anxiety after exposure to a TBH event as opposed to control [18]. Positive impacts of TBH-type events on pediatric self-reported medical-related anxiety are also demonstrated in systematic review [9]. However, Burns-Nader found that a medical play intervention prior to a physician visit actually increased children’s self-reported fear and resulted in children displaying higher distress behaviours (e.g. kicking and squirming) when assessed by nurses [22]. Burns-Nader’s analysis took place in a medical clinic, rather than the park-based setting of the Teddy Bear Bash observed in this study [22]. Additionally, Burns-Nader’s analysis made use of a provided study doll, rather than the child’s own toy [22]. Conversely, in an analysis of fear responses in children undergoing a venipuncture procedure, da Silva et al. reported improved reactions and cooperation from children after exposure to simulated venipuncture on a provided study doll [11]. This analysis took place in a hospital setting [11]. Our findings suggest that these contradictory results may be in part related to the use of a study doll rather than a toy familiar to the child, as well as the influence of the medical play occurring in a clinical versus non-clinical setting. It is additionally possible that a medical play experience occurring immediately before a non-simulated medical visit is overwhelming to the child, or alerts the child to the potential of receiving an otherwise unexpected medical procedure without time to fully process what was demonstrated in the medical play intervention. This may result in increased distress behaviours as observed in Burns-Nader’s analysis. Time constraints on the medical play activity were cited by Burns-Nader as a potential limitation in their analysis, as their medical play intervention often took place within 10–12 min to maintain clinic flow [22]. Pediatric participants in our TBH-type event were able to spend as much time at each medical play station as they wished, with no set time constraints. We cannot definitively conclude that these contradictory findings are not a result of medical play interventions becoming ineffective when introduced to actual medical settings, though our findings suggest that increased time performing the medical play activity and some delay between a medical play intervention and an actual, non-simulated medical visit may be important factors to consider when assessing the efficacy of medical play in reducing medical-related anxiety.

Limitations & future research

Our results are limited by small sample size and low response rates, particularly post-event. Many potential participants reported a lack of time as a concern regarding participation, not wanting to limit their child’s time engaging in the event itself. Time was also stated as a concern by participants who completed the pre-event survey but did not complete the post-event survey before leaving the event. More participants were lost at 4-week follow-up due to being unreachable by telephone. Additionally, while parent/guardian responses were elicited at 4-week follow-up, pediatric responses were no longer gathered at this time. Future research should explore how to decrease loss to follow-up, perhaps using online platforms for surveys or other methods that expand the opportunity for survey completion outside of the event window alone. Despite the limitation of small sample size, we believe our findings highlight important benefits of medical play as it corresponds to pediatric medical anxiety and justify further investigation into the efficacy of TBH-type medical play events. As the Teddy Bear Bash is an annual event, and similar TBH-type events are held internationally, future studies targeting a larger sample size are possible.

The 4-week follow-up timeframe could be extended to allow for more exposure to medical appointments post-event. At four weeks, a large proportion of the respondents to the follow-up survey (24 of 43, 55.8%) could not identify if attending the Teddy Bear Bash had an influence on their child’s behaviour at a medical appointment, as they had not yet attended an appointment at the time of follow-up. A longer study duration would be required to demonstrate whether subjective reports of decreased anxiety accurately translate into decreases in displayed anxiety behaviours when a child is faced with medical appointments or interventions.

While our results demonstrate improvement in a child’s self-reported medical anxiety after exposures with their own toy in a park-based environment, we cannot conclude that a familiar toy or a non-clinical environment demonstrate improved outcomes as opposed to a similar event with unfamiliar toys in a clinical setting. Future research on medical play should further explore the relationship of the pediatric participants’ reports of medical-related anxiety, the familiarity of the toy used in the analysis, the duration of time in which the medical play occurs, the delay between the medical play intervention and any following non-simulated healthcare visits, and the setting in which the medical play intervention takes place.

The Teddy Bear Bash may attract families who work in medical specialties due to the number of medical volunteers present and resulting word-of-mouth advertising. We speculate this may have resulted in a study population with more experience in the medical environment compared to the general population. Considering a majority of the formal advertising for the event takes place online, families who do not use social media or have regular access to the internet are presumably less likely to participate in the event. Additionally, our study made use of materials written only in English. We acknowledge that English-only materials limit or exclude participation from linguistically diverse groups. The availability of translation services and provision of study materials in a variety of languages should be considered in future research. We also acknowledge the cost to enter the event may have influenced families’ participation and therefore study involvement; however, we believe this was mitigated by the eighty free tickets that were supplied to the Saskatoon Public Libraries across the city. Fees for entry would ideally be avoided in future studied TBH-type events to eliminate this as a factor for sampling bias.

Conclusions

This prospective cohort study demonstrated improvement in children’s self-reports of medical-related anxiety following their participation in a Teddy Bear Hospital event with a lasting change 4-weeks following the event. The pediatric population studied demonstrated statistically significant improved feelings towards health care workers following engagement in this model of medical play. Notable decreases in pediatric reports of fears related to medical appointments were observed immediately post-event. However, improvement in anxiety-related behaviours during medical appointments post-event was not clearly elicited. Our findings suggest the Teddy Bear Bash and other TBH-type models are beneficial in promoting subjective reports of decreased medical-related anxiety in children, though further research is required to conclude whether this translates into improvements during medical appointments. Future iterations of this study should include larger sample size, longer follow-up times, and creative strategies to decrease participant attrition rates. Health care workers and organizations should consider the value of medical play in reducing children’s medical anxiety and employ these tactics to prepare children for future medical procedures.
