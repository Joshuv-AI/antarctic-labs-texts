Genomic diversity, population structure and runs of homozygosity in Spanish native chicken breeds assessed with high-density SNP data
Authors: M.S. Saleh, A. Martínez, A. Torres, M.F. Baquero, G. Rusco, A. Pons, A. Canales, V. Landi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The local genetic resources of Spanish chickens constitute important components of biodiversity. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the genetic diversity, population structure, runs of homozygosity (ROH) and inbreeding levels in Spanish local breeds. A total of 114 samples from three varieties of Andaluza Azul (AAZ), Andaluza Azul Británica (ABR) and Andaluza Azul Ceniza (ACA); Canaria (CAN); Combatiente Español (CES); Mallorquina (MLL) and Utrerana Franciscana (UF) Spanish breeds were genotyped using the Axiom 600k Chicken Genotyping Array. These genotype data were merged with 40 genotypes for the Spanish Prat (PTxx) and Red Villafranquina (RVxx) breeds from the Synergistic Plant and Animal Breeding (SYNBREED) project. The principal component analysis (PCA) results separated the Spanish local breeds into two clusters. The admixture results are consistent with the PCA results. At K2, the MLL and AAZ breeds showed clear genetic identity. The genetic differentiation results are consistent with the PCA plot, with Fst values ranging from 0.0027 (AAZ and ABR) to 0.259 (MLL and ABR). The values of observed and expected heterozygosity and minor allele frequency ranged from 0.185 to 0.353, 0.211 to 0.363 and 0.159 to 0.275, respectively, in the Spanish breeds. The average number of ROH ranged from 20.83 to 102.33 and the average length of ROH ranged from 1086 to 3310 Kb. The inbreeding coefficient based on ROH (FROH) ranged from 0.027 in the CES breed to 0.147 in the ACA and RVxx breeds. The results showed the existence of different levels of genetic diversity in local Spanish breeds. These findings will help in performing conservation strategies for native Spanish breeds and promote the utilization of these genetic resources.

Introduction

The poultry industry is a key sector in agricultural production, as it is a very important food source (Windhorst, 2006). Commercial hybrid breeds have replaced local breeds due to their high productivity, and therefore indigenous chicken breeds face challenges such as small population size, decreased diversity and the lack of appropriate breeding programs (Delany, 2004). In Europe, it has been documented that approximately 36.50 % of European chicken breeds are classified as having an unknown risk status, approximately 10.64 % are classified as extinct and 41.16 % are critically endangered (FAO, 2021). This loss of genetic diversity negatively affects the development of sustainable animal breeding and genetic improvement programs that help to adapt to new farming systems, climate change and challenges of new diseases (Groeneveld et al., 2010). Thus, minimizing the loss of genetic variability in local breeds is essential for sustainable breeding.

Spain is one of the countries actively engaged in implementing the FAO global strategy for the management of animal genetic resources. National programs focusing on classification, preservation, and sustainable use have made significant efforts to conserve and genetically characterize native livestock species, such as goats, sheep, horses, donkeys, and chickens. One of the most effective examples of these efforts is the conservation program created for the Utrerana chicken breed (Plata-Casado et al., 2023). Spanish dedication to the FAO's priorities has been strengthened by recent research on the genetic diversity and population structure of native chicken breeds (Canales et al., 2024; Castro-Rojas et al., 2024). Historically, during the second half of the 20th century, the introduction of foreign and commercial chicken breeds, together with the expansion of industrial production systems, profoundly transformed the traditional poultry landscape. Prior to this transition, numerous locally adapted chicken breeds were raised under small-scale or semi-intensive systems, supplying local markets and ensuring food security through household consumption.

In the last decades, genetic variability and population genetic structure in chicken breeds have been studied using microsatellite markers (Tadano et al., 2007; Dávila et al., 2009; Zanetti et al., 2010; Abebe et al., 2015; Ceccobelli et al., 2015; Abdel A’al et al., 2017; Iraqi et al., 2018; Canales Vergara et al., 2024; Castro-Rojas et al., 2024). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) provide greater scalability, genome-wide representation, and accuracy, allowing for more thorough examinations of genetic diversity, population structure, and inbreeding levels. These technologies have provided new insights into animal genetics by including genomic approaches in small and endangered breeds (Saleh et al., 2026). In order to estimate inbreeding coefficients and selection signals in livestock species, the identification of runs of homozygosity (ROH) is helpful for population history and breeding programs (Wang et al., 2023; Velasco et al., 2024). A more precise indicator of the actual degree of inbreeding in individuals and populations is the inbreeding coefficient estimated from runs of homozygosity (FROH). The estimated FROH inbreeding coefficient is more accurate than the pedigree-based inbreeding coefficient when pedigree data are unavailable or incomplete, but its accuracy depends critically on SNP density, FROH detection criteria, and genome coverage (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018) . Also, the short ROH segments indicate ancient common ancestry, while the long ROH segments indicate recent inbreeding (Yuan et al., 2022; Perini et al., 2025). To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate genetic diversity, population structure, and runs of homozygosity using high-density SNP array data in these Spanish chicken breeds. Therefore, the main objective of the present study is to characterize genetic diversity, population structure, ROH patterns, and genomic inbreeding coefficients across nine indigenous Spanish chicken breeds.

Materials and methods

Sampling and genotyping

A total of 114 blood samples were collected from seven Spanish breeds. The three varieties of Andaluza Azul (AAZ; 12), Andaluza Británica (ABR; 6) and Andaluz Ceniza (ACA; 6); Combatiente Español (CES; 18) and Utrerana Franciscana (UF; 24) breeds from Andalusia, Canaria (CAN; 24) a not officially recognized population from Canary Islands, Mallorquina (MLL; 24) from Balearic Islands in Spain. The birds from these breeds are not included in formal breeding or selection programs, and many of these populations are considered endangered or at risk of extinction. They are typically maintained in small backyard flocks without pedigree recording or planned mating schemes. Therefore, reproduction occurs under largely uncontrolled conditions within the flock. However, mating cannot be assumed to be strictly random, as social hierarchy, flock structure, and cockerel dominance may influence mating patterns. To reduce the likelihood of sampling closely related individuals, birds were collected from different households and flocks whenever possible, and only a limited number of individuals per flock were included. Breeders were also consulted to avoid, as far as possible, the intentional sampling of known close relatives. Nevertheless, due to the small population sizes and management conditions typical of these systems, some degree of relatedness among sampled individuals cannot be completely excluded. Collected blood samples were stored until DNA extraction at the laboratory of Animal Breeding, University of Cordoba, Spain. Genomic DNA was extracted using GenElute™ Blood Genomic DNA Kit Protocol (Sigma-Aldrich). DNA samples, after quality and concentration checking by agarose gel and spectrophotometric analysis, were sent for genotyping Axiom® chicken Genotyping Array (Affymetrix, 600 K) containing 581,000 SNPs at Xenetica Fontao Laboratory (Lugo, Spain) following standard procedures. The genotyping data were aligned using the Gallus gallus chicken assembly (GRCg6a). Genotyping data from the Synergistic Plant and Animal Breeding (SYNBREED) project (Malomane et al., 2019) were included for two Spanish chicken breeds: Prat or Catalana (PTxx; n = 20) and Red Villafranquina (RVxx; n = 20). These samples were genotyped using the same Chicken Genotyping Array. The final data set consisted of 154 individuals for nine Spanish breeds as presented in Table 1. Data quality and control

Prior to the merging process, SNP coordinates, sequence orientation, and allele encoding were standardized across the datasets to ensure consistency of marker positions and genotypes. After merging the data, PLINK 1.9 software (Chang et al., 2015) was used to eliminate SNP call rate lower than 95 %, Minor allele frequency lower than 5 % and animals with more than 10 % of missing genotypes. A total number of 504121 variants and 154 samples passed the quality control and only autosomes were retained in the analysis set.

Genetic diversity indices

The “–hardy” function of PLINK 1.9 software was used to estimate the values of observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He), while the “–freq” function was used to estimate minor allele frequency (MAF) (Chang et al., 2015). R software was used to estimate the average and standard deviation for each breed (R Development Core Team, 2017). We used cluster-stratified allele frequencies in the PLINK 1.9 software to determine the number of polymorphic SNPs per breed (Chang et al., 2015).

Genetic relationship and admixture

To remove redundant information and reduce multicollinearity, LD pruning was applied to the genotype data using PLINK 1.9 with a SNP window size of 50, a step size of 5 SNPs, and an R2 threshold of 0.60. A total of 327,122 SNPs and 154 samples passed the filtration and quality control processes and were processed for the principal component analysis (PCA) and admixture analysis. To examine the presence of potential batch effects due to differences in the genotyping source, PCA was performed on the combined dataset using a set of pruned, approximately independent SNPs. No segregation of samples by dataset was observed in the PCA plot, indicating no detectable batch effects, and the combined dataset was deemed suitable for subsequent population genomic analyses (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The PCA was conducted using PLINK v.1.9 software (Chang et al., 2015), and visualized using the R package ggplot2 (Wickham et al., 2016). Additionally, to place the nine Spanish local chicken breeds in a wider context and to detect more thinly their relationships, we also conducted a separate PCA analysis by merging our genotyping data with those of four commercial breeds Rhode Island Red (BL_A; 20), White Plymouth Rock (BL_D; 20), Broiler dam line (BRD_B; 18) and White Leghorn (WL_C; 20) involved in the SYNBREED project (Malomane et al., 2019). Phylogenetic relationships among the breeds were detected using Reynold's genetic distances in the RStudio package ‘ape’ (Paradis and Schliep, 2019). The neighbor-joining tree was performed based on individual allele-sharing distances (-distance 1-IBS in PLINK) and visualized using ggtree package (Paradis and Schliep, 2019; Yu et al., 2017). The VCFtools v.1.16 software (Danecek et al., 2011) was also used to estimate population relatedness using the Weir and Cockerham pairwise Fst (Fixation index) among all breeds (Weir and Cockerham, 1984). Population structure was conducted using genetic analysis software ADMIXTURE 1.3 (Alexander et al., 2009), with different numbers of ancestral populations (K) ranging from 2 to 18. The most likely number of ancestral populations was determined in conjunction with the lowest cross-validation error (CV), the analysis was tuned with the ideal number of clusters (K) from 2 to 8 and visualized using the BITE V2 R package (Milanesi et al., 2017).

Detection of runs of homozygosity

ROHs were determined using PLINK 1.9 software (Chang et al., 2015), using the following parameters as reported by (Cendron et al., 2020): –homozyg-kb 1000 –homozyg-window-missing 2 –homozyg-window-threshold 0.05 –homozyg-window-het 1 –homozyg-window-snp 100 –homozyg-snp 100 –homozyg-density 100 –homozyg-gap 1000. The detectRUNS R package was used to visualize the results (Biscarini et al., 2018). ROHs were allocated into 5 length categories: 0 to 2 Mb, 2 to 4 Mb, 4 to 8 Mb, 8 to 16 Mb, and >16 Mb. These size classes correspond to approximately 250-500 generations, 100-250 generations, 50-100 generations, and 50 generations ago, respectively (Ferenčaković et al., 2013; Signer-Hasler et al., 2022). We calculated the total number of ROH and the average length for each ROH length class per breed. Additionally, we calculated the mean sum of ROH per breed by summing all ROH values per bird in that class and averaging this per breed.

Inbreeding coefficients

The levels of genomic inbreeding were estimated using two complementary approaches. First, the percentage of the autosomal genome covered by ROH segments in relation to the total autosomal genome length represented on the SNP array (988,122 Kb) was used to compute the FROH. Second, PLINK v1.9 (Chang et al., 2015) was used to estimate the inbreeding coefficient based on excess homozygosity (FHOM), which calculates the difference between the observed and expected numbers of homozygous genotypes. R software was used to visualize boxplots of the estimated inbreeding coefficients for each breed (R Development Core Team, 2017).

Estimation of effective population size

The current generations effective population size (Ne) of Spanish local chicken breeds was estimated using GONE2 v2.0 (Santiago et al., 2020). According to the previous studies, the total linkage map of the chicken genome ranges from 2600 to 3800 cM (Groenen et al., 2000), and the total genome size is approximately 1100 Mb (Warren et al., 2017). Approximately 2.7 cM genetic distance is equal to 1 Mb physical distance; hence, we applied the setting of 1 Mb = 2.7 cM for estimation.

Effective population size

Ne analyses revealed a consistent pattern of progressive decline in Ne across the nine Spanish local chicken breeds over the past 150 generations, with a sharp acceleration in recent generations (Fig. 7). During the historical period (25-150 generations ago), most breeds-maintained Ne values ranging from ∼500 to >5,000, indicating the presence of relatively large ancestral populations. All breeds then underwent a severe bottleneck in the last 10–15 generations, converging toward critically low contemporary Ne values consistent with the marginalization of local breeds during the industrialization of poultry production in the 20th century. Contemporary Ne estimates (generation 1) were 23.6, 28.4, 8.4, 20.1, 26.9, 22.7, 24.6, 65.2 and 130.8 for AAZ, ABR, ACA, MLL, PTxx, RVxx, UF, CES and CAN breeds, respectively. Discussion

This study highlighted substantial differences in genetic diversity, population structure, and inbreeding coefficients among Spanish domestic chicken breeds, indicating varying conservation statuses. The marked differences in the distribution of ROH and FROH values showed that some breeds (ACA, MLL, PTxx and RVxx) are exposed to higher levels of recent inbreeding. These patterns are of particular importance for breeds conservation management, as they help identify strains at risk of genetic erosion that require targeted conservation and breeding strategies.

Traditional Spanish chicken breeds showed a moderate level of genetic diversity using different methods. The CAN breed showed the highest degree of diversity, while the ACA breed showed the lowest degree of diversity. The observed and expected heterozygosity stated different averages compared with the averages obtained by previous studies using microsatellite markers in Spanish chicken breeds (Dávila et al., 2009; Ceccobelli et al., 2015; Macri et al., 2019; Vega-Pla et al., 2019; Canales Vergara et al., 2024). However, the results of SNP were lower than those from microsatellite markers (Viale et al., 2017). The single loci analyses have shown a loss of information because of the biallelic nature of the markers, compared to multi-allelic microsatellites that have more alleles per locus and, therefore, a higher frequency of heterozygotes (Cendron et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the results were obtained using different markers, the variation may also be due to an inbreeding increase, which is associated with a decrease in the number of individuals within populations over the years. The means of Ho, He and MAF were in the same line as those obtained for Italian (Cendron et al., 2020, 2021), Dutch (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018), and French (Restoux et al., 2022) local chicken breeds, although these local chicken breeds in these countries have different breeding histories and genetic architecture. Higher diversity in the CAN breed could be consistent with multiple non-mutually exclusive scenarios, including reduced inbreeding, larger effective population size, or historical gene flow; however, the present data do not allow attribution of this pattern to a specific cause (e.g., Frankham et al., 2010; Allendorf et al., 2012). Also, the largest values of Ho and He for the UF breed maybe a result of the conservation that applied for this breed recently. These conservation measures include both in situ strategies, such as the implementation of controlled mating schemes, exchange of breeding individuals among flocks to increase gene flow, and the establishment of pedigree or genomic monitoring programs, as well as ex situ approaches, including the cryopreservation of genetic material in gene banks to ensure long-term preservation of genetic diversity. The lowest average of Ho and He in the ACA breed maybe formed by selection for plumage color type. The lower to moderate genetic diversity in the Spanish breed maybe a result of the challenges to conserve biodiversity; therefore, more actions should be taken to preserve diversity. The Ho and He across the Spanish chicken breeds are generally consistent with breeds of small effective size undergoing genetic drift. The relatively reduced heterozygosity in ACA, ABR, AAZ and MLL breeds likely reflects the cumulative effects of drift and restricted gene flow, which are expected in breed maintained under conservation programs with limited census sizes. In CAN and UF breeds where Ho is lower than He, the deficit of heterozygotes may indicate recent inbreeding or substructure.

The results of the PCA of the Spanish local chicken breeds and four commercial lines (BL_A, BL_D, BRD_B and WL_C) showed a weak genetic relationship (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The CAN breed was close to the BRD_B commercial line. It was documented that the Canarian chickens were affected by foreign and hybrid lines (Canales Vergara et al., 2024). These results are in agreement with the results of (Cendron et al., 2021), who studied the genetic relationship between 8 Italian local breeds and 12 commercial lines.

The genetic relationships, structure, distance and differentiation were analyzed using PCA, NJ tree, Fst and admixture analyses. The PCA2 separated the Andalusian breeds (AZZ, ABR, ACA, CES and UF) from the Catalonian breeds (PTxx and RV xx). Similarly, (Vega-Pla et al., 2019) reported that the southern Spanish breeds were separated from other Spanish chicken breeds using microsatellite markers. Geographic separation between Canary Island chicken breeds and other Spanish chicken breeds has also been reported using microsatellite markers (Canales Vergara et al., 2024). The NJ tree based on allele sharing distance confirmed the results obtained by the PCA, where the Spanish breeds were grouped geographically. The AAZ, ABR and ACA breeds showed overlap with each other by PCA analysis and showed a close genetic relationship by NJ tree analysis. The southern Spanish local chicken breeds were grouped in the NJ tree analysis (Vega-Pla et al., 2019). In the NJ tree, UF individuals did not form a tight, well-supported cluster; instead, they were more dispersed across the tree, indicating higher within-breed genetic heterogeneity. The pairwise Fst values were estimated between Spanish local chicken breeds. The lowest genetic differentiation values were observed between Andalusian chicken breeds (AAZ, ABR, ACA and UF). Also, the lowest Fst values were seen between the CAN breed and the Andalusian chicken breeds. The very low values for Fst between AAZ, ACA and ABR because they are varieties of Andalusian Azul chicken breed. Although most breeds were represented by adequate sample sizes, the AAZ, ACA and ABR breeds included a limited number of individuals. Small sample sizes can increase the variance of allele frequency estimates and may lead to inflated Fst values, particularly in comparisons involving these breeds. This effect is mainly due to stochastic sampling of alleles, especially rare variants, which can appear population-specific in small samples. While the high density of SNP markers partially mitigates this limitation, the Fst estimates involving these breeds should be interpreted with caution. Our Fst values were higher than the Fst values obtained by microsatellite markers in Spanish native chicken breeds (Dávila et al., 2009). The admixture results at K = 2 clearly showed two clusters, one for the MLL breed and another for the ACA breed. The UF breed did not show genetic identity until K = 8, this could be a result of crossbreeding between these breeds and other breeds. The results of the admixture analysis agree with the results obtained from PCA, NJ tree and Fst approaches. The same genetic structure and backgrounds between AAZ, UF, CES, RVxx, PTxx and MLL breeds were observed using microsatellite markers (Dávila et al., 2009; Vega-Pla et al., 2019; Canales Vergara et al., 2024).

The genome-wide analysis of ROHs is critical because of addressing major anxieties for conservation genetic programs, such as inbreeding levels and population demography (Bosse et al., 2012; Purfield et al., 2012; Bortoluzzi et al., 2018; Cendron et al., 2021). The detection of ROH focuses on the new markers information to avoid the reduction in biodiversity (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018). The occurrence of long ROHs in native chicken breeds aligns with the limitations of effective genetic management due to the lack of pedigree data and breed registry, highlighting the importance of recent inbreeding for the long-term viability of these breeds. The ROH analysis demonstrates this assumption and indicates that the practices of individual breeders and breed associations have a significant influence on inbreeding. The minimal number of ROH found in some breeds (AAZ, CES, and UF) was zero, meaning that some individuals did not exhibit any homozygous segments that satisfied the established ROH criterion. This may indicate relatively significant genetic diversity or outbred mating patterns within these communities and implies that there has been no discernible recent inbreeding in these individuals. However, since short homozygous segments below the selected thresholds would not be detected, this finding should also be considered in the context of the ROH detection criteria. Heterogeneity in inbreeding levels is further demonstrated by the occurrence of individuals with and without ROH within the same breed. This is also consistent with the lower FROH values in the AAZ, CES and UF breeds. The longest mean length was observed in the RVxx breed, while the shortest mean length was seen in the CES breed. The differences in mean length of ROH among Spanish chicken breeds could be a result of different breeding practices, management, population size and bottleneck (Howrigan et al., 2011; Ceballos et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020). The mean length of ROH ranged from 1.06 Mb to 3.31 Mb in the Spanish breeds, while Restoux et al. (2022) showed that the mean length of ROH ranged from 2.2 Mb to 4.7 Mb in French chicken breeds. The average number of ROH per individual ranged from 20.83 to 102.33 in ACA and CES breeds, respectively. This averaged number per individual is lower than (55.45 to 172.27) the estimated in the Italian chicken breeds (Cendron et al., 2021), (36.6 to 128.4) in the French chicken breeds (Restoux et al., 2022). The highest number of ROH was seen in the MLL breed, followed by the RVxx breed. Also, the highest number of ROH for 2-4, 4-8, 8-16 and > 16 categories was observed in those two breeds. This could be a result of inbreeding in the MLL and RVxx breeds because the prevalence of long ROH is an indicator of recent inbreeding (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2024). The AZZ, ABR and CAN showed the lowest number of ROH for 0-2 class, this small number is due to the presence of heterozygous individuals in these breeds. Cendron et al. (2021) stated that most of the ROHs were identified for >4Mb class in 8 Italian local chickens (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018). reported that the majority of ROHs were distributed for medium (1-3 Mb), and long (> 3 Mb) categories in the Dutch local chicken breeds.

The estimation of FROH and FHOM in Spanish chicken indigenous chicken breeds showed significant variations. Recently, the ROH method has become commonly used to detect inbreeding coefficients (FROH) in poultry (Cendron et al., 2021; Restoux et al., 2022; Saleh et al., 2024, 2025; Perini et al., 2025). For instance, the highest FROH estimates detected in ACA, RVxx and MLL breeds are important information for conservation strategies. Indeed, the presence of this information helps in mating schemes in endangered breeds to avoid the loss of biodiversity (Cendron et al., 2020; Velasco et al., 2024). The ACA breed showed the largest FHOM value, while the CAN breed showed the smallest FHOM value. The highest inbreeding level observed in the ACA breed may be related to its small effective population size, as supported by the Ne estimates obtained in this study. Similarly, the relatively high inbreeding levels observed in the CES and MLL breeds may reflect the combined effects of reduced population size, limited gene flow, and the absence of structured breeding programs, which are common characteristics of endangered local populations. In contrast, the lower inbreeding level observed in the CAN breed may be associated with a comparatively larger effective population size, historical gene flow, or lower levels of recent relatedness, although the available data do not allow us to disentangle these factors. The discrepancy between FROH and FHOM observed in ACA is likely due to the small sample size, which may have led to bias in allele frequency estimates and FHOM amplification. Additionally, the potential substructure of the population may contribute to low heterozygosity (Wahlund effect), further increasing FHOM values. The values of FROH and FHOM in Spanish indigenous chicken breeds are lower than those obtained for Italian (Cendron et al., 2020, 2021), Dutch (Bortoluzzi et al., 2018) and French (Restoux et al., 2022) local chicken breeds. Also, the means FHOM in Spanish breeds were higher than the means obtained for Italian traditional chicken breeds (Cendron et al., 2020, 2021). From a global perspective, the highest mean FROH was recorded in European breeds sampled in Germany, while the lowest mean was recorded in African chicken breeds (Gao et al., 2023).

Notably, ACA displayed the lowest Ne of all (Ne = 8.4), despite showing relatively high historical Ne, suggesting a particularly severe and recent demographic collapse. Seven out of nine breeds fell below the FAO critical threshold of Ne = 50, beyond which inbreeding accumulation and loss of adaptive potential represent serious conservation concerns. These results are consistent with the ROH-based inbreeding estimates and collectively underscore the urgent need for coordinated conservation strategies, including genomic-assisted mate selection, for these endangered genetic resources. The use of a big sample size is highly recommended to increase the accuracy of population genetic statistics. But it is often difficult to reach a big sample size in genetic variability research due to the threatened and endangered breeds (Pruett and Winker, 2008). The main limitation of this study is the small sample size due to the small effective size of the local chicken breeds in conservation programs.

Conclusions

Genomic analyses have shown that local Spanish chicken breeds are characterized by different levels of genetic diversity and inbreeding, allowing them to be classified into different conservation priority groups. Based on the genomic results obtained, conservation priorities can be established among the studied breeds. Breeds such as ACA, MLL and RVxx, which show high inbreeding levels (FROH), low genetic diversity and critically low effective population sizes, should be considered of highest priority for conservation actions. Intermediate priority should be given to breeds such as UF and PTxx, while CAN and CES breeds, although showing relatively higher diversity and/or lower inbreeding, still require monitoring and management. Conservation strategies should include the implementation of genomic-assisted mating plans to minimize inbreeding, the exchange of breeding individuals among flocks to promote gene flow, the development of structured breeding and recording systems, and the establishment of ex situ conservation measures such as cryopreservation of genetic resources. These actions are essential for preserving genetic diversity and supporting long-term improvement.
