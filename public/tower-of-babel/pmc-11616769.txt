Occupational Benzene Exposure and Lung Cancer in Human Studies: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Wenxin Wan, Susan Peters, Lützen Portengen, Ronnie Babigumira, Jo Steinson Stenehjem, David Richardson, Roel Vermeulen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Benzene is classified as carcinogenic to humans based on evidence that benzene causes acute myeloid leukemia. However, there is limited evidence that benzene causes lung cancer.Objectives:We performed a systematic review, quality assessment, and meta-analysis of published cohort and case–control studies on the association between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer risk.Methods:We reviewed the relevant human epidemiological studies from PubMed and Embase databases to 19 August 2024. Data extraction included study characteristics, effect estimates, and exposure assessment details. Two investigators independently evaluated study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) framework and exposure assessment quality based on a priori criteria. Six risk of bias (ROB) domains were constructed from the NOS criteria to identify and quantify possible biases and their impacts on parameter estimates. Meta-analysis relative risk (pooled RR) and associated confidence intervals were calculated using random-effects models, and a flexible exposure–response meta-regression was fitted to assess the shape of the association. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the consistency of results.Results:Of 252 articles identified, 13 studies covering 366,975 participants (17,030 lung cancer cases) were included in our analysis. The meta-analysis of ever occupational benzene exposure showed an elevated risk of lung cancer (pooled RR=1.14; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.27; I²=72). Subgroup analyses revealed that larger pooled RRs in studies based on highly exposed groups had higher overall quality and better exposure assessments and included both males and females (as opposed to only males). A positive linear trend was observed in the exposure–response meta-analysis.Discussion:Our meta-analysis supports an association between occupational benzene exposure and an increased risk of lung cancer. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP15086

Introduction

Benzene is a volatile aromatic hydrocarbon and a pervasive atmospheric pollutant. Historically, it served as a common solvent and constituent in products such as paint, printing inks, and adhesives. Benzene also naturally occurs in petroleum products, such as crude oil and gasoline. Regulatory measures and mitigation strategies implemented since the 1980s have significantly reduced or replaced benzene in these commodities.1,2 Despite these efforts, benzene remains a chemical compound with high production volume, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.3 Consequently, occupational exposure to benzene remains prevalent in various industries, including the petroleum, chemical, painting, rubber, coke production, and manufacturing industries.4 Benzene is also ubiquitous in the general environment, with the majority of its presence attributed to anthropogenic activities, including industrial emissions, coal and oil combustion, motor vehicle exhaust, and fuel evaporation.4

Benzene has been classified as carcinogenic to humans by a working group of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) based on evidence that benzene causes acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in adults.4 The working group classified the evidence for benzene causing lung cancer as “limited” (in part) because of the lack of control for confounding by smoking and other carcinogenic exposures in most epidemiological models.4

Since the evaluation by the IARC working group in 2017, several studies that did account for smoking and other occupational exposures have reported positive associations between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer. These include a large population-based cohort study in Shanghai,5 an internationally pooled analysis of 14 case–control studies in Europe and Canada,6 and a cohort study of Norwegian offshore petroleum workers.7 In addition, recent studies have shown that benzene exposure from nonoccupational sources, such as traffic-related air pollutants, is linked to an increased risk of lung cancer.8,9 All these studies adjusted for smoking, other occupational exposures, or both, addressing the concerns raised by the IARC working group.

Given the recent evidence, a systematic reevaluation of the relationship between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer in human studies is warranted to update the findings of the IARC working group from 2017. This study aimed to clarify the impact of occupational benzene exposure on lung cancer risk by conducting a systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression of published epidemiological studies on this association.

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

We followed the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis11 to conduct the systematic review and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to report the results (a checklist was included in the section “PRISMA Checklist” in the Supplemental Material).12 The literature search strategies were developed in collaboration with an information specialist. Details of the search strategy are described in the section “Search Strategy” in the Supplemental Material. In brief, we searched PubMed and Embase for all epidemiological studies of lung cancer and occupational benzene exposure published before 19 August 2024 (for both databases). MeSH (Medical Subject Heading) terms were used to identify all possible synonyms. Our key search terms included “benzene,” “benzol,” “cyclohexatriene,” “worker,” “occupation,” “work-related,” “work,” “occupational exposure,” “job,” “occupational groups,” “industry,” “industries,” “pulmonary neoplasms,” “pulmonary neoplasm,” “lung neoplasms,” “lung neoplasm,” “lung cancer,” “lung cancers,” “cancer of the lung,” “pulmonary cancer,” and “pulmonary cancers.” Article language was limited to English. We also manually reviewed the reference lists from the included studies and relevant review articles to identify any additional studies that may have been missed during the literature search.

We included cohort and case–control studies that reported estimates of association between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer. Studies that reported lung cancer risk estimates without identifying benzene exposure as the exposure of interest were excluded. We also excluded studies on nonoccupational sources of benzene exposure (e.g., air pollutants, drinking water) by checking the description of exposure sources or exposure assessment method in the abstract. When multiple publications from the same cohort were available, we selected the most recent follow-up. To avoid dual inclusion, we excluded individual studies that were later incorporated into pooled analyses, retaining only the pooled studies.

Based on the selection criteria, two reviewers (W.W. and S.P.) independently screened titles and abstracts of the resulting search outputs using SysRev (https://sysrev.com), a platform that masks reviewers’ responses to reduce bias. After screening, full texts were extracted to further assess eligibility for inclusion.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

For the included studies, information on study design, location, follow-up years, population, industry, estimates of association, and adjusted covariates was extracted using Microsoft Excel and subsequently reviewed by senior investigators (S.P., R.V., and D.R.). Two reviewers (W.W. and S.P.) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS).13 The NOS score ranges from 0 to 15, where the study quality is considered “high” if the NOS score is between 11 and 15, “moderate” if the score is between 7 and 10, and “low” if the score is below 7. Detailed criteria for the NOS score are listed in the section “Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cohort and case-control studies” in the Supplemental Material. We also extracted detailed information on the exposure assessment, including exposure definition, industry, job history, and details of the applied job-exposure matrix (JEM), if used. In addition, two reviewers (W.W. and S.P.) independently rated the quality of exposure assessment according to the following criterion: “high” if exposure was based on job- and study-specific information, supported by adequate exposure measurements; “moderate” if exposure was based on job titles/factory information but with limited study-specific information or measurement data; and “low” if the job- or factor-level information was not used/available or if self-reported exposures were used. This criterion was added to the NOS rating as an “exposure ascertainment” criterion for both cohort and case–control studies, where the quality of high, moderate, and low was assigned with a score of 2, 1, and 0, respectively. Disagreements between two reviewers (W.W. and S.P.) over the ratings of the six NOS items (5.1%) were resolved by the senior author (R.V.).

Based on the NOS criteria and the assigned scores, we summarized study quality into six key domains to better reflect potential sources of risk of bias (ROB), including “population inclusion,” “exposure assessment,” “outcome assessment,” “confounding,” “follow-up/participation rate,” and “statistical analysis.” We attempted to identify possible biases and evaluate their impact on parameter estimates, following the recommended approach for ROB assessment in observational studies14 and a series of reviews regarding low-dose ionizing radiation on cancer risk.15–19 For studies reporting risk estimates that significantly deviated from the null in either direction, or for those with high ROB in any of these six domains, we assessed whether potential biases—such as exposure measurement, confounding, selection bias, or outcome ascertainment—might explain the observed risk estimates. Where potential bias was identified, we assessed the likely direction of such bias (toward or away from the null, or uncertain) and its impact on a study’s effect estimates.

Meta-Analysis

In the meta-analysis, we pooled risk ratios, odds ratios (ORs), standardized mortality ratios (SMRs), standardized rate ratios (SRRs), and standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) from the included studies. For rare outcomes, ORs, SMR, SRR, and SIR can be interpreted as reasonable approximations of the relative risk (RR),20 thus we use the term “relative risk” (RR) to refer to the risk ratio, ORs, SMR, SRR, and SIR in this study. For studies that reported estimates of association by category of cumulative exposure, we estimated the RR for a contrast of ever vs. never occupational benzene exposure by pooling the estimates from each exposure category using a within-study fixed-effect meta-analysis. If the reported estimates of associations were SMRs, we estimated the RR and associated confidence interval (CI) for ever vs. never benzene exposure by summation of the reported observed and expected number of events and calculating the associated mid-P exact CI.21 Because incidence data often have higher completeness and timeliness than mortality data, owing to reporting from more sources and shorter reporting delays,22 we used the incidence (SIR) over mortality (SMR) ratio for studies that reported both.

We calculated the overall meta-analysis relative risk (pooled RR) with 95% CI for ever vs. never benzene exposure using the random-effects model and the inverse variance method, which incorporates the between-study variance into an estimate of the pooled RR’s uncertainty.23 The Hartung–Knapp method was used to provide more robust estimates of CI of the pooled RR.24 Between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method.25 Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, which can be interpreted as the percentage of variability in the estimates of association that is due to heterogeneity, and the general rule of thumb for interpreting heterogeneity is <25%=low, >25%–50%=moderate, >50%–75%=high, and >75%=extremely high.26 We also used the funnel plot and Egger’s test to examine potential publication bias.27 The asymmetry patterns related to statistical significance were additionally assessed by contour-enhanced funnel plots.28 Statistical adjustments for potential publication bias, such as the trim-and-fill method, were not conducted owing to the limited number of included studies (N=13).29 In addition, the reliability of such adjustments is highly dependent on the level of between-study heterogeneity, which may affect the accuracy of the results.30,31 In the sensitivity analysis, we replaced the RR from the included pooled analysis6 with the RRs from its 20 individual studies (from the SYNERGY project)6 in the meta-analysis to assess the potential impact on the overall risk estimate.

For studies using quantitative benzene exposure metrics on a comparable scale (in parts per million-years), we conducted a random-effects exposure–response meta-analysis to model the linear association between cumulative benzene exposure (measured in parts per million-years) and lung cancer risk. The midpoint of each exposure category range was used as the exposure estimate for the corresponding RR; for the highest open-bounded exposure category, we used 5/3 times the lower bound of the category.32,33 In the analysis, we used a one-stage (or pool-first) approach where data from all studies were pooled into a single model while incorporating fixed effects and study-specific random effects.34 We used restricted REML for parameter estimation and applied the Greenland and Longnecker method for covariance approximation.35 In addition, we explored potential nonlinear exposure–response associations by fitting restricted cubic splines with knots at the 20th, 50th, and 80th percentiles. We did not include the results from DeMoulin et al.5 because their midpoint exposure ranged from 31 to 287 ppm-years, which did not overlap with the other three included studies where exposure ranged between 0.008 and 10 ppm-years.6,7,36

Subgroup analyses were defined a priori and were conducted to explore possible explanations for study heterogeneity. We replaced RRs for ever vs. never exposure with the RRs from the highest and lowest exposure groups (if available) in the meta-analysis to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. Studies were stratified by study design (case–control, cohort), type of comparison group (internal, external), and type of population (industry-, population-based). Studies with an internal comparison group consist of unexposed participants from the same cohort, whereas an external comparison group involves selecting participants from a different cohort/population (e.g., workers in a different industry or the general population).37 We further stratified the included studies by overall study quality, quality of exposure assessment, adjustment of smoking, sex composition (only males, mixed), and continent (Asia, North America, Europe).

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3; R Development Core Team). We used R package meta38 and dmetar39 for the meta-analysis, and dosresmeta40 for the exposure–response meta-analysis. All meta-analysis R codes, including the extracted data, can be found on GitHub (https://github.com/Wenxin-Wan/ben_lc_MA).

Results

Study Selection

Figure 1 illustrates the screening process and study selection. Initially, 252 articles were identified using the specified search strategies. After removing 72 duplications, 180 articles remained and were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 130 articles were excluded, most of which were review articles (n=30) or mechanistic/animal studies (n=26). An additional 11 articles were identified from manual reference checks, leading to 61 articles for full-text review to assess eligibility. Subsequently, we excluded 26 articles where benzene was not explicitly identified as an exposure, 6 articles where association on lung cancer was not reported, 2 articles where benzene exposure was not from an occupational source, and 7 articles from other types of studies. Among the overlapping articles, studies conducted by Bond et al.41 were later updated by Collins et al.42 The Chinese multicenter cohort [Chinese Academy of Preventive Medicine and US National Cancer Institute (CAPM-NCI study)] conducted by Yin et al.43–46 was updated by Linet et al.47 Furthermore, we excluded 2 studies with the same underlying population (Montreal study),48,49 that were later included in a large pooled analysis (SYNERGY).6 Ultimately, 13 articles met the specified criteria and were included in the final review. Of these, 3 were published after the IARC review in 2017,5–7 leading to a significant increase in the overall study population—from 275,116 individuals (with 1,889 lung cancer cases) before the IARC review to 366,975 individuals (with 17,030 lung cancer cases).

The results of the NOS assessment for study quality are detailed in Tables S1 and S2, corresponding to cohort and case–control studies, respectively. Figure 2 illustrates the six NOS-derived domains of study quality. Most studies were found to have a “low” to “some concern” level of ROB in the domains of “population inclusion,” “outcome assessment,” and “statistical analysis.” Several studies exhibited a “high” ROB in the “exposure assessment,” “confounding,” and “follow-up/participation rate” domains, and three studies were classified as having high ROB across two ROB domains.50,51,55 The study by Babigumira et al.7 was categorized as having low ROB across all six domains, whereas the study by Linet et al.47 and DeMoulin et al.5 demonstrated low ROB in five domains, with “some concern” noted from confounding adjustment and exposure assessment, respectively.

NOS scores assigned to each criterion (C) by the review team were used to jointly determine the category of risk of bias (ROB) across the included studies. Details for all criteria can be found in the section “Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cohort and case-control studies” in the Supplemental Material, and the originally assigned NOS scores can be found in Tables S1 and S2. For cohort studies, “population inclusion” was determined by the aggregated scores from C1 and C2 (3=low risk, 2/1=some concern, and 0=high risk). “Exposure assessment” came from C3 directly (2=low risk, 1=some concern, and 0=high risk). “Outcome assessment” came from aggregating scores from C4, C6, and C7 (4/3=low risk, 2=some concern, and 1/0=high risk). “Confounding,” “follow-up,” and “statistical analysis” came from C5, C8, C9, respectively (2=low risk, 1=some concern, and 0=high risk). The same assignment of ROB category based on (aggregated) scores was used for case–control studies but with different criteria and associated meanings that are specific to case–control studies: “population inclusion” (C3, C4; refers to the selection/definition of controls); “exposure assessment” (C6); “outcome assessment” (C1, C2; refers to the definition and representativeness of cases); “confounding” (C5), “participation rate” (C7), and “statistical analysis” (C8).

Linet et al.47 reported the highest point estimate for ever exposure among the included studies (RR=1.5; 95% CI: 1.2, 1.9; Table 1). Their study included 74,827 benzene-exposed and 35,504 unexposed Chinese workers who had been followed up over 28 y across 12 cities. Although we identified some concern over the confounding adjustment for smoking, all other ROB domains were judged as low ROB. Linet et al. reported the same RR point estimate for male and female workers [1.5 (95% CI: 1.2, 1.9) vs. 1.5 (95% CI: 0.9, 2.5)], indicating that smoking is unlikely to confound the published results given that Chinese women in this period hardly smoked.57

Collins et al.36 reported the second-highest lung cancer risk for ever benzene exposure (RR=1.31; 95% CI: 1.16, 1.48; Table 1), along with a positive trend between cumulative exposure and increased lung cancer risk (Table 1). Notably, an elevated lung cancer risk was observed among unexposed participants (RR=1.3; 95% CI: 1.1, 1.5) as compared with the population of Illinois, which could be attributed to several factors, such as the use of an inappropriate external comparison group or potential outcome misclassification. The external comparison group may have had a lower smoking prevalence compared with the study cohort, partially explaining the elevated risk in the unexposed group. However, the study also reported a significant positive trend with cumulative benzene exposure and a 60% increased lung cancer risk associated with higher peak exposure [>40 d with peak exposure of >100 ppm (RR=1.6; 95% CI: 1.1, 2.3)].36 Although some residual confounding by smoking may exist, the large RRs observed and the consistency of the exposure–response trend for cumulative and peak exposure suggest that the lack of smoking adjustment is unlikely to fully account for the increased lung cancer risk observed in this study.

Sorahan et al.53 reported an increased RR of 1.19 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.34), with concerns about high ROB in exposure assessment and, as acknowledged by the author, the possible misclassification of lung cancer as mesothelioma.53 The exposure assessment relied on a one-time measurement of the ambient exposure level for only 130 of the 233 participating factories, and the authors noted the measurement was taken at the time of data collection (1966–1967) rather than at earlier years of exposure. This approach likely underestimated the actual exposure levels in factories, leading to reduced power and bias toward the null. Insufficient classification of outcome would further reduce power and bias the estimate toward the null. Some concerns were noted regarding population inclusion (where the external comparison group was used) and residual confounding by smoking and other factors. These residual confounding may bias the estimate away from the null; nevertheless, the residual confounding issue in Sorahan et al.53 is unlikely to fully account for the observed large RRs. In fact, the actual risk from benzene exposure may be larger or similar to the reported risk, given that the likely directions of different sources of bias could cancel each other out.

Koh et al.51 and Koh et al.50 both had high ROB in “exposure assessment” and “follow-up.” Although uncertain exposure measurement from job classification likely introduced Berkson measurement error, resulting in reduced precision but unbiased estimates. In addition, insufficient follow-up periods in both studies probably contributed to the small number of observed cases (<10). The potential impact of the healthy worker effect cannot be ruled out. We acknowledge that these issues may have biased the RR away from the null in a negative direction, as reflected by the observed small, negative, and uncertain risk estimates (Table 1).

Wan et al.6 reported increased risk with a relatively precise estimate from a pooled analysis of 14 case–control studies (Table 1), although some concerns were raised regarding the selection of controls (“population inclusion”), exposure assessment, and “high” ROB related to participation rates. The control populations in the analysis included both hospital-based and population-based participants. An internal meta-analysis stratified by control type showed no significant difference between the two subgroups.6 Exposure assessment was carried out with a quantitative, time-varying JEM using lifetime occupational records. Although no personal measurements or industry process information was available for this study, Berkson measurement error is expected, which typically reduces power without introducing significant bias.58 High ROB was noted in the participation rates for both groups. The impact of this bias is uncertain, but given the large sample size across multiple study centers and the consistent associations observed in their sensitivity analyses, it is unlikely to account for the increased risk.

In summary, the detailed ROB assessments highlighted various biases across the included studies, particularly in the domains of exposure assessment, residual confounding by smoking, and follow-up. Despite these concerns, the identified biases generally appear insufficient to fully explain the observed associations between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer risk. The consistent trends in exposure–response relationships and the robustness of the association estimates in several studies suggest that the observed risks are likely attributable to the underlying exposure effect rather than to methodological artifacts.

Meta-Analysis

The meta-analysis of ever benzene exposure resulted in a pooled RR of 1.14 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.27; Figure 3), with a high degree of heterogeneity (I²=72%; 95% CI: 51.7%, 84.2%).6 In the exposure–response meta-regression, the 10 extracted RRs from two cohort studies and one case–control study covered a cumulative exposure range of from 0.008 to 10 ppm-years based on the midpoints of exposure categories. However, the exposure levels reported by Babigumira et al.7 were significantly lower (0.008–0.285 ppm-year) compared with the other two studies (0.5–10 ppm-years). A positive linear exposure–response trend was observed (Figure 4). The fixed-effect estimate for the slope (increase in lnRR per parts per million-years) was 0.038 (95% CI: −0.028, 0.103). The nonlinear exposure–response model had a much higher Akaike information criterion of 15 compared with 9.19 for the linear model. Although it showed a similar positive trend, the nonlinear model also demonstrated greater uncertainty at lower exposure levels (Figure S2). The sensitivity analysis showed no difference in the pooled RR when the individual studies from the SYNERGY project were used instead of the pooled analysis6 (Figure S3).

Pooled RR estimates from meta-analyses stratified by study design were generally comparable. Cohort studies with an internal comparison group showed a pooled RR of 1.16 (95% CI: 0.86, 1.56; n=4), whereas those with an external comparison group showed a pooled RR of 1.18 (95% CI: 0.99, 1.42; n=6) (Figure S6). Population-based studies produced point estimates similar to those of industry-based studies (Figure S7). The meta-analysis of three case–control studies6,55,56 showed no increased RR (pooled RR=1.00; 95% CI: 0.43, 2.35) (Figure S8), although the estimate was uncertain owing to the limited number of studies and the random-effects structure (studies received a more or less equal weight in the meta-analyses). However, we note that the largest case–control study,6 with 12,329 cases vs. only 228 cases from the other two studies, reported an RR of 1.17 (95% CI: 1.10, 1.24)—an estimate very similar to the pooled RR from the cohort studies (1.15; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.32) (Figure S8).

The meta-analysis of studies identified as high overall quality produced a slightly higher pooled RR estimate (1.20; 95% CI: 0.92, 1.56; n=4) compared with studies with moderate overall quality (1.12; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.32; n=8) or studies with low overall quality (0.73; 95% CI: 0.24, 1.71; n=1) (Figure S9). Notably, three of the four high-quality studies reported significant increases in lung cancer risk associated with ever benzene exposure, with point estimates ranging from 1.17 to 1.50. Similar patterns can be identified in stratified meta-analysis by exposure quality, where studies with high-quality exposure assessment yielded a larger magnitude of an association (pooled RR=1.26; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.60; n=5) compared with studies with moderate (pooled RR=1.08; 95% CI: 0.91, 1.28; n=4) and low-quality (pooled RR=0.94; 95% CI: 0.45, 1.99; n=4) exposure assessments (Figure S10).

The meta-analysis of four studies that adjusted for smoking6,7,55 showed very similar pooled estimates (pooled RR=1.13; 95% CI: 0.96, 1.32; n=4) with studies that did not adjust for smoking (pooled RR=1.14; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.38; n=9) (Figure S11). The meta-analysis of six studies that included both males and females yielded a significantly higher pooled RR (1.21; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.34; n=6) than studies that included only males (pooled RR=1.04; 95% CI: 0.97, 1.12; n=7) (Figure S12). No clear pattern emerged in the meta-analysis stratified by geographic region (continent) across the included studies (Figure S13).

Discussion

Key Findings

This review included 13 studies with a total of 366,975 participants, with 17,030 documented lung cancer cases. The present review significantly increased the number of lung cancer cases compared with the evidence base available during the most recent IARC evaluation of benzene. Ever occupational benzene exposure was associated with an elevated risk of lung cancer (pooled RR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.27) compared with never occupational exposure to benzene. The ROB assessment of the included studies suggested that the identified ROBs was unlikely to explain the risk estimates observed. The pooled point estimate was larger in magnitude in studies that used estimates from highly exposed groups, studies with higher overall quality and better exposure assessment, and studies that included both males and females (vs. only males). Pooled RRs from other subgroup analyses were generally consistent. Our results were further strengthened by observing a positive linear trend between cumulative benzene exposure (in parts per million-years) and lung cancer based on three studies that reported risks per unit of exposure. Together, these findings provide robust evidence supporting the hypothesis that occupational benzene exposure is linked to an increased risk of lung cancer.

Synthesis with Existing Evidence

In 2017, the IARC working group on benzene identified 12 relevant epidemiological studies.36,42,47,48,50,51,53,54,59–62 and recognized statistically significant excesses of lung cancer among benzene-exposed workers in 2 of the more informative studies42,47 (i.e., studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and better exposure assessment). This systematic review expands on the IARC evaluation by incorporating 5 additional studies not included in the original assessment: 2 published before the IARC monograph evaluation52,55 and 3 published afterward.5–7 In addition, by applying stricter inclusion criteria, several studies previously included in the IARC monograph were excluded because benzene was not explicitly identified as the exposure of interest61 or exposure was not occupation related.60 We argue that these inclusion criteria are important because exposure assessments for benzene from nonoccupational settings, and in studies where benzene was not the exposure of primary interest, are often less specific and accurate than the assessments conducted in occupational settings, resulting in a high risk of exposure misclassification; in addition, benzene concentrations are generally lower in nonoccupational settings, such as in traffic-related air pollution60 or in urine samples measured only cross-sectionally.62 By focusing our review on occupational settings, we aimed to reduce bias from exposure misclassification in the evidence synthesis.

The IARC working group expressed two central reservations in their evaluation of benzene as a cause of lung cancer: a) confounding of the benzene-lung cancer association by smoking, and b) confounding by exposure to other occupational lung carcinogens.4 In our meta-analysis we found similar pooled risk estimates among studies that adjusted for smoking and those that did not. Importantly, all four studies that adjusted for smoking reported elevated risk of lung cancer (Table 1). A reanalysis of the SYNERGY population6 similarly showed no differences in the estimated associations between smoking-adjusted and unadjusted models (Table S3). Furthermore, individual ROB assessments of studies that did not adjust for smoking but reported large RRs suggests that confounding by smoking alone is unlikely to explain the significantly elevated risk observed. For example, Linet et al.47 reported the same RR point estimate for both male and female workers. Because smoking was extremely rare among Chinese women during this period,57 smoking is unlikely to confound these results. Moreover, previous quantitative evaluations of the likely importance of confounding factors suggest that confounding by smoking is unlikely to explain RRs for lung cancer exceeding 1.2 to 1.4 in occupational settings.63,64 Therefore, although smoking is a significant risk factor and potential confounder, it is unlikely to account for the elevated RRs observed, probably because smoking habits do not vary greatly across different occupational groups.65,66

The other reservation the IARC working group expressed was confounding by other occupational lung carcinogens.4 After the IARC evaluation, two recent studies extensively controlled for other occupational lung carcinogens, in addition to smoking. The study of Norwegian offshore petroleum workers7 controlled for welding fumes, asbestos, and crystalline silica, and reported an increased risk of lung cancer from ever benzene exposure (Table 1). In the pooled analysis of 14 case–control studies,6 five other lung carcinogens were adjusted for by using quantitative assessment of exposures to asbestos, hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)], polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and diesel engine exhaust (DEE), as well as adjusting for occupations with a known risk for lung cancer.67 Both studies demonstrated increased lung cancer risk from occupational benzene exposure. By addressing the two major limitations noted by the IARC working group in earlier studies—confounding by smoking and other occupational lung carcinogens—all three subsequent studies consistently showed an increased risk5–7 and clear exposure–response relationships,5,6 further strengthening the evidence that occupational benzene exposure may be a cause of lung cancer.

The exposure–response meta-analysis based on midpoint exposure levels from three studies6,7,36 showed a positive linear trend between cumulative benzene exposure and lung cancer risk within the exposure range of 0–10 ppm-years (Figure 4). This trend aligns with the exposure–response relationships observed by Wan et al.6 within the exposure range of 0–22 ppm-years. However, in studies that examined higher levels of occupational benzene exposure, the exposure–response relationship appears to be nonlinear. This was evident in the large Shanghai Men’s Health Study (SMHS),5 where a nonlinear association was observed, with a significantly higher lung cancer risk only at exposure levels exceeding 313 ppm-years. Nevertheless, the median benzene exposure level in the SMHS study was 62 ppm-years, far exceeding the maximal levels from the other three studies included in the exposure–response meta-analysis (10 ppm-years), which limits the ability to assess risk at lower levels in their study. Overall, these findings are complementary in terms of exposure coverage and suggest that the increased risk can be identified at both low and high exposure levels.

According to the evaluation by the IARC working group,4 7 of the 10 key characteristics (KCs) of carcinogenesis (as defined by Samet et al.68) were linked to benzene. Although KCs are not organ-specific, evoking 7 of the 10 KCs shows the highly carcinogenic potential of benzene. This includes the induction of lung tumors, given that benzene absorption is mainly by inhalation.69 The biological plausibility is also supported by several animal studies that demonstrated an increased incidence of tumors of the lung after chronic inhalation of benzene in mice70,71 and rats,72 as well as a recent mechanistic study that identified positive associations between occupational benzene exposure and F2RL3 hypomethylation—an important lung cancer biomarker.73

We acknowledge the recent systematic review by Chiavarini et al.74 on benzene-induced lung cancer. Although their study included both occupational and nonoccupational exposure studies, we identified several significant limitations, such as the inclusion of two studies based on the same cohort data17,45 and pooling risk estimates at different exposures scales, that is, four studies included in their review reported slope factors (e.g., risk per 1-ppb increment), whereas the others mostly reported estimates representing the differences in odds between the exposed vs. the unexposed. Most importantly, they did not include three recent and highly informative studies,5–7 which are particularly valuable owing to their large sample sizes and thorough adjustments for smoking or other occupational exposures, offering critical insights into the effect of benzene exposure. The omission of these studies, along with the highlighted methodological limitations, could result in an incomplete understanding of the true association between benzene and lung cancer.

Limitations

Most of the included epidemiological studies have “some concerns” or high ROBs across several domains. These biases primarily involved poor representativeness of the unexposed population owing to external comparison groups, uncertainties in exposure or outcome assessment, and low comparability, particularly in some historical cohort studies where study design was often limited or relevant covariate information was difficult to obtain. We applied the NOS tool to better understand the potential impact of these identified biases and showed in detailed sensitivity analyses that these concerns did not unduly influence our results.

The small number of included studies also constrained our ability to fully understand the underlying heterogeneity among the studies. For instance, we could not carry out a sex-specific meta-analysis because only two studies reported risk estimates by sex.6,47 Meta-analysis stratified by industry was not feasible owing to a limited number of studies in particular industries. The exposure–response meta-regression was also limited, given that only three studies reported risk estimates by cumulative benzene exposure levels that were on a comparable scale.

Investigation of potential publication bias using the funnel plot and Egger’s test was also constrained by the small number of included studies and the significant between-study heterogeneity. However, in the case of large, collaborative studies that have accumulated evidence over several decades (e.g., CAPM-NCI study, SYNERGY), publication bias is less likely to be a concern.75 Therefore, we believe that the asymmetry observed in the funnel plot is more likely attributable to genuine heterogeneity rather than publication bias.

Strengths

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several strengths, including a) a comprehensive literature search of primary studies published up to 19 August 2024, with refined search strategies; b) a flexible application of the NOS quality assessment tool, combined with a detailed evaluation of the likely direction and magnitude of identified biases, allowing for a better characterization of the study quality/ROB rather than relying solely on NOS scores; c) a secondary exposure–response meta-analysis that was performed to assess the shape of the underlying associations; and d) extensive subgroup analyses to thoroughly capture the potential impacts of various subgroups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study examined the association between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer through a comprehensive literature review and meta-analysis of 13 studies. Based on the included literature, we found ever occupational exposure to benzene was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer. By incorporating several recent studies, our review provides an updated understanding of the association between occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer following the assessment of epidemiological evidence from the IARC monograph on benzene. If a causal association between benzene and lung cancer is established, it would have crucial occupational health implications. Given the much higher incidence of lung cancer than AML, establishing exposure limits based on excess risk calculations would result in substantially lower thresholds than those based on AML. Furthermore, the association found in our review may also affect current occupational compensation schemes.
