A flow field reconstruction based on cylindrical wake flow data using the PSO-CNN-LSTM algorithm
Authors: Qingtong Liu, Dongxin Xu, Min Xu, Shuo Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate reconstruction of unsteady wake flow fields is essential for understanding vortex shedding dynamics and for enabling data-efficient analysis in engineering applications. This research proposes a spatiotemporal reconstruction framework based on a Particle Swarm Optimization–Convolutional Neural Network–Long Short-Term Memory (PSO-CNN-LSTM) architecture for two-dimensional incompressible cylindrical wake flows. The convolutional encoder extracts local spatial features from velocity snapshots, the long short-term memory module models temporal evolution and phase information of vortex shedding, and particle swarm optimization automatically searches key hyperparameters to reduce empirical tuning. To evaluate generalization under missing-frame reconstruction, each target snapshot is excluded from training and is reconstructed using only its preceding history frames. In addition, continuous rolling prediction is performed over the last ten steps (49.1–50.0 s) to investigate error accumulation behaviors. Results show that the proposed method reconstructs dominant wake structures with high fidelity. For missing-frame reconstruction at multiple target times, the streamwise velocity error remains at the order of 10–3, while the cross-stream velocity error is at the order of 10–2, indicating that cross-stream dynamics are more challenging due to their stronger dependence on vortex-shedding phase. Compared with LSTM, RNN and Transformer baselines in the ten-step rolling prediction, PSO-CNN-LSTM achieves markedly lower errors for the streamwise component and improved overall stability. Furthermore, an uncertainty quantification strategy based on Monte Carlo dropout is introduced, yielding pixel-wise predictive mean and standard deviation. The error–uncertainty coupling analysis demonstrates positive correlations between absolute error and predicted uncertainty. These findings indicate that the proposed framework not only improves reconstruction accuracy but also provides reliable spatial indicators of potential mismatch regions, supporting trustworthy flow-field reconstruction in unsteady wake flows.Keywords: Cylindrical wake flow, Flow field reconstruction, PSO-CNN-LSTM, Uncertainty quantificationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

The flow field is a crucial physical field that characterizes the state of fluid motion, which is of great significance to engineering design, aerospace, automobile manufacturing and other fields1–4. The accurate flow field analysis can provide effective guidance for design optimization, performance improvement and energy loss reduction. However, in practical applications, complete flow field data for many flow phenomena cannot be directly acquired via experimental or analytical methods, especially under high complexity and irregular geometry conditions. At this time, the flow field reconstruction technology is especially important. Through proper reconstruction method, the global or full-time flow field information can be inferred based on limited experimental data or simulation results, so as to realize comprehensive understanding and analysis of the flow process5,6. The flow field reconstruction not only supports the understanding of the flow characteristics, but also supports the design improvement. In the aerospace field, for example, the flow field around an aircraft wing has a direct impact on its lift, drag, etc. Through the reconstruction of the flow field, the aerodynamic performance of different design options can be evaluated more accurately, thus improving the design efficiency and flight performance of the aircraft7,8. In the automotive field, the airflow around the body plays an important role in aerodynamic characteristics, fuel consumption, etc. Through the reconstruction of the flow field, the body shape can be optimized, the resistance can be reduced and the fuel efficiency can be improved9,10. Furthermore, as computational power improves, numerical model-based flow field reconstruction becomes an increasingly common option. The flow field data calculated by numerical method can obtain high-precision flow information without actual experiment11,12. This provides new ideas for the research and application of complex flow fields.

In the field of flow field reconstruction, many studies have been devoted to the development of effective numerical methods to recover the flow field in complex flow environments. From the perspective of data generation and interpolation recovery, researchers often use CFD or other numerical methods to obtain complete or partial flow field regions, and then use the interpolation method to fill the flow field. Zhou et al13. proposed the flow field reconstruction based on POD, and combined with numerical simulation for interpolation recovery to improve the applicability of traditional interpolation methods in complex flow scenarios. Lu et al14. discussed the accuracy and reliability of interpolation techniques in flow field reconstruction, especially in the case of sparse data. The coarse mesh is converted into fine mesh by interpolation, which can also reduce the computational effort. Bai et al15. proposed a compact system identification framework that enables the identification of reduced-order models and reconstruction of full-state dynamic patterns from limited input–output data, and validated its effectiveness under noise, sensing modalities and actuation conditions for both test models and low-Reynolds-number airfoil flows. Gu et al16. and Daiki et al17. showed the application of POD and its improvements (e.g. frequency domain POD, incremental POD) in transient flow field approximation. Howard et al18. used POD to extract reduced models of wall pressure data for extracting dominant structures in the spatial domain and researching transient behavior. Li et al19. further proposed to improve the computational efficiency of flow field reconstruction by reducing the number of modes, and simultaneously overcame the efficiency bottleneck of traditional POD in flow field analysis. The advantage of the above POD method is that it can significantly reduce the data dimension and the calculation amount, but it also faces challenges such as "mode truncation error", improper mode selection method and failure of geometric change time-basis function. From the perspective of sparse measurement/sensor data recovery, researchers began to explore how to recover the entire flow field through reconstruction methods with only a few measurement points or rough simulation results20–23. This method combines numerical simulation, interpolation, dimension reduction and other techniques. The combination of traditional numerical methods and interpolation/dimension reduction reconstruction technology provides a mature tool for flow field recovery, but there are still many limitations in application scope, real-time performance and accuracy. When encountering complex geometries (such as flow around, multi-physical coupling), high Reynolds number turbulence, and large-scale flow field changes, simple interpolation or low mode reconstruction are often difficult to meet the engineering requirements.

Based on the above traditional numerical methods and dimension reduction reconstruction techniques, the data-driven and deep learning methods have gradually become an important development direction in the field of flow field reconstruction. With the strong nonlinear fitting and feature extraction capabilities of the deep neural network, the researchers try to directly return the high-resolution flow field from the low-resolution flow field data or sparse measurement point information, which significantly relieves the accuracy bottleneck of traditional interpolation and low-order modal reconstruction in complex vortex structures and high Reynolds number flows. Kong et al24. proposed a deep learning approach for fusing ground experimental and numerical simulation data, and developed a velocity field prediction model to obtain the isolator velocity field from experimental Schlieren images. Zhan et al25. proposed a neural network-based feature coding prediction model and a high-resolution turbulence reconstruction method, which can be widely used in single-point sensor data processing. Hesam et al26. introduced the Conditional Enhanced Super-Resolution Generative Adversarial Network (CESRGAN) for reconstructing high-resolution turbulent velocity fields from low-resolution inputs. In addition, the lightweight image super-resolution reconstruction method based on progressive receptive fields27, the Swin-Transformer-based model28, the generative adversarial network (GAN) framework29, and the unsupervised CycleGAN super-resolution method30have demonstrated excellent generalization capability in scenarios involving multi-Reynolds-number channel turbulence, complex wall structures and unpaired data. Huang et al31–33. have advanced data-efficient three-dimensional aerodynamic prediction under data scarcity by using active learning to adaptively select high-value samples and by using point-cloud-based transfer learning to accelerate pressure and aerodynamic characteristic inference across operating conditions. However, these frameworks are primarily deterministic and typically do not provide uncertainty-aware reliability assessment. On the other hand, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) alleviate the issues of data sparsity and extrapolation inaccuracies by explicitly incorporating physical constraints (e.g., the Navier–Stokes equations and continuity equation) into their loss function framework. Consequently, PINNs have emerged as a prominent research direction for flow field reconstruction under sparse measurement scenarios. Hoseini et al34. used reverse PINN to fuse sparse sensing and physical conservation, and realized the reconstruction of the flow field around a cylinder with very few measuring points. Compared with spline interpolation and general network, it has higher accuracy in velocity and pressure distribution prediction. Yang et al35. used only velocity data and initial temperature field to accurately deduce the temperature field at any time and space point in the measurement domain. Wang et al36. reconstructed the average flow field in a two-dimensional urban street canyon by using PINN to improve the measurement strategy in the real world using sparse sensor data. Nguyen et al37. used FLRNet’s deep learning method to reconstruct the flow field from sparse sensor measurements and achieved efficient reconstruction from limited monitoring points to the entire field in a variety of flow cases. These methods show that the introduction of physical constraints or potential spatial characterization can significantly improve the generalization performance under sparse data conditions, but there are still some problems such as prediction lag and long-term cumulative error in highly unsteady flow, strongly nonlinear separated vortex and dynamic boundary condition scenarios. Data-driven prediction and reconstruction studies are particularly active for cylindrical unsteady flows due to their prevalence in engineering scenarios such as heat exchanger tube bundles, marine risers, bridge and power line vibrations. For deep networks, Han et al38. designed a novel hybrid deep neural network (DNN) architecture to capture the temporal and spatial characteristics of unsteady flows directly from high-dimensional unsteady flow fields. Koldo et al39. connected LSTM in series with CNN, predicted the lift/drag coefficient time series through LSTM, and CNN regressed the instantaneous velocity field to improve the prediction accuracy and stability of the unsteady flow field around the cylinder. In more complex near-wall 3D cylindrical flow, the time–space network combined with attention mechanism can better capture the evolution of vortex structure and accurately predict the near-wall complex vortex system40. Most of the above methods only focus on the network structure design and loss structure, and the training hyperparameter and network scale mainly rely on empirical parameter adjustment, so it is easy to have over-fitting or low convergence efficiency under the condition of limited samples or multiple working conditions. In terms of hyperparametric optimization and hybrid intelligent algorithm, PSO is widely used in parameter optimization of complex neural network structure due to its simple implementation and strong global search ability. In the time series prediction field, PSO-LSTM has been successfully applied to the optimization scenario, generally showing higher prediction accuracy and robustness than traditional ANN and manual parameter modulation LSTM41–43. The coupling between PSO and CNN-LSTM can effectively relieve the dependency of deep network on manual parameter adjustment in complex nonlinear time series modeling, and improve the convergence speed and generalization ability of the model.

In this research, the flow field around a circular cylinder is adopted as the research object, and a flow field reconstruction framework based on the PSO-CNN-LSTM hybrid model is proposed. The structure of the research is arranged as follows: The second part presents the dataset construction method, introduces the computational domain, control equations, numerical discretization and solution process, and explains the framework and key parameter configuration of the PSO-CNN-LSTM; The third part presents and discusses the experimental results, including the analysis of original flow field characteristics, the reconstruction effect of missing flow fields, the performance and error propagation mechanism of recursive rolling prediction, as well as the ablation and comparative experiments of data volume, sampling resolution, input sequence length, and different time series baseline models; At the same time, this research further introduces Monte Carlo random deactivation to achieve uncertainty quantification, and provides error uncertainty coupling analysis and reliability evaluation of vortex core/shear layer/background region; Finally, the fourth part summarizes the work of the entire article. In this research, the output data from numerical simulations are subjected to unified preprocessing. At each time step, a result matrix containing time, spatial coordinates, and velocity components is output. The discrete point data at each time step are rearranged according to the order of spatial coordinates and reconstructed into a two-dimensional velocity field on a regular grid, thereby forming a spatiotemporal data cube stacked along the time dimension. A sliding window strategy is adopted to construct supervised samples: the previous Tin frames are used as the input sequence to predict the velocity field of the next frame, and samples are constructed independently for the u and v components, respectively. The training samples are extracted from a specified training time window, from which a validation set is divided at a fixed ratio. During the testing phase, independent target time steps or rolling prediction intervals are used for evaluation to avoid target frame leakage. Finally, the input and output data are normalized in a unified manner, and the samples are organized into tensors in the form of batch size × channels × time steps × spatial grid as the network input to ensure training stability and consistency in reproducing the experimental settings.

PSO-CNN-LSTM model

To achieve high-precision reconstruction of the transient velocity field around a cylinder, this research proposes a flow field spatiotemporal prediction framework based on the PSO-CNN-LSTM algorithm. The overall process of the algorithm is shown in Fig. 1, which includes five steps: data and time series setting, hyperparameter optimization, hybrid model structure, training and convergence monitoring, and physical consistency verification.

Workflow diagram of flow field data processing and reconstruction.

Figure 1 a shows the data and time series settings. Extract temporal snapshots of velocity components u and v within the computational domain to form a spatiotemporal data cube on a regular grid. To capture the temporal correlation during vortex shedding, the continuous snapshots are rearranged into "input–output" pairs in time steps. Using previous input frames as input sequences to predict the target frame for the next moment.

In order to improve the generalization ability of the model under different grid resolutions and data volume conditions, this research uses the PSO algorithm to jointly search for key hyperparameters. PSO iteratively updates hyperparameters through guidance of individual and group optima. Figure 1 b shows the schematic process of hyperparameter search, where the initial random population gradually aggregates towards the optimal region during iteration, thereby obtaining a more robust network configuration.

Considering the significant spatial local structure and temporal correlation of the flow field, this research constructed a hybrid network of CNN-LSTM. The convolutional encoder extracts the spatial texture and scale features of each frame, the LSTM module performs temporal modeling on the feature sequence dimension, and the convolutional decoder restores the high-dimensional features output by the temporal module to the velocity field prediction of the same size as the original grid. Figure 1 c shows the structure of the network, which retains the advantages of convolutional neural networks in spatial feature extraction and utilizes LSTM to characterize the non-stationary temporal dynamics of flow.

In order to ensure that the predicted results are not only numerically accurate but also physically reasonable, this research will conduct a physical consistency evaluation of the reconstructed flow field, including a comparison cloud map of the true value, predicted value, and error, and observe the consistency between the position and strength of the vortex street main structure from the spatial distribution.

Results and discussion

Analysis of original flow field

Flow field computation is trained on a 64-bit Windows system using multi-core CPU mode. The calculation of computer memory and disk capacity can meet the needs of single training, batch visualization, and result export. The data preprocessing and flow field data calculation were completed using MATLAB R2022, and the flow field data was exported in mat format. The flow field cloud map was also exported using the Python matplotlib library. To ensure that the cylindrical wake flow field data constructed based on numerical simulation has sufficient numerical credibility and to avoid grid discretization errors that may bias the subsequent data-driven reconstruction conclusions, this research further conducts grid independence verification after providing numerical solution methods and boundary conditions. Specifically, six sets of structured grids from coarse to fine were used for repeated calculations of the cylinder wake flow field, and the average drag coefficient was used as the evaluation index to examine the convergence trend of this index with grid refinement. When the difference in average drag coefficient between adjacent grids enters the stable platform interval, it is considered that the numerical results are no longer sensitive to grid resolution, and thus the benchmark grid scale for dataset generation is determined. The results are shown in Table 1. As summarized in Table 1, the computed average drag exhibits a clear convergence trend with grid refinement. When the grid is relatively coarse (220 × 41 and 330 × 61), the average drag changes noticeably, indicating that the solution is still affected by discretization errors. As the grid is refined to 440 × 82 and beyond, the average drag stabilizes around a nearly constant level, and further refinement produces only small fluctuations without a systematic drift. This behavior suggests that the numerical solution has entered a grid-independent regime, where the dominant flow features and the integrated aerodynamic response are no longer sensitive to the mesh resolution. Therefore, considering both accuracy and computational efficiency, the 440 × 82 grid can be selected as the benchmark mesh for constructing the wake-flow dataset, while ensuring that subsequent data-driven reconstruction results are not biased by grid discretization effects.

To verify the correctness of the flow field simulation in this research, the experimental results of particle image velocimetry around a restricted cylinder by Nguyen44 were selected as a control. This reference studied the flow around a cylinder in a parallel wall channel, using the blockage ratio (the ratio of cylinder diameter to channel height) to characterize the degree of channel confinement. The power spectrum analysis results of the wake lateral velocity were given under the condition of a blockage ratio of 0.2, thereby obtaining the dominant vortex shedding frequency and corresponding Strouhal number. The Strouhal number reported in the experimental report was 0.23. To ensure comparability, this research made corresponding adjustments to the numerical program according to the experimental conditions mentioned above. The blocking ratio of this research is consistent with the reference literature, and the frequency spectrum analysis of the velocity time series of the wake monitoring point is performed during the statistical stability stage to obtain the dominant frequency, and then the Strouhal number is calculated. The final Strouhal number obtained in this research was 0.27, which is slightly different from the experimental value of 0.23 in the reference literature. This indicates that the numerical model in this research can reasonably capture the dominant vortex shedding characteristics of the wake of a confined cylinder, and the reliability of the flow field simulation is verified from the frequency level.

The cloud map distribution of velocity components u and v at different time points in the flow field presents the entire process of vortex structure evolution from the initiation of the shear layer to the maturity of the Kármán vortex street. In the early stage of the flow field (5–15 s), the u-field exhibits streamlined diffraction characteristics and a shorter cylindrical wake; A weak bipolar band appears in the v-field behind the cylinder, indicating the initial generation of the separated shear layer. During the development stage of the flow field (15–25 s), the upper and lower shear layers undergo entrainment and rolling, forming stable vortex cores. The v-field exhibits a clear alternating positive and negative band structure, while the u-field shows a periodic stripe distribution on the trajectory of the vortex core. In the mature stage of the flow field (30–50 s), the Kármán vortex street achieves complete development, with vortex scale and spacing remaining relatively constant, and the growth of wake length being suppressed. The flow field in its entirety attains a statistically stationary limit-cycle state.

Key flow structure characteristics of cylindrical wake flow.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the initial stage of the flow field is affected by the start-up transient. The drag coefficient CD rapidly increases and enters a small oscillation near the platform value. The lift coefficient CL slowly transitions from near zero to a stable large periodic oscillation. The amplitude and frequency of CL are basically constant, corresponding to the classical Kármán vortex street alternating shedding. The CD exhibits weak second-harmonic oscillations about a non-zero mean value, a trend that reflects the dominance of the mean drag force as well as the second-harmonic contribution arising from the alternating shedding of the upper and lower shear layers.

The average u-field shows the formation of a pair of symmetrical reflux bubbles behind the cylinder, whose length and width determine the reattachment position of the wake. The u-field exhibits a jet like acceleration band along the shear layer direction, reflecting the redistribution of average momentum in the vortex channel. The overall average v-field is close to zero, with weak positive and negative bands only present near separation and reattachment, indicating that under long-term averaging, the lateral momentum fluxes cancel each other out and the wake maintains statistical symmetry.

The RMS cloud map highlights the areas with the most active energy. At the downstream of the separation point and in the shear layer suction zone, the RMS of u and v reaches its peak, revealing the intense pulsation at the site of strong shear and vortex core formation. On the trajectory of the vortex core, RMS exhibits a bead like distribution and gradually decays downstream, reflecting the diffusion of vortices and the transfer of coherent structural energy to smaller scales.

The Q criterion clearly characterizes the generation and decay process of alternating vortex trains. At 15 s-25 s, the upper and lower shear layers begin to curl up, the vortex core gradually solidifies, and the distance between vortex centers tends to stabilize. At 30 s-40 s, the vortex street is fully established, and the iso surface shifts at equal intervals, indicating a nearly constant shedding frequency and convective velocity. At 45 s-50 s, the downstream vortex core gradually disperses, accompanied by energy transfer to smaller scales.

To reveal the dominant flow structure and scale distribution in the flow around a cylinder, this research appropriately orthogonally decomposed the original transient flow field snapshot sequence to obtain spatial modes sorted from high to low in energy contribution. Figure 4 shows the first eight modal cloud maps corresponding to two velocity components (Fig. 4a represents the u component, and Fig. 4b represents the v component), with the red and blue regions representing the positive and negative wave contributions of the modal in space, respectively.

First eight modes of u and v.

The first mode usually reflects the fundamental flow structure and momentum characteristics in the time averaged sense. A core region of the wake extending along the mainstream direction is formed behind the cylinder, and a clear range of shear layer influence is observed near the wake. This mode determines the “overall shape” of the reconstructed flow field and plays a basic constraint role in the overall accuracy of the subsequent reconstruction. The second and third modes exhibit red blue clusters that periodically alternate along the downstream direction, with the most significant presence in the wake region behind the cylinder, reflecting typical Kármán vortex street dominated dynamics. The fourth mode is still spatially related to vortex shedding, but exhibits more pronounced high-frequency and localized characteristics, which can be seen as a high-order correction to the dominant vortex shedding. The fifth to eighth modes gradually exhibit smaller scale and more localized flow structures, with multi-scale patchy distributions appearing in the near wall separation zone and near wake region of the cylinder, and denser alternating stripes or fragmented scale structures downstream. These high-order modes are crucial for fine reconstruction, mainly responsible for compensating for the loss of details caused by turbulent diffusion, reproducing the steep gradient of vortex boundary, and improving the consistency of reconstructed flow field in local extremum and coherent structure shape. From the statistical analysis of the energy proportion of the u velocity component, the cumulative energy of the 1 st to 3rd modes reaches 92.97%, and the main dynamics of the u velocity component are almost entirely characterized by the first three orders. The first order determines the overall shape of the wake, the second to third orders determine the dominant coherent structure of vortex shedding, and higher-order modes are responsible for fine scale compensation. The energy of the v velocity component is more concentrated in fewer modes, with a cumulative energy of 91.39% in the first two orders and 94.59% after the third order. Therefore, the reconstruction framework based on PSO-CNN-LSTM in this research should prioritize ensuring the prediction accuracy of low order dominant mode time coefficients during training, in order to ensure the stable reproduction of dominant vortex shedding and wake skeleton. On this basis, the reconstruction reliability of local details and downstream attenuation structures is further improved through effective learning of high-order modes.

For the real frame corresponding to the given target time (referred to as the target frame), it is removed from the training samples during sample construction, and only the historical frames before the target frame are used to construct the input sequence. After training is completed, the target frame is predicted and the error is calculated.

Prediction accuracy of PSO-CNN-LSTM model at t = 40 s and t = 50 s.

The results showed that among the three representative cross-sections of the u velocity component, the predicted curve and the true curve basically overlapped in terms of overall trend, peak valley position, and amplitude changes, indicating that the model has stable reconstruction ability for the dominant low-frequency changes and large-scale structures in the wake region. There are slight deviations at certain positions, mainly concentrated near steep changes in the curve or local extrema. These areas usually correspond to strong gradient regions in two-dimensional fields, such as shear layers and vortex boundaries, which are more sensitive to predicted phase and amplitude. The intercept of the v velocity component also exhibits significant periodic oscillation characteristics. The prediction curve can accurately track the main period and phase changes of oscillations. This result demonstrates that the proposed model exhibits excellent representational capability in capturing the velocity fluctuations induced by vortex shedding, with local deviations typically emerging in the vicinity of the peak and valley regions. This also reflects that there is still a certain amount of error accumulation in the model regarding the strong nonlinear details of small-scale vortex structures.

Both the experimentally measured and numerically predicted flow fields distinctly reproduce the momentum deficit region of the horizontal velocity component, the near-wake recirculation zone, and the downstream attenuation tendency. Meanwhile, the alternating distribution of positive and negative vertical velocity components, together with the downstream vortex street structure, preserves accurate spatial configurations and scaling characteristics. These results demonstrate that the predicted flow field achieves a high level of consistency with the actual field in terms of macroscopic morphology and the dominant coherent structures. The overall prediction error exhibits pronounced localized features: the regions with the most significant errors are concentrated in the separation shear layer adjacent to the cylinder wake, the vortex cores, and their respective boundary layers. By contrast, in the outer wake regions away from strong gradient zones, the overall error is substantially attenuated and approaches zero. This error distribution pattern suggests that the proposed model enables highly accurate reconstruction of large-scale background flow fields and dominant flow structures, whereas it tends to incur minor phase shifts or amplitude deviations in small-scale, strongly nonlinear, and steep-gradient flow regions.

Experimental results of ablation studies

Data volume and sampling resolution

To investigate the impact of sampling resolution and training data volume on prediction performance, this research implemented equidistant down sampling on spatial grids. Select 1 sampling point every k grid point in two spatial directions (defined as the spatial down sampling multiple). Randomly select the training sample set and adjust the training sample size to a specified proportion of the original samples (defined as the training sample ratio). Three sets of resolution-data quantity combinations were configured in this research: 4 × spatial down sampling × 25% training samples (denoted as 4 × 0.25), 4 × spatial down sampling × 75% training samples (denoted as 4 × 0.75), and 2 × spatial down sampling × 75% training samples (denoted as 2 × 0.75). Figure 8 presents the distribution characteristics of the predicted and error fields for the u and v velocity components under three different configurations, with t = 50 as the typical moment.

Characteristics of cylindrical flow field dataset under different data volumes and sampling resolutions.

The prediction error of the velocity component exhibits the core characteristics of resolution improvement not necessarily reducing error, and insufficient data volume leading to error amplification. Under the 4 × 0.75 configuration, the predicted field of the u velocity component can accurately reproduce the main structure of the wake, and the error is only concentrated in the shear layer and local area of the vortex core near the wake of the cylinder, showing a patchy distribution and controllable amplitude. The 4 × 0.25 configuration with the same resolution significantly reduces the number of training samples, resulting in denser error patches in space. Additionally, the error diffusion effect in the downstream wake region intensifies, reflecting a significant decrease in the stability of the model’s fitting of vortex structure strength and position when the data volume is insufficient. Although the 2 × 0.75 configuration improves spatial resolution, the complex details brought by high resolution increase the difficulty of learning. Under the premise of fixed model capacity and training rounds, the increase in spatial degrees of freedom directly exacerbates the risk of underfitting, resulting in errors shifting from local patches to large-scale patchy distribution.

The v component is highly sensitive to the phase shift of vortex street position due to its strong positive negative alternation characteristics and steep spatial gradient. Compared to the 4 × 0.25 configuration, the 4 × 0.75 configuration significantly reduces the intensity of error bands and has a more localized distribution, indicating that increasing training samples at the same resolution can effectively stabilize the vortex phase and suppress large-scale positive and negative mismatches. Although the high resolution of the 2 × 0.75 configuration is beneficial for characterizing fine flow field structures, the difficulty of fitting the vortex phase increases synchronously, and a continuous red blue alternating error texture is still formed downstream. Simply increasing spatial resolution without synchronously increasing the amount of training data or model capacity cannot effectively improve the accuracy of v-component prediction. Other time series algorithms

LSTM, RNN and Transformer algorithms were chosen as time modeling baselines to verify the validity of the present method in the long-term extrapolation of the unsteady wake flow field. The two baseline models are consistent with the PSO-CNN-LSTM proposed in this research in terms of data pre-processing, training set and test set division, normalization method, loss function and rolling prediction strategy. In the test phase, continuous rolling is used to predict the last 10 moments, and the error accumulation behavior of the velocity component is evaluated time by time. Although velocity-field predictions were performed for the entire interval from 49.1 s to 50.0 s using the recurrent neural network, long short-term memory, and Transformer-based models, the manuscript presents only the results from 49.8 s to 50.0 s due to space limitations, as this sub-interval is representative of the overall predictive performance.

Figure 10 compares rolling one-step-ahead predictions of u and v at t = 49.8 s, 49.9 s, and 50.0 s using three baseline sequence models. All three baselines recover the large-scale wake topology, including the alternating vortex street signature in v. However, clear differences appear in the error maps. The RNN exhibits pronounced large-area alternating error patches downstream of the cylinder, indicating a systematic phase shift of the vortical structures during rolling prediction. The LSTM network reduces the overall error magnitude and produces smoother coherent structures, but residual banded errors remain, especially along the separating shear layers and around vortex cores, suggesting that small timing and position offsets persist in regions dominated by flow instability. In contrast, the Transformer network yields visibly more spatially widespread, high-frequency error textures, particularly in the v component, which implies oscillatory artifacts and reduced stability under the short input sequence length and limited training samples. Conclusions

This research presented a PSO-CNN-LSTM framework for reconstructing unsteady cylindrical wake velocity fields from time-resolved data. The framework combines missing frame reconstruction, continuous rolling prediction, and uncertainty quantification for systematic evaluation. The main conclusions are as follows:

A spatio-temporal wake-field reconstruction framework based on PSO-CNN-LSTM was developed for an incompressible cylinder wake in a confined channel. Grid-independence verification and Strouhal-number comparison with particle image velocimetry data confirm the credibility of the numerical dataset, and the proposed model achieves accurate full-field reconstruction of both u and v velocities, with errors primarily confined to shear layers and vortex-core neighborhoods.

A spatio-temporal wake-field reconstruction framework based on PSO-CNN-LSTM was developed for an incompressible cylinder wake in a confined channel. Grid-independence verification and Strouhal-number comparison with particle image velocimetry data confirm the credibility of the numerical dataset, and the proposed model achieves accurate full-field reconstruction of both u and v velocities, with errors primarily confined to shear layers and vortex-core neighborhoods.

The model maintains stable rolling prediction over the final ten steps with limited error growth in the streamwise component, and ablation studies on sampling resolution, training data volume, and input sequence length show that reconstruction accuracy varies only mildly within practical ranges, indicating good adaptability to different data conditions.
