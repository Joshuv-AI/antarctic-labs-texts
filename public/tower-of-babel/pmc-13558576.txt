Amiloride mitigates respiratory distress caused by WFDC2 deficiency via inhibiting the epithelial sodium channel
Authors: Jae Won Roh, Soo Kyung Seo, Jiyoung Oh, Se Jin Kim, Ji Won Hong, Jiyeon Ohk, Hyo Sup Shim, Hyung-Ju Cho, Ala Woo, Song Yee Kim, Young Ae Kang, Hosung Jung, Kyung Won Kim, Moo Suk Park, Heon Yung Gee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic airway diseases such as cystic fibrosis (CF) and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) pose substantial clinical challenges. Here, we explore the p.C97W variant in WAP four-disulfide core domain protein 2 (WFDC2), proposed as a new genetic origin of respiratory distress, especially among Koreans. Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing (WES/WGS) are performed on 64 patients from 62 families presenting with severe bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis. Pathogenic variants are found in 19.4% of families, including a novel homozygous WFDC2 missense variant (c.291 C > G, p.Cys97Trp) in five unrelated families. WFDC2 is expressed in lung epithelial cells, and the p.C97W variant impairs WFDC2 protein folding, secretion, and function. Wfdc2 p.C147W knock-in mice exhibit respiratory failure due to the hyperactive epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) linked to increased PRSS8 activity and recapitulate human disease. Treatment with amiloride, an ENaC inhibitor, improves survival and respiratory function in these mice. In conclusion, the p.C97W variant in WFDC2 is a critical genetic factor in severe chronic airway disease that shares clinical features with CF and PCD. Given its implications for diagnosis and treatment, genetic testing for WFDC2 mutations in individuals with CF- or PCD-like symptoms is recommended.

Introduction

In humans, the respiratory epithelium is critical for air transport, mucociliary clearance, and immune regulation. The airway epithelium comprises various cell types, including basal, ciliated, goblet, and club cells, as well as rare types such as tuft cells, ionocytes, and neuroendocrine cells1. The mucosal surface of the respiratory tract contributes to innate immune defense. Each epithelial cell type plays a distinct role in maintaining airway integrity and the mucociliary clearance system by producing antioxidants, protease inhibitors, antimicrobial peptides or secreting mucins and surfactants2,3. Dysregulation of these processes owing to genetic or environmental factors can lead to the pathophysiology of severe viral infections and chronic pulmonary disorders, including asthma1,4,5, cystic fibrosis (CF)1,6–8, and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD)1,9,10.

The epithelial cells of the lungs secrete numerous protease inhibitors, including serine protease inhibitors11,12, which play important roles in regulating the activity of the epithelial Na+ channel (ENaC)13–15. ENaC is a heterotrimeric protein crucial for sodium reabsorption in epithelial tissues such as the kidneys, lungs, and intestine16. Proper ENaC activation is essential for sodium balance, and its dysregulation can contribute to diseases such as CF and nephrotic syndrome16–18. Inhibiting ENaC improves mucociliary clearance by reducing sodium absorption, leading to improved symptoms in patients with CF or in mouse models19,20. This suggests that targeting ENaC in the airway could be a therapeutic approach for treating airway surface dehydration and mucus hyper-concentration in CF17,21–23.

In this study, we identify a homozygous missense variant (c.291 C > G, p.C97W) in WFDC2, a serine/threonine protease inhibitor with antibacterial activity24,25, through whole-exome and -genome sequencing (WES/WGS) in families affected by respiratory conditions resembling PCD. Individuals with clinically confirmed symptoms carrying this variant exhibit chronic inflammation, bronchiectasis, interstitial fibrosis, and infections caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Consistent with these clinical observations, recent studies have shown that individuals with homozygous WFDC2 loss-of-function mutations exhibit similar respiratory phenotypes, including chronic airway inflammation and bronchiectasis26–28. Knock-in (KI) mice carrying the Wfdc2 p.C147W mutation recapitulate the phenotypes observed in Wfdc2 knockout (KO) mice and humans with the WFDC2 variant. Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and electrophysiological studies, we demonstrate that WFDC2 protects ENaC from hyperactivation by inhibiting proteases that activate this channel. Importantly, treatment with amiloride, an ENaC inhibitor, partially rescues the pulmonary phenotypes and extends survival in both Wfdc2 KO and KI mouse models.

Results

Identification of the WFDC2 variant

The WES/WGS analysis pipeline was used to identify the potential genetic causes in patients with severe bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis (n = 64 from 62 families) (Supplementary Fig. 1)29. Pathogenic variants were identified in 7 of the 62 families (11.3%): five with variants in PCD-linked genes (six patients) and two (two patients) with CFTR variants (Fig. 1a, b, and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, a homozygous missense variant (c.291 C > G, p.Cys97Trp) in WFDC2 was identified in five unrelated families (Fig. 1c). Sanger sequencing confirmed this homozygous mutation in the affected probands and their siblings, while the unaffected mother was heterozygous (Supplementary Fig. 2). The allele frequency of this variant was 0.00001611 in the global population, 0.0004051 in East Asians and 0.0018 in the Korean population, as reported in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD; Supplementary Table 2). Haplotype analysis revealed that individuals carrying the c.291 C > G WFDC2 mutation shared a genomic region of approximately 138.5 kb surrounding the variant (Supplementary Table 3). Subsequent haplotype analysis using the LDhap Tool30 with eight selected SNPs within the shared region revealed that individuals with the c.291 C > G WFDC2 mutation shared a rare H4 haplotype, with a frequency of 0.63% in the homozygous state (Supplementary Table 2). Effect of p.C97W variant on WFDC2 function

WFDC2 contains an N-terminal signal peptide that is involved in trafficking and secretion, and two whey acidic protein (WAP) domains connected by a short loop (Fig. 1d). Each WAP domain includes eight cysteine residues that form four disulfide bonds. Structure prediction using AlphaFold231 showed that C97, located in the second WAP domain, forms a disulfide bond with C109 (Fig. 1d). Multiple sequence alignments of human WFDC2 with its orthologs from several vertebrate species showed that both C97 and C109 are highly conserved across vertebrates, consistent with previous results (Fig. 1e)32. Additionally, the in silico prediction scores classified the C97W variant as pathogenic or disease-causing (Supplementary Table 2).

Next, we predicted the structures of both the wild-type (WT) and p.C97W mutant WFDC2 using ColabFold33 and AlphaFold231. In contrast to the WT, the p.C97W mutant failed to form a disulfide bond with C109 (Fig. 2a). Additionally, the bulky side chain of the Trp (W) residue disrupted hydrogen bond formation near W97 in the second WAP domain (Fig. 2a). WFDC2 forms dimeric structures34; therefore, we predicted the dimeric structures of WT and p.C97W. Clustering of the structures from multiple AlphaFold2 runs showed that the p.C97W structure predominantly exists in a “trans” configuration, where the second WAP domain is untangled and interacts with the other subunit. We next examined the molecular phenotypes of the WFDC2 mutant. Immunofluorescence analysis in HeLa cells using untagged WFDC2 showed that both WT and p.C97W were localized to the endoplasmic reticulum with no significant difference (Fig. 2b, c). Consistent results were observed in HEK293T cells, where WT and p.C97W WFDC2, expressed with a C-terminal 10× HIS tag, colocalized with the endoplasmic reticulum marker, suggesting no defects in intracellular localization (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

WFDC2 is predicted to be secreted via the secretory pathway due to the presence of a signal peptide. Therefore, we examined the secretion of WT and mutant WFDC2. Interestingly, WFDC2 secretion in human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells overexpressing WT or p.C97W WFDC2 revealed that WT WFDC2 produced a single band of approximately 24 kDa in both the lysate and culture media (Fig. 2d). By contrast, the p.C97W variant was not secreted and showed reduced expression in cell lysates (Fig. 2d, e). Furthermore, treatment with tunicamycin, an inhibitor of N-linked glycosylation, or glycosidase digestion by incubation of cell lysates with glycosidases such as PNGase F or Endo H, showed that both WT and p.C97W were glycosylated (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c), as indicated by the shift in band molecular weight, with no difference between the WT and mutant. This finding suggested that WFDC2 was glycosylated, as previously described34. Overall, these findings indicated that the p.C97W mutation disrupts WFDC2 protein folding and secretion, impairing its function.

Clinical phenotypes of individuals with the WFDC2 variant

We next assessed clinical phenotypes of individuals with the WFDC2 variant. All six patients from five unrelated families presented with persistent wet cough and chronic rhinosinusitis (Table 1). Chest CT scans revealed bronchiectasis in five patients, while one patient (P1946-21) declined the scan (Fig. 3a). Although bronchiectasis was the most severe in the upper lobe, it affected the upper, middle, and lower lobes. Nasal endoscopic images showed polyposis and thick mucoid discharge (Fig. 3c). Pathological examination of the original lung tissue from three female patients (P1969-21, P2678-21, and P2783-21) who underwent lung transplantation at an average age of 33 years (range: 21–41 years; Table 1) revealed chronic inflammation, bronchiectasis, and interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 3d). Three patients with clinically confirmed symptoms had P. aeruginosa lung infections, and their pulmonary function demonstrated a severe airflow obstruction pattern before lung transplantation. The average age at the onset of respiratory symptoms was 7.8 years (range, 1 month to 13 years). Initial clinical diagnoses based on respiratory symptoms included neonatal pneumonia in patients P1946-21 and P1969-21; bronchiectasis in patients P2678-21, P2783-21, P5-21, and P5-22; and chronic rhinosinusitis in patients P5-21 and P5-22. Respiratory failure and histological phenotypes in Wfdc2-deficient mice

We investigated whether the phenotypes observed in humans with the WFDC2 p.C97W variant could be replicated in mice. Unlike humans, mice have two Wfdc2 splicing isoforms35. Isoform 2, which lacks exon 3, closely resembled human WFDC2, whereas isoform 1 contained a 52-residue loop between the two WAP domains (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Using PCR, we confirmed that isoform 1 is the predominantly expressed isoform in the lung and kidney tissues, consistent with that in previous studies and database annotations, whereas the exon-3-skipped transcript remains a minor species (NCBI Gene ID 67701) (Supplementary Fig. 5c)32. We generated p.C147W and p.C95W mutant plasmids for both Wfdc2 isoforms and observed no protein secretion into the media upon their overexpression in HEK293T cells, similar to that observed in the human p.C97W variant (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5d).

To further explore the role of WFDC2 in the respiratory system, we generated Wfdc2 KO mice by deleting exons 3 and 4 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b) and KI mice with the p.C147W mutation in the endogenous locus, similar to the human p.C97W variant (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Consistent with previous results36,37, homozygous Wfdc2 KO mice exhibited severe respiratory failure immediately after birth and died within 12 hours of birth (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Video 1). Although a separate study described a WFDC2-null line without evident pulmonary abnormalities38, we did not observe such an absence of a phenotype in our model. Remarkably, Wfdc2 KI mice also displayed a phenotype similar to that in the KO mice, indicating that this single-residue change functionally phenocopies the complete KO (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Video 2). We then examined the respiratory phenotypes of homozygous Wfdc2 KO and KI mice. Gross lung morphology at postnatal day 0.5 (P0.5; ~6–12 h after birth) did not reveal obvious differences among genotypes (Fig. 4c). However, higher-magnification hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that the alveolar diameter was increased, indicating alveolar dilatation (Fig. 4d, e). In addition, Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed mucus accumulation in the trachea of KO and KI mice (Fig. 4f). Because the clinical characteristics of human patients and mouse models resembled those of primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), we further analyzed ciliary morphology. Scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy of tracheal tissue on P0.5 showed that KO and KI mice had shorter tracheal cilia (Fig. 4g−j), similar to a previously reported result; however, the axoneme structures appeared intact (Fig. 4h).

Given that impaired mucociliary clearance is often associated with altered epithelial differentiation and airway inflammation, we further assessed these phenotypes in the lung. Secretory cell differentiation was impaired, as validated by immunostaining (Fig. 4k, l). In addition, neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) staining was increased in Wfdc2-deficient lungs, indicating enhanced neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 4m, n).

Overall, the Wfdc2 mouse models phenocopied human disease, exhibiting a PCD-like respiratory phenotype despite preserved axonemal structure of cilia. Notably, this phenotype was accompanied by impaired club cell differentiation and increased immune cell infiltration in the lung.

Comparison of cellular composition across genotypes revealed marked differences. Relative to WT mice, both KO and KI mice exhibited a substantial increase in immune cell populations alongside a decrease in epithelial cells (Fig. 5d). This shift was especially evident in the reduction of AT1 cells to approximately half of WT levels, while neutrophils were more than doubled (Fig. 5d). In contrast, AT2 cell proportions were relatively preserved but exhibited substantial transcriptional alterations (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Data 1). These findings are consistent with our earlier observations of increased immune cell infiltration (Fig. 4m, n).

We then examined the expression pattern of Wfdc2 in airway systems. As expected, Wfdc2 expression was completely absent in KO mice and significantly reduced in KI mice compared to the WT (Fig. 5e), consistent with our previous in vitro data showing decreased expression of the WFDC2 p.C97W variant (Fig. 2d). When examined across epithelial subtypes, Wfdc2 expression was highest in club cells and AT2 cells, but was broadly and non-specifically expressed across epithelial populations (Fig. 5f).

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of epithelial cells revealed distinct transcriptional changes associated with Wfdc2 deficiency (Fig. 5g, h). In KO epithelial cells, genes related to apoptotic processes, protein metabolism, transport, and cell adhesion were enriched, while pathways associated with epithelial cell differentiation and multicellular organismal development were relatively downregulated (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Data 1). Similarly, KI epithelial cells showed enrichment of pathways related to transport, programmed cell death, small molecule metabolism, and cell proliferation, alongside upregulation of transcriptional regulation processes (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, both KO and KI models exhibited alterations in epithelial differentiation–related pathways and a consistent upregulation of transporter-associated genes compared to WT, suggesting changes in epithelial differentiation programs and transport-related processes. Additionally, cell–cell interaction analysis using CellChat (version 2.4.0) revealed increased signaling related to inflammation, tissue repair, and immune cell recruitment in KO and KI mice, particularly involving macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes, and epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that both KO and KI mice exhibit similar phenotypic and transcriptomic alterations, characterized by disrupted epithelial cell differentiation and increased immune cell infiltration. These findings suggest that the elevated immune response is likely secondary to a primary defect in epithelial cells caused by Wfdc2 deficiency.

WFDC2 deficiency leads to hyperactivation of ENaC

Next, we elucidated the pathogenic mechanisms underlying WFDC2 deficiency in the respiratory system. The WAP domains in WFDC2 exhibit anti-protease and anti-bacterial activities24,25,39. Given that all mice were maintained under aseptic conditions, we hypothesized that respiratory failure might result from dysregulated serine-threonine protease inhibitor activity. To identify potential target proteases inhibited by WFDC2 under physiological conditions, we analyzed the human adult single-cell lung atlas (Fig. 6a)40. Consistent with our mouse models, WFDC2 was highly and exclusively expressed in the human epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Of the ~140 serine proteases, PRSS8 showed the highest expression in human epithelial cells, followed by TMPRSS2, DPP7, and PRSS22 (Fig. 6a). Some proteases, including Prss8 and Tmprss2, were also highly expressed in the neonatal lungs of mice (Fig. 6b, c). Multiple serine/threonine proteases, including PRSS8, enhance ENaC activity by cleaving the γ subunit13. To assess whether WFDC2 directly modulates such protease activities, we performed in vitro enzymatic assays using CTSB and PRSS8 (Fig. 6d). WFDC2 markedly inhibited both CTSB and PRSS8 activities in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6d). Hyperactive ENaC leads to increased Na+ reabsorption and mucus viscosity, a key feature of CF. In this study, Prss8 and ENaC expression was reduced in Wfdc2 KO and KI mice, suggesting their involvement in epithelial cell differentiation (Fig. 6b, c)41.

Considering these findings, we hypothesized that WFDC2 suppresses ENaC-activating protease activity to maintain normal mucociliary clearance in the respiratory system10. To test this hypothesis, we heterologously expressed ENaC and PRSS8 in HEK293T cells and measured the channel activity using a whole-cell voltage clamp (Fig. 6e). Immunoblotting confirmed the expression of ENaC subunits, PRSS8, and both WT and C97W WFDC2 in transfected HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 10). Quantification of ENaC activity using the amiloride-sensitive current amplitude revealed that PRSS8 co-expression increased the ENaC current amplitude four-fold (Fig. 6e, f). Co-expression with WT WFDC2, but not the p.C97W mutant, restored channel activity to resting-state levels (Fig. 6e, f).

Next, we examined whether ENaC cleavage is altered in the lungs of Wfdc2-deficient mice in vivo. Western blot analysis revealed that total γ-ENaC levels were largely comparable across genotypes, whereas the cleaved form (~35 kDa) was markedly increased in both KO and KI lungs compared to the WT (Fig. 6g−i). These findings suggest that the WT WFDC2 suppresses ENaC proteolytic activation, whereas loss or mutation of WFDC2 leads to enhanced γ-ENaC cleavage and likely increased channel activity.

To determine whether this effect was tissue-specific, we further analyzed kidney and gut tissues, where ENaC and WFDC2 are also highly expressed in epithelial cells. In contrast to the lung, γ-ENaC cleavage in both kidney and gut was comparable across WT, KO, and KI mice (Fig. 6g), and no overt morphological abnormalities were observed by H&E and PAS staining (Supplementary Fig. 11). Consistently, electrolyte measurements assessing kidney function revealed no significant differences among genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Together, these results indicate that enhanced ENaC proteolytic activation in Wfdc2-deficient mice is largely restricted to the lung. This suggests that WFDC2-dependent protease–protease inhibitor regulation plays a particularly prominent role in the pulmonary epithelium and supports the idea that lung dysfunction is the primary driver of the observed phenotype.

Amiloride rescues respiratory failure in Wfdc2-deficient mouse models

To explore whether inhibiting overactive ENaC could mitigate the effects of WFDC2 deficiency, we tested amiloride, an FDA-approved ENaC inhibitor used to treat high blood pressure by promoting diuresis42,43. Although amiloride is a partially non-selective inhibitor with potential effects on basolateral Na+/H+ exchangers44, in the airway context, its most relevant action is likely through modulation of apical ENaC activity. Hyperactive ENaC activity can lead to CF-like lung disease, and amiloride can ameliorate such phenotypes45–47. Notably, homozygous Wfdc2 KO and KI pups born to amiloride-treated females showed reduced respiratory failure and longer survival than those from untreated females (Fig. 7b, c, and Supplementary Video 3). Treated pups had a median survival of approximately 3 days, which was over six times longer than that of untreated pups (Fig. 7b). The longest-lived mouse survived for up to 40 postnatal days, although its body size was smaller than that of its heterozygote littermates (Supplementary Video 4). At P3, overall lung morphology did not show marked differences between the WT and amiloride-treated KI mice (Fig. 7d). Moreover, higher-magnification H&E analysis revealed that alveolar diameter in amiloride-treated KI mice was restored to levels comparable to WT (Fig. 7e, f). Similarly, SEM observations of amiloride-treated KO pups at P3 showed cilia length and morphology comparable to those of WT pups (Fig. 7g, h). TEM imaging further confirmed that both ciliary ultrastructure and overall cellular morphology were largely comparable between amiloride-treated mutants and the WT (Fig. 7i).

We next examined whether previously observed defects in epithelial differentiation were also rescued. Notably, the reduction in club cell differentiation observed in Wfdc2 KI mice was restored upon amiloride treatment, as evidenced by expression patterns comparable to those in WT (Fig. 7j, k), indicating recovery of epithelial differentiation.

To further assess epithelial function, we performed air–liquid interface (ALI) culture using primary airway epithelial cells isolated from P0.5 mice (Fig. 7l). KI cultures exhibited reduced mucus levels and decreased differentiation of club cells compared to the WT, recapitulating the in vivo phenotype (Fig. 7l and Supplementary Fig. 13). Importantly, amiloride treatment led to a transient restoration of mucus levels in KI cultures, demonstrating functional recovery of epithelial properties upon amiloride treatment (Fig. 7l).

To assess whether amiloride treatment restores epithelial cell states at transcriptomic levels, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing at P4 (Fig. 8a, b). Differential expression analysis revealed that amiloride treatment partially normalized the transcriptional alterations observed in WFDC2 mutant conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14). GO analysis of epithelial cells demonstrated normalization of pathways related to cellular stress responses, metabolic processes, and transcriptional regulation in amiloride-treated mice compared to untreated mutants (Fig. 8c–e and Supplementary Data 2). To further assess the cellular states following amiloride treatment, we next examined epithelial cell populations. As AT1 and AT2 cells constitute the major epithelial cell types in the lung, we focused our analysis on these populations. Overall, our in vitro and in vivo results showed that WFDC2 inhibits ENaC proteolytic activation, and that pharmacological inhibition of ENaC by amiloride partially rescues the respiratory, structural, and epithelial differentiation defects observed in WFDC2-deficient mice. In addition to improving survival, amiloride treatment restored epithelial differentiation, ciliary morphology, and mucus production, and partially normalized epithelial cell states at the transcriptomic level.

Discussion

Mutations in WFDC2 were first linked to airway pathology by Dougherty et al.26, who identified biallelic WFDC2 variants in patients with nasal polyposis and bronchiectasis and showed that WFDC2 deficiency compromises pulmonary function and predisposes them to chronic infection. Building on these results, we describe a novel autosomal-recessive WFDC2 variant, c.291 C > G; p.C97W, that segregates with severe lung disease marked by chronic inflammation, bronchiectasis, interstitial fibrosis, and severe airflow obstruction, often necessitating lung transplantation. Through multiple mechanistic studies, we demonstrated that this single cysteine-to-tryptophan substitution disrupts the WAP four-disulfide-core domain, fully phenocopies the complete Wfdc2 KO in mice, and unleashes a WFDC2–PRSS8–ENaC axis that drives neonatal respiratory failure and ciliary dysfunction. Together, our findings replicate the observations of Dougherty et al.26 and reveal the molecular pathway by which WFDC2 safeguards airway homeostasis.

Population data from gnomAD and KRGDB indicated that c.291 C > G; p.C97W allele has a relatively high frequency in East Asian populations, particularly in the Korean population. Based on these data, approximately three out of one million individuals are homozygous for this mutation, suggesting that approximately 150 individuals in South Korea could be affected. In contrast, the previously reported c.145 T > C; p.C49R variant, identified as a European founder allele, is extremely rare in East Asian populations, including Koreans. However, it has a higher frequency in the European population, with an allele frequency of 0.0001814, equivalent to three individuals per hundred million and approximately 25 patients in Europe, based on a population of 750 million. Additionally, 22 loss-of-function (LoF) alleles in WFDC2 have been reported in gnomAD, with a combined homozygous frequency of approximately 0.0000000003178. Notably, a recent study from Korea27 and Japan28 reported the identical WFDC2 variant identified in our study. These findings support the disease relevance of WFDC2 and the potential existence of population-specific founder alleles and, taken together, emphasize the importance of further investigating this locus in cohorts of patients with sinopulmonary disease who remained undiagnosed with PCD or CF.

Respiratory phenotypes of WFDC2 deficiency identified in our study and another study26 present clinical features that closely resemble CF and PCD, with symptoms including bronchiectasis, chronic rhinosinusitis, and recurrent respiratory infections. Similar to CF, patients with WFDC2 mutations develop bronchiectasis across all lung lobes and are prone to chronic P. aeruginosa infections, indicating a more severe and diffuse impact on lung structure in WFDC2 deficiency and CF than in PCD. Furthermore, nasal and sinus abnormalities, including chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps, were prevalent in all three conditions. In this study, three patients with WFDC2 mutations underwent lung transplantation because of respiratory failure, further highlighting the severe progression of the disease.

The WFDC2 c.291 C > G; p.C97W variant shares molecular similarities with the previously described c.145 T > C; p.C49R variant26. Both are autosomal recessive missense mutations in cysteine residues within the WAP domain, a critical region for disulfide bond formation, and lead to LoF by disrupting the WAP domain. The key difference between the two variants is that the p.C49R variant affects the first WAP domain, whereas the p.C97W variant affects the second. Additionally, unlike the p.C49R variant, which is associated with glycosylation defects26, our data did not reveal glycosylation differences for the p.C97W variant, nor did it alter its intracellular localization compared to the WT protein. Structural modeling using AlphaFold231 aligns well with the disruption of WAP domain integrity due to the loss of disulfide bonds, suggesting that improper dimer formation may explain the differences in the effects of the p.C97W variant. Despite these structural differences, both variants exhibit similar defects in protein secretion, suggesting comparable pathological consequences in humans.

WES/WGS analysis of patients with severe bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis revealed no significant abnormalities in axoneme ultrastructure or ciliogenesis. However, motile cilia in Wfdc2 KO and KI mice were significantly shorter than those in WT mice. The WFDC2 mutation results in excessive activation of ENaC, which is present in cilia and contributes to osmolarity sensing16,48. Furthermore, ENaC hyperactivation itself causes mucus thickening and ciliary damage10, leading to secondary cilia dysfunction and respiratory defects in these mice. Therefore, the partial rescue of the ciliary phenotype with amiloride in mice suggests that WFDC2 indirectly contributes to ciliary damage and subsequent respiratory defects observed in these mice.

Both our study and that of Dougherty et al.26 reported that individuals with homozygous WFDC2 LoF mutations exhibit respiratory symptoms that manifest across a broad age range, from the first to fifth decades of life. However, homozygous Wfdc2 KO and KI mice displayed a much more severe phenotype than that observed in humans, with perinatal lethality observed within 12 hours of birth. This disparity is mostly attributable to inter-species differences in the airway protease–antiprotease equilibrium. The murine airway could harbor a comparatively limited inhibitor repertoire, thereby giving WFDC2 a disproportionately large contribution to protease control. Consequently, WFDC2 deficiency could perturb the balance more profoundly in mice than in humans, resulting in the accentuated murine phenotype. These findings suggest the existence of additional mechanisms, beyond ENaC regulation, contributing to the severity of the phenotype in mice.

WFDC2 is broadly expressed across airway epithelial cells but shows the highest levels in AT2 and club cells (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 9). Thus, deficiency of WFDC2 is expected to exert substantial effects on these two cell types, likely contributing to the early death observed in mutant mice. In combination, AT2 dysfunction in surfactant synthesis and regeneration together with impaired club cell programs likely accelerates barrier instability, alveolar collapse, and the respiratory failure phenotype49–51. Importantly, treatment with amiloride, a specific ENaC inhibitor, attenuated many of these transcriptional alterations, suggesting that ENaC hyperactivity is a proximal driver of the maladaptive programs in both AT2 and club cells. Consistent with prior studies46,47, these findings support the idea that transient ENaC inhibition may be beneficial in a hyperactive state, although the potential contribution of systemic effects should be considered.

Wfdc2 deletion disrupts multiple epithelial cell types in the lung. Zhang et al. reported that loss of Wfdc2 results in severe dyspnea accompanied by extensive apoptosis of AT1 cells, implicating defects in alveolar differentiation or maintenance36. Consistently, Nakajima et al. showed that Wfdc2-deficient mice die neonatally from respiratory failure, with impaired differentiation across several epithelial lineages, including club cells (Scgb1a1+), ciliated epithelial cells, and both AT1 and AT2 cells37. Together, these findings support a role for WFDC2 in maintaining the protease–antiprotease balance required for epithelial maturation and lung homeostasis. By contrast, Jeong et al. reported that Wfdc2 KO mice did not exhibit a lethal phenotype38, highlighting variability across models and suggesting that strain background, targeting strategy, or environmental factors may influence disease expressivity. This discrepancy warrants further consideration when interpreting the contribution of WFDC2 to perinatal lethality.

Several considerations may explain why WFDC2 variants chiefly manifest in the airway, despite robust expression in other epithelia. First, the airway surface liquid is enriched in endogenous (e.g., PRSS8/prostasin) and exogenous (bacterial, neutrophil) serine proteases52–54. Hence, the airway epithelium likely depends critically on WFDC2 to keep ENaC cleavage as well as fluid absorption within physiological limits. Second, extra-pulmonary tissues that express WFDC2, such as the kidney, stomach, and reproductive tract, also produce other potent inhibitors such as SLPI, elafin (WFDC14), and several serpins, which could partially or fully compensate for WFDC2 loss55,56. Such redundancy appears less pronounced in the neonatal murine and adult human airway. At birth, the lung must rapidly reabsorb fetal fluid and start ciliary clearance within just a few hours57,58. Thus, even a brief overshoot in ENaC activity, or any transient disruption of mucociliary function, could precipitate respiratory failure, whereas other organs are not subject to such immediate osmotic and clearance demands. Taken together, these factors provide a plausible rationale for the airway-restricted phenotype observed in humans and mice carrying WFDC2 loss-of-function variants. Further studies will be necessary to verify the hypothesis and clarify any additional mechanisms at play.

In conclusion, we identified the p.C97W WFDC2 variant as a novel cause of severe chronic airway disease, which is physiologically distinct but clinically similar to CF and PCD. Our findings unveiled the effects of ENaC hyperactivation and demonstrated the partial phenotypic rescue with amiloride in mouse models. While these results point to ENaC inhibition as a potential therapeutic avenue, the known limitations of amiloride, including the risk of systemic hyperkalemia and its short half-life in the lung, indicate that its clinical translation would require careful consideration and optimization59–61. Further investigation into lung-compatible strategies for modulating ENaC and WFDC2 activity will therefore be necessary. Moreover, our study revealed the prevalence of the WFDC2 p.C97W variant in the Korean population, suggesting that genetic testing for this gene could be beneficial in patients with respiratory phenotypes resembling PCD or CF.

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University Health System (IRB #4-2013-0770 and #4-2024-0647). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for participation in this study and for the collection and publication of their clinical information and biospecimens. The present study used de-identified clinical information and biospecimens under these approved protocols. Sixty-four patients with severe bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis from 62 unrelated families were enrolled through institutional severe bronchiectasis and respiratory disease research cohorts at a single tertiary medical center in South Korea. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed to identify potential genetic causes. In-depth genetic analyses were performed on individuals without any potential molecular causes.

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yonsei University College of Medicine, under protocol number 2022-0303. All mice were handled following the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice used in this study were on a C57BL/6 J background, including Wfdc2 knockout mice and Wfdc2 p.C147W knock-in mice. Both mouse lines included mixed-sex litters and were analyzed between postnatal day 1 (P1) and P60. Sex was not considered as a biological variable in the study design and analysis, because Wfdc2 is located on an autosome and the majority of analyses were performed before sexual maturation.

Analysis of whole exome/genome sequencing data

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples of patients with severe bronchiectasis with chronic rhinosinusitis using a DNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed using a SureSelect V5 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and an Illumina HiSeq 2500. Raw WES FASTQ files were imported into CLC Genomics Workbench version 9.5.3 for data analysis. Sequenced reads were mapped to the human reference genome, GRCh37/hg19. Variants were detected using the Basic Variant Caller of the CLC Genomics Workbench, with a minimum coverage of 5, a count of 2, and a frequency of 20%. Low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using a PCR-free DNBSEQ platform (BGI, Beijing, China). Following GATK best practice guidelines, the reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/hg19 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM). Duplicates were marked, reads were sorted, and the mapping quality was recalibrated using PICARD-MarkDuplicates (version 2.27.4) and GATK-BQSR (version 4.3). Variant calling of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions (indels) was performed using GATK-HaplotypeCaller (version 4.3), and low-quality variants with a depth of coverage (DP) < 5 and genotype quality (GQ) < 20 were filtered out. Copy Number Variation (CNV) analysis was performed using EXCAVATOR62 version 2.2 and ExomeDepth63 (version 1.1.10) with default settings for the WES data. For WGS data, CNVnator (https://github.com/abyzovlab/CNVnator, version 0.3.2) and GATK-SV (https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk-sv, version 1.1) were used to detect CNV. Mitochondrial variants and transposable element insertion calling were performed using Mutect2 (version 4.1.0.0) and xTea (https://github.com/parklab/xTea, version 0.1.9).

SNVs and small indels were annotated using Variant Annotation tools in the CLC Workbench or ANNOVAR software (version 2025Mar02). SNVs and small indels were first filtered based on minor allele frequency (MAF), applying a threshold of MAF < 1% according to the Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms Database (dbSNP), Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), and Korean Reference Genome Database (KRGDB); variants that were nonsynonymous or located in splice sites were selected. To evaluate pathogenicity and prioritize the candidate causative variants, in silico prediction algorithms, such as MutationTaster, Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT), Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PP2) scores, and public databases including Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) and ClinVar, were used. Interpretation of the variants was performed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. To identify splice site variants, SpliceAI (version 1.3.1) was run on the variants using Ensembl’s Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) tool (version 116) and filtered using a threshold MAF of 1% and delta score threshold of 0.1. Variants were annotated based on their presence in human candidate cis-regulatory elements, as identified using the SCREEN database from the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) project. Confirmation of the detected variants was performed manually using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) and through segregation analysis using Sanger sequencing.

Structural prediction and clustering

AlphaFold231 (version 2.3.0) and ColabFold33 (version 1.6.1) were used to construct the de novo monomer and dimer WFDC2 structures, respectively. The AlphaFold2-multimer-V2 algorithm was applied without using a template or minimization using three recycle number parameters in ColabFold. The full-length WT or p.C97W human WFDC2 sequence was used as input. To generate different structural conformations, multiple runs were conducted using different seed values in ColabFold software. A total of 100 different structures were generated for the WT monomer, p.C97W monomer, WT dimer, and p.C97W dimer. The monomer and dimer structures were then structurally aligned to perform principal component analysis (PCA) using the bio3d package64 (version 2.4-5) in R (version 4.1.0). The K-means algorithm was implemented to cluster the structures. The optimal number of clusters was suggested by R. ChimeraX65 (version 1.7) was used to visualize protein structures.

Cell culture, plasmids, and transfection

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells (#CRL-3216, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) and HeLa cells (#CCL-2, ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin (50 IU/mL)/streptomycin (50 μg/mL; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were incubated in a 5% CO2 environment at 37 °C.

Plasmids encoding human WFDC2 (NM_006103.4) and mouse Wfdc2 (NM_026323.2, isoform 1 and NM_001374655.1, isoform 2) were purchased from OriGene (Rockville, MD, USA). The coding sequences were amplified by PCR and subcloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector using XbaI and AgeI sites. During PCR, 10× HIS tags were inserted directly into the 3′ end in front of the stop codon. Human and mouse WFDC2 mutants were introduced using the QuikChange mutagenesis method (Agilent Technologies). For electrophysiology, the plasmids with coding sequences of human SCNN1A (NM_001038.6), SCNN1B (NM_000336.3), SCNN1G (NM_001039.4), and PRSS8 (NM_002773.5) were purchased from GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA) in the pcDNA3.1(+)-c-FLAG backbone and used for the experiments. For immunoblotting, the coding sequences of human SCNN1A, SCNN1B, SCNN1G, and PRSS8 were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) backbone with C-terminal HA, MYC, FLAG, and V5 tags, respectively, using Gibson assembly with NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly kit (#E2621, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The pEYFP-ER plasmid was purchased from NovoPro Biosciences (San Diego, CA, USA).

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates the day before transfection. Plasmids were transfected using a PEI-MAX transfection reagent (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 0.5 μg of the desired plasmid and 2 μL of PEI-MAX were used for transfection. Cells were used for experiments 24–48 hours post-transfection.

Primary mouse airway epithelial cell culture and air–liquid interface differentiation

To obtain basal cells, tracheae were isolated from P0.5 mice and incubated in dispase at 37 °C for 30 minutes to facilitate epithelial cell dissociation. Following incubation, the tracheal lumen was flushed with 10 mL of PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to collect basal cells. Cells were centrifuged and resuspended in expansion medium containing insulin (10 µg/mL, #I1882, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), bovine insulin-transferrin-selenium (#I3146, Sigma), cholera toxin (0.1 µg/mL, #C8052, Sigma), epidermal growth factor (25 ng/mL, #5303-C08, Invitrogen), bovine pituitary extract (30 µg/mL, #13028-014, Invitrogen), basic fibroblast growth factor (25 ng/mL, #13256-029, Invitrogen), and retinoic acid (0.01 µM, #R2625, Sigma). Cells were mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio, and seeded onto 24‑well Transwell (0.4 µm pore size, #353104, SPL) inserts to establish three‑dimensional cultures. Cells were maintained in expansion medium with daily replacement of the basal compartment medium.

For air–liquid interface (ALI) differentiation, cells were seeded onto 24‑well Transwell inserts at a density of at least 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in expansion medium for two days to allow cell attachment and proliferation. The basal medium was then replaced with PneumaCult‑ALI medium (#05001, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada), and the apical medium was removed to establish the air–liquid interface. The basal medium was refreshed every two days, and cellular differentiation and phenotypes were assessed after five weeks of ALI culture.

Airway surface liquid (ASL) height measurement

Two days prior to application, the apical surface of ALI cultures was washed with PBS to remove accumulated mucus. Subsequently, 5 μL of tetramethylrhodamine (TMR)-dextran solution (1 mg/mL, #D1818, Invitrogen) was applied to the apical surface to label the airway surface liquid layer. After 24 hours of incubation, cultures were stained with Hoechst dye for approximately 10 minutes to visualize epithelial cell nuclei. Z-stack images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope, and XZ orthogonal views were generated to measure ASL thickness.

Immunoblotting

Forty-eight hours after transfection, HEK293T cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and harvested in a lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (#04693116001, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The cells were then briefly sonicated to break the cell membrane and homogenize the sample. To detect proteins in endogenous mouse lung, gut, and kidney tissues, the tissues were first chopped and homogenized using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen). The homogenized samples were then centrifuged for 15 min at 15,000 × g at 4 °C. The supernatant was then transferred into a new tube. Bradford assay was performed to measure the protein concentration at an absorbance of 595 nm. Cell lysates (40–60 μg) were used for analysis.

The samples were incubated in 2x sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer containing dithiothreitol (DTT, 0.02 g/mL) for 30 min at room temperature and subsequently separated using SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). The separated proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), which was blotted using suitable primary antibodies and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% skimmed milk66. Protein bands were visualized using the SuperSignal West Pico kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a blue film (Agfa, Belgium). The density of each protein band was quantified using the ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/, version 1.54p).

To detect secreted WFDC2 proteins, transfected cells were incubated in a serum-free medium for 24 hours. Culture media were collected and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 filters (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The concentrated media were directly heated with 2x SDS sample buffer with DTT and subjected to SDS-PAGE.

The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: anti-HA (#C29F4, Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA, USA; 1:1000 dilution), anti-MYC (#9B11, Cell Signaling Technologies; 1:1000 dilution), anti-FLAG (#2368, Cell Signaling Technologies; 1:1000 dilution), anti-HIS (#2365, Cell Signaling Technologies; 1:1000 dilution), anti-V5 (#R960-25, Invitrogen; 1:1000 dilution), anti-ALDOA (#sc-390733, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; 1:1000 dilution), anti-Scnn1g (#A6126, ABclonal, Woburn, MA, USA; 1:1000 dilution), HRP Anti-beta Actin antibody (#ab20272, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:2000 dilution), goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (HRP conjugate) (#ADI-SAB-300-J, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA), and goat anti-mouse IgG F(ab’)2 polyclonal antibody (HRP conjugate) (#ADI-SAB-100-J; Enzo Life Sciences; 1:1000 dilution).

Protease activity assay

Recombinant human PRSS8 (#4599-SE, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) activity was measured following incubation with recombinant WFDC2 (#12609-H08H, Sino Biological, Beijing, China) at the specified concentrations, at 37 °C overnight in assay buffer (50 mM Tris, 0.05% Brij 35, pH 9.5). After incubation, PRSS8 activity was assayed using the fluorogenic substrate BOC-Gln-Ala-Arg-AMC (#ES014, R&D Systems; 100 μM) in a black 96-well plate, and fluorescence was monitored kinetically at 380/460 nm.

For cathepsin B (CTSB) activity, WFDC2 and CTSB were also incubated together at 37 °C overnight before measuring CTSB activity. It was performed with a Cathepsin B activity assay kit (#ab65300, Abcam), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence was monitored kinetically at 400/505 nm after substrate addition.

Immunocytochemistry

Transfected HEK293T and HeLa cells were cultured on an 18 mm round coverslip. The cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.15% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. The cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with blocking buffer consisting of 5% donkey serum for 1 hour at room temperature67. After blocking, the cells were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were stained with secondary antibodies conjugated to a fluorescent marker for 1 h at room temperature and washed thrice with PBS. The samples were mounted onto glass slides using a mounting medium (Agilent Dako, CA, USA), and images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 700, Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a 40× objective lens.

The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: anti-HIS (#2365, Cell Signaling Technologies; 1:200 dilution), anti-WFDC2 (#14406-1-AP, Proteintech; 1:200 dilution), anti-Scgb1a1 (#213203, Abcam; 1:200 dilution), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (#A21206, Invitrogen; 1:400 dilution), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit (#A21207, Invitrogen; 1:400 dilution), Alexa Fluor 647 Phalloidin (#A22287, Invitrogen; 1:400 dilution).

Deglycosylation assay

Two methods were used to determine the glycosylation state of WFDC2. After transfection, cells were treated with 2 μM tunicamycin (Sigma) for 20 hours. A higher dose or longer incubation time with tunicamycin caused excessive cell death, preventing further analysis. The cells were harvested and subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting. Transfected cells were denatured in 2x SDS sample buffer containing DTT. Denatured samples were incubated with PNGase F or Endo H (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Deglycosylated samples were analyzed using SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. The shift in protein size was analyzed using both methods.

Generation of Wfdc2 KO and Wfdc2 p.C147W mice

The Wfdc2 knockout (KO) mouse was generated by Cyagen (Suzhou, China). The Wfdc2 gene (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_026323.2; Ensembl: ENSMUSG00000017723) is located on mouse chromosome 2. Five exons are identified, with the ATG start codon in exon 1 and the TGA stop codon in exon 4 (Transcript Wfdc2- 201: ENSMUST00000017867). Exon 3 ~ 4 was selected as the target site. Cas9 protein, gRNA (gRNA-A1: CCTGCGGGAAGAACCCCAAG-GGG, gRNA-A2: AGATGAGAGGGTCCTCAGGA-TGG) were co-injected into fertilized eggs. The embryos were transferred to a recipient female mouse to obtain F0 mice. The genotype of the Wfdc2 KO mice was confirmed by PCR using specific primers and sequencing: F, 5’-GGATTCGATTCTCTGGTGCATGA-3’; and R, 5′- CAAAGGGATACCATCTCCTCAACT-3’.

The Wfdc2 p.C147W knock-in (KI) mouse was generated by Cyagen. The p.C147 is located in exon 4. Cas9, gRNA (gRNA-1: CTGTGAGGACCAGTGTCAGG-TGG), and donor oligo were co-injected into fertilized eggs for KI mouse production. p.C147W (TGT to TGG) in the donor oligo was introduced into exon 4 by homology-directed repair. The embryos were transferred to a recipient female mouse to obtain F0 mice. The genotype of Wfdc2 KI mice was confirmed by PCR using specific primers and sequencing: F, 5′-CCCATGATGAACTCAGATAGGCA-3′; R, 5′-CCACTAGACAAGAAAACCTAGCAG-3′.

Mice housing and genotyping

Mice were housed under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and humidity of 50 ± 10%, with free access to food and water, in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Yonsei Laboratory Animal Research Center, Yonsei University College of Medicine.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the KAPA Express Extract kit (#KK7100, Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Wfdc2+/+, Wfdc2+/KO, and Wfdc2KO/KO mice were genotyped by PCR using the following primers: F1, 5′-GGATTCGATTCTCTGGTGCATGA-3′; F2, 5′-GGCATTTAAGGAAGAACAGGAAGG-3′; R, 5′-CAAAGGGATACCATCTCCTCAACT-3′. The wild-type allele yielded a 445 bp PCR product, and the KO allele yielded a 342 bp PCR product.

Wfdc2+/+, Wfdc2+/KI, and Wfdc2KI/KI mice were genotyped by PCR using the following primers: F, 5′-CCCATGATGAACTCAGATAGGCA-3′; R, 5′-CCACTAGACAAGAAAACCTAGCAG-3′, followed by restriction enzyme digestion of the PCR products with FastDigest BoxI (#FD1434, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. The KI allele (462 bp) was not cleaved by BoxI, whereas the wild-type allele was cleaved into two fragments of 286 bp and 176 bp.

Immunohistochemistry

The lung, kidney, and gut were dissected in PBS and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), after which 5 μm thick tissue sections were prepared using paraffin embedding. Tissue slides were deparaffinized and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, cells were blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 1 hour. These samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (1:200) diluted in 1% goat serum in 0.05% Triton X-100. After washing thrice with 1X PBS, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) diluted in 3% goat serum at room temperature for 60 min. The samples were counterstained with DAPI and Alexa Fluor-conjugated Phalloidin 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed on serial sections. The samples were mounted on glass slides using ProLong Gold anti-fade reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using an LSM 700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The images were processed using ZEN (Blue Edition) software.

The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: anti-Neutrophil Elastase (#68672, Abcam; 1:200 dilution), anti-MPO (#AF3667, R&D Systems; 1:200 dilution), anti-Scgb1a1 (#213203, Abcam; 1:200 dilution), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (#A21206, Invitrogen; 1:400 dilution).

Human lung tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing clinically indicated lung transplantation at Severance Hospital. Histological analyses shown in Fig. 3 were performed using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks archived in the Department of Pathology following routine clinical diagnosis. Additional sections were prepared from the archived blocks for this study; the original FFPE blocks were not destroyed and remain archived in the Department of Pathology according to institutional policy. Research biospecimens are stored in the institutional biobank under approved protocols. Requests for access to remaining samples are subject to ethical approval, patient consent, material availability, and institutional regulations. Requests should be directed to the corresponding authors.

H&E and PAS-stained histology images were acquired using an Olympus microscope with cellSens Entry software (version 1.16, Build 15404).

Scanning electron microscopy

Lung tissues were dissected and fixed for 24 hours in Karnovsky’s fixative (2% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) and washed twice for 30 min in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. The tissue was post-fixed with 1% OsO4 for 2 hours and dehydrated in an ascending gradual series (50–100%) of ethanol using a Critical Point Dryer (LEICA EM CPD300). The samples were coated with carbon via ion sputtering (LEICA EM ACE600) and observed under a field-emission scanning electron microscope (MERLIN, ZEISS). Imaging was acquired using a Bruker EDS System (X-flash 6, Bruker) with ESPRIT software (version 2.1).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy specimens were fixed for 12 h in 2% glutaraldehyde-2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and washed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, post-fixed with 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 2 h, dehydrated with an ascending ethanol series (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for 10 min each, and infiltrated with propylene oxide for 10 min. The specimens were embedded using a Poly/Bed 812 kit (Polysciences) and polymerized in an electron microscope oven (TD-700, DOSAKA, Japan) at 65 °C for 12 hours. The block was equipped with a diamond knife in an ultra-microtome (UC7, Leica Microsystems Ltd., Vienna, Austria), cut into 200 nm semi-thin sections, and stained with toluidine blue for observation under an optical microscope. The region of interest was then cut into 80 nm thin sections using an ultramicrotome, placed on copper grids, double-stained with 5% uranyl acetate for 20 min, stained with 3% lead citrate for 7 min, and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 90 kV equipped with an RC camera.

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell voltage-clamp methods were used to measure channel activity of ENaC-transfected HEK293T cells at room temperature (22–25 °C). Cells were seeded on 10 mm coverslips prior to the experiment, and the coverslips were mounted on the stage of an inverted microscope (Ti-U; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a high-density mercury lamp light source for green fluorescence excitation. Microglass pipettes (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA) were fabricated using a PP-830 single-stage glass microelectrode puller (Narishige, Japan), with a resistance of 2–5 MΩ. The liquid junction potential was rectified using an offset circuit prior to each recording. Currents were recorded using a MultiClamp 700 B amplifier and Digidata 1440 A interface, digitized at 10 kHz, and low-pass filtered at 5 kHz using pClamp software 10.7 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The whole-cell patch-clamp configuration was verified by ensuring a series resistance of less than 10 MΩ, which was compensated for before each recording. In the whole-cell configuration, currents were measured in a gap-free configuration while maintaining the membrane potential at –60 mV.

Whole-cell voltage clamp experiments were conducted using a basal extracellular bath solution containing 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM glucose, 1 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM sorbitol, adjusted to pH 7.4 using NaOH. The osmolarity of the bath solution is approximately 310 mOsm. The basal pipette solution contained 140 mM KCl, 5 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM EGTA, and 3 mM Mg-ATP, adjusted to pH 7.2 using NaOH. The osmolarity of the internal solution is approximately 294 mOsm.

Electrolyte measurement

Urine electrolyte concentrations were measured using a FUJI DRI-CHEM Na-K-Cl slide (FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) on a FUJI DRI-CHEM NX500 analyzer. Urine samples (50 μL) were applied to the ion-selective electrode slide, which quantifies electrolytes via a potentiometric method. Measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the results were expressed in mEq/L.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse lung and kidney tissue using TRIzol reagent (#15596026; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions68. RNA concentration was measured by spectrophotometry. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using PrimeScript™ 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (#6110B; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR targeting exon 3 of Wfdc2 was performed using the following primers: Wfdc2-F, 5′-GTTGCTACTGTTCACCCCCA-3′; Wfdc2-R, 5′-CACCTGACACTGGTCCTCAC-3′. Actin was used as a loading control, amplified using the following primers: Actin-F, 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′; Actin-R, 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′. All primers used in this study were synthesized by Macrogen (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on the lungs of the WT at P0.5 (n = 4), KO at P0.5 (n = 4), KI at P0.5 (Batch 1, n = 3), amiloride-treated KO at P0.5 (n = 4), WT at P4 (n = 3), amiloride-treated WT at P4 (n = 3), KI at P0.5 (Batch 2, n = 3), and amiloride-treated KI at P4 (n = 1) mice. Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed and aligned with the mm10 2020-A reference transcriptome using Cell Ranger (version 7.2). All downstream analyses were conducted using Seurat software (version 5.1.0)69. After quality control of the cells and genes70, the data were normalized using the Seurat NormalizeData function with the LogNormalize method and a scale factor (10,000). The top 3000 highly variable genes across cells were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function based on the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) method and scaled using the ScaleData function. These variables were used for further analysis. Subsequently, dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed. PCA was performed on the identified variable genes using the “RunPCA” function. The top 20 principal components were identified, and cell clustering was conducted using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. The identified clusters were visualized using a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) of the principal components in Seurat. The expression of the known canonical markers Epcam, Col1a1, Pecam1, and Ptprc was used to identify four major cell types in mouse lung or tracheal tissues: epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, and immune cells. Immune and epithelial cells were then subsetted and re-clustered, and sub-cell types were annotated following the procedure described above. Transcriptomic datasets from two independent timepoints were integrated using Harmony71 to correct for batch effects. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis for each sample was performed using the FindMarkers function based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with min.pct and logfc thresholds set to 0.25. DEGs were selected based on |fold changes|>1.5, and adjusted p-values < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the gProfiler web tool72. Additional Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)73. Pseudotime analysis of epithelial cells was performed using Monocle374.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 11.0.2). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean unless otherwise stated. Statistical tests used for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
