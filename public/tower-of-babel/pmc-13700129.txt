Investigating differential effects of interpregnancy interval on pregnancy complications by country development status: protocol for systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Richard W. Wang, Aditya Ranganath, Hui Lin Lee, Yaduza Sivarasa, Ben D. A. Gorman, Caitlyn E. Flint, Carol A. Wang, Katherine A. Birchenall, Craig E. Pennell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterpregnancy interval (IPI) has been reported to be associated with the risk of pregnancy complications. Short IPIs have been associated with higher risks of preterm birth, small for gestational age, low birthweight, fetal growth restriction, and perinatal mortality. However, there is limited information on how these associations differ according to the developmental status of a country. This review is therefore designed to examine the impact of the developmental status of a country on the relationship between IPI and perinatal complications.MethodsStudies will be identified through a comprehensive search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, Global Health, LILACS, and African Index Medicus databases, using key terms including “interpregnancy interval”, “birth interval”, “preterm birth”, “small for gestational age”, “fetal growth restriction”, and “perinatal mortality”. Studies to be included will be screened for data reporting on IPI and relevant outcomes. The study population will consist of women with two or more singleton births. The exposure of interest is short IPI, categorised as (1) < 24 monthsmonths; ≤ 18 months; and ≤ 12 months between the birth of a child and conception of the next pregnancy. These exposure groups will be compared against the World Health Organisation recommendation of an IPI of at least 24 months. Co-primary outcomes are preterm birth, small for gestational age, fetal growth restriction, low birthweight, and perinatal mortality. The developmental status of study populations will be categorised according to the World Bank income-based country classifications for low-, middle-, and high-income countries. Studies will be included if they have an observational design and excluded if they are systematic reviews with no new data. All studies will be screened, data extracted, and bias assessed by two independent authors, with all discrepancies being resolved by consensus or the advice of a third author. If studies have a low heterogeneity (I2 < 50%), the Mantel–Haenszel method, with a fixed-effects model, will be used to calculate pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. If studies have high heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 50%) a random-effects (inverse-variance) model will be used instead.DiscussionResults from this review have the potential to guide future recommendations on optimal IPI in both high and low-resource countries. Further, they have the potential to assist in developing health strategies to target specific populations at risk from short IPI-related adverse pregnancy outcomes.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251075289.

Background

Interpregnancy interval (IPI) is defined as the period between the birth of one child and the conception of the next. A short IPI is associated with an increased risk of pregnancy complications [1]. To reduce this risk, the World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends that at least 24 months be allowed before attempting the next pregnancy to avoid adverse fetal outcomes [2]. Shorter IPIs (typically defined as less than 24 months) have been associated with increased risk of preterm birth (PTB), small for gestational age (SGA), low birth weight (LBW), fetal growth restriction (FGR), and perinatal mortality (PNM) [3].

Preterm birth (PTB) (birth before 37 completed weeks gestation) is one of the most significant challenges to perinatal health worldwide, and global estimates report 15 million PTB per year [4]. Despite best efforts to reduce preterm birth and develop prediction and management strategies [5–11], rates continue to rise globally [4–12]. PTB is the leading cause of death and disability in children under 5 years of age worldwide [11] and has substantial short-, medium-, and long-term sequelae related to physical and neurocognitive [13] development [14, 15]. Long-term complications of PTB include ischemic heart disease, heart failure, insulin resistance, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, obesity, and neurodevelopmental and social disability [16]. PTB across the gestational age spectrum is also associated with early mortality in adulthood; the earlier the PTB, the greater the early mortality [17]. There is an urgent need for interventions to reduce PTB in both high- and low-income settings. Similar to PTB, being SGA or having FGR is also associated with increased risk of adverse health outcomes across the life-course of the offspring. Short-term complications include increased perinatal morbidity and mortality [18]. Long term consequences include poor neurodevelopmental outcomes [19, 20] alongside an increased risk for non-communicable diseases in adulthood, including hypertension, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and stroke [21].

There has been debate in the literature regarding the mechanisms underlying the observed association between IPI and adverse pregnancy outcomes. It has been suggested that the impact of short IPI may be secondary to confounders such as maternal age, prior pregnancy outcome, or the socioeconomic status of a country [19, 20]. Short IPIs may compromise maternal readiness for subsequent pregnancies, increasing the risk of complications such as uterine rupture and uteroplacental bleeding disorders [21]. Suggested mechanisms for the risks associated with short IPI include cervical insufficiency, incomplete uterine remodeling postpartum, and maternal depletion syndrome [21]. The maternal depletion syndrome has been hypothesised to explain the observed reduced maternal and infant health in developing countries [26]. This hypothesis suggests that women with short IPIs do not have adequate time between pregnancies to replenish sufficient key micronutrients, such as iron and folate, deficiencies of which are associated with PTB and FGR [22]. Consequently, the adverse effects of short IPIs may be exacerbated by limited access to vitamin supplementation and widespread maternal malnutrition in low-income settings.

While prior reviews have reported clear associations between short IPIs and adverse pregnancy outcomes, the role of a country’s socioeconomic status in this relationship is unclear. Disparities in the prevalence of adverse fetal outcomes may be attributable to differences in socioeconomic status, healthcare infrastructure, and quality of primary care; factors more prevalent in lower income settings [23]. There exist systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating the short IPI effect on fetal outcomes in high-resource settings, although few have compared low-resource and high-resource countries [24]. This systematic review and meta-analysis will investigate how the relationship between IPI and pregnancy complications varies depending on a country’s economic status.

The specific aims of this study are to:Assess whether there is a difference in the effect of short IPIs on the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes (including PTB, SGA, LBW, FGR, and PNM) between high-income and low- or middle- income settings; and,To determine if there is an optimal IPI range that should be recommended within each setting to minimise the risk of adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design

A systematic review and meta-analysis.

Registration

The present protocol has been registered within the PROSPERO database (registration number CRD420251075289) and is being reported in accordance with the reporting guidance provided in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) [25] (Appendix 1).

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review will be pre-specified in the PROSPERO registration.

Participants

This review will consider all studies which include women with two or more singleton births. Studies that focus exclusively on women with previous adverse pregnancy outcomes or those who conceived through assisted reproductive technologies will be excluded. In studies that include women with 3 or more singleton births, only the second singleton birth will be included in the analysis where possible. Studies that have ambiguous birth ranks will be included in the meta-analysis and excluded for the sensitivity analysis.

Exposure

This review will include studies that assess the impact on pregnancy outcomes of short IPI. Short IPI will be defined as (1) less than 24 months, (2) less than or equal to 18 months, and (3) less than or equal to 12 months between the birth of a child and the conception of the next pregnancy. Only studies that report the interpregnancy interval as the time between the previous delivery and conception of the index pregnancy (inter-delivery interval minus gestational age of the index pregnancy) will be included in this review.

Comparator

This systematic review and meta-analysis will use an IPI of at least 24 months, the WHO definition [2], as the reference category.

To compare and assess the impact of short IPI in countries of different economic status, studies will be divided by the developmental status of the country of study participants as per the World.Bank’s country development classification at the time the study was conducted. Countries will be grouped into two categories based on gross national income (GNI) per capita: low- and middle-income countries versus high income countries [26]. For studies conducted over extended periods, country income classification will be based on the World Bank income classification at the midpoint year of data collection. Where a country has changed its income classification during the data collection window for an included study, the World Bank income classification representing the country at the midpoint of data collection will be used. For studies conducted prior to the advent of the World Bank Income classification in 1987, the income classification of the country in 1987 will be used. Subnational studies will be classified according to the national World Bank income classification of the country in which the study was conducted. Studies that cannot be classified or focus exclusively on a specific socioeconomic demographic will be excluded from the study, while studies that span classifications or were conducted prior to 1987 will be excluded in the sensitivity analysis. Where studies report results from multinational populations, data will be extracted and classified separately by county or excluded if this is not possible.

Outcomes

Co-primary outcomes are (1) PTB (defined as birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation), (2) SGA (birthweight less than the 10th percentile), (3) FGR, (4) LBW (birthweight less than 2500 g), and (5) PNM. Broadly, PNM encapsulates stillbirth and early neonatal mortality, though the precise definitions vary globally [27]. To avoid exclusion of data from LMICs all studies that included PNM as stillbirth or neonatal mortality will be included in the primary analysis. Secondary analyses will be conducted using the WHO definition (22 completed weeks gestation to 7 completed days after birth) [28] and with studies that did not define perinatal mortality. Finally, stillbirth and early neonatal mortality (0–7 days after birth) will be analysed as separate outcomes using the stillbirth definitions of greater than 20 weeks and greater than 28 weeks.

Search strategy

Observational studies, such as cohort studies, cross-sectional reviews, and case–control studies, will be eligible for inclusion. Previous meta-analyses and systematic reviews that do not include original data will be excluded from the analysis.

The electronic databases MEDLINE, EMBASE, Global Health, LILACS, and African Index Medicus will be used to conduct the search. The search will include the following search terms: interpregnancy interval OR birth interval AND preterm birth OR small for gestational age OR fetal growth restriction OR perinatal mortality. There will be no restrictions placed on the length of study follow-up time, country or year; however, we will only be looking at studies published in English, and the search will be limited to studies focused on humans. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) will be used. The detailed database search strategy is summarised in Appendix 2. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarised in Table 1. Study selection and data collection

Titles and abstracts identified through all sources will be exported into Rayyan software [29] and duplicates removed. Two authors will independently screen article titles and abstracts against the pre-specified inclusion/exclusion criteria. In the case of disagreement, authors will review the papers together; in the case of remaining disagreement, the article will be discussed with a third reviewer to resolve the situation. Included studies will then be reviewed in full and data extraction performed by two independent authors, and any discrepancies will be reviewed by a third and fourth author if required. All data will be extracted using a standardised electronic data extraction sheet. For cases with prior adverse events or inconsistent outcome reporting, two independent reviewers will assess for inclusion and where data cannot be formally analysed will be described. Once extracted, after reviewer agreement, data will be transferred into Review Manager version 5.3 data-analysis software [30].

Study characteristics will be examined to identify potential overlap between study populations. Where multiple publications use data from the same cohort with overlapping data-collection windows, the study with the most comprehensive dataset for each outcome will be prioritised for data extraction. Where different outcomes are reported from the same cohort, relevant data will be extracted without double counting.

In cases of heterogeneity in IPI categorisation across studies, reported intervals bands (e.g. < 6, 6–11, 12–17, 18–23, 24–59) will be pooled into the prespecified exposure (≤ 12, ≤ 18, and < 24) and comparator (≥ 24) groups where possible. Studies that are unable to be harmonised to our predefined groups will be excluded from the review. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted with the exclusion of studies with broad or non-standard interval groupings to assess the impact of pooling heterogeneous exposure intervals into the prespecified exposure groups.

The analytical hierarchy of this study will prioritise the extraction of the frequency of the outcomes in relation to our prespecified exposures, followed by unadjusted odds ratios. Where these are unavailable, adjusted odds ratios will be extracted provided they have included at least maternal age as a confounder. Unadjusted estimates are prioritised to maximise comparability across studies, given that adjusted estimates vary in selection of confounders used. However, given the importance of confounding in this literature, adjusted estimates will also be analysed in a separate subgroup analysis to assess consistency of findings. A subgroup analysis will be conducted to examine adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios independently.

Data analysis

A meta-analysis will be performed by pooling studies using Review Manager software [30]. Results will be reported using odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI), with IPI greater than or equal to 24 months as the reference group. Study heterogeneity will be assessed visually using forest plots. Further consideration of statistical heterogeneity will be quantified by calculating the I2 statistic. The I2 statistic quantifies variance among study results due to differences between study design rather than chance. If I2 is less than 50%, it indicates that studies are comparable with negligible to moderate heterogeneity [31] where a fixed-effects (Mantel–Haenszel) model is appropriate. An I2 of greater than or equal to 50% indicates potential substantial heterogeneity and a random-effects model (inverse-variance) will be used for subsequent analysis to account for variance within and between studies For all analyses with an I2 less than 50% a fixed-effects (Mantel–Haenszel) model will be used. A fixed-effects model will also be used for all outcomes with less than five studies [31]. Additionally, a subgroup analysis will be conducted using the same methodology to examine unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios independently. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted for each perinatal outcome where there are sufficient studies by removing low and average quality studies to examine their effect on the estimates.

For outcomes where data is available from ten or more publications, funnel plots and Egger’s regression test (for funnel plot asymmetry) will be performed to identify publication bias. Funnel plots will be plotted with effect sizes on the x-axis and standard errors on the y-axis to allow the visual assessment of funnel plot asymmetry. High-income and low-to-middle-income countries will be assessed separately.

Two independent authors will assess the risk of bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa Score (NOS) for cohort and case–control studies [32] and a modified version of the cohort NOS for cross-sectional studies (Appendix 3). This modification ensures that the cross-sectional NOS is applicable to the current study (i.e. scoring for the inclusion of the confounding factor of maternal age). The NOS seeks to address bias across three domains: exposure and comparator group selection, group comparability, and assessment of the outcome variable. Any variation in judgment between the authors of this study will be discussed and resolved. The NOS is scored out of nine points; studies scoring ≥ 8 will be considered high quality. The NOS criteria are specific to a study’s methodology, and the appropriate NOS version will be utilised depending on the study design, being cross-sectional, cohort, or case–control. No studies will be excluded from the primary analyses based on these scores. Sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess the robustness of the results after excluding studies with a score of seven or less.

Consistency and confidence of cumulative evidence in the primary outcome will be assessed independently by two authors using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach [33]. Evidence will be assessed for risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias. Overall grade will be weighted based on study sample size.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the impact of IPI on perinatal outcomes and how these effects may vary according to the developmental status of a country or setting. Specifically, we will generate ORs and 95% CI for four pregnancy outcomes by IPI with countries grouped by economic status. This data will allow us to determine the minimum IPI that does not increase the odds of each adverse pregnancy outcome for high-income and low- and middle-income countries which could be used to revise clinical guidelines for best practice.

Findings from this review may also inform national and international guidelines relating to optimal birth spacing. Evidence on how the association between short IPI and adverse outcomes varies by country income status may also help inform family planning and contraceptive access programs, and antenatal counselling policies, especially in low- and middle-income countries.

The study has several strengths: first, the meta-analyses will include multiple publications for each obstetric outcome, many of which have large sample sizes. This is likely to result in relatively narrow confidence intervals for each outcome allowing the direct comparison of IPI durations within country developmental status groups. Further, the large number of studies included are likely to be populations from many countries increasing the generalisability of the results. However, there are limitations to our research paradigm, as it does not allow the examination of confounders or mechanisms as individual patient data is not available for the studies in this meta-analysis. Therefore, we will not be able to assess the biological and social mechanisms that mediate the increased risk of PTB, LBW and SGA in women with short IPI. Additionally, the inclusion of subnational studies may not reflect the national socioeconomic status. However, this is necessary due to the likely paucity of national datasets that link birth records with clinical information such as gestational age and perinatal outcomes. This limitation is mitigated by the exclusion of studies that focus on a specific socioeconomic demographic. Finally, the prioritisation of frequency data and unadjusted odds ratios over adjusted odds ratios maximises comparability between studies but limits the impact of confounding factors. This limitation is mitigated by the subgroup analysis that will examine adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios independently.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the impact of IPI on pregnancy outcomes and how these effects vary according to the developmental status of a country. Determining the optimal IPI for countries based on economic status will provide information to support the development of public health strategies to reduce the rate of PTB, SGA, FGR, LBW and perinatal mortality. By synthesising evidence from multiple studies, this review seeks to inform health authorities in creating country-specific guidelines on optimal IPI. Ultimately, this may help to reduce adverse outcomes associated with suboptimal IPI. We anticipate that the findings will benefit not only policymakers and healthcare providers but also women, families, and the broader community, providing additional information to support education and family planning for women in countries of different economic status.
