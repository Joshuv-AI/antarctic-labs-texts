Parameter response mapping does not enhance the correlations of conventional CT quantitative metrics with lung function and subjective symptoms in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Chun Fang, Shuyang Fang, Yin Wang, Jiacheng Guan, Dandan Xiao, Hua Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParameter response mapping (PRM) has been employed in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) assessments, providing a more accurate characterization of emphysema and air trapping. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the correlations of PRM and conventional computed tomography (CT) quantitative metrics with lung function and subjective symptoms in COPD patients.MethodsA total of 94 COPD patients were prospectively enrolled who underwent inspiratory and expiratory dual-phase CT scans and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) at The Second People’s Hospital of Quzhou from January 2023 to June 2024. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the most common chronic airway disease and ranks as the third leading cause of death in industrialized nations (1). The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that the prevalence of COPD will continue to rise in the future (2). Pulmonary function test (PFT) serves as the primary tool for diagnosing COPD (3), typically characterized by reductions in forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), FEV1/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) ratio, and FEV1 percent of the predicted value (FEV1%Pred). FEV1 represents a critical metric for the diagnosis and classification of COPD, as its decline reflects diminished lung tissue elasticity and increased airway resistance (4). However, PFT results depend on patient cooperation, limiting feasibility in elderly patients and individuals with poor health (5). Consequently, COPD management guidelines highlight the need for complementary assessment methods (6).

Computed tomography (CT), with its high spatial resolution and whole-lung imaging capability, plays a crucial diagnostic role in COPD (7,8). The two primary pathological changes associated with COPD are emphysema and small airway disease (9,10). Emphysema results in a decrease in lung tissue CT attenuation values, allowing CT quantitative analysis to objectively assess emphysema extent. Regions having CT values ≤–950 Hounsfield units (HU) on inspiratory CT are typically classified as emphysema (11). CT-quantified emphysema severity correlates closely with lung function impairment, clinical symptoms, quality of life, and prognosis (12,13). Conversely, evaluating small airway disease directly remains challenging due to CT resolution limitations (4). Instead, air trapping, considered an early sign of emphysema (14), serves as a surrogate marker. This phenomenon results from small airway damage causing wall thickening and increased resistance. Low attenuation areas on expiratory CT images are considered air trapping regions (11), assessed by the percentage of low attenuation area ≤–856 HU on expiratory CT (LAA%-856EX), and show strong correlation with specific PFT parameters (15,16).

Recently, parameter response mapping (PRM) has emerged for COPD assessments (17,18). This technique provides a more accurate reflection of emphysema and air trapping in COPD patients. Through biphasic CT scans and image registration, all lung voxels are classified into three categories: Areas of attenuation values ≤−950 HU on inspiratory CT are classified as emphysema (PRMEMPH); areas of attenuation values >−950 HU on inspiratory CT and ≤−856 HU on expiratory CT are classified as functional small airway disease-related air trapping (PRMfSAD); and areas of attenuation values >−950 HU on inspiratory CT and >−856 HU on expiratory CT are classified as normal lung parenchyma (PRMNORM). Despite its potential, the relationship between PRM parameters and PFT metrics remains incompletely characterized, and correlations with patients’ subjective symptoms have not been investigated.

A cross-sectional design was used in this study. By analyzing the correlation between quantitative parameters of PRM and conventional CT with lung function and patients’ subjective symptoms, this study aimed to evaluate and compare the correlations of PRM and conventional CT quantitative metrics with lung function and subjective symptoms in COPD patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1597/rc).

Methods

Patients

Patients diagnosed with COPD at The Second People’s Hospital of Quzhou from January 2023 to June 2024, based on the diagnostic criteria outlined in the COPD management guidelines (6), were enrolled in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients unable to complete dual-phase CT scans; those with poor-quality CT images preventing accurate PRM analysis; individuals unable to cooperate with PFT; those with a time interval >3 days between CT and PFT; and patients showing other significant pulmonary diseases on CT (lung tumors, tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, atelectasis, extensive pulmonary infections, interstitial fibrosis, pneumoconiosis, etc.). Of 126 initially identified patients, 32 were excluded, yielding 94 eligible cases. The study population comprised 75 males and 19 females with a mean age of 65.3±11.1 years (range, 44–91 years). According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) staging, 16 patients were classified as stage 1, 29 as stage 2, 28 as stage 3, and 21 as stage 4. A total of 87 patients had received treatment according to guidelines (6), including bronchodilators (87/87, 100%) and inhaled corticosteroids (59/87, 67.8%). Seven patients who were newly diagnosed had not yet started pharmacotherapy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second People’s Hospital of Quzhou (No. 2022-42). Written informed consent was provided by all participants.

CT scanning protocol

CT scans were conducted using a Siemens 64-slice CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition Edge, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Patients received training on inspiration and expiration prior to the scans. Scans were performed in the supine position with a tube voltage of 120 kV, automatic tube current, and a pitch of 1.0. Images were acquired at breath-hold after maximum inspiration and after natural expiration. Images were reconstructed at 1.0 mm slice thickness with 0.8 mm intervals.

CT image processing and analysis

Post-scanning images were transferred to the COPD CT automatic assessment software (Yizhiyuan Health Technology, Hangzhou, China) for analysis. The software performed automatic lung segmentation, followed by deformable image registration between inspiratory and expiratory scans using a mutual information-based algorithm with a B-spline transformation model. Cases with significant misregistration artifacts, identified visually as major anatomical mismatches (>5 mm displacement) of the diaphragm or fissures on the fused PRM overlay, were excluded during initial screening (n=7). After registration, a voxel-based PRM of the whole lung was produced, where attenuation values ≤–950 HU on inspiration CT were classified as emphysematous regions (PRMEMPH), attenuation values >–950 HU on inspiratory CT and ≤–856 HU on expiratory CT as functional small airway disease-related air trapping (PRMfSAD), and attenuation values >–950 HU on inspiratory CT and >–856 HU on expiratory CT as normal lung parenchyma (PRMNORM). Additionally, conventional CT quantitative parameters on inspiratory CT (mean lung density on inspiratory CT, MLDIN; 15th percentile of lung attenuation value on inspiratory CT, P15IN; percentage of low attenuation area ≤–950 HU on inspiratory CT, LAA%-950IN) and on expiratory CT (mean lung density on expiratory CT, MLDEX; 15th percentile of lung attenuation value on expiratory CT, P15EX; LAA%-856EX) were measured.

PFT

PFT was performed using a JAEGER pulmonary function device (MasterScreen Diffusion, CareFusion, Hoechberg, Germany) by physicians with over five years of experience. Quantitative parameters, FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC, and FEV1%Pred, were recorded.

Clinical assessment

All COPD patients were evaluated by attending physicians according to GOLD grading standards based on FEV1%Pred following bronchodilator use, classifying them into four severity levels. Subjective symptom assessment was conducted using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC) and the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the software SPSS 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were recorded as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons of multiple groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis H test were utilized based on homogeneity of variance. The correlation between quantitative parameters was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis, and comparisons of correlation coefficients were conducted with z-tests. P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

PRM and conventional CT quantitative parameters in different GOLD stages

Based on FEV1%Pred following bronchodilator use, among the 94 COPD patients, 16 patients were classified as GOLD stage 1 (FEV1%Pred ≥80%), 29 as GOLD stage 2 (50%≤ FEV1%Pred <80%), 28 as GOLD stage 3 (30%≤ FEV1%Pred <50%), and 21 as GOLD stage 4 (FEV1%Pred <30%). The quantitative parameters of PRM (PRMEMPH, PRMfSAD, and PRMNORM) and conventional CT (MLDIN, P15IN, LAA%-950IN, MLDEX, P15EX, and LAA%-856EX) showed significant variation across GOLD stages (Table 1). Generally, PRMEMPH and PRMfSAD tended to increase with advancing GOLD stage, PRMNORM exhibited a decreasing trend (Figure 1). MLDIN, P15IN, MLDEX, and P15EX decreased with increasing GOLD stage, whereas LAA%-950IN and LAA%-856EX increased with increasing GOLD stage.

Correlation of PFT with PRM and conventional CT quantitative parameters

Correlation analysis between PFT outcomes and both PRM and conventional CT quantitative parameters revealed robust relationships (Table 2). High correlation coefficients were observed between FEV1 and PRMNORM (r=0.839) and between FEV1 and LAA%-856EX (r=–0.840), with no significant difference between these two r values (z=–0.023, P=0.982). Similar results were observed for FEV1%Pred, which correlated strongly with both PRMNORM (r=0.885) and LAA%-856EX (r=–0.887), and no significant difference was found between the two sets of r values (z=–0.063, P=0.950). These results suggest similar strengths of association between the top-performing PRM and conventional CT metrics with pulmonary function.

Correlation of CAT scores with PRM and conventional CT quantitative parameters

CAT scores demonstrated strong correlations with quantitative parameters from both PRM and conventional CT. Significant correlations were observed with PRMEMPH, PRMfSAD, PRMNORM, MLDIN, P15IN, LAA%-950IN, MLDEX, P15EX, and LAA%-856EX. Notably, CAT scores correlated particularly strongly with PRMNORM (r=–0.814) and LAA%-856EX (r=0.812) (Table 2), with no significant difference between these two r values (z=0.040, P=0.968). Similarly, the correlation between CAT scores and PRMNORM also showed near symmetry with the correlation between CAT scores and LAA%-856EX in the scatter plots (Figure 2).

Discussion

PRM using dual-phase CT scans has enabled more accurate delineation of air trapping (17,18). It identifies regions with CT attenuation values >–950 HU on inspiratory scans and ≤–856 HU on expiratory scans, excluding emphysema areas, thereby providing a precise assessment of small airway disease. Micro-CT scans of lung specimens from lung transplant patients have further confirmed that non-emphysematous air trapping is associated with terminal bronchioles and is independent of the severity of emphysema (14). As two primary pathological changes of COPD, emphysema and small airway disease are closely related to lung function and patients’ subjective symptoms.

In our study, PRMEMPH and PRMfSAD in COPD patients progressively increased with higher GOLD stages, whereas PRMNORM decreased. Additionally, conventional CT quantitative parameters such as MLDIN, P15IN, MLDEX, and P15EX decreased with advancing GOLD stages, whereas LAA%-950IN and LAA%-856EX increased. Both PRM and conventional CT quantitative parameters effectively reflected the severity of COPD, with PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX demonstrating superior performance. These findings align with established evidence that GOLD grading based on post-bronchodilator FEV1%Pred correlates with disease severity (6,16,19), demonstrating that higher GOLD stages correspond to lower FEV1 levels and greater areas of emphysema and air trapping on CT images (18,20).

Our study demonstrates that in a cross-sectional cohort of stable COPD patients, PRM-derived and conventional CT quantitative metrics show similarly strong global correlations with pulmonary function and symptom burden. Particularly, PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX were highly correlated with FEV1 and FEV1%Pred, with correlation coefficients of 0.839 and 0.885 for PRMNORM, and –0.840 and –0.887 for LAA%-856EX, respectively. PFT is considered the gold standard for diagnosing COPD; it generally presents as reduced FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and FEV1%Pred. FEV1 serves as the primary diagnostic and classification metric for COPD, reflecting decreased lung tissue elasticity and increased airway resistance. However, PFT results can vary greatly depending on patient cooperation, and many elderly or health-compromised individuals may find it difficult to complete PFT. Previous studies have also shown the correlation between CT quantitative parameters and pulmonary function in patients with COPD (21-23). The strong correlations of PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX with FEV1 and FEV1%Pred in our study suggest their potential as alternative assessment methods of PFT for COPD patients. Our study indicates no significant difference in the correlation between PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX with FEV1 and FEV1%Pred, suggesting that, for assessing these global clinical measures, PRM did not provide stronger associations than the established conventional parameter in this dataset.

Patients’ subjective symptoms clearly show individual experiences and quality of life, making them essential for evaluating COPD (24). In this study, we employed mMRC and CAT for assessing patients’ subjective symptoms. It is noted that symptom assessment in COPD can be influenced by avoidant behavior (25), where patients may unconsciously reduce physical activity to minimize symptom exposure. This behavioral adaptation could potentially affect the accuracy of subjective symptom scores such as mMRC and CAT, but mMRC and CAT remain predominant scales for assessing patients’ subjective symptoms. Correlation analyses showed that PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX had the stronger correlations with mMRC and CAT scores, with correlation coefficients of –0.790 and –0.814 for PRMNORM, and 0.784 and 0.812 for LAA%-856EX, respectively. Both PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX exhibited good correlation with mMRC and CAT scores, and the difference between both was not significant. PRMNORM reflects the sum of PRMEMPH and PRMfSAD. Our results indicated that both PRMEMPH and PRMfSAD serve as indicators of patients’ subjective symptoms, but PRMNORM provides a better overall assessment of these symptoms. However, LAA%-856EX, a conventional CT parameter, also displayed a strong correlation with mMRC and CAT scores, effectively indicating subjective symptoms. PRM parameters are effective in distinguishing between emphysema and air trapping and improving our understanding of COPD phenotypes (17,18,26). PRM offers unique clinical value through its ability to visually distinguish between emphysema and functional small airway disease. This spatial characterization allows for better visualization of pathological changes and localization of abnormal lung regions, which could be valuable for phenotyping COPD patients and guiding personalized treatment strategies. The spatial distribution information provided by PRM may have particular clinical significance in identifying dominant disease phenotypes and assessing regional disease progression. However, the present cross-sectional analysis focused on global correlations suggests that PRM’s ability to differentiate emphysema and air trapping did not translate to a stronger association with patient-reported symptoms compared to LAA%-856EX. This suggests that accurate differentiation of emphysema and air trapping may not augment the evaluation of global symptom burden as captured by mMRC and CAT. The scatter plots comparing PRMNORM and LAA%-856EX demonstrated their close resemblance, indicating that LAA%-856EX values closely represent the total of emphysema and air trapping, suggesting that areas of air trapping defined by CT values ≤–856 HU also encompass a substantial proportion of emphysematous changes.

Although our study demonstrates that PRM did not provide stronger correlations than conventional CT metrics for assessing lung function and symptom burden in this cohort, recent work suggests that PRM-derived metrics, particularly PRMfSAD, may offer utility in distinguishing mixed phenotypes such as asthma-COPD overlap, where conventional CT features are less discriminative (27). This highlights the potential relevance of PRM in broader, more heterogeneous clinical contexts beyond stable COPD. Furthermore, foundational studies have established PRM’s value in predicting future lung function decline and its correlation with terminal bronchiole pathology, underscoring its potential longitudinal and mechanistic utility beyond cross-sectional correlation (16-18).

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, the relatively modest sample size (n=94) and single-center design limits the statistical power for detecting small but potentially meaningful differences between PRM and conventional CT metrics and precludes subgroup analysis. Those variations of subgroups may impact the results. Future studies should aim for larger sample sizes to validate these findings. Second, the study did not exclude patients accompanied by other mild pulmonary diseases, which may have influenced the accuracy and objectivity of PFT metrics and PRM or conventional CT quantitative outcomes, and may have impacted the results. Third, our study did not provide sufficient assessment of small airway function. PFT did not include impulse oscillometry, and lacked parameters such as forced expiratory flow at 50% of FVC, forced expiratory flow at 75% of FVC, and maximum mid-expiratory flow. Direct measurements of small airway walls were also not performed, and correlation analyses between these indices and PRM were not conducted. Finally, this cross-sectional analysis did not address PRM’s potential longitudinal or prognostic value which may be meaningful for assessment of COPD, and did not exploit its potential strengths in evaluating regional disease distribution or heterogeneity, which may be relevant for phenotyping.

Conclusions

PRM metrics demonstrated strong correlations with pulmonary function and patients’ subjective symptoms, similar to those of conventional CT quantitative parameters. Although PRM provides voxel-level classification of lung pathology, our findings suggest that its global metrics did not show stronger associations with lung function or symptom burden than the conventional parameter LAA%-856EX in stable COPD.
