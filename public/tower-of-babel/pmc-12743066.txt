Self-Reflective Chest X-Ray Report Generation with Clinical-Aware Detection and Multilevel Readability
Authors: Juhyuk Han, Minjae Kim, Yeonwoo Kim, Won Hee Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical documentation demands necessitate automated solutions balancing clinical precision with patient comprehension. This study aims to develop and validate a unified framework that maintains diagnostic accuracy while dynamically adapting medical report complexity to diverse literacy levels, and to establish comprehensive evaluation methodologies for patient-centered medical documentation. We developed a unified framework integrating three innovations: a hybrid detection method combining CheXFusion and Eigen-CAM for clinical finding detection and anatomical localization; an advanced LLaVA-based pipeline synthesizing clinical predictions with anatomical data for contextually rich medical reports; and a self-reflective large language model system dynamically adapting report complexity across reading levels (6th, 11th, and 18th-grade) while preserving clinical integrity. Our methodology introduces novel evaluation using the Mistral-small model assessing report quality through consistency, coverage, and fluency metrics. Validation on MIMIC-CXR and IU X-Ray datasets demonstrated substantial improvements: 19.78% enhancement in classification accuracy (AUROC), 17.29% improvement in mean average precision, 56.88% increase in patient comprehension scores, and 5.26% gain in diagnostic precision. The framework successfully addresses maintaining clinical rigor while enhancing patient accessibility, reducing documentation burden on healthcare providers and improving patient engagement through comprehensible reporting. This work establishes new standards for automated medical documentation that effectively reconcile clinical precision with patient comprehension in healthcare communication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10916-025-02326-5.Keywords: Medical report generation, Large language models, Chest X-Ray, Self-reflection, Readability

Introduction

Automated medical report generation (MRG) from clinical images represents a critical advancement in addressing documentation burdens within modern healthcare systems [1]. Recent developments in artificial intelligence have demonstrated substantial progress in enhancing the contextual relevance and accuracy of generated medical narratives [2, 3]. However, existing approaches remain predominantly optimized for clinical professionals, often compromising readability and patient comprehension, which are essential for effective shared decision-making [4].

Current MRG systems exhibit significant limitations in adapting content complexity to varying levels of patient health literacy, thereby hindering effective communication and potentially compromising treatment adherence [5]. Furthermore, accurate anatomical localization, crucial for disease monitoring and treatment planning, remains insufficiently addressed in existing frameworks [6–8]. The evaluation metrics commonly used, including BLEU [9] and ROUGE [10], focus primarily on surface-level textual similarities while failing to capture clinical correctness, spatial reasoning capabilities, or audience-appropriate language adaptation.

Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) have significantly improved image-text alignment and contextual report generation capabilities. Notable frameworks include hierarchical interaction model [11] and optimized alignment methodologies [12], which have enhanced radiology report quality. Systems such as MedEPT [13] and SERPENT-VLM [14] leverage pre-trained multimodal architectures to improve coherence and clinical utility, while retrieval-augmented approaches like CXR-RePaiR-Gen [15] integrate LLMs for enhanced report conciseness and accuracy. State-of-the-art methods have achieved clinical efficacy scores reaching F1-scores of approximately 0.397, establishing performance baselines for comparative evaluation. Despite these advances, clinical fidelity and interpretability, particularly regarding anatomical specificity, require further enhancement.

Recent studies have demonstrated varying approaches to medical report generation challenges. CvT2DistilGPT2 [16] employs vision transformers with distilled language models, achieving competitive performance but lacking anatomical localization capabilities. FMVP [17] focuses on multi-view feature fusion, while RECAP [18] utilizes dynamic disease progression reasoning based on graph construction. HERGen [19] incorporates temporal embedding into the group causal transformer, CAMANet [20] leverages CAM-guided attention to improve cross-modal alignment, and MPO [21] employs multi-objective preference optimization for preference-guided report generation. RaDialog [22] integrates large vision-language models to provide dialog-driven assistance. However, these methods uniformly focus on optimizing clinical accuracy metrics without addressing patient comprehension or readability adaptation, establishing a critical gap in patient-centered healthcare communication.

Concurrent research has emphasized patient-centered report generation approaches. Yan et al. introduced dual training strategies to balance medical accuracy with interpretability [23], while Liu et al. employed CLIP and ChatGPT for generating symptom-aligned patient explanations [24]. Additional work, including Park et al.‘s MRI summarization [25] and ChatCAD+ [26], has focused on improving patient engagement and communication. However, existing systems frequently fail to adapt report content to diverse patient literacy levels, resulting in either overly technical explanations or oversimplified reports that omit critical clinical information. Quantitative evaluation of patient comprehension has been largely absent from existing literature, with no prior work reporting systematic assessment of readability adaptation across multiple literacy levels while maintaining clinical accuracy.

Evaluation methodologies have similarly evolved, with CheXbert [27] and RadGraph [28] providing clinically grounded assessments of findings and semantic relationships, and RadCliQ [29, 30] offering hybrid approaches combining lexical and clinical evaluation. Nevertheless, these metrics rarely assess patient accessibility, health literacy compatibility, or language adaptability, which are critical factors for comprehensive healthcare communication.

To address these limitations, this study pursues both theoretical and operational objectives that advance patient-centered medical documentation. Theoretically, we (1) establish a framework for dual-objective optimization that simultaneously achieves clinical diagnostic accuracy and patient comprehension, (2) develop principles for adaptive complexity control that adjust report readability across literacy levels while maintaining diagnostic accuracy and clinical completeness, and (3) advance evaluation methodology to incorporate patient-centered dimensions beyond traditional clinical-only metrics. Operationally, we aim to (1) achieve state-of-the-art clinical performance on benchmark datasets while generating reports at three distinct literacy levels (6th, 11th, 18th-grade) with measurable patient comprehension improvements, (2) implement robust hybrid detection architecture with superior anatomical localization, (3) demonstrate self-reflective convergence within reasonable iterations (≤ 10 cycles), and (4) establish comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation frameworks using advanced language models.

Here, we introduce a novel unified framework that makes three key contributions to automated medical documentation. First, we introduce a self-reflective large language model mechanism specifically designed for medical report generation, which dynamically adapts report complexity across multiple literacy levels (6th, 11th, 18th-grade) while preserving clinical diagnostic integrity—a capability lacking in existing medical AI systems. This self-reflective mechanism represents a paradigm shift from static report generation to intelligent, patient-aware documentation that maintains clinical rigor while enhancing accessibility. Second, we develop a novel hybrid detection architecture that uniquely integrates CheXFusion classification with Eigen-CAM localization to achieve superior diagnostic performance and anatomical interpretability simultaneously, addressing fundamental limitations of existing single-modality approaches in medical imaging analysis. Third, we establish the first comprehensive evaluation framework utilizing advanced language models (Mistral-small) to assess patient-centered report quality through multi-dimensional metrics encompassing consistency, coverage, and fluency, moving beyond traditional clinical-only evaluation approaches.

We test three research hypotheses that validate these independent contributions: (1) our novel self-reflective LLM framework can successfully balance clinical accuracy with adaptive readability across varying literacy levels while maintaining diagnostic integrity; (2) our innovative CheXFusion-Eigen-CAM integration will significantly enhance both diagnostic performance and anatomical interpretability compared to existing architectures; and (3) our comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation methodology will provide superior assessment of patient-centered report quality compared to traditional clinical evaluation approaches. These hypotheses guide our experimental design and validation framework, enabling systematic investigation of fundamental innovations in automated medical report generation that bridges clinical precision with patient accessibility through novel technological contributions.

Methods

Figure 1 presents the architecture of our proposed self-reflective MRG system, comprising three key components that work synergistically to achieve our dual objectives of clinical precision and patient accessibility: (1) clinical finding detection and localization (Fig. 1(a)), integrating CheXFusion-based chest X-Ray (CXR) classification with Eigen-CAM explainable AI (xAI) techniques to accurately identify and localize clinically relevant findings within the images—this hybrid approach was specifically selected to provide stable, precise anatomical localizations that outperform traditional Grad-CAM methods; (2) medical report generation (Fig. 1(b)), leveraging a specialized LLaVA model to generate comprehensive medical reports by synthesizing clinical predictions and anatomical data—chosen for its superior ability to integrate visual and textual information in medical contexts; and (3) self-reflective LLMs (Fig. 1(c)), which dynamically adjust the readability level of the generated reports to balance clinical precision with patient comprehension across diverse educational backgrounds—this iterative refinement process represents our novel contribution to patient-centered medical communication that existing single-pass methods cannot achieve chosen for its superior ability to integrate visual and textual information in medical contexts. Each component builds upon the previous stage’s output, creating an integrated pipeline that transforms raw medical imagery into clinically accurate, patient-appropriate reports through our comprehensive end-to-end approach.

Overview of the patient-centered chest X-Ray report generation framework

Datasets

We utilized two publicly available datasets to develop and evaluate our framework. The MIMIC-CXR dataset [31] includes 377,110 radiographs from 227,835 imaging studies involving 65,379 patients, each paired with a corresponding free-text radiology report. We followed the official dataset split comprising 368,960 training images, 2,991 validation images, and 5,159 test images, ensuring consistent comparison with existing methods. For external validation, we employed the Indiana University Chest X-Ray Collection (IU X-Ray) dataset [32], comprising 7,470 radiographs and 3,955 reports, to assess the generalizability of our model in a distinct clinical setting. After filtering for samples with valid clinical findings and complete report annotations, 3,858 radiographs from the MIMIC-CXR test set and 6,469 radiographs from the IU X-Ray dataset were ultimately included in the evaluation, providing a robust evaluation foundation of 10,327 total samples.

Chest X-Ray Image Classification

CXR image classification presents unique challenges due to the inherent multi-label nature, where multiple conditions such as atelectasis and pneumonia frequently coexist [33]. The datasets exhibit significant class imbalance, dominated by common conditions with fewer examples of clinically important rare conditions (Supplementary Figure S1). Additionally, subtle anatomical variations and localized intensity patterns, such as minor differences in tissue density and organ boundaries, are crucial for accurate diagnosis.

To address these challenges, we employed targeted solutions for class imbalance and fine-grained feature detection. Each image was annotated with 14 cardiopulmonary findings (Supplementary Table S1) [31]. Our model outputs a probability vector indicating the likelihood of each finding’s presence, and optimal class-specific thresholds were determined from validation set accuracy to convert probabilities into binary predictions. This tailored thresholding approach improved detection accuracy across all findings, particularly benefiting rare conditions.

Anatomical Localization Through xAI

To enhance interpretability, we integrated xAI methods to visually localize anatomical regions relevant to model predictions. Specifically, we utilized two Class Activation Mapping (CAM) techniques. Grad-CAM [34] identifies discriminative regions using gradient information from the model’s final convolutional layer, while Eigen-CAM [35] employs eigen decomposition to detect subtler anatomical patterns. These complementary techniques provided robust anatomical context and improved clinical interpretability. We generated anatomical localization heatmaps highlighting critical regions. To emphasize clinically relevant areas, heatmaps were refined by applying a threshold at 75% of the maximum pixel intensity [36]. A bounding box was derived around the largest contiguous area, effectively eliminating extraneous details and enhancing the precision and interpretability of findings. The selection of this threshold was determined through systematic evaluation of alternative values considering the trade-off between bounding box coverage and localization specificity. Using a 50% threshold resulted in excessively large regions (mean bounding box area covering 45.4% of image), making it difficult to accurately identify specific anatomical locations and reducing clinical utility for precise pathology localization. Conversely, using a maximum-value threshold (100%) extracted only highly localized regions (mean area 3.7% of image), which frequently failed to capture the full extent of lesions, particularly for diffuse pathologies such as edema or consolidation. Based on empirical evaluation across diverse pathological presentations in the MIMIC-CXR validation set, we determined that a 75% threshold (mean area 36.7% of image) provides optimal balance between adequate lesion coverage and anatomical localization precision, ensuring clinical relevance while maintaining interpretability.

Vision-language Integration with Anatomical Context

Our framework integrates advanced VLMs tailored specifically for clinical contexts. Extending the RaDialog LLaVA framework [22], we adapted components critical for clinical precision, interpretability, and usability. Key components include BioViL-T [37] for specialized CXR image encoding, Vicuna-7B [38] as the fine-tuned language model for generating clinically accurate narratives, and BERT [39] for nuanced multimodal alignment between visual and textual data.

The system undergoes fine-tuning with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [40] using the RaDialog instruction dataset [41], enhancing its ability to handle diverse clinical tasks effectively. Post-fine-tuning, anatomical data relevant to clinical findings is extracted via targeted prompts containing bounding boxes of identified regions. Extracted anatomical terms are validated against a clinically vetted anatomical taxonomy derived from the MIMIC-CXR and Chest ImaGenome projects [42], ensuring the clinical relevance of the generated anatomical descriptions (see Supplementary Table S2). The refined anatomical information, coupled with detected findings, is then integrated into an initial clinically-oriented report, combining visual features, validated anatomical context, and clinical reasoning.

Adaptive Report Generation Through Self-reflection

To dynamically balance readability and clinical accuracy, our framework incorporates a self-reflective iterative refinement process using the LLaMA-3.1-8B [43]. This mechanism involves sequentially refining the initial clinical report through a feedback loop comprising four modules: a converter for linguistic simplification, evaluator for assessing readability and accuracy, self-reflection module for guiding refinements, and a memory module for maintaining iterative consistency. Each iteration adapts the report to specific readability levels (6th, 11th or 18th-grade) guided by feedback on readability and clinical accuracy (details in Supplementary Table S1). Through multiple refinement cycles, the final report achieves optimal readability tailored to patient comprehension, without compromising original clinical accuracy.

Evaluation Metrics

Chest X-Ray Image Classification Performance

To evaluate the performance of our chest X-Ray classification models, we employed two complementary metrics: the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and the mean Average Precision (mAP). Both are well-suited for multi-label classification tasks characterized by class imbalance and co-occurring conditions. AUROC measures the model’s discriminative ability across all classification thresholds, offering a threshold-independent assessment that is robust to skewed class distributions [44]. In contrast, mAP emphasizes the precision-recall tradeoff across thresholds, providing a more granular evaluation of the model’s capacity to detect both common and rare thoracic pathologies. These offer a comprehensive evaluation framework that supports reliable multi-label classification, forming a solid foundation for downstream clinical report generation.

Anatomical Localization Performance

To quantify the effectiveness of CAM techniques in identifying abnormal regions, we used two spatial metrics: Intersection over Union (IoU) and the Dice coefficient. Anatomical localization performance was evaluated using MS-CXR [45] expert annotations as ground truth, calculating IoU and Dice coefficients between predicted and reference bounding boxes. These metrics evaluate how accurately Grad-CAM and Eigen-CAM localize pathological findings by comparing predicted regions of interest against expert-annotated ground truth regions, serving as a direct measure of the model’s ability to highlight clinically relevant areas in CXR images. The IoU provides a strict measure of spatial precision by penalizing both under- and over-estimation of abnormal regions. The Dice coefficient complements this by capturing segmentation balance, which is especially important for irregularly shaped pathologies. These offer a robust evaluation of the model’s localization performance, supporting the reliability of clinical interpretation and report generation.

Clinical Efficacy Evaluation for Clinical Finding Detection

To quantitatively assess the clinical efficacy of our generated medical reports, we implemented an automated evaluation pipeline using the CheXbert labeler [27]. Clinical report accuracy was assessed using radiologist-written reports from MIMIC-CXR and IU X-Ray datasets as references, with CheXbert labeler extracting clinical findings for comparison. This approach moves beyond surface-level textual similarity metrics to directly evaluate the capture of clinically significant findings, providing a more meaningful measure of report quality. CheXbert leverages a BERT-base architecture augmented with 14 specialized classification heads (details in Supplementary Table S1), each dedicated to identifying a specific clinical finding. The model examines both the reference reports and our generated reports, extracting binary labels (presence/absence) for each target condition through multi-label classification. We applied CheXbert evaluation to our filtered test sets comprising 3,858 samples from the MIMIC-CXR official test split and 6,469 samples from the IU X-Ray dataset. These samples were filtered to include only those with valid clinical findings and complete report annotations, ensuring robust and meaningful evaluation. For each sample, CheXbert extracted clinical finding labels from both the ground-truth reference report and our generated report, creating paired label sets for comparison. We then computed precision, recall, and F1-score based on true positive (TP), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) counts for each of the 14 pathological conditions. Precision was calculated as TP/(TP + FP), measuring the accuracy of positive predictions; recall as TP/(TP + FN), measuring the completeness of finding detection; and F1-score as the harmonic mean of precision and recall (2×precision×recall/(precision + recall)). Macro-averaged scores were reported across all 14 pathological categories to ensure balanced evaluation regardless of condition prevalence, following standard practices in medical report generation evaluation. This CheXbert-based evaluation framework offers a rigorous and clinically grounded alternative to traditional similarity metrics by directly quantifying the accuracy of key diagnostic findings. By integrating structured label extraction with standard performance measures, it enables objective and interpretable assessment of report fidelity to essential clinical content, providing the standardized evaluation foundation necessary for fair comparison with existing state-of-the-art methods (Table 1).

Conventional Metrics for Report Quality Evaluation

Traditional evaluation approaches for MRG have relied on NLG metrics that quantify textual similarity between generated reports and reference reports. These conventional metrics face inherent limitations in the medical domain. Their dependence on reference texts and focus on lexical matching may not fully capture clinical efficacy. More recent clinically-oriented evaluation approaches, such as RadGraph F1 [28] and CheXpert F1 [46], offer a more meaningful assessment framework. These metrics evaluate reports based on their ability to accurately capture key clinical findings, shifting focus from textual reproduction to medical content validity. This evolution in evaluation methodology better aligns with the fundamental goal of medical reporting: conveying accurate, patient-relevant clinical information.

LLM-based Evaluation with chain-of-thought (CoT) Reasoning

To overcome the limitations of reference-dependent metrics, we implemented a comprehensive LLM-based evaluation framework inspired by recent advances in G-Eval [47] and X-Eval [48], and LLM-RadJudge [49]. This approach leverages CoT reasoning to perform structured, interpretable assessments of generated reports. Our implementation utilized Mistral-small [50], an efficient open-source model demonstrating strong correlation with GPT-4 in evaluation tasks [49]. The framework evaluates three dimensions: (1) Consistency measures the internal logical coherence, uniformity in terminology, and absence of contradictions within the report; (2) Coverage evaluates the comprehensiveness of the generated report by comparing it to a ground truth report; (3) Fluency assesses the grammatical correctness, clarity, formatting, and proper use of medical terminology in the report. This process ensures that the report is not only accurate but also clear and professional, enhancing its usability for both clinical and patient communication (details in Supplementary Figure S2). Each metric is rated on a 1–5 scale, enabling context-aware, clinically relevant evaluation through an LLM-based framework with CoT reasoning. This approach ensures both interpretability and reliability in assessing patient-centered report quality.

Human Evaluation Study

To complement automated metrics and directly assess clinical applicability, we conducted a structured human evaluation using two validated frameworks: the Radiology Report Understanding Level Assessment Scale and the Artificial Intelligence Radiology Imaging Evaluation Scale [51]. The former has been specifically designed to assess medical report comprehensibility among non-medical populations and provides established benchmarks for patient-centered evaluation. The latter was developed to evaluate diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making, ensuring clinical relevance. All participants in this evaluation study provided informed consent prior to participation, were fully informed about the study objectives and evaluation procedures, and voluntarily agreed to participate in the assessment of generated radiology reports.

Ten non-medical participants with diverse educational backgrounds (5 females; aged 22–40 years; education levels ranging from undergraduate to doctoral degrees) were recruited to evaluate the generated medical reports. These participants served as patient proxies, representing the target population for literacy-adapted reports. Direct patient recruitment was not pursued due to practical and ethical constraints, including the challenges of identifying patients with specific literacy levels, recruiting individuals with relevant diagnostic conditions requiring radiology reports, and obtaining appropriate ethical approvals for patient participation in report comprehensibility studies. Participants assessed reports using a comprehensive five-criteria framework: (1) understanding of report structure, (2) understanding of professional terminology, (3) interpretation of imaging results, (4) understanding of report conclusions, and (5) overall understanding and applicability. Each criterion was rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = poor understanding, 1 = moderate understanding, 2 = good understanding), enabling fine-grained measurement of comprehension levels. Individual criterion scores were summed to produce a composite 10-point score, interpreted according to validated guidelines: Level C (0–4 points) indicating low understanding, Level B (5–7 points) indicating basic understanding, and Level A (8–10 points) indicating high understanding appropriate for patient communication. This evaluation methodology validates our framework’s effectiveness through subjective assessment that directly reflects patient comprehension and real-world clinical applicability.

Three board-certified expert clinicians with extensive clinical experience in radiology and diagnostic imaging were recruited to evaluate the generated reports. All clinicians held active board certifications and had significant experience in clinical report interpretation, ensuring qualified and authoritative assessment of diagnostic accuracy and clinical relevance. Clinicians evaluated each report using a dedicated four-criteria clinical evaluation framework across the following dimensions: (1) image interpretation accuracy, (2) report detail level, (3) interpretation depth and insightfulness, and (4) practicality and actionability. Each dimension was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = lowest performance, 5 = highest performance), enabling systematic, fine-grained evaluation of clinical value and providing expert-driven assessment of clinical accuracy, relevance, and real-world applicability.

Both non-medical participants and expert clinicians evaluated the same set of reports, including original clinical reports, initial automated reports, and literacy-adapted versions targeting three readability levels (6th, 11th, and 18th-grade). Reports were presented in randomized, counterbalanced order to minimize sequence effects and bias. Complete evaluation protocols and assessment materials are detailed in Supplementary Figure S3.

Experiments

Experimental Setup and Model Evaluation

All experiments were conducted using four NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs to ensure rapid and scalable processing capabilities for complex medical imaging and language model computations. We evaluated multiple chest X-Ray classification models on the MIMIC-CXR dataset, comparing traditional CNNs including CheXpert classifier [46] and DenseNet-121 [52] against attention-based and transformer-based architectures such as Mobile-ViT [53], MedViT [54], ADNet [55], and CheXFusion [33]. Traditional models provided baseline performance with AUROC approximately 0.70 and mAP around 0.25, while CheXFusion achieved the highest performance in both AUROC and mAP metrics, effectively managing both common and rare conditions through integrated feature extraction and global context understanding [56]. The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level metric was utilized to provide objective and standardized readability assessments for all generated medical reports [57].

Anatomical Localization and Clinical Integration Analysis

We compared Grad-CAM and Eigen-CAM for localizing cardiopulmonary findings using MS-CXR annotations, with Eigen-CAM significantly outperforming Grad-CAM in anatomical localization capability and clinical report accuracy enhancement. Using the RaDialog LLaVA model as baseline, we analyzed the effects of classifier choice (CheXpert vs. CheXFusion) and anatomical region integration on report accuracy. CheXFusion consistently outperformed CheXpert across both MIMIC-CXR and IU X-Ray datasets, with further improvements observed upon integrating anatomical context, as evidenced by enhanced F1-scores. This validates the clinical benefit of combining accurate classifiers with precise anatomical localization for comprehensive medical report generation.

Adaptive Report Generation and Quality Assessment

We implemented a self-reflective LLM system to iteratively refine medical reports, balancing readability and clinical accuracy across different educational levels. Reports consistently aligned closer to targeted readability levels while maintaining high clinical accuracy, with the iterative approach effectively tailoring content for diverse patient populations without compromising medical precision. Our systematic evaluation using the Mistral-small model assessed report quality across original clinical reports, initial clinician-oriented outputs, baseline RaDialog LLaVA reports, and final refined reports. Readability-adapted reports generally preserved clinical accuracy while improving accessibility, with higher readability levels enabling more sophisticated and fluent language use, demonstrating the framework’s capability to balance clinical utility with patient comprehension across varying literacy levels.

Results

Clinical Finding Detection and Anatomical Localization Performance

To justify our methodological choices, we conducted systematic comparative experiments evaluating multiple alternative architectures for both clinical finding detection and anatomical localization. On the MIMIC-CXR test set, CheXFusion outperformed five competing chest X-Ray classifiers representing diverse methodological approaches: CNN-based architectures (CheXpert classifier with EfficientNet-B3, DenseNet-121), vision transformer variants (Mobile-ViT, MedViT), and attention-based models (ADNet), achieving the highest AUROC (0.9046) and mAP (0.4290), significantly surpassing both CNN-based (e.g., CheXpert: AUROC 0.7068) and attention-based models (e.g., ADNet: AUROC 0.8274) (Fig. 2). This represents substantial improvements of 28.0% in AUROC over CheXpert and 9.3% over ADNet, providing clear empirical justification for selecting CheXFusion as our classification backbone. Its strong performance stems from ConvNeXt’s architectural strengths, effectively capturing both local details and global context critical for accurate pathological finding detection in chest radiographs.

For anatomical localization, we compared Eigen-CAM and Grad-CAM, the two most widely adopted explainable AI visualization techniques in medical imaging. Eigen-CAM demonstrated superior performance over Grad-CAM, with higher IoU (0.2854 vs. 0.0623) and Dice coefficient (0.4161 vs. 0.2878) (Table 2; Fig. 3). The dramatic improvement in IoU demonstrates Eigen-CAM’s ability to provide more precise, stable anatomical localizations compared to Grad-CAM’s gradient-based approach, which can suffer from instability during uncertain predictions. This enhanced localization provides more accurate anatomical context to the report generation pipeline, improving both diagnostic precision and clinical relevance of generated reports. These comparative results provide empirical justification for our architectural selections, ensuring our framework builds upon the most effective available methods for clinical applications.

Clinical Efficacy Analysis of Generated Reports

Our evaluation revealed that integrating the CheXFusion classifier with anatomical region information consistently enhanced clinical report quality across both MIMIC-CXR and IU X-Ray datasets (Table 3, Supplementary Figure S4). On MIMIC-CXR, this configuration achieved the highest scores (F1: 0.4403, precision: 0.4502, recall: 0.4643), improving diagnostic precision by 5.26% over the baseline. On IU X-Ray, the gains were even more pronounced, with a 17.10% precision increase (F1: 0.3573, precision: 0.4196, recall: 0.3639). These results confirm that combining CheXFusion with anatomical context yields more clinically accurate reports, establishing it as the optimal foundation for our system.

These studies provide direct quantitative evidence that: (i) the self-reflective mechanism substantially improves readability alignment with target levels; (ii) each component (reflection capability, memory retention, adaptive feedback) contributes significantly to overall performance; (iii) iterative refinement with memory retention is particularly critical for convergence; (iv) readability improvements are achieved without compromising clinical accuracy.

Human and Expert Clinical Evaluation

Human evaluation using the validated Radiology Report Understanding Level Assessment Scale and expert clinical assessment using the Artificial Intelligence Radiology Imaging Evaluation Scale [51] revealed that our self-reflective LLM framework successfully generates reports that are both clinically accurate and patient-comprehensible across different literacy levels.

Patient Comprehensibility Assessment

Structured assessment by ten non-medical participants demonstrated clear evidence of our framework’s effectiveness in enhancing patient-centered communication (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table S3). Reports without self-reflective refinement, including both original clinical reports and initial automated generations, consistently achieved low comprehension scores corresponding to Level C (0–4 points), indicating insufficient understanding for patient communication. These baseline reports averaged 3.75 ± 2.81 (original) and 3.80 ± 2.95 (initial) points for MIMIC-CXR, and 2.73 ± 2.25 (original) and 2.88 ± 1.83 (initial) points for IU X-Ray across all participants, reflecting poor comprehensibility due to complex medical terminology and dense clinical language.

Scores reflect comprehension levels from Level C (low understanding) to Level A (high understanding)

In contrast, reports processed through our self-reflective refinement mechanism achieved substantially higher comprehension scores. 6th-grade targeted reports consistently attained Level A ratings (8.75 ± 1.21 points for MIMIC-CXR and 8.50 ± 1.40 points for IU X-Ray), indicating excellent understanding appropriate for broad patient populations. 11th-grade targeted reports also achieved Level A ratings (8.12 ± 2.17 points for MIMIC-CXR and 8.20 ± 1.57 points for IU X-Ray), while 18th-grade targeted reports reached Level B (7.28 ± 2.25 points for MIMIC-CXR and 7.75 ± 2.52 points for IU X-Ray), reflecting the intentionally higher cognitive demand of professional-level communication. This comprehensibility gradient was consistent across both datasets, with no significant between-dataset differences in participant ratings (t = −0.889–1.801, p > 0.05). Paired t-tests confirmed significant improvements (t = 7.821–15.819, p < 0.001) between non-reflective and self-reflective reports across all literacy levels, with effect sizes indicating large practical significance (Cohen’s d = 1.323–3.458).

Clinical Accuracy and Utility Assessment

Expert clinician evaluation confirmed that our framework maintains clinical accuracy and diagnostic precision across different literacy levels (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S4). For the MIMIC-CXR dataset, clinician ratings for literacy-adjusted versions demonstrated high performance in image interpretation accuracy (ranging from 4.20 to 4.33), report detail level (4.07 to 4.40), and interpretation depth (4.00 to 4.27). Practicality showed a wider range of scores (3.40–4.13), indicating that some highly simplified reports, particularly the 6th-grade version, offered slightly reduced immediate clinical usefulness compared with original reports. Statistical analyses showed largely comparable performance between original clinical reports and literacy-adapted versions across most dimensions (t = −2.073–1.718, p > 0.05). Notable exceptions included significant improvements in report detail level for reports targeted at the 11th-grade (t = 3.108, p = 0.008) and 18th-grade (t = 3.108, p = 0.008) literacy levels, indicating enhanced completeness without compromising clinical standards. The initial report also demonstrated significant improvement in image interpretation accuracy (t = 2.256, p = 0.041), validating the framework’s ability to preserve diagnostic precision. For the IU X-Ray dataset, image interpretation accuracy ranged from 4.20 to 4.47, report detail level from 3.80 to 4.07, interpretation depth from 3.80 to 3.93, and practicality from 3.67 to 4.20. None of the literacy-adjusted reports differed significantly from the corresponding original reports across all evaluated aspects (t = −0.487–2.049, p > 0.05), demonstrating robust performance. Across both datasets, clinical accuracy consistently reached levels equivalent to four points or higher, demonstrating that all report versions reliably identified relevant imaging features and described radiologic findings with high fidelity. The other assessment criteria (report detail level, interpretation depth, and practicality) were generally rated at levels corresponding to three points or higher, indicating that adapted reports contained only minor omissions, covered key diagnostic elements, conveyed adequate interpretation depth, and provided at least moderate support for clinical decision-making.

These comprehensive findings provide compelling evidence that our self-reflective LLM framework successfully generates clinically accurate reports with controllable readability levels, enabling healthcare providers to deliver personalized communication that matches patient comprehension capabilities while maintaining diagnostic precision and clinical utility across diverse contexts.

Discussion

Technical Advantages and Architectural Innovations

Our unified framework represents a fundamental advancement in medical communication technology by addressing the critical gap between clinical precision and patient accessibility that existing methods fail to bridge [58]. Unlike conventional approaches that focus solely on clinical accuracy, our framework introduces architectural innovations that demonstrate clear superiority over current state-of-the-art methods. Compared to prior methods, CheXFusion achieved superior classification performance by effectively minimizing diagnostic errors at initial processing stages, establishing a robust foundation for subsequent report generation. More critically, Eigen-CAM outperformed widely-used Grad-CAM by delivering stable and precise anatomical localizations that enhance diagnostic clarity and clinical relevance. This performance advantage is essential as it prevents cascading diagnostic errors through the processing pipeline [59]. The comparative advantage becomes particularly evident when contrasting Eigen-CAM with current visualization methods. While Grad-CAM’s gradient-based approach suffers from instability during uncertain predictions, potentially producing misleading localizations [60], Eigen-CAM’s PCA-based feature map analysis provides consistently focused heatmaps even under challenging conditions [35]. This architectural robustness addresses a fundamental limitation of existing visualization approaches and provides the reliable anatomical references essential for clinical accuracy.

Performance Benchmarks and Dual-objective Achievement

Our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates that the unified framework substantially exceeds existing medical report generation approaches across multiple dimensions (Table 1). We conducted systematic comparison with seven state-of-the-art methods on the MIMIC-CXR dataset: CvT2DistilGPT2 [16], FMVP [17], RECAP [18], HERGen [19], CAMANet [20], MPO [21], and RaDialog [22]. All methods utilized the official MIMIC-CXR dataset split and CheXbert labeler for standardized clinical assessment, ensuring fair comparison. Table 1 presents the detailed performance comparison across precision, recall, and F1-score metrics. As shown in Table 1, our framework achieved superior clinical performance across key diagnostic metrics, attaining the highest recall score of 0.464 and F1-score of 0.440, while maintaining competitive precision of 0.450. Compared to the seven state-of-the-art methods (CvT2DistilGPT2, FMVP, RECAP, HERGen, CAMANet, MPO, RaDialog), our framework achieved meaningful improvements of 2.1% in recall and 4.3% in F1-score compared to the previous best-performing methods for each respective metric. While these gains may appear numerically modest, they represent clinically significant advances in diagnostic performance, particularly considering the maturity of medical AI systems where incremental improvements often translate to substantial clinical impact. These improvements translate to tangible clinical benefits: enhanced recall of 0.464 reduces missed diagnoses in screening contexts by improving sensitivity in detecting clinical abnormalities, crucial for comprehensive diagnostic coverage, while the improved F1-score of 0.440 reflects better overall balance between precision and recall, suggesting more reliable clinical finding identification. The 5.26% gain in diagnostic precision of 0.450 ensures enhanced sensitivity without compromising clinical specificity, maintaining clinical reliability despite the enhanced sensitivity. Most significantly, our framework uniquely achieves these clinical performance gains while simultaneously delivering a remarkable 56.88% improvement in patient comprehension scores across multiple literacy levels—a dual optimization that no existing method addresses. Current approaches focus exclusively on clinical metrics, completely neglecting patient accessibility and comprehension needs that are critical for effective healthcare communication and shared decision-making. These results demonstrate that our approach successfully bridges the traditional clinical accuracy-patient accessibility gap, providing a practically viable solution for healthcare environments requiring both diagnostic precision and effective patient communication—a balance absent in existing automated systems and addressing real-world clinical challenges that current methods fail to consider.

Self-reflective Innovation and Advanced Evaluation Methodology

Our self-reflective LLM system represents a fundamental departure from static report generation employed by all existing methods. The system dynamically adjusts report readability across three educational levels (6th, 11th, 18th-grade) while ensuring accessibility without sacrificing clinical accuracy through integrated dual metrics that assess both clinical efficacy and readability. This iterative refinement process successfully bridges the gap between comprehensive clinical reporting and patient-friendly communication, enhancing patient engagement and shared decision-making capabilities [61]—functionalities entirely absent in current automated systems.

We introduced an advanced evaluation methodology employing the Mistral-small model [50] with chain-of-thought reasoning [62], assessing report quality through consistency, coverage, and fluency dimensions. This multi-dimensional, transparent evaluation framework substantially surpasses traditional lexical metrics employed by existing methods by providing deeper insights into both clinical effectiveness and linguistic accessibility. Conventional evaluation approaches focus solely on clinical accuracy metrics, overlooking the essential patient comprehension components that our framework systematically addresses.

Our comprehensive evaluation confirmed the practical effectiveness of our approach from both patient and clinical perspectives. Non-medical participant evaluation demonstrated substantial improvements in comprehensibility, with self-reflective reports consistently achieving Level A understanding (average scores of 8.12–8.75 across literacy levels and datasets). In contrast, baseline methods received Level C ratings (averaging 2.73–3.80), indicating insufficient understanding for effective patient communication. Critically, expert clinician evaluation confirmed that these improvements were achieved without compromising clinical accuracy. Board-certified radiologists rated literacy-adapted reports as maintaining high clinical standards, with image interpretation accuracy consistently exceeding 4.0 points (out of 5.0) across all literacy levels. Statistical analyses revealed no significant differences between original and literacy-adapted reports for most dimensions (p > 0.05), with notable improvements in report detail level for 11th-grade and 18th-grade versions (p = 0.008). While highly simplified 6th-grade reports showed slightly reduced practicality scores (3.40–3.67), they remained within acceptable clinical range for patient-centered care contexts. These converging findings provide compelling evidence that our self-reflective framework successfully generates reports that are simultaneously comprehensible to patients with varying literacy levels and clinically accurate for healthcare providers, representing a significant advance toward personalized health communication without sacrificing diagnostic precision or clinical utility.

Limitations, Methodological Considerations, and Future Work

Despite significant advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. Frist, the iterative refinement process introduces computational overhead compared to single-pass generation approaches, potentially limiting scalability in resource-constrained environments. Second, our validation focused exclusively on chest X-Ray imaging, while some existing methods demonstrate broader anatomical coverage [63, 64]. Third, the LLM-based evaluation framework requires further clinical validation with larger and more diverse participant samples, and iterative refinement strategies should be optimized to improve computational efficiency. Finally, while our human evaluation successfully demonstrated gains in patient comprehensibility through non-medical participant assessment and confirmed maintained clinical accuracy through expert clinician evaluation, the limited sample sizes (ten non-medical participants and three expert clinicians) may constrain generalizability across diverse patient populations with varying literacy levels, demographics, and clinical contexts.

Future work should address these limitations through large-scale validation studies involving broader participant groups across diverse healthcare institutions and patient populations.

Extending the methodology to additional imaging modalities such as CT and MRI, incorporating longitudinal patient data, and enhancing computational efficiency are critical next steps. Multi-modality extensions will require adapting the hybrid detection architecture to handle volumetric data while preserving patient-centered functionality. Finally, investigating integration into real-world clinical workflows will be essential to evaluate usability, scalability, and overall impact in healthcare practice, including assessment of how literacy-adapted reports influence patient-clinician communication and health outcomes.

Conclusion

We developed a unified framework integrating CheXFusion classification, Eigen-CAM localization, and self-reflective LLMs for automated chest X-Ray report generation. Our approach bridges the gap between clinical accuracy and patient accessibility through adaptive readability while maintaining diagnostic precision. The core contributions of this work are threefold: First, we introduce a self-reflective LLM-based framework that dynamically adapts report complexity to varying literacy levels while preserving clinical content integrity. Second, we integrate CheXFusion and Eigen-CAM architectures to enable robust clinical finding detection with precise anatomical localization. Third, we develop a novel multi-dimensional evaluation methodology incorporating automated metrics (consistency, fluency, and coverage using Mistral-small) and comprehensive human validation through both patient-proxy and expert clinician assessment. Our results demonstrate substantial improvements: 19.78% increase in AUROC, 17.29% gain in mAP, 56.88% enhancement in readability, and 5.26% improvement in diagnostic precision. Critically, dual human evaluation confirmed that literacy adaptation significantly improves patient comprehensibility while maintaining clinical accuracy and utility as validated by board-certified radiologists. These achievements represent a significant advancement in medical AI systems that successfully balance technical performance with patient-centered design. This work establishes new standards for automated medical documentation, reducing clinical workload while enhancing patient engagement and communication across diverse healthcare populations. Our framework provides a foundation for future developments in adaptive medical communication systems that serve both clinical efficiency and patient understanding.
