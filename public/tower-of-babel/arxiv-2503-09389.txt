Canonical equilibrium of mean-field O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) models in presence of random fields
Soumya Kanti Pal, Shamik Gupta

Abstract
We study canonical-equilibrium properties of Random Field O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) Models involving classical continuous vector spins of n n components with mean-field interactions and subject to disordered fields acting on individual spins. To this end, we employ two complementary approaches: the mean-field approximation, valid for any disorder distribution, and the replica trick, applicable when the disordered fields are sampled from a Gaussian distribution. On the basis of an exact analysis, we demonstrate that when replica symmetry holds, both the approaches yield identical expression for the free energy per spin of the system. As consequences, we study the case of n = 2 n=2 ( X ​ Y XY spins) and that of n = 3 n=3 (Heisenberg spins) for two representative choices of the disorder distribution, namely, a Gaussian and a symmetric bimodal distribution. For both n = 2 n=2 and n = 3 n=3 , we demonstrate that while the magnetization exhibits a continuous phase transition as a function of temperature for the Gaussian case, the transition could be either continuous or first-order with an emergent tricriticality when the disorder distribution is bimodal. We also discuss in the context of our models the issue of self-averaging of extensive variables near the critical point of a continuous phase transition.

The models we consider here form the class of Random Field

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{J}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{h}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{i},

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(1)}=\cos{\theta_{i}^{(1)}},

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(2)}=\sin{\theta_{i}^{(1)}}\cos{\theta_{i}^{(2)}},

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(3)}=\sin{\theta_{i}^{(1)}}\sin{\theta_{i}^{(2)}}\cos{\theta_{i}^{(3)}},

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(n-1)}=\sin{\theta_{i}^{(1)}}\sin{\theta_{i}^{(2)}}\ldots\sin{\theta_{i}^{(n-2)}}\cos{\theta_{i}^{(n-1)}},

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(n)}=\sin{\theta_{i}^{(1)}}\sin{\theta_{i}^{(2)}}\ldots\sin{\theta_{i}^{(n-2)}}\sin{\theta_{i}^{(n-1)}},

with the polar angles in the range
0\leq\theta_{i}^{(1)},\theta_{i}^{(2)},\ldots\theta_{i}^{(n-2)}\leq\pi

In Eq. (

\displaystyle P(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp{\left(-\frac{h^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)},

(3)

\displaystyle P(h)=\frac{1}{2}[\delta(h-h_{0})+\delta(h+h_{0})].

(4)

Here,
\sigma>0
and
h_{0}>0
are given parameters that may be taken to denote the strength of disorder in each of the two cases of the disorder distribution. In the case of the bimodal distribution, the parameter
h_{0}
sets the variance of the distribution, which equals
h_{0}^{2}
; the same for the Gaussian distribution equals
\sigma^{2}
.

In this work, we study the properties of the system (
1
) in canonical equilibrium at temperature
T
. To this end, we pursue two complementary approaches. The first one is that of the mean-field (MF) approximation, which is an obvious choice given the all-to-all interaction between the spins in system (
1
). However, intricacies arise in applying a naive mean-field approximation owing to the presence of the quenched-disordered fields in the system. Our modified MF approach involves two levels of approximation. Firstly, we consider the system for a fixed realization of the disordered fields and study it in terms of a mean-field Hamiltonian expressed in terms of the thermal-averaged magnetization. Here, the underlying assumption is that the thermal average of every spin component fluctuates only negligibly from one spin to another, despite the fact that each spin on its own is evolving in a different environment owing to the presence of the disordered fields varying from spin to spin. The next level of approximation is to assume that the thermal-averaged magnetization, which in principle varies from one disorder realization to another, nevertheless takes up the same value across disorder realizations (self-averaging property). This is expected to be valid provided the disorder distribution does not have fat tails, which is the case for the two representative choices in Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
). The outcome of our analysis is an explicit expression for the disorder-averaged free energy per spin, from which ordering properties of the system in equilibrium and in particular information about phase transitions in equilibrium can be straightforwardly derived.

In the second complementary approach, we invoke for the case of Gaussian distribution for the disordered fields, the celebrated replica trick commonly used to analyze systems with quenched disorder, especially, spin-glass systems. In computing the disorder-averaged free energy per spin, one is required to compute the disorder-averaged canonical partition function

\overline{\ln Z}
. Such a computation within the replica trick is reduced to computing the disorder average of the product of the partition function for
p
replicas,
\overline{Z^{p}}
, with
p
an integer, where each replica is defined as an identical copy of the system with identical disorder realizations but with dynamics leading to potentially-different equilibrium configurations. The nontriviality of the approach lies in recovering
\overline{\ln Z}
from the behavior of
(\overline{Z^{p}}-1)/p
as
p
approaches continuously to zero. Under the assumption of replica symmetry, which asserts that the equilibrium attained across the replicas is the same, we show on the basis of our exact computation that results from the MF and the replica approach coincide exactly.

II
Equilibrium properties

Here, we investigate the properties of the system (
1
) in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
and in canonical equilibrium at temperature
T=1/\beta
[we work in units in which the Boltzmann constant is unity]. Although a mean-field model, the local environment of each spin is not identical owing to the disordered fields
\vec{h}_{i}
. The exactness of mean-field theory in cases of quenched-disordered fields and couplings has been studied in Refs.
[
30
]
and
[
31
]
, respectively. The main problem in writing down a free-energy expression for a quenched-disordered Hamiltonian relates to making a distinction between computing the logarithm of disorder-averaged partition function,
\log\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
, and the disorder-averaged logarithm of the partition function,
\overline{\log\mathcal{Z}}
. It is well known that the former is unphysical, while the latter is physical, based on the self-averaging property of the free energy (for further discussions, see Refs.
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
).

II.1
Mean-field approximation

In order to study the canonical-equilibrium properties of system (
1
), we develop in this part an effective mean-field (MF) approach, which involves two levels of approximation: (i) For a fixed realization of disordered fields, we study the system in terms of a mean-field Hamiltonian expressed in terms of the thermal-averaged magnetization (averaging with respect to the canonical-equilibrium measure); (ii) Next, we invoke that the thermal-averaged magnetization is self-averaging in the limit
N\to\infty
, in that it has the same value across different disorder realizations. Let us now proceed to construct first the mean-field Hamiltonian.

II.1.1
Constructing the mean-field Hamiltonian

To proceed, let us denote the mean-field Hamiltonian by
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{MF}}(\{\vec{h}_{i}\})
for a fixed realization of disordered fields
\{\vec{h}_{i}\}
. Clearly, there are
n
components of the thermal-averaged magnetization vector
\vec{\tilde{m}}=(\tilde{m}^{(1)},\tilde{m}^{(2)},\ldots,\tilde{m}^{(n)})
, with
\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}
and
\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}\equiv\langle S_{i}^{(\alpha)}\rangle
, where angular brackets denote thermal averaging. The norm of this vector, given by
\tilde{m}=|\vec{\tilde{m}}|=\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}(\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)})^{2}}
, serves as the order parameter of the system. Notice that there is no a priori requirement for
\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}
to be independent of disorder realizations and be only dependent on
\beta
.

We now write the component of any spin in terms of its thermal-averaged realization-dependent value
\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}
as
S_{i}^{(\alpha)}=\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}+\delta S_{i}^{(\alpha)}
, where
\delta S_{i}^{(\alpha)}
denotes deviation from the thermal average. In the first step of approximation, we assume that the thermal average is the same for every spin, despite the fact that every spin has got different local environment:
\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}=\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,N
and
\alpha=1,2,\ldots,n
. The above steps result in the expression

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(\alpha)}S_{j}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)})^{2}+\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}(\delta S_{i}^{(\alpha)}+\delta S_{j}^{(\alpha)})+\delta S_{i}^{(\alpha)}\delta S_{j}^{(\alpha)}.

(5)

Next, retaining up to the linear term and neglecting higher-order fluctuations, i.e., the term
\delta S_{i}^{(\alpha)}\delta S_{j}^{(\alpha)}
, we get

\displaystyle S_{i}^{(\alpha)}S_{j}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)})^{2}+m^{(\alpha)}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)}+S_{j}^{(\alpha)}-2m^{(\alpha)})

\displaystyle=m^{(\alpha)}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)}+S_{j}^{(\alpha)}-m^{(\alpha)}),

(6)

which used in Eq. (
1
) finally leads to the following mean-field Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{MF}}=\frac{NJ\tilde{m}^{2}}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot(\vec{h}_{i}+J\vec{\tilde{m}}).

(7)

For future purposes, let us define the quantity

\displaystyle\vec{K}_{i}\equiv\vec{h}_{i}+J\vec{\tilde{m}},

(8)

which allows to rewrite Eq. (
7
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{MF}}=\frac{NJ\tilde{m}^{2}}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{K}_{i}.

(9)

The thermal average computed with respect to the above mean-field Hamiltonian gives the following self-consistent relation for
\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}
:

\displaystyle{\langle S_{i}^{(\alpha)}\rangle}=\tilde{m}_{i}^{(\alpha)}=\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}=\frac{1}{z_{i}}\int d\vec{S}_{i}~S_{i}^{(\alpha)}\exp[\beta\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{K}_{i}],

(10)

where, with the single-spin integral
\int d\vec{S}_{i}
defined as

\displaystyle\int d\vec{S}_{i}\equiv\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta^{(1)}\ldots\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta^{(n-1)}~\mathcal{J}(\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(n-1)}),

(11)

and
\mathcal{J}(\theta_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,\theta_{i}^{(n-1)})
denoting the unit
n
-sphere volume element, we have the single-spin partition function

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\int d\vec{S}_{i}~\exp[\beta\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{K}_{i}].

(12)

Having obtained the mean-field Hamiltonian as in Eq. (
7
), we now move on to obtain the mean-field partition function.

II.1.2
Computing the mean-field partition function

The mean-field partition function reads as

\displaystyle{}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{MF}}

\displaystyle=\exp{\left(-\frac{\beta NJ\tilde{m}^{2}}{2}\right)}\int\prod_{j=1}^{N}d\vec{S}_{j}~\exp{\left(\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{K}_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp{\left(-\frac{\beta NJ\tilde{m}^{2}}{2}\right)}\prod_{j=1}^{N}z_{j},

(13)

yielding

\displaystyle{}\log\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{MF}}=-\frac{\beta NJ\tilde{m}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log{z_{j}}.

(14)

Now, performing the average over all possible realizations of the disordered fields
\{h_{i}^{(1)},h_{i}^{(2)},\ldots,h_{i}^{(n)}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
in Eq. (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle{}\overline{\log\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{MF}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta NJ}{2}\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\vec{h}_{i}~P(\vec{h}_{i})\tilde{m}^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~+\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\vec{h}_{i}~P(\vec{h}_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log{z_{j}},

(15)

where we have

\displaystyle P(\vec{h}_{i})d\vec{h}_{i}=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}P(h_{i}^{(\alpha)})dh_{i}^{(\alpha)},

(16)

using the fact that for every spin, the components of the disordered field are sampled independently from the common distribution
P(h)
.
We now aim to obtain the disorder-averaged free energy of the system.

II.1.3
Obtaining the disorder-averaged free energy

At this point, we invoke the self-averaging property of the thermal-averaged magnetization appearing in Eq. (
15
). This implies that the distribution of any component of thermal-averaged magnetization across different disorder realizations is sharply peaked, indicating negligible sample-to-sample fluctuations. Such a property is expected to hold as long as the disorder distribution is not anomalously broad or pathological. The self-averaging property motivates us to replace
\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}
with the corresponding disordered-averaged quantity
m^{(\alpha)}
, given by the integral
\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\vec{h}_{i}P(\vec{h}_{i})~\tilde{m}^{(\alpha)}
. This forms the second step of our mean-field approximation, and which on using Eq. (
15
) directly leads us to the final expression for the disorder-averaged free energy per spin as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=-k_{B}T\,\frac{\overline{\log\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{MF}}}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int d{\vec{h}}~P(\vec{h})\log\left\{\int d\vec{S}\exp[\beta\vec{S}\cdot(\vec{h}+J\vec{m})]\right\}.

(17)

II.1.4
On the validity of the mean-field approximation

We conclude this part by stressing the fact that the derivation of the mean-field-approximated free energy per spin in Eq. (
17
) crucially relies on the condition that the underlying disorder distribution has sharply-decaying tails; however, the issue of how sharp the tails should be remains unsettled. Namely, for a Pareto distribution given by

\displaystyle P(h)\propto\frac{1}{h_{0}+h^{2\delta}};~-\infty<h<\infty,

(18)

one does not know exactly the threshold exponent
\delta
above which this mean-field approximation would work. In our study, the representative distributions we consider have at most exponentially-decaying tails, and therefore, the mean-field approximation is expected to be valid for them.

In the next part of the paper, we consider the random fields
\{\vec{h}_{i}\}
to follow the Gaussian distribution (
3
) and employ the celebrated replica trick as an alternative to the mean-field approximation discussed above to obtain the disorder-averaged free energy of the system. We will demonstrate that for the case of the Gaussian distribution, one obtains using the two approaches identical expression for the disorder-averaged free energy of the system by invoking the replica-symmetry ansatz.

II.2
Replica trick for Gaussian-distributed random fields

An established route to study quenched-disordered statistical systems is use of the so-called replica trick. The trick was first introduced by Anderson in 1975 in the context of spin-glass systems
[
1
]
, wherein the coupling between different spins is a random variable sampled from a Gaussian distribution. For such systems, this trick has proven to be immensely successful in characterizing the spin-glass phase. Here, we implement this trick for our class of models, by following the procedure for the mean-field version of the RFIM implemented by Schneider and Pytte in Ref.
[
35
]
. We consider the disordered fields to follow the Gaussian distribution (
3
).

II.2.1
The replica trick

By using the definition of the
\log
function, one may write

\displaystyle{}\overline{\log\mathcal{Z}}=\lim_{p\to 0}\frac{\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}-1}{p}.

(19)

In the replica trick, we consider several replicas of the same system. In each replica, the realization of quenched disorder
\{\vec{h}_{i}\}
is the same, but the dynamics of the spins may potentially lead to different equilibrium configurations, according to the multiple-equilibria picture proposed in the context of disordered systems
[
36
]
.

The replica trick was first proposed in the context of the random-bond Ising model with nearest-neighbor spin couplings that are random variables sampled from a Gaussian distribution. Random couplings lead to frustration in the system. Simply put, this refers to the effect that fixing the state of an initial spin and going around a loop while fixing the state of all spins one after another along the loop by respecting the sign of the spin-spin coupling, one may be required to return to
the initial spin and flip its state. As a result, at low temperatures and for a given disorder realization, the system possesses many configurations/states with similar energy that are separated by configurations with substantially higher energy. Consequently, prepared in one such low-energy state, low-temperature dynamics does not allow the system to access other close by states with similar energy owing to intervening large potential barriers. This leads to states within each local minima equilibrating locally with respect to a small set of energetically-accessible states. Systems prepared in each such local minima and equilibrating locally give rise to the concept of replicas that are identical copies of the same system with identical disorder realization but with potentially different equilibrium states. These states coincide when replica symmetry holds, but are different when there is spontaneous breaking of replica symmetry, as was first pointed out by Parisi for the spin-glass phase of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
[
2
]
. We now employ the replica trick to compute the disorder average of the product of the partition function, namely, the quantity
\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}
.

II.2.2
Computing the disorder average of the product of the partition function

Using Eq. (
19
), we consider the product of the partition function for
p
(integer) number of replicas and then take its disorder average, i.e., obtain the quantity
\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}
, and finally, analytically continue the result to obtain the right hand side of Eq. (
19
). We have by definition that

\displaystyle{}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\vec{h}_{i}P(\vec{h}_{i})\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left[\frac{\beta J}{2N}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\vec{S_{i}}^{(a)}\cdot\vec{S_{j}}^{(a)}+\beta\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{h_{i}}\cdot\vec{S_{i}}^{(a)}\right],

(20)

where, as mentioned earlier, the index
a
stands for the replicas, while the quantity
D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]
contains the relevant measure for
N
spins in the
a
-th replica.

Now, let us interchange the order of the two integrations in Eq. (
20
). Separating out the contribution due to a single spin-component, say, the
\alpha
-th component, of any spin in all the replicas, and integrating with respect to the measure of the same component of the random field acting on the same spin, we get

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dh_{i}^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left[\beta\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)h_{i}^{(\alpha)}-\frac{{h_{i}^{(\alpha)}}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right].

(21)

Next, we use the Hubbard-Stratonovich identity

\displaystyle{}\exp{(\lambda b^{2})}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\sqrt{2\lambda}bz\right)},

(22)

with
\lambda
real and positive, to write

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{\beta J}{2N}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\vec{S_{i}}^{(a)}\cdot\vec{S_{j}}^{(a)}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{a=1}^{p}\frac{dz_{a}^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}~\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left[\sqrt{\frac{\beta J}{N}}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\right],

(23)

where
z_{a}^{(\alpha)}
’s are the independent auxiliary variables corresponding to
\sum_{i=1}^{N}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}
. Note that obtaining Eq. (
23
) relies on the all-to-all coupling between the spins. Using these results, and suppressing for brevity the explicit appearance of the integration range for each of the auxiliary variables
z_{a}^{(\alpha)}
, Eq. (
20
) gives

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}\prod_{a=1}^{p}\frac{dz_{a}^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\right]\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\Big[\sqrt{\frac{\beta J}{N}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)z_{a}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle~~~~+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}\prod_{a=1}^{p}\frac{dz_{a}^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\right]\left(\mathcal{K}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}]\right)^{N};

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}]\equiv\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]\exp\Big[\sqrt{\frac{\beta J}{N}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big],

(25)

where in the last equation, the quantity
D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]
now contains the relevant measure for a single spin in the
a
-th replica, while the quantity
\mathcal{K}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}]
may be interpreted as the single-spin partition function.
In arriving at Eq. (
24
) from Eq. (
20
), we have done a rearrangement of terms to have a trade-off between spin-spin interaction terms such as
(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}(S_{j}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}
and replica-replica interaction terms such as
(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}(S_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{(b)}
.

We now rescale the auxiliary variables as
z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\to(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}=z_{a}^{(\alpha)}/\sqrt{N}
and drop the primes, thereby obtaining

\displaystyle{}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}\prod_{a=1}^{p}\frac{dz_{a}^{(\alpha)}\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{2}+\log(\mathcal{K}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}])\right)\right],

(26)

where we now have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}]\equiv\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]\exp\Big[\sqrt{\beta J}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big].

(27)

Let us invoke the saddle-point approximation for evaluation of the integral in Eq. (
26
). We denote for the
a
-th replica and the
\alpha
-th spin component the minimizing auxiliary variable that minimizes the quantity multiplying the factor
N
in the exponential in the above equation by
(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}
, which evidently satisfies the following relation:

\displaystyle(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\beta J}\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}~\exp\left[\sqrt{\beta J}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]}{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~\exp\left[\sqrt{\beta J}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]}.

(28)

Under the scaling
z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\to(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}=z_{a}^{(\alpha)}/\sqrt{\beta J}
, and dropping the primes, we get

\displaystyle(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}~\exp\left[\beta J\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]}{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~\exp\left[\beta J\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}z_{a}^{(\alpha)}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right)^{2}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]}.

(29)

Constructing the system (

): For the system (

) with the disordered fields sampled from a Gaussian distribution, we first consider

In order to understand physically the right hand side of Eq. (
29
), we now move on to construct a Hamiltonian in the replica space, namely, the replica Hamiltonian.

II.2.3
Constructing the replica Hamiltonian

The physical interpretation of the right hand side of Eq. (
29
) is the following. Let us single out one spin (the same spin) from each of the
p
replicas, and construct a new physical system comprising these spins
\{\vec{S}^{(a)}\}_{1\leq a\leq p}
, with each subjected to an onsite field
J\vec{z}_{a}
. Let us define the Hamiltonian of the system as (see Fig.
1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}=J\sum_{a=1}^{p}\vec{S}^{(a)}\cdot\vec{z}_{a}+\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{p}\vec{S}^{(a)}\cdot\vec{S}^{(b)}.

(30)

For
\sigma=0
, computing the canonical-equilibrium average with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle(S^{(\alpha))})^{a}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}~\exp\left[\beta\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}(\sigma=0)\right]}{\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]~\exp\left[\beta\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}(\sigma=0)\right]}

\displaystyle=\langle S^{(\alpha)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}}=m^{(\alpha)},

(31)

which coincides with the right hand side of Eq. (
29
) with
\sigma=0
. In arriving at the last equality, we have used the fact that for
\sigma=0
, the different spins in our system, coming from different replicas, are not interacting with one another. Here, we have denoted by
m^{(\alpha)}
the thermal average of the
\alpha
-th spin component with respect to the canonical-equilibrium measure with respect to the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}
and at temperature
1/\beta
. For
\sigma\neq 0
, we may then write on using Eq. (
29
) that

\displaystyle(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle={\langle(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}}=(m^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}.

(32)

In the following, we proceed with our analysis by invoking the replica-symmetry ansatz.

II.2.4
The replica-symmetry ansatz: Obtaining the disorder-averaged free energy

In principle, the values of the minimizing auxiliary variables
(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}
could be different for different replicas. However, let us invoke the replica-symmetry ansatz, which demands that these independent minimizing auxiliary variables take up the same value across the different replicas, i.e.,

\displaystyle(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=(z_{b}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}};~a\neq b;~a,b\in\{1,\ldots,p\}.

(33)

Physically, the ansatz implies that the fluctuations of
{\langle(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{replica}}}
across the different spins in the system (
30
) are negligible, and one has

\displaystyle{}(z_{1}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=(z_{2}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=\ldots=(z_{p}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=m^{(\alpha)},

(34)

which in terms of unscaled
z_{a}^{(\alpha)}
(see the discussion following Eq. (
28
)) reads as

\displaystyle{}(z_{1}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=(z_{2}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=\ldots=(z_{p}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}=\sqrt{\beta J}m^{(\alpha)}.

(35)

One may view the ansatz in Eq. (
33
) from another perspective. Had the fields
\vec{h}_{i}
in Eq. (
1
) been the same for all spins (i.e., had there been no disorder in the system), all the different replicas would have identical equilibrium configurations. The ansatz asserts that the same remains true even when there is disorder in the system.

Putting back Eq. (
35
) in Eq. (
27
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\{(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}\}]=\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left\{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left[\beta Jm^{(\alpha)}\left(A^{(\alpha)}\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(A^{(\alpha)}\right)^{2}\right]\right\},

(36)

where we have defined
A^{(\alpha)}\equiv\sum_{a=1}^{p}(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}
. Now, we use the Hubbard-Stratonovich identity in Eq. (
22
) to write

\displaystyle\exp{\left[\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(A^{(\alpha)}\right)^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dh^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp{\left[-\frac{(h^{(\alpha)})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\beta A^{(\alpha)}h^{(\alpha)}\right]},

(37)

which on using Eq. (
36
) results in

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\{(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}\}]

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]\int d\vec{h}~P(\vec{h})

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left\{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left[\beta(Jm^{(\alpha)}+h^{(\alpha)})A^{(\alpha)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\int d\vec{h}~P(\vec{h})\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]

\displaystyle~~~~\times\exp\left\{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left[\beta(Jm^{(\alpha)}+h^{(\alpha)})A^{(\alpha)}\right]\right\};

(38)

\int P(\vec{h})d\vec{h}=\int\prod_{\alpha=1}^{n}dh^{(\alpha)}/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp{\left(-(h^{(\alpha)})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})\right)}
. Also, note that the order of integrals has been interchanged in order to arrive at the last equality. After separating the different replica contributions in the exponential, as in

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\beta(Jm^{(\alpha)}+h^{(\alpha)})A^{(\alpha)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\beta(Jm^{(\alpha)}+h^{(\alpha)})(S^{(\alpha)})^{(a)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\sum_{a=1}^{p}\beta(J\vec{m}+\vec{h})\cdot\vec{S}^{(a)}\right\},

(39)

we can write the joint integral on the left hand side of the following as a product of
p
identical integrals:

\displaystyle\int\prod_{a=1}^{p}D[\vec{S}^{(a)}]\exp\left\{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\beta(Jm^{(\alpha)}+h^{(\alpha)})A^{(\alpha)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\int d\vec{S}\exp[\beta(J\vec{m}+\vec{h})\cdot\vec{S}]\right)^{p},

(40)

since the different
\vec{S}^{(a)}
’s are independent and identical.
Using the above expression, we finally arrive from Eq. (
38
) to

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\{(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}\}]=\int d\vec{h}~P(\vec{h})\left(\int d\vec{S}\exp[\beta(\vec{h}+J\vec{m})\cdot\vec{S}\right)^{p}.

(41)

This completes the computation of
\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}
, for which, using Eq. (
26
) and the saddle-point approximation, we now have the final expression:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{p}}=\exp\left[-\frac{N\beta Jp}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}(m^{(\alpha)})^{2}+N\log(\mathcal{K}_{1}[\{z_{a}^{(\alpha)}\}_{\mathrm{min}}])\right].

(42)

As suggested by Eq. (
19
), we express the free energy
\mathcal{F}=-(1/\beta)\,\overline{\log\mathcal{Z}}
as

\displaystyle{}-\beta\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to 0}\frac{1}{p}~\Big[\exp\Big(N\Big\{-\frac{\beta Jp}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}(m^{(\alpha)})^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~+\log\left[\mathcal{K}\left(\{(z_{a}^{(\alpha)})_{\mathrm{min}}\}\right)\right]\Big\}\Big)-1\Big].

(43)

After implementing the limit in Eq. (
43
) using the L’Hôspital’s rule, we get the free energy per spin as

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int d{\vec{h}}~P(\vec{h})\log\left\{\int d\vec{S}\exp[\beta\vec{S}\cdot(\vec{h}+J\vec{m})]\right\}.

(44)

II.2.5
Comparing disorder-averaged free energy expressions obtained from mean-field approximation and replica trick

Clearly, Eq. (
44
) for the replica-symmetric expression of free energy per spin, for the case of disordered fields sampled from the Gaussian distribution, coincides with the one obtained from the mean-field approximation, Eq. (
17
). However, one should note that the replica trick could be employed only for the Gaussian distribution, but the expression in Eq. (
17
) is expected to hold true for any distribution without broad tails.

models with disordered fields, at least when the fields are sampled from a Gaussian distribution. The stability of the replica-symmetry ansatz for

with

Following the standard prescription of Landau theory, we proceed to expand the free-energy expression in Eq. (
51
) around
m=0
. Let us first note that for small
m
, one has
[
38
]

\displaystyle I_{0}\left(\frac{Jmr}{\sigma}\right)=1+\frac{J^{2}m^{2}r^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}r^{4}}{64\sigma^{4}}+\ldots.

(52)

Using the above expansion in Eq. (
51
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr~r\exp{(-\frac{r^{2}}{2})}~\log{[2\pi I_{0}(\beta r\sigma)]}

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{J^{2}m^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}}{8\sigma^{4}}+\ldots\right]\left[1+\frac{J^{2}m^{2}r^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}r^{4}}{64\sigma^{4}}+\ldots\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}[r]+m^{2}\left(\frac{J}{2}-\frac{J^{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}}[r]+\frac{J^{2}}{4\beta\sigma^{2}}[r^{3}]\right)

\displaystyle+m^{4}\left(-\frac{J^{4}}{64\beta\sigma^{4}}[r^{5}]-\frac{J^{4}}{8\beta\sigma^{4}}[r]+\frac{J^{4}}{8\beta\sigma^{4}}[r^{3}]\right)+\ldots

\displaystyle=a_{0}(T)+a_{2}(T)m^{2}+a_{4}(T)m^{4}+\ldots,

(53)

where we have used the definition

\displaystyle[r^{k}]\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}dr~r^{k}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}\log{\left[2\pi I_{0}(\beta r\sigma)\right]}.

(54)

The critical temperature
T_{c}=1/\beta_{c}
is then obtained as

\displaystyle a_{2}(T=T_{c})=0,

(55)

yielding

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=-\frac{J}{\sigma^{2}}\left([r]|_{\beta_{c}}-\frac{[r^{3}]|_{\beta_{c}}}{2}\right).

(56)

Let us now discuss the weak-disorder limit, i.e.,
\sigma\to 0
. In this limit, we have on using Eq. (
52
) that

\displaystyle\log{[2\pi I_{0}(\beta r\sigma)]}\approx\log{2\pi}+\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}r^{2}-\frac{1}{64}\beta^{4}\sigma^{4}r^{4},

(57)

yielding
[r]=\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}/2-\beta^{4}\sigma^{4}/8+\log(2\pi)
and
[r^{3}]=2\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}-3\beta^{4}\sigma^{4}/4+2\log(2\pi)
. On using Eq. (
56
), we finally get
\beta_{c}=2/J
, the known critical temperature for the mean-field
XY
model.

Now, we discuss the other limit
\beta\to\infty
, for which
one has
[
38
]

\displaystyle I_{0}(\beta r\sigma)\approx\frac{\exp{\left(\beta r\sigma\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta r\sigma}}.

(58)

Using the above result and taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
in Eq. (
51
), we obtain

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\sigma\exp{\left(-\frac{J^{2}m^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr~r^{2}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}I_{0}\left(\frac{Jmr}{\sigma}\right),

(59)

which can be expanded around
m=0
in the spirit of the Landau theory by using Eq. (
52
), as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\sigma\int_{0}^{\infty}dr~r^{2}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\left[1+m^{2}\left(-\frac{J^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{2}r^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)+m^{4}\left(\frac{J^{4}}{8\sigma^{4}}+\frac{J^{4}r^{4}}{64\sigma^{4}}-\frac{J^{4}r^{2}}{8\sigma^{4}}\right)\right]+\ldots

\displaystyle=m^{2}\left(\frac{J}{2}-\frac{J^{2}}{4\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\right)+m^{4}\frac{J^{4}}{64\sigma^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}+\ldots.

(60)

By equating the coefficient of the
m^{2}
-term to zero, we obtain the critical disorder strength
\sigma_{c}=(J/2)\sqrt{\pi/2}\approx 0.6266J
, above which the system (being at
T=0
, it would be in its ground state) shows a continuous phase transition from a low-
\sigma
ferromagnetic to a high-
\sigma
paramagnetic phase, as shown in Fig.
3
. Schematic free-energy landscapes for different representative values of
\sigma
, shown in Fig.
4
, clearly indicate the existence of a continuous phase transition close to
\sigma=0.6266J
. This
\sigma_{c}
also serves as an upper bound on the disorder strength above which no ferromagnetic ordering in the system survives at any temperature.

Now, we turn to the discussion of the critical temperature at finite temperatures and disorder strengths. To proceed further, for given values of
T>0
and
\sigma>0
, we numerically integrate Eq. (
51
) to obtain the corresponding free-energy landscape as a function of
m
. We then look for the value of
0\leq m\leq 1
that minimizes the free energy for given
T
and
\sigma
. This minimizing
m
is the equilibrium magnetization value, and at the transition point, the equilibrium value becomes zero. From Fig.
5
, it is evident that the system undergoes a continuous phase transition in
m
versus
T
, just as in the case without disorder. However, the presence of disorder manifests itself by monotonically shrinking the temperature range over which the system remains ordered as the disorder strength increases.

III.0.2
Bimodal distribution

In this part, we discuss the case in which the disorder distribution is bimodal, Eq. (
4
). Equation (
47
) gives

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{4\beta}\left[\log{(2\pi I_{0}^{++})}+\log{(2\pi I_{0}^{+-})}\right]

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{4\beta}\left[\log{(2\pi I_{0}^{-+})}+\log{(2\pi I_{0}^{--})}\right].

(61)

with
I_{0}^{++}
,
I_{0}^{+-}
,
I_{0}^{-+}
,
I_{0}^{--}
denoting the cases
\{h^{x}=h_{0},h^{y}=h_{0}\}
,
\{h^{x}=h_{0},h^{y}=-h_{0}\}
,
\{h^{x}=-h_{0},h^{y}=h_{0}\}
, and
\{h^{x}=-h_{0},h^{y}=-h_{0}\}
, respectively; for example, we have
I_{0}^{+-}\equiv I_{0}\left(\beta\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(-h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}}\right)
.
Clearly, the bimodal distribution does not possess rotational symmetry, as in the case of Gaussian. This does not allow the free energy per spin in Eq. (
61
) to be written only in terms of
m=\sqrt{m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}}
. In passing, note that the expression in Eq. (
61
) remains invariant under the following transformations:

\displaystyle{}\begin{aligned} &(i)\quad m_{x}\to m_{y},\quad m_{y}\to m_{x},\\
&(ii)\quad m_{x}\to-m_{y},\quad m_{y}\to m_{x},\\
&(iii)\quad m_{x}\to-m_{y},\quad m_{y}\to-m_{x},\\
&(iv)\quad m_{x}\to m_{y},\quad m_{y}\to-m_{x},\\
&(v)\quad m_{x}\to-m_{y},\quad m_{y}\to-m_{x}.\end{aligned}

(62)

Let us first discuss the zero-temperature limit, i.e., the limit
\beta\to\infty
. Using
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}I_{0}(\beta x)=\exp{\left(\beta x\right)}/{\sqrt{2\pi\beta x}}

[
38
]
, one gets

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left[f^{++}+f^{+-}+f^{-+}+f^{--}\right],

(63)

with

\displaystyle f^{++}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}},

(64)

\displaystyle f^{+-}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(-h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}},

(65)

\displaystyle f^{-+}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{(-h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}},

(66)

\displaystyle f^{--}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{(-h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(-h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}}.

(67)

It is worth mentioning that the symmetries mentioned in Eq. (
62
) also hold for the zero-temperature free energy (the ground-state free energy) in Eq. (
63
). These symmetries manifest themselves in the landscape of the ground-state free energy as a function of
(m_{x},m_{y})
for all values of
h_{0}
, as evident from Fig.
6
, where we see that the white dashed lines divide the landscape into regions in which the free energy has identical behavior. More precisely, there is discrete rotational symmetry present in the landscape, as opposed to the continuous rotational symmetry present when the disorder distribution is Gaussian.

To obtain the ground-state magnetization
m\equiv\sqrt{m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}}
from the zero-temperature free-energy landscape, we use the discrete rotational symmetries mentioned above and numerically extract the minimizing
(m_{x},m_{y})
coordinates by restricting ourselves only to the region bounded by the two orange lines in Fig.
6
, together with the constraint
\sqrt{m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}}\leq 1
. We find that the ground-state magnetization undergoes a first-order transition between ferromagnetic and paramagnetic phases with increasing disorder strength
h_{0}
, captured by the sudden jump of magnetization at
h_{0}^{c}\approx 0.4422J
in Fig.
7
.

Let us now discuss the finite-temperature equilibrium properties of the system. In Fig.
8
, we show the free-energy behavior in the
(m_{x},m_{y})
-plane for representative values of
h_{0}
and
T
. To obtain the equilibrium magnetization at finite temperatures, we follow the same procedure as followed above. Namely, using the symmetries mentioned in Eq. (
62
), we find the equilibrium magnetization
m=\sqrt{m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}}
by extracting the
(m_{x},m_{y})
-coordinates that minimize the free energy in Eq. (
61
). In Fig
9
, we show the behavior of equilibrium magnetization
m
versus
T
, for several values of
h_{0}
. One observes that with increasing
h_{0}
, a transition that was continuous becomes first order, indicated respectively by the absence and presence of a jump in the value of
m
. The phase diagram in the
(h_{0},T)
-plane is shown in Fig.
10
, in which one may observe a continuous and a first-order transition line between a ferromagnetic and a paramagnetic phase joining at a tricritical point
(h_{0}^{\mathrm{tri}},T^{\mathrm{tri}})\approx(0.3512J,0.3006J)
.

IV
Phase transitions in the
n=3
case

We now consider the effect of disorder on the critical point for the case
n=3
, i.e., the case of classical Heisenberg spins. The spin components for this case can be written as
(S^{x},S^{y},S^{z})=(\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi},\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi},\cos{\theta})
, with
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
and
0\leq\phi<2\pi
. The free energy per spin for this case is obtained from Eq. (
17
) as

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{J(m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}+m_{z}^{2})}{2}

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{\beta}\int P(\vec{h})d\vec{h}\log\left[\int d\vec{S}~\exp\left(\beta\vec{S}\cdot(\vec{h}+J\vec{m})\right)\right],

(68)

with
\vec{m}=(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})
.

Using the identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\exp{(a\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}+b\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}+c\cos{\theta})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sinh{\left(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}}\right)}}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}}},

(69)

we get

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{J(m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}+m_{z}^{2})}{2}

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{\beta}\int P(\vec{h})d\vec{h}\log\left[4\pi\frac{\sinh{\left(\beta\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha=x,y,x}(h^{(\alpha)}+Jm_{\alpha})^{2}}\right)}}{\beta\sqrt{\sum_{\alpha=x,y,x}(h^{(\alpha)}+Jm_{\alpha})^{2}}}\right].

(70)

As for
n=2
, we consider now the distributions (
3
) and (
4
) for the disordered fields.

IV.0.1
Gaussian distribution

Using Eq. (
3
), we get

\displaystyle P(\vec{h})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}~\exp{\left(-\frac{{h^{x}}^{2}+{h^{y}}^{2}+{h^{z}}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)},

(71)

with
-\infty\leq h^{x},h^{y},h^{z}\leq\infty
. Proceeding as for the case
n=2
, we define
v_{x}\equiv h^{x}+Jm_{x},~v_{y}\equiv h^{y}+Jm_{y},~v^{z}\equiv h^{z}+Jm^{z}
, and subsequently move to the spherical polar coordinates:
(v_{x},v_{y},v_{z})=(r\sin{\tilde{\theta}}\cos{\tilde{\phi}},r\sin{\tilde{\theta}}\sin{\tilde{\phi}},r\cos{\tilde{\theta}})
, with
r\in[0,\infty)
,
0\leq\tilde{\theta}\leq\pi
,
0\leq\tilde{\phi}<2\pi
. Then, under the rescaling
r\to r/\sigma
, we obtain from Eq. (
70
) the rotationally-invariant free energy per spin as

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}}{\beta}\exp{\left(-\frac{J^{2}m^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}~dr~\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle~~~~\times\log{\left[\frac{4\pi\sinh{(\beta r\sigma)}}{\beta r\sigma}\right]}~\frac{\sinh{(\frac{Jrm}{\sigma})}}{\frac{Jrm}{\sigma}},

(72)

with
m=\sqrt{m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}+m_{z}^{2}}
.

Following the standard prescription of Landau theory, we expand the free-energy expression in Eq. (
72
) around
m=0
. Using for small
m
that

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh{\left(\frac{Jrm}{\sigma}\right)}}{\frac{Jrm}{\sigma}}=1+\frac{J^{2}m^{2}r^{2}}{6\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}r^{4}}{120\sigma^{4}}+\ldots,

(73)

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}~dr~\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}\log{\left[\frac{4\pi\sinh{(\beta r\sigma)}}{\beta r\sigma}\right]}

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\frac{J^{2}m^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}}{8\sigma^{4}}+\ldots\right]\left[1+\frac{J^{2}m^{2}r^{2}}{6\sigma^{2}}+\frac{J^{4}m^{4}r^{4}}{120\sigma^{4}}+\ldots\right]

(74)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}[r^{2}]}{\beta}+m^{2}\left(\frac{J}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}}{\beta}\frac{J^{2}[r^{4}]}{6\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}}{\beta}\frac{J^{2}[r^{2}]}{2\sigma^{2}}+\ldots\right)

\displaystyle+m^{4}\left(-\frac{J^{4}[r^{6}]}{60\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\pi}\beta\sigma^{4}}+\frac{J^{4}[r^{4}]}{6\sqrt{2\pi}\beta\sigma^{4}}-\frac{J^{4}[r^{2}]}{4\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\pi}\beta\sigma^{4}}+\ldots\right)

(75)

\displaystyle=a_{o}(T)+m^{2}a_{2}(T)+m^{4}a_{4}(T)+\ldots,

(76)

where we have used the definition

\displaystyle[r^{k}]\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r~\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}\log{\left[\frac{4\pi\sinh{(\beta r\sigma)}}{\beta r\sigma}\right]}.

(77)

The critical temperature
T_{c}=1/\beta_{c}
is then obtained as

\displaystyle a_{2}(T=T_{c})=0,

(78)

yielding

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}J}{\sigma^{2}}\Big([r^{2}]-\frac{[r^{4}]}{3}\Big).

(79)

Let us now discuss the limit
\sigma\to 0
. In this limit, we have

\displaystyle\log{\left[\frac{4\pi\sinh{(\beta r\sigma)}}{\beta r\sigma}\right]}\approx\log(4\pi)+\frac{1}{6}\beta^{2}r^{2}\sigma^{2}-\frac{1}{180}\sigma^{4}\left(\beta^{4}r^{4}\right),

(80)

yielding

[r^{2}]=(1/12)\sqrt{\pi/2}\left(-\beta^{4}\sigma^{4}+6\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}+12\log(4\pi)\right)
, and
[r^{4}]=(1/12)\sqrt{\pi/2}\left(-7\beta^{4}\sigma^{4}+30\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}+36\log(4\pi)\right)
. On using Eq. (
79
), we get
\beta_{c}=3/J
, the known critical temperature for the mean-field Heisenberg model.

Now, we discuss the other limit
\beta\to\infty
, for which one has
\sinh{(\beta r\sigma)}\approx\exp{\left(\beta r\sigma\right)}
. Using this asymptotic behavior and taking the limit
\beta\to\infty
in Eq. (
72
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}=\frac{Jm^{2}}{2}-\sigma^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}~\exp{\left(-\frac{J^{2}m^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}}{Jm}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}~dr~\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}\sinh{\left(\frac{Jrm}{\sigma}\right)},

(81)

which can be expanded around
m=0
in the spirit of the Landau theory as

\displaystyle{}\frac{F}{N}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sigma+m^{2}\left(\frac{J}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}J^{2}}{3\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle+m^{4}\left(\frac{4\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}J^{4}}{15\sigma^{3}}-\frac{J^{4}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{3}}\right)+m^{6}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}J^{6}}{5\sigma^{5}}-\frac{J^{6}}{4\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{5}}\right).

(82)

By equating the coefficient of the
m^{2}
-term to zero, we obtain the critical disorder strength
\sigma_{c}=(2/3)\sqrt{2/\pi}J=0.5319J
, above which the system (being at
T=0
, it would be in its ground state) shows a continuous phase transition from a low-
\sigma
ferromagnetic to a high-
\sigma
paramagnetic phase, as shown in Fig.
11
. This
\sigma_{c}
may also be interpreted as an upper bound on the disorder strength above which no ferromagnetic ordering in the system survives at any temperature.

IV.0.2
Bimodal distribution

For this case, the disorder distribution is given by

\displaystyle P(h_{x},h_{y},h_{z})=\prod_{\alpha=x,y,z}\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta(h^{(\alpha)}+h_{0})+\delta(h^{(\alpha)}-h_{0})\right).

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{F}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{J(m_{x}^{2}+m_{y}^{2}+m_{z}^{2})}{2}-

where the second term on the right hand side contains contributions from all possible combinations of

\displaystyle f^{+++}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh{\left(\beta\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{z})^{2}}\right)}}{\beta\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{z})^{2}}},

\displaystyle f^{+-+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh{\left(\beta\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(-h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{z})^{2}}\right)}}{\beta\sqrt{(h_{0}+Jm_{x})^{2}+(-h_{0}+Jm_{y})^{2}+(h_{0}+Jm_{z})^{2}}},

V
Absence of self-averaging near criticality

An intriguing property prevalent in typical quenched-disordered systems is the absence of self-averaging close to a critical point, implying thereby that extensive thermodynamic variables exhibit strong sample-to-sample fluctuations near the critical point. Several detailed studies on this phenomenon for systems with disordered couplings can be found in Refs.
[
9
,
10
,
39
]
. For a finite system of size
N
, the degree of self-averaging for a thermodynamic variable
X
in equilibrium at temperature
T
can be assessed by analyzing the quantity

\displaystyle R_{X}(T)\equiv\frac{\overline{X^{2}(T)}-\overline{X(T)}^{2}}{\overline{X(T)}^{2}},

(90)

where the overline denotes averaging over disorder realizations. Examining the exponent of power-law scaling
R_{X}(T)
with
N
,
R_{X}(T)\sim N^{-z}
, provides insights into the self-averaging behavior. Specifically, (i) if one has
0<z<1
, the system is said to be weakly self-averaging; (ii) if one has
z\geq 1
, the system is strongly self-averaging; (iii) if, on the other hand, one has
z\leq 0
, the system is non-self-averaging, implying that sample-to-sample fluctuations persist even in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. It is worth noting that the critical temperature
T_{c}
in a disordered system depends on specific disorder realizations, and, consequently, a detailed study of the distribution of
T_{c}
is also desirable.

In the light of the above discussion, we now present a detailed numerical analysis of the self-averaging property of two thermodynamic variables, namely, the susceptibility
\chi(T)
and the magnetization
m(T)
, for our Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) with
n=2
(
XY
spins) and Gaussian random fields, based on Monte Carlo simulation results (for details on the algorithm, see Appendix
A
). We consider here
J=1
. In our simulations, we use for a given realization of the disordered fields the following definition of the susceptibility:

\displaystyle\chi(T)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\beta[\langle M^{2}\rangle(0,T)-(\langle M\rangle(0,T))^{2}],

(91)

where
M\equiv\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left((S_{i}^{x})^{2}+(S_{i}^{y})^{2}\right)}
is the un-normalized magnetization of a spin configuration; here
\langle M\rangle(h,T)
denotes average magnetization in canonical equilibrium at temperature
T
and in presence of a magnetic field of strength
h
, see Appendix
B
.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we studied the canonical-equilibrium properties of Random
Field
O(n)
Models in presence of mean-field interactions and
disordered fields that are either Gaussian-distributed or sampled from a symmetric bimodal distribution and acting on individual spins. On the basis of an explicit expression for the free energy per spin of the system, derived using the mean-field approximation applicable for any disorder distribution without a broad tail and the replica trick, applicable when the disordered fields are Gaussian-distributed, we studied the phase transition behavior for two representative choices of
n
, namely,
n=2
(
XY
spins) and
n=3
(Heisenberg spins). For both the cases, we demonstrated that the magnetization exhibits a continuous phase transition as a function of temperature when the disorder distribution is Gaussian. Contrarily and remarkably, when the distribution is bimodal, the phase transition could be either continuous or first-order with an emergent tricriticality, which we characterize analytically. In addition to locating critical and tricritical points for different disorder distributions, our work reveals strong non-self-averaging behaviour near continuous phase transitions—an aspect that is not only nontrivial but also of independent interest in the study of disordered systems. We note that the emergence of tricriticality for a bimodal disorder distribution in the case of
O(n)
spins can be traced back to the result in Ref.
[
20
]
, where a renormalization group (RG) analysis of the Random Field Ising Model (RFIM) demonstrated that tricriticality generically arises when the disorder distribution is symmetric and possesses a minimum at zero. A similar RG treatment tailored to our model is beyond the scope of the present work and is left for future investigation.

In passing, we remark that our results are of potential experimental relevance for certain classes of layered magnets, such as
(\mathrm{C_{2}}\mathrm{H}_{5}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{2}\mathrm{Cu}\mathrm{Cl}_{4}
, which is known to be effectively described by a one-dimensional continuous spin Hamiltonian with both mean-field and local interactions
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
. Furthermore, the development of highly-controllable cavity-QED platforms has enabled the realization of long-range interacting Hamiltonians, including the Hamiltonian Mean-Field (HMF) model involving
XY
spins
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. Incorporating disorder in such setups is now within experimental reach. In view of these advances, our results can be either directly tested or taken to serve as a theoretical guide for exploring phase diagrams of experimentally-accessible long-range systems with impurities.

As follow-ups, it is of immediate interest to investigate whether the critical exponents of the bare dynamics (the one in the absence of disorder) get affected by the presence of disorder. In the context when the couplings are disordered and between nearest-neighbor spins, the celebrated Harris criterion states that when the embedding dimension
d
of the system and the critical exponent
\nu
of the correlation length have values such that
d\geq 2/\nu
, the critical behavior of the bare model remains unaffected by the presence of disorder. Whether such a statement also holds for the model of our study is an interesting open direction to pursue. Another issue of importance concerns the equivalence of statistical ensembles in equilibrium, which is not guaranteed when the system constituents have long-range interactions that include mean-field interactions considered in this work. This question has been thoroughly addressed for both classical and quantum long-range systems, albeit in the absence of any disorder
[
46
]
, and hence, it would be of great interest to pursue the study of how disorder may affect the equivalence or inequivalence of equilibrium ensembles for long-range systems.

Another interesting follow-up would be to understand the emergence of long-range order in continuous spin systems beyond the Imry-Ma argument
[
15
]
, which rules out such order below dimension
d\leq 4
for nearest-neighbour Hamiltonians with random fields. A natural extension would be to consider the class of
O(n)
spin models on finite-dimensional lattices, where the interactions decay with distance as a power law,
\sim 1/r^{\alpha},~\alpha>0
, and investigate whether long-range order can emerge under such conditions, and if so, over what range of
\alpha
the results predicted here for mean-field Hamiltonians apply. We conclude by noting that a recent study along these lines was conducted in Ref.
[
47
]
for a one-dimensional power-law diluted Hamiltonian involving
XY
spins and random fields. Extending such analyses to higher
n
and to dimensions
d=2,3
presents a promising direction for future research.
