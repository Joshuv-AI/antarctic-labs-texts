CPAP and inflammation: villain or good guy?
Authors: Liliane P de Souza Mendes, Rafaela Garcia Santos de Andrade, Rodrigo Pinto Pedrosa, Flávia Baggio Nerbass
Published: 2024

Comment

We would like to congratulate Peker and collaborators for opening the discussion whether obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) therapy with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) can cause endothelial inflammation. In their recent published study, patients treated with median CPAP levels >7 cm H2O exhibited higher angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) levels compared to those treated with lower pressures.1 Upon closer examination of the results, however, we believe that certain considerations need to be addressed.

The authors introduced the topic by referencing two previous randomised clinical trials that failed to demonstrate cardiovascular risk reduction in participants with good CPAP adherence.2,3 However, more recently, sub analysis studies from these large trials have shown positive effects of CPAP when hypoxic burden has been used to stratify participants,4 and either when CPAP was used by participants at least 4 h/day.5 Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis evaluated the effect of CPAP treatment on risk of recurrent major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), with positive results favouring CPAP.6

Patients with OSA typically experience arousals, which may serve as an additional factor contributing to sympathetic over activity, potentially exacerbating inflammation. In the present study, despite the similarity of the groups in terms of OSA severity, characterised by the apnoea-hypopnea index (AHI) and levels of hypoxemia, details regarding number of arousals of participants were not provided. It is known that the algorithm-driven increase in CPAP level has been associated with arousals during sleep. As participants were randomly assigned to either auto-titrating CPAP or usual care, this information becomes relevant.7

Important treatment-related factors such as unintentional leakage and the type of interface used were also not provided by the authors. The absence of this data is noteworthy, as they play a role in assessing the efficacy and adequacy of CPAP therapy. Despite achieving a seemingly well-controlled residual AHI (1st–3rd quartiles) 4.4 (3.3–7.1) events/hour, it is essential to ascertain whether participants were effectively managed and adequately adapted to CPAP therapy.

It is also important to highlight that in the CPAP group, there were significantly more participants with hypertension, and type 2 diabetes. These conditions are associated with renal, mitochondrial and sodium channels dysfunction, more oxidative stress, and inflammation.8 Furthermore, both diseases increase antinatriuretic hormones such as angiotensin II and produce more inflammatory cytokines, contributing to vascular injury and inflammation.9

Among participants randomised to CPAP therapy, the median CPAP pressure was 7.0 cm H2O. According to the authors, CPAP levels exceeding 7 cm H2O were linked to a higher incidence of composite cardiovascular outcomes as compared to levels <7 cm H2O (Hazard Ratio [HR] 2.81; 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 1.22–6.44, p = 0.021; adjusted for age, sex, Body Mass Index [BMI], and severity of OSA). However, it is important to note that these findings were not adjusted for the presence of type 2 diabetes or for CPAP usage per night. The current literature defines good CPAP adherence as the use of the device for at least 4 h per night on 70% of nights within the first 90 days of therapy. In the present study, the negative results for the CPAP group may be linked to the low median CPAP adherence, represented by the first to third quartiles, was 3.5 (0.0–5.7) hours/night with a median residual AHI of 4.4 (3.3–7.1) events/hour. A recent meta analysis including 4186 patients showed that whereas the results based on intention to treat reported no effect of CPAP treatment, the adherent use of CPAP treatment (≥4 h/day) was associated with a reduced recurrence risk of major adverse cardiac or cerebrovascular events.6 Furthermore, considering that OSA typically manifests throughout the entire night, this low compliance to CPAP may not be adequate to validate the current findings.

In this study, the authors recommended the use of more conservative CPAP levels for controlling daytime symptoms, even if they do not eliminate all apnoeas and hypopneas, to minimise potential side effects from lung hyperinflation. However, it is important to consider that residual higher AHI and hypoxemia may persist, contributing to inflammation and other negative effects of OSA.

Additionally, the authors provided no total sleep time data of the participants. There is growing evidence that sleep deprivation is associated with inflammation,10 which subsequently triggers the expression of Ang-2 in endothelial cells. We believe that some additional discussion on the points raised above could provide important information for future work in this area.
