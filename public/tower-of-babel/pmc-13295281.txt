Adult Gait Kinematics and Muscle Strength After Childhood Pemberton Osteotomy for Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip
Authors: Aziz Demirci, Bilal Karabak, Ferhat Yıldırım Hoyur, Azat Dzhumukov, Ali Aydın, Orhan Karsan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the long-term functional and kinematic outcomes of patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) who underwent Pemberton osteotomy (PO) during childhood and to compare their adult hip motion, muscle strength, and gait characteristics with those of healthy controls.Methods:This retrospective cross-sectional study included 12 adult patients (mean age 18.6 years) who underwent PO for DDH (6 bilateral, 6 unilateral) and 15 healthy controls (mean age 17.1 years). Hip range of motion (ROM), Harris Hip Score (HHS), and isokinetic muscle strength were evaluated. Three-dimensional gait and running analyses were performed using a motion capture system at 3, 6, and 9 km/h.Results:Mean HHSs were 92.8 ± 4.2 and 92.7 ± 5.1 in the bilateral and unilateral groups, respectively, and 100 in controls (P < .001). Hip ROM showed no significant inter- or intra-group differences. Isokinetic testing revealed reduced hip flexion total work in the bilateral (116 ± 114 J) and unilateral (181 ± 42 J) groups compared with controls (340 ± 143 J; P = .007). Similarly, abduction strength in the bilateral group (75 ± 52 J) was lower than in controls (143 ± 73 J; P < .05). During slow walking (3 km/h), the unilateral group had a shorter stance time (0.30-0.32 s) than the healthy group (P < .01), while gait symmetry was comparable across all groups during fast walking and running.Conclusion:Patients who underwent early PO and appropriate rehabilitation achieved normal gait and running kinematics in adulthood despite mild flexion and abduction strength deficits. These findings demonstrate successful long-term adaptation and highlight the importance of timely surgical correction in DDH for preserving functional capacity.Level of Evidence:Level III, Retrospective Comparative Study.

Introduction

Developmental hip dysplasia (DDH) represents a dynamic disease that disrupts the normal relationship between the structures that form the hip joint. The term “Congenital Hip Dislocation” was replaced by “Developmental Hip Dysplasia” by Klisic and Synder in 1989-1990 following recognition that infants without hip dislocation at birth can progress from dysplasia to subluxation and dislocation in time.1,2 The DDH describes a wide range of hip anatomical disorders that exist at birth and appear in infants and children.3

The most frequent reason for early-onset hip osteoarthritis is DDH. 4 Screening programs enable early detection of DDH so that primarily conservative treatment methods can be implemented.5 The treatment of choice for patients who do not respond to conservative care includes closed reduction with hip spica cast under general anesthesia and open reduction when the first method fails.6,7

Among various surgical methods, the Pemberton osteotomy (PO) described by Paul A. Pemberton in 1965 has proven to be safe and effective.7 This incomplete osteotomy technique is an acetabuloplasty procedure that allows the acetabular roof to better cover the femoral head, preserves the integrity of the pelvic ring, and allows bilateral applications in a single session.

The failure to treat DDH or the delay in its treatment results in serious disability and permanent hip problems. The quality of life of patients in adulthood depends heavily on the timing and completeness of correct treatment.8 The literature lacks sufficient studies that assess the functional outcomes of DDH patients who underwent surgery during adulthood, particularly regarding walking/running kinematics and hip circumference muscle strength. The absence of research on running-related kinematic outcomes creates challenges for understanding the functional abilities of this patient population.

The main objective of this study was to assess hip function, walking/running kinematics, and peri-hip muscle strength in patients with DDH who underwent PO in the pediatric age group. Two hypotheses were tested: (1) After appropriate surgical procedure and effective rehabilitation, the adult hip function of patients with DDH who underwent PO in the pediatric age will be close to that of healthy individuals; (2) These patients will have minimal changes in peri-hip muscle strength and walking/running pattern in adulthood.

Material and methods

Study Population and Sample

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2022 and December 2023. The study population consisted of patients treated at a tertiary care Erzurum Atatürk University Research Hospital. The sample size was calculated as at least 6 individuals for each group as a result of the power analysis performed to determine the expected difference between healthy individuals and operated individuals in hip flexion and abduction isokinetic muscle strength parameters with 80% power and a 5% margin of error. Patients who underwent PO for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) at pediatric age (under 5 years of age) and are currently in adulthood (15-23 years) were included in the study. Inclusion criteria were unilateral or bilateral surgery, regular postoperative follow-up in the hospital, and body mass index less than 40 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria were defined as the presence of additional neuromuscular diseases other than DDH, closed reduction only, additional osteotomies in addition to PO, other orthopedic surgeries affecting walking and running, presence of externally visible deformity (kyphosis, scoliosis), limb length difference of more than 2 cm, use of crutches, and the presence of avascular necrosis or severe arthrosis of the femoral head.

Data Collection

The data collection process was carried out in 3 stages. In the first stage, demographic data (age, gender, height, weight, body mass index) of the participants was recorded and hip range of motion (ROM) (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal and external rotation) was measured with a goniometer. Harris Hip Score (HHS) and Visual Analogue Scale evaluations were performed. In the second stage, walking/running analyses were carried out in the Sports Laboratory. This analysis aimed to comprehensively assess both low and high intensity activities (walking and running) as a unique aspect of the study. A 3-dimensional motion analysis system with 9 cameras capable of measuring 240 Hz, manufactured by Qualisys AB in Gothenburg, Sweden, was used for the analysis. Participants were assessed on a treadmill at 3 different speeds (3 km/h slow walking, 6 km/h fast walking, and 9 km/h running) with 19 × 17 mm diameter reflective markers placed on 35 important points of the body. Approximately 30 seconds were recorded for each speed. The biomechanical aspects of treadmill-based gait analysis differ from overground walking because of reduced proprioceptive feedback and wind resistance, yet it enables researchers to conduct standardized and controlled tests for group comparisons. Previous studies have shown that kinematic patterns remain largely similar between treadmill and overground locomotion, particularly for the parameters measured in this study.9 In the third stage, a dynamometer device (IsoMed 2000, D&R Ferstl, Hemnau, Germany) that can take bilateral measurements at an angular speed of 60°/sec was used for hip isokinetic muscle strength measurement. Hip flexion/extension and abduction/adduction forces were measured in 5 repetitions. The reliability of isokinetic measurements was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). The test-retest reliability demonstrated high consistency because ICC values for all measured parameters fell between 0.87 and 0.93.

Surgical Technique

The subjects in the study were divided into 3 groups: bilaterally operated group (B, n = 6), unilaterally operated group (U, n = 6), and healthy control group (H, n = 15). Pemberton osteotomy is an acetabuloplasty technique performed in patients with DDH through an anterior approach. A full-thickness osteotomy was started over the spina iliaca anterior inferior and continued downward and posteriorly until the Y cartilage of the acetabulum. When the osteotomy line was spaced with bone graft, the acetabular roof shifted forward and outward to cover the femoral head more. All operations were performed by the same surgical team with similar protocols, and postoperative rehabilitation programs were standardized. The mean age of the operated patients was 27.3 months (14-50 months), and the mean postoperative follow-up period was 196 months (150-244 months).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Medcalc 20.2 version (Medcalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) software. The normality of the data was evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk test. A paired sample t-test was used to determine the differences between the right and left sides of the groups. Statistical differences between the groups were evaluated by 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A post-hoc Tukey–Kramer test was used to determine which group was responsible for the difference. Data management was not performed because there were no missing data. The results of all 3 groups were compared between themselves and within groups. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. P values were calculated by 2-way tests and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Atatürk University Ethics Committee with meeting number 6 and decision number 1 on October 26, 2023. All participants received written informed consent before the study began, and participants under 18 needed parental or legal guardian consent in addition to their own consent. The researchers anonymized all personal data through coding procedures to protect patient privacy. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The authors maintain documentation of informed consent, which they keep separate from the journal submission to protect patient confidentiality.

Results

Twelve patients and 15 healthy individuals were included in the study. Six of the patients had undergone surgery for bilateral and 6 for unilateral DDH. When the demographic characteristics of the participants were analyzed, the mean age of the bilaterally operated group was 18.8 years, the unilaterally operated group was 18.5 years, and the healthy group was 17.1 years. The patients had undergone PO at a mean age of 27.3 months (range: 14-50 months), with a mean postoperative follow-up period of 196 months (range: 150-244 months) at the time of this evaluation. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of age, gender, height, weight, and body mass index. The mean HHS was 92.8 ± 4.2 in the bilateral group, 92.7 ± 5.1 in the unilateral group, and 100 ± 0 in the healthy group (P < .001). Pain scores measured before and during the test showed a minimal increase in the patient group, while no pain was recorded in the healthy group (Table 1).

When hip ROM was compared, no statistically significant difference was found between the right and left hips in flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation movements both between the groups and within the same group. Even in the unilaterally operated patient group, there was no significant limitation in terms of ROM between the operated and unoperated hips (Table 2).

In isokinetic muscle strength evaluations, the total work (TW) and average power values of both the left and right hips of the healthy group in flexion movement were found to be higher compared to both operated groups. Especially in the left hip, a statistically significant difference was found between the healthy group and bilateral and unilateral operated groups (P < .05). There was no significant difference between the groups in extension movement. In abduction movement, the TW value measured in the left hip of the healthy group (143 ± 73 J) was significantly higher than the left hip of the bilaterally operated group (75 ± 52.6 J) (P < .05). In the unilaterally operated group, there was a significant decrease in all parameters of the operated left hip in adduction movement compared to the unoperated right hip (P < .05) (Table 3).

In the walking and running tests, the stance time (0.30-0.32 s) of the unilaterally operated group was significantly shorter than the healthy group (0.40-0.42 s) and the bilaterally operated group (0.42-0.38 s) only in slow walking (3 km/h) (P < .01). During fast walking (6 km/h) and running (9 km/h), no significant difference was found between the groups in terms of stride length and stance time. While stride width tended to decrease with increasing speed, this decrease was significant in the unilaterally operated group (P < .05). The number of steps per minute (cadence) increased significantly in all groups (P < .001) as expected with increasing speed (Table 4).

In pelvic kinematic evaluations, a significant difference was found in pelvic obliquity values between the right and left step during slow walking and running in the healthy group (p<0.05). This asymmetry can be considered a part of normal gait mechanics in healthy individuals. No such difference was observed in the patient groups. There was no significant difference in pelvic tilt values between the groups and between the right and left steps within the groups. In terms of pelvic rotation, a significant difference was found between the right and left steps during slow walking in the healthy group and during running in the unilaterally operated group (p<0.05). Pelvic height increased significantly with increasing speed in all groups (p<0.001), but no significant difference was found between the groups (Table 5) (Figures 1 and 2).

Figure 3 illustrates the pelvic tilt changes across different walking and running speeds for all 3 groups. The graph demonstrates that while all groups showed an increase in anterior pelvic tilt with increasing speed, the healthy group exhibited greater pelvic tilt values compared to both operated groups. The bilateral and unilateral operated groups displayed similar patterns, with minimal asymmetry between right and left sides across all speeds. These findings suggest that patients who underwent PO developed compensatory mechanisms that resulted in more symmetric but reduced pelvic tilt motion compared to healthy controls.

Discussion

This study examined the long-term outcomes of patients who underwent PO for DDH during their pediatric years. The patients maintained typical ROM, but their strength measurements remained 30%-50% below normal in flexion and 40%-45% below normal in abduction because the surgical procedure damaged the abductor mechanism, which disrupted growth patterns. The walking and running analysis showed that patients developed speed-related movement patterns, which became symmetrical when they increased their speed. The central nervous system developed effective motor compensation strategies for dynamic activities during the 16-year follow-up period because patients showed speed-dependent normalization of their movements.

The absence of significant hip ROM differences between operated patients and healthy controls demonstrates successful preservation of joint function through PO, consistent with Liu et al10 and Ari and Aydin11 . The HHSs (92.7 ± 5.0) indicate good functional outcomes,12 similar to Aslam et al13 (2023) who reported CHOHES scores of 96.6. However, the significant strength deficits in flexion and abduction muscles observed in the study are concerning. The hip flexion strength of patients with PO decreased by 54% (175 ± 115J vs. 381 ± 178J) and abduction strength decreased by 41% (99.8 ± 66.8J vs. 170 ± 72J) compared to healthy controls. The study results show that joint structure preservation does not prevent muscle function impairment in the joint areas. The findings suggest that healthcare providers need to create targeted treatment plans and rehabilitation methods for patients with this condition.

The isokinetic evaluation revealed significant strength deficits in flexion and abduction, contrasting with findings from periacetabular osteotomy studies. While Servant et al14 reported only 9%-11% strength loss after hip surgery, a 40%-54% reduction was identified in the present findings. O’Brien et al15 found preserved strength after PAO, suggesting that the timing of intervention (early childhood vs. adolescence) and surgical technique significantly impact muscle outcomes. The technical approaches between childhood iliac osteotomies show significant differences during comparison. The PO technique includes a partial iliac cut that reaches the triradiate cartilage to maintain pelvic ring stability and enable simultaneous procedures on both sides without needing fixation. The Salter osteotomy method involves full iliac bone division with bone graft placement and internal stabilization for superior anterior-lateral coverage, although it restricts bilateral surgical procedures.16 The Dega osteotomy procedure serves to treat neuromuscular dysplasia with posterior instability by performing an incomplete posterior-lateral acetabular reorientation. 17 The recent meta-analyses showed positive results for 84% of patients who underwent Salter osteotomy, 86% of patients who underwent the Dega procedure, and 91% of patients who underwent Pemberton procedure, with Pemberton producing the best radiological and clinical outcomes.18 This greater muscle compromise with Pemberton, despite the theoretical advantages of maintained pelvic stability, may result from more extensive periosteal stripping during the incomplete osteotomy. The results indicate that surgeons should base their selection between surgical options on both the expected functional results and the desired anatomical corrections.

The shorter stance time at slow walking speeds in unilaterally operated patients aligns with Lee et al19 and Wang et al20 . The study shows successful long-term adaptation because patients achieved normal gait parameter values when walking at 9 km/h. The neuromuscular system develops speed-specific compensation techniques that help patients overcome their strength limitations. The patients reached control-like kinematics during running at speeds where they had 40%-50% strength deficits, which proves their strong adaptive abilities. The 16-year follow-up period allowed patients to develop new motor unit recruitment methods and improved neuromuscular coordination, which enabled them to achieve normal function during dynamic activities. Patients maintain normal function during dynamic activities through elastic energy storage mechanisms and momentum, even though they experience difficulties with slow controlled movements that need sustained muscle activation.

Pelvic kinematic evaluations revealed more symmetric movement patterns in operated patients compared to healthy controls, consistent with compensatory adaptations described by Pizones and García-Rey 21 and Yokoi et al22. The absence of radiographic data prevents us from understanding if the observed symmetry results from proper anatomical correction or from compensatory body movements that hide remaining dysplasia. The absence of radiographic parameters including acetabular index and center-edge angle, and extrusion index makes it impossible to determine the extent of successful anatomical correction versus compensatory mechanisms that hide remaining dysplasia. The absence of radiographic parameters makes it impossible to determine the actual success rate of the surgical method when compared to other osteotomy techniques.

Unilateral surgery patients showed significant asymmetries in stance time and muscle strength between operated and non-operated sides, while bilateral patients demonstrated more symmetric outcomes, consistent with Bravi et al23 and Wang et al20 . Alsiddiky et al24and Miller et al25support bilateral approaches for maintaining symmetry. The superior symmetry in bilateral cases occurs because both limbs develop simultaneously during essential developmental stages, which prevents compensatory pattern formation that happens when 1 hip remains dysplastic.

The research contains 3 main limitations because it used a small participant group of 12 people and conducted its study at 1 location without performing radiographic assessments. The absence of acetabular index, center-edge angle, and extrusion index measurements prevents us from studying structural changes in PO and establishing connections between functional changes and anatomical correction. The study lacks preoperative data, which prevents researchers from determining the degree of improvement. Future research needs to include radiographic assessments and direct evaluation of different osteotomy methods (Pemberton vs. Salter vs. Dega) and studies with multiple centers and larger participant numbers.
