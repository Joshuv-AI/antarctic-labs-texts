Three-Dimensional Printing in Pediatric Orthopaedics: Cost Analysis and Emerging Applications
Authors: Cameron Jeffers, Michael G Galvez, Edward Oates, Romie F Gibly, Matthew A Halanski, Kyle J Miller, Vidyadhar Upasani, Melissa Esparza, Monica Payares-Lizano, Kareem Hassan, David Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional printing (3DP) has become an established adjunct in orthopaedic surgery. Its integration into planning, education, and intraoperative execution has evolved from isolated case reports to measurable clinical outcomes. Recent research highlights reductions in operative time, blood loss, and radiation exposure when three-dimensional printed guides or models are used, with the clearest benefits in deformity correction and spinal instrumentation. Applications now extend to patient-specific instruments, custom implants, and orthotic fabrication. Emerging work on biodegradable metals and porous or lattice structures signals a move toward biologically integrated implants. While clinical data support 3DP’s utility for understanding complex anatomy, the quality of evidence varies, and standardization remains limited. This review summarizes the technical workflow, current clinical evidence, and practical implications of 3DP in pediatric orthopaedic practice.Key Concepts(1)Three-dimensional printing (3DP) has rapidly grown in popularity in recent years, becoming an important tool in medicine.(2)Through the use of 3DP, complex orthopaedic models can easily and quickly be created resulting in reduced preoperative planning.(3)3D printed anatomic patient models have increased aesthetic and reconstructive outcomes in plastic surgery procedures.Keywords: 3D printing, Orthopedics, Pediatrics, Plastic surgery

Introduction

Pediatric orthopaedic surgery often involves anatomically complex cases in which intraoperative spatial judgment is crucial to success [1,2]. Deformities of the spine [3] and limbs [4], congenital malformations [5], trauma [6], and tumor resections typically require thorough preoperative analysis and surgical planning [7]. Three-dimensional printing (3DP) converts advanced imaging data into physical models and/or surgical guides that accurately represent patient anatomy [8]. Once limited to research, this technology now plays an active role in surgical planning and execution [9]. As printing hardware, materials, and segmentation software have advanced, applications have expanded across orthopaedic subspecialties [10].

Technical overview

The 3DP process begins with high-resolution computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data exported in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format. DICOM datasets are first imported into segmentation software (e.g., Mimics, 3D Slicer) to create an anatomical surface or solid; this step differs from printer “slicing,” which generates toolpaths for manufacturing [11,12]. For pediatric osseous models, threshold-based masks typically start around 150-200 Hounsfield unit (HU) and are refined with region growing and manual editing [12], especially near physes and unossified cartilage, where Hounsfield-based selection is less reliable [13]. Thin-slice CT (≤1 mm) with pediatric low-dose protocols optimizes geometric accuracy while reducing radiation exposure [[14], [15], [16], [17]]; MRI can assist with cartilage delineation but is susceptible to motion and may require sedation in young children. Additionally, three-dimensional (3D) volume data from specific MRI sequences can now be used to generate synthetic CT datasets for 3DP [13].

When metallic implants are present, metal artifact reduction and dedicated scatter removal tools (e.g., in Mimics) help reduce streaking; otherwise, manual cleanup is more extensive. Metallic implants can also be removed by adjusting the HUs to the lower metal threshold, typically below 3000 HU. The validated segmented mesh is exported as STL for smoothing and inspection [18] and then processed in printer-specific slicing software for FDM/SLA/SLS workflows prior to fabrication [12,19,20].

Segmentation of DICOM images isolates relevant tissues, which can then be converted into a surface model in STL format [11]. That file can be cropped, edited, cleaned, and smoothed using Slicer or mesh-editing software. Slicer or printer-specific software can then adapt the STL file for printing on fused deposition modeling [21], stereolithography [22], or selective laser sintering systems [[23], [24], [25], [26]].

Material selection depends on purpose: poly lactic acid filament is affordable, easy to print [27,28], and well suited for general anatomical visualization [29]; resin materials provide greater geometric fidelity [[30], [31], [32]]; and metal printing remains largely centralized, with implants or mechanical test specimens produced by external vendors. When models are intended for intraoperative use, sterilization must be validated for the specific material-printer workflow [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. Many resins tolerate low-temperature methods (ethylene oxide or hydrogen peroxide plasma) [32,[37], [38], [39]] but deform during steam treatment. Metals and high-temperature thermoplastics can be steam sterilized if processed properly [40]. Documentation of material selection, sterilization parameters, and postprocess verification is recommended. Institutional experience indicates that hospital-based “point-of-care” printing reduces turnaround time and allows surgeons to supervise the digital workflow directly [[41], [42], [43]].

Clinical applications

Preoperative planning and surgical simulation

Printed anatomic models enhance understanding of deformities or fractures before surgery (Fig. 1) [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]]. Studies have shown that using models for rehearsal and implant contouring reduces operative time and fluoroscopy use [53]. In one study, 3D printed fracture models improved procedural efficiency without affecting alignment outcomes [10,54]. A meta-analysis of 3DP in pediatric deformity correction found an average reduction of 21 min in surgical time and three fewer fluoroscopic exposures compared with traditional planning [53]. These benefits stem from improved anatomical and spatial awareness, precontoured implants, and more efficient intraoperative decisions [55]. Complex pelvic and acetabular reconstructions may particularly benefit from 3DP as it allows review of anatomy from multiple angles, enabling precise planning of osteotomies [56].

Patient-specific surgical guides

Several software programs, including Materialise (3-Matic and Mimics Core [Leuven, Belgium]), KLS Martin (Individual Patient Solutions [Tuttlingen, Germany]), DePuy Synthes (MAXFRAME [Raynham, Massachusetts]), OrthoPediatrics (Playbook [Warsaw, Indiana]), TraumaCad (virtual only) (Brainlab; Munich, Bavaria), and Firefly (Firefly Lab; Cambridge, Massachusetts), provide services related to 3D surgical planning (Fig. 2a-d). Specific software programs (such as Materialise) have been cleared by the United States Food and Drug Administration for creating 3DP models for diagnostic use, ensuring quality and safety in the practice of anatomical modeling in hospitals [57]. Among current applications, patient-specific drilling [22] and cutting guides [58] demonstrate the strongest quantitative impact [[59], [60], [61]]. Examples of printers include the Stratasys (Stratasys; Rehovot, Israel) J750 and J735 PolyJet, Stratasys Objet30 Prime, and Stratasys J5 MediJet/DentaJet printers. The J750, J735, Objet30, and J5 MediJet produce patient-specific anatomical models for diagnostic use. Of those listed, the J5 MediJet and DentaJet use materials that are biocompatible and sterilizable (Please see Appendix A).

Patient-specific surgical guides have been explored across pediatric orthopaedics (Fig. 2). A systematic review and meta-analysis found that 3D printed pedicle screw guides increased accurate screw placement fourfold, reduced procedure times by 36 min, and decreased blood loss by approximately 80 mL compared with freehand insertion [62]. Studies of congenital scoliosis confirm these findings, reporting screw accuracy of 96% with printed templates versus 89% with traditional methods [63]. Similar advantages have been observed in upper cervical instrumentation, including atlantoaxial and occipitocervical constructs [22,[64], [65], [66], [67]]. Among pediatric spine studies, the most consistent improvements are higher screw accuracy and fewer fluoroscopic exposures; effects on operative time and blood loss vary [62,[68], [69], [70]]. Printed templates have been shown to enhance reproducibility in narrow bone corridors and reduce the need for repeated intraoperative imaging [54]. Beyond spinal applications, numerous studies support the use of 3D printing in limb deformity correction [41,42]. Evidence for its use in slipped capital femoral epiphysis fixation remains limited and should be considered investigational until comparative data are available [53].

Custom implants and biological integration

Beyond surgical guides, custom implants produced through additive manufacturing enable reconstruction in anatomical sites unsuited to standard implants. Lattice-structured titanium constructs (Fig. 3) have been used to address segmental bone loss after tumor resection, often combined with autologous fibular grafts to provide immediate mechanical stability while maintaining porosity for bone ingrowth [33]. Early experiences suggest satisfactory osseointegration and restoration of limb function [49,71,72]. Parallel developments in biodegradable magnesium and zinc alloys aim to provide temporary fixation that resorbs after healing, potentially reducing the need for implant removal [34,35]. Although currently limited to specialized centers, these approaches indicate an increasing focus on biologically active, patient-specific implants in the pediatric population.

In skeletally immature patients, reconstruction strategies prioritize joint preservation and avoidance of the growth plate, acknowledging that growth may require staged revisions [53]. Lattice titanium constructs can reduce apparent stiffness and enhance osseointegration, but concerns about stress-shielding and over-constraint in growing bones remain [38,71,73]. Early research on bioresorbable metals (magnesium, zinc, and iron) and hybrid scaffolds aims to align implant longevity with pediatric healing timelines and potentially reduce removal surgeries, but current evidence is largely preclinical or early clinical and warrants careful evaluation of degradation and infection risks [41,63,[74], [75], [76], [77]]. Although initial reports show satisfactory osseointegration and limb function, nonunion and implant-related complications still occur, and long-term survivorship through growth is not yet well established.

Orthotic and external applications

Additive manufacturing has also been integrated into orthotic and prosthetic practice. Scanning-based design and polymer printing enable the production of custom braces and casts that are lighter, better ventilated, and more comfortable. Digital scanning removes the need for plaster casting, streamlining workflows for orthotists and reducing delivery times. Systematic reviews report enhancements in patient comfort, hygiene [78], and adherence with printed orthoses [79] compared to traditional methods (Fig. 4) [78,79]. In clinical settings, this results in better brace adherence in scoliosis management and postoperative immobilization [10,38,[80], [81], [82], [83], [84], [85]]. However, data on curve modulation, skin complications, and long-term adherence remain limited [86]. These devices are best viewed as promising adjuncts while higher-quality comparative data develop.

Education and communication

The educational use of 3D printing includes surgical training [87], patient communication [55,82], and multidisciplinary planning. Studies in medical education [45] show that trainees who use printed models [27], [61] have improved anatomical understanding and procedural confidence compared with two-dimensional imaging [29,45].

In pediatric care, printed models help parents understand during informed consent discussions [88]. Research on congenital heart disease [44] shows that tactile models improve understanding of spatial relationships for clinicians and families alike [44]. Similar results are observed in orthopaedic consultations involving complex deformities [77], enhancing discussions among surgeons, families, and trainees [10,24,55,89].

Operational integration and cost

Economic analyses [[90], [91], [92], [93]] consistently show both efficiency gains and challenges associated with in-house printing [8,12,38,[94], [95], [96], [97]]. A practical cost framework includes capital costs (printers, software, and postprocessing), personnel (segmentation/engineering time), materials, machine time, sterilization and quality-assurance overhead, and turnaround time [[97], [98], [99]]. Cross-specialty analyses estimate average time savings of about 60 min for anatomical models and 20-40 min for patient-specific guides, leading to per-case cost savings of roughly USD 1,500-3,700 in high-acuity operating rooms when used regularly [31,93,95,100,101]. In-house point-of-care printing reduces lead times and enables direct surgeon oversight, but it requires sustained volume to justify capital costs and support staff (Table 1). Outsourcing shifts fixed costs to vendors with predictable per-case charges, though it involves longer turnaround times and less iterative control [84,94,95,[102], [103], [104]].

In a cross-specialty review [93], 3D-printed models and surgical guides reduced operative time by an average of 62 and 23 min, respectively, leading to estimated savings of USD 3,700 and USD 1,500 per case [93]. The same report found that these time savings offset the capital cost of mid-range printers when used regularly. Institutional programs usually involve collaboration among orthopaedic surgery, radiology, and biomedical engineering departments [37,77,105,106]. Quality assurance protocols [12,62,107,108] for image processing, print verification, and sterilization are crucial for ensuring consistency [8].

Evidence synthesis and limitations

The evidence supporting 3DP in pediatric orthopaedics now includes multiple meta-analyses and controlled trials [18,29,35,45,48,62,106,[109], [110], [111], [112], [113], [114], [115], [116], [117]]. Most studies report improvements in intraoperative precision [31] and efficiency [23] metrics. However, sample sizes remain small, and heterogeneity in techniques, materials, and outcome reporting complicates meta-analytic synthesis [31,45,97,99,101,118,119]. Long-term outcomes, such as growth, implant longevity, and function, are less frequently studied [18,19,29,36,43,73]. Randomized trials in fracture fixation [10,56] and acetabular surgery suggest no significant difference in patient-reported outcomes despite shorter operative times [[10], [56]]. These findings indicate that 3DP may improve procedural logistics more consistently than functional endpoints [30,120]. Heterogeneity in segmentation skills and validation processes also contributes to inconsistent performance across centers [12,25,64,121,122].

Importantly, many meta-analyses combine mixed-age cohorts; pediatric-only datasets remain limited but show similar trends, with stronger and more consistent signals for intraoperative logistics (operative time, fluoroscopy, and accuracy) than for patient-reported outcomes. Where possible, pediatric reports should provide absolute differences in implant placement accuracy, correction accuracy, fluoroscopic and total radiation exposures (including advanced imaging preoperatively), and operative time to contextualize effect sizes and enable comparisons across centers.

Future directions

Recent research trends move beyond mechanical replication toward biologically and digitally integrated models. Artificial intelligence–driven segmentation has been introduced to automate and standardize model creation, reducing operator dependence [81]. Radiation-free sources, including synthetic CT [13], continue to reduce radiation exposure while still providing information about 3D anatomy and understanding. Multimaterial printing now reproduces both cortical and cancellous textures for realistic surgical rehearsal and instrument testing [[99], [137]]. Biodegradable scaffolds [123] and bioactive coatings [124,125] are being studied for infection control [49,126,127], soft-tissue regeneration [128,129], and bone regeneration [130,131]. Sustainable printing materials [132] and decentralized point-of-care production [90] aim to reduce supply delays and costs [90]. As validation protocols mature, printed devices may evolve from static replicas to biologically responsive constructs [37,38,99].

Conclusion

3DP offers practical benefits in pediatric orthopaedic surgery by enhancing surgical accuracy, visualization, and efficiency in cases involving complex anatomy [24,133]. Current evidence supports its use in deformity correction and spinal instrumentation [134], with guide accuracy [114] and surgical metrics [135] showing consistent improvements. Its applications in education, orthotics [81,86], and custom implant design continue to expand. The technology’s evolution from a planning tool [77] to a biointegrative platform depends on thorough validation, cost control, and ongoing collaboration among surgeons, engineers [18,19,43,75,136], and material scientists [18,19,36].

Summary

This review covers the clinical utility, workflow, and cost considerations of 3D printing (3DP) in pediatric orthopedic surgery. Benefits in deformity correction and complex pelvic reconstruction are well-documented, though randomized trials in fracture fixation have not shown meaningful differences in patient-reported outcomes despite improved operative efficiency. Cost analyses support in-house programs at high-volume centers, with estimated per-case savings of $1,500–$3,700. Emerging technologies including lattice titanium implants, biodegradable alloys, and AI-assisted segmentation signal a shift toward biologically integrated, patient-specific implants. Overall, evidence improvements are most consistent in intraoperative logistics rather than functional endpoints.
