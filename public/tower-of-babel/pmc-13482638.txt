Integrating genetic and lifestyle determinants in a risk prediction model for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma in Taiwan
Authors: Chen-Shuan Chung, Yi-Hsun Chen, Ming-Hung Hsu, Chen-Huan Yu, Wen-Lun Wang, Hsiu-Po Wang, Ming-Shiang Wu, Chien-Hung Lee, Che-Hong Chen, I-Chen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) in East Asia is markedly male predominant, strongly associated with genetic susceptibility and modifiable lifestyle exposures. We developed and validated a risk appraisal model for ESCC by integrating genetic polymorphisms of two key alcohol-metabolizing enzymes—ADH1B and ALDH2—with major carcinogenic exposures from alcohol, tobacco, and betel quid among men. Genetic and lifestyle data were collected from independent cohorts including only male population across five tertiary centers in Taiwan. The training cohort included 396 ESCC patients and 647 controls, and the validation cohort comprised 256 ESCC patients and 260 controls. Six significant predictors were incorporated into a composite risk score derived from log odds ratios. Using a cutoff value of 5, the model achieved an AUC of 0.91, with 80.30% sensitivity and 87.33% specificity in the training cohort. In the validation cohort, the AUC was 0.96 with sensitivity and specificity of 86.33% and 94.23%, respectively. The positive and negative predictive values were 93.64% and 87.50%, respectively. This integrated model reliably identifies male individuals at high risk of ESCC and may serve as a precision screening tool in high-incidence regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55075-x.

Introduction

In 2022, esophageal cancer ranked 11th worldwide in prevalence and 7th in cancer-related mortality. In order to lessen the global burden of esophageal cancer, it is predicted that a 3% annual reduction rate is essential to achieve this goal1,2. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), the predominant subtype, is particularly common in the “Asian Esophageal Cancer Belt,” extending from Eastern Turkey, through Iraq, Iran, the southern former Soviet Union and Mongolia into northern China where incidence is 10–15 times higher in men than in women3. Acetaldehyde, a Group 1 carcinogen generated from alcohol metabolism, plays a central role in the development of upper aerodigestive tract (UADT) cancers4–9. In East Asia including southern China, Taiwan, Korea and Japan, ESCC incidence is also particularly high due in part to common genetic variants of alcohol-metabolizing enzymes and other lifestyle factors4,8,10,11. The dysfunctional aldehyde dehydrogenase-2 (ALDH2) rs671 variant, which causes acetaldehyde accumulation and alcohol flushing, affects more than 540 million East Asians (approximately 8% of the global population)12. Meanwhile, the alcohol dehydrogenase-1B (ADH1B) rs1229984 variant influences both acetaldehyde production and patterns of alcohol consumption12. Given these unique genetic risk factors, Taiwan stands at the forefront of this high risk region, with 40–50% of its population carrying the dysfunctional ALDH2 variant associated with alcohol intolerance4,6,13,14. According to previous case–control studies in Taiwan, the high-activity ADH1B GG genotype has a frequency of up to 44.7%, whereas the ALDH2 GA genotype is present in up to 54.1% of the population4,6. Lifestyle exposures—including alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and betel quid chewing—remain the most prevalent and modifiable risk factors for ESCC development in Taiwan.

Common lifestyle and genetic risk factors can therefore be used to identify asymptomatic high-risk individuals for ESCC screening. These individuals may benefit from early detection and improved outcomes by image-enhanced endoscopy (IEE) and structured surveillance. Several cancer prediction models for esophageal malignancies have been reported15–17. Most models rely on environmental exposures and demographic variables. For example, in a large Chinese cohort study representing 556,949 person-years, questionnaire-based factors such as age, sex, body mass index, alcohol consumption, tea drinking, and fruit intake were used to estimate 5-year ESCC risk16. Notably, genetic markers such as ADH1B and ALDH2 polymorphisms were not incorporated in this large-scale study. In Japan, Yokoyama et al. developed a health-risk appraisal score using questionnaires that assessed alcohol flushing tendency, drinking and smoking habits, and dietary intake of vegetables and fruits15. This tool successfully stratified risk in the general male population and proved effective for population-based screening, particularly by reaching high-risk individuals who often avoid conventional cancer screening programs. Our own previous case-control study further demonstrated that East Asian–specific alleles of ADH1B (rs1229984 G) and ALDH2 (rs671 A) substantially increase the risk of UADT cancers, including ESCC, and potentiate the carcinogenic effects of alcohol4. Betel quid chewing is common in Southeast Asia Countries. Uniquely, Taiwan is the only East Asia country where the habit of betel nut chewing is still common. It overlaps with the highest prevalence of the ALDH2 risk variant, thus, in addition to alcohol drinking and tobacco smoking, all contributed significantly to the high incidences of ESCC in men in Taiwan. Building on this evidence, the present study aims to establish an ESCC risk score that integrates both genetic variants and lifestyle exposures of alcohol, betel quid chewing, and tobacco use to build an effective precision medicine-based screening model for ESCC.

Methods

Study design and data collection

Data from a previously conducted multicenter case-control study at three Taiwanese medical centers were utilized as the training set6, while data from E-DA Hospital (EDH) and Far Eastern Memorial Hospital (FEMH) served as the validation set4. Only the male population were analyzed because there were very few female patients (N = 10 [2.46%] and N 20 [7.25%] in the training and testing cohorts, respectively) and ESCC occurs dominantly among men. We prospectively enrolled participants who were considered appropriate candidates for esophagogastroduodenoscopy, were willing to complete a carcinogen exposure questionnaire, and agreed to provide blood samples. Controls were individuals undergoing routine health check-ups without symptoms related to ESCC, whereas cases were patients with histologically confirmed ESCC. Participants were excluded if they had incomplete questionnaire data or missing blood samples, and who were unable or unwilling to provide informed consent were also excluded. The diagnosis of ESCC was established based on histopathological examination of biopsy specimens and a structured carcinogen exposure questionnaire and collection of blood samples for laboratory analysis were administered. The training cohort, established between 2000 and 2005, comprised 396 patients with histologically confirmed ESCC and 647 healthy controls matched by age, gender, and hospital. This study concluded that ESCC risk is greatest in drinkers carrying ADH1B*1/1 (G/G) and ALDH2*2 (A) alleles, particularly among smokers6. 256 ESCC patients and 260 healthy control subjects were recruited as an independent cohort for validation. This validation cohort included 260 healthy control subjects and 132 ESCC patients recruited and described previously from FEMH4 between June 2008 and June 2010 and 124 new ESCC patients recruited from EDH between March 2022 and June 2024. The training and validation sets were derived from the same underlying population using consistent inclusion criteria, minimizing potential selection bias.

Enrolled participants were recruited to investigate the association between polymorphisms in alcohol-metabolizing genes and cancer risk. Tag SNPs of ADH1B, ADH1C, and ALDH2 in the Han Chinese population were selected from the International HapMap database using a pairwise tagging approach with a linkage disequilibrium threshold of r² > 0.8 and a minor allele frequency > 0.05. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the Puregene DNA Purification Kit (Gentra Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and the resulting DNA was dispensed into 96-well plates (ABgene Limited, Epsom, UK). Samples with concentrations ≥ 2.5 ng/µl were genotyped using the Sequenom MassARRAY system (with call rate > 95%). Based on previous studies demonstrating strong associations with cancer risk, two functional polymorphisms, ADH1B (rs1229984) and ALDH2 (rs671), were selected for further analysis4,6. All participants underwent IEE screening with NBI and/or Lugol chromoendoscopy for detection of esophageal neoplasms. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of participating hospitals, and written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Quantification and integration of risk SNPs and exposure to lifestyle risk factors

Baseline questionnaires collected detailed information on alcohol, tobacco, and betel quid use. The levels of alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and betel quid chewing were categorized as none, light-to-moderate, and heavy use based on previously published criteria6. Briefly, categorization was determined according to frequency and cumulative exposure. Alcohol drinkers, tobacco smokers, and betel-quid chewers were defined as individuals who had consumed any alcoholic beverage at least once per week, smoked at least 10 cigarettes per week, or chewed at least one betel nut (quid) per day for a minimum of six months, respectively6. For alcohol, participants reported beverage types, drinking frequency and quantity per week, and duration of use. Total intake was converted into grams per day (1 drink = 15.75 g ethanol) and expressed as drink-years. Tobacco use was recorded as cigarettes smoked per week (20 cigarettes = 1 pack), and betel quid use as quids per week (10 quids = 1 pack); both were expressed as pack-years. Cumulative lifetime exposure was calculated by converting weekly use into daily averages and multiplying by years of use. Participants were then categorized into three levels for each substance: alcohol—nondrinker, light–moderate drinker (1–40 drink-years), heavy drinkers (> 40 drink-year); smoking—nonsmoker, light–moderate smoker (1–30 pack-years), heavy (> 30 pack-years); betel quid—nonchewer, light–moderate chewer (1–20 pack-years), heavy chewer (> 20 pack-years).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t test, while categorical variables are expressed as n (%) and compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. We used a three-stage process to develop risk-scoring models. First, we used multivariate logistic regression in the training set to evaluate ESCC risk factors including age, ADH1B/ALDH2 genotypes, smoking, alcohol, and betel quid. Second, significant predictors with p < 0.05 were incorporated to construct a risk predictive model: a cumulative risk score (CRS) which was assigned by dividing each significant regression coefficient (β) by the smallest coefficient and rounding the quotient to the nearest integer. The CRS was then calculated as the sum of these risk scores. The discriminative performance of the CRS model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, including the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity. Positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were also calculated for the CRS system. The optimal cutoff value was determined by maximizing the Youden index. The calibration of the model was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the model by calculating the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Third, we externally validated the CRS’s predictive performance in an independent cohort. A logistic regression model estimated the odds ratio (ORs) for high CRS18. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the use of prospective consecutive enrollment, no formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted. The statistical analysis was performed using STATA software (version 17.0; Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

We compiled and analyzed genetic, lifestyle and demographic data of 396 patients with ESCC and 647 healthy subjects as a training cohort. ORs between the case and control groups were calculated summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences in age (mean ± SD, 59.32 ± 11.85 vs. 58.73 ± 11.17, p = 0.42) between the two groups. A higher proportion of ESCC patients carried risk genotypes for ADH1B (rs1229984 homozygous GG) (28.79% vs. 6.49%, aOR = 3.49, 95%CI 1.97 ~ 6.19, p < 0.01) and ALDH2 (rs671 heterozygous GA) (68.94% vs. 43.12%, aOR = 4.52, 95%CI 2.96 ~ 6.91, p < 0.01) compared to healthy controls. A lower proportion of ESCC patients carried the ALDH2 rs671 homozygous AA genotype (3.54% vs. 6.96%) compared to healthy control subjects. Based on our statistical analysis the ALDH2 AA genotype still carried a higher risk of ESCC (aOR = 3.23, 95%CI 1.30 ~ 8.06, p = 0.01). When considering exposure to lifestyle carcinogens, a higher proportion of ESCC patients reported heavy alcohol use (55.56% vs. 9.12%, aOR = 18.27, 95%CI 10.54 ~ 31.65, p < 0.01), heavy cigarette smoking (50.25% vs. 17.93%, aOR = 5.49, 95%CI 3.21 ~ 9.41, p < 0.01), and heavy betel quid chewing (36.11% vs. 3.71%, aOR = 5.43, 95%CI 3.11 ~ 9.48, p < 0.01) compared to healthy subjects.

For risk factors that showed statistical significance and were included in our risk prediction model analysis, we used age, ADH1B genotype, ALDH2 genotype and subdivided alcohol, betel and cigarette users into two classes of light-to-moderate and heavy users (Table 2). The regression coefficients (β) of multivariable logistic regression model were calculated for the risk genotype of ADH1B GG (β = 1.3), ALDH2 GA (β = 1.5) and ALDH2 AA (β = 1.2), as well as for the habitual status of light-to-moderate and heavy use of alcohol consumption (β = 1.9 for light-to-moderate and 2.9 for heavy users), cigarette smoking (β = 1.6 for light-to-moderate and 1.7 for heavy users), and betel quid chewing (β = 1.3 for light-to-moderate and 1.7 for heavy users) to establish the cancer risk score. A simple CRS number based on variables can be tabulated by using the following formula: [AGE] (≥ 70-year-old = 2) + [GENOTYPE] (ADH1B GG = 1), (ALDH2 GA = 1), (ALDH2 AA = 1) + [ALCOHOL DRINKING] (light-to-moderate = 2), (heavy = 3 ) + [CIGARETTE SMOKING] (light-to-moderate = 1), (heavy = 2) + [BETEL QUID CHEWING] (light-to-moderate = 1), (heavy = 1). For each variable, if the corresponding risk factor is absent, then the score is 0.

We combined a total of 1,043 individuals from the training cohort, and an additional total of 516 individuals (256 ESCC, 260 healthy controls) from a validation cohort to map out the distribution of risk factors in our risk appraisal model (Supplementary Table 1). Using a cut-off value of 5 for the CRS to predict ESCC yielded a sensitivity of 80.30%, specificity of 87.33% and accuracy of 84.66%, with an AUC-ROC of 0.91 (Table 2 & Fig. 1). Notably, in individuals with the high-risk ALDH2 GA genotype who consume alcohol, the sensitivity reached 93.04%. In the nondrinking group, the model maintained high specificities (all > 82%), effectively minimizing false positives. Although some sensitivity estimates in the nondrinking strata appeared lower due to the limited number of ESCC cases in those subgroups, the overall findings confirm that the model is a robust and highly sensitive tool for identifying individuals with synergistic genetic and behavioral risks (Supplementary Table 2). The risks scoring schemes effectively stratified participants into low and high-risk groups. The odds of being an ESCC case were markedly higher in the high-risk group defined by CRS compared to the low-risk group (OR = 28.09, 95% CI: 20.01–39.43; p < 0.01) (Table 3). Using 5 as the cut-off value for the cumulative risk score, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were 86.33%, 94.23%, 93.64% and 87.5%, respectively, in the validation cohort (Table 4). The cumulative risk score demonstrated good calibration, with a Hosmer-Lemeshow p-value of 0.10, a calibration slope of 1.05, and an observed-to-expected ratio of 1.12. The model provided a positive net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, approximately 1% to 95% (Supplementary Table 3 & Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

In Taiwan, ESCC is one of the most common malignancies, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 12.85 per 100,000, ranking as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death among men14. We developed a cancer risk appraisal score model which integrates both common genetic and lifestyle risk factors with high predictive performance for ESCC in an Asian population. With a cut-off value of 5 for a CRS, the model was validated with high sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV. We believe this valuable risk appraisal score could serve as a tool for mass screening of ESCC using guidelines for individuals with CRS of 5 or above being recommended for IEE screening. A similar risk appraisal model for ESCC screening has been developed and validated with high effectiveness in Japan11,15,17. In a prospective study conducted in Japan, a detection rate of 2.91% was achieved by endoscopic screening of high risk individuals17. In Taiwan, beside alcohol and cigarette use, betel chewing is an unique high lifestyle risk for ESCC in this population19. We therefore included this unique lifestyle risk factor and expected that our model should be able to achieve a similar or better detection for the Taiwanese population.

Early stages of ESCC are often asymptomatic which often resulted in late-stage diagnosis and poor prognosis. Effective population-level risk stratification to identify high-risk individuals for IEE screening is therefore crucial to enable early detection and reduce mortality20–26. Early-stage ESCC can be effectively treated with minimally invasive endoscopic modalities such as endoscopic mucosal resection or submucosal dissection, with markedly better quality of life and survival outcomes compared to advanced-stage disease. Therefore, identifying high-risk individuals for targeted screening is of critical importance. Using magnifying endoscopy combined with NBI screening of the esophagus within 6 months of newly-diagnosed hypopharyngeal cancer regular as well as regular endoscopy surveillance, it was shown that the overall survival of patients with hypopharyngeal cancer could be improved (HR: 0.53, 0.36–0.78, p = 0.001)23. In Taiwan, oral cancer and esophageal cancer shared identical common genetic factors of the alcohol metabolizing genes (ADH1B and ALDH2) and the lifestyle risk factors (alcohol, betel nut and smoking)27,28. Patients with oral cavity cancer or oral potential malignant disease can therefore also benefit from this cumulative risk score-based prediction model and IEE screening of second primary neoplasm of esophagus. Endoscopic screening significantly improved early detection of second primary neoplasm of esophagus (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.23–0.49) and reduced all-cause mortality (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.98)24. IEE screening in high-risk populations enables detection of asymptomatic early esophageal neoplasms and allows minimally invasive curative treatment, improving quality of life compared with esophagectomy or chemoradiotherapy. Endoscopic screening, particularly IEE, is a feasible, effective, and cost-effective strategy for early detection of second primary esophageal cancer in patients with head and neck (HN) or gastric cancer25,26. Thus, genetic- and lifestyle-based risk prediction models may improve survival, quality of life, and cost-effectiveness of UADT cancer screening, although the benefit of population-wide screening remains uncertain.

Some predictive models have been developed to stratify esophageal cancer risk using lifestyle and demographic information29,30. However, limited data were developed for squamous cell histological subtype. A strong body of evidence links ESCC to exposure of environmental carcinogens, particularly alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking, and betel quid chewing with a synergistic effect in promoting UADT carcinogenesis19,28. Acetaldehyde, a carcinogenic metabolite of ethanol, plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of alcohol-related cancers4,5,7. Cigarette smoking also dramatically increases acetaldehyde concentration in the saliva and also plays key role in UADT cancer31,32. Genetic polymorphisms in key alcohol-metabolizing enzymes, notably ADH1B (rs1229984) and ALDH2 (rs671), significantly influence individual susceptibility by modulating acetaldehyde accumulation8–10. Our previous study reinforced this association, demonstrating that individuals carrying the risk alleles for ADH1B and ALDH2—particularly in conjunction with heavy alcohol intake—faced a markedly elevated risk of developing ESCC4. We found that the East Asian minor alleles of ADH1B G allele and ALDH2 A allele were not only linked to an increased risk of UADT cancers including ESCC and HN cancers (OR 3.53, 95% CI 2.14–5.80 and OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.79–3.75, respectively), but also intensified the carcinogenic effects of alcohol with OR 53.44 (95% CI 25.21-113.29) and 70.08 (95% CI 33.65-145.95), respectively4. The homozygous AA genotype of ALDH2 rs671 produces extremely low enzyme activity and is generally considered protective against ESCC due to alcohol avoidance. However, it is noteworthy that individuals with this genotype may still develop cancer if exposed to alcohol drinking social behavior. These findings highlight the importance of including both behavioral and genetic risk factors in ESCC risk assessment, particularly in East Asian populations where a high prevalence of risk alleles in alcohol-metabolizing genes exists4,6.

The health-risk appraisal (HRA) model developed by Yokoyama et al., which includes alcohol flushing tendency and substance use behaviors, has proven effective in public health screening efforts15,17. The study enrolled general population of Japanese men aged 50 years and evaluated risk for ESCC using facial flushing after alcohol drinking, status of carcinogen exposure from alcohol drinking and tobacco smoking, and fruits intake as risk factors. In this model, based on ROC analysis and the use of an HRA score cutoff of 9 for individuals aged 50–69 years and 8 for those aged 70–89 years to identify high-risk candidates for ESCC yielded a sensitivity of 52.6% and a false-positive rate of 15.2%17. The cancer detection rate in the high-risk group was 2.91% compared to 0.48% in the lower-risk group17. However, relying on facial flushing is an indirect indicator with compromised accuracy as compared to direct genotyping of ADH1B and ALDH2 for risk alleles in the alcohol-metabolizing genes. In one study, approximately one-third (35.2%) of individuals with heterozygous ALDH2 enzymes did not experience facial flushing after consuming alcohol33. Additionally, betel quid chewing is one of the common carcinogens for ESCC in India, Southern China, Taiwan, Southeast Asian and other countries in the Pacific region19. Hence, our risk appraisal system represents a novel model of by integrating direct genotyping of alcohol-metabolizing genes with region-specific lifestyle risk factors of carcinogen exposures. The model demonstrated a robust predictive value, with an AUC-ROC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.93 ~ 0.97) and acceptable sensitivity (86.33%) and specificity (94.23%) in the validation cohort. These findings support its utility as a feasible and scalable tool for mass screening, for example using a cut-off score of 5 to identify a high-risk subgroup suitable for IEE surveillance.

This study has several limitations. The model was derived from a case–control, cross-sectional design, which is subject to selection and recall bias and does not allow assessment of surveillance intervals; prospective studies are needed for validation and refinement. In addition, although major lifestyle factors were included, other potential confounders such as diet, oral hygiene, environmental or occupational exposures, and family history were not assessed34. Potential interactions between genetic polymorphisms of alcohol-metabolizing enzymes and cigarettes smoking or betel quid chewing on cancer risk were not tested in this study. While other genetic susceptibility loci (e.g., ATM, CHEK2) may contribute to cancer risk, our model was designed to prioritize clinically actionable and population-relevant variants. Future studies incorporating a broader range of genetic markers may further improve risk stratification. Tumor staging information was not available for the included ESCC patients. While staging is important for prognostic evaluation and treatment planning, it reflects disease progression after cancer development. The present study was designed to investigate genetic–environmental interactions for risk prediction and screening purposes; therefore, the absence of staging data is unlikely to affect the validity of the proposed risk model. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating staging information may help to explore whether genetic and environmental factors are associated not only with cancer risk but also with disease aggressiveness or progression. These unmeasured variables could also have an impact on disease risk. Furthermore, the cost-effectiveness and limited availability of genotyping remain major constraints for large-scale screening programs. Fortunately, rapid genotyping using DNA extracted from saliva, rather than blood samples, offers a more convenient and feasible approach for mass screening in future prospective studies. Lastly, to establish clinical applicability and robustness of our model, prospective and validation studies using larger, ethnically diverse, hospital and community-based cohorts are warranted.

In conclusion, we developed a practical ESCC risk model integrating alcohol-metabolizing gene variants and lifestyle factors. By combining two East Asian–specific SNPs with a brief lifestyle questionnaire, the model identifies high-risk individuals for targeted IEE screening, early detection, and prevention. We recommend one-time or intensified IEE surveillance for individuals with CRS ≥ 5 and anticipate broader application of this precision screening approach to other UADT cancers in East Asia.
