Parent or Child Driven? Associations Over Time Between Parental Depressive Symptoms, Parental Locus of Control, and Child Negative Emotionality in US Military Families
Authors: Lijun Li, Timothy Piehler, Abigail Gewirtz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMilitary personnel are at an elevated risk of depression after combat exposure, which can create significant stress for both the civilian spouses/partners and their children. However, the complex associations between parental mental health, family dynamics, and child characteristics have rarely been examined in post‐deployment populations. This study examined the associations between parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality using a random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model in US military families. The findings indicated that for mothers, increases in parental locus of control were associated with decreases in later parental depressive symptoms, while increases in child negative emotionality were associated with an increase in later parental depressive symptoms. For fathers, increases in parental locus of control were surprisingly associated with an increase in later child negative emotionality. These findings suggest that parenting interventions for military families should strengthen mothers' parental locus of control to buffer against depressive symptoms, while equipping fathers with noncoercive, emotionally supportive parenting strategies during reintegration.Keywords: child negative emotionality, directionality, military families, parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control

Introduction

Nearly 2 million US service members were deployed to the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan between 2001 and 2009 (Office of the Deputy Under Secretary of Defense 2016). Rates of depression are elevated in this population, with 23% of active forces and 20% of veterans reporting depressive symptoms compared to 18.4% in the general population (Lee et al. 2020; Moradi et al. 2021). Depressive symptoms are often associated with adverse consequences across various life domains such as deficits in social skills (Tse and Bond 2004) and impaired work performance (Lerner et al. 2010), placing military personnel at heightened vulnerability during and after deployment.

The Military Family Stress Model (Gewirtz et al. 2018) conceptualizes the psychological consequences of deployment as major family‐level stressors that disrupt parental and child well‐being, undermining child and family functioning. Specifically, parental psychological challenges undermine parenting which, in turn, increases children's risk for maladjustment (see, e.g., Cheng et al. 2024). Prior research has demonstrated service members' separations, combat exposure, and perceived danger contribute to psychological challenges such as post‐traumatic stress and depressive symptoms, which in turn impair marital relationships and parenting (Gewirtz et al. 2014; Guyon‐Harris et al. 2022; Lambert et al. 2012; Lovejoy et al. 2000; Turney 2011). Meanwhile, non‐deployed spouses experience increased caregiving burden and greater risks for depression (Bommarito et al. 2017; Green et al. 2013) and secondary post‐traumatic stress disorder (Dekel et al. 2023; Solomon et al. 2021; Zerach et al. 2022), and children of deployed parents often show elevated internalizing and externalizing problems (Chartrand et al. 2008; Creech et al. 2014; Mansfield et al. 2011). However, little research has documented (a) how fluctuations over time in parental psychological challenges affect either parenting or children's adjustment or (b) whether associations between factors in the family stress model are bi‐ or uni‐directional.

In this study, then, we examine proximal processes through which deployment‐related stress shapes family functioning by considering three interrelated factors: parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality. Parental locus of control reflects parents' perceived ability to influence their child's behavior (Campis et al. 1986), and lower perceived control has been linked with less effective parenting and greater child behavior problems (Freed and Tompson 2011). Child negative emotionality, defined as frequent and intense expressions of anger, fear, or sadness (Rothbart and Bates 2006), can heighten parental stress and further erode parents' sense of control. These dynamics may be especially salient in military families, in which reintegrating service members often face difficulties in parenting, regulating their own emotions, and coaching their children's emotion regulation (Saltzman et al. 2011). Examining parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality together extends understanding of the Military Family Stress Model by highlighting dynamic and potentially bi‐directional processes that remain underexplored in this population. Importantly, in the present sample, fathers were primarily military personnel, whereas mothers were typically civilian spouses. These roles may involve different forms of stress exposure (e.g., deployment‐related stress vs. secondary caregiving burden), which may shape parental functioning and family processes. This context provides a useful framework for interpreting potential differences in associations across mothers and fathers.

Mutual Influence Between Depressive Symptoms and Locus of Control

Extensive research links general locus of control with depressive symptoms. Meta‐analyses across diverse cultural contexts show that individuals with a more external locus of control report higher levels of depression (Benassi et al. 1988; Cheng et al. 2013). Most of this empirical evidence is cross‐sectional, making the direction of effect unclear. One study found that adolescents with higher external locus of control were more likely to develop depressive symptoms in later adulthood (Costantini et al. 2021). However, longitudinal evidence for the reverse direction remains limited. Theoretical perspectives suggest that bidirectional effects are possible. According to cognitive theories (Beck 1967), depressed individuals are predisposed to selectively recall failure memories and are more likely to attribute positive outcomes to external factors and internalize negative outcomes (Benassi et al. 1988). These depressive cognitions, characterized by self‐deprecation, may foster an external locus of control. At the same time, the learned helplessness hypothesis (Abramson et al. 1978) suggests that individuals with a higher external locus of control often feel less competent and take fewer proactive steps to improve their circumstances, thereby increasing depression risk (Khumalo and Plattner 2019; Taris and Bok 1997). Despite these assumptions, more longitudinal research is needed to better capture the direction of these effects.

When extended to the parenting domain, research on parental locus of control is much more limited. Cross‐sectional studies suggest that mothers of preschoolers and school‐age children with more depressive symptoms perceive less control in their parental role (Coyne and Thompson 2011; Freed and Tompson 2011), but directionality remains unclear. Despite robust evidence connecting general locus of control and depression, far less is known about whether these associations apply to parental locus of control. This gap is particularly salient in military families, where deployment‐related stressors may undermine both parental mental health and parents' perceived sense of control.

Role of Child Negative Emotionality

Parent‐Driven Effects of Depressive Symptoms on Child Negative Emotionality

Parental depressive symptoms may serve as an important driver of children's emotional development. A meta‐analysis found that earlier parental depressive symptoms predicted greater infant negative emotionality (Spry et al. 2020). This pattern is consistent with the intergenerational transmission of psychopathology framework (Goodman and Gotlib 1999), which highlights both inherited vulnerabilities and environmental mechanisms through which parental depression increases risk in children. In infancy, depressive symptoms may reflect shared genetic liability for negative affectivity and early disruptions in caregiving, which together shape children's temperamental tendencies toward heightened negative emotionality. As children grow older, parental depressive symptoms may continuously serve as a contextual stressor for children (Hammen 2009), acting both through parents' symptoms and the disruptions they create in parent–child interactions and marital relationships. Over time, these processes may together lead to increased child negative emotionality, reinforcing a cycle of risk within the family.

Mutual Influences Between Parental Locus of Control and Child Negative Emotionality

Research directly examining the association between child negative emotionality and parental locus of control is scarce. To date, only one study has found that higher infant negative emotionality predicted lower perceived parental control at 33 months (Hagekull et al. 2001). More evidence has come from work on parental efficacy, a related but distinct construct, which has been consistently linked with child negative emotionality (Cutrona and Troutman 1986; Gross et al. 1994; Porter and Hsu 2003; Solmeyer and Feinberg 2011; Teti and Gelfand 1991). Longitudinal research provides mixed findings regarding directionality. Some studies suggest a child‐driven process, in which elevated infant negative emotionality predicts subsequent declines in maternal efficacy (Lipscomb et al. 2011; Takács et al. 2019). Other work points to a parent‐driven process, where early parenting efficacy predicts later child negative emotionality (Verhage et al. 2013). These patterns can be interpreted by the transactional model of development (Sameroff 2009), which views child outcomes as emerging from ongoing interactions between children and their contexts and emphasizes the bidirectional, interdependent effects of children and their environments. Within this framework, children's emotional difficulties may heighten parental stress and erode parents' perceived sense of control, which in turn may further shape children's outcomes and emotional development.

Although related, parental self‐efficacy refers to parents' confidence in managing specific tasks and situations of parenthood (Roddenberry and Renk 2010; Strauser et al. 2002), whereas parental locus of control captures broader beliefs about the extent to which parents can influence their children's behavior and development. In addition, parental efficacy is included as one subscale within the Parental Locus of Control Scale, which also assesses parental responsibility, child control over parents' lives, parental beliefs in fate/chance, and parental control of child behavior (Campis et al. 1986). Focusing on parental locus of control therefore allows for a more comprehensive understanding of parents' broader beliefs, beyond task‐specific efficacy. Further research is needed to examine these reciprocal processes, particularly in the context of military families.

Current Study

Despite evidence linking parental depressive symptoms, locus of control, and child negative emotionality, important gaps remain. Research on parental locus of control is limited, the directionality of these associations is not well understood, and most studies focus on nonmilitary populations, overlooking the unique stressors faced by military families. Examining directionality is particularly important for distinguishing whether risk processes are primarily parent‐driven, child‐driven, or reciprocal, each of which has different implications for intervention.

Guided by the Military Family Stress Model (Gewirtz et al. 2018), this study examines how parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality are dynamically interrelated over time. Traditional cross‐lagged panel models (CLPM) have been widely used to test directional associations over time, but they conflate within‐person changes with stable between‐person differences, potentially leading to biased interpretations (Hamaker et al. 2015). The random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model (RI‐CLPM) improves upon this approach by explicitly modeling stable, trait‐like individual differences as random intercepts. This separation allows researchers to more accurately examine how within‐person fluctuations in one construct (e.g., depressive symptoms) predict subsequent changes in another construct (e.g., parental locus of control), independent of stable individual tendencies. As such, RI‐CLPM offers a stronger test of potential causal mechanisms in longitudinal data (Mulder and Hamaker 2020; Orth et al. 2021).

Using a RI‐CLPM, we assessed whether associations are parent‐driven, child‐driven, or bidirectional. Specifically, we hypothesized: (1) a bidirectional negative association between parental depressive symptoms and parental locus of control; (2) a unidirectional positive association between earlier parental depressive symptoms and later child negative emotionality; (3) a bidirectional negative association between child negative emotionality and parental locus of control. Given that most fathers were service members and most mothers were civilians, and gender differences have been well‐documented in major depression and depressive symptoms (Romans et al. 2007), analyses were conducted separately by parent gender.

Method

Participants

This study conducted a secondary data analysis of a randomized controlled trial evaluating a military parenting program, After Deployment, Adaptive Parenting Tools (ADAPT). Separate Institutional Review Board/IRB approval for this study was not required given that it was secondary data analysis (the original RCT was approved by the University of Minnesota IRB). Eligible participants were Minnesota National Guard or Reserve (MN NG/R) families with at least one parent deployed to Operation Iraqi Freedom, Operation Enduring Freedom, or Operation New Dawn and a child aged 4–12. Families completed an online screening survey and provided consent. The final sample included 294 fathers, 314 mothers, and 336 biological children from 336 US military families. Most families (n = 272) included two participating parents; of these, the vast majority were married (n = 258) with 12 unmarried and 2 not indicating marital status. The 64 parents participating alone included 41 mothers and 23 fathers, of whom 23 were married (but the partner declined to participate), 18 were divorced and 10 were single; 3 did not report marital status. Length of marriage to current partner ranged from 1 to 28 years, with mean length of marriage 9.75 years (SD = 5.3) for fathers and 9.4 years (SD = 5.3) for mothers. Number of children in a household ranged from one to six with a mean of 2.34 children in a family (SD = 0.96).

At baseline, father's mean age was 37.76 years (SD = 6.54) and mother's 35.69 years (SD = 5.89). Participants were predominantly non‐Hispanic White (89.1% of fathers and 93.0% of mothers), and about half of all participants completed at least a bachelor's degree (46.6% of fathers and 51.3% of mothers). The mean age of the focal children at baseline was 8.40 years (SD = 2.53), and 53.0% were girls. Household incomes ranged from $39,999 or less (13.8%) to $120,000 or more (14.5%), with most families reporting income between $40,000 and $79,999 (43.5%) or $80,000 and $119,999 (28.2%). Most fathers (84.3%) and about half of mothers (48.4%) were employed full‐time. In 86.7% of participating families, one parent was deployed to recent conflicts; and in the remaining 13.3% of families, both parents were deployed to recent conflict. Mothers were deployed in 18.2% of families; fathers in 95% of the families. Most parents deployed with the Army National Guard (59%); others deployed with the Air National Guard (10.7%), the Army (12.9%), Navy (6.6%), Air Force (2.8%), or Marine Reserves (0.3%). About half (51.2%) of deployed parents were deployed more than once (M = 1.73, SD = 1.16). In 58.3% of these deployments, parents were deployed for more than 12 months.

Procedure

ADAPT is an evidence‐based parenting training program adapted from the Parent Management Training‐Oregon Model (PMTO; Dishion et al. 2016; Forgatch and Patterson 2010) and aims to enhance parenting skills in military families. At baseline, eligible families completed an in‐home assessment (T1) and were then randomly assigned to either the ADAPT intervention group (60%) or a “treatment‐as‐usual” control group (40%). Families in the intervention group participated in 2‐h sessions, delivered weekly for 14 weeks in groups of 6 to 15 parents. Group facilitators delivered behavioral and emotional parenting tools via active teaching methods including role play and discussion; parents were given weekly home practice to consolidate skills. The control group received informative handouts but no active intervention. In‐home assessments were conducted again at 6 months (T2), 12 months (T3), and 24 months (T4) after the baseline assessment (T1).

Measures of Primary Variables

Parental Locus of Control (PLOC)

Parental Locus of Control (PLOC) was measured by the Parental Locus of Control Scale‐short form revised (PLOC; Campis et al. 1986; PLOC‐SFR; Hassall et al. 2005). The PLOC‐SFR has 24 items that assess parenting control orientation across four dimensions: (a) parental efficacy (e.g., “My child usually ends up getting his way or her way, so why try”), (b) parental responsibility (e.g., “My child's behavior problems are no one's fault but my own”), (c) children's control of parents' life (e.g., “My child influences the number of friends I have”), and (d) parental control of children's behavior (e.g., “My child's behavior is sometimes more than I can handle”). Parents rated items on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree. We reverse coded the items so that higher scores indicate stronger sense of internal locus of control. Cronbach's α ranges from 0.74 to 0.78 across waves for the current sample.

Parental Depressive Symptoms

Parental Depressive Symptoms (PDS) were measured by the Hopkins Symptom Checklist 25 (HSCL‐25; Hesbacher et al. 1980; Winokur et al. 1984), which comprises 25 items assessing presence of 15 depressive symptoms and 10 anxiety symptoms. This study used the 15 items assessing depressive symptoms. Participants rated each item on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 = not at all to 4 = extremely. An example symptom is “Feeling low in energy, slowed down.” Cronbach's α ranges from 0.90 to 0.92 across waves for the current sample.

Child Negative Emotionality

Child Negative Emotionality (CNE) was assessed using an updated version of the Affect Intensity Scale (Eisenberg et al. 1995). Parents rated their children's general emotions (7 items), positive emotions (6 items), and negative emotions (5 items) on a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 = never to 7 = always. This study used the 5 negative emotionality items, and the Cronbach's α ranges from 0.72 to 0.76 across waves. A sample item is “When my child experiences anxiety, it normally is very strong.” Correlation between mother and father reports was significant across all waves (r = 0.424–0.465, p < 0.001); scores were averaged to reduce informant bias (Coyne and Thompson 2011; Goodman et al. 2011; Leerkes and Crockenberg 2003).

Covariates

Child gender influences parental emotional socialization (Brown et al. 2015), and the current sample included a relatively wide child age range (4–12 years), so both were included as covariates. Intervention status was included as a covariate to ensure observed effects are not due to the program. Deployment length is related to distress level (Adler et al. 2005), and the time since the service member returned home likely influenced the process of reintegration; both were controlled for as covariates. Lastly, given that our sample included a small portion of deployed mothers, we controlled for maternal deployment status as a covariate.

Analytical Plan

Missing Data

An intent‐to‐treat approach was adopted (i.e., all participants who responded at the baseline were included in the analyses). Little's MCAR test indicated that the missing data were not completely at random (χ 2 (1787) = 1970.83, p = 0.001). Therefore, missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation. FIML uses all available observed data to estimate model parameters without imputing missing values and produces unbiased parameter estimates under the Missing at Random (MAR) assumption (Enders 2010). Compared with traditional approaches such as listwise deletion, FIML generally yields less biased parameter estimates and greater statistical power when data are incomplete because it retains partially observed cases in the analysis (Little and Rubin 2019). Accordingly, FIML is widely recommended for handling missing data in longitudinal structural equation modeling.

Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive statistics were obtained to provide an overview of all variables. Bivariate correlation was conducted to test the associations between all variables.

RI‐CLPM

We used RI‐CLPM to examine the auto‐regressive and cross‐lagged effects of PLOC, PDS, and CNE, separately for fathers and mothers. Model fit was evaluated using recommended joint fit indices (Hu and Bentler 1999) of a comparative fit index (CFI) above 0.95, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) below 0.06, and standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR) below 0.08. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp 2023) and Mplus 8.10 (Muthén and Muthén 1998‐2017) with a robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator.

Results

Descriptive Results

Sixty‐one families dropped out across the three follow‐up assessments. Independent samples t‐tests and Chi‐squared tests revealed that families who dropped out had older children than those who were retained (M dropped = 9.07, M retained = 8.24, t(333) = 0.02). No significant differences were found for all other variables. Means, standard deviations for each variable, and the correlations between variables are presented in Table S1 for mothers and in Table S2 for fathers.

RI‐CLPM for Mothers

Model fit indices suggested that the RI‐CLPM fit the mothers' data well: χ 2 (107) = 124.56, p = 0.118; CFI = 0.988; TLI = 0.985; RMSEA = 0.022, 90% CI = [0, 0.037]; SRMR = 0.077. Figure 1 shows the significant paths, and the unstandardized and standardized path coefficients are presented in Table 1. Within‐person/family autoregressive effects, which reflect within‐person/family stability across timepoints while accounting for stable between‐person/family differences, were significant from PLOC assessed at 12‐months to PLOC assessed at 24‐months, and from CNE assessed at 12‐months to CNE assessed at 24‐months. This indicates that mothers' perceived control over parenting and their perceptions of their children's negative emotionality remained relatively stable over time when adjusting for average, between‐person differences. Within‐person/family cross‐lagged effects, which indicate whether fluctuations in one variable are associated with subsequent fluctuations in another variable (while accounting for stable, between‐person/family effects), revealed a unidirectional negative association from earlier PLOC to later PDS across all waves. In other words, mothers who reported an increase in their sense of control in their parenting were more likely to report subsequent decreases in their depressive symptoms. The model also revealed a within‐person/family cross‐lagged unidirectional positive association from earlier CNE to later PDS across all waves, meaning that for children whose negative emotionality increased from typical levels, their mothers were more likely to report increased depressive symptoms at the subsequent time point.

Model fit indices suggested that the RI‐CLPM fits the father data well: χ 2 (107) = 134.09, p = 0.039; CFI = 0.981; TLI = 0.976; RMSEA = 0.027, 90% CI = [0.007 0.041]; SRMR = 0.083. Within‐person/family cross‐lagged effects revealed a marginally significant unidirectional positive association from earlier PLOC to later CNE across waves, suggesting that when fathers reported an increase in their perceived parental control, their children were more likely to display higher negative emotionality at subsequent assessments.

Discussion

This study examined the longitudinal associations among parental depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality in post‐deployed military families, highlighting different patterns for mothers and fathers.

Parental Depressive Symptoms and Parental Locus of Control

We found that both mothers and fathers who reported a greater sense of control in their parenting over the study period also tended to report fewer depressive symptoms. This aligns with and extends prior findings (Khumalo and Plattner 2019) to the parenting domain. In addition to the between‐person/family correlations, the RI‐CLPM also allowed us to examine how fluctuations in one construct were associated with subsequent changes in another, thereby providing insight into directionality of effects.

Our analyses showed that increases in mothers' parental locus of control predicted later decreases in depressive symptoms, suggesting that feeling more control over one's parenting may lower vulnerability to depression. However, fluctuation in earlier depressive symptoms did not predict fluctuation in later parental locus of control. These findings provide longitudinal evidence that parental locus of control may drive this association in a high‐stress military family contexts, where greater parenting demands during deployment‐related solo parenting likely contribute more directly to maternal depressive symptoms. This pattern is consistent with the learned helplessness hypothesis (Abramson et al. 1978), which posits that a perceived lack of control increases the risk of developing depression. It is possible that cognitive theory (Beck 1967), which emphasizes selective recall of failures and negative attributional styles, becomes more salient once clinical depression is manifest and over a longer period of time, which may not have been fully captured within the timeframe of this study. Finally, parents experiencing depressive symptoms may still maintain a sense of control through coercive parenting strategies (Lovejoy et al. 2000), which may help explain the lack of evidence for a reverse effect.

For fathers, no within‐person/family associations between parental locus of control and depressive symptoms were observed over time. This may be because most fathers in the sample were deployed service members, whose depressive symptoms may be more strongly linked to deployment‐related stress than to parenting challenges, and their lower involvement in daily caregiving limited the extent to which their sense of control was tied to fluctuations in depressive symptoms. By contrast, when mothers were deployed, fathers reported higher average levels of parental locus of control, suggesting that assuming greater caregiving responsibilities strengthened their sense of control. However, given the small number of deployed mothers in the sample, statistical power was insufficient to determine whether parental locus of control functioned differently in these families.

Parental Depressive Symptoms and Child Negative Emotionality

We found that for mothers, stable elevations in parental depressive symptoms were correlated with stably elevated child negative emotionality, as reflected in correlated random intercepts. Within families, increases in child negative emotionality predicted later increases in parental depressive symptoms, but not vice versa. These findings extend prior research (Cutrona and Troutman 1986; Gross et al. 1994; Porter and Hsu 2003; Solmeyer and Feinberg 2011; Teti and Gelfand 1991) by providing longitudinal evidence; however, in the opposite direction to our hypothesis. The intergenerational transmission of psychopathology framework and Spry et al.'s (2020) meta‐analysis suggest a parent‐driven effect, while this study found a child‐driven effect. One potential explanation is that Spry's meta‐analysis focused on infancy, in which hormonal changes and sleep disruptions play a stronger role in maternal depression (Dange and Biswas 2021), which in turn, may shape child negative emotionality (Tronick and Reck 2009). As children grow older, their autonomy and emotion expression make them more active agents in the parent–child relationship (Sameroff 2009). In this developmental stage, child emotionality may exert greater influence on parents' psychological well‐being, leading to more child‐driven than parent‐driven effects. Military family contexts may also explain why child‐driven effects were more prominent in this study. Mothers often carry greater parenting burdens, especially during deployment and reintegration, which may heighten their sensitivity to children's negative emotionality. At the same time, deployment‐related stressors may also increase the likelihood of child emotional difficulties and behavioral problems (Chartrand et al. 2008; Creech et al. 2014; Mansfield et al. 2011), thereby amplifying child‐driven effects.

For fathers, no significant association was found between child negative emotionality and parental depressive symptoms, either at the trait level or within‐person/family fluctuations. This again suggests that fathers' depressive symptoms may be more influenced by deployment‐related stressors than by child emotions. Military fathers in particular may have less frequent caregiving roles (Hoffmann and Mitchell 1998), which could potentially reduce child‐driven effects on their mental health.

Parental Locus of Control and Child Negative Emotionality

We found that greater parental sense of control in parenting across the study period was associated with lower negative emotionality among children. This finding was evident for both mothers and fathers and is consistent with broader evidence linking parental locus of control to positive child adjustment (Moreland et al. 2016; Nowicki et al. 2017).

At the within‐person/family level, however, mothers' parental locus of control was not associated with child negative emotionality in either direction. This finding contradicts our hypothesis of bidirectional influences as well as Hagekull et al.'s (2001) results showing that earlier child negative emotionality predicted later parental locus of control. One possible explanation is developmental: Hagekull et al.'s (2001) study focused on younger children, when parents may be more sensitive to child emotional challenges. As parents gain caregiving experience and establish more stable routines, their sense of control may become less susceptible to fluctuations in children's emotions (Bandura 1977; Leerkes and Burney 2007; Leerkes and Crockenberg 2002). In military families, where mothers often carry primary caregiving responsibilities, this stability may be more pronounced, even as trait‐level associations persist.

For fathers, the pattern was different. Although trait‐level results indicated that fathers who felt greater control on average tended to have children with lower negative emotionality, within‐person/family analyses revealed the opposite: increases in paternal locus of control predicted higher subsequent child negative emotionality. One possible explanation is that such increases in perceived control may stem from more directive or coercive parenting strategies that restrict children's autonomy (Kuczynski et al. 2018) rather than from supportive emotional coaching (Cherry and Gerstein 2021). These strategies may elicit child resistance and distress and thereby heighten negative emotionality. The military context may further intensify these dynamics. Fathers' caregiving roles are often disrupted by deployment, and children may be sensitive to sudden shifts in paternal involvement and control upon reintegration. A father's increase in control after a period of absence may disrupt established caregiving routines, eliciting greater child negative emotionality. Nonetheless, these explanations should be interpreted cautiously, as the effects were only marginally significant. Replication in larger samples is needed to confirm these findings.

Limitations and Future Directions

One important limitation of this study is its relatively narrow scope in assessing parental and family processes. We focused on depressive symptoms as the primary indicator of parental mental health, but other mental health problems such as anxiety and PTSD, and well‐being were not examined. These factors may interact with depressive symptoms and shape family dynamics in unique ways. Similarly, our analyses centered on parental locus of control, without considering general locus of control. Parents with lower general internal control may be more susceptible to low parental locus of control, particularly when managing children with high negative emotionality. Examining a broader range of mental health dimensions alongside both parental and general locus of control would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how parent characteristics influence child outcomes.

Although we examined mother–child and father–child interactions separately, we did not address interpersonal dynamics between parents. Considering both parents' depressive symptoms and parental locus of control simultaneously could offer insight into mutual influences and dyadic processes, but the limited sample size in this study restricted our ability to do so. Future studies with larger samples may consider incorporating both parents to capture reciprocal effects. In line with the Military Family Stress Model, deployment‐related stress can strain spousal relationships (Gewirtz et al. 2018), and mothers' and fathers' depressive symptoms may mutually influence coparenting perceptions (Williams 2018). Thus, interparental processes such as relationship satisfaction and coparenting may serve as mechanisms that mediate or moderate associations between parent and child characteristics.

Finally, this study relied on parent‐reported measures of depressive symptoms, parental locus of control, and child negative emotionality, which may introduce bias. To reduce this concern, we averaged reports from both parents for child negative emotionality, but future research would benefit from incorporating additional methods, such as observational or physiological assessments, to capture these constructs more objectively.

Conclusion and Implications

Utilizing a longitudinal design with a focus on at‐risk military families, this study found that increases in child negative emotionality predicted later increases in maternal depressive symptoms, while increases in parental locus of control predicted subsequent declines in maternal depressive symptoms. For fathers, increases in parental locus of control were associated with increases in later child negative emotionality. These findings provide longitudinal evidence of the directionality of these associations and highlight the different patterns of dynamics for mothers and fathers. Guided by the Military Family Stress Model, which highlights how deployment‐related stress shapes family functioning, this study extends the framework by specifying the roles of parental locus of control and child negative emotionality in relation to parental depressive symptoms and clarifying the directionality of these pathways.

These findings highlight several implications for intervention. For mothers, enhancing parental locus of control may help protect against depressive symptoms, suggesting that parenting interventions should incorporate strategies to strengthen parents' confidence and sense of agency. Indeed, evidence from the current program indicates that participation was associated with higher average parental locus of control, suggesting that such interventions may simultaneously bolster mothers' sense of control and reduce depressive symptoms. At the same time, because child negative emotionality predicted maternal depressive symptoms, interventions should also target children's emotion regulation skills and equip parents with emotion‐coaching strategies to manage these challenges. Clinical interventions addressing maternal depressive symptoms should also consider the additional parenting responsibilities faced by military mothers, especially those raising children with high negative emotionality.

For fathers, both depressive symptoms and parental locus of control appeared relatively stable across the study period. From an intervention perspective, this suggests that parenting‐focused programs alone may not be sufficient. Strengthening mental health alongside parenting support may be critical for military fathers. In addition, increases in paternal locus of control were linked to heightened subsequent child negative emotionality, underscoring the importance of reintegration programs that help fathers adopt sensitive, noncoercive approaches to parenting.

Taken together, these results suggest that interventions in military families should be tailored to the different mechanisms shaping maternal and paternal adjustment, with attention to both parental sense of control and child emotional characteristics.
