Practical and positive uses of AI in STEM education: taming the AI tiger
Authors: Gregory J. Crowther, Timothy D. Paustian, Samantha T. Parks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFrom website searches and social media to financial services and health services, artificial intelligence (AI) is common in our daily lives. Such an encompassing presence is also apparent in education. All fields, including education, must identify regulations and best practices for the incorporation and regulation of AI use. As members of the Special Series Editorial Team for the Journal of Microbiology & Biology Education, we are pleased to present a compendium of perspectives, suggestions, pedagogy, and research related to the Practical and Positive Uses of AI in STEM Education.

EDITORIAL

Faculty and students alike acknowledge the expanding impact of artificial intelligence (AI) in education, inclusive of exams, assessments, scholarly writing, and brainstorming, as well as the generation of images and other materials. In particular, generative AI (genAI) encompasses a broad category wherein novel content can be created and revised. For example, for text-based tasks, large language models (LLMs) can be trained on archived written works to generate novel text outputs to alleviate time constraints and further productivity.

As many academic institutions sign large contracts with AI companies, we have noticed many of our colleagues reacting with a mix of confusion, distrust, fear, and despondency. “Are my students even doing the homework I assign?” “How can I prevent them from offloading too much cognitive load to genAI?” “How do we help students explore effective uses of genAI and be able to critically evaluate the output?” Such concerns are understandable, shared by us to some extent, and beyond the power of any one individual, department, or institution to “solve.” Nonetheless, in the face of this rise of technology, we have found it therapeutic to remember that teaching has always been about using available tools as they evolve to meet our students’ needs as they evolve. Academic responsibilities related to the use, oversight, and regulation of AI remain fluid and in development (1), but fundamentally, our job as teachers has always entailed adapting to a changing world.

Many of us were acutely pushed into adaptation mode during the COVID era’s emergency remote teaching. And while COVID had negative repercussions for education and educators, it also catalyzed innovations (as covered by the 2021 special issue of the Journal of Microbiology & Biology Education [JMBE]) that persist today. Better online office hours, better online research projects, and better grading policies that are more flexible about attendance are just a few that come to mind.

As we see it, teaching through genAI is somewhat reminiscent of teaching through COVID: a challenge that we did not plan for, with unfortunate and severe collateral damage, but also an opportunity to reconsider our students’ needs and to recalibrate our support. In that spirit, the Journal of Microbiology & Biology Education (JMBE), along with the Special Series Editorial Team (Senior Editor Samantha T. Parks and Guest Editors Gregory J. Crowther, Melanie Lenahan, Logan S. McCarty, and Timothy D. Paustian), is pleased to present the Practical and Positive Uses of AI in STEM Education themed series (https://journals.asm.org/journal/jmbe/ai-in-stem-education). We view it not only as an acknowledgment of genAI’s growing importance but as a tribute to teachers’ continuing resourcefulness and creativity. We thank the authors for their worthwhile contributions. We hope you find inspiration in the series, as we have, and we highlight some of the articles below.

PERSPECTIVES

In the series’ Perspective articles, authors took on the challenge of integrating AI into classrooms in productive ways. Dr. Uminski developed a creative way to take advantage of the visual hallucinations that AI sometimes produces (2). While AI models are getting better at creating images, they still make subtle and not-so-subtle errors, including inappropriate locations and mislabeling of structures. Dr. Uminski shows how these images can be used in what, how, why, wonder, and compare and contrast active learning exercises in which students find and correct the errors. Errant AI images can also be integrated into summative assessments to gauge students' visual literacy. These images present an excellent learning opportunity for teaching visual and AI literacy in biology. Further, Dr. Espinoza-Hernández et al. explored the role of AI in facilitating student support and modernizing STEM pedagogy. They summarize options for integrating AI at the undergraduate, graduate, postdoctoral, and faculty levels and include a call to action for educators to meaningfully and equitably incorporate AI across these levels (3).

TIPS AND TOOLS

It can feel overwhelming for educators to familiarize themselves with new technology while also incorporating it into their pedagogy. In the Tips and Tools articles, authors share best practices and ideas that are readily adopted for classroom and lab use. Dr. Mosovosky et al. describe the incorporation of a survey-style playbook to facilitate generative AI incorporation into microbiology courses. AI facilitates course development, syllabi design, and even visuals to help in lab courses (4). Dr. Jackie Shay’s article on joy-centered pedagogy (5) asks whether chatbots can be engines for joy. She answers in the affirmative, offering chatbot prompts that may help learning become more personalized and thus more joyful. Note that “joy” here does not mean effortless fun; Dr. Shay’s tips promote such feelings as student agency and productive struggle, which lead to sustained engagement that may ultimately become joyful. Dr. Shay thus conveys that academic joy, like so many other academic things, is a long-term project.

Drs. Shah and Miranda describe an activity in which students have chatbots generate information on a topic in microbiology (6). The prompts generate two to eight citations. Students then verify these citations and ascertain their appropriateness and accuracy. Analyzing AI hallucination and citation errors fosters students’ information literacy and scientific writing skills and demonstrates the promise and limitations of LLMs.

RESEARCH

While AI research continues to evolve rapidly, the Research articles section shares current findings relevant to the uses and impact of AI in STEM education. An important question is the rate of adoption of AI in teaching programs. Dr. Muslu et al. explored this rate of adoption across the United States using an extensive review and survey of medical laboratory science (MLS) programs (7). Although the medical diagnostic industry is adopting AI technology to improve efficiency and diagnostic accuracy, and although directors of MLS programs rated sequencing and AI-based tools as important in the field, less than 1% of programs offered instruction in their use. The barriers to adoption included limited space in the curriculum, lack of faculty expertise, time constraints, and insufficient infrastructure. The authors thus encourage faculty education, digital learning methods, and institutional support to expand offerings in AI and bioinformatics.

CONCLUSION

With these articles and many more, “Practical and Positive Uses of AI in STEM Education” explores the nuanced struggles and opportunities that the inclusion of AI in STEM education affords. Thought-provoking perspectives, balanced with practical and ready pedagogy, encourage consideration and use of AI in pedagogy. The technology is here. How will you tame (or embrace!) the AI tiger?
