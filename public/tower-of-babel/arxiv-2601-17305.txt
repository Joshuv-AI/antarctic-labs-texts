A New Look at the Ensemble Kalman Filter for Inverse Problems: Duality, Non-Asymptotic Analysis and Convergence Acceleration Publication type: Paper
C G Krishnanunni 1, Jonathan Wittmer ∗, Tan Bui-Thanh 1,∗, Quoc P. Nguyen 2

Abstract
This work presents new results and understanding of the Ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) for inverse problems. In particular, using a Lagrangian dual perspective we show that EnKF can be derived from the sample average approximation (SAA) of the Lagrangian dual function. The beauty of this new duality perspective is that it facilitates us to prove and numerically verify a novel non-asymptotic convergence result for the EnKF. Motivated by the new perspective, we also present a new convergence improvement strategy for the Ensemble Kalman Inversion Algorithm (EnKI), which is an iterative version of the EnKF for inverse problems. In particular, we propose an adaptive multiplicative correction to the sample covariance matrix at each iteration and we call this new algorithm as EnKI-MC (I). Based on the new duality perspective, we derive an expression for the optimal correction factor at each iteration of the EnKI algorithm to accelerate the convergence. In addition, we also consider an ensemble specific multiplicative covariance correction strategy (EnKI-MC (II)) where a different correction is employed for each ensemble. By viewing EnKI through the lens of fixed-point iteration, we also provide theoretical results that guarantees the convergence of EnKI-MC (I) algorithm. Numerical investigations for the deconvolution problem, initial condition inversion in advection-convection problem, initial condition inversion in a Lorenz 96 model, and inverse problem constrained by elliptic partial differential equation are conducted to verify the non-asymptotic results for EnKF and to assess the performance of convergence improvement strategies for EnKI. The numerical results suggest that the proposed strategies for EnKI not only led to faster convergence in comparison to the currently employed techniques but also better quality solutions at termination of the algorithm.

The field of data assimilation seeks to find the best estimate for the unknown states or parameters in a dynamical system by combining appropriate mathematical models with observations and balancing the uncertainties
[
31

The EnKF addresses the issue of expensive covariance matrix computation by replacing it with the sample covariance matrix of an ensemble. The EnKF may also be viewed as a derivative-free optimization technique, with the ensemble used as a proxy for derivative information. The EnKF propagates
N
ensembles through the dynamics, and subsequently update
this prior forecast ensemble into an updated analysis ensemble that assimilates the new observation. Multiple variants of EnKF for state-parameter estimation problems in dynamical systems are available in the literature
[
21

31

[
4
,
24
]
. Albers et al.
[
3
]
have developed an Ensemble Kalman filter capable of imposing equality or inequality constraints on ensemble based parameter and state estimation problems respectively. The popularity of EnKF as a data assimilation tool may be attributed to its effectiveness even when used with small ensemble sizes
[
8

This highlights the need for a non-asymptotic convergence analysis of the EnKF that establishes how the ensemble size influences the error in approximating the solution (i.e the posterior mean and covariance).

The possibility of using the Ensemble Kalman method as an inverse problem solver has been explored recently by various researchers where it is commonly referred to as the Ensemble Kalman Inversion (EnKI)
[
42
,
26
]

Schillings et al.

2
Derivation of the Kalman Filter state update equation from Duality

In this work, all vectors are denoted in boldface and matrices are represented in
capital letters. We also use the notation
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}
to denote the solution for the
i^{th}
ensemble member at the
k^{th}
iteration of the EnKI algorithm,
\mathbf{u}_{k}=[\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)},\dots\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(N)}]
denotes the collection of all ensembles at the
k^{th}
iteration,
{C}_{k}:={C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
denotes the sample covariance matrix formed using the
N
ensembles.

We begin by looking at the state update equation (
4
) which serves as the backbone of the Kalman filter. In this section, we will derive (
4
) as the solution to a Lagrangian dual problem. The duality view not only provides new insight into the Kalman filter but also (as we shall show) leads to a new derivation of the ensemble Kalman filter as a randomization of the dual problem.

Our departure from the mainstream of Kalman filter and ensemble Kalman filter literatures is to cast the unconstrained optimization problem (
2
) into a constrained optimization problem
by introducing an artificial constraint as follows:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}{\min}\ \mathcal{J}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{d}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}_{{{\Sigma}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{C}^{-1}},

(5)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle subject\ \ to:}\quad\quad\ A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v},

where
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for the constrained problem (
5
) reads:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}_{{{\Sigma}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\|^{2}_{{C}^{-1}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left(A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\right),

(6)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the Lagrangian multiplier. The Lagrange dual function
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for (
5
) is defined as:

\mathcal{D}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}):=\inf_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}).

(7)

By setting the derivatives of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
to zeros, and then solving for
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
as a function of
\lambda
we obtain the following dual problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\mathcal{D}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}):=-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A{C}A^{T}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left(A\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{d}\right).

(8)

The first order optimality condition for (
8
) reads:

\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}=\left({{\Sigma}}+A{C}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(A\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{d}\right)

(9)

On the other hand, the KKT conditions for (
5
) reads:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}={{\Sigma}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{d}\right)-\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{d}+{{\Sigma}}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}={C}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{0}\right)+A^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}-{C}A^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}=A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}.

We conclude that the optimal solution for (
5
)—the MAP point—is given by:

\mathbf{u}^{MAP}=\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{u}_{0}-{C}A^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}=\mathbf{u}_{0}+{C}A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A{C}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}_{0}\right).

(11)

Remark 1

Note that the dual optimality condition (
9
) is part of the KKT conditions (
10
). Furthermore, the invertibility of
{C}^{-1}
and
{{\Sigma}}^{-1}
is necessary for validity of solution (
11
) and is consistent with the conditions imposed by Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula used in the conventional derivation of (
11
). In our derivation, the conditions arise naturally via the KKT conditions
(
10
) for (
5
).

3
Derivation of the Ensemble Kalman Filter as a Randomization of the Dual Problem

Let us define:

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\sim\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}:=\mathcal{N}\left(0,{{\Sigma}}\right),\quad{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\sim\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}:=\mathcal{N}\left(0,{C}\right).

(12)

Thus,
\mathcal{\pi}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right):=\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\times\mathcal{\pi}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
is the probability density of:

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}:=\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right].

Consider the following stochastic Lagrangian dual function as:

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$};\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{d},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\right):=-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A{\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{T}A^{T}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left(A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)-\mathbf{d}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right).

(13)

Clearly,

\mathscr{D}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{\pi}}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$};\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{d},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\right)\right],

(14)

that is, (
13
) is an unbiased estimator of the dual function (
8
).
Let
\left[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(1)},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(2)}...{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(N)}\right]
and
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(1)},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(2)}...{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(N)}\right]
be ensemble pairs from
\mathcal{\pi}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)
. We can approximate the expectation on the right hand side of (
14
) via the sample average approximation (SAA), i.e.,

\mathscr{D}_{N}:=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}}^{T}A^{T}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{T}}{N}\left(A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}\right)-\mathbf{d}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(i)}\right).

(15)

If we define

\Omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(1)},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(2)}...{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(N)}\right],\quad\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)},\quad\overline{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(i)},

then the SAA optimal solution for the dual problem reads:

\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{*}:=\arg\max_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\mathscr{D}_{N}=\left({{\Sigma}}+A\Omega\Omega^{T}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\right)-\left(\mathbf{d}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\right)\right),

(16)

and the optimal dual function
\mathscr{D}_{N}^{*}
is given by:

\mathscr{D}_{N}^{*}:=-\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{*})^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A\Omega\Omega^{T}A^{T}\right)\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{*}+(\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{*})^{T}\left(A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\right)-\left(\mathbf{d}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\right)\right).

(17)

Consequently the induced SAA optimal solution for the primal problem (
5
) is given as:

\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{*}:=\mathbf{u}_{0}-{C}A^{T}\overline{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{*}=\mathbf{u}_{0}+{C}A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A\Omega\Omega^{T}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}-A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\right)\right).

(18)

If we further randomize
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
{C}
with
\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
and
\Omega\Omega^{T}
in (
18
) we obtain

\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{*}:=\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}+\Omega\Omega^{T}A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A\Omega\Omega^{T}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}-A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+\overline{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\right)\right),

(19)

which is exactly the well-known ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF).
Now unrolling the sums and defining

\mathbf{u}^{{EnKF}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}}^{*},

(20)

we have:

{{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}}^{*}:=\mathbf{u}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}+\Omega\left(A\Omega\right)^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A\Omega\left(A\Omega\right)^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(i)}-A\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}\right)\right),

(21)

which is nothing more but the ensemble update equation in Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) framework. That is, we have rediscovered the EnKF from randomizing the dual problem and the relationship between the primal and dual optimal solutions.

Non-Asymptotic Convergence results for linear inverse problem

Based on the duality viewpoint presented in section
3
, we will derive a novel non-asymptotic convergence result for the EnKF. Note that the asymptotic convergence of EnKF, i.e.

\mathbf{u}^{{EnKF}}_{N}\xrightarrow{a.s}\mathbf{u}^{MAP},\quad\mathrm{as}\ N\rightarrow\infty,

is a well-known result in literature
[
47
]
. Wittmer et al.
[
47

provided a unified framework for non-asymptotic convergence analysis of several randomized methods obtained by randomizing the inverse solution (

Remark 4.10
.

The importance of Theorem
4.6
/ Corollary
4.8
is that, given the constants
c_{1},\ c_{2}
, one can determine the number of samples
N
required so that, for a given choice of
\epsilon
, the error bound
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathbf{u}}\leq c\varepsilon
holds with a prescribed high probability
p
. In particular, one need to choose the sample size
N
that satisfies:

p\leq 1-6\mathcal{E}_{3}^{c_{2}}\left(\varepsilon,C\right)-\mathcal{E}_{4}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)-\mathcal{E}_{5}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C},\Sigma\right)-\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)-2\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c_{2}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)

For above to hold, it is sufficient to choose
N
that satisfies:

6\mathcal{E}_{3}^{c_{2}}\left(\varepsilon,C\right)\leq 6\left(\frac{1-p}{11}\right),\ \mathcal{E}_{4}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)\leq\left(\frac{1-p}{11}\right),\ \mathcal{E}_{5}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C},\Sigma\right)\leq\left(\frac{1-p}{11}\right),

\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c_{1}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)\leq\left(\frac{1-p}{11}\right),\ \ 2\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c_{2}}\left(\varepsilon,{C}\right)\leq 2\left(\frac{1-p}{11}\right).

Therefore, it is sufficient to choose
N
that satisfies:

\displaystyle N\geq c_{m}\log{\left(\frac{11}{1-p}\right)}\max

\displaystyle\Biggl\{\frac{4\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left(-\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}^{2}+4\epsilon}\right)^{2}},\ \frac{16n\left\|C^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left(-\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}^{2}+4\epsilon}\right)^{2}},

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{16m\left\|\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left(-\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}^{2}+4\epsilon}\right)^{2}},\ \frac{4n\left\|C^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}},\ \frac{\left\|C\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\Biggl\}

where
c_{m}=\max\{\frac{1}{c_{2}},\ c_{1}\}
. Even when the constants
c_{1},\ c_{2}
are unknown, (
41
) can be used to understand how the choice of two prior covariances
\left\|C_{1}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|C_{2}\right\|_{\infty}
affects the ratio of required sample sizes
\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}
, for achieving a given error bound
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathbf{u}}\leq\epsilon_{1}
. Here,
\epsilon_{1}
is a fixed accuracy to be achieved and,
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
are the minimum sample size corresponding to
\left\|C_{1}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|C_{2}\right\|_{\infty}
respectively, as computed from (
41
) (ignoring the constant
c_{m}
).

Remark 4.11

(Behavior of EnKF when

is small)
.

Of particular interest is the case when

is very small in Corollary
4.8
. The papers
[
1
,
41
,
2
]
highlight the need to increase

along small noise limits, i.e when

Consider the small noise regime where

such that required number of samples

if one requires

, then, according to (
35
), one must choose:

\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{req}}{c_{1}}\ \mathrm{for\ \mu=\mu_{1}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{req}}{c_{2}}\ \mathrm{for\ \mu=\mu_{2}},

are computed using (
37
) for

and

,

,

]
.

Remark 4.12

(Related work on non-asymptotic analysis)
.

Our
\infty
-norm based analysis provides a sufficient condition on the number of samples required when comparing two prior covariance matrices
{C}_{1}
and
{C}_{2}
(see Remark
4.10
). However, as shown in
[
2
]
, the sample size requirements for EnKF updates are more directly governed by the effective dimension, which is related to the spectral properties of the underlying covariance operators. Their non-asymptotic analysis demonstrates that sample complexity scales with the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
rather than the ambient dimension
n
.

Our work complements their analysis by providing an alternative perspective via
\infty-
norm concentration inequalities.
We show that novel non-asymptotic bounds such as Theorem
4.6
can be derived for EnKF via the duality viewpoint developed in Sections
2
and
3
. Future work will explore leveraging this duality framework to derive additional non-asymptotic bounds that depend on the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
rather than
\infty
-norms.

\Omega\Omega^{T}
in (
21
)) are updated in each iteration using the current estimates
\{{{\mathbf{u}}^{(i)}}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N}

[
26
,
42
]
. In particular, given an initial set of ensembles
\{\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
(prior measurement samples of the parameter field or generated from some assumed prior distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0},{C}\right)
) let us first define the sample covariance matrix
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
and the sample mean
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle C}(\mathbf{u}_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\right)^{T},

(42)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}.

Now by replacing
\mathbf{u}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}
with
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}
, replacing the measurement
\mathbf{d}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(i)}
with
\mathbf{d}^{(i)}
and using
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
instead of
\Omega\Omega^{T}
in (
21
), the update equation for each sample is written as:

\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(i)}=\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}+{C}(\mathbf{u}_{0})A^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{0}),

(43)

where,
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{0})
is given by:

\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{0}):=\left({{\Sigma}}+A{C}(\mathbf{u}_{0})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{(i)}-A\mathbf{u}_{0}^{(i)}\right).

(44)

Note that based on the new estimates
\{\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
from (
43
), one may compute a new sample covariance
{C}_{1}
and again update the current solution
\{\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to obtain the new estimates
\{\mathbf{u}_{2}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
via (
43
). More generally, if the scheme (
43
) is continued in an iterative fashion, we arrive at the Ensemble Kalman Inversion (EnKI) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}+{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k}),

(45)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}+{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+A{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left({\mathbf{d}}-A\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}\right),

where we assumed
{\mathbf{d}}=A\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
(
\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}
is the true solution and same measurement noise is used for each ensemble), and the matrix
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
is computed based on the current estimates
\{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Now if one introduces an artificial step size
h
and defines a new noise covariance matrix
{{\Sigma}}_{h}=\frac{{{\Sigma}}}{h}
, the scheme (
45
) using the new covariance
{{\Sigma}}_{h}
can be modified as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}+{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}_{h}+A{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}+h{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}+hA{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}\right)

It is clear that if we take
h\rightarrow 0
, (
46
) is the Euler type discretization of the following set of coupled ODEs
[
42
]
:

\frac{d\mathbf{u}^{(i)}}{dt}=-{C}(\mathbf{u})\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\Phi\left(\mathbf{u}^{(i)},\ \mathbf{d}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,

(47)

where
\mathbf{u}=[\mathbf{u}^{(1)},\ \mathbf{u}^{(2)},\dots\mathbf{u}^{(N)}]
and

\Phi\left(\mathbf{u}^{(i)},\ \mathbf{d}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2},\quad{C}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{u}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}\right)^{T}.

(48)

Thus each particle performs a preconditioned gradient descent for
\Phi\left(.,\ \mathbf{d}\right)
and all the
individual gradient descents are coupled through the preconditioning of the flow by
the empirical covariance
{C}(\mathbf{u})

[
42
]
.

Schillings et al.
[
42
]
analyzed the long-time behavior of (
47
) and proved the key properties for the EnKI algorithm in continuous-time limit(considering linear setting and noise free scenario) such as: i) (
47
) has a unique solution
\mathbf{u}^{(i)}(.)\in C([0,\infty);{\mathcal{D}})
where
{\mathcal{D}}
represents the finite dimensional subspace spanned by the initial ensembles,
\{\mathbf{u}^{(1)}(0),\ \mathbf{u}^{(2)}(0),\ \mathbf{u}^{(3)}(0)\dots\mathbf{u}^{(N)}(0)\}
; ii) collapse of all ensemble members towards their mean value at an algebraic rate. Further, the solution
\mathbf{u}^{i}(t)
to (
47
) at any time always lies in
{\mathcal{D}}
. This is referred to as the “subspace property” of the EnKI algorithm. Since EnKI performs a preconditioned gradient descent for the loss function
\Phi\left(.,\ \mathbf{d}\right)
in (
48
) and the solution always lies in the subspace
{\mathcal{D}}
, previous research
[
26
]
numerically showed that EnKI approximately solves the following least-squares minimization problem:

\underset{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{D}}}{\min}\ \mathcal{J}_{EnKI}(\mathbf{u})=\min_{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|{{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(49)

We immediately see that the “subspace property” of the EnKI algorithm plays the role of an implicit regularization in minimizing the above loss function, ensuring that the solution always lies in the subspace
{\mathcal{D}}
.

The literature on the theoretical analysis of the continuous-time limit of the EnKI algorithm is extensive
[
42
,
10
,
19
]
, and numerous variants of the EnKI algorithm exist. We discuss here some of the recent developments in the field. Ding et al.
[
19
]
analyzed the continuous time-limit version of
EnKI and proved the mean field limit (as
N\rightarrow\infty
) of the resulting system of stochastic differential equation. To prevent ensemble collapse and break the subspace property, popular techniques commonly adopted in the Kalman filter community such as additive inflation, localization has been applied to EnKI
[
14
,
44
]
. However, one of the issues with breaking the “subspace property” is that this destroys the implicit regularization imposed while minimizing (
49
). To tackle this issue, Chada et al.
[
14
,
13
]
considered a Tikhonov regularization within the EnKI framework.

Remark 5.13
.

While the variants of EnKI discussed above offer various advantages, the vanilla EnKI (
46
) remains widely used, particularly in cases where limited prior knowledge is available, such as when only a few prior measurements of the parameter field
\mathbf{u}
are accessible. In such situations, one can run the iterative scheme (
46
) using these limited measurement samples as the initial ensembles. Recently, Harris et al.
[
25
]
proposed an accuracy improvement strategy for the vanilla EnKI (
46
) through data-informed ensemble selection.

It is interesting to note that most theoretical work on EnKI relies on the continuous time-limit analysis of (
46
)
[
42
,
19
]
. In this study, we approach the EnKI algorithm from the perspective of fixed-point iteration.
In particular, we propose a perturbed fixed-point iteration to accelerate the convergence of the EnKI algorithm (
46
). We will invoke the duality perspective introduced in sections
2
and
3
to derive the perturbed fixed-point iteration.

For deriving a convergence improvement strategy for EnKI, let us first rewrite (
46
) as the following iterative scheme:

\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=g(\mathbf{u}_{k}),\quad k=0,\dots\infty,

(50)

where the function
g(\mathbf{u}_{k})
is given as:

g(\mathbf{u}_{k})=\mathbf{u}_{k}+\begin{bmatrix}{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
\vdots\\
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\end{bmatrix}\odot\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k})\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(2)}(\mathbf{u}_{k})\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k})\end{bmatrix},

(51)

where
\odot
represents the element-wise multiplication of the two tuples,
\mathbf{u}_{k}=[\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{k},\ \mathbf{u}^{(2)}_{k},\dots\mathbf{u}^{(N)}_{k}]
and

\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k})=\left({{\Sigma}}_{h}+A{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}^{(i)}_{k}\right),

(52)

{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right)^{T},

In the below discussions, we will assume that
{{\Sigma}}_{h}=\mu I
, where

is the identity matrix and

.

5.1
EnKI-MC (I): A multiplicative covariance correction strategy for convergence acceleration of EnKI

In order to speed up the convergence of EnKI algorithm and improve the quality of inverse solution, we consider a multiplicative correction to the sample covariance matrix
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
as follows:

{C}_{m}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k}),

(53)

where
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Previously, a multiplicative covariance inflation in the context of the Ensemble Kalman Filter for dynamical systems was considered by Anderson et al.
[
5
]
. In our case, this leads to the new iterative scheme:

\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=g_{m}^{(I)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(N)}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\end{bmatrix}\odot\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(2)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\end{bmatrix},\quad k=0,\dots\infty.

(54)

where the function
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))
is obtained by replacing
{C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
in (
52
) with
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
. We call (
54
) as EnKI-MC (I) (EnKI with multiplicative covariance correction). Looking carefully at (
46

\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)>1
is equivalent to using a larger step-size of
h\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
in discretizing (
47
). This is expected to improve the speed of convergence provided that the iterative scheme (
54
) is numerically stable.

Remark 5.14
.

Such adaptive correction/step-size has been considered earlier by Chada et al.
[
14
]
and Iglesias et al.
[

]

[

considered a monotonically increasing stepsize for convergence acceleration of EnKI, Iglesias et al.
[

derived adaptive stepsize for a different purpose-using EnKI within the Bayesian setting for parameter identification for approximating the target posterior distribution. The constraint that the step-size needs to be less than

in Iglesias et al.
[

leads to slow convergence of the algorithm.
In our numerical results, we provide comparison of our approach with the one by Chada et al.

since our main objective is convergence acceleration of EnKI.

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{u}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{\infty}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-k_{0}-1}L^{j}+\epsilon_{2}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{1}\frac{1-L^{k-k_{0}}}{1-L}+\epsilon_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{1-L}+\epsilon_{2}\leq\epsilon,

where we defined
\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{1-L}+\epsilon_{2}
. From (
66
) it is clear that the sequence
\{\mathbf{u}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges to
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
. In addition since
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}^{1}=\mathbf{v}_{\infty}^{2}=\dots\mathbf{v}_{\infty}^{N}
(ensembles collapses by
assumption

), we also have
\mathbf{u}_{\infty}^{1}=\mathbf{u}_{\infty}^{2}=\dots\mathbf{u}_{\infty}^{N}
. That is, all the ensembles collapses for the perturbed scheme as well on convergence.
Finally, note that (
54
) can be rewritten as:

\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=g(\mathbf{u}_{k})+\left(g_{m}^{(I)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))-g(\mathbf{u}_{k})\right),\quad k=0,\dots\infty,

(67)

where
g(\mathbf{u}_{k})
corresponds to the unperturbed scheme (
50
). Taking the limit
k\rightarrow\infty
we have:

\mathbf{u}^{*}=g(\mathbf{u}^{*})+\left(g_{m}^{(I)}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\ 1)-g(\mathbf{u}^{*})\right),

where we have used the fact that
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
,
g_{m}^{(I)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))
, and
g(\mathbf{u}_{k})
are continuous functions and also used
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=1
. Now noting that

we have:

\mathbf{u}^{*}=g(\mathbf{u}^{*}).

That is,
\mathbf{u}^{*}
is a fixed point of
g
in (
50
). Therefore, the iterative scheme (
54
) converges to a fixed point of (
50
), and this concludes the proof.

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\delta(k)}\left({\left(\bar{{\bf{r}}}_{k}^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}\bar{{\bf{r}}}_{k}\right)}\frac{d\left(\bar{{\bf{r}}}_{k}^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}A{C}_{k}A^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}\bar{{\bf{r}}}_{k}\right)}{d\alpha}\right).

5.2
EnKI-MC (II): Ensemble specific multiplicative covariance correction strategy

Note that in the EnKI update equation (
54
), the multiplicative factor
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
in (
53
) is the same for each ensemble member. However, note that one may assign a distinct correction factor
\alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
to each ensemble member, allowing larger updates for ensembles that are farther from convergence compared to those that are already close. In this section, we consider a strategy where the update equation for each ensemble, uses a different multiplicative correction to the covariance. In particular, we consider the following modification to the update rule (
54
):

\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=g_{m}^{(II)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ {\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(N)}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
\alpha^{(2)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\end{bmatrix}\odot\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(2)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha^{(2)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))\end{bmatrix},

(88)

where
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=[{\alpha}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)),\dots{\alpha}^{(N)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))]
, and

\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ \alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k))=\left({{\Sigma}}+A\alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k){C}(\mathbf{u}_{k})A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-A\mathbf{u}^{(i)}_{k}\right).

(89)

We call (
88
) as EnKI-MC (II). For designing the function
\alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
in (
88
) we follow the same strategy in section
5.1.1
.
In particular, instead of considering a sample averaged dual function (
70
), one may define the perturbed sample-specific dual function (for the
i^{th}
ensemble) by introducing the multiplicative factor
\alpha_{k}^{(i)}:=\alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{i}^{k}\left(\alpha^{(i)}_{k}\right):=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{k}^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)}_{k}))^{T}\left({{\Sigma}}_{h}+\alpha^{(i)}_{k}A{C}_{k}A^{T}\right){\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{k}^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)}_{k})-({\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{k}^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)}_{k}))^{T}{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)},

(90)

where

\displaystyle{C}_{k}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right)^{T},\quad\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(i)},

(91)

\displaystyle{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}-A{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(i)},\ \ {\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{k}^{(i)}(\alpha_{k}^{(i)})=\left({{\Sigma}}_{h}+\alpha_{k}^{(i)}A{C}_{k}A^{T}\right)^{-1}\left(A{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(i)}-\mathbf{d}\right).

Note that (
90
) is the optimal sample averaged dual function
for a given
i^{th}
ensemble where the averaging is done only to estimate the covariance
{C}_{k}
. Similar to (
71
) as
k\rightarrow\infty
one has:

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{D}_{i}^{k}(\alpha_{k}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{2}\left(A{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}^{(i)}-\mathbf{d}\right)^{T}{{\Sigma}}_{h}^{-1}\left(A{\mathbf{u}}_{\infty}^{(i)}-\mathbf{d}\right),

(92)

where we assumed
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha^{(i)}(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=1
, and that the EnKI iteration (
88
) converges and all the samples collapses, i.e. one has
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{C}_{k}={\bf{0}}
. Now may now consider the following optimization problem for faster convergence of each ensemble independently:

\min_{\alpha_{k}^{(i)}}{\mathcal{S}}(\alpha_{k}^{(i)}):=\min_{\alpha_{k}^{(i)}}\left[\left(\mathscr{D}_{i}^{k}(\alpha_{k}^{(i)})-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{D}_{i}^{k}(\alpha_{k}^{(i)})\right)^{2}+\delta^{(i)}(k)\times\left(\alpha_{k}^{(i)}-1\right)^{2}\right],\quad i=1,\dots N.

(93)

where the regularization parameter
\delta^{(i)}(k)
is chosen to satisfy
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\delta^{(i)}(k)=\infty
. Following similar assumptions and derivations in Theorem
5.17
, Proposition
5.20
and Remark
5.24
, the optimal solution
\alpha_{k}^{(i)}
is computed as:

\alpha_{k}^{(i)}\approx\alpha_{k-1}^{(i)}+\frac{\zeta_{\delta}^{(i)}\left(\alpha_{k-1}^{(i)}\right)-\alpha_{k-1}^{(i)}}{1-\frac{d\zeta_{\delta}^{(i)}\left(\alpha\right)}{d\alpha}\Bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha_{k-1}^{(i)}}},\quad\alpha_{0}^{(i)}=1,

(94)

where the function
\zeta^{(i)}_{\delta}\left(\alpha\right)
is given by:

\zeta^{(i)}_{\delta}\left(\alpha\right)=1+\frac{\left(({\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)})^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)}\right)\left(({\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)})^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}A{C}_{k}A^{T}(M(\alpha))^{-1}{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)}\right)}{4\delta^{(i)}(k)},

(95)

\delta^{(i)}(k)=\frac{3}{4q}\left(\lambda_{max}^{2}\left(A{C}_{k}A^{T}\right)\frac{\left\|{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}^{4}}{(\mu+\lambda_{min}\left(A{C}_{k}A^{T}\right))^{4}}\right)+\epsilon_{\delta}\times k,

(96)

where
\epsilon_{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
M(\alpha)
is defined in Theorem
5.17
,
{\bf{r}}_{k}^{(i)},\ {C}_{k}
are given in (
91
).

and

The EnKI iteration (

Initial Condition Inversion in an Advection-Diffusion Problem

In this section we consider a linear inverse problem governed by a parabolic PDE
[
6
]
.
The parameter to observable map maps an initial condition
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to pointwise spatiotemporal observations of the concentration field
y({\bf{x}},t)
, where
{\bf{x}}=(x_{1},\ x_{2})

\displaystyle y_{t}-\kappa\Delta y+{{\bf{v}}}\cdot\nabla y

\displaystyle=0\quad{in}\quad\Omega\times(0,T),

(98)

\displaystyle y(.,0)

\displaystyle=u\quad{in}\quad\Omega,

\displaystyle\kappa\nabla y\cdot\bf{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad{on}\quad\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

where,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\kappa>0
is the diffusion coefficient,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\operatorname{Re}}\Delta{{\bf{v}}}+\nabla q+{{\bf{v}}}\cdot\nabla{\bf{v}}

\displaystyle=0\quad{in}\quad\Omega,

(99)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf{v}}

\displaystyle=0\quad{in}\quad\Omega,

\displaystyle{\bf{v}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}\quad{on}\quad\partial\Omega.

where,
q

\operatorname{Re}
is the Reynolds number.
The Dirichlet boundary condition
{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

{\mathbf{g}}={\bf{e}}_{2}
(canonical basis vector) on the left side of the domain,
and
\mathbf{g}={\bf{0}}

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{k}}{dt}=

\displaystyle v_{k-1}\left(v_{k+1}-v_{k-2}\right)-v_{k}+F,\quad k=1,\dots n

\displaystyle v_{0}=v_{n},\ \ v_{n+1}=v_{1},\ \ v_{-1}=v_{n-1},

where
v_{k}
denotes the current state of the system at the
k-
th grid point.
F
is a forcing constant with default value
8
. The initial condition to the system is denoted as
\boldsymbol{v}(0)=\left(v_{1}(0),\dots,v_{n}(0)\right)^{T}

\boldsymbol{v}(0)

k_{r}

1
and
n
. That is we measure
m
random components of
\boldsymbol{v}(t)
. The measurement is made at time

.

In (

), we set

. The number of unique measurements locations on the grid is set as
315
. We also considered repeated measurements (with different noise) for some of the grid points giving rise to the final value of
m
as
500
.

\boldsymbol{v}(0)

=2
and an exponential sine squared kernel function i.e

is the covariance matrix associated with the exponential sine squared kernel function with length scale=0.5, periodicity=20. A small zero mean Gaussian noise (with standard deviation

\boldsymbol{v}(0)

\boldsymbol{v}(0)

The ensembles are drawn from

Conclusion

This works presented a new perspective on the understanding of the Ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF)
for inverse problems. We showed that the Kalman filter equation can be derived as the solution to a randomized dual problem. The perspective allowed us to derive a new non-asymptotic result for EnKF which we verified numerically for the 1D Deconvolution problem. Theorem
4.6
also allowed us to rigorously explain the need to increase
the sample size
N
in the small noise limit, a phenomenon commonly observed in practice (see remark
4.10
).
Further, based on the duality perspective we also developed EnKI-MC (I) and EnKI-MC (II)-two variants of the Ensemble Kalman Inversion Algorithm (EnKI) for convergence acceleration. For all the numerical examples, we observed that the developed variants led to significant convergence acceleration in comparison to other methods while also producing better quality inverse solution at termination of the algorithm. Even though EnKI-MC (I) and EnKI-MC (II), presented in Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
, are variants or extensions of the vanilla EnKI algorithm, the same methodology can be incorporated into other EnKI variants currently available. For example, the method can be adapted to the TEnKI (Tikhonov EnKI) approach presented in
[
13
]
. This extension will be explored in the future.

Details of other approaches used in comparison study

The description of methods used in our comparison study are provided here.

1.

Vanilla EnKI: Iterative scheme given by (
97
).

2.

Chada et al.
[
14
]
: This method considers an adaptive multiplicative covariance correction of the form
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=k^{\beta}
(where
0\leq\beta\leq 0.8
) suggested in (
53
) for convergence acceleration. Chada et al.
[
14
]
also considered an additive covariance inflation and Tikhonov regularization in their framework which is not considered in our implementation. We choose
\beta=0.8
in our experiments for maximum speed of convergence. Higher values of
\beta>1
is not recommended as it affects the numerical stability of the algorithm and causes significant divergence from the true solution, as illustrated in Figure

.

3.

Nesterov Acceleration
[
45
]
: Implementation of Algorithm 1 in
[
45
]
.

4.

Anderson et al.
[
5
]
: In this approach one models the covariance of the innovation (residual) as
S(\alpha_{k})={{\Sigma}}_{h}+\alpha_{k}C_{pp}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
, where
C_{pp}(\mathbf{u}_{k})
is defined in (
97
). Assuming
\bar{\mathbb{r}}_{k}|\alpha_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf{0}},S(\alpha_{k})\right)
, and assuming a prior for
\alpha_{k}
as
\alpha_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({{1}},1\right)
, the Maximum A Posteriori estimate for
\alpha_{k}
is given as:

\alpha_{k}=\arg\min_{\alpha_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbb{r}}_{k}^{T}(S(\alpha_{k}))^{-1}{\mathbb{r}}_{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\mathrm{det}(S(\alpha_{k}))\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbb{r}}_{k}-1\right)^{2}.

(103)

The estimated
\alpha_{k}
from solving (
103
) is used as the multiplicative covariance correction factor in (
53
) (that is we set
\alpha(\mathbf{u}_{k},\ k)=\alpha_{k}
.
