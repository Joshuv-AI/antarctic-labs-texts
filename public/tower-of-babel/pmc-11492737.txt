Early gesture development as a predictor of autism spectrum disorder in elevated-likelihood infants of ASD
Authors: Linru Liu, QianYing Ye, Yu Xing, Yanting Xu, HuiLin Zhu, Shaoli Lv, XiaoBing Zou, HongZhu Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGesture difficulties have been reported in later-born siblings of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Careful observation of gesture development during the first two years of children at elevated likelihood (EL) of developing ASD may identify behavioral indicators that facilitate early diagnosis.MethodsThis study enrolled 47 EL infants and 27 low-likelihood (LL) infants to explore gesture developmental trajectories and the predictive value of gesture to expedite the early detection of core characteristics of ASD. Gesture frequency, communication function, and integration ability were observed and coded from a semi-structured assessment administered longitudinally across 9–19 months of age. We conducted the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule assessment at 18–19 months for ASD’s core characteristics.ResultsThe development of joint attention (JA) gestures was slower in the EL than in the LL group. The trajectories of the two groups began to diverge at 14–18 months. Children who reached the diagnostic cutoff point for ASD showed reductions in social interaction gestures at 12–13 months, in gestures integrated with any two communication skills (G-M) at 15–16 months; and in gestures integrated with eye contact (G-E) at 18–19 months. Overall gesture and G-M integration were associated with an overall ADOS communication and social interaction score.ConclusionsThe developmental trajectories of JA gestures of EL and LL children differed. G-M gestures represent early indicators that may be a predictor of ASD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06173-5.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Infant sibling, Gesture, Developmental trajectory, Early detection

The study design

A repeated measurement study: 1. The change in the gesture variables of EL and LL groups over time. 2. The difference of gesture variables between Group-A and Group-N at different time points. 3. The correlation between gesture variables and ADOS score.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a collection of childhood neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by core difficulties in social interaction, communication, and repetitive behavior [1]. The prevalence of ASD is estimated at 1% worldwide [2]. The sibling recurrence rate of ASD has been estimated at 18.7% [3, 4]. Twenty to thirty percent of the later-born siblings of children with ASD, a population that is at elevated likelihood (EL) of developing ASD will experience one or more atypical outcomes—mild-to-moderate developmental delays and subthreshold autistic characteristics [5–7]. Because EL infants as a group are at risk for ASD or delays in other developmental milestones, they are worth studying in their own right, even apart from early identification of autism. Prospective studies of EL individuals have further delineated early features of ASD [8]. One of the earliest signs of ASD is the absence or the delayed onset of gestures [9]. This research focuses on gesture use in this EL group.

Development of gestures in typically developing infants

Gestures appear at 7-9 months of age and are refined before 18 months in typically developing (TD) children [10]. Early gestures can be classified into three communication functions: behavior regulation (BR), social interaction (SI), and joint attention (JA). BR gestures are used to control another person’s behavior; SI gestures are used to direct another person's attention to oneself; JA gestures are used to direct another person’s attention to an event, object, or person to share interest. TD infants usually master these three types of gestures by 12 months of age [10]. By the 16th month of age, infants steadily exhibit gesture-speech integration and begin to refine their linguistic skills [11]. Infants gesture more as they age before 26 months, followed by a subsequent decline in gesture use and a greater reliance on verbal expressions [12, 13].

Early gesture development in infants at elevated-likelihood of ASD

Features of gesture development have been reported consistently in EL infants later diagnosed with ASD. EL infants with ASD exhibit reduced gesture use and a lower proportion of gesture-speech integration compared to both EL infants without ASD and LL infants [14]. In addition, EL-ASD infants perform different communication functions of gesture use, including challenges in joint attention (JA) gestures and comparative advantages in behavior-regulating (BR) gestures [10]. JA gesture of EL-ASD infants is deficient in frequency (number of occurrences), quality (the integration of multiple modalities of communication, such as gestures with eye contact and/or vocalizations), and variety (different behaviors used to express IJA; e.g., using eye contact or gaze alternation to gestures such as pointing or showing) [15]. Furthermore, gesture production can predict ASD outcomes within the EL group [14]. A review suggests that the trajectory of the gestures of ASD starts deviating from the TD trajectory at some point after the first year of life [16]. This conclusion above is confirmed by gesture studies and proposes a potential developmental turning point for gesture use in EL-ASD individuals after the first year of life [16–18]. Several studies have observed that EL infants without ASD also demonstrate a reduced frequency of overall gestures before the age of two compared to LL groups, as well as delayed or atypical emergence of gesture use when compared to typically developing control infants [19, 20]. In sum, we propose that gesture use in the EL group as a whole, not just in EL-ASD, is worth studying. However, information on this topic remains limited. Several studies have shown that relative to LL peers, EL infants as a group employ significantly fewer BR gestures and JA gestures [18, 21, 22]. Only one study has observed the integration ability of gesture use of EL infants as a group and has found that EL infants exhibit a reduction in the combination of speech and gestures at 18 months than LL infants [23]. Other aspects of gesture study in the EL group that have not received much attention are communication functions, integration ability, development trajectory, and the predictive effect of ASD's core characteristics.

The Current study integrated information from multiple domains (frequency, communication function, integration ability) of gesture use and multiple time points to describe the characteristics and development trajectory of gesture use in EL infants as a group. In addition, We Study these fine-grained gesture behaviors in EL infants to search for potential early indicators before ASD core characteristics emerge. Our study aimed to answer two questions: 1) When do developmental trajectories in EL infants deviate from that of LL peers? 2) Which gestures are behavioral indicators of the core characteristics of ASD? We tested the hypothesis that a time range after the first year of life is the window for gesture differentiation and that both the integration ability and the communication function serve as key indicators of ASD's core characteristics.

Methods

Participants

We referred to an accelerated cohort design. EL and LL infants were recruited through announcements published online and during healthcare at the Child Development & Behavior Center Clinic of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. A total of 20 people were recruited from the online questionnaire platform (https://www.wjx.cn). Another 54 participants were recruited from Child Development & Behavior Center Clinic of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Inclusion criteria were: age in the ranges of 9-10 months, 12-13 months, 15-16 months, and 18-19 months; first language of Mandarin or Cantonese; voluntary parental cooperation with assessments and follow-up and ability to complete questionnaires independently. Children with a first or second-degree relative with ASD (first-degree relative refers to elder full siblings, second-degree relative refers to elder Maternal Half siblings or Paternal half-siblings and cousins ) were assigned to the EL group, while those without first- or second-degree family members with ASD were designated as LL (children from single-child families were required to have at least one cousin; those with or without cousins diagnosed with ASD were classified as either EL of LL, respectively). Infants born prematurely (gestational age <37 weeks) or with low birth weight (<2500 g) and those with central nervous system diseases including visual or hearing disorders and those diagnosed with congenital disorders often associated with autism (i.e., tuberous sclerosis; fragile X and Rett syndromes) were excluded. The selection of families who volunteer to visit our center may have led to selection bias. We used the Overall issues section of the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3) to investigate whether parents were concerned about their child’s development at the time of enrollment. We obtained informed consent from parents before participation.

A total of 47 EL and 27 LL subjects were enrolled as shown in Figure 1. Among them, 35 were enrolled at the age of 9-10 months, 25 were enrolled at the age of 12-13 months, 11 were enrolled at the age of 15-16 months, and 3 were enrolled at the age of 18-19 months. Two EL and three LL subjects completed all follow-ups. Nine EL and seven LL subjects completed three follow-ups. Fifteen EL and 10 LL subjects completed two follow-ups. Twenty-one EL and seven LL subjects completed one follow-up. Fifteen EL and 11 LL subjects completed the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) assessment at 18-19 months. Among the 26 children who completed the ADOS assessment, 10 EL and seven LL infants reached the cutoff point of ASD. The loss rate of follow-up (the proportion of infants reaching 18-19 months of age while failing to complete ADOS) was about 30%.

Procedure

According to age at enrollment, children and their primary caregivers visited the Child Development and Behavior Center of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between 1 and 4 times at 9-10 months, 12-13 months, 15-16 months, and 18-19 months.

Gesture studies with EL infants have typically employed the Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales, Developmental Profile (CSBS DP) [23]. Our study assessed and recorded gestures through a semi-structured protocol during each visit. The Griffiths Development Scales-Chinese (GDS-C) was used to evaluate developmental levels. The time interval between these two assessments was less than one week. For all subjects (including the EL group and the LL group) aged 18-19 months, Module 1 of ADOS was used to assess the core characteristics of ASD. As a part of a prospective study, this study did not establish definitive clinical diagnoses of ASD in children aged 18-19 months. Based on its research purpose, this study divided participants who either met or did not meet cutoffs for the autism spectrum diagnosis into Group A or Group N, respectively.

Materials

Communication and symbolic behavior scales—developmental profile (CSBS DP)

Wetherby and Prizant developed the Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales (CSBS) and its shortened version, the CSBS Developmental Profile (CSBS DP) [24]. The CSBS DP is used for infants and young children with physiological ages of 6 months to 2 years or preschool children with a developmental age younger than 24 months to identify communication disorders at early stages of life. It comprises two parental report measures (Infant-Toddler Checklist and the Caregiver Questionnaire) and a direct observation measure (Behavior Sample, CSBS-DP-BS). Raw scores yielded seven subscales of CSBS-DP-BS that include communication, gesture, emotion and eye gaze, sounds, words, understanding, and object use. The Interrater reliability of the CSBS-DP-BS was found to be high for each cluster, including gesture, as well as for the total score, with generalizability (g) coefficients all above 0.8. The consistency of test and retest raw scores was found to be significant and large, ranging from 0.77 to 0.91, demonstrating stability over time. Significant correlations were observed between the CSBS-DP-BS and outcome measures related to language outcomes, providing support for its predictive validity [25].The observational assessment of gestures in this study is referred to as CSBS-DP-BS. Gestures were elicited based on 11 communicative temptation activities as shown in Appendix S3. This measure was administered in a child-friendly laboratory playroom (Appendix S4) by trained researchers who had demonstrated experience with young children and test administration of standardized measures. Nine frequency variables of gesture (Appendix S3 and as described in “Video coding procedures” below) yielded three domains including Overall frequency, Communication function, and integrated gesture were coded for statistical analysis. The coders were previously trained and passed the standard consistency test (as described in “Video coding procedures” below).

Griffiths mental development scales-chinese (GDS-C)

The GDS-C was revised by the Association for Research in Infant and Child Development based on the English-language version (2006) of the Griffiths Development Assessment Scale [26]. The GDS-C was selected, as it is comprehensive in assessing all developmental domains for children up to 8 years of age. It is a popular tool in the Chinese social context and has good reliability and validity [27]. The subscales measured by this tool are Locomotor, Personal-Social, Language, Eye-Hand Coordination, Performance, and Practical Reasoning. Children aged under 2 years were assessed on the first five subscales. The subscale developmental quotients are calculated by dividing the developmental age by the actual chronological age and multiplying the result by 100. Developmental quotients below 2 standard deviations (< 70) indicate a significant delay. [28]. The developmental age of each subscale was used for statistical analysis. Clinicians who had completed an accredited training course on the GDS-C assessed the children.

The Mandarin version of the autism diagnostic observation schedule-generic (The Mandarin-ADOS-G)

The Mandarin-ADOS-G was developed by the Taiwan Autism Study Group based on the original English version [29]. The Mandarin-ADOS-G has been used widely in autism research in Mandarin-speaking populations. We chose Module 1 in this study which is for individuals with pre-language/single-word status (no spoken words, only words, or occasional phrase expressions. Higher scores indicate more autistic traits. The interrater reliability for Module 1 items was reported to be over 80%. The administration and scoring of Module 1 were conducted by examiners who had received research-level training in our study. When using the autism spectrum cutoff, the sensitivity is 94%–100%, and specificity is 52%–76% [30]. The algorithm cutoffs were used to categorize participants into one of two groups: Group A, reaching the cutoff point for autism spectrum (scores of “Communication” item ≥ 2 points, “Social Interaction” item ≥ 4 points, and the total score of two items ≥ 7 points); and Group N, not reaching the cutoff point. The sum of the "communication" score and the" social interaction" score (hereinafter, ADOS score) was used as the core characteristic index of ASD for statistical analysis.

Video coding procedures

For this research, gestures were defined as behaviors involving intentional movements interpretable by others as having a communication intent. Head nods and shakes were regarded as gestures. During the gesture measures (CSBS behavior sample), we first calculate the total number of gestures observed in the gesture measure video as the variable "overall gestures". Then, the frequency of three communication function categories was coded: BR (behavior regulation), SI (social interaction), and JA (joint attention). We further classified overall gestures into five categories and counted for each category: G-E (Gesture-eye gaze integration); G-V (Gesture-vocalizations integration); G-W (Gesture-words integration); G-M (Gesture integrated with more than one of the aforementioned communication skills, Gesture-multiple ways integration); and G-N (Gesture only; not integrated with other communication skills). Eye gaze refers to an interaction behavior in which the gaze is fixed on the eye area of ​​others. Vocalizations are vocal utterances that do not contain words or speech sounds consistently used to refer to a specific object or event. Words refer to verbal utterances containing at least one “word”.

We set the time of gesture assessment at approximately 30 min. Although the assessment videos of the participants varied in actual duration, there was no statistically significant difference observed between the EL and LL groups (Appendix S13). We divided the number of gestures by the actual duration (min) and multiplied the result by 30 min to derive the number of gestures for analysis. We calculated the number of overall; BR; SI; JA; G-V; G-W; G-E; G-M; and G-N gestures for for statistical analysis. These dependent variables are described in Appendix S5.

Two researchers coded eight videos for training independently until coding reliability was greater than 0.80. Two trained researchers coded the research videos independently; Researcher A coded all videos, and Researcher B coded a random selection of 20% of the videos for a reliability check. We estimated reliability between coders with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using the two-way random effects models of absolute agreement. ICCs for independently classified gestures were 0.92 (95% confidence interval [CI] [0.84, 0.97]) for overall; 0.87 (95% CI [0.73, 0.94]) for BR; 0.91 (95% CI [0.81, 0.96]) for JA; 0.89 (95% CI [0.77, 0.95]) for SI; 0.94 (95% CI [0.86, 0.97]) for G-E; 0.81 (95% CI [0.62, 0.91]) for G-V; 0.80 (95% CI [0.60, 0.91]) for G-W; 0.96 (95% CI [0.91, 0.98]) for G-M; and 0.80 (95% CI [0.60, 0.91]) for G-N gestures.

Data analysis

Data of gesture and developmental age departed from a normal distribution. The chronological ages of children and their parents conformed to normal distribution characteristics. We used the arithmetic mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data for statistical description. For non-normally distributed data, we used median and full range for statistical description. Intergroup comparisons were performed by using non-parametric statistics (Mann-Whitney) for skewed data, two-sample t-tests for normally distributed data, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. SPSS Statistics (version 23) software was used for analysis. The Bonferroni method was applied to account for difference analysis between multiple groups, with P<0.05/N deemed significant (N= number of variables). For multiple group comparisons of gestures, P<0.0055 (0.05/9) was deemed significant (Tables 1 and 2) and P<0.0083 (0.05/6) was deemed significant for multiple group comparisons of developmental age (Appendix S1 and S2). For other analyses, we followed convention with P<0.05 indicating significance.

We analyzed the longitudinal changes in gestures and comparisons of longitudinal changes between EL and LL groups with the generalized additive mixed model (GAMM). Mixed-effect models can be recommended as a primary analysis for missing data [31]. All models included intercept and age as random terms. Groups were entered into the models as fixed effects. In mixed-effects regression models, the interaction term between a fixed effect variable and age evaluates whether this variable was a predictor of the longitudinal changes in the gesture variable. We performed linear model and quadratic model fitting on nine gesture variables and determined the most suitable model through the likelihood ratio test. Among them, linear models were used for JA gesture (Equation 2- 2) and quadratic models for other gesture variables (Equation 2- 1) [32]. Analyses were performed using EmpowerStats (version 2.0) software. The Bonferroni method was applied to account for multiple comparisons in regression models, with P<0.00714 deemed significant (=0.05/7).

Here, G represents the group. T represents time (age). β0 and β4 represent deviations in the initial level (i.e., intercept). β2 and β7 represent the difference in intercept between the EL group and the LL group. β1 and β5 represent instantaneous linear growth, β6 represents quadratic growth, β3, and β8 represent the deviation of the EL group from the LL group in instantaneous linear growth, and β9 represents the deviation of the EL group from the LL group in quadratic growth (i.e., acceleration or deceleration).

We used partial least squares regression (PLS-R) to analyze the relationship between ADOS scores and gestures. PLS-R included 26 individuals who were observed until 18 months of age and completed the ADOS at that time. We performed PLS-R at 12-13 months, 15-16 months, and 18-19 months separately. We first determined the number of components based on the PRESS value calculated by leave-one-out cross-validation and the root mean square prediction error. Further, we established the final model based on the components and obtained regression coefficients for different explanatory variables (gestures). We used the Quenouille-Tukey jackknife method to estimate the variance of regression coefficients and conduct a significance test [33]. We used R3.6.1 software with an alpha set at 0.01.

The developmental ages of Groups A and N were comparable (Appendix S2).

Developmental trajectory of gestures

We used the GAMM model to look at Group × Age interactions across the nine gesture variables between EL and LL groups. Analyses of Group × Age interactions were statistically significant for JA gestures (β3=-0.88, P<0.001) and nonsignificant for overall (β8=7.25, P=0.36; β9=-0.32, P=0.25); BR (β8=2.87, P=0.56; β9=-0.15, P=0.38); SI (β8=2.80, P=0.02; β9=-0.10, P=0.02); Gesture-vocalizations integration (β8=0.20, P=0.94; β9=-0.04, P=0.67); Gesture-words integration (β3=-0.05, P =0.60); Gesture-eye gaze integration (β8=-0.21, P=0.90, β9=-0.01, P=0.96); Gesture-multiple ways integration (β8=0.85, P=0.76, β9=-0.04, P=0.71); and G-N gestures (β8=-2.45, P=0.39, β9=0.07, P=0.51). Gesture predictive value for early core characteristics of ASD

We further analyzed the correlation between gesture measure and ASD score after 12 months. We first examined the gesture differences between Groups A and N and then conducted the PLS-R using gestures and ADOS scores. We combined the above two-step analysis results to determine gesture indicators’ predictive value for early core characteristics of ASD. Planned group contrasts indicated that Group A used fewer SI gestures (Z=-3.14, P=0.002) at 12-13 months and fewer G-M (Gesture-multiple ways integration) gestures (Z=-2.84, P=0.003) at 15-16months. Fewer G-E (Gesture-eye gaze integration) gestures (Z=-3.14, P=0.002) were exhibited by Group A at 18-19 months. Groups A and N did not differ significantly on overall gestures; BR; JA; G-V (Gesture-vocalizations integration); G-W (Gesture-words integration); G-M; and G-N gestures (Gesture only; not integrated with other communication skills) at 12-13 months (P>0.05). There were no significant group differences for overall; BR; JA; SI; G-V; G-W; G-E and G-N gestures at 15–16 months (P>0.05). overall; BR; JA; SI; G-V; G-W; G-M; and G-N gestures were similar at 18–19 months (P>0.05) (Table 1).

Additional PLS-R analyses tested the association between gestures and ADOS score. Based on the PRESS value (Appendix S7) and the mean square error (Appendix S8), we determined one component for 12-13 months,15-16 months, and two components for 18-19 months. Overall gestures (β=-0.14, P=0.004), G-M gestures (β=-0.15, P=0.004) at 15-16 months, and G-M gestures (β=-0.30, P=0.0002) at 18-19 months were significantly correlated with ADOS score. Discussion

The study aims to integrate information from multiple domains (frequency, communication function, integration ability) of gesture use and incorporate data from multiple time points to delineate the developmental trajectory of gesture use in EL infants as a collective group. Additionally, it seeks to identify key indicators associated with core characteristics of ASD. Our first hypothesis posits that the period after the first year of life is crucial for differentiating gestures between EL infants and LL infants. Furthermore, we hypothesize that the integration ability and communication function of gestures serve as significant indicators of core characteristics associated with ASD. The main findings are as follows: (1) The trajectories of JA gestures in the EL and LL groups diverged between 14 to 18 months; (2) Multiple integration ability of gesture at 15–16 months old may be a predictor of ASD.

Atypical gesture development among EL infants

The gesture development of EL children is not behind that of LL children until 14 months of age, with both groups showing increasing developmental trajectories over time. However, in the EL group, the rate of development for JA gestures is relatively slower than that observed in the LL group, leading to differentiation between the two groups during the 14–18 months period. This current study aligns with previous literature finding that EL children do not use fewer IJA (Initiating joint attention) behaviors than their TD peers prior to 14 months of age [17]. Differentiation in JA gestures between EL infants and LL infants after 14 months of age may be related to a reduced floor effect and JA use increases for typically developing infants between 12 to 14 months.

We found no significant intergroup differences of trajectories in overall gestures; BR gestures; SI gestures; gesture-vocalizations integration(G-V); gesture-words integration(G-W); gesture-eye gaze integration(G-E); gesture-multiple ways integration(G-M); and G-N gestures. Still, it is worth noting that although the interactions between group and age were statistically nonsignificant for these gestures, the gesture development of the EL group showed a trend of earlier decline in gesture frequency than that of the LL group which may correspond to the general pattern of the onset of ASD [34]. The LL group also showed a tendency to reduce their gesture use after 16 months, which may reflect developmental effects. Specifically, the decline of BR gestures in the LL group combined with the increase of SI and JA gestures may reflect the progress of children's social reciprocity. The decline of G-V gestures in the LL group combined with the increase of G-W gestures may reflect the conversion from vocalization to word. However, in order to detect differences, further research should be conducted with larger sample sizes. Nevertheless, we briefly discuss some tentative interpretations of the different trajectory patterns as they may inspire future research questions.

The clinical value of gestures in the early detection of ASD

Infants who meet the ADOS cutoff point for autism spectrum disorder exhibit notable deficits in gestures integration ability and social-interactive (SI) gestures compared to infants who do not reach the cut-off score. Of these deficits, there is a negative correlation between multiple integration ability at 15–16 months old and ADOS scores, serving as an early indicator of ASD emergence. For SI gestures, children reaching the cutoff point for autism spectrum used fewer SI gestures at 12–13 months. This result aligns with previous study finding that reduced frequency of social interaction gestures was a striking feature in ASD infants at 12 months [35]. The lack of SI gestures reflects the reduction of ASD infants' early social-orienting which will increase the ASC (Autism Spectrum Condition) diagnosis risk [36]. However, the limited use of SI gestures in both EL and LL groups across different age groups poses challenges in identifying additional features of SI gestures. This may be attributed to the lack of dyadic interactions (focus on self and others, such as face-to-face games) during the gesture evaluation process, which may not effectively stimulate social interaction (SI), where SI gestures are commonly employed.

The predictive effect of insufficient gesture frequency on the diagnosis of ASD has been confirmed by multiple studies. Studies have suggested that no matter whether in semi-structured situations or natural situations, ASD children use fewer gestures to communicate than TD children before the age of two [10, 37, 38]. Our study has detected this predictor of core features of ASD at 15–16 months of age. Perhaps because of the increase in overall gesture use in the EL group and the ceiling effect in the LL group, the differences between the groups were reduced and the predictive effect became insignificant at 18–19 months of age.

Our previous research demonstrated that ASD children aged 2–4 years showed fewer eye-gaze-integrated gestures [39]. Under the same coding system, we found this gesture characteristic appeared at 18 months. Our research further analyzed eye gaze co-occurred with concurrent communicative gestures and words or vocalizations to predict core social communication features of ASD at the age of 15 months, and this predictive ability was stable during 18–19 months. The deficiency of integrated gesture expression is observed in infants who reach the threshold for ASD, even when their motor and language skills measured by the GDS-C are equivalent to those of a control group. This suggests that the deficiency is specific to ASD characteristics rather than being a more general marker of other developmental delays. Difficulty in integrating gestures, visual attention, and language systems across multiple channels in infancy might be the core feature of ASD on the level of early gestures. Motor-auditory-visual integration has not received much attention in the study of the EL group [23]. The mirror neuron system is primarily responsible for the integration of motor-auditory-visual information processing and is related to aspects of language learning that include action understanding and recognition [40]. The further study of the neural mechanisms of multiple integrations in EL children will be of crucial significance.

To our knowledge, we report the first study to describe gesture development in children with elevated likelihood of ASD from different dimensions in China. This study has several strengths, such as direct observation measures under a semistructured play-based interaction that ensured conducting gesture evaluation from a similar communication context across all infants and ages. The study is limited by a relatively small sample size and a dropout rate of approximately 30%, which results in insufficient statistical power and the results need to be interpreted with caution. Specifically, although distinct developmental patterns were observed between EL and LL through visualization, no statistically significant differences were found. Adjusted p-values were further filtered to be 0.05/N (here N equals the total number of variables) to control for false positives. Another important limitation is that the rate of reaching the ASD cutoff point in EL infants in our study was approximately 66.6%. However, previous studies have shown that the recurrence rate of autism in EL infants is approximately 18.7% [4]. One potential explanation for this discrepancy could be the difference in diagnosis age; while the aforementioned study used a diagnosis age of 36 months, our grouping based on ADOS results occurred at 18 months and a definitive clinical diagnosis has not yet been established. Another possible reason could be sampling bias; for example, mothers concerned about their infant’s development might have been more likely to participate in follow-up than those whose infants did not display early developmental abnormalities. The Overall Issues section of the ASQ-3 was utilized to investigate whether parents had concerns about their children’s development at enrollment. We observed high levels of concern from caregivers in both groups with no significant difference between them (Appendix S10). Given these limitations, our research findings may not be generalizable to a broader population of EL infants, particularly those who have not been identified by parents as experiencing developmental difficulties within the first year. To better assess change over time, our future research should consider implementing planned missingness that incorporates multiple overlapping time points, and potentially additional cohorts to expand the period under investigation. We will continue to proceed with more rigorous design investigation and large-sample, high-quality longitudinal studies, to provide more persuasive evidence. Real-time telephone consultations, child health services, or an expert outpatient service will be enhanced after the interview to improve follow-up compliance. In addition, we will expand recruitment channels, such as online and community recruitment, and better control bias.

Conclusion

We observed a trend that requires further confirmation: the developmental trajectories of JA gestures of EL and LL children diverged at 14–18 months of age. Our study suggests that the age 14–18 months is a critical period for gesture development and that the ability to integrate gestures with other communication skills may be a key factor in facilitating the early detection of ASD. This study highlights the need for dynamic monitoring of EL children. This monitoring does not necessarily entail a comprehensive assessment, but rather an evaluation of more fine-grained behaviors. Taken together, our findings provide a starting point for future research. Our future research will continue a longitudinal study on this cohort to comprehensively investigate the relationship between early nonverbal communication and diverse outcomes, specifically across different subtypes of ASD. Additionally, we aim to explore underlying factors contributing to various facets of gesture expression features encompassing early attention characteristics and mechanisms involved in initial learning processes.
