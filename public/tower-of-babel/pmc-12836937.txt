Application of DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistant in teaching anesthesiology theories
Authors: Hao Fan, Wenqin Du, Jia Yan, Hong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical education, particularly in specialized fields like anesthesiology, faces challenges of extensive knowledge systems and low teaching efficiency. Traditional teaching methods often fail to meet individualized learning needs and effectively assess students’ mastery of key learning points. Anesthesiology education is further complicated by its broad learning points and densely constructed knowledge systems, yet it occupies a small portion of undergraduate medical curricula. Recent advancements in generative artificial intelligence (AI) offer promising solutions to enhance medical education. This study explores the application of a DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistant in teaching anesthesiology theories.MethodsA single-center, randomized controlled trial was conducted with 48 participants, including 24 fourth-year medical students and 24 non-anesthesiology resident physicians. Participants were stratified by trainee type, then randomized 1:1 via a random-number table. Whereas the control group received traditional teaching (lectures, Q&A, clinical rotations); the experimental group received traditional teaching (same as the control group) plus 24/7 online support, personalized learning plans, and real-time performance analysis from the AI assistant.ResultsThe experimental group achieved significantly higher theoretical test scores immediately after the course compared to the control group (P = 0.007). However, no significant difference was observed in knowledge retention one month later (P = 0.277). Student satisfaction surveys showed significantly higher scores in learning engagement and responsiveness to questions for the experimental group (P < 0.05), but no significant differences in learning interest or satisfaction.ConclusionsThe DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistant enhanced short-term knowledge acquisition and learning engagement in anesthesiology education. However, long-term knowledge retention and overall satisfaction were not significantly improved. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer observation periods are needed to further validate the effectiveness of AI teaching assistants in medical education and explore their potential in promoting clinical skill training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08494-9.Keywords: Medical education, Anesthesiology education, Artificial intelligence, DeepSeek, AI teaching assistant

Medical education has long grappled with the dual challenges of a voluminous knowledge system and low teaching efficiency. As noted in a recent commentary, mere knowledge memorization is no longer the core value of medical education in the current era, which highlights the inefficiency of traditional models [1]. Specifically, the traditional Lecture-Based Learning (LBL) approach, plagued by inherent limitations such as information overload, inconsistent content quality, and difficulties in standardization [2, 3], tends to foster passive knowledge acquisition among students and fails to accommodate individualized learning needs. Furthermore, the improvement of teaching effectiveness is also constrained by faculty members’ own assessment capabilities. Empirical studies have demonstrated that when providing feedback, faculty members are often hindered by concerns about interpersonal relationships and their own cognitive biases, thereby compromising the accuracy and effectiveness of assessments [4].

Anesthesiology, as a highly specialized clinical discipline, presents unique challenges in its theoretical teaching. The subject covers a broad range of learning points as well as densely constructed knowledge systems, requiring students’ dedication of large amounts of time and concentration. Yet anesthesiology occupies only a small proportion of undergraduate medical curricula (e.g., accounting for only 7.8% of clinical courses at our institution). Compounded by limited time and opportunities of early clinical exposure, the acquirement of foundational anesthesia knowledge was found to be unsatisfactory among students [5]. Thus, proposing feasible solutions to enhance the precision (i.e., aligning teaching with individual learning needs) and efficiency (i.e., optimizing knowledge acquisition within limited curriculum time) of anesthesiology education remains an urgent priority.

Recent breakthroughs in generative artificial intelligence (AI) offer promising solutions for upgrading medical education paradigms [6]. Large language models (LLMs) like DeepSeek, leveraging deep learning and knowledge distillation, demonstrate robust natural language processing and multimodal reasoning capabilities [7, 8]. For instance, anatomical courses overcome cadaver scarcity and gain flexibility via AI tools [9]; surgical and clinical trainees improve knowledge and reasoning with LLMs [6, 10]; anesthesiology students boost board-relevant mastery through LLM tutoring [11]; and a 2024 systematic review of 87 adaptive systems confirms “significant gains in learning outcomes” throughout medical education [2]. Existing literature primarily sheds light on technical feasibility or general knowledge assessments of AI-assisted medical teaching. Critical aspects such as learning situation analysis, personalized test generation, and early performance prediction remain unexplored. The lack of empirical evidence supporting whether DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistants can overcome the limitations of traditional methods calls for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in real-world teaching scenarios.

This study pioneers the application of a DeepSeek-facilitated AI teaching assistant in imparting foundational anesthesiology theories, and designs a single-center RCT to explore the efficacy and application value of the AI teaching assistant in optimizing the acquiring of knowledge, learning experiences, and teaching feedback efficiency, providing evidence-based insights into AI-driven advancements in anesthesiology education.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study included 24 fourth-year undergraduates majoring in clinical medicine, enrolled in 2021, who were completing their clinical rotations in the Department of Anesthesiology at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in April 2025, and 24 non-anesthesiology resident physicians rotating in the same department from March to May 2025. The inclusion criteria are ① voluntary participation in the study; ②proficiency in using smartphones, computers, and online learning platforms; ③resident physicians not specializing in anesthesiology. While the exclusion criteria are: ①resistant to participating the study; ②rotation lasting less than one week. All 48 participants (24 fourth-year clinical medicine undergraduates and 24 non-anesthesiology resident physicians) were first stratified by participant type. Within each stratum, participants were randomly assigned to the experimental group or control group using a random number table. Each group (24 participants per group) contained 12 undergraduates and 12 resident physicians, with the balanced distribution of the two participant types achieved through stratified randomization rather than simple randomization. The control group adopted traditional teaching method, whereas the experimental group was taught using an AI teaching assistant based on DeepSeek. The study flow is presented in Fig. 1.

Methods of developing a DeepSeek-Based AI teaching assistant

An AI teaching assistant system powered by the DeepSeek was built on the online learning platform of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (http://jdyxy.fanya.chaoxing.com/portal) through following steps. First, the DeepSeek model was integrated into the online teaching platform, and the system was configured the system to ensure the smooth operation of the AI teaching assistant tool. Next, developed a knowledge base by uploading course materials and lesson plans to the platform, and created a Q&A database and test bank to enrich teaching resources. A knowledge graph covering the core system of anesthesia and critical care emergency medicine was constructed, covering 1,215 high-frequency knowledge points, including basic theories, clinical pathways, treatment processes, typical cases and complication management, and other dimensions. Then, refine the AI assistant’s response skills to ensure it provides more professional and precise responses to students’ questions based on the uploaded course content, rather than general answers based on public knowledge. Following this, customize intelligent agents tailored to actual and specific teaching needs, such as a “Clinical Thinking Training Assistant”. Once implemented, open the student interface and arrange training for students so that they can skillfully utilize the AI assistant for learning. Finally, maintain optimization and iteration to improve the AI assistant’s performance and teaching outcomes according to students’ feedback and real-world teaching situations. The anesthesia-related knowledge graph and the detailed process of building the AI Teaching Assistant are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Research methods

Both groups (stratified by undergraduate/resident type and randomized) adopted a unified teaching model of ‘theoretical instruction combined with clinical rotations under supervising physicians.’ Four senior lecturers were randomly assigned to cover both the experimental (AI-assisted) and control groups, and all received uniform training on the study protocol, standardized teaching content, and assessment criteria to ensure consistent instructional quality. The experimental group received traditional teaching (same as the control group) plus 24/7 online support, personalized learning plans, and real-time performance analysis from the AI assistant.

Control group

Traditional teaching methods were implemented in the control group to impart fundamental anesthesiology theories. The specific teaching process was as follows. First, lectures covering all key theoretical concepts were delivered systematically by experienced anesthesiology faculty in classrooms where students were obliged to attend the entire series of classes and absorb as many key points as possible. Subsequently, a certain period was allocated for instructors to address student questions and provide additional clarification on challenging topics. Further, students participated in clinical rotations under the guidance of supervising physicians, during which they could associate theoretical knowledge with clinical practices and obtain more profound comprehension and mastery. A standardized theoretical assessment was required upon course completion.

Experimental group

An AI teaching assistant was incorporated in the didactic lecturing courses for students in the experimental group. During lessons, the AI assistant provided 24/7 online support services, offering not only precise explanations of course contents but also putting forward targeted practice questions for each key concept. It could develop personalized study plans and recommend tailored exercises by accurately assessing students’ learning progress. After monitoring and analyzing students’ responses and interactions in real-time, it produced detailed performance reports for each student. When common errors were identified across tests, the system would prompt teachers, providing insights into teaching strategies’ adjustment and specific knowledge points’ reinforcement.

Evaluation metrics

Theoretical test scores

This study’s primary outcome is the theoretical knowledge test. All test questions were developed collaboratively by four senior anesthesiology lecturers (with educational assessment expertise); no AI was involved. The test exclusively used multiple-choice questions (MCQs), covering core anesthesiology theories. And to avoid potential advantages for the experimental group (as noted), all knowledge test questions previously covered by the AI assistant were explicitly excluded to ensure assessment fairness.

Knowledge retention rate

A follow-up test was conducted one month after the course to assess long-term memory retention among students in both groups.

Student satisfaction survey

A 5-point Likert scale [12] (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied) was used to evaluate students’ satisfaction in terms of learning interest, teaching quality, engagement level, and responsiveness to questions during the learning process.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Quantitative data (e.g., test scores, 30-day post-course test scores) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), with between-group comparisons analyzed by independent samples t-test. Ordinal data (e.g., satisfaction scores) were presented as median (IQR), with between-group comparisons assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. When P < 0.05, it was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

We conducted a single-center RCT to explore the application of a DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistant system in teaching foundational anesthesiology theories. Our findings demonstrated that the experimental group significantly outperformed the control group in theoretical test scores. However, no significant differences were observed in knowledge retention rate or other dimensions of student satisfaction. Specifically, the AI teaching assistant significantly improved students’ short-term knowledge mastery; however, no significant changes were observed in long-term memory effects, learning interest, teaching satisfaction, and other dimensions due to its intervention.

Notably, the DeepSeek large language model exhibited multiple prominent advantages in this study [7, 8]. First, as a free and open-source model, DeepSeek supports local deployment, which effectively mitigates the risk of privacy data leakage involving students and patients [8]—a critical consideration in the current educational context where data privacy is highly emphasized, thereby enhancing its practical applicability. Second, DeepSeek possesses robust natural language understanding and generation capabilities, enabling it to tackle diverse complex linguistic tasks. It can comprehend students’ intricate questions and needs, and deliver answers and explanations in natural, fluent language. This allows students to experience interactions similar to conversing with a human instructor when using the AI teaching assistant, thereby boosting their learning interest and enthusiasm. Third, although AI “hallucinations” are unavoidable in large language models [13, 14], DeepSeek minimizes such occurrences in the specialized field of anesthesiology by relying on a customized localized knowledge base. This ensures the delivery of accurate and authoritative knowledge responses, which is indispensable for anesthesiology teaching—characterized by strong professionalism and a rigorous knowledge system—since it guarantees the accuracy and reliability of knowledge acquired by students. Fourth, DeepSeek exhibits powerful reasoning capabilities with a transparent, visualizable reasoning process. This not only helps teachers and students promptly identify potential logical errors in the AI teaching assistant, avoiding the “black-box effect,” but also guides students to gradually develop clinical thinking [15]. For instance, during the learning process, students can observe the AI teaching assistant’s reasoning process, learn problem-analysis approaches. While the potential of AI to support problem-solving remains a topic for future investigation, the current study focuses on evaluating AI’s role in knowledge acquisition.

In this study, the results indicate that the experimental group achieved significantly higher theoretical assessment scores and markedly greater learning engagement compared to the control group. This finding aligns with the conclusions reported by Li et al. [5], and the observed improvements are closely attributed to the AI teaching assistant’s ability to enhance educational precision and learning efficiency—two key limitations of traditional anesthesiology teaching that the AI system was specifically designed to address [6, 11, 16]. Regarding educational precision, the AI teaching assistant overcomes the “one-size-fits-all” constraint of traditional lectures by delivering targeted instruction. It leverages a structured anesthesiology knowledge graph to map students’ mastery of core content. Through real-time analysis of student interactions, the AI identifies individual knowledge gaps: it provides reviews of foundational concepts for learners with limited prior knowledge and expands on advanced clinical cases for proficient students. This design adheres to Vygotsky’s “Zone of Proximal Development” theory [17, 18], which emphasizes scaffolded instruction tailored to individual cognitive levels. Additionally, DeepSeek’s local deployment further enhances precision by prioritizing responses from the customized anesthesiology database over generic public knowledge—minimizing AI “hallucinations” [8, 9] and ensuring that all delivered information aligns with clinical guidelines, thus safeguarding knowledge reliability. In terms of learning efficiency, the AI teaching assistant optimizes both student learning cycles and teacher feedback processes. For students, its 24/7 online support eliminates delays in question resolution—a common issue in traditional teaching, where students often wait for scheduled lectures or limited instructor availability. This advantage is reflected in the experimental group’s significantly higher “responsiveness to questions” scores. Furthermore, the AI automatically generates personalized study plans and targeted practice questions based on individual learning progress, reducing the time students spend on unstructured self-study. For teachers, the AI’s real-time performance analysis automates the generation of detailed student reports and flags class-wide knowledge gaps. This reduces the workload of manual grading and lesson adjustment, allowing teachers to focus on reinforcing high-priority topics. Collectively, these efficiency-driven features streamline knowledge acquisition, directly explaining why the experimental group achieved superior immediate theoretical scores.

Although the AI teaching assistant significantly improved immediate theoretical test scores and learning engagement, no significant differences were observed in 1-month knowledge retention or student satisfaction, suggesting limited impact on long-term memory consolidation, sustained motivation, or overall teaching experience. This null finding likely stems from multiple interrelated factors. First, the AI system—designed primarily for 24/7 online support and targeted practice—lacked core evidence-based strategies for durable learning, such as spaced repetition and active recall, which are critical for long-term retention. Second, the 1-month follow-up, while practical, may be insufficient to fully manifest differential forgetting curves or the delayed benefits of AI-supported learning. Third, the sample size provided adequate power only to detect large effect sizes, rendering it underpowered to capture the modest 3.4-point retention gap observed. Finally, uncontrolled individual differences in baseline memory capacity, self-directed review behaviors, and prior knowledge likely introduced noise that masked potential AI effects. Together, these factors—notably the absence of retention-optimized AI features and limited statistical sensitivity—explain the lack of significant long-term outcomes despite clear short-term gains. For students’ satisfaction, the results of the survey showed that the experimental group rated significantly higher scores than the control group in terms of learning participation and the timeliness of answering questions, but the interest and satisfaction were not synchronously improved, suggesting that the current system can be optimized by embedding emotionally friendly functions such as incentive mechanisms and gamification elements [19].

Admittedly, this study has several limitations. The 48-participant sample was powered to detect only large retention effects, so small but educationally relevant gains may have been missed. The AI lacked spaced-repetition or active-recall modules, and no post-course reinforcement was provided, both of which are critical for long-term consolidation; uncontrolled individual review further diluted any signal. Although four senior lecturers, randomly assigned to both groups, delivered identical curricula and clinical rotations, full instructor blinding was impossible; real-time AI feedback on learner gaps could have subtly biased teaching, yet the large, immediate between-group gains indicate that this bias did not override the AI’s independent short-term benefit. Finally, the design confounded which AI components (24/7 online support, personalised plans, etc.) drove the effect; future trials should compare passive versus active AI arms while equating study time.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the DeepSeek-based AI teaching assistant significantly enhances short-term outcomes in teaching anesthesia theories, particularly in knowledge retention and learner engagement. Although long-term effects and clinical skill transferability require further validation, this research establishes an empirical foundation for deeper integration between the AI technology and anesthesia education. While these findings are encouraging, the extent to which these outcomes reflect the AI’s personalization features versus increased access to textbook material remains to be further clarified.
