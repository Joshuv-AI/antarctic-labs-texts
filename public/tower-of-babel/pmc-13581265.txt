Analysis of EBV Infection and Gene Integration in Cervical Exfoliated Cells from HPV-Positive Women
Authors: Liang Lai, Lu Ruijiao, Yangchun Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEpstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection and gene integration have been detected in cervical cancer tissues, but the association between the two has not yet been confirmed. This study employed a high-throughput viral integration detection method to analyze EBV infection and integration status in cervical exfoliated cells from 88 HPV-positive women, investigating whether a correlation exists between the two.Patients and MethodsCervical exfoliated cell samples from 88 HPV-positive female patients were obtained from the Cancer Hospital Affiliated to Xinjiang Medical University. Liquid-based cytology was diagnosed by hospital pathologists, followed by high-throughput detection to evaluate EBV and HPV infection and integration.ResultsWe found that EBV was detected in 34.09% (30/88) of cervical exfoliated cells from HPV-positive females. The EBV positivity rate was 31.65% (25/79) in cases with multiple HPV infections and 55.56% (5/9) in those with single HPV subtype infection. The EBV infection rate in samples with HPV16/18 subtype infection was 41.86% (18/43), while in samples without HPV16/18 subtype infection, the EBV infection rate was 26.67% (12/45). According to the 2019 guidelines of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP), based on the risk of developing CIN III or higher lesions within five years, the EBV infection rates were 25.93% (7/27) in the normal group, 32.35% (11/34) in the low-risk group, and 44.44% (12/27) in the high-risk group. The most common sites for EBV self-integration occur within protein-coding regions, while integrations at repetitive sequences are relatively rare. In cases of HPV and EBV co-infection, HPV and EBV exhibit correlations in integration events and the distribution of integrated genes on host chromosomes. Under high-risk conditions, the number of EBV integrations, the genes involved in integration, and differentially expressed genes all exhibit significant differences.ConclusionEBV potentially contributes to cervical cancer progression, and additional research and experimental verification are required.Keywords: cervical cancer, human papillomavirus, gene integration, Epstein-Barr virus, oncogenes, co-infection

Introduction

In recent years, cervical cancer has ranked among the top four malignant tumors affecting women. Although humanity has achieved significant breakthroughs in preventing cervical cancer through the expanding coverage of HPV vaccination, early screening remains indispensable for the prevention and control of this disease. We already know that high-risk HPV types are closely associated with the development of cervical cancer. However, not all women infected with high-risk HPV will develop cervical cancer. Most women can clear the virus through their own immune systems after infection, while only a small proportion progress to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia or even cervical cancer.1 Meanwhile, we did not detect HPV in the lesion tissues of another group of cervical cancer patients.2 We have reason to believe that other factors are involved in the process of cervical carcinogenesis during cervical lesion development.

The EBV is a human herpesvirus primarily containing double-stranded linear DNA, belonging to the γ subfamily of the genus Human Herpesvirus. Its length varies among different strains, averaging 172 kb. Since its initial discovery in 1964, it has been widely prevalent in human populations. There are reports indicating the presence of EBV in cervical cancer tissues and cervical exfoliated cells,3 while other studies suggest EBV can be transmitted sexually.4 Given its carcinogenic role in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, lymphoma, and certain types of gastric cancer,5 it is reasonable to suspect that EBV also plays a significant role in the development and progression of cervical cancer.6

Viral oncogenesis is mainly achieved through multifaceted intricate mechanisms, including insertion of viral genes, regulation of host cell genomes and signaling pathways mediated by viral proteins, chronic inflammation resulting from persistent infection, and disorder of tumor suppressor genes and host oncogenes involved in cell growth.7 Among them, gene integration plays an important role. This process enables mutated cells to gain selective growth advantages, evade immune surveillance, and establish a carcinogenic microenvironment.8 The carcinogenicity of viral integration manifests in multiple ways: host genome instability caused by viral integration leads to chromosomal inversions, translocations, deletions, and rearrangements, thereby inducing mutations in host cells. Additionally, the insertion of viral genes can silence certain key host tumor suppressor genes or activate certain proto-oncogenes, thereby inducing mutations in host cells. Additionally, viral integration can also cause changes in the copy number of host genes, thereby altering gene phenotypes. Most notably, it was mentioned in the literature that viral integration can cause the rearrangement of viral coding sequences with those of the host cell genome, resulting in the emergence of certain fusion genes with oncogenic functions.9 Previous literature has suggested that HPV and EBV share a common insertion mechanism.10

In recent years, next-generation high-throughput detection technologies, including second-generation sequencing, have been increasingly applied in addressing medical research challenges, particularly in the study of human diseases. Compared to previous-generation sequencing technologies, second-generation sequencing has made significant strides in accuracy, sequencing costs, and sequencing speed, facilitating our ability to study the impact of viral integration on disease at the genetic level.11 This study simultaneously performed HPV and EBV virus chip capture sequencing on cervical exfoliated cells from HPV-positive women (FHGC) to explore the distinct integration characteristics of HPV and EBV within host cells, as well as investigate the molecular biological features during dual infection by these two oncogenic viruses.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We collected 100 cases of cervical exfoliated cells from HPV-positive patients at the Cancer Hospital Affiliated to Xinjiang Medical University. These patients should be those who have been newly diagnosed, have not received the HPV vaccine, do not have any other type of cancer, and do not have immune deficiencies.

Sample Collection and HPV Testing

The HPV of these patients was detected by the flow-through hybridization chip method(HIVID). These patients were examined by a professional pathologist for liquid-based cytology. Firstly, we performed density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll lymphocyte separation medium on the collected samples to remove any lymphocytes that might be present. After that, DNA was extracted from the corresponding specimens and subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis and Qubit assay to assess DNA concentration and content. Substandard samples were excluded, resulting in 88 specimens ultimately included in the experiment (age range 23–72, median age 47).

Gene Integration Analysis

The obtained DNA samples undergo ultrasonic fragmentation and purification, followed by end repair, 3′-end A-addition, sequencing adapter ligation, library fragment screening, PCR amplification and DNA library preparation, library hybridization, hybridized library washing and purification, PCR amplification, and sequencing (This section of testing was commissioned to Shanghai Tianhao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Detailed testing and analysis procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information S1.12 The main equipment and reagents used in the experiment are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.12

Ethical Statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Cancer Hospital Affiliated to Xinjiang Medical University.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 software and Excel. Quantitative data were described using means ± standard deviations, analysis of variance was used to compare quantitative data, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. All p values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Detection of EBV in 88 Specimens

As shown in Table 1, We found that EBV was detected in 34.09% (30/88) of cervical exfoliated cells from HPV-positive females. The EBV infection rate was 31.65% (25/79) among samples with HPV co-infection (two or more subtypes). Among samples with HPV monoinfection (a single subtype), the EBV infection rate was 55.56% (5/9). The difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (χ2= 2.056, P > 0.05). The EBV infection rate in samples with HPV16/18 subtype infection was 41.86% (18/43), while in samples without HPV16/18 subtype infection, it was 26.67% (12/45). The difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (χ2= 2.259, P > 0.05).

EBV Integration Under Different Risk Conditions

In conjunction with exfoliative cytology testing, subjects can be categorized into normal, low-risk, and high-risk groups based on the risk of developing CIN III or higher lesions within five years, according to the 2019 guidelines of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP). Our study found that in the 7 co-infected specimens from the normal group, EBV was detected with a total of 59 integrations involving 40 genes. In the 11 co-infected specimens from the low-risk group, EBV was detected with a total of 143 integrations involving 111 genes. In the 12 co-infected specimens from the high-risk group, EBV integrations were detected 147,828 times, involving 17,895 genes.

Based on data from the TGCA database, we identified genes involved in integration that exhibited differential expression in cervical cancer tissues (screening criteria: |LogFC| > 2 and P.adj < 0.05). Among these, 3 genes were found in the normal group, 14 in the low-risk group, and 2302 in the high-risk group. Interestingly, we examined the relationships among differentially expressed genes across these three groups and found that the differentially expressed genes in both the normal group and the low-risk group were entirely contained within those of the high-risk group, as shown in Figure 14. We investigated these common differentially expressed genes and found that in the normal and low-risk groups, these genes exhibited few integration events, whereas in the high-risk group, integration events were frequently observed. Discussion

Our analysis revealed that the EBV infection rate was higher in the HPV co-infection group (two or more subtypes) compared to the HPV single-infection group (one subtype). Additionally, the EBV infection rate was higher in the group with high-risk HPV subtypes (HPV16/18) than in the group without these two subtypes. The EBV infection rate in the normal group (HPV+Normal) was lower than that in the low-risk group (HPV+ASC-US and HPV+LSIL) and the high-risk group (HPV+ASC-H and HPV+HSIL). The only slight regret is that the above results did not show statistically significant differences. However, considering the enormous size of the EBV genome, which makes gene integration events less likely to occur and results in a low integration frequency, and given that only 30 samples with EBV integration were detected in this experiment, the lack of statistical support may be due to insufficient sample size. Previous studies have demonstrated that both HPV co-infections and HPV16/18 subtypes serve as markers of increased mutational burden in patients.13,14 Furthermore, EBV infection rates increase alongside rising cervical cancer risk in the risk stratification system based on the 2019 guidelines of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP). This intriguing phenomenon suggests that EBV infection may potentially serve as a driving factor for cervical cancer development.

Compared to HPV, the EBV genome is significantly larger. Its representative strain B95-8 contains approximately 171,823 base pairs and 85 genes, which influences its integration frequency within host cells. In most studies, EBV exhibits low-frequency integration.1 In this study, the number of EBV integration events detected in HPV-positive cervical exfoliated cells and the number of genes involved were higher compared to previous data detected in other tumor cells. A total of 148,030 integration events were detected, involving 17,908 genes. It is likely due to the use of liquid-phase enrichment technology in this experiment, which enhanced detection sensitivity. Simultaneously, HPV integration into the host cell genome disrupted the stability of the host cell genome, making it easier for the oncogenic virus to undergo integration.

Our study found that EBV integration occurs throughout the host cell genome, including mitochondrial genes, but this distribution is uneven. By standardizing chromosome lengths, the expected values for integration events on each chromosome were obtained. Comparative analysis revealed a higher distribution on chromosomes 2, 10, and 19, which broadly aligns with the distribution patterns of HPV integration events on host cell chromosomes observed in previous studies. This further corroborates our earlier conjecture that EBV infection and HPV share some form of connection.15 So what exactly is the relationship between these two carcinogenic viruses? Is EBV’s high-frequency integration merely a consequence of HPV integration causing genomic instability in host cells—a mere hitchhiker? Or is it a mutually reinforcing driver propelling the process forward—the engine of malignant transformation?

This study detected a total of 109,231 breakpoints involving 17,908 genes. Unfortunately, no high-frequency breakpoints supported by multiple samples were identified. The vast majority of genes were detected in fewer than two samples, with only CADPS2 being detected simultaneously in four specimens. The CADPS2 gene is located at 7q31.32 and encodes a member of the calcium-dependent secretion activator (CAPS) protein family. The CAPS protein family consists of calcium-binding proteins that regulate exocytosis of synaptic and dense core vesicles in neurons and neuroendocrine cells. Additionally, studies have suggested a potential association with the treatment of metastatic breast cancer.16

By analyzing the breakpoint locations of EBV insertion into the host cell genome, we identified distribution patterns consistent with those previously observed in HPV studies.17 This further confirms that integration events occurring in the host cell genome during co-infection by these two oncogenic viruses share a common mechanism.

By performing cluster analysis on genes containing high-frequency breakpoints, we found that genes involved in integration events primarily influence the following functional pathways: transforming growth factor activation (BP), transcription regulatory factors (CC), nuclear dots (CC), and transforming growth factor beta receptor activity (MF). These genes are enriched in pathways including EB virus infection, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling, and Notch signaling. Among these, the functions of transforming growth factor activation and transcription regulatory factors involve cellular transcriptional regulation, which can influence the host cell’s own genetic expression. This enables oncogenic viruses to alter the host cell’s epigenetic biological activity. Additionally, TGF-β plays a crucial role in numerous biological processes, including cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, and the initiation and progression of cancer. Recent studies have revealed that TGF-β plays a crucial role in the transition from non-cancerous conditions to cancer in gastrointestinal, liver, and pancreatic cancers. Additionally, reports indicate that TGF-β participates in regulating the immune system, aiding cancerous cells in evading immune system clearance.18–22 Among these, the EMBP1 gene has been confirmed to be closely associated with breast cancer. LTBP1 has been demonstrated to encode a protein that serves as a key regulator of TGF-β.23 TGF-β plays diverse roles in controlling cell proliferation and differentiation, wound healing, and immune system function. Reports indicate that during carcinogenesis, tumor cells often exploit TGF-β to drive unregulated wound healing processes in cancer-associated fibroblasts while suppressing both the adaptive and innate immune systems. The HAS2-AS1 gene has been implicated in oral squamous cell carcinoma and may exert its effects by influencing the TGF-β signaling pathway.24 The DEAD box protein encoded by the DDX10 gene is a putative RNA helicase involved in numerous cellular processes associated with RNA secondary structure changes. Studies have identified a driving relationship between this protein and lung cancer, colorectal cancer, chondrosarcoma, and glioma.25,26 The protein encoded by the E2F7 gene plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle process. It primarily participates in various carcinogenic processes by modulating gene expression (transcription) and influencing the transcription of cell cycle genes regulated by TP53. TP53 has been confirmed to be closely associated with the development and progression of cervical cancer. Additionally, it functions as a promoter of sprouting angiogenesis, thereby facilitating vascular growth.27 The NCOR2 gene encodes a nuclear receptor co-repressor that mediates transcriptional silencing of certain target genes, and its abnormal expression is associated with multiple cancers.28 The POLDIP3 gene encodes a protein containing an RRM (RNA recognition motif), which participates in translational regulation by recruiting ribosomal protein S6 kinase β-1 to mRNA.29 In recent years, multiple studies have highlighted its crucial role in activating DNA damage checkpoints in vivo. The above evidence leads us to believe that EBV insertion may alter the phenotype of these potential oncogenes, thereby inducing malignant cellular changes.

The outcome of integration events depends not only on the insertion site within the host cell genome but also on the location of breakpoints within the oncogenic virus’s own genome. Previous studies have demonstrated that HPV integration disrupts the E2 gene and abrogates its function; the E6 protein degrades p53 while E7 inactivates pRb. Together, they synergistically trigger genomic instability, leading to enrichment of viral integration breakpoints at chromosomal fragile sites. The E5 protein further amplifies oncogenic effects and mediates immune evasion.30 In addition, existing literatures have proposed that latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) and nuclear antigens of the EBNA family (EBNA1, EBNA2, EBNA3A/3C) encoded by EBV can induce double-strand breaks in host genomic DNA and suppress DNA damage repair pathways through multiple pathways. This results in massive exposure of chromosomal fragile sites, generating abundant integration breakpoints for insertion of the EBV genome into host DNA. Meanwhile, these two types of proteins synergistically mediate extensive immunosuppression to sustain persistent latent viral infection.31 This study found that breakpoints in EBV’s own genes predominantly occur within protein-coding regions. This implies that following integration events, the resulting viral-host fusion genes possess biological activity to express viral proteins, thereby altering the host cell’s genetic epigenome and laying the groundwork for potential carcinogenesis. When EBV and HPV are co-infected, LMP1 and the EBNA family can work together with HPV E6/E7 proteins to disrupt the stability of the cellular genome, driving the integration of both oncogenic viruses into the host genome, and ultimately causing the distribution patterns of EBV and HPV integration breakpoints to become more consistent.10

We investigated differences between single HPV infection and HPV-EBV co-infection through grouped analysis. No differences were observed between the single infection group and the co-infection group in terms of integration events or the chromosomal distribution of genes involved in integration. Within the co-infection group, HPV integration patterns showed no differences from EBV integration patterns but instead exhibited a correlation. Another intriguing finding emerged when we performed cluster analysis on the intersection of differentially expressed genes integrated by HPV in the single-infection group and those integrated by EBV in the co-infection group. This analysis revealed that these overlapping genes were enriched in pathways associated with cervical carcinogenesis. We speculate that this portion of genes may contain driver genes for cervical carcinogenesis. In the single-infection group, HPV integration caused mutations in these genes, while in the co-infection group, EBV integration led to mutations in these genes.

Finally, based on the 2019 guidelines of the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP), we examined differences in EBV integration across varying risk conditions using the five-year risk of developing CIN III or higher lesions as a benchmark. We found that both the normal group and the low-risk group exhibited significantly fewer integrations, fewer genes involved in integration, and fewer differentially expressed genes compared to the high-risk group. While investigating the relationships among differentially expressed genes in cervical cancer tissues across the three groups, we discovered an intriguing phenomenon: the differentially expressed genes in the normal group and the low-risk group both intersected with those in the high-risk group, yet they showed no overlap between each other. Additionally, the number of integrations for differentially expressed genes in the intersection group was significantly lower in both the normal and low-risk groups compared to the high-risk group. As is well known, increased integration frequency exacerbates the mutation burden. This finding supports our previous hypothesis that EBV integration plays a crucial role in the progression of cervical carcinogenesis.

This experiment was the first to analyze the integration of EBV in cervical exfoliated cells infected with HPV. Unfortunately, only DNA level research has been carried out, and the genes involved in the integration have not been further studied at the transcript and protein levels. The bioinformatics analysis was mainly based on the previous patient data in the TCGA database, and the obtained biological markers still need further research for verification. In the future, we need to conduct more in-depth studies at various levels along the genetic central dogma to further explore the interactions between these two viruses.

Conclusion

EBV may be involved in the process of cervical cancer development. EBV and HPV share similar characteristics in their integration into the host cell genome. The chromosomal distribution of EBV integration sites within the host cell genome correlates with HPV. EBV integration in the co-infection group may activate proto-oncogenes in some cervical cancers.
