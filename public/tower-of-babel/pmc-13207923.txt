Vesicle-Mediated Delivery of Carbon Dots: Synthesis, Properties, and Biomedical Applications
Authors: Xiaowen Xu, Jiahe Zhao, Shihan Tao, Cheng Miao, Chunjie Li, Weitong Cui, Zhiyong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon dots (CDs), as an emerging class of carbon nanomaterials, exhibit exceptional optical properties, biocompatibility, and functional tunability, rendering them highly promising for applications in biosensing, bioimaging, antibacterial therapy, and phototherapy. However, their small size leads to a poor enhanced-permeation-and-retention (EPR) effect and inadequate targeting capability, which hinders their further translation into biomedical applications. Vesicles, including natural extracellular vesicles (EVs), liposomes, and synthetic vesicles, serve as ideal nanocarriers due to their unique bilayer structure, excellent biocompatibility, and intrinsic targeting properties. The integration of CDs with vesicles forms a novel multifunctional hybrid system, which can effectively address the delivery limitations of CDs while endowing vesicles with additional functionalities such as fluorescence tracing and phototherapeutic effects. This review systematically summarizes the synthesis methods (top-down and bottom-up) and key physicochemical (structural and optical) properties of CDs, as well as the classification and characteristics of various vesicles. Furthermore, it focuses on the construction strategies, interaction mechanisms, and loading methods of CD-vesicle hybrids, and comprehensively elaborates on their applications in targeted drug delivery, bioimaging, tumor therapy, antibacterial treatment, and wound healing. In addition, we critically discuss the key bottlenecks currently limiting the clinical translation of CD–vesicle hybrid therapeutics, including the thermodynamic limitations of drug/CD loading efficiency, burst release of payloads, in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior, and the effects of CDs on vesicle interfacial stability and leakage kinetics. Finally, the current challenges (e.g. unclear interaction mechanisms, limited scalable production, and narrow application scope) and future development directions (e.g. expanding antimicrobial applications, promoting clinical translation, and exploring CD self-assembled vesicles) of CD-vesicle hybrid systems are discussed, aiming to provide a comprehensive reference for the rational design and biomedical application of CD-vesicle hybrid nanomaterials.Keywords: carbon dots, hybrid vesicles, nano-drug delivery carriers, biomedical applications, targeted delivery

Introduction

Carbon dots (CDs) represent a novel class of carbon nanomaterials, first discovered in 2004.1 These nanostructures consist of sp2/sp3 carbon backbones and abundant functional groups/polymer chains, with sizes typically less than 10 nm. The research community has focused extensively on CDs due to their unique optical and chemical properties. This is due to their optical properties, such as bright fluorescence emission in the visible region, and excellent optical properties such as photostability and resistance to photobleaching,2,3 which have led to their wide use in the fields of bio-imaging, biosensing, and so on.4 In addition, when compared with other semiconductor quantum dots, CDs exhibit superior biocompatibility and low toxicity,5,6 which has led to their extensive utilization in biomedical fields as potential novel nanomedicines. Recent studies have shown that CDs have great potential as multifunctional biomedical nanoplatforms. Owing to their simple preparation processes and stable photophysical properties, second near-infrared (NIR-II) CDs have attracted increasing attention in the field of deep-tissue imaging.7 The absorption/emission spectra of NIR-II CDs can be shifted to the NIR-II window, enabling high-contrast angiography, hepatobiliary imaging, and image-guided cancer therapy.8 CD-based nanozymes have emerged as enzyme-mimicking platforms capable of regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pathological microenvironments, showing broad prospects in antibacterial therapy, anti-biofilm treatment, diabetic wound healing, and inflammation modulation.9,10 CD-based nanozymes derived from traditional Chinese medicine precursors can inhibit tumor growth.11 In addition, CDs have been widely used as therapeutic nanomaterials, such as in antitumor therapy12 and in the inhibition of cellular senescence and injury.13,14 The abundant functional groups and polymer chains of CDs permit their modification by various organic or polymer molecules. The synthesis methods of CDs can be classified as top-down and bottom-up, and their physicochemical properties depend largely on the synthetic routes and raw materials. In order to improve the properties of CDs, atoms such as nitrogen, boron and sulfur or metals such as iron and copper can be added by appropriate doping methods,14,15 and surface passivation of CDs is also possible. The emergence of artificial intelligence technology has opened up new avenues for the synthesis optimization, performance enhancement, and biomedical engineering applications of CDs.16 However, when considering deeper biological applications, CDs are often too small in size, resulting in poor EPR and their targeting is not prominent enough to allow them to accurately target the target site and stay in action for a long period of time.

Vesicles are a class of molecularly ordered assemblies with a closed bilayer structure, and the properties of EVs, liposomes and vesicles composed of surfactants have been the focus of significant research interest due to their unique characteristics.17,18 In recent years, it has been discovered that some functionalized inorganic nanoparticles (NPs) can also form vesicles, a finding that has rapidly attracted attention. The distinctive origin, structure, and physiological functions of these vesicles frequently render them an optimal nano-delivery system for both endogenous and exogenous drug delivery. EVs, as membrane vesicles originating from the extracellular environment, exhibit structural similarities to biological membranes and are often targeted.19 In the case of non-targeted vesicles, surface modification or functionalization with molecules, proteins or nucleic acids can be achieved by loading these onto the vesicle surface or lumen, or by targeting the vesicles to specific cell or tissue types.20 This has the effect of improving the delivery performance of vesicles from a number of perspectives, and is of great significance in promoting the clinical application of vesicles. Vesicles have been extensively utilized in biomedical fields, and the integration of CDs into vesicles will result in the creation of a new type of multifunctional material. Sun et al were the first to construct stable fluorescent vesicles by mixing CDs with surfactants in water, thereby significantly enriching the theory of vesicle formation and expanding the potential applications of CDs in biology and life sciences.21

CDs find extensive application within biological systems owing to their favorable biocompatibility. However, their size fails to align with biological fate: their diminutive dimensions result in rapid clearance within the body, hindering the generation of an EPR. Concurrently, the abundance of diverse functional groups on their surface may lead to recognition and phagocytosis by the immune system. These issues constrain the application and efficacy of CDs. Conversely, vesicles, with their larger size and inherent biological interface, can improve the distribution of CDs, reduce their clearance, and enhance their biological effects. Moreover, the two exhibit functional complementarity: CDs excel in fluorescence imaging, ROS generation, and photothermal effects, while vesicles provide targeting capabilities, high loading capacity, excellent biocompatibility, and controlled release. Together, they achieve synergistic functional enhancement. The necessity of integrating CDs with vesicular systems lies in resolving the fundamental mismatch between the physicochemical advantages of CDs and the biological requirements for in vivo functionality.

A plethora of review articles have hitherto been published on the applications of CDs themselves. However, there is a dearth of review papers that specifically address the delivery strategies of CDs and elaborate on their synthetic protocols as well as their biomedical applications via combination with vesicles. This paper reviews the synthetic methods and biological applications of CDs, with a particular focus on the biological applications derived from the conjugation of vesicles and CDs. Based on the currently published relevant research reports, this work not only presents a systematic analysis of the structures and properties of both vesicles and CDs but also conducts a comprehensive discussion on the limitations associated with the synthetic methods for vesicle-CDs conjugates and their potential biological applications in the future.

History of CDs

At the beginning of the 21st century, nanoscience is developing rapidly. CDs are fluorescent carbon nanomaterials with particle sizes below 10 nm. In 2004, Xu et al discovered CDs for the first time1 Since then, for nearly two decades, various researchers have intensively investigated their structural properties, preparation methods, biological applications, etc. In 2006, Sun et al prepared CDs by laser ablation of a carbon target which is a mixture of graphite powder and cement and improved the fluorescence yield of CDs by PEG1500N passivation, which promoted the biological application of fluorescent CDs.22 Liu et al obtained multicolor fluorescent carbon nanoparticles (CNPs) from the combustion soot of candles,23 which provided a new idea for the preparation of CDs and opened up a new field of research on the luminescence mechanism of CDs Since their first observation in carbonaceous nanofragments, CDs have evolved from serendipitously discovered fluorescent carbon nanoparticles into a broad family of engineered carbon nanomaterials. Early studies on photoluminescent carbogenic dots and systematic reviews of carbon nanoallotropes established CDs as an important member of the carbon nanomaterial family.24,25 Subsequent work by Kang and co-workers summarized their synthesis, physicochemical properties, and applications, while Rogach and co-workers highlighted their potential as emerging light emitters for bioimaging, cancer therapy, and optoelectronics.26,27 In the following years, electrochemical, hydrothermal, microwave and other synthesis methods emerged rapidly, and the preparation method gradually changed from top-down to bottom-up. Meanwhile, Prato and co-workers advanced the field from empirical synthesis toward separation, purification, functionalization, and tailored molecular design of carbon nanodots.28–30 These milestones indicate that the biomedical performance of CDs is not determined solely by their carbon core, but by a combination of size, surface chemistry, doping, molecular fluorophores, purification quality, and interfacial interactions. Nowadays, CDs have been widely used in biology, medicine and other fields.31

Overview of CDs

Synthesis Methods of CDs

There are several methods for CDs synthesis. Based on the mechanism involved in the synthesis, they can be of two types (Figure 1).

Synthesis Methods of CDs. Top-down methods: Arc-discharge method, Electrochemical method, Laser ablation, Chemical oxidation. Bottom-up methods: Hydrothermal/Solvothermal method, Pyrolyzation, Microwave method.

Top-Down Methods

In the early stage of the development of CDs, the top-down method was the main method of preparing CDs, which involves cutting or breaking down large carbon materials (carbon fibers, graphite, carbon nanotubes, etc) into smaller carbon structures. The top-down method mainly includes arc discharge, electrochemical, laser ablation and chemical oxidation.

Fluorescent CDs were first discovered through an arc-discharge-related process. In 2004, Xu et al isolated fluorescent carbon nanomaterials emitting green-blue, yellow, and orange fluorescence during the electrophoretic purification of SWNTs derived from arc-discharge soot.1 Bottini et al later obtained fluorescent nanoparticles from pristine and nitric acid-oxidized carbon nanotubes produced by an electric arc method.32 Although arc-discharge-derived CDs exhibit favorable luminescence, they often contain impurities and require strict reaction control.

Electrochemical methods generate CDs by corroding or exfoliating carbon electrodes under controlled electrochemical conditions. This method is relatively simple, controllable, and widely used. Zhou et al first reported blue-luminescent carbon nanomaterials prepared electrochemically using tetrabutylammonium perchlorate as the supporting electrolyte.33 Lu et al further produced fluorescent carbon nanoribbons, nanoparticles, and graphene from graphite electrodes through ionic liquid-assisted electrochemical exfoliation, achieving high yield and tunable surface passivation.34

Laser ablation is another top-down strategy. Sun et al prepared fluorescent CDs by laser ablation of a carbon target composed of graphite powder and cement, followed by organic passivation.22,35 However, this method requires expensive equipment and complex operation, limiting its broader application. Chemical oxidation, by contrast, uses oxidative treatment to break down carbon materials while introducing hydrophilic groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. For example, Liu et al obtained fluorescent carbon nanoparticles from oxidized candle soot purified by PAGE.23

Bottom-Up Methods

The bottom-up method includes microwave, pyrolyzation, hydrothermal/solvothermal method, in which CDs are usually synthesized by molecular or/and polymeric precursors. Compared with top-down routes, bottom-up methods are generally more convenient for regulating composition, size, surface functional groups, and optical properties.

Pyrolysis involves direct carbonization of small molecular precursors at high temperature. Bourlinos et al prepared CNPs smaller than 10 nm through one-step thermal decomposition of ammonium citrate salts or 4-aminoantipyrine.36 Wang et al synthesized strongly luminescent oil-soluble CDs by carbonizing citric acid in a non-coordinating solvent, achieving a quantum yield of up to 53%.37

Microwave-assisted synthesis uses microwave energy to rapidly pyrolyze and carbonize precursors. Liu et al prepared CDs with a quantum yield of 12.02% by microwave treatment of a homogeneous solution containing TTDDA, glycerol, and phosphate buffer.38 Jiang et al reported room-temperature phosphorescent CDs with an ultralong lifetime of up to 1.46 s using microwave-assisted heating of ethanolamine and phosphoric acid.39 This method significantly shortens reaction time and improves synthesis efficiency.

Hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis is one of the most widely used bottom-up approaches. It involves heating carbon sources in water or organic solvents under sealed high-temperature conditions. Liu et al prepared water-soluble, multicolor photoluminescent CDs using surfactant-modified silica nanospheres and resoles as precursors. Another study obtained deep-red carbonized polymer dots from taxus leaves, with quantum yields of 59% under 413 nm excitation and 31% under 660 nm excitation.40 Hydrothermal/solvothermal methods usually offer simple post-treatment, tunable reaction conditions, and excellent optical performance, making them highly suitable for preparing CDs with diverse biomedical functions.

Structural Properties

CDs are mainly composed of elements such as carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with a relatively high content of carbon. CDs can be categorized into GQDs and amorphous CDs based on their structure. GQDs are usually composed of no more than 10 layers of graphene with a distinct lattice structure with lattice spacing similar to that of graphite, and are mainly composed of sp2 hybridized carbon. Amorphous CDs are usually amorphous spherical carbon particles without lattice, mainly composed of sp3 hybridized carbon. The particle size of the CDs is very small, usually no more than 10 nm.3,41 Based on this property, CDs can be easily endocytosed into cells or various types of carriers, thus playing an important role in areas such as bioimaging.

Optical Properties

CDs possess characteristic ultraviolet–visible absorption and photoluminescence properties. Most CDs show strong absorption in the ultraviolet region, typically between 250 and 320 nm, while some also exhibit absorption bands in the visible region depending on their carbon core structure, surface states, and functional groups. Their absorption spectra may contain one or multiple peaks, or appear as broad featureless curves.

Photoluminescence is one of the most important optical features of CDs. CDs can emit fluorescence across the visible region and, in some cases, extend into the near-infrared (NIR) range. Their emission spectra are usually broad and excitation-dependent, which is closely associated with particle size, surface defects, heteroatom doping, and surface passivation. These optical properties make CDs useful for bioimaging, biosensing, and fluorescence-guided drug delivery. In CD–vesicle hybrid systems, vesicle encapsulation or surface conjugation can further improve the photostability, dispersibility, and biological applicability of CDs, while CDs provide vesicles with fluorescence-tracking ability. Deep-red/NIR-emissive CDs are particularly attractive for biomedical imaging because of their improved tissue penetration and reduced background interference. Liu et al prepared deep-red-emitting CPDs with a quantum yield of 59% under 413 nm excitation, demonstrating their potential as fluorescent probes for bioimaging applications.23,24,41

Biological Applications of CDs

Carbon is the most essential element for living organisms. Therefore, CDs have higher biocompatibility and lower cytotoxicity than other fluorescent nanomaterials. Chen et al42 used AFM to reliably reveal the cytotoxicity of nanomaterials from biophysical aspects by utilizing its sensitive nanomechanical measurements and high-definition imaging capabilities They used this method to study the effects of CDs on SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells and found that the effects of CDs on SH-SY5Y cells were dose-dependent and time-dependent, thus enabling the combination of AFM nanomechanical measurements with AFM HD imaging. CDs are widely used in biosensing and bioimaging because of their high biocompatibility and low toxicity (Figure 2).

Biosensing

Nanomaterial-based fluorescent probe methods have the advantages of high specificity, high efficiency, ease of use, clear visualization and rapid response. Therefore, CDs can also be used for biosensing. Liu et al utilized the fluorescence properties of Hg2+-specific CDs and CdTe quantum dots to detect breast cancer35 He et al used citric acid and urea as precursors to prepare graphitic carbon nitride quantum dots (g-CNQDs) using home microwave oven, whose yellow fluorescence could be specifically and effectively quenched by Tetracycline (TC).43 g-CNQDs was used to detect TC in real water samples with a spiked recovery of 97.77%-103.4%. The method is simple and fast with high detection accuracy, which presented a feasible analytical method for TC determination in water samples. However, these carbon points are susceptible to environmental disturbances, resulting in a significant reduction in sensing accuracy. Yang et al44 prepared CDs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites with red emissive CDs prepared from taxus leaves, including the excellent optical properties of CDs and the high surface area and cavity structure of ZIF-8, which, on the one hand, adsorbed long ssDNA on its surface to quench the fluorescence of FAM, and on the other hand, encapsulated the CDs in its inner cavity to obtain a stable self-luminescence. Thus, a proportional fluorescence sensor for quantitative detection of Pb2+ was constructed, and the CDs could reduce the influence of interference and improve the sensing accuracy. Not only is the accuracy of the readings improving, but so is the convenience. Guo et al45 synthesized amino-rich nitrogen-doped carbon dots (N-CDs) by hydrothermal method using 2,3-diaminopyridine as the raw material. Nitrite can make the fluorescence intensity of the N-CDs decrease, so they designed and fabricated a hand-held sensing platform (HSP) with this method, and it can be used to detect the content of nitrite in food which provided a new way for developing portable detection equipment for food safety control and environmental analysis. Wang et al46 synthesized the GL-CDs (Fe) using Ganoderma lucidum waste as a precursor, and prepared a ratio fluorescence/colorimetric/smartphone triple-mode sensing platform based on GL-CDs (Fe) for the detection of glucose in serum samples, which CDs also have the function of destroying tumor cells.

Bioimaging

Since their initial preparation, CDs have been extensively employed in bioimaging applications due to their superior photoluminescent characteristics. In recent years, there has been a notable surge in research interest in red fluorescent carbon dots (RCDs) in the field of bioimaging, largely driven by their exceptional optical properties and enhanced resistance to photobleaching. Wang et al47 constructed multifunctional therapeutic nanocomposites, HSA-BFP@CDs, using serum albumin and CDs. The red fluorescence of HSA-BFP@CDs appeared upon mixing with Aβ aggregates, a feature that was maintained in vivo and in vitro, and therefore it can be targeted to detect amyloid formation in vivo. Zhang et al48 constructed amphiphilic CDs, named CL-CDs, using L-tryptophan, urea and CR as ternary carbon sources. α-syn-F significantly turned on the fluorescence of CL-CDs, and the fluorescence intensity showed a linear relationship with the concentration of α-syn-F. Meanwhile CL-CDs successfully imaged α-syn protofibrils in NL5901 showy cryptic rod nematode. Robust solvated carbon quantum dots (RS-CDs) co-doped with manganese, chlorine and nitrogen were prepared by a one-pot hydrothermal method by Wang et al49 The CDs are not only capable of detecting Sn4+, but also can be used for lipid imaging. RS-CDs are soluble in CA and emit orange-red fluorescence with an FQY of 5.12%, while in living cells, RS-CDs emit bright red fluorescence in lipid-rich cell membranes and cytoplasm. In the in vivo experiments, the stained lipids of zebrafish embryos and larvae could emit increasingly strong red fluorescence as the concentration of RS-CDs increased, and the intensity of red fluorescence was the highest when the concentration of RS-CDs reached 70 μg⋅mL−1, and RS-CDs could target the lipids of zebrafish embryos and larvae and perform lipid-specific imaging, and it was more resistant to photobleaching than the commercial dyes are more resistant to photobleaching. This also indicates that solvatochromic CDs have great potential for bioimaging applications.

Antibacterial

Some of the CDs themselves have strong antimicrobial activity and can be used as bio-antimicrobial agents. However, due to their molecular weight being below the threshold for effective transport, they are often unable to remain in the designated site for a sufficient period of time, resulting in a significant reduction in their antibacterial effect. Therefore, combining CDs with hydrogels is an effective method to enhance their antimicrobial activity. Doping of CDs into hydrogels significantly enhanced the antibacterial activity against L. monocytogenes and E. coli by Alzahrani.50 Sheng et al51 used garlic CDs to make a composite hydrogel, which was more than 99.9% bactericidal against MRSA after contact with MRSA for 24 h. The release of GCDs greatly enhanced its antimicrobial effect. When the hydrogel was covered on the wound of mice, the wound healing rate was as high as 93.29%. Zhang et al52 prepared CDs-Cu-N by doping Cu and N into CDs, which significantly enhanced the antimicrobial activity of CDs, and its bactericidal rate against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida albicans exceeded 99.999%. For better application in wound repair, CDs-Cu-N-H2O2-CS hybridized hydrogels were prepared to significantly improve the action time of CDs-Cu-N on infected wounds, which could effectively promote the healing of infected wounds. Nevertheless, these techniques continue to present challenges, including intricate preparation procedures and a relatively low carbon dot binding rate.

Phototherapy

PDT has gained attention as a non-invasive treatment that kills cells by locally generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and combining it with multiple biocides, with a short duration of action and low toxicity. Some CDs can also produce reactive oxygen species, which can be used as photosensitizers to kill target cells. Photothermal therapy (PTT) kills cells through the heat generated by laser irradiation. PTT of tumor causes oxidative stress and related inflammation in adjacent normal tissues, to solve this problem, Ye et al53 prepared a nitrogen-doped carbon dotted ET-CD, a solution of ET-CD with a concentration of 400 μg/mL was irradiated by a laser at 808 nm for 10 min, and the temperature was elevated to 35.8 °C. This photothermal effect significantly ablated and inhibited tumors in vivo. Meanwhile, ET-CD could significantly remove the reactive oxygen species in the cells and significantly reduce the pro-inflammatory response induced by PTT. Liu et al54 encapsulated CDs with other drugs in liposomes to construct a folic acid (FA) targeted photothermal multifunctional nanoplatform, which specifically targets tumors, improves the in vivo circulation time of the drugs, and enhances the therapeutic efficacy.

Despite their promising performance in biosensing, bioimaging, antibacterial therapy, and phototherapy, CDs still face major delivery-related challenges in biomedical applications. Their ultrasmall size, typically below 10 nm, often leads to rapid renal clearance, short circulation time, weak EPR-mediated accumulation, limited tissue retention, poor active targeting, and possible uptake by the reticuloendothelial system. These drawbacks indicate that the therapeutic and diagnostic efficacy of CDs is strongly dependent on an appropriate delivery platform.

Vesicle-based systems provide a feasible solution to these limitations. Compared with free CDs, vesicles possess more favorable biological sizes, membrane-enclosed structures, and tunable surface properties. They can protect CDs from premature clearance, prolong circulation, enhance tissue accumulation, and enable active targeting through surface ligands or membrane proteins. In addition, their internal aqueous cavity and lipid bilayer allow the co-delivery of CDs with drugs, genes, or photosensitizers, thereby expanding the multifunctionality of CD-based nanomedicine. Therefore, vesicles serve not only as carriers for CDs but also as biological interfaces that improve their stability, targeting, and therapeutic performance.

Overview of Vesicles

Vesicles are molecularly ordered assemblies with enclosed membrane structures, typically spherical in shape with diameters ranging from 30 nanometers to several micrometers. Composed of lipid or polymer bilayers, they contain hydrophilic internal structures capable of carrying various substances including proteins, nucleic acids, and small-molecule drugs.55–57 Based on their origin, vesicles can be classified into natural vesicles and synthetic vesicles (Table 1).

Classification of Vesicles

Natural vesicles are either produced by cells or derived from cell membranes, exhibiting membrane compositions highly similar to cell membranes and demonstrating excellent biocompatibility. Among these, EVs and liposomes have been most extensively studied and widely applied.

EVs are nano-sized membrane vesicles (30–150 nm) secreted by nearly all cell types through the endosomal pathway. They can be categorized into subtypes such as exosomes, microvesicles, membrane vesicles, and apoptotic bodies based on their size and composition.19 (Figure 3) Alternatively, they may be classified as animal-derived EVs (secreted by eukaryotic or prokaryotic cells, typically obtained from body fluids, cell culture supernatants, and animal tissues) or plant-derived EVs (secreted by plant cells).60 Characterized by their minute structure and protein-rich membranes, EVs have been widely employed in drug delivery as nanoscale drug delivery platforms due to their inherent targeting capability, exceptional stability, minimal immunogenicity, and ability to cross physiological barriers like the blood-brain barrier (BBB). For example, exosomes derived from CD16 antibody-modified human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells and loaded with glucocorticoids (GCs) exhibit enhanced targeting toward natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages, achieving a 200% increase in delivery efficiency.61 These engineered exosomes significantly suppress NK cell cytotoxicity and inhibit M1 macrophage polarization. Similarly, EVs can be loaded with therapeutic miRNAs such as miR30d for targeted delivery to hypertrophic cardiac tissue, resulting in a twofold accumulation of the miRNA at the site of action.

Structure of EVs and production of EVs.

Liposomes, first artificially synthesized in the 1960s, are artificial vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers.62 They can be classified based on vesicle size, number of lipid and/or phospholipid bilayers (lamellae), charge, and surface characteristics, with sizes ranging from 50 nm to several micrometers. Capable of encapsulating hydrophilic, amphiphilic, and lipophilic drugs, liposomes have been extensively utilized in drug delivery.63 Their amphiphilic nature, resembling natural cell membranes, enables favorable interactions with cellular membranes and facilitates efficient tissue and cellular uptake. For instance, EVs can be engineered to encapsulate miR30d for targeted delivery to hypertrophic cardiac tissue, resulting in a twofold increase in miR30d accumulation.64

Synthetic vesicles are primarily chemically synthesized, such as polymersomes formed from amphiphilic block copolymers. Compared to biological vesicles, they exhibit superior mechanical stability and controllable release properties.65 Their stable chemical characteristics and scalable production make them particularly suitable for drug delivery applications.

Although vesicles have been widely applied in drug delivery, their loading capacity for biomolecules or drugs remains limited. Consequently, researchers have been exploring integration with other entities, such as hybrid vesicles. Worldwide investigations have been conducted on attaching proteins, enzymes, DNA, and RNA to vesicles for advanced material preparation, with nanomaterials being prime target molecules. CDs, as emerging nanomaterials, possess intrinsic properties including antioxidant, antibacterial, and fluorescent characteristics, enabling their encapsulation within vesicles for targeted delivery to specific sites. Furthermore, CDs contain diverse functional groups that can bind to vesicles, enhancing both loading efficiency and functional performance. Therefore, the combination of vesicles and CDs not only facilitates targeted delivery of CDs but also improves vesicle performance, thereby broadening their applications.

Carbon Dot-Vesicle Hybrids

The strategic integration of CDs with vesicles holds significant promise for biomedical applications, particularly in targeted drug delivery and bioimaging. A pivotal study by Boruah et al systematically investigated the interactions between phosphatidylcholine vesicles—employed as a model cell membrane—and functionalized CDs.66 It was revealed that amine-terminated CDs (PEICDs) exhibit markedly stronger binding affinity to vesicles compared to CDs functionalized with –COOH, –OH, or protein coatings. In QCM experiments, the Δm value for the PEICD-vesicle system was measured to be 1.496 μg·cm−2 higher than that of the blank control. This preferential binding is attributed to enhanced hydrogen bonding between the –NH2 groups on the CDs and the phosphate head groups of phospholipids, a mechanism corroborated by quartz crystal microbalance (QCM), isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The incorporation of CDs did not compromise vesicular stability and facilitated efficient nanobiointeractions. Such functional group-dependent binding behavior underscores the critical importance of surface chemistry in the rational design of CD-vesicle hybrids, which can significantly improve biocompatibility and targeting efficiency-echoing the natural amine-mediated transport mechanisms observed in neurotransmitter trafficking.

In recent years, the combination of CDs with vesicles has become a popular research direction in the biomedical field. For neurological diseases, exosomes or liposomes encapsulating CDs have enhanced BBB-crossing ability and have been used to treat or improve Parkinson’s disease and ischemic stroke.67,68 In cancer therapy, breast cancer cell-derived exosomes have been used to coat or encapsulate drug-loaded CDs, thereby improving HSPG- or folate receptor-mediated tumor targeting, mitochondrial damage, and apoptosis.69 Liposomes can also improve the delivery stability and cellular efficacy of CDs, further enhancing their antitumor effects.70 Recent studies have shown that, in regenerative medicine, EVs encapsulating CDs can target mitochondria, regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and autophagy, and promote regeneration.71 These findings indicate that carbon dot-vesicle hybrids are evolving from simple carrier–cargo combinations toward synergistic biomimetic platforms integrating targeting, catalytic activity, immune regulation, and microenvironment remodeling.

Physicochemical Basis of CD-Vesicle Interactions

Surface Charge & Electrostatic Matching

The contact and anchoring between vesicles and CDs are mainly driven by electrostatic interactions. Since most natural extracellular vesicles or liposomes composed of phosphatidylcholine (PC) possess a net negative surface charge, they exhibit strong affinity toward positively charged CDs. Changes in ζ-potential can directly reflect the binding of CDs to membrane structures. When positively charged CDs bind to the membrane structure, the ζ-potential of the complex changes; for example, the ζ-potential of the initial liposomes (−31.6 ± 1.9 mV) gradually increased to −30.6 ± 1.1 mV at 250 µg/mL CDs and −28.1 ± 2.1 mV at 500 µg/mL CDs.72 This indicates that electrostatic interaction is one of the dominant forces driving the binding between the two components and enables them to associate tightly. The surface charge density of CDs not only allows them to bind closely to vesicles but also enables nanoscale localization and participation in functional interactions. By interacting with phospholipids, CDs can enhance membrane permeability and promote the rapid release of encapsulated drugs without inducing large-scale bilayer disruption.72,73

Thermodynamic Origins of Hydrogen Bonding & Hydrophobic Interactions

Techniques such as isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), combined with Gibbs free energy analysis, can provide deeper insight into the thermodynamic principles underlying the binding between CDs and lipid membranes. Gibbs free energy is expressed as ΔG = ΔH − T × ΔS. When two materials bind to each other, changes in enthalpy and entropy occur; by measuring the associated energy changes, the thermodynamic variations during their interaction can be explored. Boruah et al investigated the binding between CDs with different surface modifications and lipid vesicles, and found that amino-modified CDs bound strongly to vesicles and exhibited a significant negative enthalpy change. This suggests that the two hydrogen atoms in the amino groups form hydrogen bonds with the lone-pair electrons and negatively charged oxygen atoms in the phosphate groups, thereby promoting strong interactions between the two components.66 In addition, when hydrophobic CDs adsorb onto the lipid layer, a large number of water molecules are expelled, leading to a significant increase in system entropy. This hydrophobic effect therefore serves as another major driving force for maintaining the thermodynamic stability of the hybrid system.74

Membrane Fusion Mechanisms & Configurations

CDs mainly adopt three spatial configurations in vesicle systems: lumen-encapsulated, surface-tethered, and membrane-embedded states. These configurations depend on factors such as the hydrophobicity and surface charge of CDs, as well as the membrane fluidity and molecular packing density.75 When CDs are partially embedded in the membrane, they can act as counterions for negatively charged phospholipid entities, leading to an increase in the effective size of the head groups in the outer monolayer. This further induces changes in liposome membrane curvature and increases repulsion between vesicles, thereby reducing liposome size. These observations suggest that CDs within the lipid bilayer can interfere with the arrangement of lipid head groups and induce curvature changes, ultimately contributing to membrane structural instability.72 Small, weakly hydrophobic nanomaterials can interact with lipids and reduce the energy barrier for membrane fusion when vesicles come into contact with target cells.76 Meanwhile, CDs can interact with phospholipid head groups at the vesicle interface and serve as active sites for further binding with adjacent lipid membranes, helping lipids overcome the energy barrier and enter the fusion state, promoting crosslinking between vesicles and ultimately leading to membrane fusion.77

Thermodynamic Loading Limits, Interfacial Stability & Leakage Kinetics

Although CD-vesicle hybrids show excellent potential for delivery applications, their drug loading capacity and delivery stability are simultaneously constrained by thermodynamic loading limits and interfacial stability. During interactions with lipid membranes, the surface charges or functional groups of CDs can alter the head groups in the lipid bilayer, thereby changing the ζ-potential, thermodynamic phase-transition behavior, and membrane permeability of the lipid bilayer, ultimately affecting the drug release behavior of liposomes.72 This indicates that the surface charge and functional group composition of CDs are key factors regulating CD-membrane interfacial stability. Hydrophobic quantum dots can also directly affect the conformation of hydrocarbon chains; lipid tails tilt preferentially to interact with hydrophobic quantum dots, inducing substantial structural reorganization, resulting in a thinner lipid bilayer and making vesicles more prone to rupture.78

Under high-loading conditions, such CD-membrane interactions may further induce membrane structural rearrangement, local membrane defects, and increased permeability. When excessive nanocomponents, salt ions, or drug molecules are loaded inside the system, a significant osmotic pressure gradient may also form across the vesicle membrane. Osmotic pressure is an important physical factor regulating the burst-release or sustained-release behavior of nanocarriers.79 Excessively high internal osmotic pressure, local aggregation of CDs, or uneven distribution of CDs within the membrane may all reduce lipid bilayer stability and trigger premature leakage of encapsulated contents.

Minor structural and chemical changes in vesicle membranes can affect drug release rates, which are closely associated with the surface area, size, and distribution of pores on the membrane surface.80 Aggregation of domains within lipid membranes can significantly promote pore growth and cause membrane damage.81 CDs within the membrane may undergo lateral migration, local enrichment, or aggregation, thereby forming membrane domains with distinct interfacial properties and accelerating content leakage. When hybrids are exposed to changes in the external environment, local membrane defects may further evolve into transient pores or even irreversible rupture, resulting in uncontrolled burst release before the contents reach the target site.

To suppress premature leakage caused by membrane fluidity, CD aggregation, and interfacial instability, PEGylation or optimization of cholesterol content has been widely adopted in recent studies to enhance liposome interfacial stability. PEG-lipid modification can form a hydrophilic steric barrier on the vesicle surface, thereby reducing vesicle aggregation, fusion, and instability induced by complex media. For example, Izumi et al reported in 2024 that PEG-lipid modification significantly improved the long-term stability of liposomes in complex media such as natural seawater.82 On the other hand, cholesterol can improve the colloidal stability, bilayer stability, drug encapsulation efficiency, and release behavior of liposomes by regulating lipid bilayer order, membrane rigidity, and permeability.83 Therefore, for liposome-CD hybrids, PEGylation and optimization of cholesterol ratio can reduce nonspecific leakage risks through steric hindrance, membrane rigidity regulation, and bilayer stabilization, and may further prolong their blood circulation time and storage half-life.

Carbon Dot-Extracellular Vesicles Hybrids

CDs and EVs can form functionally complementary hybrid systems through encapsulation, membrane adsorption, electrostatic interactions, or membrane fusion. In these systems, CDs endow EVs with fluorescence imaging, drug-loading, ROS regulation, photothermal/photodynamic activity, and therapeutic traceability, whereas EVs provide CDs with biological membrane protection, prolonged circulation, improved biocompatibility, active targeting, and enhanced cellular uptake. Owing to this bidirectional synergy, EV-CD hybrids have shown potential in tumor therapy, antibacterial treatment, wound healing, neuroprotection, and biosensing.

Among these applications, antitumor therapy has been the most extensively investigated because CDs are already widely used as antitumor nanomedicines, serving as drug carriers, photosensitizers, photothermal agents, and ROS-regulating therapeutic platforms. However, free CDs are typically smaller than 10 nm, leading to rapid renal clearance, poor tumor retention, weak EPR-mediated accumulation, and limited active targeting. EV-mediated delivery can effectively overcome these limitations by protecting CDs from premature clearance, enhancing tumor accumulation through passive and receptor-mediated targeting, and improving intracellular delivery. Therefore, EV-CD hybrids can strengthen CD-based imaging, drug-release monitoring, phototherapy, chemotherapy enhancement, and overall antitumor efficacy while reducing systemic side effects. Wang et al developed a nanotherapeutic agent (MMV-Au-CDs-DOX) for the treatment of lung metastatic breast cancer by encapsulating smart nanomaterials Au-CDs with macrophage-derived microvesicles (MMVs).84 The AuNPs bind to the N/O donor dentate atoms on the surface of the cpc-CDs, assembling into spherical Au-CDs with a diameter of approximately 200 nm, thereby enhancing the EPR effect. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), the Au-CDs can be disassembled to restore fluorescence emission, and can be employed to detect glutathione (GSH) in the TME, thereby reducing the detection time to 5 minutes. The Au-CDs-DOX construct has been designed to target tumors by passive targeting of the EPR effect, thereby producing a therapeutic effect and enhancing the effectiveness of the EPR mechanism. In order to improve its targeting, Au-CDs-DOX has been modified by sonication using cell-derived MMVs with organ- or cell-specific characteristics. The targeting efficacy of MMV-Au-CDs-DOX was evaluated by measuring the fluorescence intensity of PL signals in 4T1 cells. The results demonstrated that the green and red fluorescence intensities of the MMV-Au-CDs-DOX-treated cells were significantly higher than those of the Au-CDs-DOX group, indicating that MMV-Au-CDs-DOX exhibited superior targeting efficiency compared to Au-CDs-DOX. The DOX group demonstrated that MMV could enhance cellular uptake and facilitate widespread accumulation in the tumor site within two hours of intravenous injection. Consequently, the modification of MMV enhanced the targeting of Au-CDs-DOX and improved the therapeutic effect.

Pratiksha Tiwari et al prepared DC@CDs by loading dacarbazine (DC) onto CDs and encapsulating exosomes isolated from breast cancer cells onto DC@CDs by extrusion/ultrasound to obtain Ex-DC@CDs with a diameter of 100–200 nm, which were employed in the targeted therapy of breast cancer.69 In comparison to DC@CDs lacking Exo encapsulation, Ex-DC@CDs exhibited heightened fluorescence intensity in MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells, augmented toxicity towards cancer cells, and a total of 65.5% ± 7.5% apoptotic cells following treatment with Ex-DC@CDs, which was superior to the percentage observed following treatment with DC@CDs. This can be attributed to the increased targeting of DC@CDs by exosomes. The exosome-derived proteins heparanase, syndecan-1 and glypican-1 bind specifically to the HSPG receptor, enabling the targeted delivery of Ex-DC@CDs to cancer cells. Furthermore, cytochromes on Exo can facilitate the direct activation of DCs in the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby enhancing efficacy. Consequently, Exo not only optimizes the targeting of drug delivery, but also promotes the release and activation of drugs, improves the precision and effectiveness of drug delivery, reduces side effects, and has the potential to become a powerful new therapeutic pathway.

In addition to their role in tumor treatment, EVs can also enhance the antimicrobial effect of CDs. Xiang et al developed a nanomedicine compliant for the treatment of sepsis by piggybacking Cu-doped carbon dots (Cu-CDs) on apoptotic vesicles (apoVs).85 The Cu-CDs enhance the bactericidal capacity of exogenous macrophages in septic mice; however, factors such as their in vivo biodistribution and the host response to infection limit the bacterial clearing process. The positively charged Cu-CDs were combined with apoVs using electrostatic forces. The resulting CC-apoVs were observed to be surrounded by a large number of Cu-CDs and to maintain an intact membrane structure. Compared with Cu-CDs, the cellular uptake of CC-apoVs by macrophages was increased by threefold, which further enhanced the bactericidal capacity of macrophages. This may be attributed to the assistance of apoVs in internalizing Cu-CDs in macrophages and their trophic effect on lysosomes. Additionally, the systemic administration of CC-apoVs was observed to rescue damaged liver resident macrophage Kupffer cells (KCs) and augment the number of bacteria captured by them. These findings indicate that the notable hepatic biodistribution and macrophage-targeting characteristics of apoVs facilitate the targeting of Cu-CDs to KCs, and that its specific biomarker, GTPase Rab5, regulates macrophage bactericidal effects. This provides a potential therapeutic strategy for targeting KCs in sepsis. However, this study was unable to demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of CC-apoVs in other sepsis models due to the differences between mouse and human sepsis models and the interaction between CC-apoVs and macrophage endocytosis was not fully investigated (Figure 4).

In a recent study, Safoora Zaffar and colleagues extracted EVs from rose petals (REVs) and combined them with citrate-based CDs for use in wound healing applications in diabetic rat models with local Escherichia coli infections.86 Research has demonstrated that both REVs and CDs possess antibacterial properties; however, when used in isolation, they are unable to kill Escherichia coli. The combination of these two components has been shown to promote robust interactions, resulting in a 50% increase in antibacterial efficacy, with REV-CDs exhibiting a distinctive permeation effect on membranes with high concentrations of lipopolysaccharides (LPS), while CDs generate ROS. This process increases the permeability of the bacterial peptidoglycan layer and outer membrane lipopolysaccharide layer, as well as inducing oxidative stress, leading to cell lysis and damage. This approach offers a promising antibiotic alternative for the treatment of bacterial infections, while simultaneously accelerating wound healing in cases of diabetes. While the conditions under which plants grow may have an effect on the production and composition of REV, this does not preclude the product from future applications. The efficacy of this application is likely to be significantly enhanced in the future if global standardized protocols are adopted for high-throughput extraction and preservation of EVs.

In the study by Ding et al, differential centrifugation was utilized as a method for extracting EVs from cacti.87 These vesicles were then combined with CDs derived from herbs, with the aim of facilitating the healing of burn wounds. The retention of the functional groups of their precursor compounds by CDs activates the endogenous coagulation cascade, enhances platelet interactions, significantly reduces activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and exhibits concentration-dependent effects. Conversely, EVs have been shown to markedly diminish ROS within the wound microenvironment, thereby promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 state. This, in turn, has been demonstrated to attenuate inflammation and fibrosis. The combination of these two factors has been shown to effectively stop bleeding, inhibit bacteria, and reduce inflammation, thereby promoting wound healing. Incorporating these materials with hydrogels, for instance, has been demonstrated to facilitate adhesion to the wound, thus offering a potentially efficacious approach for advanced wound management.

Chen Yang and colleagues prepared carbon dots (PDA C-dots) using dopamine hydrochloride as the raw material.67 The CDs were then loaded into exosome-derived from macrophages via ultrasonic treatment. The resulting PDAC-dots@Exosomes (PEs) were then used to protect neurons and alleviate the motor and non-motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease. PDA C-dots have been shown to possess substantial antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, with 25 μg/mL of PDA C-dots demonstrating the capacity to scavenge approximately one-fifth of reactive oxygen species. Despite their ability to traverse the BBB, their limited capacity for accumulation in brain regions remains a hindrance. However, it has been demonstrated that exosome derived from macrophages possess protein components that are similar to those of macrophage membranes, thereby enabling them to interact with endothelial cell ICAM-1 and target brain tissue. In vivo and in vitro imaging revealed that brain fluorescence in the PEs group was significantly higher than in the PDA C-dots group. Furthermore, the exosome-PDA C-dot system exhibits synergistic effects and possesses sustained-release properties, enabling continuous action on neurons and enhancement of therapeutic efficacy. Despite the absence of comprehensive research on the mechanism of exosome-carbon dot binding, this study offers novel insights into the potential of vesicle-carbon dot conjugates for crossing BBB and provides a foundation for future Parkinson’s disease therapeutic development. The study not only leverages the targeting properties of vesicles but also delves into the synergistic effects between vesicles and CDs.

EVs can also be used in conjunction with CDs for the purpose of cellular sensing. Huang et al prepared DNA logic-gated nanovesicles for monitoring intracellular redox status.88 This was achieved by wrapping gold CDs inside the nanovesicles and immobilizing DNA logic-gated modules on the vesicle surface. The DNA-based logic gate is composed of the aptamer Sgc8 and the I-motif, which are two different basic functional units. When the environment is acidic, the structure of the I-motif is altered, exposing Sgc8, which then binds to the membrane protein PTK7, which is expressed in cancer cells. The DNA logic gate is modified by cholesterol, which facilitates its immobilization to vesicles through hydrophobic interactions between cholesterol and giant cell membrane vesicles. GCDs were loaded into giant cell membrane vesicles by physical extrusion, and it was demonstrated that the vesicle-loaded GCDs could target cancer cells and that the fluorescence intensity of the GCDs increased in response to an increase in intracellular GSH. This can be attributed to the fact that the logic-gated modified nanovesicles enhanced the targeting of GCDs, enabling real-time monitoring of intracellular GSH fluctuations.

EVs and CDs can both be used as nano-loading platforms for drug delivery, but due to their respective shortcomings, combining the two to form a composite nano-loading platform has opened up new avenues for drug delivery development. Not only can this improve drug loading efficiency, but it also allows for the use of CDs’ fluorescence to monitor drug release, while enhancing the controllability of drug release and reducing side effects. Rui Su and colleagues utilized a hydrothermal method to synthesize fluorescent CDs and introduced them into microvesicles (MVs) secreted by macrophages.89 This approach was employed to prepare pH-responsive, trackable nanocarriers for the release of chemotherapy drugs and the monitoring of the drug release process in real time. The natural composition and structure of MVs enable them to evade immune surveillance, circulate in the bloodstream for extended periods, and actively target tumor tissues. However, the site and process of drug unloading from MVs are unpredictable Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that CDs exhibit charge reversal under different pH conditions. In the context of an acidic environment, electrostatic repulsion has been observed to occur between CDs and chemotherapy drugs, resulting in the expeditious release of the latter. At a pH of 7.4, strong electrostatic interactions have been shown to significantly retard the release of drugs. As the drugs are released into the cell nucleus, the fluorescence intensity of the CDs increases concomitantly, thus enabling the monitoring of the drug release behavior via the measurement of fluorescence intensity (Figure 5). This study fully leverages the distinct characteristics of MVs and CDs, enabling simultaneous drug delivery and monitoring. This, in turn, provides new insights for the functional development of drug delivery platforms.

Zhang et al prepared a type of GQDs derived from D-cysteine with right-handed chirality (D-GQDs).90 These were then combined with small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) to develop a chiral GQDs sEVs-loaded platform for drug delivery. Under confocal microscopy, at equivalent sEV concentrations, D-GQDs demonstrated higher fluorescence density and more effective penetration of the lipid bilayer. Taking doxorubicin (DOX) loading as an example, the loading efficiency of D-Cys-GQDs/Dox into sEVs was increased by more than fivefold. This phenomenon may be attributed to chiral interactions between chiral nanoparticles and biological lipid membranes, as well as synergistic effects on the surface. The loading platform has been demonstrated to enhance the efficiency of drug loading, whilst also allowing for the loading of drugs irrespective of their properties.

Fahimeh Kazeminava and colleagues developed a nanocarrier that integrates folate-derived CDs (FA-CDs) into exosome-derived from breast cancer cells for the delivery of the chemotherapy drug methotrexate (MTX) to treat breast cancer.91 Exosomes have been shown to retain their structural and functional properties after being extracted from breast cancer cells, with key markers such as CD81 and CD63 being maintained. The targeting of cancer cells in conjunction with FA-CDs via heparan sulphate proteoglycans (HSPGs) and folate receptors has been demonstrated to enhance targeting precision, reduce side effects caused by low targeting efficiency, and improve treatment efficacy. However, it should be noted that this study did not involve in vivo experimentation; therefore, its findings are only able to demonstrate in vitro efficacy. The complexity of the in vivo environment may result in alterations to the targeting properties of this composite nanocarrier. The present study capitalizes on the shared properties of exosomes and CDs, with the objective of enhancing delivery efficiency. In doing so, it fully utilizes the advantageous characteristics of both materials to develop new applications.

In recent years, CDs have shown promising application potential in tumor therapy, antibacterial treatment, wound healing, neuroprotection, and cellular sensing. However, their small particle size leads to drawbacks such as poor EPR effect, rapid in vivo clearance, and insufficient targeting, which limit their practical efficacy. EVs, with their unique biological interface, targeting ability, high loading capacity, and controlled release performance, can effectively make up for the aforementioned deficiencies of CDs, achieving functional complementarity and synergistic enhancement. Existing studies have combined EVs from various sources with CDs and their derivatives to construct a variety of nanotherapeutics. These composites exhibit excellent efficacy in cancer treatment, anti-infection, and biosensing, significantly improving the targeting, cellular uptake efficiency, biostability, and therapeutic effects of CDs, while realizing additional functions such as real-time monitoring of drug release by virtue of the fluorescent properties of CDs. Despite limitations including the lack of in vivo experiments, unclear composite mechanisms, and restricted result extrapolation due to model differences, the EVs-CDs composite system provides a novel strategy for nanodrug delivery, biodetection, and disease treatment, holding important research value and application prospects.

Carbon Dot-Liposomes Hybrids

In addition to EVs, lipids, which are the primary components of biological cell membranes, can also encapsulate CDs for targeted delivery of CDs.

RCDs have emerged as a prominent area of research interest due to their favorable fluorescent luminescence properties. However, their hydrophobic nature and limited tissue penetration restrict their potential applications. Zhao et al synthesized stable long-wavelength red light-emitting carbon dots (R-CDs) by solvothermal method and modified R-CDs with liposomes to achieve effective visualization of deep brain glioblastoma (GBM).92 R-CDs have passive nuclear targeting properties; however, they are unable to enter deep brain glioblastomas due to the limitations of the BBB and blood-brain-tumor barrier (BBTB). In comparison to R-CDs, liposome-modified R-CD-Ang2 demonstrated a six- to eleven-fold enhancement in BBB transcytosis while maintaining robust signaling expression at the GBM site 22 hours post-injection. This can be attributed to the fact that liposomes utilize vascular angiopep-2 peptides to localize the GBM and increase the permeability and EPR of the R-CDs. The resulting GBM diagnosis develops a rapid, accurate, low-toxicity new method and new ideas.

In addition to their applications in bioimaging, RCDs can also be employed as photothermal therapeutic agent in cancer therapy. Vinod Ravasaheb Shinde et al prepared lipid-coated red fluorescent carbon dots (LRCDs) as a photothermal agent for the treatment of breast cancer93 The LRCDs were prepared by rotary evaporation using soybean phospholipids coated with RCDs. The resulting LRCDs exhibited enhanced solubility and biocompatibility compared to RCDs, which resulted in increased cellular uptake. Following a five-minute exposure to a near-infrared laser (690 nm), the temperature of the LRCDs increased to a value exceeding 47 °C, representing a significant enhancement in photothermal efficacy compared to that observed for RCDs. Concomitantly, a greater number of cells perished in the LRCDs group, resulting in elevated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, LRCDs generated ROS following NIR light irradiation and reduced cell survival (Figure 6). These findings substantiate the assertion that LRCDs are more efficacious phototherapeutic agents than RCDs in eradicating cells through PTT and PDT effects. This can be attributed to the fact that lipids enhance the hydrophobicity of RCDs and augment their therapeutic efficacy, thereby rendering them suitable for utilization as effective phototherapeutic agents.

In addition to their use as therapeutic agents, CDs can also be employed as drug tracers. Bilal Demir et al prepared liposomes using a bi-encapsulation approach, with CDs in the aqueous zone and the therapeutic agent curcumin in the lipid bilayer.94 Furthermore, they modified the liposome surface with an anti-CD44 antibody for the purpose of imaging and tracking of cancer chemotherapy. The cellular uptake profiles demonstrated that the liposome-encapsulated CDs and curcumin resulted in a higher cellular uptake than the free CDs, with the maximum uptake efficiency reaching twice as much. This can be attributed to the targeting of the liposomes to the cancer cells and the specific binding of the anti-CD44 antibody to CD44 on the surface of the cancer cells. In addition to enhancing cellular uptake, liposome encapsulation of CDs has the potential to elevate their photoluminescence intensity. This can be attributed to the fact that liposome encapsulation mitigates the degradation of CDs, thereby enhancing their bioimaging capabilities.

Applications of Other Types of Vesicles Combined with CDs

Additionally, CDs are capable of self-assembly to form liposome-like structures, which are designated as “CDsomes.” Due to their amphiphilic nature, lipids are capable of forming vesicle-like structures. Similarly, CDs synthesized by a mild pyrolysis/hydrolysis process are amphiphilic, enabling them to form vesicle-like structures. Wu et al extracted CDs of triglyceride origin in butanol, with the hydrophobic portion collected in the organic phase and the hydrophilic portion retained in the aqueous phase.95 Following self-assembly to form liposome-like structures, amphiphilic CDs can selectively and rapidly stain nucleoli, cytoplasm and cell membranes, exhibiting faster uptake kinetics than free CDs.

In addition to biological vesicles, there are also synthetic vesicles chemically synthesized. Afreen Zaman et al utilized the hydrophobic structure of trimellitic acid to form amphiphilic substances with other compounds, thereby enabling them to spontaneously assemble into vesicles (TMAV) in water.96 Furthermore, the synthesis of carbon dots (HACD) was accomplished through the utilization of hyaluronic acid as a precursor. The two components were combined through electrostatic interactions to form a carbon dot-vesicle conjugate (HACD-TMAV), which was used for targeted drug delivery and selective bioimaging. HACD exhibits targeted specificity for CD44 and fluorescent properties; however, due to its small size, it cannot carry excessive amounts of drugs and is easily cleared from the body. Consequently, the combination of TMAV with HACD enhances the drug loading capacity by 10% in comparison with HACD alone, thereby augmenting drug loading efficiency whilst preserving targeted specificity. Furthermore, CD44-positive cancer cells have been shown to successfully internalize HACD-TMAV, and HACD-TMAV has been demonstrated to exhibit stable, intense blue fluorescence, thus rendering it suitable for targeted bioimaging. The utilization of vesicles and CDs in this approach provides a novel direction for the development of therapeutic and diagnostic tools for cancer treatment.

Besides EVs, lipids, as major components of biological cell membranes, can also achieve targeted delivery of CDs by encapsulating them. Studies have confirmed that similar to the EVs-CDs composite system, the lipid-CD/RCD composite system exhibits good efficacy in bioimaging, tumor phototherapy, and drug tracing, such as penetrating the BBB and other biological barriers to achieve accurate visualization, and enhancing photothermal and photodynamic therapeutic effects. Overall, lipids can effectively overcome the inherent defects of CDs, strengthen their biological functions, and provide a feasible strategy for biomedical applications in multiple fields.

Loading Methods for Carbon Dot-Vesicle Hybrids

Physical methods represent the most widely adopted approaches for loading CDs into vesicular carriers, owing to their mild reaction conditions and minimal disruption to vesicle structural integrity. Sonication and extrusion are classic physical strategies: vesicles and CDs are co-incubated under ultrasonic irradiation (typically 20–40 kHz) to transiently loosen lipid bilayer packing, followed by sequential extrusion through polycarbonate membranes (0.1–1.0 μm pore size) to promote membrane fusion and CD encapsulation. For instance, sonication-extrusion has been applied to load Au-doped CDs into MMVs, achieving structural preservation of MMVs while yielding a CD loading efficiency of ~35–40%.84 Electroporation,89 another physical technique, uses short electric pulses (0.5–2 kV/cm) to create transient pores in the vesicle membrane, enabling CD diffusion into the lumen; this method has been validated for loading hydrophilic CDs into sEVs with <5% loss of vesicle surface markers (eg, CD63, CD81) Despite their biocompatibility, physical methods suffer from low loading efficiency—sonication/extrusion typically achieves 30–45% CD encapsulation, while electroporation yields 25–38% for hydrophilic CDs—limiting their scalability for high-dose therapeutic applications.

Chemical and biological interactions-driven loading methods prioritize stability over mildness, with trade-offs in vesicle/CD bioactivity. Electrostatic adsorption is the most straightforward chemical approach:85,96 positively charged PEICDs bind spontaneously to negatively charged phosphatidylcholine vesicles via hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions, achieving loading efficiencies of 60–75% but potentially altering vesicle membrane fluidity. Covalent conjugation further enhances stability, with CD retention rates >80% over 72 h in physiological buffers, yet may compromise vesicle targeting ability by modifying surface receptors. Biological ligand-receptor interactions enable targeted CD loading with high specificity, but the conjugation process can denature surface proteins, reducing cellular uptake efficiency by 15–20% in vitro. Thin-film hydration and rotary evaporation—adapted from liposome fabrication—bridge physical and chemical strategies:93,94 lipid films are hydrated with CD solutions, and rotary evaporation (37–40 °C, 100–200 rpm) promotes CD entrapment in lipid bilayers, yielding 50–60% loading efficiency but risking CD aggregation at high concentrations. Notably, self-assembly of amphiphilic CDs into CD-somes (mimicking lipid vesicles) avoids exogenous loading entirely,95,97 yet this approach is restricted to CDs with well-defined hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance and has not been validated for in vivo applications. However, the molecular mechanism of CD-vesicle membrane fusion remains largely unexplored, particularly regarding vesicle membrane protein–CD interaction.

Conclusion and Future Perspective

CDs–vesicle hybrids are gradually becoming a unique and valuable research direction in nanomedicine. They address the mismatch between the excellent physicochemical properties of CDs and the biological requirements for effective in vivo delivery. CDs have outstanding physicochemical properties and good biocompatibility. However, their ultrasmall size often leads to rapid renal clearance, short circulation time, weak EPR-mediated accumulation, poor tissue retention, and limited active targeting ability. Vesicles can reshape the biological fate of CDs by providing a membrane-enclosed interface, a more suitable size range, intrinsic or engineered targeting ability, and the capacity to co-deliver drugs or other cargos. Therefore, vesicles are not only passive carriers. They also act as biological interfaces that improve the interaction between CDs and cells in pathological microenvironments, enhance their ability to cross biological barriers, and increase payload release.

The value of CDs–vesicle hybrids also lies in their bidirectional complementarity. Vesicles can improve the biodistribution and targeting ability of CDs. Meanwhile, the intrinsic properties of CDs and their interactions with vesicular structures can endow vesicles with imaging visibility, therapeutic effects, and real-time drug-release monitoring ability. This synergy has promoted their application in tumor therapy, bioimaging, antibacterial treatment, regenerative medicine, and other fields. Among these applications, cancer therapy remains the most widely explored. This is because CDs have been extensively studied for antitumor applications, while EVs, liposomes, and synthetic vesicles can further enhance the intracellular delivery and therapeutic efficacy of CDs.

Mechanistically, the performance of CDs–vesicle hybrids depends not only on vesicle-mediated targeting, but also on complex interfacial interactions. Electrostatic matching, hydrogen bonding, membrane insertion, and lipid reorganization jointly affect drug-loading efficiency, vesicle stability, drug release, leakage kinetics, and cellular uptake. CDs can bind to vesicles in different states, and each state may have different effects on membrane curvature, permeability, and interfacial stability. Therefore, understanding the CDs–vesicle interface is essential for moving this field from empirical formulation toward rational nanocarrier design. It is also important to develop convenient, efficient, and broadly applicable combination strategies for scalable production.

However, this field is still at an early stage. Current challenges include the thermodynamic limitations of CDs/drug loading, burst release, CDs aggregation within vesicle membranes, vesicle leakage, insufficient long-term stability, unclear pharmacokinetics, low yield, complex purification, and poor scalability. Future studies should focus on mechanism-guided rational interface engineering. By studying the interfacial mechanisms between CDs and vesicles, the size, charge, hydrophobicity, and other physicochemical properties of CDs can be matched with vesicle type, membrane composition, and disease microenvironment. With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, together with advanced characterization techniques, molecular dynamics simulations, and high-throughput screening, these emerging technologies may help clarify CDs–membrane interactions and provide evidence for the engineering design of CDs–vesicle hybrids. In particular, vesicle-like structures formed by CDs self-assembly, known as CDsomes, represent a highly promising next-generation platform. Overall, CDs–vesicle hybrids should be regarded as programmable biomimetic interfaces rather than simple nanocarriers.
