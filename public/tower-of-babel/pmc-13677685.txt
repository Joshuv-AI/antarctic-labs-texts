Efficient conversion of syngas into sustainable aviation fuel
Authors: Jian Han, Shenggang Li, Guofei Shen, Wei Liu, Jian Zhang, Dong Fang, Bohui Ye, Yifan Sun, Haofan Wang, Xianni Bu, Chengguang Yang, Hao Wang, Haiyan Yang, Jiong Li, Yuhan Sun, Peng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fischer−Tropsch synthesis to paraffinic kerosene is a crucial pathway to obtain sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), but a more efficient catalyst is needed for practical applications. Here, we report a cobalt-manganese catalyst supported on tetrapropylammonium hydroxide-etched Beta zeolites that breaks the long-standing activity-selectivity trade-off. The obtained catalyst achieves 82.8% CO conversion and 67.5% C8−16 selectivity, yielding a high productivity. It also exhibits remarkable stability and strong tolerance to CO2-rich feeds, thus enabling it to process real-world syngas feedstocks. The observed Co0−isolated Mnδ+ dual-site configuration identified by operando spectroscopies, along with the support’s hierarchical porosity and moderate acidity, is responsible for the highly efficient synthesis of jet fuel. In our pilot-scale test, the shaped pellet catalyst delivers a productivity of 5.154 g gCo−1 h−1 with 78.9% C8−16 hydrocarbons in liquid products. Excellent performance in industrial test and techno-economic analysis further demonstrates the strong potential of this catalyst for scalable production of SAF.

Introduction

Compared to conventional jet fuel, sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) enables the reduction of lifecycle carbon emissions by up to 80%, and is thus positioned as a key pathway to decarbonizing the aviation industry, which accounts for 2%−3% of global anthropogenic CO2 emissions. This promise is reinforced by strong global policy and financial support. The International Civil Aviation Organization Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for international aviation targets net-zero emissions of the aviation sector by 20501,2. The Aviation Regulation of the European Union (EU) mandates the increase of SAF blending from 2% in 2025 to 70% by 20503. The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act offers SAF production tax credits of $1.25−$1.75 per gallon4,5. Therefore, there are strong incentives to develop advanced catalysts for the selective, scalable, and cost-effective conversion of diverse carbon resources into SAF for realizing a net-zero aviation future.

As one of the four major American Society for Testing Materials-certified pathways for SAF production, the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) route offers high feedstock flexibility, well-established industrial infrastructure with strong scalability, and the ability to produce fully drop-in hydrocarbon fuels6–9, and is therefore expected to play a critical role in achieving the 2050 SAF targets10,11. FT synthesis for jet fuel production from syngas obtained from the conversion of various woody biomass via gasification depends critically on a highly active and selective catalytic system12–16. Cobalt-based catalysts are widely regarded as the most promising candidates for high-quality liquid fuel synthesis under low-temperature FT conditions, owing to their superior intrinsic activity, higher chain-growth probability, and negligible water-gas shift (WGS) activity17–23. However, the conventional FT reaction over these catalysts is inherently limited by the Anderson−Schulz−Flory (ASF) distribution, which restricts the maximum selectivity toward jet fuel-range (C8–16) hydrocarbons to ~41%24,25.

Bifunctional cobalt-zeolite catalysts recently emerged as promising in overcoming the above limitation18,26,27. Here, cobalt sites active CO and enable chain growth, while zeolite acid sites tune product distribution via hydrocracking and/or isomerization, resulting in C8−16 range products28–30. Despite the intensive effort and the notable progress, the jet fuel selectivity over most reported catalysts remains below 50%, with the catalyst time yield (CTY) rarely surpassing 1.8 g gCo−1 h−1 (Supplementary Table 1). Although Li et al. recently reported a high jet fuel selectivity of 72% over a La-modified mesoporous Y-type zeolite-supported cobalt catalyst for syngas conversion, their CO conversion was relatively low at only ~40%18. Due to the usual trade-off between catalytic activity and selectivity, simultaneously achieving high CO conversion and high C8–16 selectivity is greatly challenging.

Here, we demonstrate that rational engineering of both the pore architecture and acid sites in the zeolite support leads to significantly enhanced catalytic performance. A mild etching treatment of the Beta zeolite using tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH) yields a hierarchical support, which substantially promotes cobalt dispersion and reducibility while resulting in acid sites with moderate and uniform strength. The resulting cobalt-zeolite catalyst with unique active sites overcomes the conventional activity-selectivity trade-off in syngas conversion to jet fuel, simultaneously achieving high CO conversion and C8−16 selectivity of 82.8% and 67.5%, respectively, enabling a jet fuel productivity of 1.96 g gCo−1 h−1.

Results

Etching of Beta zeolite for Co-based catalysts

Mild etching was applied to a parent Beta (β) zeolite using a TPAOH solution (Supplementary Fig. 1). The resulting samples are denoted as βa, with a corresponding to the TPAOH concentration (0.1, 0.2, or 0.5 M). The βa zeolites retained the typical BEA crystal structure with a reduced SiO2/Al2O3 ratio (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Table 2). Morphologically, the parent β exhibited smooth ellipsoidal surfaces, whereas the βa samples displayed cuboidal or rectangular shapes with roughened surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 3). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements further demonstrated βa to possess a higher mesopore volume than the parent β, with the pore sizes mainly distributed between 3 − 12 nm (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations of a hierarchical mesoporous structure31 (Supplementary Fig. 5). NH3 temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) and pyridine adsorption infrared spectra (Py-IR) revealed that the strong acid sites of β zeolite were partially neutralized in βa, resulting in milder acidity. Importantly, the nearly unchanged ratio of Brønsted-to-Lewis (B/L) acid sites suggested that the mild etching was applied uniformly, thereby maintaining the intrinsic acid site structure32 (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 6, and Supplementary Table 3). Unlike conventional alkaline treatment that preferentially consumed Brønsted acid centers and broke the B/L balance, uniform mild etching preserved the intrinsic matching relationship between Brønsted and Lewis acid sites. This well-maintained B/L synergy guaranteed efficient skeletal isomerization of long-chain n-alkanes: Brønsted acid sites generated carbocation intermediates for hydrocarbon rearrangement, while adjacent Lewis acid sites stabilized these intermediates and suppressed undesired β-scission over cracking33,34. 27Al multiple-quantum (MQ) magic angle spinning (MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra further indicated that the Al coordination environment remained largely preserved after TPAOH etching (Fig. 1d and e). The dominant signals assigned to the framework tetrahedral Al species were well-maintained in β0.2, indicating that the integrity of the zeolite framework was largely retained. The engineered βa support was then impregnated with the CoMn active species via the incipient wetness method. Successful deposition of Co3O4 nanoparticles (NPs) on the zeolites was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Notably, the stronger intrinsic acidity of the parent β induced a stronger metal-support interaction between the cobalt species and the zeolite framework, as evidenced by the positive shift of the Co 2p binding energy in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 9). H2 temperature-programmed reduction experiments revealed a lower onset reduction temperature for CoMn/βₐ (Supplementary Fig. 10), reflecting enhanced reducibility due to the more accessible pore structure and the reduced metal-support interaction. Consequently, hierarchical βa-supported Co3O4 NPs exhibited smaller and more uniform particle sizes around 12.1 nm, unlike the larger and more broadly distributed particles formed on the parent β zeolite (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 11). H2 chemisorption further confirmed that Co dispersion in CoMn/βₐ was much higher than that in CoMn/β (Supplementary Table 4). These findings establish that mild etching with TPAOH serves as an effective strategy for simultaneous engineering of the pore structure and acidity. The resulting improvements in metal dispersion and metal-support interactions are key to the markedly enhanced catalytic performance.

Enhanced selective jet fuel synthesis over CoMn/β0.2

Catalytic performance evaluation reveals the critical role of the acidic zeolite support in tailoring the product distribution. In contrast to conventional SiO2- and Al2O3-supported catalysts, which produce significant amounts of heavy wax (C21+), CoMn-based catalysts supported on acidic zeolites (e.g., ZSM-5, Y, and β) effectively suppress chain overgrowth and promote the formation of C5−20 liquid hydrocarbons (Fig. 2a). Among them, the CoMn/β catalyst achieved 61.4% CO conversion and 75.3% C5–20 selectivity, with C8−16 selectivity reaching 47.8%, thereby breaking the selectivity limit by ASF distribution. Meanwhile, the C21+ fraction was markedly reduced from 30.8% over CoMn/SiO2 to 3.6% over CoMn/β. Upon TPAOH etching, this effect was further strengthened. The optimized CoMn/β0.2 catalyst delivered simultaneously higher CO conversion of 82.8% and C8−16 selectivity of 67.5%, corresponding to a CTY of 1.96 g gCo−1 h−1 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 5). Analyses of the product distribution and chain growth probability (α) revealed that TPAOH etching optimized secondary pathways, directing selectivity toward C8−16 hydrocarbons (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Figs. 12–14). Moreover, five independent batches of the CoMn/β0.2 catalyst showed consistent CO conversion and product distribution, confirming excellent reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 15). The CoMn/β0.2 catalyst also exhibited robust performance under elevated temperatures. Under the harsh reaction temperature of 250 °C, a nearly complete single-pass CO conversion of >90% was achieved, while retaining a remarkable C8−16 selectivity of 54.1% (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 6). The increase in CO conversion at higher temperature was attributed to acceleration of CO dissociation and C−O bond cleavage on Co0 sites. Detailed compositional analysis further revealed that increasing the reaction temperature markedly promoted hydrocarbon isomerization, with the Ciso/Cn ratio of the C8−16 fraction growing from 0.11 at 230 °C to 1.42 at 250 °C (Supplementary Fig. 17), demonstrating the excellent intrinsic isomerization capability of the catalyst. However, this enhanced isomerization was accompanied by lower C8−16 selectivity and higher CH4 selectivity, indicating a trade-off between isomerization activity and jet-fuel yield. Further pressure-dependent studies reveal a pronounced optimization window: increasing the pressure from 1.0 to 1.5 MPa improves the C8−16 selectivity sharply from 48.7% to 67.5%, while higher pressure shifts the product distribution toward heavier C17+ fractions (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 7). This behavior reflected the pressure dependence of chain growth: higher pressure increased the surface coverage of adsorbed CO and the concentration of reactive CHx monomers, thus favoring chain propagation over chain termination. However, at excessively high pressure, chain growth became dominant, resulting in the accumulation of heavy hydrocarbons. Notably, the catalyst works well at 2.0−4.0 MPa, which matches the typical outlet pressure of syngas derived from woody biomass35,36. Furthermore, the CTY of C8−16 hydrocarbons increases at higher space velocity, reaching a jet fuel CTY of 2.508 g gCo−1 h−1 at 4500 mL gcat−1 h−1, alongside a CO conversion of 74.7% and C8−16 selectivity of 63.8% (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Table 8). The increase in CTY at higher WHSV reflected the greater throughput of reactants per unit catalyst mass. The slight decrease in CO conversion at higher WHSV was due to the reduced residence time, while the moderate decline in C8–16 selectivity resulted from insufficient secondary cracking of C17+ hydrocarbons over acid sites at shorter contact time.

Tuning the H2/CO ratio from 1.0 to 3.0 further enhanced the CO conversion up to 89.5%, while the C8−16 selectivity remained at ~60% (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Table 9). The continuous rise in CO conversion with increasing H2/CO ratio originated from elevated surface hydrogen coverage, which accelerated CO dissociation and hydrogenation of the adsorbed carbon intermediates over Co0 sites. However, excessive hydrogen also promoted undesired hydrogenolysis and chain termination, thus leading to higher CH4 selectivity. Meanwhile, the acid-catalyzed cracking of C17+ hydrocarbons over zeolitic sites followed statistical β-scission, yielding only a portion of C8–16 products37,38. Consequently, the C8–16 selectivity reflected the balance between FT chain growth and secondary hydrocarbon cracking. Low H2/CO ratios limited chain growth and precursor supply, while high ratios caused over-cracking to form light products, with the optimal C8–16 selectivity only achieved at a moderate H2/CO of 2. We further investigated the influence of CO2 with the total gas flow rate fixed at 3000 mL gcat−1 h−1. Notably, over a wide range of CO2 concentrations (0−50% of total carbon) in the feed syngas, the C8−16 hydrocarbon selectivity was maintained at a high level, with the maximum exceeding 70% at 5% and 15% CO2. Critically, CO2 conversion was negligible (Supplementary Fig. 21), indicating that CO2 acts primarily as a diluent rather than a reactant39,40. This underscored the inertness and tolerance of the catalyst to CO2, which enables the direct use of CO2-rich crude syngas from woody biomass gasification or electrochemical CO2 reduction36,41. The stability of CoMn/β0.2 was also evaluated under industrial-relevant conditions. As depicted in Fig. 2e, activity and product distribution of the catalyst remained stable during over 500 h of continuous operation. When benchmarked against state-of-the-art bifunctional catalysts under comparable conditions (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 1), the catalyst not only exhibited superior C8−16 selectivity but also achieved a record productivity of C8−16 hydrocarbons.

Structural evolution of active sites

We investigated the structural stability and resistance to deactivation of the CoMn/β0.2 catalyst during the reaction through a series of characterizations. TEM analysis revealed the formation of Co0 NPs (with an average size of ~11.9 nm) after reduction, and no significant sintering was observed after the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 22). High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images and energy dispersive X-ray mappings revealed that Co and Mn species were uniformly dispersed on the β0.2 support (Si, Al, O) after both reduction and reaction, with no detectable segregation or migration (Supplementary Figs. 23–26). Thermogravimetric and Raman analyses of samples collected at different reaction times showed that the amount of carbon deposition remained essentially unchanged during prolonged operation (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). Furthermore, N2 physisorption measurements of the spent catalyst (after CS2 washing to remove surface wax) revealed only a marginal decrease in the BET surface area (from 486 to 454 m2 g–1) and mesopore volume (from 0.34 to 0.29 cm3 g–1) (Supplementary Fig. 29), indicating the structural robustness of the catalyst. Post-reaction XRD analysis revealed only the main characteristic diffraction peaks of metallic Co and the β zeolite, with no detectable Co2SiO4 phase associated with catalyst deactivation (Supplementary Fig. 30). These results collectively demonstrated the excellent structural stability and anti-deactivation capability of our catalyst, consistent with its high stability.

We further employed a series of in situ characterization techniques to systematically investigate the structural evolution of the CoMn/β0.2 catalyst under both reduction and reaction conditions. In situ XRD revealed the stepwise reduction of Co3O4 to metallic Co0 via a CoO intermediate between 250 and 300 °C, with complete conversion achieved above 350 °C (Fig. 3a). Under FT conditions, only Co0 diffraction peak was observed, confirming it as the steady-state active phase42 (Fig. 3b). Operando XAS was employed to track the evolution of the local coordination environments of Co and Mn in the CoMn/β0.2 catalyst. During H2 reduction at 400 °C, the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra at the Co K-edge (Fig. 3c) exhibited a continuous decrease in the white-line intensity and a shift of the absorption edge toward lower energy, approaching that of the Co foil after 120 min. Under standard reaction conditions, the edge position and near-edge structure remained unchanged (Fig. 3d), indicating the stability of the reduced cobalt species. Concurrently, the Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectra (Fig. 3e) showed a persistent Co−Co coordination shell at ~2.1 Å throughout the reaction, a distance characteristic of metallic cobalt. The absence of a significant Co−O scattering path further confirmed that cobalt exists as stable metallic Co0 NPs under working conditions43. In contrast, the Mn K-edge XANES spectra were consistently positioned between the Mn foil and MnO2 references (Supplementary Fig. 31), indicating a partially oxidized Mnδ+ state. The corresponding FT-EXAFS spectrum (Fig. 3f) displayed only a dominant peak at ~1.7 Å, characteristic of the Mn−O coordination, with no detectable Mn−Mn or Mn−Co scattering path. This unambiguously indicated that the Mn species were anchored on the β0.2 support in an atomically dispersed Mn−O configuration44,45. Wavelet transform EXAFS further distinguished the scattering contributions (Fig. 3g, h and Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). To decouple their functions, reference catalysts containing only Mn (Mn/β0.2) and only Co (Co/β0.2) were prepared and evaluated. The Mn/β0.2 exhibited negligible activity, while Co/β0.2 achieved only moderate CO conversion with high methane selectivity and a low yield of the target C8−16 fraction (Supplementary Fig. 35). In contrast, both CO conversion and C8−16 selectivity were markedly enhanced with the bimetallic CoMn/β0.2 catalyst. To further unravel the promotional role of Mn, comprehensive characterizations were carried out on Co/β0.2 and CoMn/β0.2 catalysts. CO-TPD, XPS, and TEM measurements confirmed that Mn modified the cobalt active sites via electronic and geometric effects. Electron transfer from Mn to Co increased the electron density of metallic Co0 and strengthened CO adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 36); meanwhile, Mn improved cobalt dispersion due to reduced Co nanoparticle size (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 37). NH3-TPD and Py-IR measurements revealed that Mn moderately decreased the Brønsted acidity but slightly increased the Lewis acidity, indicating that isolated Mn species partially neutralized the Brønsted acid sites and simultaneously generated additional Lewis acid sites (Supplementary Fig. 38). This moderate acidity regulation effectively suppressed excessive secondary cracking while preserving sufficient acidity for hydrocarbon isomerization. A series of characterizations and control experiments were combined to reveal the effective synergy between metallic Co0 nanoparticles and isolated Mnδ+ species. Thus, the superior catalytic performance of CoMn/β0.2 in FT synthesis was attributed to the dual-site structure.

Mechanistic studies

To identify the factors that break the activity–selectivity trade-off, we decoupled the contributions of metal Co dispersion, pore structure, and acidity. Correlation plots confirmed that improved Co dispersion was the primary factor in boosting catalytic activity, while appropriate mesoporosity and Brønsted acid density collectively enhanced jet fuel selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 39). Further mechanistic studies were conducted to elucidate the underlying mechanism for the simultaneous optimization of pore structure and acidity in β zeolite via TPAOH etching. CO-TPD showed that the CO desorption peak shifted to a lower temperature on CoMn/β0.2, indicating weakened C−O bonding (Supplementary Fig. 40). In situ high-pressure CO diffuse reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (DRIFTS) further revealed stronger bands corresponding to gaseous, linear, and bridge CO species on CoMn/β0.2 than on CoMn/β, suggesting higher CO surface coverage on Co0 NPs and thus enhanced CO activation relative to CoMn/β (Fig. 4a)46. This is consistent with a decrease in the apparent activation energy from 138.1 kJ mol−1 for CoMn/β to 93.8 kJ mol−1 for CoMn/β0.2 (Fig. 4b). Stronger H2 dependence was indicated from the estimated reaction orders, reflecting an H-assisted CO activation pathway in which adsorbed hydrogen participates in C−O bond cleavage and formation of hydrogenated intermediates (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 41)47,48. Compared to CoMn/β, enhanced hydrogen activation over CoMn/β0.2 was further inferred from H2/D2 exchange and C2H4 pulse hydrogenation experiments, promoting the conversion of surface intermediates from CO activation (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). To elucidate the role of zeolite acidity in the secondary conversion of long-chain hydrocarbons, dual-bed mixing experiments were performed. An FT-active but non-acidic CoMn/SiO2 catalyst was physically mixed with acidic but FT-inert β or β0.2 zeolites, thereby spatially decoupling the chain growth from the acid-catalyzed reactions. Compared with CoMn/SiO2 alone, adding either zeolite markedly suppressed the formation of heavy hydrocarbons and shifted the product distribution toward the C8−16 range (Fig. 4e). Notably, the CoMn/SiO2 + β0.2 system exhibited the highest C8−16 selectivity alongside an enhanced CO conversion, whereas the CoMn/SiO2 + β system yielded a comparatively higher selectivity toward C2−7 light hydrocarbons. Detailed analysis of the jet fuel fraction further revealed that β0.2 substantially increased the fraction of iso-paraffins (30.9%), compared to β (19.1%), while simultaneously reducing the olefins content (Fig. 4f). These results demonstrated that the moderate acidity of β0.2, achieved via TPAOH etching, effectively promoted isomerization while suppressing excessive cracking, thereby directing product selectivity toward the jet fuel-range.

To further reveal the evolution of surface species under reaction conditions, temperature-dependent operando DRIFTS experiments were conducted (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 44). These spectroscopic studies showed that, at higher temperatures, vibrational bands of the surface formate and alkoxy (OCHx) species, which are key intermediates in the CO insertion pathway, emerged but remained relatively weak48,49. In contrast, the characteristic C–H stretching vibrations of alkyl (CHx) species were significantly more intense, indicating that CHx species were the dominant surface intermediates under reaction conditions. This spectroscopic observation strongly suggested that the chain propagation proceeded primarily through the CH2 carbene mechanism50. Moreover, compared with atmospheric-pressure conditions, operando DRIFTS under elevated pressure showed weaker linear CO adsorption but a significantly higher contribution from bridge CO species (Supplementary Fig. 45). Such a shift is attributed to the multi-coordinated CO adsorption, which weakens the C−O bond and facilitates its cleavage for the insertion steps51,52. Moreover, CHx intermediates were formed more extensively on CoMn/β0.2 than on CoMn/β, indicating that the hierarchical β0.2 promoted C−C coupling (Supplementary Fig. 46).

Based on the above evidence, Fig. 4h presents a schematic of the proposed reaction network. The reaction is initiated by H-assisted CO dissociation on Co0 sites, which generates surface carbon species. These surface carbon species are subsequently hydrogenated to form CH2 intermediates, which serve as the key monomers for chain growth. Chain propagation then proceeds via the insertion of CH2 species into the growing alkyl chain. The resulting hydrocarbon intermediates subsequently undergo isomerization and controlled cracking at the zeolite acid sites, thus enabling the selective synthesis of C8−16 products.

Analysis of industrial application prospects

The improved performance of CoMn/β0.2 in terms of activity, selectivity, stability, and feedstock flexibility has prompted us to scale up and shape this catalyst for potential industrial applications. However, there is a considerable challenge, as many catalysts with excellent performance at the laboratory gram scale frequently undergo severe performance deterioration during scale-up, attributed to reduced active site accessibility, pore blockage, or insufficient mechanical strength53. In this study, we successfully achieved the kilogram-scale preparation of the CoMn/β0.2 catalyst, which was shaped into cylindrical pellets with a diameter of 2.5 mm and a height of 5.0 mm and evaluated in a pilot-scale fixed-bed reactor (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 47). The characterization and evaluation results confirmed the great potential of the shaped CoMn/β0.2 catalyst for industrial applications (Supplementary Fig. 48). The cobalt content in the shaped catalyst is 11.3 wt.%, reflecting dilution by the binder, which likely accounts for its lower CO conversion relative to the granular catalyst under comparable conditions. The pelleted catalyst delivered a CO conversion of 67.3% with an impressive C8−16 selectivity of 69.2%, corresponding to a jet fuel CTY of 2.537 g gCo−1 h−1 (Supplementary Fig. 49 and Supplementary Table 10). The pelleted catalyst was further tested under tail-gas recycling, an industrially common practice for improving feed utilization efficiency. When the tail gas was recycled at a ratio of three, the overall CO conversion rose to 92.4%, and the C8−16 selectivity calculated from condensed liquid products reached 78.9%, and the jet fuel CTY was up to 5.154 g gCo−1 h−1 (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 50, and Supplementary Table 11). Notably, the C8−16 selectivity showed only a modest increase at higher recycle ratios, whereas the overall CO conversion and catalyst space-time yield improved significantly. This observation was consistent with previous reports that the recycle ratio influences product distribution through competing effects of olefin re-adsorption and steam removal54. The mild change in selectivity can also be explained by the fact that the cracking capacity of acid sites on the bifunctional catalyst became saturated under the given reaction conditions. Building on these promising results, we performed a 1000 tonnes per year FT-to-jet fuel pilot test, which was completed in 2025 using a similar catalyst system (Supplementary Figs. 51–53). At present, we plan to build a factory with a 100,000-tonnes annual capacity using biomass gasification-derived syngas as the feedstock in Luzhou city, Sichuan Province, China, before 2028. Simulations using Aspen Plus based on the experimental catalytic data confirmed the technical feasibility of this scaled process (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Tables 12–15). Techno-economic analysis (TEA) of CoMn/β0.2 at this scale indicated that, under a baseline of 8000 operating hours per year, the total annual operation cost is estimated at USD 113.89 million, while the annual SAF sales revenue reaches USD 302.43 million, yielding a remarkable annual profit of approximately USD 164.73 million (Supplementary Table 12). This profit is much higher than that of the CoMn/SiO2-based syngas-to-SAF route (Supplementary Tables 16–19). A detailed cost breakdown reveals that biomass feedstock is the largest expense, constituting 58.5% of the total operating cost (Supplementary Fig. 54). This highlights that securing a stable and cost-effective biomass supply chain is pivotal for the project’s economic viability. Sensitivity analysis further shows that the SAF selling price has the greatest impact on profitability, underscoring the importance of policy incentives and low-carbon fuel premiums (Fig. 5c). Life-cycle assessment further demonstrates that the CoMn/β0.2-based FT-to-SAF pathway reduces greenhouse gas emissions by approximately 80% compared with the conventional jet fuel production route (Supplementary Fig. 55). Further integration of renewable electricity and green hydrogen could enhance both the environmental and economic competitiveness of this pathway.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a hierarchical CoMn/β0.2 bifunctional catalyst via a mild TPAOH etching strategy that simultaneously tailors the pore structure and tunes the acidity of Beta zeolite. Such structural modulation enhanced metal dispersion, improved reactant accessibility, and optimized metal-acid site synergy, resulting in the formation of a Co0−isolated Mnδ+ dual-site configuration under reaction conditions. This synergy helps to overcome the activity-selectivity trade-off in Fischer−Tropsch synthesis for jet fuel. The catalyst sets a benchmark by delivering concurrently high CO conversion (82.8%) and high jet fuel-range selectivity (67.5%), leading to a record C8−16 productivity, which overcomes the long-standing activity-selectivity trade-off for previous catalysts. Importantly, this catalyst can be synthesized at the kilogram-scale level, and after being shaped into pellets, it exhibits excellent performance with 78.9% C8−16 hydrocarbons in liquid products and a remarkable jet fuel CTY of 5.154 g gCo−1 h−1 during a 1000 tonnes per year industrial pilot test. The excellent pilot test results and the positive techno-economic and lifecycle assessments underscore its strong potential for industrial deployment. This work not only provides a viable route for large-scale SAF production from diverse carbon resources, but also establishes a general design principle for breaking intrinsic selectivity limitations during syngas conversion.

Methods

Synthesis of βa

A commercial β zeolite (SiO2/Al2O3 = 40, Nankai University Catalyst Co.) served as the parent material and was structurally modified via an alkaline etching strategy using tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH). Specifically, a measured quantity of the parent zeolite was dispersed in an aqueous TPAOH solution of a designated concentration (a = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 M). The mixture was continuously stirred at 80 °C for 2 h to facilitate controlled desilication. Subsequently, the solid product was isolated by high-speed centrifugation, followed by repeated washing with deionized water until the supernatant reached neutral pH, ensuring the complete removal of residual alkali and dissolved silicates. The washed filter cake was dried in an air oven at 120 °C for 12 h, and finally transferred to a muffle furnace. The sample was calcined in static air with a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1 to 550 °C and held at this temperature for 4 h to remove the templating agent and stabilize the zeolite framework. The resulting modified zeolite samples are denoted as βa.

Synthesis of CoMn-based catalysts

The catalysts were synthesized via a strategy combining incipient wetness impregnation with vacuum freeze‑drying. Using the βa zeolites obtained above as the matrix, a mixed precursor solution containing cobalt nitrate and manganese nitrate was prepared with a Co: Mn molar ratio of 20:1, which was determined to be optimal through preliminary screening experiments across a range of Co/Mn ratios (from 10:1 to 40:1), with a cobalt loading of 20 wt% (Supplementary Fig. 56). The calculated volume of the salt solution was added slowly and dropwise to the zeolite powder, allowing uniform penetration until a homogeneous wet paste was obtained. The wet paste was then flash‑frozen in an ultra‑low‑temperature freezer at −80 °C for 24 h. The thoroughly frozen sample was immediately transferred to a freeze dryer and subjected to vacuum freeze‑drying at −50 °C under a pressure of <10 Pa for 24 h to remove water while preserving the porous architecture of the precursor. The resulting fluffy solid was ground into a fine powder and calcined in a tubular furnace under a 10% NO/Ar (v/v) atmosphere. The temperature was ramped at 2 °C/min to 250 °C and held for 5 h, yielding the final CoMn/βa catalysts. For comparison, a series of reference catalysts were prepared in parallel following the identical synthesis route, metal ratio, and loading, using commercial SiO2, Al2O3, ZSM‑5 (SiO2/Al2O3 = 36), and Y zeolite (SiO2/Al2O3 = 5.6, all from Nankai University Catalyst Co.) as supports.

Catalyst tests

The FT was conducted in a fixed-bed steel reactor. Before the catalytic tests, the catalyst was ground and sieved to a particle size of 60–80 mesh. Prior to the reaction, the catalyst was pretreated in pure H2 gas (50 mL min−1) at 400 °C for 8 h. Afterward, the temperature was lowered to 200 °C, and syngas was introduced under the specified reaction conditions for catalytic testing. The FT performance of the pellet catalyst (10 g) was investigated in our pilot-scale fixed-bed reactor (internal diameter 19 mm, length 1180 mm), which was equipped for operation at industrial working conditions with a recycled tail gas. The oil and wax products generated during the FT reaction were collected using a cold trap (0 °C) and a hot trap (120 °C), and analyzed using a flame ionization detector (FID) equipped on a Shimadzu GC-2010. The tail gas, consisting of H2, CO, N2, CO2, and gas-phase hydrocarbons (C1−7), was analyzed using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and an FID, also on a Shimadzu GC-2010. Detailed product analysis and calculation methods for CO conversion and selectivity are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Catalyst characterization

Structural and chemical characterization, including powder XRD, X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy, inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy, N2 physisorption, thermogravimetric, XPS, Py-IR, and temperature-programmed analyses (H2-TPR, CO-TPD, NH3-TPD) were performed following standard protocols detailed in Supplemental Methods.

SEM images were collected on a ZEISS SUPRATM 55 SAPPHIRE microscope, while TEM characterizations were performed on a JEOL-JEM 2000FX microscope to investigate the catalyst morphology. Prior to measurements, the samples were dispersed in ethanol with ultrasonication, and then dropped onto carbon-coated copper grids for observation.

27Al MQ MAS NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance NEO 600 MHz spectrometer (14.1 T), using a 3.2 mm field gradient MAS probe. The 27Al MAS NMR spectra were acquired at a spinning rate of 12.5 kHz using a π/12 pulse (1.25 μs), a recycle delay of 0.8 s, and 4096 scans. Two-dimensional Fourier transformation combined with subsequent shearing transformation was applied to obtain pure absorption-mode 2D contour plots.

In situ XRD was performed on a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with a high-temperature in situ reaction cell, using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. For the reduction process, the sample was heated from room temperature to 400 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min⁻1 under a continuous H2 flow (50 mL min⁻1) and held at 400 °C for 2 h. After complete reduction, the sample was cooled to 200 °C under Ar purge, then heated to the reaction temperature of 230 °C, and exposed to syngas (H2/CO = 2, 50 mL min⁻1) to initiate the reaction. Patterns were collected at 2° min⁻1 (2θ range of 5−90°) under continuous flow. The XRD measurements of the ex situ samples were conducted on the same diffractometer at room temperature in air.

In situ high-pressure CO chemisorption DRIFTS measurements were performed on a ThermoFisher Scientific Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. Twenty milligrams sample was in situ reduced at 400 °C for 2 h in a H2 flow of 50 mL min−1. The gas was then switched to Ar to purge the cell for 0.5 h, after which the reactor was cooled to 230 °C for background spectrum collection. Subsequently, the system was pressurized to 1.5 MPa, and the Ar flow was replaced with CO for high-pressure adsorption, followed by the collection of CO adsorption DRIFT spectra.

Operando XAS experiment was performed at Beamline BL11B in the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The operating voltage in “charging” mode is 3.5 GeV and the maximum current is 240 mA. The catalyst was pressed into a 13 mm-diameter pellet and loaded into a home-built reaction cell capable of withstanding pressures up to 5 MPa. Precise gas delivery was controlled by mass flow controllers, while a K-type thermocouple and a back-pressure regulator were installed for in situ temperature monitoring and pressure control. The catalyst was heated from room temperature to 400 °C and reduced for 2 h, during which Co K-edge and Mn K-edge XANES spectra were recorded. Co K-edge data were acquired in transmission mode, while Mn K-edge measurements were performed in fluorescence mode with a Lytle detector owing to the low Mn loading. After reduction, the system was cooled to 230 °C, fed with syngas (H2/CO = 2), and slowly pressurized to 1.5 MPa for spectral data collection. All the obtained spectra were calibrated and normalized using the Athena program. FT-EXAFS and WT-EXAFS analysis were obtained via Athena and the Hama Fortran program, respectively.

Operando DRIFTS was acquired on a spectrometer (Bruker, TENSOR-II) equipped with an in situ cell at the BL06B beamline of SSRF to analyze the catalytic behavior under reaction conditions. The sample was first in situ reduced under a flow of H2 at 400 °C for 2 h, followed by in situ purging with Ar at the same temperature for 1 h to remove residual hydrogen. The cell was then cooled to 30 °C, and a background spectrum was collected at this temperature under Ar flow. Subsequently, syngas with an H2/CO molar ratio of 2 was introduced into the cell, and the pressure was slowly increased to 1.5 MPa via a back-pressure regulator. Operando DRIFTS spectra were recorded continuously at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with 32 scans accumulated per spectrum, over the wavenumber range of 4000–650 cm−1.

Techno-economic analysis

A rigorous process model from biomass gasification to SAF was developed using Aspen Plus V11 to obtain the system‑wide material and energy balances. The Peng‑Robinson equation of state was applied for thermodynamic calculations of the vapor‑phase mixtures (primarily light gases and hydrocarbons) throughout the process to ensure accuracy. The model encompasses all key conversion steps: biomass gasification, syngas purification and conditioning, Fischer‑Tropsch synthesis, product separation, and SAF hydro‑upgrading and isomerization. The biomass feedstock was defined by its detailed elemental composition (C, H, O, N, S) and ash content. Following the simulation, an economic analysis was performed using Aspen Capital Cost Estimator V11. Furthermore, a life‑cycle carbon emission assessment was conducted by coupling the simulation output data within a Python programming environment.
