Associations of Disease Activity, Depression Severity, and Quality of Life in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria
Authors: Tuba Vural, Hacer Vural Karatoprak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is often a psychophysiological comorbidity. Depression is believed to both cause and sustain CSU.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the severity of depression in patients with CSU and the impact of CSU on quality of life.MethodsWe enrolled 150 CSU and 75 healthy controls. We calculated the disease activity with Urticaria Activity Score 7 (UAS7) and divided the chronic urticaria patients into two groups as mild-moderate CSU (N=75) and severe CSU (N=75). We used the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) to measure the severity of depression in all patients and the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) to measure the quality of life in 150 CSU patients.ResultsSignificant main effects were observed for disease severity (P<0.001) and sex (P<0.001) on rank-transformed DLQI scores and PHQ-9 scores. Patients with severe CSU had substantially higher adjusted rank-transformed DLQI and PHQ-9 scores than those with mild–moderate CSU (P<0.001). Similarly, females exhibited higher rank-transformed DLQI and PHQ-9 scores compared to males (P<0.001). Among covariates, disease duration exerted a strong positive effect on rank-transformed DLQI and PHQ-9 scores (P<0.001). Additionally, the presence of angioedema was associated with increased depressive symptoms (P=0.042), whereas allergic comorbidities had a minor protective effect (P=0.001).ConclusionThis multicenter study demonstrated that disease severity and duration are the primary determinants of elevated rank-transformed DLQI and PHQ-9 scores in patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria, while female sex contributes a modest but significant effect.Keywords: Chronic spontaneous urticaria, Depression, Dermatology Life Quality Index

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent urticaria and/or angioedema lasting more than six weeks without identifiable external triggers [1]. Its point prevalence is 0.5–1%, and it most commonly affects adults aged 20–40 years [2]. CSU causes intense itching, wheals, and swelling, leading to sleep disturbances and reduced daily functioning. Psychiatric comorbidities, particularly anxiety and depression, are common and often parallel disease activity [2,5,6].

Unlike acute urticaria, which resolves with removal of triggers, the etiology of CSU is unknown in approximately 70% of cases, making its management difficult.

The disease persists for over one year in 70% and over five years in 14% of patients [3,4], significantly impairing quality of life.

Persistent itching, cosmetic concerns, and disrupted sleep negatively affect both physical and psychological well-being, while psychological distress may further worsen disease perception and treatment response.

Stress-induced mast cell activation and neuropeptide release through the neuroendocrine-immune axis can aggravate symptoms, reinforcing the bidirectional link between CSU severity and psychological health [7].

Most previous studies investigating the association between CSU, depression, and quality of life have relied on simple correlation or intergroup comparisons. While these methods reveal relationships, they often fail to control for confounding variables that may influence outcomes.

Objectives

This study aimed to compare depression severity (Patient Health Questionnaire-9, PHQ-9) and dermatology-related quality of life (Dermatology Life Quality Index, DLQI) among groups categorized by disease severity (Urticaria Activity Score over 7 days, UAS7) and categorical variables (sex, presence of angioedema, allergic comorbidities), while controlling for continuous covariates such as age and disease duration.

A deeper understanding of these relationships may support more comprehensive and individualized management strategies for CSU, addressing both physical and psychological aspects of patient care.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This multicenter cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines between 19 August and 21 October 2025 at the Immunology and Allergy Diseases Clinic of Isparta City Hospital and the Dermatology and Venereal Diseases Clinic of Sehit Yunus Emre State Hospital, Turkey.

Participants

Sample size calculations using G*Power 3.1.9.7 indicated that to detect a small effect (Cohen’s f=0.10) at 80% power and α=0.05, at least 159 participants were required for three-group comparisons (healthy controls, mild-moderate CSU, severe CSU) with one covariate (age) and three fixed factors (group, sex, allergic comorbidities). At least 128 participants were required for two-group CSU comparisons with two covariates (age, disease duration) and four fixed factors (group, sex, angioedema, allergic comorbidities).

A total of 400 individuals were screened, and 225 met inclusion criteria: 75 with mild-moderate CSU, 75 with severe CSU, and 75 healthy controls. Participants were consecutively recruited from clinic attendees.

Inclusion criteria for CSU patients were age 18–65 years, confirmed CSU diagnosis (>6 weeks), discontinuation of antihistamines for seven days before testing, no use of omalizumab or immunosuppressive drug, and provision of informed consent.

Healthy controls were aged 18–65 years with normal C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate results.

Exclusion criteria for all groups included chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases, chronic infections, malignancy, other chronic dermatological disorders, major psychiatric conditions, or inability to complete questionnaires.

Variables and Measurements

Participants were classified as having mild-moderate CSU, severe CSU, or being healthy controls. Disease severity was assessed using UAS7, where scores <28 indicated mild-moderate and ≥28 indicated severe CSU [1,14].

The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI, 10 items) assessed dermatology-related quality of life, and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9, 9 items) evaluated depressive symptoms [8,9]. DLQI and PHQ-9 were administered to CSU patients, while only PHQ-9 was applied to controls.

A strong correlation between DLQI and PHQ-9 scores (r=0.959) indicated multicollinearity; therefore, they were analyzed separately using Rank ANCOVA [11]. Age was included as a covariate in all models, and both age and disease duration were included for CSU-only analyses. Sex, presence of angioedema, and allergic comorbidities (asthma, allergic rhinitis, or atopic dermatitis) were included as fixed factors.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with group differences analyzed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were tested for normality using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since PHQ-9, DLQI, age, and disease duration were non-normally distributed, they were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise analyses (α=0.0167). Although age showed approximate normality, heterogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) justified the use of Welch’s ANOVA.

Due to multicollinearity and non-normality, separate Rank ANCOVA models were performed for the PHQ-9 and DLQI scores. These models assessed the effects of disease severity, sex, angioedema, and allergic comorbidities, adjusting for age and disease duration.

Additional multiple linear regression analyses were performed using rank-transformed scores to assess the effect of each independent variable on the dependent variable. A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bias Reduction and Ethical Considerations

To minimize selection and measurement bias, participants were consecutively recruited, and standardized procedures were used for questionnaire administration. Covariates (age and disease duration) were statistically controlled in all analyses.

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Suleyman Demirel University Faculty of Medicine on 18 August 2025 (Approval No: 2025/34). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and data confidentiality was ensured. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study groups are summarized in Table 1. Median age was similar among healthy controls, mild-moderate CSU, and severe CSU groups (38 [31–43], 40 [30–45], and 36 [26–48] years, respectively; P=0.701), as was the proportion of female participants (43 [57.3%], 38 [50.7%], and 38 [50.7%]; P=0.640). Disease duration was reported only for patient groups, with medians of 14 [10–22] months in the mild-moderate group and 14 [9–20] months in the severe group (P=0.848). Angioedema was observed in seven (9.3%) patients with mild-moderate CSU and 15 (20%) with severe CSU, showing a trend toward higher prevalence in the severe group (P=0.065). Allergic comorbidities were present in 16 (21.3%) healthy controls, 15 (20%) mild-moderate CSU patients, and 15 (20%) severe CSU patients, with no significant difference (P=0.973). Overall, there were no significant difference in age, sex distribution, disease duration, or comorbidity status among the groups.

Discussion

In our study, the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants were well balanced across the groups. Median age was comparable among healthy controls, mild-moderate CSU, and severe CSU patients, indicating that age was unlikely to confound the observed differences in disease-related outcomes (38 [31–43], 40 [30–45], and 36 [26–48] years, respectively; P=0.701) [1]. Similarly, the proportion of female participants did not differ significantly among the groups (57.3%, 50.7%, and 50.7%; P=0.640), suggesting uniform sex distribution. Disease duration was also comparable between mild-moderate and severe CSU (median 14 [10–22] vs 14 [9–20] months; P=0.848), indicating that differences in disease chronicity were unlikely to explain variations in patient-reported outcomes. Although angioedema appeared more frequently in severe CSU (20%) than in mild-moderate CSU (9.3%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.065) [14]. Allergic comorbidities were similarly distributed (21.3%, 20%, and 20%; P=0.973), suggesting minimal impact on primary outcomes.

Depression levels and dermatology-related quality of life were proportional to disease severity. Median PHQ-9 scores were 3 [2–4] in healthy controls, 5 [4–6] in mild-moderate CSU, and 10 [8–12] in severe CSU, with statistically significant differences among groups (P<0.001). Post-hoc comparisons using Mann-Whitney U with Bonferroni correction revealed progressive increases in PHQ-9 scores with higher disease severity (healthy controls vs mild–moderate CSU, P<0.001; healthy controls vs severe CSU, P<0.001; mild-moderate vs severe CSU, P<0.001) [15]. Similarly, DLQI scores worsened with disease severity (5 [3–6] vs 14 [11–16]; P<0.001) [12, 22].

These findings are consistent with previous reports by Tawil et al. (2023) and Berberi et al. (2025), who highlighted the link between higher depressive symptoms and poorer dermatology-related quality of life in CSU [15,22].

Spearman correlation indicated a very strong positive relationship between DLQI and PHQ-9 scores (r=0.959), highlighting potential multicollinearity. To control for inflated type I error, DLQI and PHQ-9 were analyzed in separate Rank ANCOVA models.

As presented Table 4, for rank-transformed DLQI scores, significant main effects were found for disease severity (P<0.001) and sex (P<0.001). Severe CSU patients had higher DLQI ranks than mild-moderate patients, while females scored higher than males [12,18,19].

Disease duration positively influenced DLQI (B=2.175 per month), whereas age, angioedema, and allergic comorbidities did not have significant effects [16,17,20].

These results align with prior studies by Ye et al. (2020), Zachariae et al. (2000), and Soni et al. (2025) demonstrating that higher disease severity and longer disease duration adversely affect quality of life [16,17,18,22].

As presented in Table 5, for rank-transformed PHQ-9 scores, disease severity (P<0.001) and sex (P<0.001) were significant, indicating higher depressive symptoms in severe CSU and among females [10,12,13,15,23].

Angioedema was associated with a modest increase in rank-transformed PHQ-9 scores (P=0.042) Conversely, allergic comorbidity was linked to a small decrease in rank-transformed PHQ-9 scores (P=0.001).

Disease duration remained strongly associated with rank-transformed PHQ-9 scores (P<0.001) [21].

This suggests a robust positive relationship between longer disease duration and higher levels of depressive symptoms in CSU patients. Similar to our study, in the 2020 study by Karaman et al., a positive correlation was observed between disease duration and depression and anxiety scores in children with chronic spontaneous urticaria, indicating that longer disease duration was associated with higher levels of psychological symptoms [21]. These findings align with previous research showing that CSU patients, especially females, exhibit higher levels of depression, and that disease activity and longer duration are closely linked to increased psychological burden, as reported by B. Memet et al. (2021), Tawil et al. (2023), and Gowda & Athani (2019) [10,15,23].

Age had no significant effect (P=0.609) [15] and higher- order interactions were nonsignificant (P>0.05).

Study Limitations

Because this study used data collected at a single time point, causal relationships cannot be confirmed. It is unclear whether the observed worsening of depression or quality of life is due to disease severity or other confounding factors. Since tools like the DLQI and PHQ-9 are self-reported, they may involve subjectivity and be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Moreover, previous studies used different questionnaires and methods, making direct comparison with our findings difficult.

Conclusions

In summary, this multicenter study demonstrated that disease severity and duration are the primary determinants of impaired dermatology-related quality of life and elevated PHQ-9 rank scores in patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria, while female sex contributes a modest but significant effect. Additionally, the presence of angioedema and allergic comorbidities did not significantly affect rank-transformed DLQI scores respectively (P=0.266, P=0.172). But the presence of angioedema was associated with increased depressive symptoms (P=0.042), whereas allergic comorbidities had a minor protective effect (P=0.001). These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive assessment and management of both physical and psychological aspects in CSU patients.
