In situ measurement of damage evolution in shocked magnesium as a function of microstructure
Authors: Brianna MacNider, David Jones, Jesse Callanan, Matt Beason, George T. Gray, Dana M. Dattelbaum, Nicholas Boechler, Saryu Fensin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Accurate modeling and prediction of damage induced by dynamic loading in materials have long proved to be a difficult task. Examination of postmortem recovered samples cannot capture the time-dependent evolution of void nucleation and growth, and attempts at analytical models are hindered by the necessity to make simplifying assumptions, because of the lack of high-resolution, in situ, time-resolved experimental data. We use absorption contrast imaging to directly image the time evolution of spall damage in metals at ∼1.6-μm spatial resolution. We observe a dependence of void distribution and size on time and microstructure. The insights gained from these data can be used to validate and improve dynamic damage prediction models, which have the potential to lead to the design of superior damage-resistant materials.

INTRODUCTION

Predicting nucleation and growth of void damage in ductile metals under dynamic loading remains a challenging problem to which much effort has been devoted (1–6). Under shock and subsequent release loading, it is well known that the interaction of the resulting rarefaction waves can lead to the creation of a region of transient tensile stress, eventually inducing nucleation of voids at defects in the microstructure. This phenomenon, known as spall (7, 8), eventually leads to material failure as nucleated voids grow and coalesce. It is therefore of interest to investigate void formation and evolution within the context of the development of more robust damage-resistant materials (9).

To model and predict the overall dynamic failure process, it is important to have a quantitative understanding of the contributions and evolution of the underlying mechanisms. Extensive experiments and simulations have been performed to investigate the effects of relevant parameters on spall strength. It has been found that loading conditions (10–12), crystal orientation (13–16), structure (17, 18), grain boundaries (19–23), defects (24, 25), and anisotropy (26) have an effect on material behavior and void damage evolution under spall loading. Many analytical void damage models have been developed that attempt to predict spall strength and void evolution over time (5, 27). Models have incorporated effects such as void nucleation and growth (28), rate dependency (29, 30), and void coalescence (31), to name a few, with varied degrees of success in predicting experimental data.

We suggest that insufficient understanding of void nucleation and growth is a critical barrier to accurately predicting void-driven damage and, thereafter, ultimate failure in ductile materials. While molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been used to capture many features of spall experiments at high strain rates [e.g., (32)], they have shown substantial discrepancies at lower strain rates because of limits on system size and computation time (33, 34). This is particularly relevant to the gas gun impact experiments shown herein (with strain rates ≲105 s−1) and is important because it has been shown that strain rate has a notable effect on total damage in materials (35). Deficiencies in current analytical strain-equivalent damage models (5, 27–29, 31) for nucleation and growth include assumptions and simplifications of the initial positioning of void nucleation sites, void growth rates, and void shapes. This lack of understanding is, in part, related to the absence of direct, real-time quantitative measurements of void nucleation and early-stage growth. Such in situ observations had, until recently (36–39), remained a grand experimental challenge, largely because of the extremely fine spatial (in μm), as well as simultaneously narrow (in ps) and extended (in μs) temporal, scales involved in shock-induced spall experiments. In recent years, studies using postmortem measurements (8, 10–12, 23) have well established that under dynamic loading conditions, microstructural features such as grain boundaries, inclusions, vacancies, and heterogeneities can preferentially act as void nucleation sites. Although there has been some progress in predicting void nucleation at various microstructural features (13–15), void growth and evolution modeling remain a challenge. Single postmortem images lack insight into time-dependent processes, and no real-world, time-resolved data exist regarding void growth in ductile metals.

In this work, we present in situ, time-resolved, high-resolution measurements of early-stage void growth in ductile metals experiencing spall under shock loading. These new data propose to fill a gap in our understanding of dynamic damage evolution and inform current void damage models, as well as lead to the development of new ones. We focus on high-fidelity (∼1.6-μm spatial resolution) void distribution data in magnesium, which was chosen as a model material because of interest in the effects of its high anisotropy. In addition, substantial interest has been shown in its behavior under spall conditions (6), and its low absorption length for a metal makes it especially suitable for experiments at the x-ray energies and flux available at the Dynamic Compression Sector (DCS). While radiography data have been used to investigate dynamic fracture and crack propagation previously (36, 38–40), these experiments have not been used to investigate spall-driven void evolution in metals. Still other attempts at investigating spallation in metals have used proton radiography (41), but crucially, this approach is limited in the achievable spatial resolution [maximum of 60 μm (42)], restricting this previous analysis to the scale of spall layers (∼0.6 to 1 mm) rather than nucleation and growth on the level of individual voids. Some work has been done in using x-ray diffraction to investigate time-resolved spallation in metals (43), but these data crucially lack direct measurements of void distribution, shape, and growth. Our measurements of spallation in magnesium as a function of varying microstructure demonstrate the dependence of damage evolution upon microstructure and loading direction, highlighting a divergence from the simplifying assumptions often made in predictive models. We furthermore show that spall strength alone is not a good indicator of how void damage will evolve, underscoring the importance of these types of high-resolution in situ measurements to obtain a complete picture of material behavior during spallation. These data provide critical insights into a regime that has, up until now, been characterized by approximations.

RESULTS

Flyer plate–driven incipient spall experiments were performed on three varieties of magnesium to systematically determine the effect of microstructure and chemistry on spall-induced void damage and failure. The microstructural effects were captured by investigating single-crystal and polycrystalline pure Mg (99.99%). The effects of sample chemistry were sampled by including polycrystalline AZ31B Mg. Figure 1A shows electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis of the polycrystalline and AZ31B samples. It was found that AZ31B had grain sizes ranging from approximately 10 to 40 μm, and the average grain size of the pure magnesium was approximately 100 μm. X-ray absorption contrast imaging allowed the capture of a series of eight frames showing the evolution of the spallation process through the thickness of each sample, achieving a frame rate and spatial resolution of 153 ns and 1.6 μm, respectively. We note that, while this spatial scale does not enable the discernment of submicrometer void nucleation sites, it allows the characterization of micrometer-scale voids. In general, voids nucleate, coalesce, and grow throughout the damage process. Because of our resolution limit, the voids that we measure at a given time are a subset of this greater population. As the damage process evolves in time, we expect that previously unmeasured voids add themselves to the measured population via coalescence and growth. Postprocessing and normalization of the x-ray images is discussed in Materials and Methods. A schematic of the experiment and diagnostics is shown in Fig. 1B.

Examination of the x-ray imaging data provides an in situ view of void evolution as a function of time, which provides insights into the evolution of void distribution and the effects of crystal orientation and chemistry on the evolution of void damage in metals. A complete set of frames from a single dynamic experiment on polycrystalline Mg impacted at 0.39 mm μs−1 is shown in Fig. 2 as a representative set of the type of data collected for all samples. The higher-intensity red and white regions in this figure represent areas of low density (free surface or voids). An evolution can clearly be seen not only of the width of the spall plane but also of its morphology. We note that it is possible for intensity distribution to be affected by changes in the tilt of the sample during impact; thus, a combination of image analysis of the rear free surface and previous photon Doppler velocimetry (PDV) velocity-time data from a similar system was used to estimate an expected tilt of 1 to 2 mrad (see the Supplementary Materials). This work therefore assumes that any changes in the intensity are associated solely with changes in void number and size.

In general, microstructure, as defined by chemistry and orientation, is known to substantially affect void nucleation and growth under shock loading (14, 15, 44), as well as spall strength and morphology of the damage plane (18). Figure 3 shows x-ray images of different Mg samples, impacted at the same velocity, at approximately the same time with respect to impact. We note that additional brightness adjustments have been performed on these images to more easily identify void characteristics. Images without brightness adjustment and with the same intensity range have been included in the Supplementary Materials, and a more thorough quantitative examination of accumulated void damage without brightness adjustments is examined later in our results. Through qualitative examination, we see that there is a marked difference in void shape and distribution between the various microstructures. Figure 3 (top) compares results as a function of orientation in single-crystal Mg and highlights variations in the total amount and morphology of the void distribution. In particular, it is of note that the x-ray images corresponding to the [0001] orientation show low porosity, implying a high resistance to spall damage (Fig. 3A). In addition, there are clearly differing shape and preferential growth directions of the void damage plane in the [112¯0] (Fig. 3B) and the [11¯00] orientations (Fig. 3C). Both the [112¯0] and [101¯0] orientations show long, thin extensions of void damage away from the spall plane, in line with observations in MD simulations in (45), which note the nucleation of voids at planar stacking fault interactions and the nonspherical nature of the resulting voids. In addition, MD investigations performed in (25) identify that void shape and growth are dominated by different mechanisms depending on the crystal orientation, noting the domination of {112¯2}{112¯3} pyramidal dislocations and twinning in the [0001] orientation, the domination of {11¯00}{112¯0} prismatic dislocations, and a lack of twinning, in the [101¯0] case, as well as the presence of nonspherical voids in both single-crystal orientations. While we note that previous MD work cannot be compared quantitatively with the experimental work herein because of inherent differences in model assumptions, nonetheless, it can provide valuable information concerning trends and insight into mechanisms.

Calculated spall strengths for the single crystals are shown in Fig. 4 as a function of orientation and impact velocity, and the values of the other parameters relevant to the spall calculations for these experiments are included in the Supplementary Materials. Several experiments were repeated with similar conditions to test for repeatability (see the Supplementary Materials for more information). Notably, the [0001] orientation is shown to have, by far, the highest spall strength, consistent with the qualitative resistance to void formation and growth noted in the images in Fig. 3.

The qualitative observations noted in Fig. 3 were quantified in each case through the use of intensity averaging techniques, described in more detail in Materials and Methods. A compilation of vertically averaged intensity plots for the different microstructures at a similar impact velocity are shown in Fig. 5, which examines intensity values that have been normalized relative to one another. Although we note that there may be lower scale damage that is not captured by the resolution of the technique and that the through-thickness void damage has been compressed into a two-dimensional representation, the examination of void-induced intensity (corresponding to x-ray transmission) presented in this work can still provide valuable insights into void distribution evolution and growth rates. This figure highlights differences in the width, shape, and growth rate of the relative intensity curves. In general, it can be seen that the total porosity increases with time and that the void distribution begins to suggest a change from Gaussian to bimodal in some microstructures as a function of time, with the polycrystalline and AZ31B experiments showing a broadening of the damage peak and the emergence of bimodal damage distribution at intermediate times. In addition, as sample microstructures become complex because of the presence of grains of multiple orientations and varying chemistry, an increase in void damage as compared to the single crystals is observed. This is highlighted by an increase in peaks and widths of intensity in Fig. 5 and markedly different morphology of the spall plane in Fig. 3 (D and E). This observation is expected, as the existence of a greater number of potential nucleation sites in the polycrystalline samples is expected to decrease spall strength and produce a greater number of voids in comparison to single crystals. In addition, we note differences in the width and intensity of the spall plane among the polycrystalline (AZ31B and pure Mg) samples. In general, chemistry of the material, texture, grain size, and other microstructural features can affect spall. Because of the higher impurity content of AZ31B potentially providing more nucleation sites, it might be expected for pure Mg to have higher resistance to void nucleation. However, here, pure Mg is observed to have greater void damage. We hypothesize that the grain size is the dominant factor driving this, as the material characterization showed the polycrystalline Mg to have larger grains than AZ31B, and previous works have shown that materials with larger grain size tend to promote easier and higher amounts of void growth in comparison to materials with a smaller grain size (48). This is qualitatively consistent with the data from the x-ray images. However, it is important to note that the varying chemistry between AZ31B and polycrystalline Mg may also be contributing by altering the deformation mechanisms during shock loading, affecting the number of void nucleation sites available.

To quantify the variations in the spall behavior, the intensity measurements shown in Fig. 5 were integrated across the width of the spall plane, and this area was taken as the approximate accumulated void damage through the depth of the sample, at that time. Note that this approach incorporates low-intensity regions through the thickness of the sample and is not an absolute measure of damage, but it does provide valuable insights when comparing relative void damage between experiments run with different samples and impact speeds. In Fig. 6A, the areas were compared as a function of time from impact for different samples under the same loading conditions (an impactor velocity of 0.4 mm μs−1). At this lower impact velocity, when accounting for both width of the spall plane and intensity, polycrystalline magnesium was shown to accumulate the highest amount of void damage. This is followed by AZ31B, single-crystal [11¯00] and [112¯0], and lastly the [0001] samples. These results are consistent with the previous understanding regarding the role microstructure plays in determining void nucleation and growth, as well as our observations in Fig. 5. When examining the spall strength of single-crystal Mg, we do note a slight discrepancy in comparison to other results in literature, namely, that the [0001] orientation is found by Kanel et al. (49) to have the lowest spall strength among the crystal orientations. Meanwhile, the high spall strength and drastically lowered void damage profile in the [0001] orientation is very clear in our results. As spall strength can vary widely depending on loading conditions, we hypothesize that the variation between our works in sample thicknesses, loading stresses, and peak strain rates during experiments makes a direct comparison of single-crystal trends between the two works invalid. In addition, at the comparatively low impact velocities used in this study (0.4 mm μs−1 to 0.6 mm μs−1), there is some agreement with our observed spall strength trends in MD work (25).

(A) Integrated intensity for an impact speed of 0.4, highlighting the substantial differences in accumulated damage between various microstructures at lower impact speeds. (B) Integrated intensity at higher impact speeds, including 0.6 (single-crystal samples) and 0.8 (polycrystalline and AZ31B samples), highlighting the reduced (but still present) importance of microstructure as impact speed is increased.

As the impact velocity was increased, a shift in some of the porosity trends is observed, as seen in Fig. 6B. For instance, the high void damage resistance in the [0001] orientation reduces at the higher impact velocity of 0.6 mm μs−1, bringing the damage amount in line with that in other orientations, although the spall strength remains high, as per Fig. 4. This suggests that, while spall strength and void damage evolution are related, spall strength alone is not a complete descriptor for void formation and eventual failure in materials. Another key observation is the altered void accumulation in AZ31B in comparison to pure Mg. For the 0.4 mm μs−1 experiments, AZ31B showed substantially lower porosity than polycrystalline Mg, but at impact velocities of 0.8 mm μs−1, their performance is comparable. Both trends are consistent with the understanding that as the impact velocity is increased above a critical value, microstructure plays less of a role in controlling damage and failure (48). We hypothesize that the impact speeds of 0.6 and 0.8 mm μs−1 are in the region of this impact velocity, resulting in the reduced differences noted between disparate samples, and that at even higher impact velocities than those tested here, even less of a disparity would exist between the void damage curves of the single-crystal and polycrystal Mg.

The rate of damage accumulation is an important parameter needed to calibrate void growth models and can now be estimated through examining the time-resolved data presented in this work. The slopes of the curves in Fig. 6 were examined, allowing for an estimation of the rate at which this damage accumulated in the sample (see the Supplementary Materials for more details) as a function of microstructure (orientation and chemistry). In general, these accumulation rates were in line with expectations based on the spall strength values and the areas under the damage curves presented in Fig. 5. At lower impact velocities, pure Mg displayed the highest damage accumulation rate, while the single-crystal [0001] displayed the lowest damage accumulation rate. At higher impact velocities, the two AZ31B rates fell on either side of the pure Mg rate, and the single-crystal rates are similar, as expected from examining Fig. 6. However, we note two interesting features. First, while the lower-velocity AZ31B case seems, at first glance, to have greater damage than the single-crystal cases, the rate of damage accumulation is in line with the [112¯0] and [11¯00] orientations. This suggests that, while AZ31B experiences onset of spall damage very quickly, likely because of a high number of potential nucleation sites, the rate of void growth from that point onward is not notably greater than the [112¯0] and [11¯00] orientations. Second, although these damage accumulation rates were fit with linear slopes, it is apparent from Fig. 6A that the rate of damage accumulation is not always linear. The [0001], [112¯0], and pure Mg cases, in particular, are poorly approximated by a single, linear damage growth rate. There are clear periods of accelerated and reduced growth, suggesting that the rate at which the damage plane grows fluctuates as a function of time and microstructure.

In addition to this examination of damage plane growth rates, the high spatial resolution in this study (1.6 μm) allows the identification and calculation of individual void growth rates from the x-ray images. However, we also note the limitations of this measurement due to void layering in the through-thickness direction, uncertainty in void shape in the through-thickness direction, and the possibility of voids merging in the through-thickness direction before this process can be visually identified in the visible (two-dimensional) plane. Given these limitations, an example of void growth rate calculation is provided in Fig. 7, where a single void from Fig. 2 is identified and tracked in each image before it coalesces with neighboring voids (see the Supplementary Materials for further details). This results in an estimated void growth rate of roughly 7000 μm2/μs. This void tracking process was repeated for six different voids at different locations across the sample (see the Supplementary Materials). The average void growth rate was found to be roughly 13,000 μm2/μs, with an SD of about 5000 μm2/μs. We note that the growth rates fluctuate, both between voids and (in some cases) across time for each void. This may partially be the result of uncertainties in the measurement, as noted above. However, it may also be due, in part, to the void growth rate not being constant across the damage plane or throughout time. This is an important observation to make, as many currently used damage models implement a single void growth rate function for all voids across a sample (28, 29, 31). While these calculations do require assumptions of negligible tilt and void layering, as discussed above, this estimate is the first time such a rate has been experimentally quantified. Using the data extracted from the in situ images, one could implement either a single averaged void growth rate or a distribution of void growth rates (across space and/or time) extracted from multiple void measurements.

DISCUSSION

In this work, in situ absorption contrast imaging was used in plate-impact spall experiments to study time-resolved, high-fidelity void and damage evolution in Mg with varying microstructures. These experiments captured the time evolution of void shape and morphology in a spall plane in metals at micrometer scale spatial resolution. Analysis of the data from these experiments qualitatively and quantitatively showed the following: (i) At low impact speeds, spall strength increased, and the total void damage decreased in the order of polycrystalline pure Mg, AZ31B, [11¯00] and [112¯0], and [0001] magnesium. (ii) Void distribution, shape, and growth direction varied widely from one microstructure to another. (iii) Spall strength alone was not an adequate indicator of accumulated voids. (iv) The void damage profile began to shift from Gaussian to bimodal as a function of time in the polycrystalline microstructures.

These data can be used to evaluate and improve the accuracy of current analytical void-based damage models, which generally require the parameterization of several terms associated with specific materials and loading conditions to adequately represent and predict damage. These terms have previously been informed by velocity-time data obtained from the free surface of the material, porosity analysis from recovered samples, or few-frame or low-resolution time-resolved x-ray imaging, which may fail to capture the relevant physics because of not being high resolution or time resolved. With the work performed here, it is now possible to refine these models through direct experimental measurement of parameters such as early-stage distribution, growth rates, and shape evolution of both individual voids and accumulated damage. We suggest that the intended use for the technique shown herein should be in conjunction with the previously mentioned traditional techniques, including both preexperiment and postmortem characterization (e.g., high-resolution static x-ray tomography in both cases and electron microscopy in the latter).

We highlight several unique contributions that this technique can make. Our results make it clear that damage growth is not always linear in nature, meaning that growth rates can fluctuate in a way that requires time-resolved data to capture properly. This is true of not only growth rates but also void distribution, which was shown to shift throughout the process of damage evolution. More traditional techniques, such as postmortem characterization, do not capture these time-dependent details. Nor do current damage models, which use first-order approximations for growth rate and Gaussian approximations for void distribution. While postmortem characterization has proven useful for estimating void nucleation sites (50–52), we suggest that the time-resolved technique shown here can add confidence to these inferences, particularly when paired with damage models. We also suggest that there is value in such time-resolved measurements, particularly in very high-energy and destructive contexts where recovery is not possible. Furthermore, we expect that for materials with more complex microstructures than those examined herein, the nonlinear, time-varying effects that we see will worsen, resulting in increased modeling difficulty, which could be mitigated with time-resolved measurements.

It is similarly important to note that while these data provide information about quantitative evolution of damage in the samples, it does not offer direct insights regarding the exact deformation mechanisms that lead to the varying behaviors. Coupled with improved computational models and current analysis techniques (e.g., velocimetry and postmortem characterization), we suggest that these data will lead to new mechanistic insights to spall failure and void nucleation and growth processes. In addition, we expect that as the beam energy at synchrotrons is increased, other metals of interest will become accessible for this type of examination.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Single-crystal magnesium

Single-crystal magnesium samples were obtained from Princeton Scientific, in the form of discs nominally 10-mm diameter by 2 mm thick. Three orientations were chosen—[0001], [11¯00], and [112¯0]—with these directions aligned with the through-thickness direction of the sample to within one degree. Purity was specified as at least 99.99% for all samples.

Polycrystal pure magnesium and AZ31B

Polycrystalline magnesium and AZ31B samples were machined from plate stock such that the shock direction was along the plate through-thickness direction, in an 8-mm-diameter by 1.6-mm-thick disc format. The pure magnesium plate was hot rolled, and the AZ31B plate was a commercially pure plate in the stress-relieved H24 condition. The Supplementary Materials includes information on the chemistry of each material.

Light optical microscopy and EBSD were performed for both the pure Mg and AZ31B materials to produce a detailed microstructural analysis. Samples for this analysis were prepared using standard metallographic techniques. They were mechanically ground from 240 to 800 grit and then polished using 0.3-μm colloidal alumina, followed by electropolishing. This polishing of the EBSD samples was done with a 60% ethanol and 40% phosphoric acid mixture at room temperature using 3 V for approximately 10 to 15 s. Samples were then rinsed with methanol, followed by immersion in a 10% nitric acid and water mixture. Last, the sample surface was thoroughly cleaned with methanol. The EBSD data were acquired using a Philips XL30 field emission gun scanning electron microscope and analyzed using software by EDAX.

Plate impact experiments

Shock compression experiments were performed on multiple samples at the DCS at the Advanced Photon Source (Argonne National Laboratory, IL). Further details of the experiments are included in the Supplementary Materials. Briefly, a gas gun was used to launch a projectile with a flyer plate into each magnesium sample, driving compression, which was then followed by spall fracture. The polycrystalline samples were impacted by a magnesium flyer plate, half the sample thickness. The single-crystal samples were impacted by a 6061 aluminum flyer plate (thickness, 1.6 mm). Both types of flyer plate were backed with Lexan to provide support for the flyer plate during launch while allowing an almost full release. We note that the presence of the polycarbonate backing (see Fig. 1B) reduces the tension generated in the spall plane, and thus, a full release is not achieved. However, experiment parameters were chosen to ensure that spall would still be generated. A PDV probe was placed behind the sample to measure the rear free-surface velocity. X-ray pulses were passed through the sample (normal to the loading direction) every 153 ns and captured by a detector bank consisting of a scintillator, intensifier, and multiple cameras. This allowed a total of eight x-rays to be recorded, with a field of view of approximately 1.7 mm by 1.7 mm and a spatial resolution of roughly 1.6 μm. The sample was placed in the beam to image the area where spall was expected to occur. For more details, we refer to works that have used a similar experimental setup at this facility (37, 40, 53). The raw x-ray images were processed in ImageJ and MATLAB. Image correction was first performed, consisting of normalization using accompanying white and dark-field images for each shot, followed by an alignment correction of each camera relative to camera 1 using a reference grid, and lastly a straightening correction with respect to the window edge to orient the target relative to the gun barrel, and thus, the spall plane is vertical. Each set of images associated with a single experiment therefore has a consistent orientation.

Analysis

Once corrections were applied, analysis to quantify void distribution and porosity as a function of time was performed in MATLAB. Each 1024 × 1024 pixel image was sliced into a number of vertical “bins.” The intensity data were averaged vertically over each bin, resulting in a 1 × number of bins–sized vector representing the averaged intensity values across the horizontal width of the sample. The chosen bin size was varied to examine the effect on the resulting data and was found to coarsen the data and remove details as the bin size was increased. However, the overall trends in the data did not change with the bin size. Using this study, a bin size of two pixels was chosen to retain a high level of detail. The averaged intensity curves were plotted together for each experiment (see Fig. 5). To account for frame-to-frame variations in intensity, the intensity curves were normalized with respect to the flattest (least changing) portion of the curve, outside of the spall damage region where we assume the x-ray path length through the sample was constant; hence, this portion undergoes little density variation during the course of the experiment. All times are given with respect to impact, which is defined as time zero.

In the analysis of void distribution via intensity averaging, we assumed a negligible effect on intensity measurements from tilt. The images captured are a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional space, so it might be expected for there to be some contribution from void layering in the through-thickness direction on the identifiable edges of the visible voids. We expect that this will introduce some additional uncertainty and fluctuation in rate calculations when tracking individual voids and acknowledge the limitations inherent in the measurements. However, this technique provides the first direct experimental measurement of time-resolved evolution of voids, which can be used to directly estimate parameters such as distribution and growth rate despite such through-thickness limitations.
