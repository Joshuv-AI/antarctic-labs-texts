Nano-kinetic implant surface mediates osteo-antibacterial synergy through electromagnetic-mechanical coupling
Authors: Yangmengfan Chen, Hao Du, Heng Sun, Xingcan Huang, Duan Wang, Xuming Chen, Jiehao Chen, Xuanjun Zhang, Liqiang Zhou, Zongke Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a devastating complication of total joint arthroplasty, mainly caused by bacterial biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance. Traditional static surfaces rely on contact killing-based bactericidal properties and cannot reliably clear mature biofilms. In this study, we fabricate a remote-activated dynamic biomimetic system, namely a movable magnetic topographic surface (MMTS). Here we show that under the exposure of external electromagnetic field (EMF), the anchored Janus nano-heterodimers oscillate periodically to mechanically disrupt bacterial membranes and eradicates biofilms of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. This mechanical force can also activates Piezo1 mechanosensitive ion channel in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), inducing calcium oscillations, upregulating gene expression of Runx2 and BMP2, and enhancing extracellular matrix mineralization. In vivo, MMTS combined with EMF exposure eliminates local infection and promoted osseointegration. This remote-activated dynamic biomimetic strategy offers a promising multifunctional platform for PJI treatment, and improved implant osteointegration in clinical orthopedics.

Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is a well-established intervention for end-stage joint disease, as it effectively alleviates pain and restores physical function in patients with osteoarthritis. It has been reported that approximately 800,000 TJAs are performed annually in the UK and the United States, and this number is projected to surpass 4 million by 20301. Nevertheless, the growing volume of TJA surgery has led to a rising rate of postoperative complications, with periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) being the most prominent concern. PJI occurs in approximately 1% of total hip arthroplasties (THA) and 1–2% of total knee arthroplasties (TKA), imposing substantial clinical and economic burdens2. Patients with PJI suffer from higher morbidity and mortality rate1, while revision surgeries for PJI also place a heavy financial burden on healthcare systems. It was reported that treating a single PJI case costs an average of €95,000, which is five times the cost of a primary arthroplasty, owing to repeated surgical interventions, prolonged rehabilitation, and long-term administration of antibiotics and analgesics3. The increase in revision surgery poses a major threat to global healthcare systems, highlighting an urgent need to develop more effective strategies for PJI prevention and treatment.

Cefazolin (CEZ) is a guideline-recommended antibiotic against methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus4. Owing to its favorable safety, it has been widely applied in clinical practice5. For TJA, CEZ is the standard of care for preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis6. However, bacterial biofilm formation on implant surfaces constitutes a major obstacle in treating PJI. Biofilms are organized bacterial communities embedded within a protective extracellular polymeric matrix. This matrix functions as a barrier, protecting bacteria from antimicrobial agents including CEZ and host immune defenses, thereby leading to chronic infections7. Another challenge is the growing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens. For example, in the United States, up to 46.7% of S. aureus strains are methicillin-resistant (MRSA), and up to 23% of Enterococci spp. are vancomycin-resistant1. It is projected that drug-resistant pathogens will cause 10 million deaths worldwide each year by 20508; there is an urgent need for innovative antimicrobial strategies, particularly non-antibiotic therapeutics9. Current biomaterial-based strategies often rely on antibiotic coatings or metallic antibacterial agents, which face limitations such as potential cytotoxicity, burst release profiles, and the risk of inducing further bacterial resistance10. For instance, antibacterial coatings on titanium (Ti) implants often suffer from poor stability, rapid decomposition11, and might lead to implant failure over time12. Collectively, these limitations have driven extensive research into non-antibiotic and physical antimicrobial modalities13.

In this context, biomimetic design has emerged as a promising research direction14. In particular, bionic approaches that mimic natural antibacterial surface topographies or inherent physiological defense systems offer viable strategies for antimicrobial therapy without leading to bacterial resistance15. A conventional example is the micro-nanostructure of insect wings, which exhibits contact-killing effects by physically rupturing bacterial cells without inducing resistance16. However, our previous studies revealed that many static biomimetic designs only focus on structural imitation, ignoring adaptability to the dynamic physiological environment, leading to unsatisfactory bactericidal performance17–19. To overcome this limitation, dynamic bionic strategies that simulate responsive biological behaviors have been developed20. A recent breakthrough in this field is the fabrication of magnetic urchin-like capsule robots. Driven by an external magnetic field, these robots form microswarms that mechanically disrupt bacterial biofilms, showing great potential for the treatment of implant-associated infections21.

The utilization of precisely regulated physical stimuli represents a particularly promising direction of research. Mechanical forces are essential regulators of bone remodeling22, and they can also directly impair bacterial viability and disrupt biofilm23. An ideal therapeutic strategy for PJI should not only eradicate infection without inducing bacterial resistance, but also restore the local physiological environment to facilitate tissue repair and functional recovery24. In this study, we developed a dynamic bionic system. By integrating magnetic components, the system mimics both the nanostructure of insect wings and their flapping motions upon the application of an electromagnetic field (EMF) (Fig. 1). This platform is composed of Fe3O4-Ag Janus nano-heterodimers (JNHDs) tethered to a nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA)-modified titanium (Ti) substrate through a flexible linker, forming a movable magnetic topographic surface (MMTS). Upon exposure to an EMF with designated frequencies and intensities, the JNHDs generate periodic mechanical vibration. This design serves two major purposes: (1) mimicking insect wing flapping kinematics (30–50 Hz)25, to achieve contact-killing through remotely modulated mechanical vibration; (2) mimicking exercise-induced mechanical stimulation (10–128 Hz)26 to accelerate bone regeneration and osseointegration. In this study, a non-antibiotic strategy that synergistically combines remote-activated mechano-bactericidal activity with mechano-stimulated bone regeneration (Fig. 1) was compared with the first-line clinical antibiotic CEZ in the orthopedic department27. This advanced electromagnetic-mechanical coupling offers a promising therapeutic approach to eliminate infection and restore function.

Results

Preliminary exploratory analysis of S. aureus and bone harvested from human hip prosthesis suggest the key role of mechanical stimulation

To provide preliminary exploratory insight into bacterial and host adaptations in the periprosthetic microenvironment, we first conducted transcriptomic and metabolomic sequencing of S. aureus isolated from the femoral component of patients undergoing revision total hip arthroplasty for PJI (Fig. 2A–E). The diagnosis of PJI was confirmed by the radiographic identification of bone destruction, focal osteosclerotic lesions, and inflammation (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Analysis of Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) revealed differences in energy production, cell wall/membrane biogenesis, and structural organization between patient-derived S. aureus and laboratory reference strains (Fig. 2F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis also indicated alterations in drug resistance, membrane transport, and metabolism of essential nutrients (Fig. 2G). To further evaluate the alterations in metabolism of the periprosthetic S. aureus derived from patients, metabolomic sequencing was conducted. A Venn plot revealed 1022 metabolites that were common to both groups, while a considerable number of metabolites were uniquely identified in each group (Fig. 2H). A volcano plot confirmed significant differences in the abundance of metabolites between the two groups (Fig. 2I). Subsequent compound classification showed that these metabolic differences primarily involved monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, phospholipids, and fatty acids, which are key components associated with the bacterial wall and membrane biosynthesis (Fig. 2J). The pathway classification also highlighted changes in amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and ABC transporters. Taken together, our multi-omics data (Supplementary Fig. 4) suggested that patient-derived S. aureus undergoes remodeling of the cell wall and membrane synthesis pathways. Bone tissue was also collected from the cementless acetabular cup of the PJI patient (Fig. 2L–O). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2P) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 2Q) revealed new bone formation at the interface of bone and acetabular cup, despite the presence of focal fat deposition. However, no bacterial colonies were observed upon Gram staining, probably due to effective pre-surgical infection control (Fig. 2R). Furthermore, immunofluorescent staining for BMP2 (Fig. 2S) and COL1A1 (Fig. 2T) demonstrated active bone formation at the implant–bone interface. Notably, Piezo1 was also widely distributed along this interface, suggesting its potential involvement in the process of osteointegration (Fig. 2U). Taking the obtained findings together, the transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed alterations in the synthesis and metabolism of the membrane and cell wall of S. aureus around the implant. Together with the observed bone formation and localization of Piezo1, these preliminary exploratory findings underscore the critical role of mechanical stimulation in modulating host-pathogen interactions and suggest its potential therapeutic application in the management of PJI.

Characterization and principle of the dynamic biomimetic MMTS

Inspired by biomimetic principles, we examined the micro/nanostructural features of the wing of Pomponia linearis (Fig. 3A) as well as those of other winged insects (Supplementary Fig. 5). To emulate the surface topography of the P. linearis wing, we first synthesized Fe3O4-Ag JNHDs, as shown by transmission electron microscopy imaging (Fig. 3B, C). Elemental mapping confirmed the spatial distribution of Fe, Ag, and O within these JNHDs (Fig. 3D–G), while energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) revealed characteristic peaks consistent with their expected composition (Fig. 3H). Particle size analysis indicated average diameters of 18.54 nm for Fe3O4 nanoparticles (NPs) and 5.18 nm for Ag NPs (Fig. 3I). The paramagnetic behavior of (a) Ag NPs, (b) Fe3O4 NPs, and (c) JNHDs was assessed under a static magnetic field (SMF). To endow titanium (Ti) orthopedic implants with dynamic biomimetic properties, JNHDs were either directly deposited onto nHA-Ti surfaces to create a static magnetic topographic surface (UMTS), or elastically tethered via a flexible linker (MAL-PEG-NHS) to form a MMTS. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images illustrated the nanostructural features of the Ti substrate, UMTS, and MMTS (Fig. 3K). EDS spectra of the Ti substrate showed only Ti peaks (Fig. 3L), whereas UMTS exhibited additional Fe and Ag signals (Fig. 3M). Importantly, the presence of a Si peak in MMTS was noted (Fig. 3N). Elemental mapping further verified the distribution of O, Ca, Si, Ti, Fe, and Ag across all specimens (Fig. 3O), confirming successful functionalization. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) revealed that both UMTS and MMTS surfaces exhibited greater roughness and higher spatial frequency than the pure Ti substrate (Fig. 3P, Q), as supported by representative AFM images (Fig. 3R). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy identified vibrational bands associated with C–N, Si–O–Si, and C=O bonds, corroborating the successful conjugation of the flexible linker to the functionalized JNHD and nHA-Ti substrate (Fig. 3S). The incorporation of the polymer linker and SiO2 coating significantly enhanced the hydrophilicity of UMTS (Fig. 3T). Critically, to evaluate the stability afforded by the flexible linker, we measured the release kinetics of Fe (Fig. 3U) and Ag ions (Fig. 3V). The results demonstrated that the flexible linker substantially decelerated ion release. Although exposure to an EMF induced mechanical instability in the JNHDs, MMTS exhibited superior sustained-release properties compared with UMTS, attributable to the covalent bonding provided by the flexible linker (Fig. 3W).

Dynamic biomimetic MMTS under EMF exposure enhances cell viability and adhesion

Live/dead staining was performed to evaluate the biocompatibility of each group. The results showed that UMTS led to substantial cell death, especially upon EMF exposure. Whereas the cytotoxicity of MMTS was reduced, and CEZ was similar to Ti, which exhibited no obvious cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A). CCK-8 assay showed slight reductions in cell viability in UMTS, MMTS, and CEZ groups compared with Ti (Fig. 4B). This reduced cytotoxicity was correlated with the increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (Fig. 4C). For BMSCs, the cell spreading suggests a high capacity for activity and differentiation. Quantitative analysis indicated that UMTS, with or without EMF exposure, significantly reduced cell spreading. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between Ti, MMTS, and CEZ groups. This improvement of MMTS can be attributed to the bendable linker that mitigates the burst release of JNHDs from MMTS (Fig. 4D). As the morphology of BMSCs showed, BMSCs on UMTS and UMTS + EMF appeared slightly crumpled compared with those on Ti, likely due to the cytotoxicity of Ag NPs. However, the crumpled morphology was relieved on the surface of MMTS and MMTS + EMF, while CEZ had almost no effect on the morphology of BMSCs (Fig. 4E, F). Due to the importance of cell adhesion to BMSC function, the integrin levels were also evaluated in BMSCs with immunofluorescent staining. Both UMTS and MMTS surfaces markedly elevated Itg-β3 levels, which were further enhanced upon EMF exposure, while Itg-β3 levels remained at basal levels in the CEZ group (Fig. 4G). Similarly, the level of Itg-α5 also showed increased levels in both UMTS + EMF and MMTS + EMF, but showed no change in the CEZ group (Fig. 4H). As a key transcription factor, immunofluorescence staining of Runx2 revealed increased fluorescence intensity in UMTS + EMF and MMTS + EMF (Supplementary Fig. 7). The nuclear translocation was also enhanced in both UMTS + EMF and MMTS + EMF groups, while CEZ had no effect on the level or nuclear translocation of Runx2 in BMSCs (Fig. 4I).

Periodic mechanical stimulation induced by dynamic biomimetic MMTS under EMF exposure enhances osteogenesis of BMSCs

Periodic mechanical stimulation plays a crucial role in promoting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and preventing bone loss. In this study, such stimulation was applied to the dynamic biomimetic MMTS surface under an EMF. To investigate the molecular response of BMSCs to this stimulation, RT-qPCR was performed to assess the expression of Itg-α5, Itg-β3, OPN, Runx2, and BMP2. The results revealed that both UMTS and MMTS upregulated the expression of these integrin and osteogenic genes in the BMSCs. Moreover, EMF exposure further significantly enhanced their expression in both UMTS + EMF and MMTS + EMF groups (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis (Fig. 5B) and quantification (Fig. 5C) corroborated these findings, demonstrating markedly elevated protein levels of ITG-α5, ITG-β3, OPN, Runx2, and BMP2 in the MMTS + EMF group. The osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs was further evaluated using an ALP activity assay. The results showed that both UMTS and MMTS slightly increased ALP activity, while EMF exposure substantially enhanced it (Fig. 5D). Matrix mineralization, assessed by Alizarin Red S staining after 28 days of its induction, showed that EMF strongly promoted new bone formation on both UMTS and MMTS (Fig. 5E). Similarly, ALP staining confirmed the strong pro-osteogenic effect of MMTS upon EMF exposure (Fig. 5F). The enhanced osteogenic effects conferred by MMTS and EMF are attributed to the periodic mechanical stimulation generated on the MMTS surface upon the application of an EMF. This mechanism is biomimetically designed to simulate the natural mechanical stimulation to which bone is subjected during exercise. Specifically, upon exercise, mechanical force is transduced into the bone, thus activating the mechanical sensor Piezo1, leading to calcium influx that in turn promotes new bone formation (Fig. 5G). In summary, both UMTS and MMTS promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and EMF-induced periodic mechanical stimulation further amplified this osteogenic response.

Activation of Piezo1 and calcium ion oscillations induced by dynamic biomimetic MMTS upon EMF exposure

Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive ion channel present on most mammalian cells, capable of sensing mechanical stress and transducing it into biochemical signals to regulate cellular functions. Initially, we assessed the protein level of Piezo1 across different stages of BMSC differentiation. Western blot analysis revealed that the level of Piezo1 on days 7 and 14 was higher than on day 3, while it decreased after day 28. This indicated that the level of Piezo1 is crucial in the middle stage of osteogenic differentiation and determines the fate of BMSCs (Fig. 6A, B). Mechanical activation of Piezo1 triggered calcium influx, leading to elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels and resulting in calcium oscillations (Fig. 6C). To investigate the functional role of Piezo1 in BMSCs, we employed its pharmacological activator Yoda1. Treatment with Yoda1 induced calcium influx and oscillations in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6D). Conversely, pretreatment with the Piezo1 antagonist Dooku1 effectively blocked Yoda1-induced calcium responses (Fig. 6E), confirming that Piezo1 is functionally expressed on BMSCs and essential for mechanotransductive signaling (Supplementary Fig. 9). We next evaluated the osteogenic ability of Piezo1. To determine whether the dynamic biomimetic MMTS under EMF exposure could generate periodic mechanical stimulation sufficient to activate Piezo1, we monitored real-time calcium dynamics in BMSCs. No calcium oscillations were observed in cells cultured on Ti, UMTS, or MMTS in the absence of EMF. However, EMF exposure induced pronounced calcium oscillations and increased fluorescence intensity in both UMTS and MMTS groups (Fig. 6H). These effects were abolished by pretreatment with Dooku1 (Fig. 6I), confirming the involvement of Piezo1 in this mechanoresponsive process (Supplementary Fig. 10). As primary cilia serve as critical mechanosensors in mammalian cells, immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate their presence and structural integrity in BMSCs. The results confirmed that primary cilia were present in BMSCs, and their formation was effectively inhibited upon transfection with si-IFT20 (Fig. 6J). Meanwhile, knockdown of IFT20 disrupted ciliary integrity, leading to suppressed matrix deposition (Fig. 6K) and reduced ALP activity (Fig. 6L). These findings indicate that the presence and structural maintenance of primary cilia in BMSCs are essential for Piezo1-mediated perception of extracellular mechanical signals and subsequent osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 6M). In summary, Piezo1 is functionally expressed in BMSCs and plays a pivotal role in osteogenic differentiation. The dynamic biomimetic MMTS, when activated by EMF, generates periodic mechanical stimuli that activate Piezo1 and induce calcium oscillations, thereby promoting osteogenesis.

The bactericidal efficacy against adherent bacteria was evaluated using live/dead staining. Discussion

Despite the widespread use of antibiotics in reducing infection-related morbidity, bacterial infections remain a severe threat to global public health. A major concern lies in the remarkable ability of bacteria to evolve sophisticated drug-resistance mechanisms. First, planktonic bacteria secrete enzymes that degrade or structurally modify antibiotics, blocking their binding to molecular targets and diminishing therapeutic effects28. Second, mutations in genes encoding penicillin-binding proteins, DNA helicase, or enzymatic alteration of drug target sites further reduce antibiotic efficacy29. Third, bacteria also restrict intracellular drug accumulation by altering membrane permeability, downregulating drug influx channels or upregulating efflux pumps, constituting another critical resistance mechanism30. Most importantly, the formation of biofilms further complicates antibiotic resistance. As structured communities encased in extracellular polymeric substances, biofilms protect resident bacteria by hindering antibiotic penetration, driving metabolic heterogeneity, and facilitating horizontal gene transfer. In the context of anti-biofilm, the “race to the surface” concept is well known, as it highlights the competition between host cells and bacteria to colonize implant surfaces. This race is between osseointegration and bacterial colonization of the implant. In 2019, CEZ was recommended by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons guidelines as the gold standard prophylactic antibiotics31. Therefore, the CEZ group was included as a clinical reference, given its extensive usage32 and high bactericidal ability33 in orthopedic surgery. In this study, we demonstrated that nano-kinetic surface of MMTS can be activated with EMF exposure, to induce a electromagnetic-mechanical coupling and disrupt the bacterial wall and membrane (Supplementary Fig. 16), without the generation of ROS (Supplementary Fig. 17). It not only produce a comparable bactericidal ability as CEZ against S. aureus and E. coli, but also exhibited similar bactericidal ability of vancomycin against MRSA (Supplementary Fig. 21). More importantly, this electromagnetic-mechanical coupling can activate Piezo1 and Ca2+ influx to strongly promote new bone formation and osteointegration, which cannot be achieved by the antibiotic like CEZ.

Preventing bacterial colonization is critical for rapid osseointegration. Once bacterial colonization adherent to the implant surface first, they substantially impair host cell adhesion and tissue repair, thereby accelerating biofilm formation and hindering implant osseointegration34. Accordingly, an effective anti-biofilm strategy needs to fulfill the following requirements: (1) prevention of initial bacterial adhesion while maintaining high viability of host cells on implant surfaces in the early post-implantation stage; (2) delivery of antimicrobial agents specifically to infected sites to improve local therapeutic efficacy; (3) improve penetration of antimicrobial agents across the biofilm’s extracellular polymeric substances, so that bactericidal concentrations can reach embedded bacteria; and (4) exert broad-spectrum activity against polymicrobial communities to address diverse and coexisting pathogenic populations21.

To meet these requirements, surface engineering has undergone rapid development35. Specifically, it has been shown that micro- and nanostructural modifications of implants can inhibit bacterial adhesion and kill bacteria via either contact-killing or localized antimicrobial release, providing important protection36. It has been reported that mechanical stress within the bacterial wall disrupts the tripartite efflux complex CusCBA in E. coli. This disassembly increases bacterial susceptibility to metal toxicity, demonstrating that mechanical forces can directly compromise bacterial survival37. To endow implant surfaces with bactericidal capacity and optimize their biological performance, biomimetic modification approaches have attracted substantial research attention in recent years. Integrating nanotechnology with bioinspired design principles has been proven to improve targeting accuracy, therapeutic efficacy, and biocompatibility of implant materials38. Particularly, biomimetic antibacterial materials represent an emerging class of nature-derived biomaterials. By virtue of anti-adhesive and bactericidal mechanisms, these materials exhibit high efficacy in eliminating planktonic bacteria and biofilms and can effectively treat implant-associated infections via anti-adhesion and bactericidal mechanisms39. These advantages have generated growing interest in the use of bionic strategies for treating PJI due to their great potential for effectively preventing and eradicating infection. To date, many studies have explored various antibacterial approaches40. For instance, Dai et al. developed cobalt-doped peony flower-like Bi2O3 (CBO). The CBO sample with a cobalt doping concentration of 0.4% exhibited a narrowed band gap, which significantly facilitated electron-hole separation under light irradiation. This structural optimization enhanced the generation of superoxide anions, hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), thereby enhancing the laser-mediated bactericidal effect against E. coli41. Similarly, Wang et al. designed a biomimetic nanoadjuvant to combat biofilm-related infections. This nanoplatform is based on hollow mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles coated with macrophage membranes, which enables precise targeted delivery to infection sites. Loaded with immunomodulators (CpG-ODN or miR-21-5p), the platform provides sequential immunotherapy for infection treatment. In the early stage of infection, CpG-ODN induces the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps and promotes M1 macrophage polarization via the TLR9/MyD88/TRAF6 pathway. During the tissue repair phase, miR-21-5p induces M2 macrophage polarization through the PTEN/PI3K/Akt pathway. Combined with sonodynamic therapy that generate ROS, this integrated system effectively disrupts biofilms, eliminates bacteria, and enhances tissue integration, offering a multifunctional and comprehensive antibacterial therapeutic strategy42. Additionally, Zhang et al. developed a biodegradable Janus sonozyme (MON(CuMn)-Q) for the treatment of infected bone defects. This sonozyme exerts sonodynamic antibacterial activity through dual active sites (MnN and CuO) to generate antibacterial ROS. After degradation, the released Cu2+, Mn2+, and quercetin provide sustained antimicrobial effects. In a rat MRSA-infected cranial defect model, this sonozyme rapidly eliminated bacteria, alleviated local inflammation, and promoted bone regeneration, demonstrating effective and continuous antibacterial functionality alongside osteogenic promotion43. Moreover, mussel-inspired hydrogels have also been reported to exhibit strong tissue adhesion and anti-infective capabilities44. While conventional static biomimetic surfaces successfully replicate morphological features, they fundamentally overlook the dynamic responses that living organisms or implants exhibit in response to changing natural conditions45. For instance, in our previous study, only limited intrinsic bactericidal ability was exerted by the insect wing-inspired TiO2 nanotube via its morphology. Instead, its antibacterial effect was primarily dependent on the controlled release of encapsulated bactericidal agents18. This suggests that more potent bioinspired biomaterials can be achieved via dynamic rather than static mimicry. Advanced biomimicry is not limited to the replication of biological morphology, more importantly, it focuses on emulating the dynamic functions and adaptive responses to environmental stimuli. Based on the flapping kinematics of insect and human exercise-induced stimulation, we have performed a screening of EMF parameters; the results showed that EMF frequencies (0–100 Hz) and intensities (0–5 mT) have no obvious effects on cell viability, ALP activity, intracellular ROS level (Supplementary Fig. 24), and infection (Supplementary Fig. 25). These results further clarified it was the mechanical stimulation that induce significant biologiccal effects on cells.

Two critical biosafety concerns associated with the dynamic mimicry have been carefully considered. Although JHNDs can be effectively aligned in situ with an external field (Supplementary Movie 2, Supplementary Fig. 19), the mechanical vibration generated on the surface of MMTS was strictly controlled (Supplementary Fig. 20), within a physiologically safe mechanical window that mimics human exercise-induced stimulation. The systemic evaluation showed that JHNDs have a stable characterization after long-term daily EMF exposure (Supplementary Fig. 26). The flexible linker further served as a mechanical buffer to avoid excessive shear force or burst release to host cells. Notably, in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility results confirmed no obvious cytotoxicity or mechanical impairment, indicating negligible harmful shear stress to host cells and major organs (Supplementary Fig. 22). To evaluate the potential damage caused by EMF-induced magnetic hyperthermia, real-time thermal imaging was used; the results showed that magnetic hyperthermia does not occur in our system and poses no adverse effects (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Dynamic control biomaterials, such as magnetic ligand nano-assemblies on Fe3O4, enable the remote regulation of ligand presentation. This tunable property further modulates stem cell adhesion and differentiation through mechanotransductive pathways46. It has also been reported that actuated anisotropic magnetic nanochains can effectively disperse biofilms via mechanical force, showing the effective removal of >90% of adherent bacteria under a magnetic field, without harming host epithelial cells47. Notably, most conventional antimicrobial strategies primarily focus on preventing infection, while largely neglecting their potential effects on osseointegration has often been overlooked11,48. However, it has been reported that mechanical stimulation plays a vital role in bone regeneration and osteointegration. For example, low-magnitude high-frequency vibration (LMHFV) at 35 Hz and 0.3 g was reported to accelerate fracture healing and rescue impaired healing in osteoporotic models by enhancing callus formation49. In addition, in rat implant models, LMHFV (0.2–0.5 g; 30–45 Hz) significantly increased bone–implant contact, bone volume ratio, and mineral density50. During such mechanical stimulation, the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 is a critical mediator of mechanical signaling in bone cells. It operates on a force-from-lipid principle, being activated upon changes in membrane tension51. For instance, exercise-induced osteogenic gene expression was found to be increased in Piezo1fl/fl mice but not in Piezo1-deficient mice52, suggesting its key role in bone mechanotransduction and its potential as a target for treating disuse osteoporosis53. Yoda1, the agonist of Piezo1, can stabilize the channel’s open state, lowering its activation threshold54. Yoda1 has thus been incorporated into biomaterials to promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis via the Piezo1/RhoA/ROCK1/YAP pathway55. Meanwhile, Guo et al. developed a composite scaffold (PGS-P@MGel) that delivers dynamic mechanical stimulation under external fields, activating Piezo1 and enhancing osteogenesis via β-catenin and YAP nuclear translocation56. Mechanical stimulation also upregulates specific integrins, which cooperate with Piezo1 to promote the nuclear entry of YAP and Runx2 expression, driving osteogenic differentiation57. In this study, we carefully evaluated the influences of MMTS and EMF exposure on the activation of Piezo1 in BMSCs. The findings demonstrated that calcium influx and calcium oscillations were induced to facilitate osteogenesis. However, it has been reported that Piezo1 deletion does not fully abolish the skeletal mechanoresponse, indicating contributions from other sensors including integrins, focal adhesions, or primary cilia58. Moreover, beyond mechanical and electromagnetic stimulation, triboelectric stimulation via wearable nanogenerators (30 µA peak current) was shown to promote the osteogenic differentiation of aged BMSCs through Piezo1 activation59. Piezo1 may thus function not just as a mechanosensor; however, its potential roles require further investigation to elucidate its full mechanistic implications.

Immunomodulatory biomaterials that trigger controllable immune responses have attracted extensive research attention, as they can enhance tissue regeneration and indirectly prevent bacterial infection60. For instance, breathing-mimetic mechanical motions have been reported to suppress viral replication by activating innate immune responses via TRPV4 and RAGE signaling61. In addition, anisotropic hydrogels were shown to promote integrin β1 expression and polarization of macrophages toward the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, accelerating wound healing62. However, it has remained unclear whether the dynamic biomimetic MMTS with EMF exposure can affect the osteoimmune microenvironment, such as macrophage polarization19 and NETosis63, to promote bone regeneration and eliminate infections. Another limitation of this study is that human multi-omics and histological are a preliminary exploratory finding, further studies with larger patient cohorts are needed.

Despite the rapid advances in this field, the fabrication of high-performance bioinspired biomaterials for preventing PJI remains a major challenge. On one hand, implant materials are required to possess biocompatibility, mechanical robustness, and corrosion resistance64. On the other hand, beyond basic material performance, the mechanical cues play a pivotal role in regulating bone remodeling and host-pathogen interactions65. This study integrated advanced antimicrobial and mechanobiology-based strategies into a dynamic biomimetic implant surface, which showed greater advances than the clinical gold standard CEZ. Therefore, this work holds great promise for developing implants that not only resist infection but also promote functional tissue regeneration.

Synthesis process of specimen

Fe3O4-Ag JNHDs were synthesized based on an established method66. In detail, 500 μL Fe3O4 NPs were dispersed in 20 mL trisodium citrate solution (25% w/v in ddH2O), and stirred at 60 °C. Subsequently, 250 μL AgNO3 solution (10 mM) was added to the mixture and stirred for 2 h at 60 °C. To harvest JNHDs, the mixture was exposed to UV irradiation for 30 min. For the SiO2 coating of JNHDs, 250 μg JNHDs were dispersed in 500 μL of ethanol with 25 μL NH3·H2O and 75 μL ddH2O. Then, the suspension was vigorously stirred for 10 min. Subsequently, the suspension was supplemented with 0.2 μL tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and was stirred for 2 h. Lastly, SiO2@JNHDs were washed three times with ethanol. SiO2@JNHDs were dispersed in 500 μL ethanol with 10 μL (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES), then it was stirred for 24 h. Afterward, the amino-functionalized SiO2@JNHDs were washed three times with ddH2O. To synthesize the transformable topological surface, the amino-functionalized SiO2@JNHDs was stirred in 500 μL ddH2O with 5 mg of long bendable linker [maleimide-poly(ethylene glycol)-succinimidyl carboxymethyl ester] for 5 min, then added with 2 μl of N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) and stirred for 24 h. The PEGylated SiO2@JNHDs were then washed three times with ddH2O. For the synthesis of a non-transformable topological surface, the amino-functionalized SiO2@JNHDs were not PEGylated. Similarly, for the SiO2 coating of Ti substrate, the pure-Ti substrates (purity: 99.9%) were placed in a 24-well plate and immersed in 500 μL hydroxyapatite nanoparticle (nHA) suspension (1 mg/mL). The specimen was thereafter dried for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the specimen was dispersed in 500 μL of ethanol with 25 μL NH3·H2O and 75 μL ddH2O. Then, the plate was shaken for 15 min. Afterward, 0.2 μL TEOS was added to each well, and the plate was shaken for a further 2 h. Lastly, SiO2-nHA@Ti were washed three times with ethanol. To thiolated the specimen, each specimen was then immersed in a mixture of 475 μL ethanol and 25 μL (3-mercaptopropyl)trimethoxysilane (MPTMS) for 24 h. Afterward, the specimen was gently washed with ddH2O. The thiolated specimen was treated with 500 μl PEGylated SiO2@JNHDs (1:10 diluted in ddH2O) for 16 h to create the transformable conjugation and fabricated MMTS. For UMTS synthesis, JNHDs were directly deposited on the surface of nHA-Ti. To compare with regular clinic treatment, Ti substrate was immersed in 50 μg/ml CEZ (Cat. No. HY-B1892, MedChemExpress, China) for 24 h, after air dry it was then used as positive control. To facilitate high-resolution imaging of specific staining assays, the conventional Ti substrate was substituted with a silicon dioxide round coverslip (Cat. No. WHB-12-CS, WHB Scientific, China). All subsequent fabrication steps for UMTS and MMTS coatings remained identical to those applied on Ti substrates, ensuring consistent surface modification and functionalization across different material bases (Supplementary Fig. 13). Requests for experimental materials should be directed to the corresponding authors.

Characterization of specimens

The morphology and elemental distribution of JNHDs were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL JEM-F200, Japan). Particle size distribution was quantified from TEM images using ImageJ software. Chemical composition and elemental mapping were conducted via EDS. The surface morphology and elemental distribution of Ti, UMTS, and MMTS samples were examined using SEM (ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany). Additionally, SEM was employed to observe the nanostructure of insect wings as well as bacterial adhesion on implant surfaces. Surface topography and roughness parameters were assessed using an atomic force microscope (AFM; Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany). Chemical bond characteristics of Ti, UMTS, and MMTS were evaluated by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA). The release profiles of Ag and Fe ions from each specimen were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry/mass spectrometry (ICP-OES/MS; Agilent 7800, USA). Pre-dissected insect wings were obtained from a commercial supplier, Beijing Jiaying Art Insectarium (Beijing, China).

Magnetic hysteresis loop measurement

Magnetic hysteresis loops of Ti, UMTS, and MMTS were recorded at room temperature using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, LakeShore 7404/8604, USA). The maximum applied magnetic field was set to ±2 T. Hysteresis data were collected and analyzed to characterize the magnetic properties of the samples.

Magnetothermal effect assessment

The magnetothermal performance of Ti, UMTS, MMTS, and JNHDs was evaluated using a thermal imaging camera. Each sample was placed in an alternating EMF coil. Thermal images were captured before and after EMF exposure. The temperature at the sample surface (marked ROI) was recorded, and the temperature change was calculated. All measurements were repeated in triplicate.

Magnetic-field-induced motion of JNHDs

The directional movement of JNHD nanoparticles under an external MF was observed by microscopy. JNHDs were placed in a custom MF. Bright-field images and cross-sectional fluorescence scans (X–Z plane) were captured. For the static control, images were recorded without the applied field. Under the aligned MF, JNHDs’ movement in both horizontal and vertical directions was visualized. Multiple fields of view were captured to assess the consistency of directional alignment.

Human specimens

Bone tissue samples were collected from a patient undergoing one-stage revision surgery for severe chronic periprosthetic hip infection. The diagnosis of periprosthetic joint infection was confirmed by senior orthopedic surgeons, radiologists, and supporting laboratory results. Following removal of all infected prosthetic components, the femoral component was gently rinsed with sterile physiological saline. Both the saline solution and the explanted components were promptly transferred to the laboratory for further processing. To isolate bacteria on the prosthetic surfaces, the saline was centrifuged, and the pellet was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (Cat. No. L1010, Solarbio, China). After 48 h of incubation, bacterial cultures were collected for subsequent analysis. Concurrently, the acetabular cup and any adherent bone tissue were subjected to fixation and decalcification for further histological or morphological examination.

Cell isolation and culture

Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the tibiae and femurs of 2-week-old C57 mice via whole bone marrow flushing and adherent culture. Briefly, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Intact tibiae and femurs were aseptically dissected, and all adjacent soft tissues were carefully removed. The epiphyses were trimmed, and the marrow cavities were flushed repeatedly using a sterile 25-gauge needle and syringe filled with α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM; Gibco, Cat. No. 12571063) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat. No. 16010159) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat. No. C0222).

The resulting cell suspension was collected and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in fresh complete medium and seeded into culture flasks. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After 24 h, non-adherent cells were removed by medium replacement. The medium was refreshed every 2 days thereafter. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, adherent cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Cat. No. 25200056) at a split ratio of 1:3. BMSCs between passages 3 and 5 were used for all experiments.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Cat. No. C0038, CCK-8; Beyotime Biotechnology, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. BMSCs were seeded onto the specimens and cultured with or without daily EMF exposure. To prevent interference between experimental groups, the culture plates for EMF-treated cells were separated from non-exposed conditions during stimulation. After 3 days of culture, the cells were gently rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). CCK-8 working solution was then added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA).

Live/Dead staining

Cytotoxicity and bactericidal ability were assessed using a Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit (Cat. No. L3224, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). BMSCs and adherent bacteria were seeded onto each specimen and cultured with or without daily EMF exposure. After 24 h, the cells were gently washed three times with PBS and incubated with a solution containing Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). Bacterial viability was evaluated under identical staining conditions.

Cytoskeleton staining

Cellular morphology was evaluated through cytoskeleton staining. Briefly, BMSCs were gently washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were then permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by refixation with 2% paraformaldehyde for an additional 10 min. After washing, samples were incubated with 0.5 µg/ml Phalloidin (Cat. No. Y249127-10μg, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and DAPI (5 μg/mL, Cat. No. C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Imaging was performed using a fluorescent microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). Cell spreading area was quantified with ImageJ software.

DCFH-DA assay

Intracellular ROS levels in BMSCs and adherent bacteria were detected using a fluorometric assay kit (Cat. No. E-BC-K138-F, Elabscience, China). Following treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, unincorporated dye was removed by washing three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence scanning microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

SYTOX Green staining

SYTOX Green staining was performed to evaluate the disruption of bacterial cell membranes and cell walls. Bacterial suspensions were incubated with Ti, UMTS, MMTS specimens with or without EMF exposure and NAC (Cat. No. S0077, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the samples were gently rinsed twice with PBS. Then, the specimens were stained with SYTOX Green staining buffer (Cat. No. C1186-100ml, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) in the dark at room temperature for 60 min. Following three washes with PBS, the fluorescent images were immediately captured using a fluorescent microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). The fluorescence intensity, indicative of membrane and wall damage allowing dye penetration, was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

BMSCs were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by refixation with 2% paraformaldehyde for an additional 10 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Boster, China, Cat. No. AR1006) under gentle shaking for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Itg-β3 (Cat. No. 66952-1-Ig, 1:1000, mouse monoclonal; Proteintech), Itg-α5 (Cat. No. 27096-1-AP, 1:500, rabbit polyclonal; Proteintech), and Runx2 (Cat. No. 20700-1-AP, 1:500, rabbit polyclonal; Proteintech). After washing with PBS, samples were incubated for 2 h in the dark with CoraLite 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat. No. SA00013-2, 1:100; Proteintech, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG (Cat. No. SA00013-1, 1:100; Proteintech, China). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (5 μg/mL, Cat No. C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 30 min in the dark. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and Runx2 co-localization was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescent staining of primary cilia in BMSCs

BMSCs were first transfected with or without si-IFT20, then undergo a serum-starved for at least 48 h to induce primary cilia formation. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. A second fixation step was performed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. BMSCs were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against acetylated tubulin (1:200l; Cat No. 66200-1-Ig, Proteintech, China). After PBS washes, samples were incubated for 2 h in the dark with a CoraLite 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:100; Cat No. SA00013-1, Proteintech, China). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (5 μg/mL; Cat No. C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 30 min protected from light. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs using the Eastep® Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. LS1040, Promega Biotech, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Cat. No. 840-317400, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (No. D7190S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China, Cat.). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with the SYBR Green One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (Cat. No. D7268S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the provided protocol. All reactions were run in triplicate. The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The primer sequences used for amplification were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech, Beijing, China.

Primary cilia knockdown

IFT20 mRNA in BMSCs was knocked down using IFT20 siRNA (Forward Primer: GAAUAUGAAGCUUUGUGUA, Reverse Primer: UACACAAAGCUUCAUAUUC) with Lipofectamine 2000 (Cat. No. 11668019, Invitrogen, USA). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/well in complete medium without antibiotics and transfected at 60–70% confluence. For each well, 50 nM siRNA was diluted in 250 µL Opti-MEM (Cat. No. 11058021, Gibco, USA), mixed with 5 µL Lipofectamine 2000 in 250 µL Opti-MEM, and incubated for 20 min. The complex was added dropwise to wells containing fresh antibiotic-free medium. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with standard complete medium. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for subsequent research.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Cat. No. R0016, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer and a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer. Only high-quality RNA samples (OD260/280 = 1.8-2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RQN ≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ≥ 1.0, total RNA > 1 μg) were used for subsequent library preparation. Sequencing libraries were constructed using either the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation Kit (1 μg input) or the SMART-Seq V4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (10 ng input), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols. Library quantification was performed using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using fastp. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome with HISAT2. Transcript assembly and expression quantification were conducted using StringTie and RSEM, respectively. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 and DEGseq, with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.05 (DESeq2) or FDR ≤ 0.001 (DEGseq). For bacterial metabolomic analysis, microbial metabolism was rapidly quenched, and metabolites were extracted accordingly. Metabolic profiling was performed using LC-MS and GC-MS platforms. Raw data were processed to align peaks, identify metabolites by matching against standard databases, and were subjected to multivariate statistical analysis for biological interpretation. Functional enrichment analyses of DEGs, including Gene Ontology and KEGG pathways, were conducted using the DAVID bioinformatics database.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from BMSCs using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Cat No.PC101, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Cat No.GRF103, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). Lysates were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat No.ZJ102, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (25 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% or 15% acrylamide-bisacrylamide gels at 120 V for 150 min and then transferred to 0.22 µm PVDF membranes at 400 mA for 30 min using Ice-Bath Free Western Superquick Transfer Buffer (Cat No.PS201S, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China).

Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Itg-β3 (1:2000, mouse, Cat No. 66952-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), Itg-α5 (1:2000, rabbit, Cat No. 27096-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Runx2 (1:2000, rabbit, Cat No. 20700-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Bmp2 (1:1000, mouse, Cat No.66383-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), OPN (1:1000, rabbit, Cat No.22952-1-AP, Proteintech, China), Piezo1 (1:1000, rabbit, Cat No.82625-4-RR, Proteintech, China), and GAPDH (1:5000, rabbit, Cat No.10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 in TBST) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection kit (Cat No.SQ202, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) and imaged with a CCD camera. Signal intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Fabrication and calibration of EMF exposure system

EMF exposure system (Supplementary Fig. 14) was custom-designed and consisted of four main components: a one-dimensional Helmholtz coil for in vivo animal experiments (custom model based on Cat. No. DXHC15-10, Dexin Mag, Xiamen, China), a solenoid for in vitro cell experiments (custom design, Dexin Mag, Xiamen, China), a power amplifier (Cat. No. DXACP-0332, Dexin Mag, Xiamen, China), and a multi-waveform generator (Cat. No. 8300S, Dexin Mag, Xiamen, China). The magnetic field intensity was emulated (Supplementary Fig. 15) and calibrated before each experiment using a Gaussmeter (Cat. No. DX-102F, Dexin Mag, Xiamen, China).

Intracellular Ca2+ staining

BMSCs were seeded on each specimen for 24 h in a Petri dish, and then loaded with 2 µM Fluo-4 AM (Cat No. S1060, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, BMSCs were gently washed three times with PBS and incubated in Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (with Ca2+, Cat No. C0219, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). BMSCs were then undergo real time video recording with a fluorescent microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan). Piezo1 activator Yoda1 (Cat No. HY-18723, MCE, China) and Yoda1 antagonist Dooku1 (Cat No. HY-126010, MCE, China) were used to pharmacologically modulate the activity of Piezo1. Yoda1 were used at 10 µM for subsequent experiments, as dose-response screening showed that while 20 µM yielded slightly higher signals, the marginal gain did not justify the increased risk of off-target cytotoxicity at the higher concentration. The quantitative analysis was conducted with ImageJ software.

ALP staining and Alizarin red staining

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and Alizarin red staining were performed to evaluate early osteogenic differentiation and late-stage matrix mineralization, respectively. For ALP staining, BMSCs were induced under osteogenic conditions for 14 days, with or without pharmacological treatment. Cells were washed three times with PBS and stained using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Cat No.C3206, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For Alizarin red staining, BMSCs were osteogenically induced for 28 days, with or without pharmacological treatment, then fixed with ice-cold 99% ethanol at −20 °C for 1 h. After fixation, cells were gently washed three times with tap water and incubated with Alizarin red solution (Cat No.C0138, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Unbound dye was removed by washing three times with tap water. Stained samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Nikon, Japan).

Antibacterial assay

Antibacterial activity was evaluated against both Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative Escherichia coli. Bacterial suspensions collected from specimen surfaces or culture suspension (with or without EMF exposure) were inoculated into LB culture medium (Cat No. L1010, Solarbio, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with shaking. Subsequently, the suspensions were appropriately diluted, spread onto LB agar plates (Cat No. L1015, Solarbio, China), and incubated statically at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. The number of bacterial colonies and macroscopic morphology of the agar plates were documented to assess antibacterial activity.

Crystal violet staining

Bacterial suspensions collected from specimen surfaces with or without EMF exposure were cultured in LB medium (Cat No. L1010, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 24 h under shaking conditions. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of each suspension was mixed with an equal volume of fresh LB medium and transferred to a 24-well plate for static incubation at 37 °C for 48 h to allow biofilm formation. After careful removal of the supernatant, the adherent biofilms were stained with crystal violet solution (Cat No. C0121-100ml, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 10 min at room temperature. The wells were gently rinsed with ddH2O to remove unbound dye. Images of the stained biofilms were acquired using an optical microscope. For quantitative analysis, the bound dye was dissolved in 95% ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm to evaluate anti-biofilm activity.

Animal experiment

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (4 weeks old) were obtained from Dashuo Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (China). A tibial osteomyelitis model was established in these animals. A total of 42 male Sprague–Dawley rats (4 weeks old) were used in this study. Rats were randomly assigned to seven experimental groups (n = 6 per group) using a computer random number generator: Ti, UMTS, MMTS, Ti+EMF, UMTS + EMF, MMTS + EMF, and CEZ. All rats completed the experiment without exclusion or replacement.

Following anesthesia, the surgical site was shaved and disinfected. An incision was made along the knee joint to expose the proximal tibial metaphysis. A bone channel was drilled from the tibial plateau into the marrow cavity using an electric drill. Pre-inoculated specimens were then implanted into the tibia. Muscle, fascia, and skin were sutured layer by layer. Postoperative health status and activity of the rats were monitored daily. Animals in the EMF exposure groups were subjected to daily EMF (50 Hz, 5 mT) treatment for 30 min. The CEZ control group did not receive EMF exposure. The clinical dose for adults is 0.5–1 g, i.v., t.i.d, accordingly, an intraperitoneal dose of 50 mg/kg, q.d., was administered to the SD rats of the CEZ group in this study.

Micro-CT evaluation

Bone tissue samples from the tibia were analyzed using a high-resolution μ-CT system (NMC-100, PINGSENG Healthcare, Shanghai, China). Scanning parameters were set at 80 kV and 0.08 mA. Consistent regions of interest (ROIs) were selected and scanned under identical conditions for all samples. Coronal CT images and three-dimensional reconstructions were generated, and quantitative analyses were performed using Imaris software (version 10.2, Oxford Instruments, USA).

Histological analysis

Tissue specimens were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 30 days, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 10 μm thickness were prepared using a rotary microtome. Consecutive sections were stained with H&E for general histoarchitecture evaluation, Masson’s trichrome for collagen deposition, Gram staining, and Giemsa staining for bacterial visualization. For immunofluorescence analysis, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL2A1, BMP2, VEGFA, Piezo1, and HIF1A, following established protocols67. After washing, appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All stained sections were imaged under a fluorescence microscope under consistent exposure settings.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replications. Error bars represent the standard deviation of the mean. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed with the GraphPad Prism software V.8.0.1 (El Camino Real, USA).
