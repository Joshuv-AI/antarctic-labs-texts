Climate change projections using CMIP6 GCMs and downscaling approaches in the Upper Wabe Shebele Basin, Ethiopia
Authors: Siraj Beshir, Awdenegest Moges, Mihret Dananto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate change presents considerable challenges, particularly for agrarian countries like Ethiopia. This study employed modeling and scenario-based methodologies to examine anticipated changes in precipitation and temperature in the Upper Wabe-Shebele River Basin. The study compared baseline data from 1986 to 2022 with two future projection periods: 2023 to 2053 and 2054 to 2086. Various bias correction techniques were applied to the precipitation and temperature datasets. For precipitation, the methods utilized include linear scaling, distribution mapping, empirical quantile mapping, and power transformation were utilized. Conversely, for temperature, power transformation was substituted with variance scaling, while the other three methods were employed for both datasets. The statistical downscaling model and climate model data for hydrologic modeling were calibrated and validated. The findings show a 35.89% reduction in precipitation and a 2.03 °C increase in temperature compared to the baseline. Future projections suggest that under the high emissions scenario SSP5-8.5, mean annual rainfall is expected to decline by 41.76% in the 2040s by 50.33% in the 2070s. Even under the lowest emissions scenario SSP1-2.6, rainfall is anticipated to decrease by 21.19% in the 2040s and by 30.25% in the 2070s. Temperature is projected to increase by 0.89–3.6 °C depending on the emissions scenario and time period. Additionally, both precipitation and temperature are also expected to show variations in their monthly distributions throughout the year. These climatic changes are expected to significantly reduce water availability and agricultural productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23194-6.

Introduction

Climate change is driven by a combination of natural and human factors, constituting a global phenomenon with significant regional variations in its impacts1. It presents a complex challenge due to factors like deforestation, alteration in land use and land cover, industrialization, resource constraints, ineffective policy frameworks, agricultural practices, inadequate monitoring, and unsustainable consumption patterns2–6.

Ethiopia, an agrarian country, particularly vulnerable to the effects of climate change, which manifest as rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and more frequent extreme weather condition7. In the last 30 years, the country has undergone an increase in temperature between 0.3 °C and 0.66 °C, whereas rainfall declined with different ranges with spatial variation8. Hence, it has resulted in a decline in crop yield, seasonal variability, shrinking of arable land, reduced water availability, and livestock feed resources, mostly impacting animal production. Under the influence of climate change, GDP that comes from agriculture will decline by 31.1% by 20509–12. It is imperative for Ethiopia potential future climate scenarios and their implications for key sectors in order to develop effective adaptation and mitigation strategies13. For instance, under continued climate change, wheat yield is expected to decrease by about 40%14. Therefore, to reduce the impacts of climate change, the country has prepared the Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) strategy and a National Adaptation Plan15. The major identified adaptation techniques under this plan framework consists planting date adjustment, the inclusion of drought tolerant crop varieties, crop diversification, supplemental irrigation, agroforestry, and soil and water conservation.

According to the literature compiled in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Changes’ Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC AR6), approximately 14% of the projected a global mean temperature increase exceeding the critical threshold of 2 °C by the end of the twenty-first century16,17. This indicating a likely chance of remaining below this threshold. Similarly, in Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), the updated Shared Socio-Economic Pathway with low emission and 2.6 radiative forcing level (SSP1-2.6) scenario reveals that the global mean temperature is likely to continue below 2 °C, with probability of exceedingly less than a 33%, While all higher-emission scenarios are predicted to exceed 2 °C with likely greater than 66%16.

In a similar situation, Liu and Raftery18, estimate a potential increase in global mean temperature ranging from 2.1 to 3.9 °C by the year 2100. In the Africa context, the average near and long-term temperature varies between 1.3 and 3.1 °C in twenty-first century16. Ethiopia, despite negligible contributions to global greenhouse gas emissions, the mean annual temperature will rise between 0.5 and 3.6 °C by 2070, while it projected a range of 26.92–3.84 °C by the year 2070 to 209019. Furthermore, these authors report average daily rainfall will reduce by 3.5% by the end of the century. However, there is also a potential increase of 2.55 °C in maximum temperatures and a 23% rise in rainfall between 2011 and 205020.

To come up with good climate change results, researchers have utilized a range of climate modeling techniques to explore future climate change scenarios for Ethiopia20–23. Both global and regional climate models provide projections of temperature, precipitation, and other climatic variables, while integrated assessment models combine climate and socioeconomic factors16,24. These diverse modeling approaches have generated critical data pertaining to future climate change in Ethiopia, thereby facilitating the development of effective adaptation and mitigation strategies25–27.

The Upper Wabe-Shebele River Basin (UWSRB) is currently found at the infant stage in climate change related studies, with limited information on climate change analysis25. While existing literatures acknowledge the basin’s susceptibility to climate change impacts, including water scarcity, diminished agricultural productivity, and infrastructure vulnerabilities, such as the proliferation of invasive species28–30. A critical gap exists in the application of strong, site-specific climate change modeling and scenario analysis for the UWSRB. Although recent studies have provided valuable insights into the Malka Wakana sub-basin28,31, their focus is limited and failing to encompass the entire of the UWSRB, particularly the area situated between the second Wabe Bridge and the Manya Sub-basin28. In addition, the implication of climate change modeling and scenario analysis for water resource management have not been adequately studied in the UWSRB. As a result, the findings from existing research cannot be effectively implemented or translated into actionable water resource management strategies that align with the objectives set forth by the Ministry of Water and Energy for the UWSRB. It is imperative to address this knowledge gap through comprehensive climate change modeling and scenario analysis for the entire basin is crucial to inform appropriate adaptation and mitigation strategies.

The comprehension of spatiotemporal variability in PCP and temperature is key to the analysis of climate change. Ultimately, such methodologies are essential for the developing of effective adaptation strategies and policies aimed at ensuring the resilience of the UWSRB in the face of a changing climate20,32. Recent studies have also indicated that there are climate change issues that require government concern to combat, influencing the socio-economic and biophysical aspects of the basin28,33–36. This understanding is crucial for water flow (hydrological balances), evaluating the potential of freshwater resource, conducting real-time flood forecasting, and developing effective water management strategies26,37–41. Bridging the gap between the coarse spatial resolution of Global Climate Models (GCMs) and the finer-scale data needed for localized analyses is essential42. Therefore, statistical downscaling models and Climate Model data for hydrologic modeling (CMhyd) have important impression into local climate studies focused in areas characterized by a diverse and heterogeneous land surfaces in terms of topography, vegetation cover and climate like UWSRB.

In the UWSRB, the application of the Statistical Downscaling Model (SDSM) and CMhyd has not been studied. Hence, this study addresses the gap by systematically evaluating their performance for the basin. The main novelty of this study lies not only in the comparison of downscaling approaches but also in producing high-resolution and better climate projections using CMIP6 GCMs under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios for a climatically vulnerable and data scarce basin. Significantly, the study also addresses the limitations issues of data gaps and spatial sparsity for observed meteorological datasets, as well as the uncertainties associated with CMIP6 outputs. The integration of downscaling approaches with climate change scenario analysis ensures a more detailed understanding of the basin’s diverse climatic conditions and regional variations, while also accounting for the inherent uncertainties in climate projections. This research will provide valuable findings to inform the development of effective adaptation strategies in the study area.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Location of the study area

The study area is a sub-basin of the Wabe Shebelle River Basin in southeastern Ethiopia, encompassing a total area of 10,259.03 km2. Geographically, the study area is located between 39°24′30.65′′to 39°29′49.13′′ E and 6°53′44.63′′ to 7°27′37.75′′ N (Fig. 1). It comprises six districts from the West Arsi Zone: Adaba, Dodola, Asassa, Kofale, Kokosa, and Kore; eight districts from the Arsi Zone: Limu Bilbilo, Inkolo Wabe, Sirka, Tana, Arsi Robe, Hamda Diksis, Amigna, and Bele Gesgar; and as well as three districts from Bale Zone Dinsho, Gasara, and Agarfa whereas Gololcha district from East Bale zone43.

Bio-physical characteristics

The mean annual temperature within the study area ranges from 2 to 15 °C in the higher altitude areas, while in the lower plateau areas, it varies between 16 and 34 °C. Particularly, there is a slight variation in temperature on monthly basis. The period from October to May is characterized as the hottest, whereas June to September represents the cooler months. The study basin is classified into two agro- climatic region: the warm temperate region (Badda Daree), which constitutes 24% of the total area, and the cool temperature region (Badda), which encompasses 76%. The primary rainy season for most areas starts in June and persists through July and August, with some higher regions experiencing rainfall extending into September. The mean annual PCP in the study area ranges from 1200 to 2940 mm. Furthermore, the intensity and duration of rainy days and seasons varies between the highland and midland regions, with a gradual decrease as one goes down the Desert areas along the Wabe River course downstream of the dam.

Natural vegetation

The study area is characterized by diverse vegetation types, notably in the Adaba-Dodola, Chilalo-Galema and Arba-Gugu forest areas. These forests are among the few remaining examples of Afromontane forests44. The central and upper part of the river basin, particularly in the Kokosa, Kofale, Kore, Adaba, Dodola, Limu-Bilbilo, Robe-Dida’a, and Onkolo-Wabe areas, are primarily composed of by dry evergreen Afro-alpine and grassland complexes. Similarly, the mountainous regions of Adaba-Dodola and Chilalo-Galema are dominated by Afro-alpine vegetation, as well as dry evergreen Afromontane forests and grassland complexes. Common species within this vegetation type include Juniperus procera, Olea europaea subsp. cuspidiata, Podocarpus falcatus, and Eucalyptus.

The Ericaceous belt is located in the southern parts of Adaba-Dodola and the northern parts of Kore and Limu-Bilbilo, situated at lower altitudes within the Afro-alpine zone. Additionally, the southern part of Robe Dida’a and the northern edges of Gasera are characterized by Vachellia-Commiphora woodlands and bushland vegetation. In terms of vegetation distribution within the basin, the dry evergreen Afromontane Forest and grassland complex ranks second, following the Vachellia-Commiphora woodland and bushland vegetation45,46.

Socio-economic characteristics

Crop production, livestock rearing, and income are major components of the socio-economic profile of the study area. Based on projections derived from the 2007 Population and Housing Census, the total population of the study area was estimated to be 1,496,768 in the year 2014.E.C., resulting in a population density of approximately 137.33 persons/km2, which exceeds the national average of 84 persons/km247. Agriculture serves as the primary source of livelihood and dominant economic activity in the study area48. Both crop and livestock production are the main means of household livelihood strategies. Agricultural mechanization and investments are widely practiced in the study area. Government-owned farm and enterprise found in the Upper Wabe Shebele River Basin includes Hunte, Serofta, Heraro, Gara Dela, Tibila, Gofer, and Ardayita.

Methodology

Study area and climate context

The UWSRB exhibits diverse landforms that influence climate patterns, ranging from highlands to lowlands. The basin experiences bimodal rainfall patterns, characterized by the Belg (spring/autumn) season (February–May) and the Kiremt (summer) season (June–September), making it particularly vulnerable to climate variability. Hence, this study depends on the concern of climate change and how it can be determined from historical and future scenarios of precipitation, temperature, and seasonal variation in the basin.

Historical climate data collection and pre-processing

Historically, daily PCP, maximum, and minimum temperatures from 1986 to 2022 were collected from six meteorological stations (Adaba, Hunte, Kofale, Gobessa, Meraro, and Arsi Robe) from the National Meteorological Services of Ethiopia (Table 1). To address missing values in the meteorological data, averages from corresponding months in the other years and data from the nearest meteorological stations were employed. Additional datasets from the Climate Forecast System Reanalysis (CFSR) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) were used to fill the remaining gaps. Meteorological stations having missing data of up to 20–25% were considered suitable for inclusion in the current study, in accordance with Ethiopian National Meteorological Agency (NMA) policies49 and international standards50. All selected stations (Table 1) had missing information ranging from 15 to 23%, which is within the permitted range.

Selection of climate model and data sources

Climate models are crucial tools for enhancing our understanding and ability to predict climate patterns and changes on various timescales. These models help investigate the extent to which observed climate changes can be attributed to natural variability, human-induced factors, or a combination of both16,55. A global climate model (GCM) is a sophisticated mathematical representation of the major components of the Earth’s climate system, the atmosphere, land surface, oceans, and sea ice, as well as the complex interactions between them56. The fundamental driver of long-term climate prediction is the energy balance between these four key components of the climate system. GCMs aim to accurately capture and model this energy balance to project future climate patterns and changes57.

Since 1988, the IPCC has conducted six assessment cycles, producing AR6, the most comprehensive scientific reports on climate change worldwide16,57,58. The CMIP6 model, the latest phase of the CMIP initiative, is selected for the following key reasons. Firstly, CMIP6 models incorporate more comprehensive representations of physical processes and earth system components, and many have higher spatial resolutions, allowing for better representation of regional and local climate features. Secondly, it also includes a broader and more diverse set of future emissions and land-use scenarios, known as Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), enabling a more thorough exploration of potential future climate trajectories and their associated uncertainties. Thirdly, the CMIP6 framework has benefited from lessons learned in previous phases, leading to improved coordination, data standards, and model output accessibility, making CMIP6 data more readily available, consistent, and comparable across modeling centers and research groups16,57. The diverse socio-economic activities and significant land use/land cover changes in the UWSRB show the importance of using the CMIP6 climate modeling framework35.

The study used large-scale atmospheric variables as predictors that were prepared by the National Center for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) and extracted from the Canadian Centre for Climate Modeling and Analysis (CCCMA). Data from the Canadian Centre for Climate Modeling and Analysis (CCCMA) were downloaded from the nearest grid box to the study area using longitude and latitude, and the resulting zipped file (named “All_CMIP6_BOX_015X_35Y”). It includes CanESM5, MPI-ESM1-AR, and NorESM2-MM simulations59–61. Among these, this study adopted the CanESM5 model based on its demonstrated suitability for climate projection in Ethiopia and it models performance evaluation results (Appendix Table 2). A desk review found that CanESM5 has a better ability to capture the spatiotemporal patterns of temperature and PCP in Ethiopian highlands under dynamic topographic influence, agro-ecological variation, and various regional climate change drivers62–64. It consists of the latest and most advanced physical parametrization that can be supported and integrated into CMIP6 standards and enables an all-inclusive approach for future scenario analysis across multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). Moreover, this model is compatible with the establishment of SDSM and CMhyd. Similarly, its systematic biases are thoroughly recorded and modifiable21,59,65,66.

Shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs) and scenario selection

In this study, the future climate change scenario analyses were generated using three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. Each selected scenario has a distinct representation and emission trajectory. Specifically, SSP1-2.6 shows a sustainable pathway with rapid technological adoption, strong climate policies, and low emissions, which keeps global warming below 2 °C. Similarly, SSP2-4.5 reflects a medium climate change-related scenario, where partial emission reductions and moderate socio-economic growth stabilize radiative forcing at medium levels. Contemporary SSP5-8.5 explains a fossil-fuel-intensive, high-emission pathway with weak mitigation, resulting in the most extreme warming projections. Therefore, the selection of the three scenarios indicates the coverage of the complete spectrum of existing climate futures, from low-emission to high-emission patterns. Their inclusion enables a strong climate change impact assessment under diverse climate conditions.

Downscaling approach

Raw GCM outputs are too coarse for local-scale applications, making downscaling essential. Hence, for better data interpretation and correlation, two complementary downscaling approaches were adopted:

Statistical downscaling model (SDSM)

To address the limitations associated with the coarse-resolution data derived from GCMs, dynamical and statistical downscaling techniques are utilized to extract local-scale climate information67–69. Dynamical downscaling methods utilize higher-resolution Regional Climate Models (RCMs) for a selected portion of the globe compared to GCMs42,70. But statistical downscaling (SDS) techniques establish quantitative relationships between large-scale atmospheric variables (predictors) and local surface variables (predictands). Essentially, it uses historical data to identify patterns and statistical connections between broader climate trends and their local indicators42. For this study, SDSM was selected because the statistical nature of the downscaling approach allows for more straightforward quantification of uncertainty in the downscaled projections, which is important for decision-making.

The SDSM is a hybrid downscaling approach that combines regression-based and weather generator techniques to establish statistical relationships between large-scale atmospheric variables (predictors) and local-scale climate variables (predictands) at the study site53. The first step was to identify the most relevant large-scale atmospheric predictors for temperature, and PCP was identified. It was done through correlation analysis and the screening of the available predictor variables. Subsequently, the SDSM model was calibrated using the historical observed data for the predictands alongside the corresponding predictor variables, developing multiple linear regression equations that delineate the relationship between predictors and predictands for the period from 1986 to 2014 for this study. This calibration primarily utilized data from the NCEP, which covers the specified timeframe.

Thirdly, the model was validated by comparing the simulated historical climate variables with the observed data to ensure its ability to reproduce the observed climate characteristics. Then, after screening of variables, model calibration, validation, scenario generation, and trend analysis for future PCP and temperature projections downscaling from global climate models, CanESM5, to the UWSRB were done. Inherent uncertainties in the downscaling process were accounted for by running the SDSM model multiple times, using different sets of predictor variables or model parameter configurations, providing a range of possible future climate projections for assessing the associated uncertainties. With this in SDSM downscaling, the selection of appropriate predictors is one of the most significant points. In this study, the best correlated predictor variables for minimum temperature, maximum temperature, and PCP were selected for each station based on their significance level (Appendix Table 1). A monthly based downscaling process is modeled for maximum and minimum temperature as unconditional, while PCP is modeled as a conditional process.

Hydrologic model (CMhyd)

In the field of hydrology, the CMhyd software has emerged as a valuable tool for the analysis and projection of historical and global climate data. This software proves particularly useful for studies focused on water resources, sedimentation patterns, and streamflow regimes71. Watershed models are often used to assess the impact of future climate change on hydrologic processes22,24,72,73. However, climate model simulations concerning temperature and PCP can have significant biases due to systematic errors or spatial averaging within the model grid cells. These biases, often attributed to systematic errors or spatial averaging within model grid cells, can compromise the accuracy of the data16,56,57,74,75. Consequently, it is inadvisable to utilize raw output from climate models as inputs for hydrological models76,77. To address this critical issue, the present study extracts and performs bias correction on data derived from both global and regional climate models using CMhyd78. The primary objective is to minimize the disparity between observed and simulated climate variables daily.

To secure comparability, spatial resemblance between station locations and climate model grid cells was established. Then, four methods for bias correction of PCP and temperature were selected and implemented during the data analysis phase. Specifically, Linear Scaling (LS), Distribution Mapping (DM), Empirical Quantile Mapping (EQM), and Power Transformation (PT) were used for precipitation, whereas DM, EQM, LS, and variance scaling (VARI) were used for temperature correction through CMhyd software. The proximity of each grid cell centroid to the corresponding station is then evaluated. Finally, a performance assessment is conducted by comparing the observed historical data from the stations with the relevant historical simulations generated by the climate model71.

A method called bias correction adjusts daily climate model predictions by comparing them with real-world observations from a specific time period (Fig. 2). This method serves to intermediate future climate model projections with historical observations. Hence, raw model outputs (TRAW) for future periods are statistically adjusted by incorporating the differences (Δ) between past model simulations (TREF) and observed data (OREF) from a designated historical reference period. This technique refines the model’s projections or output (TBC) to achieve greater alignment with historical climate patterns (Navarro et al. 2020).

It is highly recommended to use an ensemble approach, where bias-corrected data from multiple climate models and downscaling methods are used79,80. This methodology is essential for addressing the uncertainties that are inherent in the process of climate modeling and downscaling processes79. The implementation of bias correction, along with an ensemble-based approach, was applied to improve the reliability of hydrological simulations that are informed by climate model data. This enhancement is crucial for understanding the potential impacts of future climate change on watershed hydrology.

Model calibration and validation framework

Baseline period selection

The study covers a baseline period from 1986 to 2022 for CMhyd and from 1986 to 2014 for SDSM. This timeframe was selected for its importance to climate change observance in the study area. The establishment of the Arsi Bale State Farm in the 1980s, the resettlement of communities, and the commissioning of the Malka Wakana Lake between 1980 and 1988 are significant events that signify the selection of this period. Additionally, significant PCP and temperature data for meteorological stations are available in this timeframe.

Calibration period and validation period

The data from the station is divided into two different periods for the purpose of calibration (2011–2018) and validation (2019–2022) purposes for the CMhyd model. However, 1986–2006 is the calibration period and 2007–2014 is the validation period for the SDSM, as many climate models and datasets, including the NCEP dataset from the CMIP, provided projections and historical data primarily covering the years 1979 to 2014. During this period, significant advancements in SDSM techniques were made, enabling researchers to refine their models and enhance predictive capabilities, which were not as strong in earlier years. As a result, this timeframe limited the temporal scope for downscaling applications. With this, during the calibration of the SDSM, the parameters adjusted or changed include predictor variables, transformation methods, and transformation coefficients. These changes were made to enhance their relevance to local climate conditions and to optimize the model’s outputs. The calibration phase is essential for refining the models, while the validation phase is critical for assessing their accuracy against previously unobserved data81.

Model performance evaluation

In this study, Correlation Coefficient (R), Percent Bias (PBIAS), Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) were used to assess the agreement between the observed data and the model outputs (Eq. 1–4). A value of R close to 1 and -1 indicates strong positive and negative correlation, respectively. A PBIAS of 0% indicates perfect agreement, while positive values indicate overestimation and negative values indicate underestimation. A value of NSE close to 1 indicates a very good match between the observations and the model, while values closer to 0 indicate progressively worse agreement. Climate Scenarios Generation and Future Change Analysis

Scenario framework

This study incorporated future climate projections for temperature and PCP based on the latest data from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Phase 6 (CMIP6). Particularly, CMIP6 demonstrate enhanced resolution and accuracy in comparison to its predecessor, the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, Phase 5 (CMIP5)4,84–86. The new scenarios presented in CMIP6 give more emphasis to socio-economic aspects of climate change81,84,87. Hence, this study employed the latest Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) framework established by the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP) in conjunction with CMIP6. This framework encompasses a range of priority scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5) as well as additional scenarios of interest (SSP1-1.9, SSP4-6.0, SSP4-3.4, and SSP5-3.4), which consider diverse potential future socioeconomic developments and their corresponding climate implications81,87,88.

The focus of this study is to critically assess future climate changes under three key Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. These scenarios represent a range of potential socio-economic and emission pathways, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of prospective climate conditions81. The analysis encompasses both the near-to-medium term (2023–2054) and the long term (2055–2086), with comparisons made against the baseline period of 1986–2022.

Ensemble data generation

This study employed a statistical downscaling technique known as SDSM to analyze climate model outputs. The calibration process of SDSM yielded regression coefficients, which were subsequently applied to time series data derived from the Global Climate Models (GCMs) corresponding to the aforementioned SSPs. This resulted in the generation of twenty independent ensembles of daily climate data encompassing 99 years (1986–2086). To facilitate further analysis and comparisons between the scenarios, the individual SSPs were averaged and transformed into monthly and annual timescales. This processed data serves as valuable input for subsequent impact studies.

Additionally, a complementary scenario analysis was conducted using the CMhyd model, which incorporated a slightly modified baseline period of 1986–2022 for comparative purposes. To achieve a comprehensive understanding, the outputs from both SDSM and CMhyd were strategically combined. This combined dataset, encompassing information from both methodologies, was subsequently presented through a combination of graphs and tables to facilitate effective visualization and comparative analysis.

Spatial analysis of rainfall and temperature in the UWSRB

Spatial analysis of PCP and temperature was done through the technique of interpolation for the UWSRB using ArcGIS 10.7. First, historical data from weather stations is collected, cleaned, and formatted for GIS compatibility. Next, an interpolation method, Kriging, was selected to generate continuous raster surfaces for PCP and temperature, as it effectively captures spatial relationships and accounts for spatial autocorrelation, treating nearby measurements as more similar. It also provides uncertainty estimates and can incorporate secondary variables like elevation, enhancing accuracy. Hence, Kriging’s flexibility makes it the preferred method for mapping climatic variables in the UWSRB.

The resulting thematic maps visualize spatial patterns and trends and can incorporate future climate change projections to assess impacts on water resources and agriculture. Validating these maps against independent data ensures reliability, enabling decision-makers to leverage insights for effective water management and agricultural planning.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data on climate change experience were collected from various stakeholders and community representatives on change perceptions through key informant interviews (KIIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) using purposive sampling to assist data interpretation and result validation. Hence, six FGDs were employed in 18 districts covered by the study, in which there was one FGD per three districts, with 10 participants in each group that represented various groups, elders, experts, and officials. This allowed us to get information on climate change, the accuracy and the interpretation approaches. Additionally, 54 KIIs were employed, three per district, from elders, experts, and officials were used for available data management and result interpretation. Across the four seasons of Ethiopia, one field visit was undertaken to estimate and validate the results and level of data accuracy. Results and discussions

Evaluation of downscaling method performance

In this study, a detailed comparative assessment of the SDSM and CMhyd models were formulated to identify the most reliable tool for generating high-resolution climate projections. This was incorporated examining the performance of each downscaling method using calibration and validation of model results. Likewise, the results of both models were compared pre and post bias correction in detail in the following sections.

SDSM and CMhyd calibration and validation

The baseline data used for the evaluation of the SDSM covers the period between 1986 and 2014, focusing on PCP, as well as maximum and minimum temperature (Tmax and Tmin). Within this baseline period, two-thirds (1986–2006) was used for model calibration, while the remaining one-third (2007–2014) was used for model validation. To verify the calibrated model, twenty mean ensembles of synthetic daily weather series were generated using NCEP-reanalysis data. The statistical parameters (r2, Pbias, NSE, and MAE) used for evaluating SDSM performance are listed in Table 2. In the statistical downscaling model, the sensitivity of the parameter was identified based on the partial r values. Additionally, across all stations, the EQM (Empirical Quantile Mapping) method consistently outperforms the other bias correction methods PT (Polynomial Transformation), DM (Delta Method), and LS (Linear Scaling) in terms of R2, NSE, and MAE, while also maintaining low PBIAS values. This indicates that EQM is the most sensitive parameter for bias correction in the examined hydrological models, effectively enhancing overall model performance. The sensitivity analysis highlights that EQM should be prioritized in future hydrological modeling efforts due to its superior effectiveness. In contrast, the other methods demonstrate varying levels of effectiveness, but none consistently match the performance of EQM, underscoring its critical role in improving the accuracy of hydrological predictions.

Performance evaluation and bias correction for temperature.

In terms of R2, the bias corrected PCP and temperature were able to capture the monthly distribution of these variables when compared to the raw data from CMhyd. Notably, SDSM outperformed in comparisons involving bias-corrected PCP and temperature, with exception of the bias-corrected outputs by the LS method. The least performer of bias corrected methods was EQM among utilized bias-correction techniques. The simulated and bias-corrected PCP performed in consistent with the findings of92. The R2 values for SDSM prior to bias correction by CMhyd ranged from 0.88 to 0.98, while those for the raw PCP and temperature ranged from 0.67 to 0.74. Following bias correction, the R2 values were estimated to be between 0.74 and 0.88, 0.78 and 0.89, 0.81 and 0.91, and 0.88 and 0.96 for the EQM, PT, DM and LS methods, respectively. As a result, the performance evaluation of the bias corrected data, in terms of R2, showed a decreasing order from LS to DM, PT, and finally to EQM (Table 3). Additionally, the error-based bias correction also reveals similar sequence of results concerning the R2 values across each bias correction method.

The analysis indicates that bias-corrected PCP data outperformed performance relative to raw PCP and temperature data, in terms of R2, MAE, Pbias, and NSE (Table 3). This finding suggests that bias-corrected PCP provides a more accurate representation of the observed data. Specifically, the Pbias values for bias-corrected PCP ranged from 0.02 to 0.08, compared to 0.06 to 0.49 observed for raw precipitation. A lower Pbias values signifies an enhanced capability of the model to reproduce the observed distribution of precipitation. Furthermore, the NSE for bias-corrected PCP ranged from 0.61 to 0.92, whereas raw PCP produced NSE values between 0.39 to 0.51. A higher NSE indicates a greater proficiency of the model in simulating the observed variability in precipitation. Additionally, the MAE for bias-corrected PCP ranged from 0.24 to 0.49, compared to a range of 1.05 to 1.63 for raw PCP (Table 3). A lower MAE reflects the model’s improved accuracy in predicting observed PCP values. Collectively, these results suggest the reliability of bias-corrected PCP is a more reliable data source for applications such as climate change modeling and hydrological forecasting.

The temperature was analyzed using SDSM through the same key performance metrics indicated above for precipitation. Most stations display high R2 values (0.84 to 0.93), indicating a strong fit between predicted and observed temperatures, which are maintained post bias correction. NSE values improve significantly post-correction, enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy. PBIAS values show a reduction, indicating less bias, while MAE values decrease, reflecting improved precision in temperature predictions.

Station-specific reality reveal varying degrees of performance, with Kofele showing the highest R2 (0.93) and NSE (0.88) (Table 4). Adaba and Hunte also show strong results, with notable improvements post-correction. Meraro and Gobessa demonstrate enhanced reliability post bias correction, while Arsi Robe maintains consistent performance. Overall, the bias correction applied through CMhyd significantly enhances the SDSM’s predictive capabilities across all stations, underscoring the importance of bias correction in refining the accuracy of statistical downscaling models for climate-related applications.

Selection of the preferred downscaling approach

The Statistical Downscaling Model (SDSM) was selected the most appropriate method for generating future climate projections after evaluating its performance through calibration and validation approach, with comparison of SDSM and CMhyd pre and post bias correction. This resulted from statistically better performance of SDSM in reproducing observed historical climate data. The results showed that R2 values for SDSM ranged from 0.88 to 0.98, although they were much higher than the raw results from the CMhyd tool prior to bias correction, which varied between 0.67 and 0.74 for all stations and variables.

Bias correction approaches (EQM, PT, DM, and LS) improved CMhyd outputs, with the LS method obtaining R2 values up to 0.96. However, these findings did not exceed the baseline accuracy of SDSM. The strength of SDSM was further assessed using error statistics: Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) values above 0.7 throughout both calibration and validation, suggesting good predictive capability, while PBIAS values stayed below 7%, indicating minimal systematic error. SDSM was selected as the main downscaling strategy for this investigation based on this result. This reduced uncertainty and improved the legitimacy of later impact assessments and adaptation planning by guaranteeing the production of trustworthy, station-specific, high-resolution climate forecasts under CMIP6 SSP scenarios for the Upper Wabe Shebele River Basin.

Historical climate analysis (baseline period)

Analyzing historical monthly precipitation distribution and variability

Comparative analyses of the historical monthly mean PCP encompassing observed, simulated (SDSM-output), and bias-corrected (CMhyd-output) in the study area show a better degree agreement. Specifically, the monthly PCP values were 117.5 mm for observed data, 115.26 mm for simulated data, and 105.33 mm for bais-corrected data. In the month of August, the stations Meraro, Adaba, and Arsi Robe recorded the highest PCP, with observed, simulated, and corrected values of 300 mm, 292 mm, and 256 mm, respectively (Fig. 4a–f). Particularly, the simulated mean monthly PCP was slightly higher than the observed values. The lowest PCP values were documented in December for all three data types at the Meraro station.

Spatial and temporal distribution of historical mean monthly PCP in the UWSRB (1986–2022).

Generally, the majority of stations showed the highest monthly PCP during the wet season, which occurs from July to September, while the lowest monthly PCP was recorded during the dry season, occuring from December to January (Fig. 4a–f). These observations are consistent with the findings of93, which reported that the Kombolcha and Dessie stations received maximum PCP amounts of 280.4 mm and 318.7 mm in August and July, respectively. Additionally,94 reported a maximum of 374.11 mm for July and a minimum of less than 12.3 mm for January. The long-term mean monthly PCP in the central highlands of Ethiopia ranged from a low of 9.9 mm in December to high of 242.1 mm in August95. Furthermore, the maximum PCP observed for Choke in Ethiopia about 300 mm, 290 mm, and 160 mm for July, August and September, repectively96. These findings consistent with other studies indicating that the months of July to September and April to May are characterized by the highest PCP95. The observed mean monthly PCP in Ethiopia typically ranges from 100 to 140 mm97, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. In contrast, a semi-arid region in the Borana zone of southern Ethiopia received a mean monthly PCP of 39.19 mm between 1981 and 201898.

The mean monthly PCP distribution from the period 1986 to 2022 was observed using a box-whisker plot. The boxes indicate the lower and upper quartiles, while the solid and dotted lines within the boxes denote the mean and median values, respectively. The whiskers indicate the minimum and maximum PCP values, and the dots represent outliers. The analysis reveals two distinct seasonal patterns in PCP distribution in the study area: the primary rainy season occurs from July to September, and the secondary rainy season ranges from March to May. The driest months are identified as December to February (Fig. 4 g–l). These study findings regarding bimodal PCP patterns are in agreement with previous studies99. In Ethiopia, the observed monomodal PCP in the northern regions and bimodal peaks in the southern regions are attributed to the latitudinal oscillation of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), which shifts between 15°N in July and 15°S in January100,101.

The months of January to February and November to December show significant uniformity, as evidenced by the consistently short box sizes. In contrast, the months from March to October display longer boxes and whiskers, indicating greater variability in PCP amounts during this period. The peak variability is observed in April, characterized by the longest upper whisker, which indicates a wider range of PCP values101. In contrast, the least variation in PCP is noted in January, February, November, and December, where the boxes are narrower, indicating minimal variation within each quartile and suggesting consistent PCP during these months102.

Despite the general seasonal trends, outliers are present in all months and stations, with August showing the highest number of outliers’ observations for all stations, suggesting increased unpredictability in PCP during this period. This observation is consistent with a study that found the annual and seasonal trends and spatial distribution of PCP in Ethiopia Cheung et al.103 and in line with the findings of104. In the Tana Lake basin, the monthly mean PCP variability ranged between 90 and 128 mm, with distribution patterns similar to the present study94.

At the Meraro station, the whiskers for April and May are much longer than those for December and January, suggesting a wider variability in PCP during the wet season. This is in line with the relatively high PCP during the Belga and Kiremt seasons, which is responsible for 50% of water utilization for agricultural activities105. At the Gobessa station, there is considerable variability in the amount of PCP that falls from month to month. For example, the whisker for April is much longer than the whisker for December, which means that there is a much wider range of PCP values in April. At the Arsi Robe station, a few outliers were observed, particularly in August and September (Fig. 4g–l). These represent months where the PCP amount was much higher than usual. In line with this,62,90,106 also reported similar findings.

Both models show increasing trend in temperature under baseline climate data analysis from 1986 to 2014 and 1986 to 2022 for SDSM and CMhyd, respectively. In Ethiopia, climate change modeling for East Africa indicates an increasing trend in annual PCP in the northern and western regions, while a decreasing trend is noted in central and eastern parts of the country117.

Second, utilizing both datasets for projections and scenario analysis can complicate interpretation, making it essential to focus on a single model.

The implications for surface and groundwater interactions are critical. In the study area, the anticipated decline in PCP could lead to reduced recharge rates for groundwater, exacerbating water scarcity in an already vulnerable basin. As PCP becomes less reliable and more variable, surface water resources may also diminish, impacting agricultural productivity and overall water availability. The interconnectedness of these systems highlights the urgent need for adaptive management strategies to mitigate the impacts of climate change on water resources.

Spatiotemporal dynamics of climate variables

Seasonal dynamics of PCP and temperature

In the Bega season, PCP levels are relatively low, with Gobessa recording the highest at 19.62 mm, while Meraro has the lowest at 12.79 mm. These low PCP amounts indicate that the Bega season may not provide sufficient moisture for agricultural activities, posing challenges for farmers relying on this period for crop growth. Conversely, the Belg season experiences a significant increase in PCP, showed by Arsi Robe, which receives 38.63 mm, and Kofele, which records 39.08 mm. Adaba and Gobessa also show notable precipitation, with 25.33 mm and 32.00 mm, respectively. This PCP is essential for supporting crops and replenishing water supplies after the drier Bega season. The Kiremt season is characterized by substantial PCP, particularly in Adaba, which receives a remarkable 119.12 mm. Other stations, such as Hunte and Kofele, also report high levels of PCP, with 110.03 mm and 102.58 mm, respectively. This season is critical for agriculture, as it provides the necessary moisture for crops during the main growing period (Table 5).

The interaction between surface and groundwater during these seasons is critical. Low PCP during the Bega season can lead to decreased groundwater recharge, adversely affecting water availability for irrigation. Conversely, the higher PCP in the Belg and Kiremt seasons enhances both surface water and groundwater levels, supporting agricultural productivity and ensuring sustainable water resources for farming.

Additionally, Temperature data reveals important variations across the stations. In the Bega season, minimum temperatures are relatively warm compared to other regions, with Hunte recording the lowest Tmin at 11.68 °C and Adaba at 11.44 °C. These milder temperatures may allow for a wider range of crops to be cultivated, although the overall PCP remains a limiting factor. Mean temperatures show variation across the seasons, with Arsi Robe experiencing a higher mean temperature of 17.45 °C during Bega, suggesting conditions that could support crop growth. However, the Bega season generally has lower mean temperatures, which might affect crop development negatively. Maximum temperatures are notably higher during the Belg season, especially in Arsi Robe, which reaches a Tmax of 22.68 °C. This increase in temperature can stress crops if not complemented by adequate PCP, while the Kiremt season shows lower Tmax values, which can help mitigate heat stress (Table 5).

These temperature variations have important implications for surface and groundwater interactions. Milder temperatures during the Bega season could enhance evaporation rates, potentially reducing surface water availability and impacting groundwater recharge. In contrast, the higher temperatures in the Belg season necessitate adequate PCP to prevent crop stress, emphasizing the interconnectedness of temperature, precipitation, and both surface and groundwater resources. The lower Tmax in the Kiremt season can further support groundwater stability, ensuring that crops receive adequate moisture during critical growth periods.

Implications of a changing climate

Based on desk reviews, field observations, focus group discussions, and informant interviews, several key factors were identified as affecting climate change in the study area. Deforestation, topography, and proximity to the equator were found to be the most important factors. The basin has diverse topographical features, including mountains, valleys, and plains, and its location close to the equator, so it is affected by the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), Beshir et al. (2023). Changes in land use and land cover, such as converting forests and water bodies to agricultural land, were also identified as a major driver of climate change in the study area35,36.

Decreasing PCP can lead to imbalances in the hydrological cycle. This can result in changes to the timing, intensity, and distribution of PCP, affecting the replenishment of surface water and groundwater resources. This increases vulnerability to drought, which is in agreement with29,128–131. This study illustrates distinct seasonal climate patterns, with the Bega season generally being drier, the Belg season providing essential PCP for agriculture, and the Kiremt season being critical for replenishing water supplies and supporting crop growth. Understanding these climatic variations is vital for effective agricultural planning and water resource management in Ethiopia. Regions that experience higher PCP during the Kiremt season can focus on water-intensive crops, while those with lower PCP may need to adopt drought-resistant varieties.

Uncertainties and limitations

Uncertainties are present in this investigation regarding scenario selection, model structures, and observational data. Uneven spatial distribution, limited station coverage, and missing values all limit observational data in the UWSRB. Although gap-filling methods such as monthly mean replacement and inverse distance weighting were used, they were unable to properly capture fine scale variability, which could affect model calibration and validation accuracy. Model-related uncertainty persists. While SDSM exceeded CMhyd in replicating historical climate data and was selected for projection, both techniques simplify complicated atmospheric processes and are susceptible to biases introduced by the main GCM. Although numerous bias correction strategies increased CMhyd performance, the results did not consistently outperform SDSM, demonstrating the reliance of downscaling outcomes on methodological selection. Furthermore, variation in climate sensitivity among SSP scenarios, which are connected to different representations of radiative forcing, government policies, cloud feedbacks, population growth, and land–atmosphere interactions, increase uncertainty to regional forecasts11.

Additionally, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 introduces scenario uncertainty. These represent a conceivable range of scenarios but do not account for all possible socioeconomic pathways. Similarly, the relatively short calibration and validation periods, which are limited by data availability, may reduce temporal representativeness. Despite these limitations, rigorous model evaluation and the integration of multiple scenarios produce credible, high-resolution projections that can inform UWSRB adaptation strategies, emphasizing the need for improved observational networks and continued refinement of regional climate modeling. Beyond these methodological limitations, uncertainties persist regarding the physical factors that influence forecasted wetness and dryness in East Africa. Evidence suggests that warming is intensifying land–atmosphere feedbacks, altering monsoon circulation, and modifying large-scale climate drivers including ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole132–134‚135‚136. These fluctuations are not fully reflected in statistical downscaling, raising questions about the reliability of rainfall and drought estimates. Despite these constraints, the inclusion of several scenarios and careful model evaluation yields reliable, high-resolution estimates for UWSRB, emphasizing the importance of enhanced observational networks, sensitivity assessments, and the incorporation of physical mechanisms into regional models.

Conclusions and future direction

In the study area, the period between 1986 and 2022 showed changing patterns for temperature and precipitation. Monthly and annual variations of both parameters have been observed for the last three decades. The point of this PCP is that it is decreasing, whereas temperature increases on a monthly and annual basis. The monthly mean of the PCP showed a decreasing trend, with a maximum at a base period of 300 mm and a maximum PCP projection of 247 mm, with differences of 53 mm, while the mean annual PCP illustrates decreasing trends of 35.89% on average compared to the baseline of 1197.26 mm for the study area. Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, PCP is projected to decrease by 41.76% in the 2040s and 50.33% in the 2070s, while under the SSP1-2.6 scenario, the decrease is estimated to be 21.19% in the 2040s and 30.25% in the 2070s. The projected decrease ranges from 21.19% to 50.33%, highlighting the significant impact of future climate change in the study area. Likewise, in the study area, the three scenarios and two break timeframes of mean annual temperature for future projection show an increasing trend by 0.89 °C, 1.2 °C, 1.94 °C, 2.21 °C, 2.42 °C, and 3.6 °C using SSP1-2.6 (2040s), SSP2-4.5 (2040s), SSP1-2.6 (2070s), SSP5-8.5 (2040s), SSP2-4.5 (2070s), and SSP5-8.5(2070s), respectively. The future mean annual temperature of the study area will be increased by 2.03 ºC (18.04 ºC projected mean) compared to the historical mean annual temperature of 16 ºC for the study area. Accordingly, the future direction is indicated as expanding the study to an ensemble model and considering more details in the study.
