High-Order Flux Splitting Schemes for the Euler Equations of Gas Dynamics
Shaoshuai Chu, Michael Herty, Eleuterio F. Toro

Abstract
We develop high-order flux splitting schemes for the one- and two-dimensional Euler equations of gas dynamics. The proposed schemes are high-order extensions of the existing first-order flux splitting schemes introduced in [ E. F. Toro, M. E. Vázquez-Cendón , Comput. & Fluids, 70 (2012), pp. 1–12], where the Euler equations of gas dynamics are split into two subsystems: the advection and pressure systems. In this paper, we formulate the TV splitting within the semi-discrete framework to extend it to higher orders of accuracy for the first time. The second-order extension is obtained by using piecewise linear interpolant to reconstruct the one-sided point values of the unknowns. The third- and fifth-order schemes are developed using the finite-difference alternative weighted essentially non-oscillatory (A-WENO) framework, which is particularly effective in handling multidimensional problems and provides a more straightforward approach to constructing higher-order WENO schemes. These extensions significantly improve the resolution of discontinuities and the accuracy of numerical solutions, as demonstrated by a series of numerical experiments of both the one- and two-dimensional Euler equations of gas dynamics.



Key words:
Flux splitting schemes, Euler equations of gas dynamics, advection system, pressure system, A-WENO schemes

Introduction

This paper focuses on numerical solutions of the Euler equations of gas dynamics, which in the one-dimensional
(1-D) case, read as

\bm{U}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{U})_{x}=\bm{0},

(1.1)

with

\bm{U}:=(\rho,\rho u,E)^{\top},\quad{\rm and}\quad\bm{F}(\bm{U})=(\rho u,\rho u^{2}+p,u(E+p))^{\top},

(1.2)

where
t
is the time,
x
is the spatial variable,
\rho
,
u
,
p
, and
E
are the density, velocity, pressure, and total energy, respectively. The system is completed through the following equations of state (EOS):

p=(\gamma-1)\Big[E-{\frac{1}{2}}\rho u^{2}\Big],

(1.3)

where the parameter
\gamma
represents the specific heat ratio.

It is well-known that equations (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) model a wide range of physical phenomena such as shock waves, turbulence, and compressible flows. These systems often involve complex wave patterns, including discontinuities like shocks and rarefactions, even when the initial data are very smooth, which presents significant challenges for numerical methods. Traditional approaches, particularly first-order methods, may struggle to resolve these discontinuities accurately, often requiring very fine meshes to achieve satisfactory results.

Since the pioneering works of
[
17
,
34
,
19
]
, numerous methods have been developed to solve hyperbolic systems like the one in (
1.1
); see, e.g., the monographs and review papers
[
24
,
35
,
28
,
47
,
55
,
4
]
and the references therein. Here, we focus on flux splitting methods, which are fundamental in computational fluid dynamics due to their ability to decompose fluxes into components corresponding to different wave families, such as shock, contact, and expansion waves. This decomposition enhances the resolution of shock waves and discontinuities, which is crucial for accurate simulations of compressible flows; see, e.g.,
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
29
,
67
]
. While classical flux splitting schemes (e.g.,
[
2
,
1
,
50
,
62
,
61
]
) are effective, they often struggle to resolve intermediate characteristic fields, leading to excessive dissipation or numerical artifacts. To overcome these limitations, more advanced methods, such as the advection upstream splitting method (AUSM), have been developed (see, e.g.,
[
37
]
) offering better handling of contact waves and improved resolution of wave patterns. AUSM has since garnered considerable attention and undergone refinement, with further developments
[

,
39
,
40

. Additionally, a flux splitting approach similar to AUSM was proposed in

,

,

, further enriching the field of computational fluid dynamics.

Recently, a new flux splitting method, known as the TV splitting scheme, was introduced for the 1-D Euler equations (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) in
[
60
]
and later extended to higher dimensions in
[
56

, where the system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) has been divided into two subsystems: the advection and pressure systems. These subsystems are analyzed, and corresponding Godunov-type discretization schemes are formulated. The proposed schemes are characterized by their simplicity, robustness, and accuracy, offering significant improvements over existing flux splitting methods. In particular, they effectively capture contact and shear waves while precisely preserving isolated stationary contacts. Beyond its application to high-dimensional Euler equations, it has also been extended to other systems, including magnetohydrodynamics and shallow water equations; see, e.g.,
[
56
,
3
,
13
,

,

The TV splitting schemes introduced in
[
60
,
56
]
offer a robust and accurate first-order approach for solving the Euler equations of gas dynamics. However, their accuracy is inherently limited. These methods typically require very fine meshes to achieve acceptable resolution for shock waves and other important wave structures. As the complexity increases, higher-order methods are necessary to improve the resolution and reduce the computational cost associated with fine grids. In this paper, we extend these schemes to higher orders of accuracy—second, third, and fifth—in the semi-discrete framework for the first time. The second-order extension is achieved through applying piecewise linear interpolant to reconstruct the one-sided point values of the unknowns, while third- and fifth-order schemes are developed within the finite-difference (FD) alternative essentially non-oscillatory (A-WENO) framework. This framework has been proven to be a powerful tool for generalizing low-order finite-volume (FV) schemes to higher-order FD ones, particularly in multidimensional cases. Its “dimension-by-dimension” reconstruction process simplifies the development of higher-order WENO schemes; see, e.g.,
[
42
,
65
,
64
,
63
,
27
,
26
]
.

The proposed TV splitting schemes are applied to both 1-D and two-dimensional (2-D) Euler equations of gas dynamics. We test the first-, second-, third-, and fifth-order versions of these schemes on various numerical examples. As expected, the resolution improves with higher-order schemes used, especially when transiting from the first-order scheme to the second-order one. Additionally, we compare the studied TV splitting schemes with the Central-Upwind (CU) schemes (see, e.g.,
[
33
,
32
,
31
]
), or HLL schemes (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
), and the HLLC schemes (see, e.g.,
[
58

), demonstrating the high efficiency of the proposed methods.

One-Dimensional TV Splitting Schemes

In this section, we first describe the first-order TV splitting scheme for the 1-D Euler equations (
1.1
)–(

) from
[

]

2.1
1-D First-Order TV Splitting Schemes: A Brief Overview

Supposing the computational domain is covered with uniform cells
C_{j}:=[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}]
with
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta x
centered at
x_{j}=(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}+x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})/2
,
\,j=1,\ldots,N
, we assume that the cell average values

\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}(t):\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int\limits_{C_{j}}\bm{U}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x

are available at a certain time level
t
. Note that all of the indexed quantities are time-dependent, but from here on, we suppress the time-dependence of all of the indexed quantities for the sake of brevity.

The computed cell averages
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}
of the 1-D system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) are evolved in time by solving the following semi-discrete system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs):

\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x},

(2.1)

where
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)
is the numerical flux, defined by

\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)=\bm{{\cal F}}^{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)+\bm{{\cal F}}^{P}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big).

(2.2)

with
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
being the left/right-sided point values of
\bm{U}
at the cell interfaces
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
. For the first order scheme, we take
\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{U}_{j+1}
and
\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{U}_{j}
.

Here,
\bm{{\cal F}}^{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)
is the advection flux given by

\bm{{\cal F}}^{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)=\begin{cases}u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[3.44444pt]
(\rho u)^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[3.44444pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}\rho^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(u^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\end{pmatrix},&\mbox{if }u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0,\\[34.44434pt]
u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\rho^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[3.44444pt]
(\rho u)^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[3.44444pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}\rho^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(u^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\end{pmatrix},&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

and
\bm{{\cal F}}^{P}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)
is the pressure flux given by

\bm{{\cal F}}^{P}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{+}\big)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\[3.44444pt]
p^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\\[7.74998pt]
\dfrac{\gamma u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}p^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{\gamma-1}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle u^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}u^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}u^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}-\frac{2}{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}(p^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-p^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle p^{*}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}p^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}p^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{2(C^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-C^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})}(u^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-u^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\pm\sqrt{\big(u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}+4\big(c^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}}\bigg),

with

u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\dfrac{(\rho u)^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}},\,\,\,p^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=(\gamma-1)\Big(E^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(u^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\Big),\,\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\,c^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{{\gamma p^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}/{\rho^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}}.

2.2
1-D Second-Order TV Splitting Scheme

We now extend the first-order TV splitting scheme introduced in §
2.1
to the second order of accuracy. The resulting scheme (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) achieves second-order accuracy provided that the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
, used to compute the numerical flux
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
in (
2.2
), are second-order of accurate. To this end, we approximate
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
using a piecewise linear interpolant

\widetilde{\bm{U}}(x)=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}+(\bm{U}_{x})_{j}(x-x_{j}),\quad x\in C_{j},

(2.4)

which leads to

\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{U}_{x})_{j},\quad\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{U}_{x})_{j+1}.

(2.5)

In order to ensure the reconstruction (
2.4
)–(
2.5
) is non-oscillatory, one needs to compute the slopes
(\bm{U}_{x})_{j}
in (
2.4
) with the help of a
nonlinear limiter. In all of the numerical experiments reported in §
4
, we have used a generalized minmod limiter

[
36
,
44
,
51
]
:

(\bm{U}_{x})_{j}={\rm minmod}\left(\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j-1}}{\Delta x},\,\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j-1}}{2\Delta x},\,\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j}}{\Delta x}\right),\quad\theta\in[1,2],

(2.6)

applied in a component-wise manner. Here, the minmod function is defined as

{\rm minmod}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots):=\begin{cases}\min_{j}\{z_{j}\}&\mbox{if}~z_{j}>0\quad\forall\,j,\\
\max_{j}\{z_{j}\}&\mbox{if}~z_{j}<0\quad\forall\,j,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.7)

The parameter
\theta
in (
2.6
) is used to control the amount of numerical viscosity present in the resulting scheme, and larger values of
\theta
correspond to sharper but, in general, more oscillatory reconstructions. In this paper, we use
\theta=1.3
.

2.3
1-D Third-Order TV Splitting Schemes

In this section, we extend the second-order TV splitting scheme introduced in §
2.2
to the third-order of accuracy in the framework of the finite-difference A-WENO scheme introduced in
[
27
]
(see also
[
42
,
65
,
63
,
64
]
), which has been proven to be a powerful tool for generalizing low-order FV schemes to higher-order FD ones.

Following
[
27
]
, the point values
\bm{U}_{j}
are evolved in time by solving the following system of ODEs:

\frac{{\rm d}\bm{U}_{j}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{{\bm{H}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}-{\bm{H}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}}{\Delta x},

(2.8)

where
{\bm{H}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}
is the (third-order accurate) numerical flux defined by

{\bm{H}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}=\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{24}(\Delta x)^{2}(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}.

Here,
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the FV numerical flux as in (
2.2
) and
(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the higher-order correction term used to increase the order of the numerical flux. The correction term
(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
can be approximated using the standard central FDs:

(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2(\Delta x)^{2}}\big[{\bm{F}}_{j-1}-{\bm{F}}_{j}-{\bm{F}}_{j+1}+{\bm{F}}_{j+2}\big].

We stress that the resulting scheme is third-order once the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
employed to compute the numerical flux
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
are third-order accurate. This can be done by implementing a certain nonlinear limiting procedure like the third-order WENO-type interpolation (see, e.g,
[
64
,
65
,
7
]
) applied to the local characteristic variables; see Appendix
A
for a detailed explanation.

2.4
1-D Fifth-Order TV Splitting Schemes

According to
[
27
]
, to achieve fifth-order accuracy, the point values
\bm{U}_{j}
are evolved in time by solving the system (
2.8
) with the (fifth-order accurate) numerical flux

{\bm{H}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}=\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{24}(\Delta x)^{2}(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{7}{5760}(\Delta x)^{4}(\bm{F}_{xxxx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}},

where
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the FV numerical flux as in (
2.2
),
(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
and
(\bm{F}_{xxxx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
are the higher-order correction terms computed by the fourth- and second-order accurate FDs, respectively; see, e.g.,
[
65
,
63
,
11
,
6
,
9
]
. Here, we have used the following
higher-order correction terms:

\displaystyle(\bm{F}_{xx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{48(\Delta x)^{2}}\Big[-5\bm{F}_{j-2}+39\bm{F}_{j-1}-34\bm{F}_{j}-34\bm{F}_{j+1}+39\bm{F}_{j+2}-5\bm{F}_{j+3}\Big],

\displaystyle(\bm{F}_{xxxx})_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2(\Delta x)^{4}}\Big[\bm{F}_{j-2}-3\bm{F}_{j-1}+2\bm{F}_{j}+2\bm{F}_{j+1}-3\bm{F}_{j+2}+\bm{F}_{j+3}\Big],

where
\bm{F}_{j}:=\bm{F}(\bm{U}_{j})
. In order to ensure the resulting scheme is fifth order, the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
employed to compute the numerical flux
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
need to be at least fifth-order accurate. This can be done by using a certain nonlinear limiting procedure like the fifth-order WENO-Z interpolation from
[
12
,
18
,
27
,
42
,
65
]
applied to the local characteristic variables; see Appendix
B
for details.

Remark 2.1

Note that we extend the first-order TV splitting schemes to third- and fifth-order accuracy in the framework of FD A-WENO methods. It is also easy to extend it to even higher-order accuracy in this framework; see, e.g.
[
18
]
.

3
Two-Dimensional Schemes

In this section, we first briefly overview the first-order TV splitting scheme for the 2-D Euler equations of gas dynamics and then extend it to the higher order of accuracy.

The 2-D Euler equations of gas dynamics read as

\bm{U}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{U})_{x}+\bm{G}(\bm{U})_{y}=\bm{0},

(3.1)

with
\bm{U}:=(\rho,\rho u,\rho v,E)^{\top}
,
\bm{F}(\bm{U})=(\rho u,\rho u^{2}+p,\rho uv,u(E+p))^{\top}
, and
\bm{G}(\bm{U})=(\rho v,\rho uv,\rho v^{2}+p,v(E+p))^{\top}
. Here
v
is the
y
-velocity and the rest of the notations are the same as in the 1-D case (
1.2
)–(
1.3
). The system is completed through the following
EOS:

p=(\gamma-1)\Big[E-\frac{\rho}{2}(u^{2}+v^{2})\Big].

(3.2)

3.1
2-D First-Order TV Splitting Schemes

Supposing that the computational domain is covered with uniform cells
C_{j,\,k}:=[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}]\times[y_{k-\frac{1}{2}},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}]
centered at

(x_{j},y_{k})=\big((x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}+x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})/2
,
(y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}+y_{k-\frac{1}{2}})/2\big)
with
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta x
and
y_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-y_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv\Delta y
for all
j,k
, we
assume that the cell averages

\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}(t):\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x\Delta y}\int\limits_{C_{j,k}}\bm{U}(x,y,t)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

are available at a certain time level
t
. The cell averages
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}
are then evolved in time by numerically solving the following system of ODEs:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}}{\Delta x}-\frac{\bm{{\cal G}}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-\bm{{\cal G}}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k-\frac{1}{2}}}}{\Delta y}.

(3.3)

Here,
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big(\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{-},\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{+}\big)
and
\bm{{\cal G}}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big(\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{+}\big)
are the numerical fluxes, defined by

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big(\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{-},\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{+}\big)=\bm{{\cal F}}^{A}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big(\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{-},\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{+}\big)+\bm{{\cal F}}^{P}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}\big(\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{-},\bm{U}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}^{+}\big),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal G}}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big(\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{+}\big)=\bm{{\cal G}}^{A}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big(\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{+}\big)+\bm{{\cal G}}^{P}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\big(\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{-},\bm{U}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}^{+}\big),

where
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are the left/right-sided point values of
\bm{U}
at the cell interfaces
(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}},y_{k})
and
(x_{j},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}})
, respectively. In the first-order scheme, we take
\bm{U}^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\bm{U}_{j+1,k}
,
\bm{U}^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\bm{U}_{j,k+1}
, and
\bm{U}^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\bm{U}^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\bm{U}_{j,k}

Here,

3.2
2-D Second-Order TV Splitting Scheme

As in the 1-D case, the resulting scheme (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) is second-order accurate once the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
employed to compute the numerical fluxes (
3.4
) are second order. To this end, we approximate
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
by

\widetilde{\bm{U}}(x,y)=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}+(\bm{U}_{x})_{j,k}(x-x_{j})+(\bm{U}_{y})_{j,k}(y-y_{k}),\quad x\in C_{j,k},

which leads to

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{-}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{U}_{x})_{j,k},\quad\bm{U}^{+}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1,k}-\frac{\Delta x}{2}(\bm{U}_{x})_{j+1,k},

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{-}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}+\frac{\Delta y}{2}(\bm{U}_{y})_{j,k},\quad\bm{U}^{+}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1,k}-\frac{\Delta y}{2}(\bm{U}_{y})_{j+1,k},

where

\displaystyle(\bm{U}_{x})_{j,k}={\rm minmod}\left(\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j-1,k}}{\Delta x},\,\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1,k}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j-1,k}}{2\Delta x},\,\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+1,k}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}}{\Delta x}\right),

\displaystyle(\bm{U}_{y})_{j,k}={\rm minmod}\left(\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k-1}}{\Delta y},\,\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k+1}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k-1}}{2\Delta y},\,\theta\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k+1}-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{U}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j,k}}{\Delta y}\right).

Here, the minmod function is defined by (

).

3.3
2-D Third-Order TV Splitting Schemes

Following
[
27
]
, the point values
\bm{U}_{j,k}
are evolved in time by solving the following system of ODEs:

\frac{{\rm d}\bm{U}_{j,k}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{{\bm{H}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}-{\bm{H}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}}}{\Delta x}-\frac{{\bm{H}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}-{\bm{H}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}}{\Delta y},

(3.7)

where the numerical fluxes
\bm{H}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{H}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are defined by

{\bm{H}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}}=\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}-\frac{1}{24}(\Delta x)^{2}(\bm{F}_{xx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k},\quad{\bm{H}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}=\bm{{\cal G}}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}-\frac{1}{24}(\Delta y)^{2}(\bm{G}_{yy})_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}.

Here,
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
{\bm{G}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}
are the FV numerical fluxes as in (
3.4
),
(\bm{F}_{xx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
(\bm{G}_{yy})_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are the higher-order correction terms computed by the standard central FDs:

\displaystyle(\bm{F}_{xx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\frac{1}{12(\Delta x)^{2}}\big[\bm{F}_{j-1,k}-\bm{F}_{j,k}-\bm{F}_{j+1,k}+\bm{F}_{j+2,k}\big],

\displaystyle(\bm{G}_{yy})_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{12(\Delta y)^{2}}\big[\bm{G}_{j,k-1}-\bm{G}_{j,k}-\bm{G}_{j,k+1}+\bm{G}_{j,k+2}\big].

To ensure the resulting scheme is third-order accuracy, the one-sided point values
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
\bm{U}^{\pm}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are also computed using third-order WENO-type interpolation applied to the local characteristic variables. Note that this can be done in a “dimension-by-dimension” manner as in the 1-D case, we therefore omit the details for the sake of brevity.

According to

27

Here,
\bm{{\cal F}}^{\rm FV}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
and
{\bm{G}^{\rm FV}_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}}

3.4
),
(\bm{F}_{xx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
,

(\bm{F}_{xxxx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
,
(\bm{G}_{yy})_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}

(\bm{G}_{yyyy})_{j,{k+\frac{1}{2}}}
are approximations of the second- and fourth-order spatial derivatives of
\bm{F}
at
(x,y)=(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}},y_{k})
and
\bm{G}
at
(x,y)=(x_{j},y_{k+\frac{1}{2}})

[
6
]

\displaystyle(\bm{F}_{xx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\frac{1}{48(\Delta x)^{2}}\left(-5\bm{F}_{j-2,k}+39\bm{F}_{j-1,k}-34\bm{F}_{j,k}-34\bm{F}_{j+1,k}+39\bm{F}_{j+2,k}-5\bm{F}_{j+3,k}\right),

\displaystyle(\bm{F}_{xxxx})_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}=\frac{1}{2(\Delta x)^{4}}\left(\bm{F}_{j-2,k}-3\bm{F}_{j-1,k}+2\bm{F}_{j,k}+2\bm{F}_{j+1,k}-3\bm{F}_{j+2,k}+\bm{F}_{j+3,k}\right),

Appendix A
One-Dimensional Local Characteristic Decomposition Based Third-Order WENO-Type Interpolant

In this appendix, we briefly describe the 1-D local characteristic decomposition (LCD) based third-order WENO-Type interpolant.

Supposing the point values
\bm{U}_{j}
are given at uniform grid points
x=x_{j}
, we now demonstrate how to compute the
interpolated left-sided value of
\bm{U}
at
x=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
, denoted as
\bm{U}^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
. The corresponding right-sided value
\bm{U}^{+}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
, can be computed
in a mirror-symmetric manner.

The value of
(U^{(i)}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{-}
is computed using a weighted average of the two linear interpolants
\bm{{\cal P}}_{0}(x)
and
\bm{{\cal P}}_{1}(x)
, which are obtained using the stencils
[x_{j-1},x_{j}]
and
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
, respectively:

(U^{(i)}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{-}=\omega^{(i)}_{0}{\cal P}^{(i)}_{0}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})+\omega^{(i)}_{1}{\cal P}^{(i)}_{1}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),

(A.1)

where

{\cal P}^{(i)}_{0}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=-\frac{1}{2}U^{(i)}_{j-1}+\frac{3}{2}U^{(i)}_{j},\quad{\rm and}\quad{\cal P}^{(i)}_{1}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{1}{2}U^{(i)}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}U^{(i)}_{j+1}.

(A.2)

By performing a straightforward Taylor expansion, one can demonstrate that (
A.1
)–(
A.2
) achieve third-order accuracy if the weights
\omega_{k}
in (
A.1
) are chosen as:

\omega^{(i)}_{k}=\frac{\alpha^{(i)}_{k}}{\alpha^{(i)}_{0}+\alpha^{(i)}_{1}},\quad\alpha^{(i)}_{k}=d_{k}\left[1+\frac{\tau^{(i)}_{3}}{\beta^{(i)}_{k}+\varepsilon}\right],

(A.3)

where
d_{0}=\frac{1}{4}
and
d_{1}=\frac{3}{4}
,

\tau^{(i)}_{3}=\big|\beta^{(i)}_{2}-\beta^{(i)}_{3}\big|^{p},

and the smoothness indicators
\beta^{(i)}_{k}
are given by (see e.g.,
[
7
]
)

\beta^{(i)}_{0}=(U^{(i)}_{j-1}-U^{(i)}_{j})^{2},\quad\beta^{(i)}_{1}=(U^{(i)}_{j}-U^{(i)}_{j+1})^{2},\quad

(A.4)

and

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}(U^{(i)}_{j-1}-2U^{(i)}_{j}+U^{(i)}_{j+1})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(U^{(i)}_{j+1}-U^{(i)}_{j-1})^{2},

(A.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{13}{12}(U^{(i)}_{j}-2U^{(i)}_{j+1}+U^{(i)}_{j+2})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(3U^{(i)}_{j}-4U^{(i)}_{j+1}+U^{(i)}_{j+2})^{2}.

Finally, in all of the numerical examples reported in §
4
, we have chosen
p=1.4
and
\varepsilon=10^{-12}
.

Although the third-order interpolant (
A.1
)–(
A.5
) is essentially non-oscillatory, it is well-known that applying it to the conservative variables
\bm{U}
in a componentwise manner can result in spurious oscillations in the computed solution. To address this, we adopt the reconstruction procedure within the LCD framework.

To this end, we first introduce the matrix
\widehat{A}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}:=A(\widehat{\bm{U}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})
, where
A=\frac{\partial\bm{F}}{\partial\bm{U}}
and
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is either a simple average
(\bm{U}_{j}+\bm{U}_{j+1})/2
or another type of average of the
\bm{U}_{j}
and
\bm{U}_{j+1}
states (in the numerical examples reported in §
4
, we have used the simple average). We then compute the matrices

Appendix B
1-D Local Characteristic Decomposition Based Fifth-Order WENO-Z Interpolant

In this appendix, we briefly describe the 1-D LCD based fifth-order WENO-Z interpolant.

Given the point values
\bm{U}_{j}
at uniform grid points
x=x_{j}
, the value
(U^{(i)})^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
is computed using a weighted average of the three parabolic interpolants
\bm{{\cal P}}_{0}(x)
,
\bm{{\cal P}}_{1}(x)
and
\bm{{\cal P}}_{2}(x)

obtained using the stencils
[x_{j-2},x_{j-1},x_{j}]
,
[x_{j-1},x_{j},x_{j+1}]
, and
[x_{j},x_{j+1},x_{j+2}]
, respectively:

(U^{(i)})^{-}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{2}\omega^{(i)}_{k}{\cal P}^{(i)}_{k}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),

(B.1)

where

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle P}^{(i)}_{0}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{3}{8}U^{(i)}_{j-2}-\frac{5}{4}U^{(i)}_{j-1}+\frac{15}{8}U^{(i)}_{j},

(B.2)

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle P}^{(i)}_{1}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=-\frac{1}{8}U^{(i)}_{j-1}+\frac{3}{4}U^{(i)}_{j}+\frac{3}{8}U^{(i)}_{j+1},

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle P}^{(i)}_{2}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{3}{8}U^{(i)}_{j}+\frac{3}{4}U^{(i)}_{j+1}-\frac{1}{8}U^{(i)}_{j+2}.

To ensure (
B.1
)–(
B.2
) is fifth-order accurate and nonoscillatory, one can take the weights
\omega_{k}

in (
B.1
) to be

\omega^{(i)}_{k}:=\frac{\alpha^{(i)}_{k}}{\alpha^{(i)}_{0}+\alpha^{(i)}_{1}+\alpha^{(i)}_{2}},\quad\alpha^{(i)}_{k}=d_{k}\left[1+\left(\frac{\tau^{(i)}_{5}}{\beta^{(i)}_{k}+\varepsilon}\right)^{p}\right],\quad\tau_{5}=|\beta^{(i)}_{2}-\beta^{(i)}_{0}|,

(B.3)

where
d_{0}=\frac{1}{16}
,
d_{1}=\frac{5}{8}
, and
d_{2}=\frac{5}{16}
, and the smoothness indicators
\beta^{(i)}_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle\beta^{(i)}_{0}=\frac{13}{12}\big(U^{(i)}_{j-2}-2U^{(i)}_{j-1}+U^{(i)}_{j}\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\big(U^{(i)}_{j-2}-4U^{(i)}_{j-1}+3U^{(i)}_{j}\big)^{2},

(B.4)

\displaystyle\beta^{(i)}_{1}=\frac{13}{12}\big(U^{(i)}_{j-1}-2U^{(i)}_{j}+U^{(i)}_{j+1}\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\big(U^{(i)}_{j-1}-U^{(i)}_{j+1}\big)^{2},

\displaystyle\beta^{(i)}_{2}=\frac{13}{12}\big(U^{(i)}_{j}-2U^{(i)}_{j+1}+U^{(i)}_{j+2}\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\big(3U^{(i)}_{j}-4U^{(i)}_{j+1}+U^{(i)}_{j+2}\big)^{2}.

We have used
p=2
and
\varepsilon=10^{-12}
in all of the numerical examples reported in this paper. The corresponding right-sided value,
(U^{(i)}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})^{-}
, can also be derived using a mirror-symmetric approach.

,
\bm{\Gamma}_{j}
,
