Diabetes Mellitus Is Associated With Increased Risk for Subsequent Hip Arthroscopy After Index Hip Arthroscopy
Authors: Nicholas J. Morriss, Justin Harrington, Jacob Waldron, Brian Giordano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine whether diabetes mellitus is associated with higher systemic and hip‐specific complications and to evaluate reoperation rates after hip arthroscopy.MethodsUsing the TriNetX Research Network and Current Procedural Terminology and International Classification of Diseases codes, we identified diabetic and nondiabetic patients aged 12 to 65 years who underwent hip arthroscopy between 2005 and 2025. Ninety‐day systemic complications and 6‐month, 1‐year, and 3‐year hip‐specific outcomes were evaluated. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed on 35 demographic and comorbidity variables. Risk analyses, Kaplan‐Meier curves, and Cox proportional hazards models compared outcomes, with significance set at α = 0.05.ResultsAfter matching, 1784 diabetic and 1784 nondiabetic patients were included. At 90 days, diabetes was associated with a higher hospital admission rate (odds ratio [OR] 1.83, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.16‐2.87, P = .01). Diabetic patients had higher rates of subsequent hip arthroscopy at 1 year (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.12‐1.79, P = .004) and 3 years (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.14‐1.69, P = .001). Rates of arthroscopic irrigation and debridement were higher in patients with diabetes at 6 months (OR 1.81, 95% CI 1.09‐3.00, P = .019), 1 year (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.32‐2.92, P = .001), and 3 years (OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.29‐2.37, P < .001). Infection diagnoses were also higher among diabetic patients at 6 months (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.06‐2.52, P = .033) and 3 years (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.24‐2.33, P = .008).ConclusionsDiabetes was not associated with higher rates of most early systemic complications after hip arthroscopy, although hospital admissions were more common among patients with diabetes. Diabetes was associated with higher rates of subsequent hip arthroscopy procedures, arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, and infection diagnoses after index hip arthroscopy; however, these findings should be interpreted in the context of database limitations, including the inability to confirm laterality or ipsilateral revision status.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.

The use of hip arthroscopy has grown rapidly over the past decade, with an 85% increase in procedural volume in the United States and a steady shift toward outpatient surgery. 1 Although major complications remain rare, as overall case numbers continue to rise, even small increases in adverse events become meaningful, creating a need to identify patient characteristics that increase the risk of perioperative complications to allow more informed discussion between patient and surgeon.

Diabetes mellitus has been identified as a comorbidity that may increase perioperative risk in patients undergoing orthopaedic surgery. Diabetes is common among patients undergoing orthopaedic procedures and is associated with higher rates of infection, impaired wound healing, and medical complications throughout arthroscopic and other orthopaedic surgeries. 2 , 3 As the incidence of diabetes in patients undergoing orthopaedic surgery continues to rise, 4 it becomes increasingly important to identify the impact of diabetes on outcomes after orthopaedic surgery. 2 , 5 , 6 Evidence specific to hip arthroscopy, however, remains limited and mixed. A matched cohort study found that patients with diabetes experienced similar improvements in patient‐reported outcomes at 2 years postoperatively but observed increased complication and revision rates compared with those of nondiabetic controls that did not reach statistical significance. 7 Although broader sports medicine literature suggests that diabetes increases short‐term morbidity and readmissions after arthroscopic procedures, with a mixed influence on long‐term outcomes, there is a dearth of literature evaluating the impact of diabetes on outcomes after hip arthroscopy. 2 , 8 The currently published data do not clearly establish whether diabetes increases complications after hip arthroscopy.

This investigation used a large, federated electronic health‐record network to evaluate the population‐level impact of diabetes mellitus on outcomes after hip arthroscopy. The purposes of this study were to determine whether diabetes mellitus is associated with higher systemic and hip‐specific complication and to evaluate reoperation rates after hip arthroscopy. Our hypotheses were that diabetes would be associated with higher short‐term complication rates, an increased long‐term likelihood of subsequent hip arthroscopy and higher rates of subsequent arthroplasty.

METHODS

A retrospective deidentified database cohort study was performed to evaluate the association between pre‐existing diabetes mellitus and adverse outcomes after hip arthroscopy. Data were obtained from the TriNetX Research Network, a federated electronic health record network composed of participating health care organizations that contribute deidentified patient‐level demographic, diagnostic, procedural, laboratory, and encounter data. Patients in this analysis therefore represent individuals receiving care within participating health systems rather than a single‐institution surgical registry. Ethical approval for this study was waived by the University of Rochester Institutional Review Board as this was deemed a deidentified study.

Patients who underwent hip arthroscopy between October 1, 2005, and November 19, 2025, were identified using the Current Procedural Terminology hip arthroscopy family of codes, including 29860, 29861, 29862, 29863, 29914, 29915, and 29916. Eligible patients were 12 to 65 years old at the time of the index procedure. Two cohorts were constructed: a diabetes cohort, defined by a diagnosis code for diabetes mellitus (ICD‐10 E08‐E13) at any time, and a control cohort with no diabetes codes. The index event was the first qualifying hip arthroscopy, and patients whose index procedure occurred more than 20 years before the analysis date were excluded per platform rules.

Outcomes were defined a priori based on clinical relevance and were organized, where possible, using the Clavien‐Dindo‐Sink framework for surgical complication severity. Because TriNetX captures diagnosis and procedure codes rather than granular clinical treatment details, formal grade assignment was limited to outcomes that could be reasonably classified by the type of event or intervention. General systemic complications were evaluated within 90 days after surgery and included emergency department visits, hospital admissions, thromboembolic events, cardiopulmonary complications, renal failure, transfusion, wound disruption, and mortality, using the definitions listed in Table 1. Hip‐specific outcomes, defined in Table 2, were separately evaluated and included subsequent hip‐related outcomes (chondroplasty or labral resection, synovectomy, femoroplasty, acetabuloplasty, and labral repair), arthroscopic irrigation and debridement (subsequent arthroscopy with debridement or synovectomy, open incision and drainage, and soft tissue or osseous debridement around the hip), infection outcomes (postoperative infection, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, sepsis, infectious bursitis, and pathogen‐specific hip infections), subsequent labral repair, and subsequent acetabular or femoral work. Subsequent hip‐related procedural and infection outcomes were assessed after the index procedure at 6 months and 1 and 3 years. Because procedure laterality is not available within TriNetX, these outcomes should be interpreted as subsequent hip‐related diagnoses or procedures after index hip arthroscopy, rather than confirmed ipsilateral revision procedures. Similarly, infection diagnoses occurring beyond the early postoperative period should be interpreted as subsequent coded infection diagnoses after index hip arthroscopy rather than confirmed surgical‐site infections attributable to the index procedure.

Hemoglobin A1c values recorded within 3 months of the index arthroscopy were identified for descriptive analysis. A hemoglobin A1c below 7.0% was considered generally consistent with adequate glycemic control based on contemporary diabetes management guidelines. 9 Because the diabetes cohort differed from controls in demographic and comorbidity profiles, the 1:1 nearest‐neighbor propensity score matching without replacement that is built into the TriNetX analytics environment (caliper 0.1 standard deviation of the logit) was performed. The model included age, sex, race, ethnicity, and baseline comorbidities (malignancy, anemia, coagulation disorders, hypothyroidism, obesity, electrolyte and acid‐base disorders, chronic liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, hypertension and cardiovascular disease, asthma, chronic pain, mood and anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, nicotine dependence, and congenital hip deformities). TriNetX generates postmatching cohort characteristics and balance diagnostics for each matched variable. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values less than 0.10 considered acceptable.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed to determine the sample size required to detect a difference in proportions between the 2 cohorts, based on results by Quesada‐Jimenez et al. evaluating revision arthroscopy in hip arthroscopy between diabetic and nondiabetic patients. 10 In the diabetes cohort, 24.1% of diabetic hips required revision arthroscopy, compared with 9.2% in controls. With a 2‐sided α of 0.05, and a desired power of 80%, the calculated effect size corresponded to a 14.9% absolute difference between groups. Based on these inputs, the required sample size was approximately 194 patients per cohort to achieve adequate power for detecting this difference in outcomes. Risk analyses were used to calculate event proportions with risk differences, risk ratios, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To preserve patient confidentiality, TriNetX inherently censors outcome results where 10 or fewer patients in both cohorts experienced that outcome. Therefore, statistical analysis was not performed on results with 10 or fewer patients per cohort. Time‐to‐event differences were evaluated using Kaplan‐Meier survival curves with log‐rank tests and hazard ratios estimated by Cox proportional hazards models. Number of instances analyses were used descriptively to summarize the distribution of subsequent procedures and infections. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

RESULTS

Propensity Matching and Glycemic Control

Before matching, the diabetes cohort included 1846 patients and the control cohort included 33,780 patients. After 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching, 1784 patients remained in each cohort. The results of propensity score matching are presented in Table S1. All standardized mean differences for matched variables were less than 0.10, showing adequate covariate balance between groups. Within the diabetic cohort, 872 (48%) had a recorded A1c within 3 months of arthroscopy, and the mean hemoglobin A1c was 6.59 ± 1.64.

Systemic Complications at 90 Days

At 90 days after index hip arthroscopy, hospital admissions were more common in the diabetes cohort (3.0% vs 1.7%; OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.16‐2.87, P = .01). Emergency department visits were not statistically different (6.2% of vs 5.3%, OR 1.18, 95% CI 0.89‐1.56, P = .34) (Table 3). Deep vein thrombosis occurred at comparable frequencies (0.8% vs 0.7%; OR 1.17, 95% CI 0.54‐2.53, P = .74), as did acute posthemorrhagic anemia (1.2% vs 0.8%; OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.76‐2.97, P = .25). For all other systemic complications, including mortality, wound disruption, myocardial infarction, pneumonia, acute renal failure, cerebrovascular accident, transfusion, pulmonary embolism, respiratory failure, atelectasis, urinary tract infection, and hardware removal, TriNetX censored results because fewer than 10 events occurred in each group, and statistical analyses were not performed.

Hip‐Related Outcomes

At 6 months, rates of subsequent coded hip arthroscopy were similar between groups (6.2% vs 4.8%; OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.97‐1.73, P = .078) but were higher in the diabetes cohort at 1 year (10.1% vs 7.3%; OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.12‐1.79, P = .004) and 3 years (14.9% vs 11.2%; OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.14‐1.69, P = .001) (Table 4). Arthroscopic irrigation and debridement procedures were more common in the diabetes cohort at 6 months (2.4% vs 1.3%; OR 1.81, 95% CI 1.09‐3.00, P = .019), 1 year (4.1% vs 2.1%; OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.32‐2.92, P = .001), and 3 years (6.7% vs 3.9%; OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.29‐2.37, P < .001). Coded infection diagnoses, defined using the International Classification of Diseases codes listed in Table 2, were similar at 1 year (3.8% vs 2.8%; OR 1.38, 95% CI 0.95‐2.01, P = .144) but were more common in the diabetes cohort at 6 months (3.1% vs 1.9%; OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.06‐2.52, P = .033) and 3 years (5.9% vs 3.6%; OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.24‐2.33, P = .008). Rates of subsequent labral repair procedures were similar at 6 months (4.1% vs 3.9%; OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.75‐1.46, P = .798), 1 year (6.2% vs 5.8%; OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.82‐1.43, P = .573), and 3 years (9.0% vs 8.1%; OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89‐1.43, P = .309). Subsequent coded acetabular or femoral procedures were similar at 6 months (4.8% vs 4.1%; OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.86‐1.63, P = .292) and 1 year (7.5% vs 6.0%; OR 1.27, 95% CI 0.98‐1.66, P = .072) but were more common in the diabetes cohort at 3 years (11.3% vs 8.8%; OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.06‐1.65, P = .012).

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this study was that no significant differences were observed for most analyzable 90‐day systemic outcomes between patients with and without diabetes, and overall rates of serious medical events were low. Hospital admission was the only systemic outcome that differed significantly between groups. Emergency department visits, deep vein thrombosis, and acute posthemorrhagic anemia did not significantly differ between cohorts. Diabetes was also associated with higher rates of subsequent hip arthroscopy procedures, arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, and infection diagnoses after index hip arthroscopy, but these hip‐related outcomes cannot be confirmed as ipsilateral complications or revisions because laterality is unavailable in TriNetX. Prior work examining diabetes in this setting has been limited to small, single‐center series with modest diabetic sample sizes and a primary focus on patient‐reported outcomes rather than complications or reintervention burden. 7 , 10 The present study was able to evaluate outcomes and risk of subsequent arthroscopy as well as infection burden after arthroscopy in an externally valid sample size.

In this matched cohort, there were no consistent differences in 90‐day systemic complications between patients with and without diabetes, and overall rates of serious medical events remained low. Only hospital admission differed between groups. Rates of emergency department visits, deep vein thrombosis, anemia, and other measured systemic complications were similar. These findings align with the established safety profile of hip arthroscopy, in which most complications are minor and major events such as deep infection, thromboembolic events, femoral neck fracture, and dislocation are rare. 11 , 12 The current body of literature regarding orthopaedic surgery in the setting of diabetes confirms that glycemic control is a critical determinant of postoperative outcomes, with higher rates of postoperative medical complications, impaired wound healing, and readmission linked to hyperglycemia. 3 , 13 , 14 The mean hemoglobin A1c was 6.59 ± 1.64, suggesting generally adequate glycemic control among those with recorded laboratory values. This may have contributed to the low rates of analyzable 90‐day systemic outcomes observed in the present study. 9 , 15 , 16 However, the study was not designed to stratify outcomes by glycemic control. In addition, these findings inform evidence presented by Nicolay et al., who revealed that the combination of diabetes and obesity was predictive of 30‐day postoperative complications in hip, knee, and shoulder arthroscopy, but diabetes alone was not predictive. 8 The present analysis controlled for the presence of obesity and failed to find a significant impact of diabetes on acute systemic outcomes, indicating that the interaction between obesity and diabetes may be more important than the presence of either comorbidity alone. The presence of diabetes in conjunction with obesity, if accompanied by hypercholesterolemia or hypertension, is named metabolic syndrome. Metabolic syndrome has been well established as a risk for acute systemic complications in total joint arthroplasty, 17 , 18 and some evidence implicates metabolic syndrome as a risk factor for higher rates of medical complications after shoulder arthroscopy. 19 Taken together with the results of the present study, well‐controlled diabetes alone may not be an independent risk factor for complications after hip arthroscopy, and future research may investigate the role of metabolic syndrome and metabolic derangements that impact systemic illness postoperatively.

In contrast to the largely similar systemic profile, several hip‐specific outcomes differed between groups. There were higher rates of subsequent hip arthroscopy, arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, and infection among patients with diabetes, whereas rates of subsequent labral repair were similar. These should be interpreted cautiously. Events occurring at 1 and 3 years may reflect progression of underlying hip pathology, contralateral hip disease, differences in health care use, infection diagnoses unrelated to the index procedure, or true ipsilateral reintervention. Because laterality and clinical documentation are unavailable in TriNetX, these possibilities cannot be distinguished. These findings should also be considered alongside prior studies. Perets et al. reported that diabetic patients achieved similar improvements in patient‐reported outcomes and had comparable revision and arthroplasty conversion rates to matched controls at 2 years. 7 In contrast, Quesada‐Jimenez et al. evaluated 29 diabetic hips with 75 matched nondiabetic hips at 2 years and found an increased rate of subsequent arthroscopy in the diabetic cohort, with 7 hips (24.1%) requiring subsequent arthroscopy compared with 8 (9.2%) in the control cohort. 10 Our results coincide with those of Quesada‐Jimenez et al., with diabetic patients more likely to undergo debridement and revision femoroplasty/acetabuloplasty. Regarding infection, our results are similar to those of other database studies showing that diabetes, especially in the presence of obesity or poor glycemic control, increases infection risk after knee and shoulder arthroscopic procedures. 2 , 20 Events occurring at 1 and 3 years may reflect progression of underlying hip pathology, contralateral hip disease, differences in health care use, infection diagnoses unrelated to the index procedure, or true ipsilateral reintervention. Because laterality and clinical documentation are unavailable in TriNetX, these possibilities cannot be distinguished. Together, these findings indicate that diabetes may be associated with a higher burden of reintervention and infection at the population level, particularly over longer follow‐up.

Overall, the current analysis has important clinical implications. Serious systemic complications at 90 days remained uncommon and largely similar between groups, supporting the safety of hip arthroscopy in patients with diabetes. However, higher rates of several hip‐specific adverse outcomes, including subsequent arthroscopy, arthroscopic debridement, and infection, were observed over midterm follow‐up. These findings may aid in preoperative counseling and expectation setting, guide perioperative planning, and reinforce the importance of optimizing glycemic control in accordance with contemporary guidelines. 9 , 15 Further research linking glycemic metrics, pathology severity, and long‐term patient‐reported outcomes will help refine risk stratification and management strategies for diabetic patients undergoing hip arthroscopy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The principal limitation of this analysis is the lack of laterality in the TriNetX platform. Therefore, subsequent hip arthroscopy, labral repair, femoroplasty, acetabuloplasty, and debridement codes cannot be confirmed to represent revision procedures on the index hip and may include contralateral hip procedures. For this reason, the results are framed as subsequent hip‐related procedures after index hip arthroscopy rather than confirmed ipsilateral revisions. This limitation precludes causal inference regarding whether later procedures were directly attributable to the index operation. The use of retrospective administrative data introduces the potential for misclassification of exposures and outcomes. Hemoglobin A1c values within 3 months of arthroscopy were available for only 872 patients, representing 48.9% of the matched diabetes cohort. Consequently, the reported mean hemoglobin A1c may not represent glycemic control across the entire cohort, and selection bias may have been introduced if patients who underwent perioperative A1c testing differed systematically from those without recorded values. The incomplete laboratory data also precluded meaningful stratification of outcomes according to glycemic control. Preoperative and operative findings, such as structural pathology, radiographic parameters, and surgical technique, are unavailable. Patient‐reported outcomes cannot be assessed but were not the focus of this investigation, which targeted objective adverse events. Despite matching on 35 characteristics, unmeasured confounders such as socioeconomic factors, surgeon experience, and rehabilitation adherence may persist. 2 TriNetX suppresses event counts below 10, limiting assessment of rare but clinically important complications, and our study was likely underpowered to detect these rare complications. Follow‐up is constrained to encounters within participating health systems, which may underestimate procedures performed elsewhere. Finally, sex‐stratified outcome analyses were considered; however, because several outcomes were rare and TriNetX suppresses event counts when 10 or fewer patients experience an outcome, disaggregation by sex would have resulted in substantial censoring of clinically relevant endpoints.

CONCLUSIONS

Diabetes was not associated with higher rates of most early systemic complications after hip arthroscopy, although hospital admissions were more common among patients with diabetes. Diabetes was associated with higher rates of subsequent hip arthroscopy procedures, arthroscopic irrigation and debridement, and infection diagnoses after index hip arthroscopy; however, these findings should be interpreted in the context of database limitations, including the inability to confirm laterality or ipsilateral revision status.
