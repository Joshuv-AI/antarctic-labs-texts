The Tapu–Noa Matrix for Determining Whether the Use or Registration of a Trade Mark Is Likely to Offend Māori
Authors: Jayden Houghton (Rereahu Maniapoto)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Aotearoa New Zealand, the Māori Trade Marks Advisory Committee advises the Commissioner of Trade Marks on whether trade mark applications that are, or appear to be, derivative of a Māori sign, are likely to be offensive to Māori. The Committee uses a Tapu–Noa Matrix to help ascertain offensiveness. This article explains and evaluates the Matrix, which is not examined in the existing scholarship. First, it explains the tikanga Māori legal system and the tikanga concepts mana, tapu and noa. Second, it outlines two iterations of the Tapu–Noa Matrix: a prototype matrix and the published practice note matrix. Third, it explains the axes of the matrices and provides examples of trade marks at different points. Fourth, it provides examples at coordinates on the matrices to illustrate their application. Finally, it evaluates the matrices and suggests that the published practice note matrix is more accurate and useful than the prototype matrix. In doing so, it demonstrates developments and challenges in applying tikanga concepts to serve tests under the state law.Keywords: customary law, indigenous rights, intellectual property, mātauranga Māori, taonga, tikanga Māori, traditional cultural expressions, traditional knowledge, Treaty of Waitangi

Introduction

Section 17(1)(c) of the Trade Marks Act 2002 provides that the Commissioner of Trade Marks (Commissioner) ‘must not register as a trade mark or part of a trade mark any matter … the use or registration of which would, in the opinion of the Commissioner, be likely to offend a significant section of the community, including Māori’. The Māori Trade Marks Advisory Committee, comprising individuals with extensive knowledge of te ao Māori (the Māori world) and tikanga Māori (customary practices), advises the Commissioner on whether trade mark applications that are, or appear to be, derivative of a Māori sign are likely to be offensive to Māori (Trade Marks Act 2002, ss 177–180). The Committee's advice is not binding (IPONZ [date unknown e], para 11.2.4), but, in practice, the Commissioner has always followed the Committee's recommendations (2023 email from Principal Trade Mark Examiner to the author).

The Committee operates in accordance with the Trade Marks Act and various guidelines, including the terms of reference (IPONZ [date unknown e]), a memorandum of understanding (IPONZ [date unknown c]) and internal general guidelines on how to exercise its functions (IPONZ [date unknown a]). Trade mark applications are decided by the Commissioner on a case‐by‐case basis in light of the Committee's advice, and, due to the changing and evolving nature of mātauranga Māori, the Committee's reports do not serve as precedents for future applications. Importantly, findings of offensiveness prevent the registration of trade marks that might misappropriate mātauranga Māori (the knowledge sourced from Māori ancestors, including the Māori worldview and cultural practices) and taonga Māori (tangible and intangible treasures). Denying the registration of such trade marks is a form of what is known as defensive protection (Zografos Johnsson and Tualima 2017, p. 221), meaning that it prevents unauthorised persons from securing trade mark rights over mātauranga Māori and taonga.

This article presents, applies and discusses the Tapu–Noa Matrix, which the Committee uses to assess whether a trade mark will likely be offensive to Māori (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 5.2.2). It treats the Matrix as one of several interpretive frameworks through which IPONZ and the Committee implement s 17(1)(c). The analysis asks three questions: first, how each matrix conceptualises tapu and noa in relation to ‘Māori elements’ and ‘goods/services’; second, whether those conceptualisations cohere with the statutory requirement that registration not be ‘likely to offend’ a significant section of the community, including Māori; and third, how each matrix performs against examples and hypotheticals drawn from Committee advice and practice. In doing so, it demonstrates developments and challenges in applying tikanga concepts to serve tests under the state law.

Section 2 defines the tikanga Māori legal system and the tikanga concepts mana, tapu and noa. Section 3 outlines two iterations of the Tapu–Noa Matrix: a prototype matrix and the published practice note matrix. Section 4 explains the axes of the matrices and provides examples of trade marks at different points. Section 5 presents coordinates to assist with the analysis of the matrices and provides examples at each coordinate to illustrate the matrices’ application. Section 6 evaluates the matrices.

It is challenging to illustrate points on the matrices with examples and hypotheticals. No doubt tohunga (priests or learned persons) from different iwi and hapū would disagree on hierarchies and distinctions due to the subjectivity of the criteria. An important proviso to this analysis is that the hypotheticals and the placement of hypotheticals and actual examples on matrices reflect the author's interpretation of Committee practice and guidance and are only intended to be illustrative; they are not intended to be instructive or to pre‐empt the Committee's expert assessments.

In line with trade mark practice, this article uses upper case to denote registered or applied‐for word marks (e.g., KERERU) and uses sentence case when referring to kupu (words) or concepts more generally (e.g., mana, tapu and noa). The distinction signals when a term is functioning as a trade mark and when it is being discussed as a tikanga or linguistic concept.

Tikanga Concepts

The IPONZ practice note on the Māori advisory committee and Māori trade marks (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 1.1) provides that:

If the use or registration of a trade mark that contains, or appears to be derived from, Māori elements is inconsistent with a Te Ao Māori view and the values and principles that underpin mātauranga, there is a risk the trade mark's use or registration may be offensive to Māori.

Mātauranga is ‘a body of knowledge that whānau, hapū, iwi, and other Māori groups can utilize, share, and benefit from’ that comes with an accompanying ‘collective responsibility to use in accordance with tikanga’ (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 1.1.2). Tikanga Māori, also referred to as Māori customary law, is Aotearoa New Zealand's first law and comprises concepts and processes and procedures to enforce such concepts (Houghton 2025b, p. 4). 1

Consequently, in assessing whether a proposed trade mark may be offensive to Māori, the Committee must consider its consistency with relevant tikanga concepts. Three of the most critical concepts to understand ‘to avoid causing offence’, including ‘inadvertently’, are the relational concepts of mana, tapu and noa (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 1.2.6.1).

Mana is a broad concept that ‘[combines] notions of psychic and spiritual force and vitality, recognised authority, influence and prestige, and … power and the ability to control people and events’ (Benton et al. 2012, p. 154). Mana is established and maintained by carrying out responsibilities to its source. There are many overlapping sources of mana, including mana atua (derived from the gods), mana tūpuna (mana derived from one's ancestors) and mana tangata (mana derived from one's actions). Mana can increase and diminish depending on the discharge of such responsibilities, as well as extrinsic factors. Mana determines one's status within the collective and structures Māori society and organisations.

IPONZ describes tapu as ‘the strongest force in Māori life’ (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 1.2.6.2). Tapu is viewed as the power of the atua (gods) and means something is ‘sacred, prohibited, restricted, set apart, forbidden, under atua protection’ (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). Unless mitigated, violating tapu automatically results in some form of utu (reciprocation), often including the violator's death (Benton et al. 2012, p. 404). Tapu takes two forms (Quince and Houghton 2023, p. 49): First, intrinsic tapu is permanent, with spiritual origins, and, second, temporary tapu refers to human‐proclaimed extensions of tapu (such as ritual restrictions on a resource) which protect sources of intrinsic tapu. Tapu is influential in regulating Māori society.

Noa means ‘to be free from the extensions of tapu, ordinary, unrestricted’ (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). It refers to ‘the ordinary world, where there is a state of ease, limited restrictions and freedom’ (Kruger 2020, para 58). Whilst noa is ‘the opposite of tapu and includes the concept of common’ (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 1.2.6.3), tapu and noa are ‘not negations’ (Henare 1988, p. 19). As such, something may be intrinsically tapu and noa at the same time. Noa is achieved through whakanoa, ‘the process or achievement of lifting tapu’ (Stephens and Boyce 2013, p. 46). As intrinsic tapu is permanent, whakanoa can only lift temporary tapu (Houghton 2025c, p. 86).

Mana and tapu (and noa) are closely related. As a person, place, or object gains mana, it usually becomes more tapu because it holds greater spiritual significance (and vice versa). However, a loss of mana can only reduce tapu to a degree. As Quince and Houghton write: ‘The notion of intrinsic tapu relates to a person's self‐worth, dignity and essential humanity, and it is fully realised upon an individual coming into existence. This is what sets intrinsic tapu apart from the concept of mana. Mana is more flexible than tapu …’ (p 51–52).

Tapu–Noa Matrix

The use or registration of a trade mark may be offensive to Māori on various grounds. There are two grounds that concern tapu. The first ground is where the mark contains something tapu that, because of its contingent ‘rules and prohibitions’, cannot be used in such a manner (IPONZ 2016, p. 11). The second ground is where the mark ‘associate[s] something that is extremely tapu … with something that is noa’ (p. 11).

The Committee uses a Tapu–Noa Matrix to assist in ‘determining whether the use of elements of Māori culture in a trade mark might offend’. 2 The Tapu–Noa Matrix assesses whether the mark contains tapu elements and whether it associates tapu and noa elements. According to the Matrix, ‘inappropriate’ in the context of Committee assessments means that a mark, whilst not necessarily offensive, does not align with the respectful and correct usage of Māori elements. Therefore, ‘inappropriate’ and ‘offensiveness’ are seen as distinct concepts.

In the prototype matrix (Figure 1), ‘inappropriate’ marks are distinctly categorised in the top left and bottom right quadrants; ‘offensive’ marks require associations with more noa goods/services (moving the mark right) or more tapu Māori elements (moving the mark up), respectively. In the practice note matrix (Figure 2), ‘inappropriate’ marks exist on a spectrum approaching offensiveness, but they do not meet the higher threshold for offensiveness, which requires a significant level of disrespect or harm.

Prototype Matrix

In September 2023, the Chair of the Committee, Karen Te O Kahurangi Waaka, presented at the Twenty Years of the Trade Marks Act 2002 Conference at Auckland Law School (Waaka 2023). Waaka presented what she called a ‘Tapu–Noa Matrix’. Waaka said it ‘is in the practice guideline, which are currently being updated for our website’ and go into ‘a lot more of the analysis of our Committee’. Waaka explained the relationship between inappropriateness and offensiveness as functions of mana and tapu, and noa. She presented a graph with four quadrants.

The x‐axis labelled ‘products’ is a spectrum from low degrees to high degrees of noa. The y‐axis labelled ‘Māori words and images’ is a spectrum from low degrees to high degrees of mana and tapu.

It is unclear why the prototype matrix includes both mana and tapu on the y‐axis, whereas, as discussed below, the practice note matrix only includes tapu. Mana may have been included because kupu may have mana if they are closely associated ‘with a whānau, hapū, iwi, or taonga’ (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 5.2.1.3), making these words tapu (para 5.2.11). The practice note matrix does not provide much clarity on how mana and tapu would function together on that y‐axis.

Given the close connection of mana and tapu, and the lack of guidance on what function mana serves on the y‐axis of the prototype matrix, the analysis in this article will proceed on the basis that the y‐axis of each matrix is a spectrum of degrees of tapu.

Practice Note Matrix

In April 2024, IPONZ updated its practice note on the Committee and Māori trade marks. The practice note introduced a Tapu–Noa Matrix adapted from a kaupapa Māori (Māori values) assessment matrix for Māori trade marks produced by Grant Bulley and based on an original sketch by Pare Keiha, a current member of the Committee (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 5.2.2).

The y‐axis is labelled ‘Māori elements in the trade mark’ and the x‐axis is labelled ‘goods/services covered by the trade mark’. Each is a spectrum from tapu to noa. Figure 2 presents an alternative way of representing the matrix (Houghton 2025e, p. 950). The proportions of the different zones are illustrative only and not necessarily to scale.

The practice note provides only limited guidance on how the Committee differentiates ‘inappropriate’ and ‘offensive’. However, it does provide some illustrations. For example, it suggests that associating certain goods and services with Māori elements may convey an inappropriate inference that could cause offence (IPONZ [date unknown b], s. 5.2.6). Additionally, trade marks featuring or comprising Māori artwork and designs may be considered offensive if the context in which they will be used is inappropriate (para 5.2.9). Furthermore, inappropriate use of macrons or double vowels in a written kupu in a trade mark may convey unintended offensive meanings and result in offensive outcomes (paras 5.1.5 and 5.2.1).

This raises the question of whether ‘inappropriate’ is synonymous with ‘offensiveness’ or if it represents a distinct concept on a spectrum leading up to, but not necessarily reaching, offensiveness. Alternatively, is there a degree of ‘inappropriateness’ that can be equated to ‘offensiveness’? The practice note is unsatisfactory because it fails to differentiate between inappropriateness and offensiveness in the Committee's assessments.

The Axes

The matrices each have two axes. The y‐axis is labelled ‘Māori words and images’ or ‘Māori elements in the trade mark’. The x‐axis is labelled ‘products’ or ‘goods/services covered by the trade mark’. Section 4 explains each axis and provides hypotheticals at points along them. Whilst four bands are presented for each matrix, the proportions of the different zones are illustrative only and, accordingly, the four bands are not necessarily quartiles.

Y‐Axis: Māori Elements in the Trade Mark

‘Māori Elements’ vs. ‘Māori Words and Images’

The practice note matrix extends beyond ‘words and images’ to include any element of a trade mark that is ‘sourced and generated from a Māori world view, te ao Māori’ (IPONZ [date unknown b]), whether or not the element is in the public domain. Such elements encompass a broad range of expressions, practices and knowledge that are integral to Māori identity and heritage.

Waaka (2023) provides some guidance on how to identify such Māori elements. In terms of language, Māori elements include kupu, whakataukī (proverbs), naming conventions, dialects and expressions. In terms of connection to people and place, Māori elements include whakapapa (genealogy) and references to specific whānau, hapū and iwi, and their social structures, lands, waterways, flora and fauna. In terms of folklore, Māori elements include pūrākau (sacred Māori stories) and allusions to Māori characters, locations and protocols. In terms of traditional and contemporary cultural expressions, Māori elements include waiata (songs), haka (posture dances), toi (art), whakairo (carvings), tā moko (tattoos), raranga (weaving) and tākaro (games). In terms of taonga more broadly, Māori elements include heirlooms, photographs and smells. Ultimately, Māori elements include mātauranga Māori: ‘anything that is sourced in the wide context of Māori knowledge, its expression and cultural practice’ (Waaka 2023).

Māori elements with higher degrees of tapu ‘have special social, cultural and spiritual significance or meaning for Māori’, thus making them inappropriate ‘to copy or use’ (IPONZ 2016, p. 9). Being in the public domain does not necessarily mean a Māori element will be relatively noa; Māori elements that are ‘familiar to the public’ may be ‘significant to Māori and therefore deserve special consideration and respect’ (IPONZ 2016, p. 10).

Band Y1 contains Māori elements with a low degree of tapu (or which are relatively noa). A mark with such elements will be inappropriate when the elements are used in respect of goods or services approaching or with a heightened degree of tapu and offensive when the elements are used in respect of goods or services approaching or with a high degree of tapu. ‘Kawhe’ is a transliteration from the English ‘coffee’ (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). ‘Angitu’ is a common Māori word that means successful or fortunate (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). Each word has day‐to‐day use.

Band Y2 contains Māori elements with a moderate degree of tapu. The modern usage of ‘Wharekura’ refers to a secondary school house where te reo Māori is spoken and mātauranga Māori is transmitted (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). ‘Rangimārie’ means peace or harmony (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). Whilst the words allude to culturally significant concepts, it is possible for a mark with such words to be appropriate, which precludes their categorisation in the next band.

Band Y3 contains Māori elements with a heightened degree of tapu such that their use in trade marks will be at least inappropriate if not offensive. ‘Te Kore’ refers to the realm of potential being (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). ‘Te Ao Wairua’ refers to the spiritual realm (Te Aka Māori Dictionary), where ‘Wairua’ itself refers to the spirit of a person that exists beyond death (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). Each element is fundamental to Māori cosmogony and spirituality (Marsden 2003 p. 20; Tomas 2006 p. 66) and the whakapapa and practice of tikanga Māori (Houghton 2025b p. 6–7, 50). A mark with such words will be at least inappropriate but may not necessarily be offensive.

Band Y4 contains Māori elements with such a high degree of tapu that their use will be offensive. Tohunga were ‘the conduit between tapu knowledge sourced in the non‐human realms of the universe and the human members of their communities’ (Quince and Houghton 2023, p. 55). Tohunga were themselves considered highly tapu and often required to live apart from the community (p. 55–56). ‘Papatūānuku’ is one of the most significant atua in Māori cosmogony (IPONZ [date unknown d]).

A community language school provides resources and lessons for learning and practising te reo Māori. Rākau is a common Māori word for wood (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). Whakairo are taonga. The facilitation of engagement with the taonga language, and the commercialisation of furniture incorporating taonga whakairo, may involve tapu cultural protocols, procedures and processes (Houghton 2025d). However, trade marks for a community language school for learning te reo Māori and a business that sells furniture incorporating whakairo may be appropriate in some circumstances, which precludes their categorisation in the next band.

Band X3 contains goods and services with a heightened degree of tapu such that their use will be at least inappropriate if not offensive. Menemene is a common Māori word for smile (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). A hei tiki is a greenstone neck pendant that depicts a human image (Te Aka Māori Dictionary). The hei tiki is ‘culturally significant, as it represents the unborn child and is associated with Hineteiwaiwa, the Māori goddess of childbirth’ (IPONZ 2016, p. 10). Tūpāpaku (deceased persons) are highly tapu. Funeral services which deal with tūpāpaku are themselves tapu (Mead 2016b). However, funeral services do not have such a high degree of tapu that marks with respect to them will always be offensive: for example, the registered combination trade mark ‘AROHA FUNERALS A TIME OF LOVE’ was not rejected on a tapu–noa analysis (IPONZ 2020).

Band X4 contains goods and services with such a high degree of tapu that their use will be offensive. Tohunga were introduced at Y4. Tohunga practices are highly tapu. ‘Tō Kōrero’ might be translated as ‘your story’. Whakapapa information is highly tapu. Traditionally, it was ‘rarely shared with outsiders other than in carefully prescribed contexts’ and ‘held and controlled by delegated persons on behalf of the greater whānau, hapū or iwi group’ (Quince and Houghton 2023, p. 58). The research and recording of whakapapa are highly tapu practices.

Evaluation

The prototype and practice note matrices are each designed to visualise and understand the Committee's assessment of whether trade marks are likely to be offensive to Māori. Section 6 will contend that the practice note matrix is both more accurate and useful in illustrating the relationship between Māori elements and the associated goods/services for four reasons. It also uses hypotheticals as a stress test of the matrices’ internal logic.

Practice Note Matrix More Accurate and Useful

First, the practice note matrix better accounts for inherently tapu Māori elements. Some Māori elements are so tapu that it is inappropriate for a single entity to obtain exclusive statutory rights to use them as a trade mark in the course of trade. For example, the Committee has stated that the word ‘mana’ is inherently tapu, and granting a single trade exclusive rights to the kupu ‘mana’ would be offensive to Māori (Trade mark compliance report “MANA” 2023). This remains true regardless of the type of goods or services the mark is associated with, even if those goods or services are also tapu. The prototype matrix is inaccurate in this respect because it implies that a mark pairing a highly tapu Māori element with a similarly tapu good or service could become non‐offensive. In contrast, the practice note matrix more accurately represents this concept by illustrating that some Māori elements, such as ‘mana’, remain offensive in any commercial context, irrespective of the associated good or service.

Second, the practice note matrix better captures the contextual rather than linear nature of the tapu–noa relationship. The prototype matrix assumes a rigid, linear transition from tapu to noa, implying that moving along the x‐axis directly affects the offensiveness of a mark. However, offensiveness does not follow a linear gradient; rather, it emerges relationally from the relationship between the Māori elements and goods/services.

Third, the practice note's emphasis on ‘Māori elements’ allows for a more comprehensive approach to assessing offensiveness. ‘Māori words and images’ risks excluding Māori elements such as conceptual references and design motifs that do not strictly contain Māori words or images but nonetheless evoke Māori culture and identity. For example, the practice note matrix allows the Committee to advise on a trade mark that phonetically mimics te reo Māori without explicitly using Māori words (Trade mark compliance report “Keyora” 2019) or a trade mark that indirectly references kōwhaiwhai (traditional Māori patterns). The consideration of such marks would be awkward in the prototype matrix.

Fourth, the practice note matrix is more intuitive. In the prototype matrix, the directions of the axes are contrary: The y‐axis represents increasing tapu, whilst the x‐axis represents decreasing tapu. In the practice note matrix, the directions of the axes are the same.

Conceptual Stress Test

The matrices are contradictory. Take, for example, ‘MENEMENE MEN’ using menemene (word mark element) for a hei tiki range (goods), where the element is low tapu and the goods have heightened tapu. In the prototype matrix, the mark is categorised as ‘appropriate’ (AA), but in the practice note matrix, it is categorised as ‘not offensive but inappropriate’ (I3). By contrast, take, for example, ‘RANGIMĀRIE CREATIONS’ using rangimārie (word mark element) for a stationery range (goods), where the element is moderate tapu and the goods have low tapu. In the prototype matrix, the mark is categorised as ‘not offensive but inappropriate’ (BA), but in the practice note matrix, it is categorised as ‘appropriate’ (A1). The opposite results indicate that at least one of the matrices is conceptually flawed.

Further, a superlatively tapu element and a superlatively tapu good/service would be ‘offensive’ in the practice note matrix but ‘not offensive but inappropriate’ in the prototype matrix.

The author tentatively suggests that the practice note matrix leads to more accurate and useful outcomes, which would make sense given that the Committee revised the prototype matrix and published the practice note.

However, whilst it is to be preferred, the practice note matrix does sometimes lead to questionable outcomes, such as with goods that convey an inappropriate inference. Take any Māori element. If applied to goods like alcohol or cigarettes (considered very noa), the mark's coordinates would move to the left, making it more likely to be appropriate. However, if applied to a funeral service (considered relatively tapu), the mark's coordinates would move to the right, making it more likely to be offensive. This would seem counterintuitive as pairing a Māori element with a good/service with an inappropriate inference would seem to be more offensive than pairing it with a relatively tapu good/service. The Committee addresses these issues in its guidance by explaining that there are other grounds on which to find the use or registration of a mark offensive, such as where there is an inappropriate inference (IPONZ [date unknown b]).

Ultimately, it is highly challenging to design a matrix that accurately represents the relationship between tapu and noa, and appropriate, inappropriate and offensive uses, without exceptions. In most cases, the practice note matrix is a helpful guide for the Committee and prospective applicants when interpreted and applied in context. With time, the Committee will be able to refine its guidance and develop further guidance to enhance transparency in the trade mark regime.

Conclusion

The Committee's more than 20‐year history of providing advice on whether the use or registration of a trade mark would be offensive to Māori makes it one of the most effective forms of defensive protection for mātauranga Māori and taonga, effectively preventing inappropriate registrations of trade mark rights over mātauranga and taonga Māori. Comparing the prototype and practice note matrices shows that design choices, such as the direction of an axis or the treatment of inherently tapu Māori elements, can materially change how marks are categorised as appropriate, inappropriate or offensive. The analysis suggests that the practice note matrix better reflects tikanga and s 17(1)(c) but also identifies areas where further guidance from IPONZ and the Committee could improve transparency for applicants.

The Committee only provides advice to the Commissioner, and, consequently, the Matrix is only effective when the Commissioner accepts the Committee's advice. Whilst the Commissioner has always followed the Committee's advice, there remains the possibility that it could not be followed in the future. A statutory decision‐making (rather than advisory) power for the Committee would further safeguard mātauranga Māori and taonga from exploitation (Houghton 2024, p. 331–337).
