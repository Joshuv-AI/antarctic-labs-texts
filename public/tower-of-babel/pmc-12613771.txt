Sex-specific associations of episode type in bipolar I disorder with neuro-metabolic lateralization of the cortico-striatal-cerebellar (CSC) circuit
Authors: Ran-chao Wang, Jin-xin Wang, Yang Li, Yu-hao Xu, Yu Shen, Jian Chen, Yue-feng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to examine whether neuro-metabolic lateralization of the cortico-striatal-cerebellar (CSC) circuit is associated with sex-specific mechanisms underlying episode types in bipolar I disorder (BD-I).MethodsA total of 109 patients in the acute phase of BD-I were included in the analysis. For comparisons of general clinical characteristics, CSC circuit structural volumes, metabolic features, and laterality indices (LI), analyses were conducted between the male group (n = 49) and the female group (n = 60). For predictive modeling, sex-stratified XGBoost models were built separately: the male cohort (n = 49) was subdivided into mania (n = 31) and depression (n = 18), and the female cohort (n = 60) was subdivided into mania (n = 17) and depression (n = 43). Structural MRI and MRS were utilized to obtain volumetric and metabolic features within the CSC circuit. Key sex-related features were identified through inter-group consistency analysis, Mann-Whitney U tests, and LASSO regression, followed by the calculation of laterality indices (LI). Sex-stratified XGBoost models were constructed to evaluate the predictive performance of CSC circuit features and LI for symptom onset, with SHAP analysis used to determine feature contributions.ResultsLI analysis demonstrated that males had significantly higher LI for ACC-sV, ACC-NAA, BG-Cho, and ACC-Glx (all P < 0.007), whereas females exhibited significantly higher LI for BG-sV (P < 0.007). In the sex-stratified XGBoost models, the LI-based models significantly outperformed the absolute-value-based models in predicting manic episodes in males (AUC = 0.803) and depressive episodes in females (AUC = 0.824) (Delong test: P < 0.05). However, the cross-sex prediction performance remained limited. Additionally, SHAP analysis indicated that ACC-Glx LI contributed most to manic episode prediction in males, while ACC-NAA LI and BG-Cho LI had the highest contributions to depressive episode prediction in females.ConclusionIn patients with BD-I, significant sex differences are evident in the structural and metabolic characteristics of the CSC circuit. Neuro-metabolic lateralization alterations in the ACC and BG regions may be involved in sex-specific mechanisms underlying different symptom onset types.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07537-1.Keywords: Cortico-striato-cerebellar circuit, Bipolar I disorder, Neuro-metabolic lateralization, Sex differences, Machine learning

Introduction

Bipolar Disorder (BD) is a severe and recurrent psychiatric disorder characterized by alternating episodes of mania, hypomania, and depression, frequently accompanied by cognitive impairments and significant functional disability [1, 2]. Its clinical manifestations show significant sex heterogeneity: depressive episodes are more frequent and prolonged in female patients, while males are more prone to manic episodes and comorbid substance abuse [3, 4]. Although epidemiological and clinical observations have confirmed the existence of sex differences, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear [5, 6].

Extensive neuroimaging research has consistently implicated the cortico-limbic circuitry—particularly the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), prefrontal cortex (PFC), and hippocampus—in the pathophysiology of BD, highlighting their roles in emotion regulation, cognitive control, and vulnerability to recurrence [7, 8]. Beyond these classical cortico-limbic regions, emerging evidence also suggests that the cortico-striatal-cerebellar (CSC) circuit contributes to BD pathophysiology. Specifically, abnormalities have been observed in key CSC nodes when comparing BD patients with healthy controls: the ACC shows volumetric reduction and altered energy metabolism linked to affective polarity [9]; the basal ganglia (BG) exhibits heightened reactivity during reward processing and abnormal dopaminergic activity [10, 11]; thalamic subregional connectivity is disrupted and associated with manic symptoms [12]; and the cerebellum (CB), though traditionally considered a motor region, demonstrates reduced connectivity with the cerebrum and aberrant metabolic profiles correlating with mood state [13]. Moreover, alterations in periventricular white matter (PWM) integrity have also been reported, reflecting vulnerability to stress-related regulation [14]. In addition, studies based on unmedicated BD-II populations have also suggested that CSC dysfunction may represent a shared pathological basis of bipolar disorder [15]. Collectively, these findings indicate that, in addition to the well-established cortico-limbic system, the CSC circuit represents a complementary network integrating emotional regulation, reward processing, and cognitive control, and may play a significant role in shaping affective polarity and symptom severity in BD.

Building on this, the present study further focuses on its sex-specific mechanisms. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is responsible for monitoring emotional conflict and active regulation, and its metabolic abnormalities are more prominent in depressed female patients [16]; the basal ganglia (BG), as the central hub of the reward system, is closely associated with abnormal activation of the dopamine pathway in male manic episodes [17]; the thalamus (Tha) and periventricular white matter (PWM) jointly participate in cortico-subcortical emotional information integration and rhythm regulation, with differing neuroregulatory effects under the influence of sex hormones — the male thalamus is more sensitive to reward and motivation processing [18], whereas the female PWM region exhibits higher white matter vulnerability in stress regulation [19]; the cerebellum (CB), in addition to its role in motor function, has also been shown to be extensively involved in emotional regulation networks, with greater involvement in emotional processing observed in females [20]. Therefore, the core nodes of the CSC circuit — PWM, ACC, BG, Tha, and CB — are considered key regions for uncovering the sex-specific neuro-pathological mechanisms of BD-I.

Meanwhile, neuro-metabolic lateralization provides a new perspective for understanding sex differences in emotional states. Asymmetry in metabolic activity between brain hemispheres has been shown to be closely related to lateralized emotional processing [21]. For instance, enhanced metabolism in the left prefrontal cortex is more associated with positive emotion regulation, whereas excessive activation on the right side may drive negative emotional responses [22]. In BD-I patients, such metabolic lateralization may be further amplified due to sex-specific hormone levels or neurotransmitter regulation patterns. However, existing MRS studies are mostly limited to the analysis of single brain regions, lacking systematic integration of the metabolic features across multiple nodes within the CSC circuit [23], and have yet to clarify how these features mediate the sex-specific expression of symptoms.

Based on the above evidence, we hypothesize that BD-I patients may exhibit sex-related differences in key brain regions and metabolite profiles within the CSC circuit. Specifically, we propose that there are sex-specific structural and metabolic lateralization patterns, and that these lateralization features are associated with the type of affective episode (manic or depressive). To test this hypothesis, the present study integrates structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), selecting the core nodes of the CSC circuit as regions of interest to quantify their structural volumes and neuro-metabolic characteristics, and calculating lateralization indices (LI) to systematically evaluate sex-specific CSC circuit structural and metabolic lateralization patterns in BD-I patients and their relationship with episode type.

Methods

Participants

The study’s final evaluable cohort included 109 patients with bipolar I disorder during a mood episode, comprising 49 men and 60 women, assessed between July 2020 and July 2023. To control for neuroimaging heterogeneity among subtypes of bipolar disorder, this study strictly limited the sample to BD-I patients. All diagnoses were made by two senior psychiatrists according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and all participants met the diagnostic criteria for either a manic or depressive episode and completed clinical assessment and MRI examination during the current episode. For acutely manic participants, a standardized quality-control protocol was implemented to ensure safe and reliable scanning: pre-scan orientation and behavioral guidance (including posture rehearsal and breathing/relaxation instructions), continuous accompaniment and real-time monitoring by trained clinical staff, additional head stabilization with foam padding to minimize motion, segmentation of the protocol into shorter acquisition blocks with brief rest periods as needed, and immediate re-acquisition of sequences showing visible motion artifacts. The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 software. A two-tailed test was adopted, with α set at 0.05, power (1–β) at 0.8, and an intergroup effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.6, indicating that at least 44 participants were required in each group to meet statistical power requirements. Considering potential data loss and image quality issues, the actual sample size was increased by an additional 20%.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 60 years; (2) current episode met DSM-5 criteria for manic or depressive episode; (3) at least one clearly documented history of BD-I episode; (4) no severe physical illness. Exclusion criteria were: (1) comorbidity with other major psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, severe anxiety disorders); (2) comorbid organic neurological diseases (e.g., stroke, traumatic brain injury); (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) history of severe substance dependence (e.g., opioids, cocaine); (5) inability to complete MRI examination or poor image quality. The presence of other major psychiatric disorders and organic neurological diseases was ruled out through a comprehensive clinical assessment conducted by two senior psychiatrists, which included a structured interview based on DSM-5 criteria (SCID-5), review of detailed medical records, neurological examination, and, when necessary, neuroimaging or laboratory tests to exclude relevant conditions. All enrolled participants were in a medication-free state at the time of scanning. A medication-free state was defined as discontinuation of oral antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and antipsychotics for at least 2 weeks, and discontinuation of short-acting benzodiazepines or hypnotics for at least 7 days, in order to minimize the potential influence of residual pharmacological effects on MRI/MRS measurements. Accordingly, the medication history reported in Table 1 reflects prior clinical exposure rather than concurrent pharmacological effects at the time of imaging. The main clinical reasons for the medication-free state included self-discontinuation due to poor adherence, discontinuation due to adverse effects, and referral to our hospital after medication cessation. Participants were grouped according to sex, and their current episode type (manic or depressive) was recorded. General clinical data were collected via the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including sex, age, education level, age at first onset, time since most recent episode, number of previous episodes, family history, and past medication history. “Time since most recent episode” was defined as the interval from the most recent episode onset to the time of enrollment. “number of previous episodes” was counted only when a clear clinical diagnosis with objective documentation was available, while patient self-reports without confirmatory evidence were not included. After applying the inclusion/exclusion criteria and quality control for imaging data, the final effective sample sizes for the sex-episode subgroups were: male mania (n = 31), male depression (n = 18), female mania (n = 17), and female depression (n = 43). These subgroup numbers were used in the corresponding sex-stratified analyses.

The Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [24] and the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [25] were used to quantify the severity of manic and depressive symptoms, respectively. A YMRS total score ≥ 20 indicated a manic episode, and a HAMD-17 total score ≥ 8 indicated a depressive episode. These scores were used as auxiliary evaluation indicators and not as independent diagnostic criteria. All scale ratings were independently completed by two psychiatrists, and the average value was used as the final score. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients’ legal guardians.

MRI acquisition

All scans were performed on a Siemens 3.0T Trio MRI scanner. Subjects were placed in the supine position with a dedicated head coil. Earplugs and foam padding were used to reduce noise and minimize head motion. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, relax, and remain still during the scan. The scan parameters were as follows: T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 24 ms; T2-weighted imaging (T2WI): TR = 4600 ms, TE = 106 ms, number of excitations (NEX) = 1, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm, slice thickness = 5 mm.

After ruling out organic brain lesions such as tumors or vascular abnormalities, axial T2WI images were used for MRS anatomical localization. Multi-voxel ¹H-MRS scanning was performed using the point-resolved spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence. Parameters were set as: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 35 ms, NEX = 2, voxel size = 10 × 10 × 10 mm³. FOV and spatial matrix were adjusted according to the region of interest (ROI). The regions of interest (ROIs) for the MRS scan included the prefrontal white matter (PWM), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), basal ganglia (BG), thalamus (Tha), and cerebellum (CB). Voxel placement was performed by a senior radiologist, as shown in Fig. 1. To reduce lipid contamination from the scalp, saturation bands were placed around the VOI. Automatic shimming, water suppression, and tuning were performed by the scanner. Endogenous water signal was simultaneously acquired and used as a reference for absolute quantification.

Imaging data processing and analysis

Structural image preprocessing and volumetric analysis were conducted using the FSL software package. First, raw DICOM-format data were converted to NIfTI format, and the images were reoriented to MNI space. Each subject’s T1-weighted image was linearly registered to the MNI152 standard space (2 mm resolution) using the FLIRT tool with 12 degrees of freedom and mutual information as the similarity metric. Non-linear normalization to the MNI152 space was then performed using the FNIRT tool. Intracranial volume (ICV) correction was performed using the SIENAX tool, which extracted ICV values for use as covariates in subsequent volumetric analyses. Segmentation and ROI definition followed a modular pipeline. The basal ganglia (BG) and thalamus (Tha) were segmented using FSL FIRST. The cortical anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) was derived from a probability-thresholded Harvard–Oxford atlas mask, inverse-warped to native space and constrained by subject-specific GM maps from FSL FAST (p > 0.5). The prefrontal white matter (PWM) was defined by intersecting prefrontal lobe masks in standard space with native-space WM probability maps from FAST (p > 0.5). The cerebellum (CB) was isolated using the SUIT toolbox. Segmentation results were visually inspected and manually corrected by two experienced neuroimaging experts to ensure accuracy. Standardized volume (sV) of each ROI was calculated using the fslstats tool and exported for subsequent statistical analysis.

MRS metabolic analysis followed strict quality control criteria and quantitative analysis methods. All spectra included in the analysis were required to meet the following QC standards: full width at half maximum (FWHM) ≤ 16 Hz, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ≥ 15, and Cramér–Rao lower bounds (CRLB) < 20%. Voxels with high baseline noise or significant artifacts were excluded. Analyzed metabolites included N-acetylaspartate (NAA), creatine (Cr), choline (Cho), myo-inositol (mIns), and glutamate–glutamine complex (Glx). Spectral preprocessing was conducted using Jmrui (v5.2), including baseline correction, phase correction, and frequency alignment. Metabolite concentrations were quantified using the endogenous water signal as an internal reference. Partial volume effects were corrected based on the cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter, and white matter proportions in each voxel using FSL. Final metabolite concentrations were expressed in absolute units (mM). The lateralization index (LI) was defined as left/right [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. General clinical characteristics between the two groups were compared as follows: for continuous variables, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess distribution. Variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-sample t-tests. Variables with non-normal distribution were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Table 1 provides only descriptive statistics for the full analysis set (FAS, n = 109; male 49, female 60) and does not involve inferential analyses across episode types.

For brain structure and metabolic feature analysis, a preliminary univariate analysis was performed on the standardized volumes of the bilateral PWM, ACC, BG, Tha, and CB, as well as the neuro-metabolic indices in those ROIs. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to screen candidate features showing significant intergroup differences. These features were then subjected to LASSO regression for dimensionality reduction, and 10-fold cross-validation was used to optimize the regularization parameter (λ), identifying key sex-related features with non-zero coefficients. These analyses were based on sex grouping (male n = 49 vs. female n = 60), rather than mixing manic and depressive subgroups. To provide a state-specific biological reference, we additionally included a group of healthy controls (HC, n = 20). We then performed three-group comparisons among reclassified manic patients (n = 48), depressive patients (n = 61), and HC (n = 20). Between-group differences were examined using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Group comparisons of LIs were conducted in an exploratory manner. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, with the adjusted significance threshold set at P < 0.007 (0.05/7). Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were also reported to provide a more comprehensive assessment. The LI comparisons were likewise performed between the male group (n = 49) and the female group (n = 60).

To evaluate the predictive value of CSC circuit structural and metabolic features for BD-I episode types—and to minimize confounding effects between sex and affective polarity—a sex-stratified modeling strategy was employed. The prediction task was defined as distinguishing mania vs. depression within the same sex, rather than across sexes. Accordingly, the prediction datasets consisted of: male group (mania n = 31 vs. depression n = 18) and female group (depression n = 43 vs. mania n = 17). Based on this, XGBoost models were constructed using either the absolute structural/metabolic features (7 variables) or the lateralization indices (5 variables). Model training and evaluation were conducted using 10-fold cross-validation to reduce overfitting, and performance metrics were averaged across folds. Model hyperparameters were optimized using GridSearchCV on top of default values. Sex-stratified models were trained and tested independently. For the cross-sex prediction, existing models were tested directly on samples of the opposite sex without retraining, to assess generalizability.

Model performance was primarily evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). DeLong tests were used to compare AUCs between models for statistical significance. Additionally, to assess robustness and mitigate potential overfitting in small samples, 1,000-label permutation testing was conducted, and permutation-based p-values were reported alongside the original results. To further interpret model mechanisms, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed to evaluate the contribution of each feature. SHAP dependence plots were drawn to visualize the relationships between feature values and prediction outputs.

Results

Comparison of general clinical characteristics between groups

A total of 109 patients with BD-I during an episode were included in this study, comprising 49 males (31 manic episodes, 18 depressive episodes) and 60 females (17 manic episodes, 43 depressive episodes). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, education level, age of onset, time since most recent episode, number of previous episodes, or positive family history (P > 0.05). However, significant sex differences were observed in episode type distribution and medication history (P < 0.05): the proportion of depressive episodes was significantly higher in females, whereas the proportion of manic episodes was significantly higher in males. Sex differences in CSC circuit structural and metabolic features

A total of 10 structural volume features and 50 metabolic features of the CSC circuit were extracted based on independent measurements by two raters. Inter-rater consistency evaluation showed an ICC of 0.836 (95% CI: 0.685–0.914, P < 0.001), with 48 features demonstrating high consistency (ICC > 0.75). Mann–Whitney U tests were used to screen features with significant between-group differences, retaining 21 candidate features. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was then applied to optimize the regularization parameter (λ) and select key sex-related features. Finally, 7 features were retained, including 2 structural volume features—L-ACC-sV and R-BG-sV—which were significantly higher in males (P < 0.05); and 5 metabolic features—L-ACC-Glx, L-ACC-NAA, and L-BG-NAA were significantly higher in males (P < 0.05); R-BG-Cho and L-Tha-Cho were significantly higher in females (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Compared with HC, patients in the depressive phase showed lower L-ACC-NAA (q = 0.020) and higher R-BG-Cho and L-Tha-Cho (q = 0.004 and 0.030), while no significant differences were observed for the remaining measures. Sex differences in CSC circuit lateralization indices (LIs)

As an exploratory step, lateralization indices (LIs) were calculated for the 7 previously selected features to examine potential sex differences. After applying Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/7), structural features showed that ACC-sV LI was higher in the male group, whereas BG-sV LI was lower in males compared with females. For metabolic features, males exhibited higher ACC-NAA LI, ACC-Glx LI, and BG-Cho LI than females. No significant sex differences were observed for BG-NAA LI or Tha-Cho LI. Performance of sex-stratified XGBoost prediction models

Sex-stratified XGBoost models were constructed using the 7 absolute structural and metabolic features as input variables to evaluate their predictive performance for different episode types. Results showed that the model’s performance was limited when predicting manic episodes in the male group (Model A: AUC = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.391–0.728, P = 0.481) and depressive episodes in the female group (Model B: AUC = 0.587, 95% CI: 0.434–0.726, P = 0.337).

After reconstructing the models using the 5 LI features, prediction performance significantly improved: In the male group, Model C achieved an AUC of 0.803 for predicting manic episodes (95% CI: 0.675–0.938, P < 0.001; DeLong test: P = 0.002); In the female group, Model D achieved an AUC of 0.824 for predicting depressive episodes (95% CI: 0.713–0.937, P < 0.001; DeLong test: P < 0.001). However, performance in cross-sex prediction was poor: When applied to the female group, Model C achieved an AUC of only 0.623 for predicting manic episodes (95% CI: 0.463–0.779, P = 0.147); When applied to the male group, Model D achieved an AUC of only 0.604 for predicting depressive episodes (95% CI: 0.436–0.768, P = 0.237) (Table 3). The AUCs of the significant models remained robust according to permutation testing (P < 0.05).

Feature contribution analysis based on SHAP values

To further interpret the prediction mechanisms of the models, SHAP values were used to assess the contribution of each feature variable to model decisions. In the XGBoost model for predicting manic episodes in the male group, SHAP dependence plots showed a certain degree of correlation between observed values and SHAP values for the five feature variables, with ACC-Glx LI exhibiting the most prominent dependency trend. In the model predicting depressive episodes in the female group, SHAP dependence plots revealed that ACC-NAA LI had the strongest dependency trend, followed by BG-Cho LI (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study integrated magnetic resonance structural imaging and spectroscopic metabolic imaging techniques to systematically investigate the neuro-lateralization characteristics of the cortico-striatal-cerebellar (CSC) circuit during episodes of bipolar I disorder (BD-I) and its sex-specific regulatory mechanisms. The findings revealed not only sexual dimorphism in the neuroanatomical structure of the CSC circuit but also showed sex-specific associations of key neuro-metabolites. Notably, in our exploratory within-sample analyses, the quantified neuro-metabolic lateralization indices (LIs) suggested potential sex-related patterns that may be associated with symptom-onset types, although these findings should be interpreted with caution. SHAP analysis further indicated that ACC-Glx LI may provide information for manic episodes within the male subgroup, whereas ACC-NAA LI and BG-Cho LI were associated with depressive episodes within the female subgroup. These findings provide preliminary neuroimaging evidence consistent with the hypothesis of “affective polarity–neural circuit imbalance,” which may partially account for sex differences in symptom onset types in BD-I [26].

This study found that male BD-I patients had significantly greater volumes in the left ACC and right BG compared to females, which may originate from sex-specific neurodevelopmental patterns and hormonal regulation. In healthy individuals, larger ACC volume is typically associated with stronger emotional regulation capabilities [27]; however, in BD-I patients (especially males), such volume enlargement may reflect altered activation of emotional regulation circuits, which could be related to a greater tendency toward manic symptoms. In contrast, the relatively smaller ACC volume in female patients may indicate reduced emotional regulation capacity, which might contribute to a higher vulnerability to depressive symptoms [28]. Additionally, the increased right BG volume in males may be related to stronger reward sensitivity and impulsive behavior tendencies, whereas the relatively smaller volume in females may be associated with greater emotional susceptibility [29]. During acute manic episodes, higher ACC-NAA levels and larger ACC volume observed in males should not be directly interpreted as evidence of cortical integrity. NAA measured by MRS reflects not only neuronal viability but also mitochondrial energy metabolism and cellular osmotic regulation; it may increase under conditions of heightened energetic demand or compensatory processes. Therefore, when elevated ACC-NAA and volume occur together with concurrent increases in Glx, a more plausible interpretation may be a “state-trait layering”: acute glutamatergic hyper-excitation (state effect) superimposed on compensatory structural-metabolic alterations (trait effect), rather than a simple marker of preserved cortical integrity. Since structural volume changes more often reflect stable neurodevelopmental trajectories, while clinical symptoms are fluctuating and episodic in nature, their dynamic relationship may be difficult to fully capture in cross-sectional studies—consistent with our SHAP analysis results suggesting that volume features had limited direct predictive value for symptom types.

This study further suggested that metabolic differences and lateralization patterns of the ACC and BG might be related to the type of BD-I episode, but these observations remain exploratory and require validation in larger, independent samples. The higher left-sided ACC-Glx levels in males may indicate relatively increased excitatory neurotransmission in this region, which could contribute to emotional disinhibition and impulsive behaviors. Animal studies have shown that testosterone enhances glutamatergic activity, while estrogen may influence glutamate release by modulating synaptic plasticity [30, 31]. In contrast, female patients exhibited a different metabolic profile, characterized by reduced left-sided ACC-NAA and increased right-sided BG-Cho. Decreased NAA typically indicates impaired energy metabolism or neuronal injury [32], aligning with clinical observations that female BD-I patients are more prone to cognitive impairment [33]. Increased BG-Cho suggests glial activation or heightened neuroinflammation [34], which might disrupt the efficiency of cortico-limbic circuit regulation. Together, these two patterns may represent a dual mechanism of “impaired neural energy metabolism and enhanced inflammation/demyelination,” which could underlie vulnerability to depressive symptoms such as affective blunting and reduced motivation.

Building upon this, the lateralization index (LI) provides a global assessment of bilateral structure and metabolic activity, allowing for a potentially more sensitive detection of functional imbalances between the two hemispheres. Relying solely on the absolute values of unilateral metabolic features is prone to being obscured by individual variability; this is supported by the modest performance of the prediction models based on unilateral absolute values in this study. In contrast, LI integrates information from both hemispheres and more directly reflects the degree of disruption in interhemispheric network coordination. In this study, prediction performance consistently improved within cross-validation when models were constructed using LIs. For example, when the left-sided ACC-Glx in males was relatively elevated relative to the right (leftward lateralization), this pattern may be linked to heightened emotional excitability and impulsivity; in females, lower LIs (rightward lateralization) for ACC-NAA and BG-Cho indicated that neuronal dysfunction and inflammation/demyelination might be linked to alterations in emotion and cognition networks, potentially contributing to vulnerability to depressive episodes. Previous studies have shown that the left cortico-striatal pathway plays a more facilitative role in motivation and active emotional regulation, whereas the right pathway is more involved in emotion recognition and negative affect processing [35, 36]. This sex-specific lateralization pattern may partly help explain the tendency toward heightened reward drive and impulsive decision-making observed in males during manic episodes, while in females it may relate to increased emotional reactivity and reduced regulatory capacity, thereby contributing to a greater vulnerability to depressive episodes. In summary, LIs may provide an exploratory perspective on sex-specific pathophysiological mechanisms in BD-I, highlighting potential directions for future studies. However, these findings should not be regarded as definitive evidence, and their potential as biomarkers needs further validation in independent and longitudinal cohorts.

In this study, no significant sex differences in structural or metabolic features were detected in the cerebellar region. This negative finding may be due to several factors. On the one hand, compared to cortical or striatal regions, the cerebellum’s role in emotional regulation is more indirect, possibly exerting its modulatory effects through relay functions or cooperative engagement in distributed cognitive-emotional regulatory networks [37, 38]. As a result, potential sex effects in the cerebellum are more difficult to detect at the macroscopic structural or metabolic level. On the other hand, the spatial resolution of MRS voxels in the cerebellum is limited. Coupled with the cerebellum’s inherently complex anatomical configuration and structural heterogeneity [39], this may reduce the sensitivity for detecting sex differences in metabolic features. Although the current findings indicate possible sex-related lateralization differences in the structure and metabolism of the CSC circuit in BD-I patients, imaging-based sex differences should not be directly interpreted as a causal basis for sex differences in symptom onset types. To minimize potential confounding between sex and affective polarity, we employed a sex-stratified modeling strategy, treating episode type as a within-sex classification target. However, due to the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional design, some uncontrolled interaction confounders may still exist—such as differences in disease stage, neuroendocrine regulation, time since most recent episode, or age distribution—which may contribute to the observed biological heterogeneity.

Traditional clinical diagnosis of BD-I primarily relies on symptom-based assessments. However, objective neuroimaging biomarkers may provide more targeted intervention strategies for patients of different sexes. This study still has several limitations. First, although 109 acute-phase BD-I patients were included, the effective sample sizes decreased after sex-episode subgrouping. This uneven distribution among subgroups may increase the risk of bias and reduce the statistical power of subgroup analyses, particularly for smaller subgroups. The relatively small sample sizes in the four subgroups may have reduced statistical power and widened confidence intervals. In the future, we plan to expand the sample through multicenter collaboration and implement stratified recruitment by sex-episode type at the enrollment stage to verify the reproducibility and robustness of our findings. Second, although marked sex differences in prior medication exposure were observed, all patients were scanned in a strict medication-free state, making acute drug effects unlikely to confound the imaging or LI results. Nevertheless, lifetime medication exposure may reflect illness severity and could contribute to long-term neurobiological alterations, which warrants consideration in future longitudinal studies. Third, due to the voxel resolution limits of MRS, a voxel-wise match between structural and metabolic data was not feasible, which may affect the accuracy of assessing spatial distributions of metabolites. Moreover, the study lacked an independent control group, and although 10-fold cross-validation with hyperparameter optimization was employed to mitigate overfitting, this cannot fully replace external validation. Future multicenter collaborations are still needed to establish independent BD-I cohorts to verify the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Fourth, as laboratory and neuroimaging examinations were conducted only when clinically indicated, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of latent somatic disorders (e.g., hypothyroidism), which may have influenced the observed findings. Fifth, in the sex-stratified prediction models, manic and depressive patients were combined within each sex-specific dataset. This approach was necessary to maintain modeling feasibility but may have introduced within-group heterogeneity, potentially affecting model stability and limiting interpretability. Lastly, as a cross-sectional study, causal relationships between these features and disease progression cannot be determined. Future longitudinal studies with multiple time points are needed to explore the evolving trajectories of CSC circuit lateralization and its potential predictive value in affective polarity shifts.
