Development of a fetal renal ultrasound score for predicting neonatal renal dysfunction
Authors: Alessia Maria Merone, Sara Mannolini, Marco Zaffanello, Elena Mantovani, Irene Maria Padovani, Milena Brugnara, Valentino Bergamini, Gabriele Saccone, Francesca Presti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNeonatal renal dysfunction is a congenital abnormality of renal development caused by disrupted interaction between the ureteric bud and the metanephric blastema, resulting in disorganized renal parenchyma and variable degrees of functional impairment. Prenatal diagnosis remains challenging due to the limited specificity of ultrasound findings and the frequent need for postnatal confirmation. A standardized prenatal ultrasound score may improve identification of fetuses at higher risk of neonatal renal dysfunction among those with suspected renal abnormalities.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational study including 59 pregnancies with fetuses showing ultrasound features suggestive of renal pathology. A prenatal ultrasound score was constructed using five routinely assessed parameters: renal parenchymal hyperechogenicity, hydronephrosis severity, renal longitudinal diameter, corticomedullary differentiation, and amniotic fluid index. Postnatal renal outcome was determined using clinical, radiological, and/or histopathological data.ResultsA prenatal ultrasound score ≥ 6 was associated with a predicted probability of neonatal renal dysfunction exceeding 50%. A progressive dose-response relationship was observed between increasing prenatal scores and adverse renal outcomes. Fetuses diagnosed postnatally with neonatal renal dysfunction had significantly higher prenatal ultrasound scores compared with those with similar prenatal findings but preserved renal function at birth.ConclusionsA composite prenatal ultrasound score integrating routinely assessed renal parameters is associated with neonatal renal dysfunction in fetuses with suspected renal pathology. Higher scores identify an increasing risk of adverse renal outcome and may support prenatal prognostic assessment.

Introduction

Neonatal renal dysfunction results from abnormal development and differentiation of the metanephric blastema and impaired interaction with the ureteric bud, leading to structural and histological abnormalities of the developing renal parenchyma [1]. The condition may involve the entire renal parenchyma, including both cortical and medullary components, present as subtotal dysplasia affecting either the cortex or the medulla, or manifest as focal dysplasia.

When neonatal renal dysfunction is total, the developmental insult occurs early in gestation, resulting in hypoplastic or atrophic kidneys, frequently associated with absence or atresia of ureteral or ureteropelvic structures, or, conversely, with enlarged kidneys exhibiting a multicystic appearance.

In subtotal or focal neonatal renal dysfunction, part of the renal parenchyma develops normally while another portion is dysplastic. Renal size may be reduced, normal, or mildly increased, and renal function is more often relatively preserved [2].

A definitive diagnosis can only be established postnatally through a comprehensive approach that includes imaging investigations, clinical and biochemical assessment, or histopathological examination following termination of pregnancy or spontaneous abortion [3]. Although prenatal genetic testing plays a fundamental role in the diagnosis of congenital renal disorders, genotype-phenotype correlations remain highly variable in many inherited kidney diseases. Therefore, high-quality prenatal ultrasound is important for accurate fetal phenotypic characterization and prenatal risk stratification.

Limitations of prenatal ultrasound include subjectivity in image interpretation and the frequent incompleteness of prenatal information, which complicate counseling, particularly when sonographic features are considered in isolation rather than within a broader, integrated assessment [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Several sonographic parameters have been proposed as potential predictors of postnatal renal outcome, however, no single ultrasound finding can reliably predict neonatal renal function. Furthermore, there is currently no validated and widely accepted fetal renal ultrasound scoring system that integrates multiple sonographic features for prognostic purposes.

Renal hyperechogenicity, for instance, is the ultrasound feature most strongly associated with non-functioning fetal kidneys, although it is not specific for neonatal renal dysfunction. All fetuses with hyperechogenic kidneys require careful evaluation, however, prognosis varies on a case-by-case basis depending on the degree of hyperechogenicity, laterality (unilateral or bilateral involvement), renal size and volume, amniotic fluid index (AFI), and preservation of corticomedullary differentiation.

Prognosis is highly heterogeneous and also depends on additional factors, including family history of renal disease (such as autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease), the presence or absence of associated structural anomalies, and the presence or absence of chromosomal abnormalities and/or rare genetic diseases or syndromes, including Bardet-Biedl, Meckel-Gruber, Beckwith-Wiedemann, Perlman syndromes, among others [9].

Objective

The aim of this study was to construct a prenatal ultrasound score based on standardized fetal renal parameters and to evaluate whether this score is associated with neonatal renal functional outcome by applying it to fetuses with ultrasound features suggestive of suspected renal pathology.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at the Fetal Medicine and Prenatal Diagnosis Unit, Division B, Obstetrics and Gynecology, University of Verona. All examinations were performed as part of routine prenatal care or referral second level evaluation. The study period extended from 1 of October 2018–1 of June 2025.

All pregnant women undergoing prenatal ultrasound during the study period who were found to have a fetus with suspected renal abnormality were screened for eligibility. Cases were identified through the institutional ultrasound database and prenatal diagnosis records.

A higher score [2] was assigned to hypoplastic kidneys (renal longitudinal diameter <5th percentile) than to enlarged kidneys (renal longitudinal diameter >95th percentile). This weighting was based on published evidence showing that renal hypoplasia shares a common embryological basis with neonatal renal dysfunction, both resulting from impaired nephrogenesis and reduced nephron endowment. Winyard and Chitty [7] described reduced fetal renal size as a characteristic prenatal feature of renal hypo/dysplasia, while Bonsib [13] highlighted the close developmental continuum between renal hypoplasia and hypo/dysplasia, emphasizing their shared embryological basis. In contrast, nephromegaly may be observed in a broader spectrum of congenital renal disorders and does not necessarily reflect irreversible neonatal renal dysfunction.

Cortico-medullary differentiation was evaluated based on the preservation of the normal distinction between the renal cortex (extending from the external renal profile to the base of the pyramids, with normal echogenicity) and the hypoechoic medullary pyramids. Loss of corticomedullary differentiation has been reported as a characteristic sonographic finding in severe fetal renal disease, supporting its inclusion as one of the components of the proposed ultrasound score [14]. Polyhydramnios was assigned 1 point, whereas oligohydramnios and anhydramnios were assigned 2 and 3 points, respectively, reflecting the stronger association between reduced amniotic fluid volume and impaired fetal renal function.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary postnatal outcome was neonatal renal dysfunction, defined as an adverse renatal outcome. In live-born infants, outcome classification was based on the overall postnatal nephrological assessment, incorporating renal morphology on postnatal ultrasound, renal functional imaging when clinically indicated, and the subsequent need for nephrological or urological follow-up or intervention. A single predefined serum creatinine threshold was not used to classify the outcome. In pregnancies ending in termination, postnatal functional assessment was not available, and renal abnormalities were assessed by pathological/postmortem examination. These outcome components therefore represent clinically distinct endpoints and were combined for the exploratory analysis

Following birth, all neonates were followed by a pediatric nephrologist. The initial postnatal assessment included serum creatinine measurement and postnatal renal ultrasound, performed within the first month of life to confirm the prenatal findings and assess postnatal renal morphology. When clinically indicated, further diagnostic evaluation included renal scintigraphy and/or pediatric urological surgical assessment. Cases with normal renal findings and preserved renal function constituted the comparison group.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate based on data distribution. Categorical variables were reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between fetuses with normal postnatal renal function and those with neonatal renal dysfunction were performed using the Student t test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The association between the fetal renal ultrasound score and neonatal renal dysfunction was assessed using univariable logistic regression analysis, with neonatal renal dysfunction as the dependent variable and the total ultrasound score as a continuous independent variable.

Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the change in odds of renal dysfunction associated with each one-point increase in the score.

The discriminative performance of the fetal renal ultrasound score was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the ability of the score to distinguish between fetuses with and without neonatal renal dysfunction. The 95% confidence interval for the AUC was estimated using bootstrap resampling. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS.

Results

Fifty-nine fetuses with prenatally suspected renal abnormalities were included, of whom 38 (64.4%) had normal postnatal renal function and 21 (35.6%) developed neonatal renal dysfunction. Maternal age differed between groups, with younger maternal age in the dysfunction cohort (mean 30.4 vs. 34.9 years). A family history of renal disease was more frequently reported in fetuses who subsequently exhibited renal impairment (7/21 vs. 3/38) ( Table 1). Across prenatal ultrasound assessments, several abnormalities were more prevalent or more severe in the dysfunction group. Mean parenchymal echogenicity scores were higher in fetuses later diagnosed with neonatal renal dysfunction (1.05 vs. 0.41), and impaired cortico-medullary differentiation was nearly twice as common (1.55 vs. 0.73). A score equal to or greater than 6 was associated with a predicted probability of neonatal renal dysfunction exceeding 50% according to the logistic model. This reflects that the majority of fetuses with a score ≥ 6 subsequently developed an unfavorable renal outcome, although this threshold did not correspond to certainty of renal non-function at the individual level. Fetuses with higher scores typically exhibited either multiple concomitant ultrasound abnormalities or one to two particularly severe features

Univariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated a positive and statistically significant association between the ultrasound score and neonatal renal dysfunction. Each one-point increase in the score was associated with a 32% increase in the odds of neonatal renal dysfunction (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.06–1.65), indicating that the score functioned as a progressive risk indicator.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.70, indicating moderate discriminative performance (95% CI 0.55–0.84).

Discussion

Comparison with prior literature

In this single-center retrospective observational study, fetuses who developed neonatal renal dysfunction demonstrated significantly higher prenatal ultrasound scores compared with those with preserved postnatal renal function. The scoring system showed a clear dose-response relationship, with a score ≥ 6 corresponding to a predicted probability of neonatal renal dysfunction greater than 50%. These findings indicate that a structured composite score captures clinically relevant variations in prenatal renal morphology and may support improved risk stratification. Previous studies have highlighted the limited prognostic utility of isolated sonographic markers, such as hyperechogenicity, aberrant renal size, or abnormal amniotic fluid volume,given their variable expression across different renal pathologies and gestational ages [12]. Consistent with this literature, our results reinforce the notion that single features rarely provide adequate discrimination between dysplastic and non-dysplastic kidneys. Similarly, Baudin et al. [15] evaluated 66 fetuses with renal oligohydramnios secondary to congenital kidney disease and identified early-onset oligohydramnios, anhydramnios before 32 weeks' gestation, and sonographic evidence of renal hypodysplasia detected during the second trimester as independent prenatal predictors of poor neonatal survival.

Previous evidence consistently indicates that no single prenatal ultrasound finding is sufficiently accurate to predict postnatal renal outcome in fetuses with congenital kidney disease. In a systematic review of fetuses with congenital lower urinary tract obstruction, Morris et al. [16] demonstrated that although several prenatal sonographic parameters were associated with postnatal renal function, each individual marker showed limited predictive accuracy when considered in isolation. These findings support the need for an integrated prenatal assessment rather than reliance on isolated ultrasound features [[17], [18], [19]]. Although the present study included a broader spectrum of congenital kidney abnormalities than lower urinary tract obstruction alone, the same principle applies. The proposed fetal renal ultrasound score was therefore developed by combining five routinely assessed ultrasound parameters (renal longitudinal diameter, parenchymal echogenicity, corticomedullary differentiation, hydronephrosis, and amniotic fluid volume), into a single structured assessment, with the aim of providing a more comprehensive estimation of postnatal renal outcome. Among these parameters, amniotic fluid volume deserves particular attention because it reflects residual fetal renal function. Baudin et al. [15] demonstrated that early-onset oligohydramnios or anhydramnios, particularly when associated with sonographic evidence of renal hypodysplasia, was strongly associated with poor neonatal outcome, emphasizing that prognostic assessment should integrate both functional and structural prenatal findings. Similarly, Oliveira et al. [17] reported that, in fetuses with prenatal hydronephrosis, pelvic dilatation alone was insufficient to predict postnatal outcome, whereas oligohydramnios emerged as an independent prognostic factor. Taken together, these observations support the concept that integrating multiple routinely assessed sonographic parameters provides a more clinically meaningful approach to prenatal risk stratification than reliance on any individual ultrasound marker.

Clinical implication

The observed ability of the proposed score to identify fetuses at increased risk of neonatal renal dysfunction suggests a possible role in prenatal counseling, particularly in clinical contexts in which sonographic findings are equivocal or demonstrate variability over time. The identification of a score range associated with a higher estimated risk may assist clinicians in framing counseling discussions, supporting perinatal planning, and considering closer postnatal surveillance, rather than directing management decisions.

Overall, the findings suggest that a composite prenatal ultrasound score may provide incremental information beyond individual sonographic markers and may contribute to prenatal risk stratification for neonatal renal dysfunction. The presence of a dose-response relationship supports the internal coherence of the score.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The relatively small sample size, the single-center design, the partial heterogeneity of the outcome definition, and the restriction of the analysis to a univariable model limit the generalizability of the findings. These characteristics are typical of a pilot study and should be interpreted accordingly. Accordingly, the present study should be considered an exploratory association study, and the proposed score should be regarded as preliminary, as its predictive performance has not yet been internally or externally validated. Within this context, the present work should be regarded as preliminary and may serve primarily as a foundation for future research, including prospective studies, multivariable modeling, and external validation before any broader clinical application can be considered. Another limitation of the present study is the use of different reference standards to define neonatal renal dysfunction and therefore adverse renal outcome. In live-born infants, outcome classification was based on postnatal nephrological assessment, whereas in pregnancies ending in termination, renal abnormalities were assessed by pathological/postmortem examination. These represent clinically distinct endpoints and should not be considered equivalent. Moreover, a single predefined biochemical threshold was not used to define postnatal renal dysfunction. Given the limited sample size, a sensitivity analysis restricted to live-born infants was not performed. Future studies should evaluate the proposed score against a prospectively defined, homogeneous functional renal endpoint in live-born infants

Histopathological confirmation was available in cases of pregnancy termination or spontaneous abortion, whereas in live-born infants the diagnosis was based on postnatal functional assessment, including serum creatinine, postnatal renal ultrasound, nephrological evaluation, and renal scintigraphy when clinically indicated. This may have introduced some heterogeneity in outcome classification.

It should also be acknowledged that the proposed score was evaluated as a single time-point prenatal assessment in a heterogeneous population of fetuses assessed at gestational ages ≥ 20 weeks, following the routine second-trimester anatomical ultrasound examination. Consequently, the study does not provide information on the temporal evolution of the score or on the optimal gestational age at which its prognostic performance may be maximized. Since several sonographic parameters included in the score, such as renal echogenicity, corticomedullary differentiation, hydronephrosis, and amniotic fluid volume, may evolve throughout pregnancy, prospective longitudinal studies with serial ultrasound evaluations are warranted to investigate the dynamic changes of the score over time and to identify the most appropriate gestational age for its clinical application. No fetuses with posterior urethral valves were present in our study cohort, precluding evaluation of the proposed fetal renal ultrasound score in this specific clinical setting. Future studies including these patients are warranted to further assess the applicability of the score and to provide more robust evidence regarding its role in individualized prenatal counseling and perinatal management. The proposed score demonstrated moderate discriminative ability (AUC = 0.70), which is consistent with the marked biological heterogeneity of congenital renal anomalies and the multifactorial determinants of postnatal renal dysfunction. Therefore, the score should be considered a tool for prenatal risk stratification and parental counseling rather than a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Conclusions

This single-center retrospective study suggests that a standardized composite prenatal ultrasound score may provide additional prognostic information in fetuses with suspected renal pathology. By integrating multiple routinely assessed ultrasound parameters, the score captures the cumulative burden of renal abnormalities and supports risk stratification within an already pathological population, rather than serving as a diagnostic tool. Given the retrospective design, limited sample size, and moderate discriminative performance, these findings should be considered preliminary. The next step should be to evaluate the proposed fetal renal ultrasound score in a multicenter study before broader clinical application can be recommended.
