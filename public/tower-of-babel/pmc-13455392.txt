Background

Bronchopleural fistula following lung resection remains a significant challenge in thoracic surgery, with reported incidences of 0.5% after lobectomy and up to 4.5–20% after pneumonectomy [1, 2]. Endobronchial valves have gained increasing acceptance as a minimally invasive treatment option, with recent national data demonstrating a rise in EBV procedures from 65 to 230 annually between 2016 and 2021 [3]. Published series report efficacy rates of approximately 73% for EBV treatment of BPF [4], though outcomes in the specific context of chronic MRSA empyema remain incompletely characterized.

While EBV complications including migration, dislodgement, and expectoration have been described [5–7], reports specifically documenting the mechanism of valve failure are uncommon. Jenkins et al. reported valve migration to distant bronchial segments following placement for persistent air leak [5], and Lee et al. described cutaneous migration of an EBV through a chronic wound tract [6]. However, direct visualization of valve displacement at the bronchial stump site in the setting of chronic empyema has not been well documented.

Open window thoracostomy, including the Eloesser flap and its modifications, remains an important option for patients with chronic empyema refractory to less invasive interventions. Thourani et al. reported a 26-year experience with 78 patients undergoing modified Eloesser flap, demonstrating that this procedure can be performed safely as a definitive treatment, particularly when other interventions have failed [8]. We present a case in which direct intraoperative observation during Eloesser flap creation revealed the mechanism of EBV failure, contributing to understanding of EBV limitations in chronic infection.

Case presentation

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

A 74-year-old man with a history of left-sided radiation therapy for non-small cell lung cancer presented with worsening cough, dyspnea, and increased purulent secretions from a right chest tube. His surgical history included right lower lobectomy complicated by BPF and recurrent MRSA empyema. Fourteen months prior to this presentation, two Zephyr endobronchial valves (PulmonX Inc., Redwood City, CA) had been placed bronchoscopically at the bronchial stump, resulting in initial clinical improvement. However, symptom recurrence occurred at approximately 8–10 weeks post-procedure, with subsequent failure to achieve durable fistula closure despite continued medical management.

On admission, the patient appeared chronically ill. Chest computed tomography demonstrated a large loculated right pleural fluid collection with air-fluid levels consistent with empyema and evidence of persistent air leak (Fig. 1). Despite aggressive medical management including intravenous vancomycin and image-guided drainage, the air leak persisted and the empyema failed to resolve.

Preoperative chest CT demonstrating large loculated right pleural fluid collection with air-fluid levels consistent with empyema and persistent bronchopleural fistula

Given the failure of conservative measures and prior EBV therapy, the decision was made to proceed with open drainage via Eloesser flap. At redo right thoracotomy, purulent material was encountered throughout the pleural space. Examination of the bronchial stump area revealed that both endobronchial valves had displaced from their original positions. One valve was found completely dislodged and lying free within the pleural cavity, while the second remained partially attached but was surrounded by purulent material and granulation tissue. The bronchial stump itself showed evidence of erosion, with friable tissue and no intact mucosal surface at the original valve insertion site.

An Eloesser flap was created using standard technique: segments of the 6th and 7th ribs were resected, a U-shaped skin flap was fashioned, and the skin edges were sutured to the parietal pleura to establish a permanent drainage window (Fig. 2). The displaced valves were removed from the operative field. Postoperative CT imaging confirmed resolution of the loculated empyema collection and appropriate positioning of the thoracostomy window (Fig. 3).

Postoperative CT imaging confirming resolution of empyema collection and appropriate positioning of Eloesser flap drainage window

The postoperative course was complicated by transient delirium, acute kidney injury, and Candida superinfection of the wound, all of which resolved with appropriate management. The patient required prolonged hospitalization and subsequent transfer to a long-term acute care facility for continued wound care and rehabilitation.

At three-month follow-up, the Eloesser window showed healthy granulation tissue with minimal drainage, and repeat cultures were negative for bacterial and fungal growth. The patient reported improvement in respiratory symptoms. However, he also expressed that despite preoperative counseling, he had not fully anticipated the extent of chest wall exposure or the intensity of wound care required. This highlights a potential gap between standard verbal preoperative education and patient comprehension for procedures involving permanent anatomical changes. Enhanced educational approaches, potentially including visual aids, wound care demonstrations, or peer counseling from previous patients, may improve informed consent and patient preparedness for open thoracostomy procedures [9].

Discussion

This case provides direct intraoperative documentation of EBV displacement in the context of chronic MRSA empyema. Several factors may have contributed to valve failure in this patient. First, chronic infection likely caused ongoing tissue destruction at the bronchial stump, compromising the mucosal surface against which the valve seats. MRSA empyema is associated with particularly aggressive tissue inflammation and necrosis [10]. Second, the patient’s radiation history, though contralateral to the affected lung, raises the possibility of impaired tissue healing capacity. Third, persistent coughing associated with chronic infection generates repetitive mechanical stress that may gradually dislodge valves from their original position, particularly if tissue integrity is already compromised. Finally, purulent material accumulating around the valve may interfere with the seal mechanism and contribute to progressive loosening.

These observations align with prior reports of EBV complications. Valve migration has been documented in the emphysema literature, where changes in bronchial anatomy following treatment can lead to valve displacement [5, 7]. In the BPF context, the chronically infected environment may accelerate this process. The finding of one valve completely free within the pleural space, rather than migrated to another bronchial segment, suggests that in severe empyema the tissue destruction may be sufficient to allow complete valve expulsion from the airway.

Our findings have potential implications for patient selection when considering EBV therapy for BPF. While EBVs offer clear advantages in appropriate candidates, particularly those who are poor surgical candidates, the presence of active or chronic infection may represent a relative contraindication or at minimum warrant close surveillance. The early symptom recurrence in our patient (8–10 weeks) suggests that chronic MRSA empyema may be an unfavorable environment for sustained valve function. Prospective data correlating infectious status with EBV outcomes would help refine selection criteria.

The successful salvage with Eloesser flap in this case supports the continued importance of maintaining familiarity with open drainage techniques. In their 26-year experience, Thourani et al. found that all patients failed conservative interventions prior to modified Eloesser flap, and 29% had also failed prior surgical interventions [8]. A more recent series examining open window thoracostomy outcomes in the era of EBVs and fibrinolytic therapy similarly concluded that these procedures remain necessary for a subset of patients with refractory empyema [11]. Our patient’s clinical improvement, including negative cultures and improved respiratory status at follow-up, demonstrates that even after multiple prior interventions, definitive drainage can achieve meaningful outcomes.

The patient’s feedback regarding preoperative education deserves attention. Open thoracostomy creates a visible, permanent chest wall defect requiring ongoing wound care, which represents a significant departure from most patients’ surgical expectations. Studies of preoperative education demonstrate that visual aids and experiential learning improve patient comprehension and reduce postoperative anxiety [9, 12]. For procedures like Eloesser flap, incorporating photographs, videos, or meetings with prior patients may better prepare patients for the realities of long-term wound management. Principles of management of empyema related to chronicBPF include antibiotics and infection control with nutritional optimization, as well as obliteration of the fistula and infected pleural space. The endobronchial valve assists with infection control but with an existing significant infection burden with the fistula and infected pleural space, the obliteration of this infection is not possible with just the valve. This highlights the utility of the Eloesser flap through its utility in infection control and obliteration of the infected space [13].

This report has limitations inherent to single case reports, including inability to establish causation or generalize findings. We cannot determine with certainty whether valve failure would have occurred in the absence of chronic MRSA infection, nor whether earlier intervention might have prevented progression. The mechanism we observed may not apply to all cases of EBV failure. Additionally, without bronchoscopic surveillance imaging prior to failure, we cannot characterize the timeline of valve displacement.

Conclusions

This case documents direct intraoperative visualization of EBV displacement in chronic MRSA empyema, with one valve found free within the pleural space and evidence of bronchial stump erosion. The finding suggests that chronic infection may compromise valve stability through tissue destruction and persistent mechanical stress. These observations support careful patient selection for EBV therapy when chronic infection is present and highlight the continued role of open drainage procedures as salvage therapy. Enhanced preoperative education with visual aids may improve patient preparedness for procedures involving permanent anatomical changes.
