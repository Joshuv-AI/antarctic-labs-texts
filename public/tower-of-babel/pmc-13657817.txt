Personalized prediction of sequential therapeutic responses in HR+/HER2-low breast cancer using patient-derived organoids
Authors: Wei Zhao, Zhongni Wu, Chengpan Li, Hongbo Zhang, Dabing Huang, Lijuan Wang, Xiaodan Zhang, Juanjuan Zhang, Xinyu Ma, Yifan Wen, Joseph Benjamin Holman, Xudong Yuan, Tingting Wang, Wei Wang, Weiping Ding, Yueyin Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinically recommended treatment for hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-low (HR+/HER2-low) advanced breast cancer involves endocrine therapy (e.g., fulvestrant) followed by antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) such as trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd; formerly DS-8201) and sacituzumab govitecan (SG). However, the therapeutic outcomes of this sequential regimen remain suboptimal, and direct evidence is lacking as to whether this sequence is optimal. Additionally, the sequence of DS-8201 and SG is also unknown. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a promising preclinical tool, shown to predict personalized drug responses and correlate well with clinical outcomes. Therefore, using PDOs from HR+/HER2-low breast cancer, this study aims to systematically evaluate the antitumor effects of different administration sequences involving fulvestrant and ADCs (i.e., DS-8201 or SG), as well as DS-8201 and SG and to provide evidence for optimizing treatment sequencing.MethodsPDOs were established from fresh breast cancer tissues and characterized by immunohistochemistry (IHC), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and immunofluorescence (IF) to confirm histopathological and phenotypic features fidelity to original tumors. Organoid viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo 3D (CTG-3D) assay, and morphological changes were monitored by bright-field microscopy. After treatment with fulvestrant, DS-8201, or SG alone, IHC and IF were performed to detect key proteins involved in the mechanism of action of SG and DS-8201.ResultsOur results demonstrated that administering DS-8201 or SG followed by fulvestrant yielded superior antitumor effects compared with the reverse sequence (fulvestrant followed by ADCs). Moreover, organoids revealed marked patient heterogeneity in response to the sequential administration of DS-8201 and SG (i.e., DS-8201 then SG vs. SG then DS-8201). Notably, relying solely on expression levels of biomarkers alone was insufficient to accurately predict ADCs sensitivity in organoids that had received prior therapy.ConclusionsThe sequence of DS-8201/SG followed by fulvestrant is a more suitable treatment regimen than the conventional sequence (fulvestrant followed by ADCs) for HR+/HER2-low breast cancer. This study provides important preclinical evidence supporting the optimization of therapy sequences in personalized medicine and advances the application of organoid-based models in oncology research.Keywords: Patient-derived organoids (PDOs), hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-low breast cancer (HR+/HER2-low breast cancer), sequential therapy, fulvestrant, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd; formerly DS-8201)/sacituzumab govitecan (SG) followed by fulvestrant yields better efficacy than the reverse sequence in hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-low (HR+/HER2-low) breast cancer.

• Relying solely on the expression levels of biomarkers is insufficient to predict the sensitivity of treated organoids to antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)

What is known and what is new?

• Current clinical guidelines recommend endocrine therapy (e.g., fulvestrant) followed by ADCs (DS-8201 or SG) for HR+/HER2-low breast cancer, but outcomes are suboptimal and the best sequence between the ADCs is unknown.

• This study proved that optimizing the sequential regimen of ADCs and fulvestrant achieves greater efficacy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The conventional fulvestrant followed by ADCs should be reconsidered, and personalized sequencing guided by patient-derived organoid is warranted. Prospective clinical trials are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, with its global incident cases continuing to rise markedly (1,2). It is generally classified into three main subtypes: hormone receptor-positive/ human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+/HER2−), HER2-positive (HER2+), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (3). Among these, HR+/HER2− is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 68% of all BC cases (4). Within this subtype, there is significant heterogeneity and the tumors with HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC) scores of 1+ or 2+ and no in situ hybridization (ISH) amplification are classified as HER2-low, which constitutes nearly two-thirds of HR+/HER2− cases (5).

Currently, the clinically recommended treatment paradigm for HR+/HER2-low breast cancer consists of first-line endocrine therapy (ET), followed by antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) in the second-line or later settings (6-8). Fulvestrant, a potent selective estrogen receptor down-regulator (SERD), reduces estrogen receptor (ER) protein levels and inhibits ER activity, making it a widely used ET agent in clinic practice (9). Trastuzumab deruxtecan (formerly DS-8201; T-DXd) is an ADC composed of a humanized anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody, a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload (DXd), and a tetrapeptide-based cleavable linker (10). Sacituzumab govitecan (SG) is another ADC that targets trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP2) and carries a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, SN38 (11). Both DS-8201 and SG have received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicine Agency for routine clinical use in patients with unresectable or metastatic HR+/HER2-low BC (12-15). Although this recommended treatment paradigm has demonstrated notable efficacy, responses vary considerably among individuals, underscoring the urgent need to optimize current sequential treatment strategies.

Recently, several clinical trials have been initiated to evaluate the efficacy of different treatment sequences. In the first-line setting, the global phase II clinical trial PONTIAC is comparing DS-8201 monotherapy and the CDK4/6 inhibitor in combination with ET in patients with HR+/HER2-low advanced breast cancer. Meanwhile, a phase II neoadjuvant clinical trial is investigating DS-8201 in combination with anastrozole (an ET agent) in early-stage HR+/HER2-low breast cancer (16,17). In the sequence of DS-8201 and SG after ET, a real-world data from MD Anderson Cancer Center presented at the San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium (SABCS) involving 33 patients found no significant difference in progression-free survival or overall survival between the sequences of SG followed by DS-8201 and the reverse order (18). However, a large real-world cohort of US patients with metastatic BC receiving DS-8201 suggested that prior treatment with SG may diminish the efficacy of subsequent DS-8201 (19). These studies underscore the importance of not only selecting appropriate first-line therapies but also determining the optimal sequence of later-line treatments.

In recent years, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have been developed rapidly (20). PDOs can closely recapitulate the heterogeneity, genetic features and histological architecture of in vivo tumors, offering a fast and effective preclinical tool for drug screening (21,22). Compared with two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures (23-25), three-dimensional (3D) PDOs offer advantages including faster establishment, higher success rates, and lower sample requirements (26). Today, BC organoids have been successfully employed to predict the patient drug response (27-29). Thus, organoid models may serve as an ideal platform for evaluating complex multi-step sequential treatment regimens.

In this study, we evaluated, for the first time, the antitumor efficacy of various sequential treatment regimens involving fulvestrant and the ADCs DS-8201 and SG, using PDOs. Initially, organoids established from BC patients were successfully cultured, characterized, and validated for their histological features. Subsequently, the sensitivity of organoids derived from 2 patients with HR+/HER2-low BC to fulvestrant, DS-8201 and SG as monotherapies was assessed. Finally, the antitumor efficacy of different sequential regimens involving fulvestrant, DS-8201 and SG was evaluated in the organoids and compared with that observed in the MCF-7 BC cell line. The study provides preclinical evidence to inform the need for optimizing clinical sequential treatment strategies for HR+/HER2-low BC. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0950/rc).

Methods

Patient samples collection and transportation

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (USTC), Anhui, China (No. 2022KY237). The study utilized de-identified residual tumor tissues from patients who underwent surgical resection. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the biospecimens. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Breast cancer tissues from 26 patients were obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC. Immediately, after the samples were obtained via surgery or biopsy, they were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The tissues were then placed in a transport solution and transported on ice to the laboratory, where they were immediately processed (30). Detailed transport solution composition is provided in Appendix 1.

Compounds

SG (NDC 55135-132-01, Immunomedics, USA) was diluted from the infusion with a concentration of 9 mg/mL. DS-8201 (No. 389085, Daiichi Sankyo, Japan) was diluted with water for injection to 20 mg/mL. The two ADCs were stored at 4 ℃, and protected from light. Fulvestrant (No. 129453-61-8, MedChemExpress, USA) was diluted with DMSO to 1 or 10 mM and stored at −80 ℃.

Organoid establishment

Organoids were established as previous research findings (28,30,31). Fresh tumor samples were minced and digested with collagenase IV (No. C8160, Solarbio, Beijing, China). After filtration, the cell suspension was mixed with BC organoid medium (PRS-BCM-3D, Hefei PreceDo Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) and Matrigel (No. 354248, Corning, USA) in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, seeded into ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning), and cultured with medium changes every 3 days. Detailed procedures are provided in Appendix 1.

Organoid passaging

For passaging, organoids were incubated with 1 µg/mL dispase II (No. D6430, Solarbio) for 40 min to 1 h to remove the Matrigel. The organoid suspension was collected and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the organoid pellets were resuspended in 1 mL TrypLE Express (No. 12604-013, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and incubated at room temperature (RT) was for 5–10 min, accompanied by gentle trituration with 1 mL pipette (more than 10 times). Digestion was terminated by adding 10 mL PBS and the suspension was filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer. The filtrate was collected and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. After centrifugation, the organoid fragments or single cells were resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of BC organoid medium and Matrigel and re-inoculated at the ratios mentioned above (1:2–1:5) to form new organoids.

Section preparation and IHC

Organoid were fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and IHC using standard protocols. Primary antibodies against ER, progesterone receptor (PR), HER2, Ki-67, TROP2, and Schlafen 11 (SLFN11) were used. Detailed antibody information and staining conditions were provided in Appendix 1.

Organoid staining

Live/dead staining, cytoskeletal staining, and immunofluorescence (IF) for epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) and HER2 were performed on organoids. To remove Matrigel, organoids were first treated with 1 µg/mL dispase II at 37 ℃ for 40 min. Organoids were collected and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. For live/dead staining, Calcein-AM (No. C2012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and Hoechst (No. BMD0062, Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were added simultaneously and incubated for 20–30 min at 37 ℃, while propidium iodide (PI; ST511, Beyotime) was added during the last 10 min of incubation. Organoids were then washed once with PBS. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM980 with Airyscan, Germany). Detailed staining procedures for cytoskeletal staining, and IF for EpCAM and HER2 were provided in Appendix 1.

Drug response assays

Two organoids (PDO0906 and PDO1023) from HR+/HER2-low BC patients were used to evaluate sequential drug administration. The two organoids are both derived women aged between 50 and 60 years. Organoids were treated with the first drug for 144 h, followed by the second drug for an additional 144 h after washing. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® 3D (CTG-3D; G9681, Promega, USA) assay. To investigate the administration sequence of fulvestrant and ADCs, breast cancer tissues with pathologically confirmed HR+/HER2 (IHC 1+ or 2+ with FISH-negative) were included in the experiment. No exclusion criteria were applied; all samples that were successfully cultured and yielded a sufficient number of viable organoids were included. Sample size was not predetermined. Due to the scarcity of PDO samples that successfully expanded to sufficient numbers, only two organoid lines (PDO0906 and PDO1023) met the inclusion criteria. Due to the simultaneous processing of all samples, randomization was not applicable in this experiment. Detail treatment regimens and assay conditions are provided in the Appendix 1.

Cell culture and viability assay

The MCF-7 cell line was obtained from a colleague in our laboratory. The cell line was routinely maintained in our lab and used without further authentication. MCF-7 was culture with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; No. 8122452, ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (No. C2910-0500, VivaCell, Shanghai, China), 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. Cell cultures were maintained in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. The passage of cells occurred at 80% confluence every two to three days. The viability of MCF-7 cells treated with fulvestrant, SG, or DS-8201 was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Detailed culture conditions and treatment protocols are described in Appendix 1.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviation. All assays were performed in at least triplicate technical replicates. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed for statistical significance using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests without or with Welch’s correction; ns, no significance; *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001.

Results

Establishment of patient-derived breast cancer organoids

Patient-derived breast cancer organoids were established from surgically resected tumor tissues. Briefly, tissues were minced, followed by enzymatic digestion, agitation, and filtration to isolate tumor cells. The harvested cells were embedded in Matrigel and seeded into ultra-low attachment 96-well plates. The cultured organoids were passaged and expanded for subsequent drug sensitivity assays (Figure 1A). To monitor organoids growth, bright-field images were acquired every 2 days, and organoid diameters were measured. Over 16 days of culture, organoid diameters increased from initial diameters of 30–100 to nearly 300 µm, reaching a plateau stage by day 14. Organoids exhibited progressive enlargement, followed by a stable size (Figure 1B,1C). Besides, high organoid viability was confirmed by Calcein-AM/PI/Hoechst staining (Figure 1D).

Morphology and histopathological features of breast cancer organoids

ER, PR, and HER2 statuses are essential for BC staging and treatment decisions (28). H&E staining revealed that organoids derived from both patients exhibited enlarged and pleomorphic nuclei, with increased nuclear-to cytoplasmic ratios. These morphological features were similarly observed in the corresponding primary tumor tissues. According to clinical immunohistochemical analysis from the original tumor specimens, patient 1 was positive for ER (90%, 3+), HER2 (2+), and Ki-67 (50%), but negative for PR; patient 2 was positive for HER2 (3+) and Ki-67 (+, approximately 40%), with no detectable expression of ER or PR. Organoids derived from patient1 retained ER, HER2, and Ki-67 expression, while remaining negative for PR. Organoids derived from patient 2 maintained HER2 and Ki-67 expression, with ER and PR absent (Figure 2A).

F-actin, a key component of the cytoskeleton, regulates various essential cellular processes (32). To visualize cellular architecture, organoids were stained with TRITC-conjugated phalloidin for F-actin and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) for nuclei. F-actin was organized into filamentous structures distributed throughout the cytoplasm, and nuclei appeared as distinct, rounded organelles (Figure 2B). Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) was expressed in 97% of breast cancer, is a well-established marker for cancer cells (33). IF staining demonstrated that the majority of cells within the organoids were EpCAM-positive (Figure 2C).

Viability and morphological changes of organoids in response to DS-8201, SG and fulvestrant

To characterize the sensitivity of HR+/HER2-low PDOs to DS-8201, SG, and fulvestrant, and to identify appropriate concentrations for subsequent sequential treatment experiments, we assessed morphological changes and cell viability following exposure to escalating doses of each agent.

PDO0906 and PDO1023 derived from HR+/HER2-low breast cancer patients were treated with DS-8201, SG, and fulvestrant across a range of concentrations. Following treatment with DS-8201 at concentrations below the maximum tested dose, no obvious morphological changes were observed in either organoid line with both maintaining intact three-dimensional structures and clear boundaries (Figure 3A; Figure S1). Cell viability assessed using the CTG-3D viability assay revealed comparable sensitivity of the two organoid lines to DS-8201 with no significant difference in viability reduction between them (Figure 3B).

In contrast, treatment with SG induced more pronounced morphological alterations compared with DS-8201. At low concentrations, the peripheries of organoids became blurred, accompanied by the appearance of dead cell fragments. At high concentrations, structural fragmentation of organoids was observed. Notably, PDO1023 exhibited more severe damage than PDO0906 at equivalent concentrations (Figure 3C; Figure S2). Consistent with these morphological findings, the CTG-3D assay confirmed that both organoid lines were highly sensitive to SG, with PDO1023 exhibiting greater sensitivity (Figure 3D).

In contrast to the marked responses observed with the two ADCs, both PDO lines were relatively resistant to fulvestrant. Across a range of concentrations, no morphological alterations were observed in either line. Organoids retained their typical architecture, and even at the highest concentration tested, no evidence of fragmentation, peripheral blurring, or cell debris accumulation was noted (Figure 3E). Consistent with these morphological observations, the CTG-3D viability assay revealed only a modest reduction in cell viability at high concentrations, with no substantial cytotoxicity detected across the tested dose range (Figure 3F; Figure S3).

To determine appropriate concentrations for subsequent sequential experiments, the inhibitory concentration values of 30% (IC30) were calculated. The IC30 value for DS-8201 is 7.2 µg/mL for PDO0906, and 6.25 µg/mL for PDO1023. For SG, the IC30 values were 1.19 µg/mL for PDO0906 and 0.64 µg/mL for PDO1023.

Preliminary investigation into the treatment sequence of DS-8201, SG and fulvestrant

Following 5 days of culture after plating, organoids were treated with medium containing the first drug for 144 hours. After removal of the first drug and washing, organoids were subsequently exposed to the second drug in fresh medium for an additional 144 hours (drug 1 → drug 2) (Figure 4A). Based on previous studies demonstrating that 1 μM fulvestrant is effective in breast cancer organoid models (34), this concentration was selected for the subsequent experiments. As assessed by the CTG-3D assay in PDO0906, sequential administration of DS-8201 followed by fulvestrant (D → F) elicited a stronger response than the reverse sequence (F → D), with cell viability reduced to 59.27%±4.77% vs. 101.70%±9.76% (P=0.003). Furthermore, D → F resulted in superior efficacy compared with the sequential administration of DS-8201 followed by drug-free medium (D → M; 59.27%±4.77% vs. 84.84%±11.69 %; P=0.02). In addition, F → D resulted in reduced efficacy compared with drug-free medium followed by DS-8201 (M → D) in the same PDO (101.70%±9.76% vs. 78.27%±3.56%, P =0.02; Figure 4B). In PDO1023, D → F also showed a stronger response than the reverse sequence (63.15%±2.00% vs. 82.37%±4.15%; P=0.02). Moreover, F → D resulted in reduced efficacy (82.37%±4.15%) compared with M → D (69.61%±0.32%) in PDO1023 (P=0.03; Figure S4A). Organoids exposed to D → F exhibited a significantly slower rate of volume increase compared to those treated with F → D (Figure 4C).

Sequential administration of SG followed by fulvestrant (S → F) elicited a stronger response than the reverse sequence (F → S) in PDO0906 (45.47%±3.36% vs. 84.70%±9.79%, P=0.003). When reciprocal effects were evaluated, S → F and SG followed by drug-free medium (S → M) did not differ significantly in antitumor efficacy (45.47%±3.36% vs. 59.58%±12.18 %, P=0.13). Moreover, F → S showed no significant difference in efficacy compared with sequential administration of drug-free medium followed by SG (M → S) in the same PDO (84.70%±9.79% vs. 79.75%±8.83%, P=0.55; Figure 4D). Pericellular debris was more pronounced in organoids treated with S → F than in those treated with F → S (Figure 4E). Similarly, in PDO1023, S → F showed a stronger response than F → S (40.80%±1.69% vs. 96.06%±1.61%, P<0.001). However, unlike in PDO0906, F → S resulted in reduced efficacy compared with M → S in PDO1023 (96.06%±1.61% vs. 78.41%±8.2%, P=0.02; Figure S4B).

Sequential administration of SG followed by DS-8201 (S → D) showed similar response to the reverse sequence (D → S) in PDO0906 (57.77%±3.42% vs. 68.58%±8.06%, P=0.10; Figure 4F). In PDO0906, increased in cellular debris was observed surrounding organoids treated with S → D (Figure 4G). In contrast, for PDO1023, S → D showed better efficacy than D → S (33.58%±0.81% vs. 59.20%±2.62%, P<0.001). For PDO1023, D → S showed no significant difference in efficacy compared with D → M (59.20%±2.62 % vs. 61.92%±2.72%, P=0.28), whereas, S → D outperformed S → M (33.58%±0.81% vs.44.50%±4.81%, P=0.02; Figure S4C). Compared with the control group, S → D and S → F led to an increase in Pericellular debris in PDO1023 (Figure S4D).

In 2D MCF-7 cell line, the IC30 values were 0.03 µg/mL for SG, 50 nM for fulvestrant, and 9 µg/mL for DS-8201. When the sequence of administration was explored, results for fulvestrant followed by DS-8201 showed superior efficacy to the reverse order (21.94%±1.97% vs. 31.83%±3.00%, P=0.002), in contrast to the findings in organoids. In contrast, the sequential efficacy of fulvestrant and SG were consistent with the results in organoids (Figure S5).

Protein expression following ADCs and fulvestrant treatments in organoids

The expression levels of key biomarkers, including HER2, TROP2, and SLFN11, were assessed in PDO0906 following various drug treatments using IHC and IF.

IHC showed that SG and fulvestrant monotherapy did not visibly alter HER2 expression compared with the control group, with comparable staining intensity and distribution observed across groups (Figure 5A). Notably, treatment with DS-8201 or fulvestrant was associated with visibly increased expression of both TROP2 and SLFN11, as evidenced by stronger and more diffuse staining compared with the control group (Figure 5B). IF analysis revealed that SG monotherapy led to a marked reduction in HER2 membrane fluorescence intensity compared with the control group, suggesting that SG may downregulate HER2 at the cell surface (Figure 5C). Taken together, these findings suggest that while SG primarily affects HER2 membrane expression, DS-8201 and fulvestrant may promote upregulation of TROP2 and SLFN11, which could contribute to their therapeutic effects in this organoid model.

Discussion

The management of HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer typically involves sequential systemic therapies, including ET (such as SERD like fulvestrant), chemotherapy and ADCs (e.g., DS-8201 and SG). However, the potential impact of the sequence of administration on treatment efficacy has not been comprehensively investigated. Current clinical paradigms for HR+/HER2-low breast cancer often consists of first-line therapy with ET, followed by therapy with DS-8201, and then sequenced with SG (8). Under the current sequential paradigm, disease progression is difficult to avoid (35). The treatment failures highlight the urgent need to redefine the therapeutic boundaries. A French multicenter retrospective study evaluated the efficacy of different sequential regimens of DS-8201 and SG in HER2-low patients and indicated that the exposure to the first ADC may compromise patients’ sensitivity to the second ADC (35). Similarly, in a large real-world cohort of US patients with MBC receiving DS-8201, the researchers found SG may reduce the efficacy of subsequent DS-8201 (19). Therefore, further exploration into the optimal sequencing of these treatments is necessary. In this study, for the first time, we comprehensively investigated the efficacy of sequential administration of fulvestrant and DS-8201/SG, based on PDOs, and provides the preclinical evidence supporting the necessity of optimizing sequential administration for personalized treatment. In the sequence of DS-8201 or SG followed by fulvestrant, we found that it presented superior antitumor efficacy compared to the reverse order (i.e., sequential administration of fulvestrant followed by DS-8201 or SG). That is, reversing the conventional administration sequence of fulvestrant and ADCs (DS-8201 or SG) yields better therapeutic outcomes. The results suggested that the standard sequence in maximizing clinical benefit for all patients is questionable. In the sequence of DS-8201 and SG, A real-world data from MD Anderson Cancer Center presented at SABCS showed no significant difference in progression-free survival (PFS; hazard ratio 1.02, P=0.97) or overall survival (OS; hazard ratio 0.78, P=0.984) between the sequences of SG followed by DS-8201 and DS-8201 followed by SG among 33 patients who received sequential ADC treatments (18). However, we found that in some patients, the sequence of SG followed by DS-8201 presented superior antitumor efficacy to the reverse order (Figure 4B,4D; Figure S4). Therefore, it is necessary to provide each patient with a personalized medication schedule.

The results implied that it might be unreliable to determine the suitability for ADCs relying solely on immunohistochemical detection of targets and predictive markers for payloads. DS-8201 and SG are cleavable ADCs composed of antibody (anti-HER2 and anti-TROP2, respectively), a cleavable linker, and a cytotoxic payload (the DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor) (36). SLFN11 has been identified as a dominant prediction biomarker for response to DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor (37,38). It was reported that the upregulation of TROP2 or SLFN11 expression sensitizes tumor cells to SG (39). Form the view of biomarkers, upregulation of target antigen and SLFN11 expression may enhance the efficacy of ADCs. However, the findings on organoids in this study presented a more complex situation: fulvestrant pretreatment did not significantly affect the sensitivity of organoids to SG and reduced the efficacy of DS-8201 (Figure 4B,4D), whereas molecular analysis revealed that fulvestrant upregulated the expression of TROP2 and SLFN11 without altering HER2 expression (Figure 5A,5B). Cleavable ADCs exert antitumor effects primarily by releasing payload by antibody-dependent and antibody-independent pathway. The abovementioned discrepancy may result from the combined action of antibody-dependent and antibody-independent pathway. In the antibody-dependent pathway, target internalization or lysosomal function may be inhibited while in the antibody-independent pathway, fulvestrant may remodel the tumor microenvironment that affects the secretion or activity of extracellular protease such as CTSL (40). In addition, ABC transporter-mediated drug efflux also limits the efficacy of DS-8201 and SG in some tumors (41-43). Fulvestrant may increase the expression of drug efflux pumps, thereby counteracting the therapeutic outcomes from upregulation of targets and SLFN11 expression. Form the view of organoids, multiple mechanisms are combined and exerted after drug administration. Thus, PDO may directly serve as a more pervasive platform than molecular biomarkers alone for exploring personalized sequential treatment strategies involving ADCs.

Moreover, in the efficient dosing sequence prediction, we presented a divergence between PDO and the 2D MCF-7 cell line, highlighting the importance of the choice of preclinical models in evaluating sequential administration. For PDO, the sequential administration of DS-8201 followed by fulvestrant was more effective. However, for MCF-7, the reverse order was better. 2D cell lines are valuable for early drug screening due to their low cost and high-throughput compatibility (44). However, they fail to replicate the tumor microenvironment (TME), lack three-dimensional architecture, and exhibit homogeneous target expression that obscures the bystander effect (the process by which hydrophobic payloads kill adjacent antigen-negative cells) (45). In addition, long-term passaging alters their biological traits (24). reduce their reliability in predicting clinical responses to cleavable ADCs. In contrast, tumor organoids more accurately preserve the structure, TME, and cellular heterogeneity, histological phenotype, genomic alterations, and transcriptomic profiles of primary tumors (28,46). They better recapitulate the bystander effect and enable precise evaluation of payload release kinetics, making them a superior and more clinically translatable model for predicting personalized treatment outcomes (24,44-46).

In future work, several aspects require in-depth investigation. First, future targeted or whole-exome sequencing of key driver mutations will complement our current histological and protein-level validation and enable exploration of genotype–drug sensitivity correlations. Second, it should be noted that our current evaluation primarily relies on survival rates and morphological changes, which may not capture the full spectrum of anti-tumor effects and resistance dynamics. Future studies will incorporate apoptosis, proliferation, clonogenic survival, and dynamic growth curves to enable a more multidimensional and systematic assessment of sequential treatment efficacy. Third, the tumor microenvironment of organoids should be further optimized. By incorporating immune cells (e.g., T cells, macrophages), vascular endothelial cells, and matrix components, organoids can more precisely simulate in vivo drug responses and immune interactions, thereby enhancing their predictive capability for clinical efficacy. Fourth, large-scale, multicenter clinical trials are required to further confirm the potential of the PDO-guided sequential therapy. Although integrating organoid-based research into clinical practice and revising the related guidelines accordingly will be a protracted process, our preliminary findings underscore the importance of organoids in guiding individualized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated, for the first time, the therapeutic efficacy of different administration sequences of fulvestrant and ADCs (DS-8201 and SG) using organoids derived from patients with HR+/HER2-low breast cancer. Our findings demonstrate that the sequence of DS-8201/SG followed by fulvestrant yields greater efficacy in HR+/HER2-low breast cancer. Moreover, our results also indicate that relying solely on molecular expression and 2D cell lines is insufficient for guiding the optimal sequencing of fulvestrant and ADCs. This study underscores the necessity of optimizing therapy sequences in personalized medicine and advances organoid-based studies in oncology.
