Duality for Constructive Modal Logics: from Sahqlvist to Goldblatt-Thomason
Jim de Groot, Ian Shillito, Ranald Clouston, [1em] Mathematical Institute, University of Bern, Bern, Switzerland[-.2em] jim.degroot@unibe.ch, [.2em] School of Computer Science, University of Birimingham, Birmingham, UK[-.2em] i.b.p.shillito@bham.ac.uk, [.2em] School of Computing, The Australian National University, Canberra, Australia[-.2em] ranald.clouston@anu.edu.au

Abstract
We carry out a semantic study of the constructive modal logic 𝖢𝖪 \mathsf{CK} . We provide a categorical duality linking the algebraic and birelational semantics of the logic. We then use this to prove Sahlqvist style correspondence and completeness results, as well as a Goldblatt-Thomason style theorem on definability of classes of frames.

Duality

In the field of (modal) logic, duality theorems are used to bridge the
gap between the algebraic and geometric
(frame-based) views of a logic.
They can be used to prove completeness results, as well as (analogues of)
Sahlqvist completeness theorems, bisimilarity-somewhere-else, and
Goldblatt-Thomason style theorems.

The study of dualities dates back to
Stone’s representation theorem for Boolean algebras
[
46
]
,
which establishes a categorical duality between Boolean algebras
and certain topological spaces (now called Stone spaces).
Subsequent notable results include
the McKinsey-Tarski representation theorem for closure algebras

[
34
]
, the Priestley duality theorem
[
40
]
linking distributive
lattices and spaces now known as Priestley spaces, and Esakia
duality for Heyting algebras (the algebraic semantics of intuitionistic logic)
[
14
,
15
]
.

Dualities for modal logics often build on a duality for the underlying
propositional logic. The first such result was Goldblatt’s duality for modal
algebras
[
20
,
21
]
, which extends Stone duality.
This establishes a duality between the algebraic semantics of normal
modal logic on the one hand, and Kripke frames with extra structure on the
other. The resulting frames are called
descriptive
Kripke frames,
and consist of a Kripke frame together with a collection of
so-called
admissible
subsets of the frame that satisfy certain
conditions.
Similar dualities have been derived for monotone modal
logic
[
31
]
and neighbourhood logic
[
13
]
,
where in each case the geometric side of the duality is given by
frames for the logic with a suitable collection of admissible sets.

Mirroring this, various dualities for modal extensions of positive logic
were derived by
extending Priestley duality
[
9
,
25
,
28
]
.
When working over intuitionistic logic, one often builds on
Esakia duality
[
38
,
52
,
26
]
.
Again, the geometric side of these dualities is given by frames of the logic
with a collection of admissible subsets.

But in the case of
\mathsf{CK}
something interesting happens: the duality
between
\mathsf{CK}
-algebras and a descriptive analogue of
\mathsf{CK}
-frames cannot
piggy-back on Esakia duality.
The intuitive reason is that the birelational semantics for
\mathsf{CK}

makes crucial use of the fact that the intuitionistic accessibility relation
is a preorder, while Esakia duality requires a partial order.
More precisely, the
\mathsf{CK}
-frame dual to a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra is not based on a set
of prime filters, but on a set of
segments
. These are pairs

(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
consisting of a prime filter
\mathfrak{p}
together with
a set of prime filters
\Gamma
. The intuitionistic accessibility relation
is then given by inclusion of the first argument, and since we can have
different segments headed by the same prime filter, this gives rise to a
preorder but not a partial order.
Hence the duality of this paper is not merely a mathematical tool for further study of the logic,
but also has interesting features in its own right.
2
2

2

It would still be possible to piggy-back on Esakia if we used a
different semantics, for example trirelational frames.
Since we insist on using the birelational
\mathsf{CK}
-frames
we disregard such alternatives.

Sahlqvist correspondence and completeness

After the introduction of the Kripke semantics for classical modal logic,
it was rapidly noticed that some modal axioms, such as
\Box p\to p
and
\Box p\to\Box\Box p
,
correspond to properties on frames, e.g. reflexivity and transitivity.
These insights led the community to wonder about the existence
of a general theory of correspondence.

In 1975, Sahlqvist gathered the results from his master’s thesis
into what constitutes the best-to-date attempt at providing such
a theory
[
42
]
.
While modal formulas naturally correspond to second-order frame properties,
Sahlqvist’s work syntactically characterises a large class of modal formulas
which correspond to computable
first-order
frame properties.
Moreover, the addition of any subset
\Lambda
of formulas of this class to the
axioms of the classical modal logic
\mathsf{K}
yields a logic that is complete
with respect to the class of frames captured by the frame properties
corresponding to
\Lambda
.
The first result, which is known as the “Sahlqvist correspondence theorem”,
was proved independently by Van Benthem
[
4
]
,
while the second, also called “Sahlqvist completeness theorem”,
was given a simplified proof via duality by Sambin and Vaccaro
[
43
]
.

While Sahlqvist-like results have been proved for other classes of
logics
[
10
,
30
,
19
,
17
]
,
none currently exists with
\mathsf{CK}
as a basis.
In this paper we define a class of formulas for which
we establish correspondence and completeness results.
The class of formulas we capture is more restricted
than the classical one, as we notably forbid the presence of
diamonds in the antecedent of implications.
Despite this restriction, many important axioms fall under
the scope of our definition, including
the two intuitionistic versions of the
T
axiom (
\Box p\to p
and
p\to\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}p
),
the
\Box
version of the
4
axiom (
\Box p\to\Box\Box p
),
and the seriality axiom (
\Box p\to\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}p
).

Definability via a Goldblatt-Thomason theorem

A prominent question in the study of (modal) logics and their semantics
is what classes of frames can be defined as the class of frames satisfying
some set of formulas.
Such classes are often called
axiomatic
or
modally definable
.
In the context of classical normal modal logic, a partial answer to this question
was given by Goldblatt and Thomason
[
23
]
,
who proved that an elementary class
{K}
of Kripke frames is axiomatic if and only if
it reflects ultrafilter extensions and is closed under p-morphic images,
generated subframes and disjoint unions.
Instead of assuming
{K}
to be axiomatic, it suffices to assume
that it is closed under ultrafilter extensions.
The proof in
[
23
]
essentially dualises Birkhoff’s variety
theorem
[
6
]
, using ultrafilter extensions and the duality for
modal algebras to bridge the gap between the algebraic semantics and
Kripke frames.
A model-theoretic proof was provided almost twenty years later by
Van Benthem
[
5
]
.

A similar result for intuitionistic logic (without modal operators)
was proven by Rodenburg
[
41
]
(see also
[
22
]
),
where the interpreting structures are
intuitionistic
Kripke frames and models.
While this uses the same notion of p-morphic images, generated subframes
and disjoint unions, it replaces ultrafilter extensions with
so-called prime filter extensions.
More recently, Goldblatt-Thomason style theorems for many other logics have
been proven, including for positive normal modal logic
[
10
]
,
graded modal logic
[
44
]
,
modal extensions of Łukasiewicz finitely-valued logics
[
47
]
,
and modal logics with a universal modality
[
45
]
.
General approaches to such theorems for coalgebraic and dialgebraic logics
were given in
[
33
,
27
]
and
[
26
, Section 11]
.

In this paper we derive an analogue of the Goldblatt-Thomason theorem
for
\mathsf{CK}
.
Since our duality for
\mathsf{CK}
is not based on Esakia duality,
the general approach from
[
27
]
is not applicable.
But this does not stop us: using the proof idea from
[
23
]
,
we can still prove the desired analogue.
This requires two interesting modifications of the original result.
First, we modify the definition of the disjoint union of a family of frames.
Since our frames are equipped with an inconsistent world, the disjoint
union should identify all these worlds. (From a categorical point of view
nothing changes, because the resulting construction is still given by
the coproduct in the category of frames and appropriate morphisms.)
Second, we replace ultrafilter extensions with
segment extensions
.
These provide the bridge between
\mathsf{CK}
-frames and their descriptive counterparts,
and together with the duality theorem allow us to transfer Birkhoff’s
variety theorem to the class of frames.

Constructive
\mathsf{K}

2.1 Definition
.

Constructive

\varphi::=p\mid\bot\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\mid\Box\varphi\mid\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\varphi

\neg\bot

Where

□

[

For interesting examples of such

2.9 Theorem
.

The logic
\mathsf{CK}\oplus\mathsf{Ax}
is the logic preserving degrees of truth over
\Alg Ax

Proof.

Suppose

1

By its finiteness we can create the conjunction of its elements,

Instantiating the definition with

\text{if }(\forall\psi\in\Lambda^{\prime}.\;\llbracket\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}\rrbracket\leq I^{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{}}}(\psi))\text{ then }\llbracket\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}\rrbracket\leq I^{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{}}}(\varphi)

By the properties of meet,

\forall\psi\in\Lambda^{\prime}.\;\llbracket\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\psi\rrbracket
,
and so by Lemma
2.8
we have the antecedent of the implication above.
Therefore
\llbracket\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}\rrbracket\leq I^{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{}}{}}(\varphi)

\llbracket\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}\rrbracket\leq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket

Hence by a standard argument on Heyting algebras,

\llbracket(\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime})\to\varphi\rrbracket

This means that
{}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{\top\to((\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime})\to\varphi)}

{}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{(\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime})\to\varphi}

By deduction-detachment (see
[
29

{\bigwedge\Lambda^{\prime}}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{\varphi}

{\Lambda^{\prime}}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{\varphi}
,
hence
{\Lambda}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{\varphi}
.

The other direction, showing

from
{\Lambda}\vdash_{\mathsf{}}{\varphi}
, is a straightforward soundness argument.
∎

2.3
Frames, Models, and Bounded Morphisms

We recall the definition of a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame and define disjoint unions,
generated subframes and bounded morphic images.

2.10 Definition
.

A
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
is a tuple
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R)
where

(X,\leq)
is a preordered set of
worlds
,
the
exploding
or
inconsistent world

\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\in X
is maximal with respect to

\leq
, and
R
is a binary relation on
X
such that

\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}Rx
if and only if
x=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}
, for all
x\in X
.

We write
R[x]
for
\{y\in X\mid xRy\}
,
and
x\sim y
where
x\leq y\leq x
,
i.e. if two worlds are in the same cluster.

Some intution may be helpful for this definition. The two binary relations

\leq
and
R
represent the
intuitionistic
and
modal

reachability relations, respectively.
The exploding world

can be understood as the
unique
world at which all
formulas are satisfied, including
\bot

This allows us to see that

2.15 Lemma
.

Let

1.

2.

3.

If

Proof.

The first item requires first, that

is a function

containing

This follows from (

We then must check that

Preservation of

For the other operators we present

\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}

Let

3  Duality

Complex algebras provide a method of turning
\mathsf{CK}
-frames into

\mathsf{CK}
-algebras. Can we also go in the reverse direction?
In other words, given a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra, can we construct a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame?
Usually in intuitionistic (modal) logic, a frame constructed from an algebra
is based on the collection of
prime filters
of the
algebra, see for example
[
11
, Section 8.2]
,
[
52
]
or
[
38
]
.
When ordered by inclusion, these form an intuitionistic Kripke frame.
Inspired by the classical modal setting
[
7
, Section 5.3]
,
the modal relation can then be defined by letting
\mathfrak{p}R\mathfrak{q}
if
for all elements
a
of the algebra we have that

\Box a\in\mathfrak{p}
implies
a\in\mathfrak{q}
, and

a\in\mathfrak{q}
implies
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in\mathfrak{p}
.

However, in case of
\mathsf{CK}
this does not work because the frames constructed
in this way validate formulas that are not derivable, such as

\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}(\varphi\vee\psi)\to\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\varphi\vee\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi
.
To remedy this, the dual of a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra is based on
segments
,
i.e. pairs
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
consisting of a prime filter
\mathfrak{p}

together with a set of prime filters
\Gamma
which intuitively encodes
the successors of the segments.

In Section
3.1
, we start by defining general
\mathsf{CK}
-frames.
These are
\mathsf{CK}
-frames a collection of “admissible” subsets.
The idea behind these is that denotations of formulas always need to be admissible.
We then use segments to construct a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame from a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra,
and show that the action of starting with a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra, constructing its
dual general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame, and then taking the
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra of admissible subsets
gives rise to an isomorphism of
\mathsf{CK}
-algebras.

Next, in Section
3.2
we restrict the class of general

\mathsf{CK}
-frames to
descriptive

\mathsf{CK}
-frames in order to obtain a duality between

\mathsf{CK}
-algebras and descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frames. Finally, in Section
3.3

we extend this to morphisms, resulting in a full duality between the category
of
\mathsf{CK}
-algebras and homomorphisms on the one hand, and descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frames
and general bounded morphisms on the other.

3.1
General
\mathsf{CK}
-Frames

We generalise the notion of
\mathsf{CK}
-frame, by equipping it with a collection of
admissible subsets. We then evaluate formulas only in admissible sets,
rather than all upsets containing

.
Abstractly, the collection of admissible subsets can be seen as subalgebra
of the complex algebra of the frame.

3.1 Definition
.

A
general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame

\mathfrak{G}
is a tuple
(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
consisting of
a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R)
and a set

containing
\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\}
and
X
, which is closed under
\cap,\cup
and
under the operations
\Rightarrow,\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt},\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}
as defined in Section
2.3
.

It is straightforward to observe that a (complex) algebra may be defined on the set
A
of
admissible upsets of a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{G}
, as the definition ensures closure under
all operations. We write this
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra as
\mathfrak{G}^{*}
.

We now show that every
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra
{A}
gives rise to a
general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame, denoted by
{A}_{*}
.

3.2 Definition
.

Let
{A}
be a Heyting algebra.
An
ideal
is a nonempty downset of
A
that is closed under finite joins.
Dually, a
filter
is a nonempty upset
\mathfrak{p}
of
A
that is closed under finite
meets. A filter is
prime
if
a\vee b\in\mathfrak{p}
implies

a\in\mathfrak{p}
or
b\in\mathfrak{p}
. A (prime) filter is
proper
if it is not the whole of
A
, but
we do not in general require our filters be proper.
We write
\pf(A)
for the set of prime filters of
A
.
If
a\in A
then we denote by
\theta(a)
the set of prime filters containing
a
,
that is,
\theta(a):=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\pf(A)\mid a\in\mathfrak{p}\}
.

3.3 Lemma

(Prime Filter Lemma)
.

Let
{A}
be a Heyting algebra,
F
be a filter of
{A}
and
I
be
an ideal of
{A}
. If
F\cap I=\emptyset
then there exists a
prime filter
\mathfrak{p}
extending
F
that is disjoint from
I
.

Proof.

See e.g. Wolter and Zakharyaschev
[
51
, Theorem 7.41]
.
∎

3.4 Definition
.

Let
{A}
be a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra and
\Gamma\cup\{\mathfrak{p}\}\subseteq\pf(A)
.
We call the pair
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
a
segment
if for all
a\in A
:

•

If
\Box a\in\mathfrak{p}
then
a\in\mathfrak{q}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\in\Gamma
;

•

If
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in\mathfrak{p}
then
a\in\mathfrak{q}
for some
\mathfrak{q}\in\Gamma
.

Let
\mathrm{SEG}
be the set of all segments.
Let

be
(A,\{A\})
,
and define binary relations
\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}
and
R_{A}
on
\mathrm{SEG}
by

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)R_{A}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{q}\in\Gamma

Then we denote by
{A}_{+}
the
\mathsf{CK}
-frame

{A}_{+}:=(\mathrm{SEG},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}},R_{A}).

Finally, for each
a\in A
let
\bar{\theta}(a)=\{(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathrm{SEG}\mid a\in\mathfrak{p}\}

and
\bar{\theta}(A)=\{\bar{\theta}(a)\mid a\in A\}
, and define

{A}_{*}:=(\mathrm{SEG},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}},R_{A},\bar{\theta}(A)).

It is clear that
{A}_{+}:=(\mathrm{SEG},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}},R_{A})
is a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.
To show that
{A}_{*}
is a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
we must verify that
\bar{\theta}(A)
is closed under the desired operations.
We have
\bar{\theta}(\bot)=\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\}\in\bar{\theta}(A)
and
\bar{\theta}(\top)=\mathrm{SEG}\in\bar{\theta}(A)
,
and it easy to see that
\bar{\theta}(a)\cap\bar{\theta}(b)=\bar{\theta}(a\wedge b)

and
\bar{\theta}(a)\cup\bar{\theta}(b)=\bar{\theta}(a\vee b)
, so
\bar{\theta}(A)
is closed under
binary intersections and binary unions.
Closure under
\Rightarrow,\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}
and
\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}
will be verified in Lemma
3.6
.

3.5 Lemma
.

Let
{A}
be a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra and
\mathfrak{p}\in\pf(A)
.

1.

(\mathfrak{p},\{A\})
is a segment.

2.

If
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}b\in\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\notin\mathfrak{p}
,
then there exists a prime filter
\mathfrak{q}
such that

\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
and
b\in\mathfrak{q}
,
but
a\notin\mathfrak{q}
.

Proof.

The first item follows immediately from the definition of a segment.
For the second, note that
{\downarrow}a:=\{c\in A\mid c\leq a\}
is an ideal,
and let
F
be the smallest filter containing
\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
and
b
.
We claim that
F\cap{\downarrow}a=\emptyset
.
If this were not the case then, since
\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
is closed under finite meets,
we could find
c\in\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
such that
c\wedge b\leq a
.
Then
\Box c\wedge\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}b\leq\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}(c\wedge b)\leq\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a
.
But
\Box c\wedge\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}b\in\mathfrak{p}
, which is upwards closed,
so
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in\mathfrak{p}
, a contradiction.
So we can use the prime filter lemma
3.3

to extend
F
to a prime filter
\mathfrak{q}
disjoint from
{\downarrow}a
.
∎

3.6 Lemma
.

Let
{A}=(A,\Box,\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}})
be a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra.
Then for any
a,b\in A
we have

\bar{\theta}(a\to b)=\bar{\theta}(a)\Rightarrow\bar{\theta}(b)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\theta}(\Box a)=\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}\bar{\theta}(a)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\theta}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a)=\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}\bar{\theta}(a).

Proof.

Case for implication.

Suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\bar{\theta}(a\to b)
, so
a\to b\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Then for any segment
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)
such that

(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

we have
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
, so
a\to b\in\mathfrak{q}
.
If
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)\in\bar{\theta}(a)
then
a\in\mathfrak{q}
,
hence by deductive closure of
\mathfrak{q}
also
b\in\mathfrak{q}
,
so that
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)\in\bar{\theta}(b)
.
This proves that
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\bar{\theta}(a)\Rightarrow\bar{\theta}(b)
.

Conversely, suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\bar{\theta}(a\to b)
.
Then
a\to b\notin\mathfrak{p}
, so by the deduction theorem
\mathfrak{p},a\not\vdash b
.
Therefore the deduction theorem yields a prime filter
\mathfrak{q}
that
contains
\mathfrak{p}
and
a
but not
b
, so
\mathfrak{q}\in\bar{\theta}(a)

but
\mathfrak{q}\notin\bar{\theta}(b)
.
We can extend
\mathfrak{q}
to a segment, say,
(\mathfrak{q},\{A\})
.
Since
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
we have

(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\{A\})
, which witnesses

(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\bar{\theta}(a)\Rightarrow\bar{\theta}(b)
.

Case for boxes.

Let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
be a segment.
Suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\bar{\theta}(\Box a)
, i.e.
\Box a\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)R_{A}(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)
.
Then
\Box a\in\mathfrak{q}
by definition of
\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}

and
a\in\mathfrak{s}
because
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)
is a segment
and
\mathfrak{s}\in\Delta
by definition of
R_{A}
. So
(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)\in\bar{\theta}(a)
.
This proves
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}\bar{\theta}(a)
.

For the converse, suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\bar{\theta}(\Box a)
,
so that
\Box a\notin\mathfrak{p}
.
Then
\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
is a filter of
A
that does not contain
a
,
so we can extend it to a prime filter
\mathfrak{q}
that does not contain
a
.
Then
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\})
and

(\mathfrak{q},\{A\})
are segments.
Now we have
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma\cup\{\mathfrak{q}\})R_{A}(\mathfrak{q},\{A\})

and by construction
a\notin\mathfrak{q}
, so
(\mathfrak{q},\{A\})\notin\bar{\theta}(a)
.
Therefore
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}\bar{\theta}(a)
.

Case for diamonds.

Let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
be a segment.
Suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\bar{\theta}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a)
, i.e.
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)
.
Then
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
so
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in\mathfrak{q}
,
hence by definition of a segment there exists some
\mathfrak{s}\in\Delta
such
that
a\in\mathfrak{s}
. This implies that
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)R_{A}(\mathfrak{s},\{A\})

and
(\mathfrak{s},\{A\})\in\bar{\theta}(a)
.
Therefore
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}\bar{\theta}(a)
.

Conversely, suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\bar{\theta}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a)
,
so
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\notin\mathfrak{p}
.
Then for each
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}b\in\mathfrak{p}
we can use Lemma
3.5

to find a prime filter
\mathfrak{q}_{b}
containing
\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{p})
and
b
but
not
a
.
Let
\Theta:=\{\mathfrak{q}_{b}\mid\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}b\in\mathfrak{p}\}
.
Then by construction
(\mathfrak{p},\Theta)

is a segment such that
(\mathfrak{p},\Theta)R_{A}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

implies
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)\notin\bar{\theta}(a)
.
Moreover
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{p},\Theta)
,
hence
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\notin\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}(\bar{\theta}(a))
.
∎

We conclude from Lemma
3.6
that
{A}_{*}
is well defined.
Furthermore, the map
\bar{\theta}
witnesses:

3.7 Proposition
.

The map
\bar{\theta}:{A}\to({A}_{*})^{*}
is an isomorphism.

3.2
Descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-Frames

We have seen that the double dual of a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra is isomorphic to itself.
In this section we investigate when the same is true for general
\mathsf{CK}
-frames.
That is, we identify conditions for a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{G}
ensuring
that it is isomorphic to
(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
.
(An isomorphism between two general
\mathsf{CK}
-frames is
a bijective function that preserves and reflects the inconsistent world,
both orders, and the
admissible sets.)

3.8 Definition
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.
Then for
x\in X
and
B\subseteq X
, we define

\eta(x)=\{a\in A\mid x\in a\}

and
\eta[B]=\{\eta(a)\mid a\in B\}
.

3.9 Lemma
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame and

(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}=(\mathrm{SEG},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}},R_{A},\bar{\theta}(A))
its double dual.
Then the function

\bar{\eta}:X\to\mathrm{SEG}:x\mapsto(\eta(x),\eta[R[x]])

is a well-defined order-preserving map
\mathfrak{G}\to(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
.

Proof.

First we need to verify that it is well defined, i.e. that

\bar{\eta}(x)
is a segment for any
x\in X
.

•

Suppose
\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}a\in\eta(x)
.
Then
x\in\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}a
, so
x\leq yRz
implies
z\in a
.
In particular,
xRz
implies
z\in a
, so
a\in\eta(z)

for all
\eta(z)\in\eta[R[x]]
.

•

Suppose
\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a\in\eta(x)
.
Then
x\in\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a
, so there exists some
z\in X

such that
xRz
and
z\in a
. This implies that

\eta(z)\in\eta[R[x]]
is such that
a\in\eta(z)
.

So
\bar{\eta}(x)=(\eta(x),\eta[R[x]])
is a segment.

Now let
x,y\in X
. If
x\leq y
then
x\in a
implies
y\in a
for
all
a\in A
, so
\eta(x)\subseteq\eta(y)
, and therefore
\bar{\eta}(x)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}\bar{\eta}(y)
.
Lastly, suppose
xRy
. Then
\eta(y)
is in the tail of
\bar{\eta}(x)
,
and hence
\bar{\eta}(x)R_{A}\bar{\eta}(y)
.
∎

3.10 Lemma

(Injectivity)
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.
If
\mathfrak{G}
satisfies:

(D1)

for any
x,y\in X
, if
x\not\leq y
then there exists
a\in A
such that

x\in a
and
y\notin a
;

then
x\leq y
iff
\bar{\eta}(x)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}\bar{\eta}(y)
.
If moreover it satisfies

(D2)

for all
x,y,z\in X
, if
xRy\sim z
then
xRz
;

then
\bar{\eta}
is injective and an embedding with respect to
R
.

Proof.

We have already seen that
\bar{\eta}
is order-preserving with respect to

\leq
and
R
, so we only prove it to be order-reflecting.
If
x\not\leq y
then it follows immediately from (
D1
) that

\eta(x)\not\subseteq\eta(y)
, hence
\bar{\eta}(x)\not\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}\bar{\eta}(y)
.
If
\bar{\eta}(x)R_{A}\bar{\eta}(y)
then
\eta(y)\in\eta[R[x]]
,
so
\eta(y)=\eta(z)
for some
z\in R[x]
.
This implies
xRz\sim y
, hence by (
D2
)
xRy
.
∎

3.11 Lemma

(Surjectivity)
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.
For each
a\in A
define
-a=X\setminus a
, and let

-A=\{-a\mid a\in A\}
.
If
\mathfrak{G}
satisfies:

(D3)

if
B\subseteq A\cup-A
has the finite intersection property,
then
\bigcap B\neq\emptyset
;

then for every
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathrm{SEG}
there exists an
x\in X
such that

x\sim(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
.
Property (
D3
) is also known as
compactness
.
If moreover,

(D4)

if
x\in X
and
U\subseteq X
is such that for all
a\in A
:

•

x\in\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}a
implies
U\subseteq a
,

•

x\in\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a
implies
U\cap a\neq\emptyset
,

then there exists a
x^{\prime}\in X
such that
x\sim x^{\prime}
and
R[x^{\prime}]=U
;

then
\bar{\eta}
is surjective.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathfrak{G}
satisfies (
D3
)
and let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathrm{SEG}
.
We need to find an element
x
such that
x\in a
if and only if
a\in\mathfrak{p}
.
If
\mathfrak{p}=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}
(in
\mathrm{SEG}
) then we can take
x=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}
(in
\mathfrak{G}
),
so we may assume that
\mathfrak{p}
is a proper prime filter.
Let
{C}=\mathfrak{p}\cup\{-b\mid b\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{p})\}
.
Then we seek some
x\in\bigcap{C}
.
By (
D3
) it suffices to prove that
{C}
has the finite intersection property.
For any finite
B_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
we have
\bigcap B_{1}\in\mathfrak{p}

and
\bigcap B_{1}\neq\emptyset
because
\mathfrak{p}
is a proper filter.
Similarly, for any finite
B_{2}\subseteq(A\setminus\mathfrak{p})
we have

\bigcup B_{2}\notin\mathfrak{p}
because
\mathfrak{p}
is prime,
hence
\{-b\mid b\in A\setminus\mathfrak{p}\}
has the finite intersection property.
So it suffices to prove that for any
a\in\mathfrak{p}
and
b\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{p})

we have

a\cap-b\neq\emptyset.

Suppose towards a contradiction that
a\cap-b=\emptyset
.
Then
a\subseteq b
, so that
a\in\mathfrak{p}
implies
b\in\mathfrak{p}
, a contradiction.
This proves that
{C}
has the finite intersection property,
hence by (
D3
)
\bigcap{C}\neq\emptyset
and by construction we
have
\eta(x)=\mathfrak{p}
, so
\bar{\eta}(x)\sim(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
, for any
x\in\bigcap{C}
.

Next, assume that additionally (
D4
) holds and let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathrm{SEG}
.
Let
x\in X
be such that
\eta(x)=\mathfrak{p}
,
and
U:=\{y\in X\mid\eta(y)\in\Gamma\}
.
Then
\eta[U]=\Gamma
by (
D3
).
Moreover, if
a\in A
and
x\in\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}a
then

\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}a\in\mathfrak{p}
so
a\in\eta(y)
for every

,
hence
y\in a
. Therefore
U\subseteq a
.
Also, if
\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a\in\mathfrak{p}
then there exists a
\eta(y)\in\Gamma

such that
a\in\eta(y)
, i.e.
y\in a
.
Therefore
a\cap U\neq\emptyset
.
So by (
D4
) there exists
x^{\prime}\in X
such that
x\sim x^{\prime}
and
R[x^{\prime}]=U
,
and by design
\bar{\eta}(x^{\prime})=(\eta(x),\eta[R[x]])=(\mathfrak{p},\eta[U])=(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)
.
So
\bar{\eta}
is surjective.
∎

3.12 Definition
.

A
descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
is a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame that satisfies
(
D1
), (
D2
), (
D3
) and (
D4
).

3.13 Lemma
.

If
{A}
is a
\mathsf{CK}
-algebra, then
{A}_{*}
is a descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.

Proof.

We know that
{A}_{*}
is a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame, so we only have to verify
that it satisfies (
D1
) up to (
D4
).

(
D1
)

Suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\not\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)
.
Then
\mathfrak{p}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{q}
, so there
exists some
a\in A
such that
a\in\mathfrak{p}
but
a\notin\mathfrak{q}
.
This implies that
\bar{\theta}(a)\in\bar{\theta}(A)
is such that

(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\bar{\theta}(a)
and
(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)\notin\bar{\theta}(a)
.

(
D2
)

Suppose
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)R_{A}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)\sim(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.
Then
\mathfrak{q}\in\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}
,
hence
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)R_{A}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.

(
D3
)

Suppose
B\subseteq\bar{\theta}(A)\cup-\bar{\theta}(A)
has the finite
intersection property.
We start by constructing a prime filter in
\bigcap B
.
To this end, define sets
F,I\subseteq A
by

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle={\uparrow}\{a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge a_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\bar{\theta}(a_{1}),\ldots,\bar{\theta}(a_{n})\in B\}

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle={\downarrow}\{c_{1}\vee\cdots\vee c_{n}\in A\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }-\bar{\theta}(c_{1}),\ldots,-\bar{\theta}(c_{n})\in B\}.

Then
F
is a filter and
I
is an ideal, by construction.
Moreover,
F\cap I=\emptyset
, for if this were not the case then
there would exist

\bar{\theta}(a_{1}),\ldots,\bar{\theta}(a_{n}),-\bar{\theta}(c_{1}),\ldots,-\bar{\theta}(c_{m})\in B

such that
a_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge a_{n}\leq-c_{1}\vee\cdots\vee-c_{m}
,
hence

\bar{\theta}(a_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\bar{\theta}(a_{n})\cap-\bar{\theta}(c_{1})\cap\cdots\cap-\bar{\theta}(c_{m})=\emptyset,

contradicting the assumption that
B
has the finite intersection property.
So we can use the prime filter lemma to find a prime filter
\mathfrak{p}

containing
F
which is disjoint from
I
.
By construction any segment of the form
(\mathfrak{p},\Delta)
is in
\bigcap B
.
So extending
\mathfrak{p}
to a segment, say,
(\mathfrak{p},\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\})
,
proves
\bigcap B\neq\emptyset
.

(
D4
)

Let
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in\mathrm{SEG}
and
U\subseteq\mathrm{SEG}
be such that
the given conditions are satisfied.
Then by definition
(\mathfrak{p},U)
is also a segment,
and by construction
R_{A}[(\mathfrak{p},U)]=U
.

So
{A}_{*}
is descriptive.
∎

3.14 Proposition
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame. Then
\mathfrak{G}\cong(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
if and
only if
\mathfrak{G}
is descriptive.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{G}
is descriptive then
\bar{\eta}:\mathfrak{G}\to(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
is an
isomorphism by Lemmas
3.10
and
3.11
.
Conversely,
(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
is descriptive by Lemma
3.13
so
if
\mathfrak{G}\cong(\mathfrak{G}^{*})_{*}
then
\mathfrak{G}
is also descriptive.
∎

3.3
Full Duality

The previous two subsections established a duality between the class of

\mathsf{CK}
-algebras and that of descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frames.
We now extend this to a categorical duality by extending the duality
to morphisms.

3.15 Definition
.

A
general bounded morphism
from between general
\mathsf{CK}
-frames

(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
and

(X^{\prime},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime},R^{\prime},A^{\prime})

f:(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R)\to(X^{\prime},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime},R^{\prime})
such that

for all
a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
.

3.16 Lemma
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
and
\mathfrak{G}^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime},R^{\prime},A^{\prime})

are two general
\mathsf{CK}
-frames and
f:\mathfrak{G}\to\mathfrak{G}^{\prime}
a bounded morphism.
Then
f^{*}=f^{-1}:(\mathfrak{G}^{\prime})^{*}\to\mathfrak{G}^{*}
is a homomorphism of
\mathsf{CK}
-algebras.

Proof.

Clearly
f^{-1}(A^{\prime})=A
so
f^{*}
preserves the top element,
and (
B
) implies that
f^{*}
also preserves the bottom element.
Meets and joins in complex algebras are given by intersections and unions,
which are preserved by the nature of the inverse image.
The proof that
f^{*}
also preserves
\Rightarrow
,
\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}
and
\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}

is analogues to the proof of Lemma
2.15
(
1
).
∎

So we can define a contravariant functor

(\cdot)^{*}:\mathsf{CKDescr}\to\mathsf{CKAlg}

by sending a descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{G}
to
\mathfrak{G}^{*}
and
a bounded morphism
f
to
f^{*}
.
For a functor in the opposite direction we have to work a bit harder:

3.17 Lemma
.

Let
{A}=(A,\Box,\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}})
and
{A}^{\prime}=(A^{\prime},\Box^{\prime},\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime})

be two
\mathsf{CK}
-algebras with dual descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frames

{A}_{*}=(\mathrm{SEG},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}},R,\bar{\theta}(A))

and
{A}^{\prime}_{*}=(\mathrm{SEG}^{\prime},\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}^{\prime},R^{\prime},\bar{\theta}(A^{\prime}))
.
If
h:{A}\to{A}^{\prime}
is a homomorphism,
then

h_{*}:{A}^{\prime}_{*}\to{A}_{*}:(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\mapsto(h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}),h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}])

is a general bounded morphism.

Proof.

We split the lemma into five steps.

Step 1:
h_{*}
is well defined.

We need to verify that
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})=(h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}),h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}])\in\mathrm{SEG}

for any
(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\in\mathrm{SEG}^{\prime}
. We go over the two items defining a segment:

•

If
\Box a\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})
then
\Box^{\prime}h(a)=h(\Box a)\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
,
so
h(a)\in\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}
for all
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, because

(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})
is a segment. Therefore
a\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})
.
Since every element of
h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}]
is of the form
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})
for
some
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, this shows that
a\in\mathfrak{r}
for all

\mathfrak{r}\in h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}]
.

•

If
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})
then
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime}h(a)=h(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a)\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
,
so there exists some
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
h(a)\in\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}
.
This implies
a\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})\in h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}]
.

So
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})
is indeed a segment.

Step 2:
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}
iff
(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime}
.

Clearly
h^{-1}(A^{\prime})=A
, so
h_{*}(\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime})=h_{*}(A^{\prime},\{A^{\prime}\})=(A,\{A\})=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}
.
Conversely, suppose
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})=(A,\{A\})
.
Then
A=h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})
, so
h(\bot)=\bot^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
, hence
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
.
Similarly, for any
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
we have

h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})=A
, so that
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
.
So
(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})=(A^{\prime},\{A^{\prime}\})=\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\prime}
.

Step 3:
h_{*}:(\mathrm{SEG}^{\prime},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}^{\prime})\to(\mathrm{SEG},\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}})
is a bounded morphism.

Suppose
(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.
Then
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}
, hence
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})\subseteq h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})
,
so that
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}h_{*}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.

Now suppose
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)

for some segment
(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)\in\mathrm{SEG}
.
Then
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
.
We need to find a segment
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
such that

\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
and
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{s}
.
Since there are no conditions on
\Sigma^{\prime}
, we can focus on finding
a suitable prime filter
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
.
If
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
or
\mathfrak{s}=A
then it can easily be verified that we
can take
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
,
so assume that
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{s}
are proper prime filters.
Then we need to find some
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
in the intersection of the following
collection of sets:

\underbrace{\{\theta(a^{\prime})\mid a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\}}_{{C}_{1}}\cup\underbrace{\{\theta(h(b))\mid b\in\mathfrak{s}\}}_{{C}_{2}}\cup\underbrace{\{\pf(A^{\prime})\setminus\theta(h(c))\mid c\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{s})\}}_{{C}_{3}}

Indeed,
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\in\bigcap{C}_{1}
implies
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
,

\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\in\bigcap{C}_{2}
implies
\mathfrak{s}\subseteq h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})
,
and
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\in\bigcap{C}_{3}
implies
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
.
By compactness of
{A}^{\prime}_{*}
, it suffices to prove that

{C}_{1}\cup{C}_{2}\cup{C}_{3}
has the finite intersection property.
Since
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{s}
are proper prime filters,
each of
{C}_{1},{C}_{2}
and
{C}_{3}
is
closed under finite intersections, so it suffices to prove that for
any
a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
,
b\in\mathfrak{s}
and
c\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{s})
we have

\theta(a^{\prime})\cap\theta(h(b))\cap(\pf(A^{\prime})\setminus\theta(h(c)))\neq\emptyset.

Suppose towards a contradiction that this is not the case.
Then

\theta(a^{\prime})\cap\theta(h(b))\subseteq\theta(h(c)),

and hence

\theta(a^{\prime})\subseteq\theta(h(b))\Rightarrow\theta(h(c))=\theta(h(b)\to h(c))=\theta(h(b\to c)).

This implies
a^{\prime}\leq^{\prime}h(b\to c)
.
Since
a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
this implies
h(b\to c)\in\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
and hence

b\to c\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
.
But then the assumption that
b\in\mathfrak{s}
implies
c\in\mathfrak{s}
, a contradiction.
We conclude that
{C}_{1}\cup{C}_{2}\cup{C}_{3}
has the finite
intersection property, hence we can find a suitable
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
in its intersection.
Extending this to a segment, say,
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\{A^{\prime}\})
yields the
boundedness property.

Step 4:
h_{*}:(\mathrm{SEG}^{\prime},R^{\prime})\to(\mathrm{SEG},R)
is a bounded morphism.

Suppose
(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})R^{\prime}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.
Then
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, so
h^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})\in h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}]
,
hence
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})Rh_{*}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
.

Now suppose
h_{*}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})R(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)
.
We need to find a segment
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
such that

(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})R^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
and
h_{*}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})=(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)
.
By assumption
\mathfrak{s}\in h^{-1}[\Gamma^{\prime}]
,
so there exists some
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{s}=h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})
.
By construction, any segment of the form
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
is such that

(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})R^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
, so we are left to construct
\Sigma^{\prime}

such that
h^{-1}[\Sigma^{\prime}]=\Sigma
.

If
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
then
h(\bot)=\bot^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}

hence
\bot\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{s}
, so that
\mathfrak{s}=A
.
Then we must have
(\mathfrak{s},\Sigma)=(A,\{A\})
,
and taking
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})=(A^{\prime},\{A^{\prime}\})
gives the desired result.

Now assume
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\neq A^{\prime}
, so
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
is proper, and let

\Sigma^{\prime}=\{\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\pf(A^{\prime})\mid h^{-1}(\mathfrak{r}^{\prime})\in\Sigma\text{ and }\Box^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\}.

(The second part simply states that
\Box^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
implies

a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}
for all
a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
.)
Let us verify that this yields a segment.
By construction
\Box^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
implies
a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}
for all
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
.
If
A\in\Sigma
then
A^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
, and we automatically have
that
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
implies
a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}
for some
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}

(namely
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
).
So assume
A\notin\Sigma
and
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
.
We need to find some
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
that contains
a^{\prime}
,
i.e. some
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}
in the intersection of

{B}:=\{\theta(b^{\prime})\mid\Box^{\prime}b^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\}\cup\{\theta(a^{\prime})\}.

To prove that
\bigcap{B}
is nonempty,
by compactness it suffices to prove that
{B}
has the finite
intersection property. The set
\{\theta(b^{\prime})\mid\Box^{\prime}b^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\}

has the finite intersection property because
\Box^{\prime}
distributes over
meets and
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
is a proper filter, so it suffices to prove that for any

\Box^{\prime}b^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
we have

\theta(b^{\prime})\cap\theta(a^{\prime})\neq\emptyset.

Suppose the contrary, then
b^{\prime}\wedge a^{\prime}\leq\bot^{\prime}
,
hence
\Box^{\prime}b^{\prime}\wedge\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}a^{\prime}\leq\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime}\bot^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
.
Since
h(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\bot)=\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}^{\prime}\bot^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{s}=h^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime})
this
implies
\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\bot\in\mathfrak{s}
, so that the diamond-condition of a segment
forces
A\in\Sigma
, a contradiction.
It follows that
{B}
has the finite intersection property,
hence we can find a suitable
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\bigcap{B}

witnessing the fact that
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
is a segment.

Lastly, in order to show that
h^{-1}[\Sigma^{\prime}]=\Sigma
, we need to show that
for each
\mathfrak{r}\in\Sigma
there exists some
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
such that

h^{-1}(\mathfrak{r}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{r}
. So pick such
\mathfrak{r}\in\Sigma
.
If either
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
or
\mathfrak{r}=A
then we can take
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}
,
so we may assume that both
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{r}
are proper prime filters.
We need to find some
\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}
in the intersection of

\underbrace{\{\theta(a^{\prime})\mid\Box^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}\}}_{{C}_{1}}\cup\underbrace{\{\theta(h(b))\mid b\in\mathfrak{r}\}}_{{C}_{2}}\cup\underbrace{\{\pf(A^{\prime})\setminus\theta(h(c))\mid c\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{r})\}}_{{C}_{3}}

As before, it suffices to prove that
{C}_{1}\cup{C}_{2}\cup{C}_{3}

has the finite intersection property.
Since
\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{r}
are proper prime filters, each of the
{C}_{i}
is
closed under finite intersections. So it suffices to prove that for any

\Box^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}
,
b\in\mathfrak{r}
and
c\in(A\setminus\mathfrak{r})
,

\theta(a^{\prime})\cap\theta(h(b))\cap(\pf(A^{\prime})\setminus\theta(h(c)))\neq\emptyset.

Suppose towards a contradiction that this is not the case.
Then as before we obtain
a^{\prime}\leq^{\prime}h(b\to c)
,
and hence
\Box^{\prime}a^{\prime}\leq^{\prime}\Box^{\prime}(h(b\to c))=h(\Box(b\to c))
.
This implies
\Box(b\to c)\in h^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})
, and hence
b\to c\in\mathfrak{s}
.
Since
b\in\mathfrak{s}
we find

, a contradiction.
So the given intersection is nonempty, which implies the existence of some

\mathfrak{r}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{r}=h^{-1}(\mathfrak{r}^{\prime})
,
hence

.

Thus, the segment
(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
is such that

(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})R^{\prime}(\mathfrak{s}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
and

,

Ultimately, this proves that

Step 5:
(h_{*})^{-1}(\bar{\theta}(a))\in\bar{\theta}(A^{\prime})
for any
\bar{\theta}(a)\in\bar{\theta}(A)
.

We conclude the lemma by proving that
h_{*}
is a
general
bounded
morphism. To this end, observe that any
\bar{\theta}(a)\in\bar{\theta}(A)
we have

\displaystyle(h_{*})^{-1}(\bar{\theta}(a))

4  Sahlqvist Correspondence and Completeness

Two central theorems in classical modal logic bear the name “Sahlqvist”:
a
correspondence
and a
completeness
theorem
[
7
, Chapters 3 and 5]
.
The first establishes a correspondence between a class of formulas,
called “Sahlqvist formulas”, and first-order properties on frames:
a Sahlqvist formula is valid on a frame if and only if its corresponding
first-order frame property holds on the frame
[
42
]
.
The second furthers this result by showing that any extension of the classical
modal logic
\mathsf{K}
with a set
\Lambda
of Sahlqvist formulas is complete
with respect to the class of frames described by the first-order correspondents
of formulas in
\Lambda
.
Sahlqvist completeness obviously relies on Sahlqvist correspondence,
but not only:
it make essential use of a duality between descriptive (classical)
frames and Boolean algebras with operators
[
43
]
.

In this section, we provide Sahlqvist style results:
we define a class of Sahlqvist formulas,
establish a correspondence result for these,
and then demonstrate completeness for extensions of
\mathsf{CK}

with Sahlqvist formulas leveraging the duality established in
Section
3
.

4.1
The Standard Translation

We take our first steps towards building frame correspondents by introducing
the standard translation of intuitionistic modal formulas into a first-order
language.

4.1 Definition
.

We take a classical first-order correspondence language
\mathrm{FOL}

with equality
=
,
and predicate symbols
\leq
and
R
(corresponding to the intuitionistic
and modal accessibility relation of
\mathsf{CK}
-frames), a predicate
P
for each
proposition letter
p\in\mathrm{Prop}
, and a constant symbol
\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
.
Given a variable
x
, we recursively define the
standard translation
of an
intuitionistic modal formula on its structure as follows:

\displaystyle\st_{x}(p)

\displaystyle=Px

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\bot)

\displaystyle=(x=\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}})

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\psi\wedge\chi)

\displaystyle=\st_{x}(\psi)\wedge\st_{x}(\chi)

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\psi\vee\chi)

\displaystyle=\st_{x}(\psi)\vee\st_{x}(\chi)

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\psi\to\chi)

\displaystyle=\forall y(x\leq y\wedge\st_{y}(\psi)\to\st_{y}(\chi))

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\Box\varphi)

\displaystyle=\forall y(x\leq y\to\forall z(yRz\to\st_{z}(\varphi)))

\displaystyle\st_{x}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\varphi)

\displaystyle=\forall y(x\leq y\to\exists z(yRz\wedge\st_{z}(\varphi)))

\mathrm{FOL}
is a convenient language to talk about models.
In fact, every
\mathsf{CK}
-model
\mathfrak{M}
yields a structure
\mathfrak{M}^{\circ}

interpreting
\mathrm{FOL}
: the intuitionistic and modal relations of
\mathfrak{M}

interpret the corresponding binary predicates in
\mathrm{FOL}
,
the valuation interprets the unary predicates,
and

interprets
\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
.

The standard translation exactly shows what a formula expresses
about the model satisfying it.
More formally, a straightforward induction on the structure of
\varphi
gives:

4.2 Proposition
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a
\mathsf{CK}
-model.
Then for any formula
\varphi
and world
w
in
\mathfrak{M}
we have

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\varphi\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{M}^{\circ}\models\st_{x}(\varphi)[x\mapsto w]

\mathfrak{M}\Vdash\varphi\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{M}^{\circ}\models\forall x(\st_{x}(\varphi)).

To obtain a similar result for frames, rather than models, we make use
of a
second
-order translation. The target second-order language
of this translation, called
\mathrm{SOL}
, is
\mathrm{FOL}
but where unary predicates
are now second-order variables, making
\mathrm{SOL}
a
monadic

second-order language.
In
\mathrm{SOL}
we can quantify over the unary predicates, thereby mimicking valuations.
Since this second-order quantification
interprets predicates as subsets of the model and we are only interested
in certain subsets, namely
upsets
, we extend the second-order translation with formulas
ensuring that only correct interpretations of unary predicates are picked.
More precisely, for an unary predicate
P
we define

\mathsf{isup}(P):=P\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}\wedge\forall x\forall y((x\leq y)\wedge Px\to Py).

When true of
P
, this formula ensures that the interpretation of this predicate
is an upset containing the exploding world

.

4.3 Definition
.

Let
\varphi
be formulas all of whose proposition letters
are among
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
, and let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
be the
corresponding unary predicates.
Then the
second-order translation
of
\varphi
is defined as

\so(\varphi):=\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\mathsf{isup}(P_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathsf{isup}(P_{n})\to\st_{x}(\varphi)).

Note that in
\so(\varphi)
we quantify over
x
, indicating that we are looking
for
global
frame correspondents.
This globality is witnessed in the following lemma,
where
\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}
is the second-order structure interpreting
\mathrm{SOL}

emerging from the
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{X}
.

4.4 Proposition
.

For an
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{X}
and formula
\varphi\in\mathrm{Form}
,
we have
\mathfrak{X}\Vdash\varphi
iff
\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}\models\so(\varphi)
.

We are now in possession of (global) frame correspondents
for all formulas. However, these are expressed
in
\mathrm{SOL}
as they contain second-order quantifiers.
Next, we turn to the objective of showing that for a certain class of
formulas the second-order correspondent can be algorithmically reduced to an
equivalent first-order correspondent, via the elimination of its second-order quantifiers.

4.2
Sahlqvist Correspondence

We introduce a class of syntactically defined formulas, called
Sahlqvist formulas
,
whose structure allows us to eliminate the second-order quantifiers from their second-order translation,
while preserving equivalence.

4.5 Definition
.

1.

A formula
\varphi\in\Form
is called
positive
if it
is constructed from
proposition letters,
\top
,
\bot
,
\wedge
,
\vee
,
\Box
and
\Diamond
.

2.

A
boxed atom
is a formula of the form

\Box^{n}p=\underbrace{\Box\cdots\Box}_{\text{$n$ times}}p
, where
p\in\mathrm{Prop}
.
We view
p=\Box^{0}p
as a boxed atom as well.

3.

A
Sahlqvist antecedent
is a formula constructed from boxed
atoms using
\top
,
\bot
,
\wedge
and
\vee
.

4.

A
Sahlqvist formula
is an implication

\varphi\to\psi
where
\varphi
is a Sahlqvist antecedent
and
\psi
is a positive formula.

4.6 Example
.

Some examples of Sahlqvist formulas are

\Box p\to p
,
p\to\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}p
,
p\to\Box p
,
\Box p\to\Box\Box p
,

\Box p\to\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}p
,
p\to\Box\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}p
and
\Box\Box p\to\Box p
.

The use of positivity in Sahlqvist formulas is motivated by
the fact that it preserves truth when expanding a valuation.

4.7 Definition
.

A formula
\varphi
is
upward monotone
if
for any model
(\mathfrak{X},V)
and world
x\in X

and valuation
V^{\prime}:\mathrm{Prop}\to\upp(\mathfrak{X})
such that
V(p)\subseteq V^{\prime}(p)
for all
p
,
the following holds:

(\mathfrak{X},V),x\Vdash\varphi\quad\text{implies}\quad(\mathfrak{X},V^{\prime}),x\Vdash\varphi.

4.8 Lemma
.

If
\varphi
is positive then it is upward monotone.

Proof.

This follows from a routine induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
∎

We finally establish a correspondence theorem in the style of Sahlqvist
[
42
]
,
between formulas and first-order properties.
As alluded to earlier, we obtain this result by eliminating second-order
quantifiers, by notably leveraging the upward monotonicity of the
(positive) consequent of any Sahlqvist formula.

4.9 Theorem
.

Every Sahlqvist formula has a computable, first-order, global correspondent on frames.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
we have
\mathfrak{X}\Vdash\psi\to\chi

if and only if
\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}\models\so(\psi\to\chi)
.
We assume that no two quantifiers bind the same variable.
Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
be the propositional variables occurring in

\psi
, and write
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
for their corresponding unary predicates.
We assume that every proposition letter that occurs in
\chi

also occurs is
\psi
, for otherwise we may replace it by
\bot
to
obtain a formula which is equivalent in terms of validity on frames.

Step 1.

By unfolding
\so(\psi\to\chi)
we get the following formula, where
\operatorname{ISUP}
is the conjunction
of all the
\mathsf{isup}(P_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\to\forall y(x\leq y\to\st_{y}(\psi)\to\st_{y}(\chi)))

Given that
\forall x\forall y(x\leq y\to\st_{y}(\psi)\to\st_{y}(\chi))
is equivalent to

\forall x(\st_{x}(\psi)\to\st_{x}(\chi))
, we can simplify the SO-translation of our implication to

\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\st_{x}(\psi)\to\operatorname{POS})

where we named
\operatorname{POS}
the positive formula
\st_{x}(\chi)
.

Step 2.

Using distributivity, we can write
\psi
as a disjunction

\psi_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\psi_{k}
such that each of the
\psi_{i}
is a
conjunction of boxed atoms. Next, we rewrite

\displaystyle\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}

\displaystyle\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\st_{x}(\psi)\to\operatorname{POS})

\displaystyle=\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x\Big(\Big(\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\st_{x}(\psi_{i})\Big)\to\operatorname{POS}\Big)

\displaystyle=\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x\Big(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\st_{x}(\psi_{i})\to\operatorname{POS})\Big)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\big(\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\st_{x}(\psi_{i})\to\operatorname{POS})\big)

It suffices to find a correspondent for each of the conjoints,
as the conjunction of these will then be a correspondent of
\psi\to\chi
.
So, without loss of generality, we assume that
\psi
is a conjunction of
boxed atoms, so that
\st_{x}(\psi)
is a conjunction of formulas of the form

\forall y(xR_{\Box}^{m}y\to P_{i}y)
, where
xR_{\Box}^{m}y
is a notation for

\exists z_{1}\dots\exists z_{2m}(x\leq z_{1}\wedge z_{1}Rz_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge z_{2m-2}\leq z_{2m-1}\wedge z_{2m-1}Rz_{2m}\land z_{2m}\leq y)

where all
z_{i}
are different and different from
x
and
y
. Observe the critical case
xR_{\Box}^{0}y=x\leq y
.
So we can write
\so(\psi\to\chi)
as

\forall P_{1}\cdots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS}),

(4)

where
\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}
is a conjunction of boxed atoms.

Step 3.

Now we read off the minimal instances of the
P_{i}
making the antecedent
true. Let
P_{i}
be a unary predicate and let

\forall y_{1}(xR_{\Box}^{\ell_{1}}y_{1}\to P_{i}y_{1}),\ldots,\forall y_{t}(xR_{\Box}^{\ell_{t}}y_{t}\to P_{i}y_{t})

be the boxed atoms in
\psi
containing
P_{i}
.
Then we define

\sigma(P_{i}):=\lambda u.((u\neq\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}})\to((xR_{\Box}^{\ell_{1}}u)\vee\cdots\vee(xR_{\Box}^{\ell_{t}}u))).

Then for each second-order model
\mathfrak{M}
we have

\mathfrak{M}\models(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT})[w]\quad\text{implies}\quad\mathfrak{M}\models\forall y(\sigma(P_{i})y\to P_{i}y)[w].

(5)

Note that the presence of
\mathfrak{M}\models\operatorname{ISUP}[w]
above is crucial as it
makes sure that
\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
, an element satisfying
\sigma(P_{i})
, is in the interpretation of
P_{i}
.

Step 4.

We now use the
\sigma(P_{i})
as instantiations of the
P_{i}
.
Let

[\sigma(P_{1})/P_{1},\ldots,\sigma(P_{n})/P_{n}]\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS})

be the formula arising from removing the second order quantifiers
in (
4
) and replacing each
P_{i}
with
\sigma(P_{i})
.
By construction,

[\sigma(P_{1})/P_{1},\ldots,\sigma(P_{n})/P_{n}](\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT})
is true:
while it is obvious for
\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}
, some care is required for
\operatorname{ISUP}
.
First observe that the implication
\sigma(P_{i})\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
is trivially true,
as
\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}\neq\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
is a contradiction.
Second, we can establish that
\forall y\forall z((y\leq z)\wedge\sigma(P_{i})y\to\sigma(P_{i})z)

by noting that if
y=\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}
then
z=\mathord{\mathfrak{ff}}

and that if
xR_{\Box}^{\ell}y
then
y\leq z
gives us

xR_{\Box}^{\ell}z
.
With this established, we simplify our formula to the equivalent

\forall x([\sigma(P_{1})/P_{1},\ldots,\sigma(P_{n})/P_{n}]\operatorname{POS}).

(6)

Since every proposition letter in
\chi
is assumed to be in
\psi
,
the formula
\forall x([\sigma(P_{1})/P_{1},\ldots,\sigma(P_{n})/P_{n}]\operatorname{POS})

contains no unary predicates. Hence it is a first order frame condition.

We complete the proof by showing that for any
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{X}
,
we have
\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}
satisfies the formula from (
4
)
(which is equivalent to
\so(\psi\to\chi)
) if and only if it satisfies
the formula from (
6

The implication from (
4
) to (
6
) is an instantiation
of the second order quantifiers. For the other implication, assume

\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}\models\forall x([\sigma(P_{1})/P_{1},\ldots,\sigma(P_{n})/P_{n}]\operatorname{POS})

and let
\mathfrak{M}
a second-order model on
\mathfrak{X}^{\circ}
interpreting
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}

and
w
an interpretation of
x

such that

\mathfrak{M}\models(\operatorname{ISUP}\wedge\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT})[w]

We need to show that
\mathfrak{M}\models\operatorname{POS}[w]
.
From the above and (

), we get for every
P_{i}
that
its interpretation in
\mathfrak{M}
is an extension of

4.3
Sahlqvist Completeness

We leverage the correspondence just established to show that adding a set
\Lambda
of Sahlqvist
formulas as axioms to
\mathsf{CK}
leads to a logic which is complete with respect to the
class of frames
\Lambda
corresponds to.

In the classical case, this is proved by showing that Sahlqvist formulas are

d-persistent
: their validity on a descriptive frame entails their validity
on the underlying frame.
By algebraic completeness the formulas in
\Lambda
are always evaluated to
\top

in the Lindenbaum algebra
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}
,
hence valid in the dual descriptive frame
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}
.
D-persistence then entails that
\Lambda
is valid on the underlying
(non-descriptive) frame
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{+}
.
By Sahlqvist correspondence, this ensures that the “canonical model” considered
in the completeness proof, i.e.
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{+}
, is indeed in the adequate class of frames.

Unfortunately, the simplicity of this argument cannot be replicated here.
The dual of the Lindenbaum algebra is to blame:
it is very “noisy”, in the sense that the
\leq
-clusters contain too wide a variety of segments.
This prevents minimal valuations on this descriptive frame from being

closed
, a crucial component in the Sahlqvist completeness proof.
We resolve this by introducing an intermediate step before
forgetting the admissible structure: we massage the descriptive frame
into a
semi-descriptive
frame by pruning
\leq
-clusters.
This procedure ensures that the minimal valuation is closed,
allowing us to prove Sahlqvist completeness.

4.14 Definition
.

A

\mathsf{CK}
-frame
is a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame

\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}
.
If
x\in\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a
for some

U\cap a\neq\emptyset

Using the fact that
\mathfrak{D}
satisfies (
D4
), we find some

\widehat{x}\in X

Then clearly

\displaystyle\overline{R}[\widehat{x}]

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\cap R[\widehat{x}]

(definition of
\overline{R}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\cap\bigcap\{a\in A\mid R[x]\subseteq a\}

(definition of
\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}\cap\bigcap\{a\in A\mid\overline{R}[x]\subseteq\overline{a}\}

(definition of

∎

Recall that we can extend the valuation

4.18 Lemma
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a descriptive frame
and let

be an admissible valuation for
\mathfrak{D}
.
Define the valuation
\overline{V}
for
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}
by

\overline{V}(p)=V(p)\cap\overline{X}

Then for any formula

\overline{V}(\varphi)=\overline{V(\varphi)}
.

Proof.

This follows from a straightforward induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
While the base case holds by definition, for the inductive cases
we use that
x\in a
iff
\overline{x}\in\overline{a}
for all
x\in X
and
a\in A

We give the cases of
\Diamond
.

From
x\in\overline{V}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\varphi)

have
x\in\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}\overline{V(\varphi)}
, hence
x\in\overline{\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}V(\varphi)}
via the equality

\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}\overline{a}=\overline{\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}a}

\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}
above.
As all transformations we performed are equivalences, we established
\overline{V}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\varphi)=\overline{\mathord{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\kern-5.38193pt\cdot\kern 2.58334pt}V(\varphi)}
.
∎

It follows from Lemma

that a descriptive frame
\mathfrak{D}

and its pruning
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}
(in)validate precisely the same formulas.
In what follows, we prove that validity of Sahlqvist formulas is preserved

\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\kappa\mathfrak{G}
.
Combining these, we conclude the completeness of extensions of
\mathsf{CK}

\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{}}
to the frame

4.19 Definition
.

If
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)

denotes the

\overline{\mathfrak{D}}
.

4.20 Definition
.

A formula
\varphi
is called
pruning persistent

if for every descriptive

\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{D}
,

\mathfrak{D}\Vdash\varphi\quad\text{implies}\quad\kappa\overline{\mathfrak{D}}\Vdash\varphi.

4.21 Definition
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a general
\mathsf{CK}
-frame.
We call a (not-necessarily admissible)

of
\mathfrak{D}

closed

V(p)=\bigcap\{a\in A\mid V(p)\subseteq a\}

for all

.
Furthermore, we write

for all

.

4.22 Lemma
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R,A)
be a semi-descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
and let
V
be a closed valuation of the proposition letters.
Then for any positive formula
\varphi
we have

V(\varphi)=\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}U(\varphi).

Proof.

We use induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
If
\varphi
is a proposition letter then the lemma follows immediately from
the definition of a closed valuation, and if
\varphi=\bot
then it follows
from the fact that
\bot
is always interpreted as
\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\}
.
The cases for conjunction and disjunction proceed as in
[
7
, Theorem 5.91]
.
We consider the remaining cases.

Case
\varphi=\Box\psi
.

In this case, we have

V(\Box\psi)=\{x\in X\mid R[{\uparrow}x]\subseteq V(\psi)\}=\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}\{x\in X\mid R[{\uparrow}x]\subseteq U(\psi)\}=\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}U(\Box\psi).

Case
\varphi=\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi
.

It follows from the fact that
\psi
is positive that
V\lessdot U
implies

V(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)\subseteq U(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)
,
so
V(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)\subseteq\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}U(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)
.
For the converse inclusion, suppose
x\notin V(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)
.
Then there exists some
y\geq x
such that
R[y]\cap V(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)=\emptyset
.
Using the induction hypothesis and the fact that
\mathfrak{G}
is semi-descriptive,
we find

\underbrace{\bigcap\{a\in A\mid R[y]\subseteq a\}\cap\bigcap\{-b\in-A\mid R[y]\subseteq-b\}}_{=R[y]}\cap\underbrace{\bigcap\{U(\psi)\in A\mid V\lessdot U\}}_{=V(\psi)}=\emptyset.

Since
\overline{\mathfrak{G}}
is compact (by Lemma
4.17
),
we can find a finite number
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
of admissible valuations
such that
R[y]\cap U_{1}(\psi)\cap\cdots\cap U_{n}(\psi)=\emptyset
.
Now define admissible valuation
U^{\prime}
by
U^{\prime}(p)=U_{1}(p)\cap\cdots\cap U_{n}(p)

for all
p\in\mathrm{Prop}
. Then
V\lessdot U^{\prime}
,
and by construction
x\notin U^{\prime}(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)
.
Therefore
x\notin\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}U(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)
.
This proves
\bigcap_{V\lessdot U}U(\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.9pt}{$\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}{\raisebox{-0.2pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}\psi)\subseteq V(\psi)
.
∎

4.23 Theorem
.

Every Sahlqvist formula is p-persistent.

Proof.

Let
\varphi=\psi\to\chi
be a Sahlqvist formula and
\mathfrak{D}
a descriptive

\mathsf{CK}
-frame such that
\mathfrak{D}\Vdash\varphi
.
Then by Lemma
4.18
,
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}\Vdash\varphi
.
We prove that
\kappa\overline{\mathfrak{D}}\Vdash\varphi
by proving that

\overline{\mathfrak{D}}
satisfies the Sahlqvist correspondent of
\varphi
, which,
after steps 2 and 3 of the proof of Theorem
4.9
,
is equivalent to

\overline{\mathfrak{D}}^{\circ}\models\forall P_{1}\dots\forall P_{n}\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\land\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS})

Noting that
x
does not appear free in
\operatorname{ISUP}
, to establish our result we need to pick arbitrary interpretations of all
P_{i}

satisfying
\mathsf{isup}(P_{i})
.
Additionally, the formula
\forall x(\operatorname{ISUP}\land\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS})
is insensitive to the interpretation
of second-order predicates not present in it.
Consequently, without loss of generality we can restrict our attention to the interpretation of all predicates satisfying
\mathsf{isup}
,
that is
valuations
.
This reduces our goal to

\forall V.\;\;(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V)^{\circ}\models\forall x(\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS})

Let us therefore pick a world
w
of
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}
, and consider the minimal valuation
defined below.

V_{m}(p):=\bigcup\{\overline{R}_{\Box}^{\ell}[w]\mid\text{the formula }\forall y(w\overline{R}_{\Box}^{\ell}y\to Py)\text{ appears in }\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\}

Next, we note the equivalence between

\forall V.\;\;(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V)^{\circ}\models\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS}[w]
and

(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V_{m})^{\circ}\models\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}\to\operatorname{POS}[w]

One direction of the equivalence is immediate: instantiate
V
by
V_{m}
.
For the other direction, recall that
V_{m}
is a minimal valuation for
\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}[w]
, which
tells us of any valuation satisfying this formula that it is an extension of
V_{m}
.
Now observe that
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V_{m})^{\circ}\models\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}[w]
holds
by construction of
V_{m}
, which entails that
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V_{m})^{\circ}\models\operatorname{POS}[w]

by assumption.
It suffices to use together the positivity of
\operatorname{POS}[w]
, this last fact, and Lemma
4.8
to get

(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V)^{\circ}\models\operatorname{POS}[w]
.

With this equivalence and the observation
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V_{m})^{\circ}\models\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}[w]
,
we reduce our goal to

\;\;(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},V_{m})^{\circ}\models\operatorname{POS}[w]

To establish this fact, we leverage an important property of our minimal valuation:

V_{m}
is closed.
By applying finitely many times the items of Lemma
4.15
, we can reach this conclusion.
Indeed, item 1 informs us that singleton set are closed,
the upset of a singleton set is also closed by item 2,
and the union of the sets of modal successors of these upsets is also closed by item 3.
By finitely repeating this process, we can show that
V_{m}
is closed, given
that it is characterised by finite paths in
\overline{R}_{\Box}^{\ell}[w]
.

As it is closed, we can apply Lemma
4.22
on
V_{m}
to put our goal to its final form:

\forall U.\;V_{m}\lessdot U\;\to\;(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},U)^{\circ}\models\operatorname{POS}[w]

Let
U
be a valuation such that
V_{m}\lessdot U
, making
U
an
admissible
valuation extending
V_{m}
.
Therefore, we obtain
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},U)^{\circ}\models\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}[w]
.
Given that
\operatorname{BOX\mathchar 45\relax AT}
is the
first-order
correspondent of
\psi
,
we straightforwardly get
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},U),w\Vdash\psi
.
Our initial assumption
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}\Vdash\psi\to\chi
therefore leads to
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},U),w\Vdash\chi
.
We finish our proof by exploiting once more the first-order correspondence to get
(\overline{\mathfrak{D}},U)^{\circ}\Vdash\operatorname{POS}[w]
.
∎

4.24 Theorem
.

For any set
\Lambda
of Sahlqvist formulas,
the logic
\mathsf{CK}\oplus\Lambda
is complete w.r.t.
the class of frames characterised by the first-order frame correspondents generated from
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Assume
\not\vdash_{\Lambda}\varphi
for
\varphi\in\mathrm{Form}
.
The proof of Theorem
2.9
informs us that
\top\not\leq I^{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}(\varphi)
.
Therefore, we get that
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}\not\Vdash\varphi
,
so Lemma
4.18
yields
\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}\not\Vdash\varphi
.
Since every admissible valuation is in particular a valuation, this impleis

\kappa\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}\not\Vdash\varphi
.

Now, it suffices to show that
\kappa\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}

is in the adequate class of frames.
For any
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have
\top=I^{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}(\lambda)
and
hence
\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}\Vdash\lambda
.
Any such
\lambda
is a Sahlqvist implication and therefore p-persistent by Theorem
4.23
,
giving us
\kappa\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}\Vdash\lambda
.
By Theorem
4.9
the first-order correspondent of
\lambda
holds of
\kappa\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}
.
As
\lambda
is arbitrary, we get that
\kappa\overline{\mathbb{LT}^{\mathsf{\Lambda}}_{*}}
is indeed in the
class of frames characterised by the first-order frame correspondents generated from
\Lambda
,
allowing us to conclude completeness.
∎

5  Goldblatt-Thomason theorem

In this section we use the duality between descriptive
\mathsf{CK}
-frames and

\mathsf{CK}
-algebras to obtain a definability theorem akin to Goldblatt and Thomason’s
definability theorem from
[
23
]
.
This gives sufficient conditions for a class of
\mathsf{CK}
-frames to be axiomatic.
More precisely, it states that a class of
\mathsf{CK}
-frames that is closed under
so-called
segment extensions
is axiomatic if and only if it reflects
segment extensions and is closed under disjoint unions, generated subframes
and bounded morphic images.
The proof is obtained by dualising Birkhoff’s variety theorem,
using the segment extensions as a bridge between frames and algebras.

The Goldblatt-Thomason theorem for classical modal logics usually makes use
of some form of
ultrafilter extension
of a frame to bridge the gap
between algebras and frames
(see e.g.
[
7
, Definition 2.57]
,
[
30
, Definition 4.34]

and
[
33
, Section 3.2]
).
When working with positive modal logics or intuitionistic modal logics, this is
often replaced by the
prime filter extension
of a frame
(such as in
[
9
, Section 5]
,
[
22
, Section 6]
and
[
27
]
).
In either case, this extension of a frame
\mathfrak{X}
is obtained as the double
dual of a frame, that is, as the frame underlying the descriptive frame dual
to the complex algebra of
\mathfrak{X}
.
When we employ the same method, we do not end up with a frame based on the
collection of prime filters of
\mathfrak{X}
, but rather it is based on the
set of
segments
. Accordingly, we define the
segment extension

of a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame as follows.

5.1 Definition
.

The
segment extension
of a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame
\mathfrak{X}
is defined as

\seg\mathfrak{X}=(\mathfrak{X}^{+})_{+}.

Concretely,
\seg\mathfrak{X}
consists of the collection of segments of the
complex algebra
\mathfrak{X}^{+}
of
\mathfrak{X}
,
with
\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\seg}=(\upp(\mathfrak{X}),\{\upp(\mathfrak{X})\})

and relations given by

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\mathrel{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\subset\\[-0.86108pt]
\textstyle\sim\end{subarray}}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{q}

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)R^{\seg}(\mathfrak{q},\Delta)

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathfrak{q}\in\Gamma

5.2 Definition
.

The
segment extension
of a
\mathsf{CK}
-model
\mathfrak{M}
is given by

\seg\mathfrak{M}=(\seg\mathfrak{X},V^{\seg}),

where
V^{\seg}(q)=\{(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in X^{\seg}\mid V(q)\in\mathfrak{p}\}
.

\mathsf{CK}
-frames and -models are closely related to their segment extensions:

5.3 Lemma
.

Let
\mathfrak{X}=(X,\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}},\leq,R)
be a
\mathsf{CK}
-frame and

\mathfrak{M}=(\mathfrak{X},V)
a
\mathsf{CK}
-model.

1.

For all segments
(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in X^{\seg}
we have

\seg\mathfrak{M},(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\Vdash\varphi
iff
V(\varphi)\in\mathfrak{p}
.

2.

For all worlds
x\in X
we have

\mathfrak{M},x\Vdash\varphi
iff
\seg\mathfrak{M},\bar{\eta}(x)\Vdash\varphi
.

3.

If
\seg\mathfrak{X}\Vdash\varphi
then
\mathfrak{X}\Vdash\varphi
.

Proof.

Since
\bar{\theta}(V(\varphi))=\{(\mathfrak{p},\Gamma)\in X^{\seg}\mid V(\varphi)\in\mathfrak{p}\}
, the first item is equivalent to

V^{\seg}(\varphi)=\bar{\theta}(V(\varphi)).

We prove this by induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
If
\varphi=\bot
then

V^{\seg}(\bot)=\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}^{\seg}\}=\bar{\theta}(\{\text{\scalebox{.8}{\faIcon{bomb}}}\})=\bar{\theta}(V(\bot)).

If
\varphi\in\mathrm{Prop}
then the result follows immediately from the definitions
of
V^{\seg}
and
\bar{\theta}
.
The inductive cases for
\varphi=\psi\wedge\chi
and
\varphi=\psi\vee\chi

are routine, and
the cases for implication, box and diamond follow immediately from Lemma
3.6
.
For example, if
\varphi=\Box\psi
then we find

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}(V(\Box\psi))

\displaystyle=\bar{\theta}(\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}V(\psi))

(by definition of
\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}
)

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}\bar{\theta}(V(\psi))

(by Lemma
3.6
)

\displaystyle=V^{\seg}(\Box\psi)

(by definition of
\mathord{\Box\kern-5.16663pt\cdot\kern 2.36806pt}
)

The second item then follows from the first and the definition of

\bar{\eta}
via

\mathfrak{M},x\Vdash\varphi\quad\text{iff}\quad x\in V(\varphi)\quad\text{iff}\quad V(\varphi)\in\eta(x)\quad\text{iff}\quad\seg\mathfrak{M},\bar{\eta}(x)\Vdash\varphi.

For the third item, let
V
be any valuation for
\mathfrak{X}
and
x\in X
.
Then
V^{\seg}
is a valuation of
\seg\mathfrak{X}
and by assumption

(\seg\mathfrak{X},V^{\seg}),\bar{\eta}(x)\Vdash\varphi
, so by (2)
we get
(\mathfrak{X},V),x\Vdash\varphi
.
Since
V
and
x
are arbitrary, this proves
\mathfrak{X}\Vdash\varphi
.
∎

5.4 Lemma
.

{C}\subseteq\mathsf{CKAlg}
is axiomatic
if and only if it is a variety of algebras.

Proof.

If

{C}=\{{A}\in\mathsf{CKAlg}\mid{A}\models\Phi\}
,
then it is precisely the variety of algebras satisfying

\varphi^{x}\leftrightarrow\top
, where
\varphi\in\Phi
and
\varphi^{x}
is
the formula we get from
\varphi
by replacing the proposition letters with
variables from some set
S
of variables.
Conversely, suppose
{C}
is a variety of algebras given by a set

E
of equations using variables in
S
. For each equation
\varphi=\psi

in
E
, let
(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)^{p}
be the formula we get from
uniformly replacing the variables in
\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi
with proposition
letters. Then we have

\mathcal{C}=\Alg\{(\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi)^{p}\mid\varphi=\psi\in E\}
.
∎

For a class
{K}
of
\mathsf{CK}
-frames, write

{K}^{+}=\{\mathfrak{X}^{+}\mid\mathfrak{X}\in{K}\}
for the collection
of corresponding complex algebras.
Also, if
{C}
is a class of algebras, then we write

H{C}
,
S{C}
and
P{C}
for its closure under

h
omomorphic images,
s
ubalgebras and
p
roducts,
respectively.

5.5 Lemma
.

A class
{K}\subseteq\mathsf{CKFrm}
is axiomatic
if and only if

{K}=\{\mathfrak{X}\in\mathsf{CKFrm}\mid\mathfrak{X}^{+}\in HSP({K}^{+})\}.

(8)

Proof.

Suppose
{K}
is axiomatic, for the set of formulas
\Phi
.
Then it follows from Lemma
2.13
and the fact that
H
,

S
and
P
preserve validity of formulas that
(
8
) holds.
Conversely, suppose (
8
) holds.
Since
HSP({K}^{+})
is a variety, Birkhoff’s variety theorem
states that it is of the form
\Alg\Phi
.
It follows that
{K}
is axiomatic for
\Phi
.
∎

We now have all the ingredients to prove the Goldblatt-Thomason theorem.

5.6 Theorem
.

Let
{K}\subseteq\mathsf{CKFrm}

6  Conclusion

We have continued the semantic study of the logic
\mathsf{CK}
by providing a duality
theorem and using this to obtain analogues of Sahlqvist results and of the
Goldblatt-Thomason theorem. This paves the way for further
investigation of the semantics of
\mathsf{CK}
and related logics.
In particular, we are interested in the following questions:

Extending the class of Sahlqvist formlas

While the class of Sahlqvist formulas from Definition
4.5

covers a wide variety of examples, it does not admit the use of
the diamond operator in the antecedent.
The difficulty in handling diamonds is caused by the
\forall\exists
-pattern
of its interpretation. It would be interesting to investigate
whether we can ease this restriction, allowing (some appearances of)
diamonds in the antecedent.

Different dualities

As we have seen, descriptive frames for
\mathsf{CK}
are necessarily based on
preordered sets, rather than partially ordered ones.
However, there appears to be a choice in how big we make our clusters,
i.e. which segments we admit.
In Section
3
we opted for the largest possible
solution: we use all segments. An advantage of this is that we can
define bounded morphisms between (descriptive) frames as usual.
On the other hand, as we saw in Section
4.3

this choice of descriptive frames forced us to add an extra
construction to the proof of Sahlqvist canonicity.
It would be interesting to see if there exists a middle ground
between these competing interests.

Expressivity of the diamond-free fragment

Many developments of intuitionistic modal logic eschew the
\Diamond
modality,
proceeding with
\Box
alone. There hence may be value in expressivity results, such
as a Goldblatt-Thomason style theorem, for the logic without
\Diamond
. In particular the
comparison of these results for the logics with and without
\Diamond
would help us
understand when
\Diamond
is in fact necessary to capture frame conditions of interest.
However the problem of the axiomatisation of the
\Diamond
-free fragment of Intuitionistic

\mathsf{K}

[
12
]
will not be resolved so easily, because there the question is
the
finite
axiomatisability of a logic, a considerably more difficult phenomenon to study.
