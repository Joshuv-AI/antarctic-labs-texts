Development and validation of a multiplex TaqMan real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction assay for selected pro-inflammatory immune-response gene expression in chickens
Authors: Xin Ye, Chuan-Yu Hsu, Linan Jia, Christopher Magee, Spencer Leigh, Jeffrey Evans, Li Zhang, Kelsy Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate quantification of host immune gene expression is essential for elucidating host–pathogen interactions and inflammation regulation in poultry infection studies. However, many existing chicken immune gene-expression assays rely on singleplex reactions or intercalating-dye chemistry, which may limit throughput and target specificity. Here, we developed and validated a probe-based multiplex TaqMan real-time qPCR assay for simultaneous quantification of four selected chicken pro-inflammatory immune-response transcripts: IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF, with 18S rRNA included as a separately quantified reference target. RNA quantity and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Plasmid DNA standard curves demonstrated amplification efficiencies within 90–110% and strong linearity with R² ≥ 0.98 in both singleplex and multiplex formats, while RT-derived biological cDNA dilution series confirmed target-specific linearity within biological template matrices. The plasmid-based standard curves covered an approximate Ct range of 15–32, supporting quantification across the validated linear dynamic range. Lung samples collected at 1 and 3 days post-infection from an established avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) challenge study were selected for proof-of-concept testing based on prior SYBR Green qPCR results. The assay detected significant upregulation of IL-1β and IL-8L1 at 1 day post-infection, with no significant changes in IFN-γ and LITAF. Follow-up SYBR-based checks supported the same interpretation and argued against chemistry- or primer-set artifacts. This focused multiplex assay provides a practical research tool for simultaneous measurement of selected chicken inflammatory immune-response transcripts from limited template input. Its application was demonstrated in APEC-infected lung tissue, and additional tissue-, pathogen-, and reference-gene-specific validation will be required before broader application.

Introduction

Chicken infection and challenge models are widely used to evaluate host inflammatory responses and to support the development of vaccines, therapeutics, and management strategies in poultry. Across these settings, changes in pro-inflammatory immune-response gene expression provide important insight into host–pathogen interactions and intervention responses (Ghunaim et al., 2014; Monson and Lamont, 2021). Studies that evaluate multiple immune-response genes across experimental groups, tissues, or sampling points require measurement approaches that are rapid, specific, and scalable. A focused multiplex gene-expression assay can improve screening efficiency in chicken infection studies, provided that its analytical performance is validated in the intended sample matrix and experimental context.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) is widely used for immune gene-expression analysis because of its high sensitivity, specificity, and quantitative accuracy (Singh and Roy-Chowdhuri, 2016). However, conventional singleplex qPCR is limited in throughput and consumes substantial reagent and sample volume per target when multiple genes must be measured across large study designs. Multiplex qPCR can address these constraints by enabling simultaneous amplification of multiple targets in a single reaction, improving efficiency and reducing cost (Markoulatos et al., 2002), but requires assay-specific optimization and validation.

In a shared multiplex reaction, empirical validation is needed to confirm that co-amplification does not introduce cross-reactivity, target competition, or efficiency shifts that could bias quantification (Elnifro et al., 2000; Sint et al., 2012). These effects are particularly important for gene-expression studies, where modest efficiency changes can affect comparisons among treatment groups, tissues, or time points. Multiplex RT-qPCR adds further complexity because reverse transcription introduces additional variability, and large differences in transcript abundance can affect reaction balance and detection of lower-abundance targets in complex tissue matrices. Therefore, reliable multiplex expression assays require empirical demonstration of target specificity, balanced amplification performance, and linear quantification within the intended sample matrix before biological interpretation (Sint et al., 2012; Bustin et al., 2025).

Previous chicken immune-profiling platforms have demonstrated the value of multiplex measurement, including highly multiplexed qPCR-based transcript panels and protein-level cytokine assays (Mucksová et al., 2018; Borowska et al., 2019). Commercially available and custom-designed TaqMan gene-expression assays also provide useful target-specific options; however, adaptation to multiplex formats still requires empirical evaluation of dye compatibility, target abundance, amplification efficiency, and performance within the expected sample Ct range (Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, a focused, sequence-transparent, probe-based multiplex assay implemented on standard real-time PCR instruments could provide a practical approach for targeted measurement of selected chicken inflammatory immune-response transcripts.

In this study, we developed and analytically validated a chicken-targeted multiplex TaqMan qPCR assay for IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF. These targets were selected to represent complementary components of early inflammatory activation, including innate cytokine induction, chemokine-mediated heterophil recruitment, cell-mediated immune activation, and LITAF-associated inflammatory regulation (Peng et al., 2019; Weerts et al., 2021; Rodríguez et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2025). LITAF was included as an inflammation-associated TNF-regulatory marker historically used in chicken inflammatory gene-expression studies; however, it is distinct from the authentic chicken TNF-α ortholog described by Rohde et al. (2018). The four-target format was selected to provide representative coverage of early inflammatory activation while remaining compatible with the channel capacity of most standard real-time PCR instruments.

Here, we describe primer and probe design, screening, optimization, and efficiency testing using recombinant plasmid DNA standards and reverse transcription-derived biological complementary DNA (cDNA) templates. Assay performance was further evaluated using cDNA generated from lung tissues collected from chickens challenged with avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), a common cause of colibacillosis and systemic inflammatory disease in poultry. Using lung tissue from this challenge model as a proof-of-concept biological application, we evaluated whether the assay could quantify infection-associated changes in selected chicken inflammatory immune-response transcripts within a biological tissue matrix. Broader use in additional tissues, pathogen models, or avian species will require matrix-, model-, and species-specific validation.

Materials and methods

cDNA synthesis

Banked lung RNA from APEC-infected and control chickens at 1- and 3-day post-infection (dpi) in a prior in-house APEC-infection study (Ye et al., 2025) was used for cDNA synthesis in this study. RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis through intact 28S and 18S rRNA bands. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA in a single batch using the SuperScript™ VILO™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. No-reverse-transcription (−RT) controls were prepared from all extracted RNA samples for the assessment of genomic DNA contamination.

Primer and probe design for multiplex qPCR assay

The multiplex panel included four selected pro-inflammatory immune-response transcripts: IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF. IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL-8L1 were selected based on prior evidence of early inflammatory regulation in APEC-infected lung tissue (Ye et al., 2025), and LITAF was included as an inflammation-associated TNF-regulatory marker. 18S rRNA was selected as the reference target based on prior use in chicken immune gene-expression studies and its consistent amplification in our random-primed cDNA workflow (Kuchipudi et al., 2012; Jia et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). Because of its high abundance, 18S rRNA was quantified separately in singleplex.

Primers and hydrolysis probes were designed from NCBI RefSeq transcripts to amplify gene-specific regions and, where feasible, to span exon–exon junctions. Candidate primer pairs were screened in silico using NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) and Genuine Primer (Premier Biosoft, Palo Alto, CA) against the Gallus gallus genome/transcriptome to confirm target specificity and exclude predicted off-target amplification of paralogous or related host genes. Final primer and probe sequences for the four immune-response targets were also screened by BLASTn against representative pathogen sequences, including Escherichia coli and Influenza A virus, to assess potential non-host cross-reactivity. Amplicons were designed to be 150–200 bp, and probes were designed with melting temperature 5–10°C higher than those of their corresponding primer pairs. Distinct fluorophores were assigned to the four immune-response targets to minimize spectral overlap. Oligos were synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Plasmid construction for qPCR assay design and validation

Target amplicons were PCR-amplified for each gene using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) as described in Ye et al. (2025) (Table S1). For target amplification prior to cloning, pooled lung cDNA derived from APEC-infected chickens served as the PCR template. Amplicons were purified, A-tailed, and cloned into pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI). Positive clones were confirmed by colony PCR, and representative inserts were Sanger-sequenced (Eurofins Genomics, Louisville, KY). The Sanger sequencing results were aligned to the intended NCBI RefSeq targets by BLAST to confirm identity. Verified plasmids were used as qPCR templates for standard curve construction of all target genes.

qPCR platform and run conditions

All reactions were run on a QuantStudio™ 6 Pro real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) in 10-µL volumes. TaqMan assays used TaqMan™ Multiplex Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and SYBR-only confirmation assays used PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix for qPCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The cycling program for TaqMan assays was 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 3 s and 60°C for 30 s. SYBR Green assays were conducted as described previously (Ye et al., 2025). No-template controls (NTC) and no-reverse-transcription controls (−RT) were included on each plate to monitor non-specific amplification and genomic DNA contamination. Ct thresholds and baselines were set automatically by instrument software and visually reviewed. Plates were accepted only when NTC and −RT controls showed no amplification within the target-specific Ct range or only late amplification near the 40-cycle endpoint. The same profile was used for plasmid DNA and cDNA standard curves and for both singleplex and multiplex assays unless otherwise noted.

Assay validation with plasmid DNA and cDNA dilution series

To assess amplification efficiency and potential multiplex competition in a plasmid-based, inhibitor-free template system, matched 10-fold serial dilutions of recombinant plasmid DNAs were prepared and tested in both singleplex and multiplex formats. Primer/probe concentrations, plasmid starting inputs, and dilution-point design are summarized in Table S2. For singleplex standard curves, each immune-response target was measured individually using its corresponding recombinant plasmid DNA. For multiplex standard curves, a plasmid mixture containing the four immune-response targets was diluted in parallel so that the per-target input at each dilution level matched the corresponding singleplex reaction. Potential multiplex interference was evaluated by comparing slopes, amplification efficiencies, R² values, and Ct shifts between matched singleplex and multiplex dilution series. Starting inputs were 0.0176–0.0332 fmol per reaction for all targets, including 18S rRNA (fmol, femtomoles; 1 fmol = 10⁻¹⁵ mol) (Table S2). Dilution series included six points for four immune-response targets and seven points for 18S rRNA (all with 10-fold dilution series), spanning approximate Ct values of 15–32 depending on the target.

To evaluate assay performance in a RT-derived biological matrix, singleplex standard curves were generated using cDNA synthesized from archived RNA collected in prior in-house APEC studies, including infected tissues and cell culture samples. For each target, a cDNA pool prepared within a single matrix, either tissue type or cell type, was selected, and the starting RNA-equivalent input was adjusted to maximize the Ct range spanned by the dilution series (Table S3). Target-specific primer/probe concentrations and dilution-series designs, including starting RNA-equivalent inputs, dilution factors, and number of points, are provided in Table S3. Each target was serially diluted across six to seven points using its specific dilution factor, and thermal cycling matched the qPCR profile described above.

Multiplex assay application in biological samples

Lung tissues from APEC-infected and control chickens at 1 and 3 dpi (n = 7 per group per time point) were profiled by multiplex and singleplex TaqMan assays to assess agreement. All samples originated from the same RNA bank. cDNA was synthesized in a single batch and aliquoted so that singleplex and multiplex runs were performed on the same instrument under the same cycling program. Final primer/probe concentrations for singleplex immune-response assays, the 18S rRNA assay, and the empirically optimized multiplex assay are summarized in Table 2. Multiplex primer/probe concentrations were first evaluated in singleplex reactions and then adjusted empirically after assembly of the four-target multiplex using representative lung cDNA. The final multiplex concentrations were selected to balance target Ct values, minimize Ct shifts relative to singleplex reactions, and account for target-abundance differences and Ct ranges observed in this matrix. Biological sample Ct ranges were recorded for each target to confirm that sample measurements fell within the validated linear range established by the standard-curve experiments. SYBR cross-check of discrepant gene–time point comparisons

Discrepant gene–time point comparisons from the prior SYBR Green study (Ye et al., 2025) were cross-checked by singleplex SYBR qPCR using the same cDNA samples analyzed by TaqMan. SYBR reactions were run with (i) the newly designed TaqMan assay primer pair under SYBR conditions with the probe omitted or (ii) the previously published SYBR primer pair (Ye et al., 2025). In both SYBR assays, 18S rRNA served as the reference using assay-specific primer pairs. Melt-curve analysis showed a single dominant peak for each primer set, supporting amplification of a single specific product.

Data processing and statistics

Ct values were exported and processed in R (v4.4.2). For standard curves, Ct was regressed against log10 input (fmol per reaction) to obtain slopes and R² values. Amplification efficiency was calculated as E=(10(−1slope)−1)×100% (Pfaffl, 2001). Primer–probe sets were accepted if amplification efficiency was 90–110% and R² ≥ 0.98 (Bustin et al., 2025). Potential multiplex interference was evaluated by comparing amplification efficiencies, R² values, and Ct shifts between matched singleplex and multiplex plasmid dilution series, where Ct shift was calculated as Ct_multiplex − Ct_singleplex at matched dilution points.

Intra-assay variation was calculated from technical replicate Ct values within the same qPCR run. Inter-assay variation was calculated from run-level mean Ct values generated from the same pooled lung cDNA template across three independent qPCR runs. For both analyses, variation was reported as CV% = SD/mean × 100. Biological sample Ct ranges, including 18S rRNA Ct values, were summarized across the APEC lung sample set to compare sample measurements with the validated plasmid-based linear range. Gene-expression analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10.1. ΔΔCt values were computed using 18S rRNA as the reference target and time-matched controls as the normalizer. Infected and control groups were compared for each target and dpi using two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests. Bar graphs were created in Prism and displayed individual data points with mean ± SEM. Assays were repeated when control reactions or amplification metrics did not meet acceptance criteria.

Results and discussion

Pro-inflammatory immune-response transcripts provide useful readouts of host immune activation in chicken infection studies (Alber et al., 2021; Monson and Lamont, 2021). We developed and validated a four-plex TaqMan qPCR panel targeting IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF using plasmid DNA standards, RT-derived cDNA templates, and APEC-infected lung tissue as a proof-of-concept biological matrix (Peng et al., 2018; Ye et al., 2025). Assay performance was evaluated by comparing singleplex and multiplex formats, assessing linearity and amplification efficiency, and cross-checking selected discrepant comparisons using SYBR chemistry and alternative primer sets.

Plasmid standards confirm robust multiplex analytical performance

Plasmid-based standard curves generated from 10-fold serial dilutions were linear across at least five orders of magnitude for all four immune-response targets (IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF) in both singleplex and multiplex formats (Fig. 1). 18S rRNA was assessed in singleplex only. Singleplex assays achieved amplification efficiencies of 90.5–106.6% with R² values of 0.995–1.000, whereas multiplex assays yielded efficiencies of 94.1–109.7% with R² values of 0.986–0.995 (Table 3). Because multiplex qPCR can be more challenging than singleplex qPCR due to potential primer-probe interactions, spectral overlap, and reagent competition (Elnifro et al., 2000), matched singleplex and multiplex plasmid dilution series were compared to evaluate potential target-to-target interference. Multiplex curves were comparable to the corresponding singleplex curves across matched inputs, with similar slopes, amplification efficiencies, and linearity (Fig. 1 and Table 3). Mean Ct shifts between matched singleplex and multiplex plasmid dilution points were small across the four immune-response targets, ranging from 0.11 to 0.27 cycles, although individual dilution-point shifts varied more widely (Table S4). These shifts were not accompanied by loss of linearity or unacceptable amplification efficiency in the multiplex format. The comparable slopes, amplification efficiencies, and R² values provided no evidence of substantial competitive inhibition under the optimized four-target configuration, indicating that multiplexing preserved analytical performance within the plasmid-based validated range. These results met the performance expectations aligned with the Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments (MIQE) guidelines (Bustin et al., 2025).

Biological cDNA standards support linearity across study-relevant Ct values

Starting inputs and dilution factors were selected on a target-by-target basis to account for differences in transcript abundance across biological matrices (Table S3). Although a uniform Ct window was not feasible for every target, the cDNA dilution series covered broad, target-specific Ct ranges, and biological APEC lung sample Ct values were within or near the corresponding plasmid-validated ranges (Table S5). 18S rRNA Ct values showed limited variation across the biological APEC lung samples (16.79–18.94), supporting its use as the reference target for this lung-tissue application.

The cDNA dilution series was run in singleplex to evaluate matrix/RT-associated effects on amplification behavior, including biological background, tissue-associated inhibitors, and potential carryover, without introducing target-to-target competition (Fig. 2, Table 4 and Table S3). Multiplex compatibility and potential competition were evaluated separately using matched plasmid DNA-based singleplex and multiplex standards (Fig. 1, Table 3 and Table S4). Together with the biological sample Ct-range comparison (Table S5), these complementary standard-curve approaches support consistent analytical performance in plasmid standard and RT-derived biological matrices within the current validation framework.

APEC-infected lung data supports concordant singleplex and multiplex inference

To evaluate multiplex assay performance in biological samples, lung tissues from control and APEC-infected birds at 1 and 3 dpi were analyzed by singleplex and multiplex TaqMan qPCR. Across the four immune-response targets, the direction of change and statistical inference were concordant between assay formats at both time points (Fig. 3). At 1 dpi, IL-1β expression was higher in infected than control lungs in both formats (singleplex P = 0.004; multiplex P = 0.017), and IL-8L1 showed a similar increase (singleplex P = 0.017; multiplex P = 0.011). In contrast, IFN-γ and LITAF did not differ between groups at 1 dpi in either format (all P > 0.05). Probe-based multiplexing reduces reagent use and sample input by measuring multiple transcripts from the same template, but shared-reaction effects such as competition or reduced sensitivity must be evaluated, especially for low-abundance targets. In this lung proof-of-concept application, multiplexing did not change the biological conclusions relative to singleplex analysis for any target at either time point (Fig. 3). This indicates that multiplex-associated competition or loss of sensitivity was not sufficient to alter inference within the observed expression ranges. Nevertheless, low-abundance targets near the upper Ct range are intrinsically more variable and may show format-dependent fluctuations; therefore, marginal fold-changes at high Ct values should be interpreted cautiously and, when decision-critical, confirmed in singleplex (Elnifro et al., 2000; Markoulatos et al., 2002; Teo et al., 2002; Borowska et al., 2019).

Assay repeatability and reproducibility were further evaluated using technical replicates and repeated measurements of the same pooled lung cDNA template across independent runs. Intra-assay Ct CV values ranged from 0.45% to 0.71%, and inter-assay Ct CV values ranged from 0.60% to 1.89% across the four immune-response targets and the 18S rRNA reference assay (Table S6). These results support repeatable Ct measurement within runs and reproducible amplification across independent runs.

Cross-checks support high Ct RT variability as the likely source of SYBR discordance

In our prior SYBR Green analysis (Ye et al., 2025), selected gene–time point comparisons showed infected–control differences for IFN-γ and IL-8L1 that were not reproduced in the current multiplex TaqMan dataset. Specifically, Ye et al. (2025) reported that IFN-γ and IL-8L1 were upregulated in infected versus control lungs at 1 dpi and then decreased from 1 to 3 dpi within the APEC group (P < 0.05), whereas this pattern was not reproduced in the current dataset (Fig. 3). This raised the possibility that detection chemistry or primer/amplicon design contributed to the discrepancy. To test this directly, the discrepant gene–time point comparisons were cross-checked by SYBR qPCR using both the TaqMan assay primer pair (probe omitted) and the previously published SYBR primer pair. Because intercalating-dye assays require post-amplification specificity assessment, melt-curve analysis was performed for each SYBR primer set (Mackay et al., 2002). Melt-curve analysis showed a single dominant peak for each SYBR primer set, supporting amplification of a single specific product. For IFN-γ at 1 and 3 dpi and IL-8L1 at 3 dpi, neither primer pair detected an infected–control difference (all P > 0.05; Fig. 4). The discrepant comparisons clustered near the high-Ct end of the assay range (Ct values approximately ≥32), where low-abundance measurements are more sensitive to upstream variability, including RNA integrity, effective template input after storage, residual inhibitors, and reverse-transcription batch effects (Fleige and Pfaffl, 2006; Vermeulen et al., 2011). Although the RNA isolates were the same, cDNA for the present study was synthesized anew from stored RNA, introducing a new RT batch that could disproportionately affect marginal signals at high Ct. Accordingly, we recommend routine RNA QC, aliquoting stored RNA to minimize freeze–thaw exposure, and batch-standardized reverse transcription (with cross-run calibrators when appropriate) to improve comparability across studies (Ståhlberg et al., 2004; Bustin et al., 2025). Overall, the remaining discordance is more consistent with upstream handling, RT variability, and/or cohort-level biological variation than with assay chemistry or primer design.

Practical QC considerations and matrix-specific revalidation needs

Based on the analytical validation and APEC lung proof-of-concept application presented here, use of this focused multiplex panel is most reliable when expected sample Ct values, input amount, and dilution strategy are aligned with the validated linear range for the intended sample matrix. Target abundance should also be considered because the relative expression levels of the four targets may shift across tissues or experimental contexts. Large Ct separations among targets can increase the potential for competition in a shared reaction and may require modest primer/probe rebalancing, increased template input within the validated range, or separation of low-abundance targets into a smaller panel (Elnifro et al., 2000; Sint et al., 2012). In the optimized four-target configuration used here, the limited Ct shifts and preserved amplification efficiencies likely reflected the focused panel size, similar amplicon lengths, target-specific hydrolysis probes, well-separated fluorophores, and empirical primer/probe concentration balancing. Assay design and run-level QC should include short amplicons, matched primer melting temperatures, probe melting temperatures modestly higher than the primers, well-separated fluorophores, appropriate instrument calibration, technical replicates, NTC and −RT controls, and cross-plate calibrators when cross-run comparability is required (Bustin et al., 2025).

Matrix effects, including residual inhibitors, RNA quality differences, and background nucleic acid composition, may vary among tissues such as liver, spleen, and cecal tonsils. The validation presented here supports quantification across the Ct windows and template matrices evaluated in this study. Matrix-specific revalidation should be performed when sample Ct values fall substantially outside the validated range, relative target abundance shifts across tissues or models, or sample processing and reverse-transcription conditions differ from those used here. This can be performed using a pooled-cDNA dilution series spanning the anticipated Ct window, followed by review of standard-curve metrics, including slope, amplification efficiency, and R², to determine whether additional RNA cleanup, template dilution, input adjustment, primer/probe rebalancing, or panel splitting is warranted (Bustin et al., 2025).

Panel modularity, limitations, and future directions

Probe-based TaqMan chemistry enables target-specific, multi-channel detection and is well suited for focused host immune-response transcript profiling. The value of the present panel is that four selected chicken inflammatory targets were sequence-defined, empirically optimized, and analytically characterized as a multiplex combination rather than assembled only as independent singleplex assays. Compared with broader immune-profiling platforms that may rely on specialized platforms or proprietary assay formats, this panel is intended for targeted, sequence-transparent use on standard real-time PCR instruments. The panel is also modular and can be deployed as a two-, three-, or four-target assay to match study goals, target abundance, and instrument channel availability. This configurability supports targeted pro-inflammatory gene-expression profiling in chicken studies, provided that expected Ct range, target balance, and reference-target stability are confirmed for the intended sample matrix.

Several limitations should be considered. The biological application was limited to APEC-infected lung tissue at 1 and 3 dpi, which were selected based on prior evidence of early inflammatory changes in this model (Ye et al., 2025), and was intended as a proof-of-concept application rather than comprehensive validation across tissues, pathogens, doses, or disease-severity categories. Although many inflammatory-response pathways are shared across infection and challenge models, relative transcript abundance can vary by tissue, time point, infection context, and sample-processing workflow. Therefore, applications beyond the APEC lung matrix evaluated here should be validated within the intended matrix and experimental context. Although 18S rRNA showed limited Ct variation in the current APEC lung sample set and was compatible with the random-primed reverse-transcription workflow used here, its high abundance required separate singleplex quantification with substantially diluted cDNA. In addition, 18S rRNA should not be assumed to be suitable for workflows relying primarily on oligo(dT)-primed cDNA synthesis or for applications in additional tissues, pathogen models, or cross-tissue comparisons without independent reference-gene validation. In those settings, validated mRNA reference genes may be preferable. Because TaqMan assays are sequence-specific, this panel should be considered chicken-targeted unless further validated. Use in turkey, duck, or other avian species would require confirmation of primer/probe binding-site conservation and empirical validation of assay performance; species-specific redesign may be needed if key mismatches are present. Future work will apply this framework to larger multi-tissue APEC datasets and expanded immune-response panels, including complementary anti-inflammatory targets, to further evaluate matrix-specific performance and biological applicability.

Conclusion

This study establishes a probe-based multiplex TaqMan qPCR panel for quantifying four selected pro-inflammatory immune-response transcripts in chickens: IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-8L1, and LITAF. Plasmid DNA standards and reverse transcription-derived cDNA dilution series demonstrated acceptable linearity and amplification efficiency, and APEC-infected lung samples supported concordant singleplex–multiplex biological inference. SYBR cross-checks at discrepant comparisons further argued against chemistry- or primer/amplicon-driven artifacts. The panel is modular and can be configured as a two-, three-, or four-plex to match study goals, target abundance, and instrument channel availability. Overall, this focused, sequence-defined assay provides a practical tool for targeted pro-inflammatory gene-expression profiling in chicken studies, with broader application requiring matrix-, model-, and sequence-specific validation.
