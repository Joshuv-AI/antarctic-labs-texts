Ecthyma gangrenosum in a patient with metastatic leiomyosarcoma undergoing chemotherapy: a case report
Authors: Grace Getman, Taryn Boucher, Vicki Keedy, Melinda New, Alison Barlow, Lauren Prescott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pancytopenia from chemotherapy is a risk factor for EG with gynecologic malignancy.•EG commonly presents with painful localized lesions, often in the apocrine areas.•EG often presents with signs of sepsis, and rarely without systemic symptoms.•Prompt initiation of anti-pseudomonal antibiotics are the first line treatment.•Early detection can mitigate the significant morbidity and mortality of EG.

Introduction

Ecthyma gangrenosum (EG) is a rare skin infection characterized by indurated necrotic plaques with an erythematous rim that most commonly affects immunocompromised patients (Morgan et al., 2007). Lesions typically start out as painless macules that become hemorrhagic and ultimately form necrotic ulcers, which can be painful. Multiple lesions can occur and are typically found in the apocrine areas of the axillae, inguinal folds, and perineum. Biopsy shows bacterial invasion of the interstitium and adventitia of venules with dermal edema. Prompt initiation of antibiotics is necessary to avoid life-threatening complications including septicemia (Morgan et al., 2007).

Historically, EG was considered pathognomonic for Pseudomonas septicemia (Greene et al., 1984); however, a variety of organisms including Citrobacter freundii, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumonia, Aeromonas hydrophilia, and Serratia marcescens have been associated with EG (Reich et al., 2004, Vaiman et al., 2015a). Though EG predominantly affects neutropenic patients, it has also been reported in healthy patients and in the absence of bacteremia (Huminer et al., 1987, Song et al., 2001). Approximately 50 to 60% of patients will present with bacteremia, with up to 38% developing sepsis (Muggeo et al., 2022, Lau et al., 2023).

Here we describe a rare case of EG of the vulva in a pancytopenic patient with metastatic leiomyosarcoma undergoing chemotherapy who presented without septicemia. To our knowledge, this is only the second case of EG reported in a patient with a gynecologic cancer.

Case

A 40-year-old patient with recurrent Stage IIB uterine leiomyosarcoma presented with painful lesions on her labia. She denied history of similar lesions and had no history of herpes simplex virus (HSV). She received Cycle 1 Day 1 of doxorubicin/dacarbazine 14 days prior to presentation. On presentation, she was afebrile and tachycardic to 139, with normal blood pressure and oxygen saturation. Labs were notable for pancytopenia (white blood cell count 1.4, absolute neutrophil count 0.57, hemoglobin 8.1, platelets 15). On physical exam, she had several tender, circumferential, and necrotic ulcers surrounded by an erythematous ring on her bilateral labia (Fig. 1). No other lesions were found elsewhere on her body. She was treated empirically with PO ciprofloxacin 750 mg BID and valacyclovir 1 g BID, and was given topical lidocaine cream. The lesions were cultured and grew Pseudomonas. HSV culture was negative and valacyclovir was discontinued. She continued PO ciprofloxacin for 5 days. On Day 6, she was hospitalized for oncologic complications unrelated to her ecthyma gangrenosum and received IV cefepime on admission. By Day 7, the inferior lesions were completely resolved and the patient’s symptoms were greatly improved. Discussion

EG was first described in the literature as early as 1897 when describing clinical manifestations of an infection caused by Baccilus pyocyaneus, which was a previous denomination for today’s Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Marques and Abbade, 2020). The terminology “ecthyma gangrenosum” was then popularized following a 1951 case report by RH Broughton and has since been used to describe cutaneous findings associated with either primary Pseudomonas infection or seeding from Pseudomonas bacteremia (Morgan et al., 2007, Broughton, 1951). The cutaneous findings are characterized by the rapid evolution of one or multiple localized lesions, which start as a vesiculobullous or papulonodular lesion on an erythematous, edematous background and evolve into a necrotic lesion within 12 to 24 h (Marques and Abbade, 2020). The lesions can be singular or multiple, and can be at different stages of evolution. The lesions preferentially arise in the perineum, buttocks, inguinocrural fold, intergluteal cleft, and axillae, though the extremities and face can also be affected (Morgan et al., 2007, Marques and Abbade, 2020).

EG most commonly affects immunocompromised patients and has previously been associated with bacteremia and risk of sepsis. However, EG can affect immunocompetent individuals and is not always accompanied with septicemia (Vaiman et al., 2015b). Approximately 40% of EG cases associated with bacterial infection affect immunocompetent patients, whereas nearly all cases from a fungal infection involve immunocompromised patients (13 cases out of 15, 86.66%) (Vaiman et al., 2015b). EG has been most commonly reported in the setting of leukemia and lymphoma, as well as in the setting of other malignancies, cancer treatment, severe burns, immunosuppressive therapies, transplants, and rheumatoid arthritis (Vaiman et al., 2015a, Marques and Abbade, 2020).

While Pseudomonas still comprises a majority of cases of EG (Vaiman et al., 2015b), a variety of bacterial and fungal infections have been reported. In a review of 167 cases of ecthyma gangrenosum from 1975 to 2014, Pseudomonas was found in 123 cases (74%) while other bacteria were detected in 29 cases (17%) and fungi were detected in 15 cases (9%) (Vaiman et al., 2015b). Similarly, a retrospective study of 28 patients with varying etiologies of ecthyma gangrenosum found no difference in the lesion number, location, or treatment strategy between the pseudomonal and non-pseudomonal cohorts (Vaiman et al., 2015a). Given that these lesions behave similarly and require a similar treatment plan, ecthyma gangrenosum is no longer only associated with Pseudomonas, but other etiologies as well.

Early recognition and diagnosis are the key to treating EG. Differential diagnoses include small vessel leukocytoclastic vasculitis, sepsis skin lesions, disseminated vascular coagulation, septic emboli associated with endocarditis, arachnid or arthropod bite reaction, anthrax, tularemia, diphtheria, syphilitic chancre, herpes simplex virus infection, and Fournier’s gangrene (Morgan et al., 2007, Marques and Abbade, 2020). Nearly all reported cases of EG presented with fever and pain at the site of the lesion, though our patient was notably afebrile (García-López et al., 2023). Early empiric antibiotic treatment with an agent that covers Pseudomonas is the recommended initial step (Marques and Abbade, 2020). Wound culture should be collected from a non-necrotic area, along with blood cultures and the option of skin biopsy with touch prep, microbial stain, and culture (Morgan et al., 2007, Marques and Abbade, 2020). Consult to Dermatology and Infectious Disease may also be considered. EG has a high mortality rate, ranging from 38% to 77% in patients with septicemia secondary to Pseudomonas, and about 15% mortality in patients without sepsis (Martínez-Longoria et al., 2017). While antibiotic treatment can be sufficient, up to 82% of patients require surgical debridement, with similar surgical approach in both Pseudomonas and non-Pseudomonas cases (Vaiman et al., 2015a, Vaiman et al., 2015b). Surgical intervention is indicated in cases of rapidly progressing lesions or lesions that fail to improve with antibiotic treatment alone (Khalil et al., 2008).

Our patient represents the second case of EG reported in a patient with gynecologic cancer. The first case was a 77-year-old with endometrial carcinoma who underwent bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and hysterectomy prior to starting paclitaxel-carboplatin chemotherapy (Ishihara and Ozawa, 2021). Nine days after the initiation of chemotherapy, she developed painless erythema on her right lower abdomen, which progressed to a hemorrhagic pustule and subsequently a gangrenous ulcer. The patient was febrile and neutropenic, and wound cultures grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa. She was diagnosed with EG and was treated with antibiotics and surgical debridement with improvement. While this patient was undergoing treatment with a different chemotherapy than that of our patient, development of EG appears to be related to severity of neutropenia rather than to a specific chemotherapeutic agent (Martínez-Longoria et al., 2017).

Overall, our case reinforces the known risk of EG in pancytopenic patients receiving chemotherapy. Similar to the majority of EG cases, our patient grew Pseudomonas on wound culture and was appropriately treated with antibiotics. Unlike a majority of cases, which present with fever, bacteremia, or other systemic symptoms of infection, our patient presented only with painful lesions. This case illustrates the importance of early recognition and treatment with antibiotics, even in cases where systemic symptoms are absent. Fortunately, our patient’s lesions resolved with antibiotics alone and did not require surgical debridement. Additionally, while our patient was pancytopenic and was at a higher risk for EG, there are numerous documented cases of EG in immunocompetent patients. EG must therefore remain on the differential diagnosis for any patient who presents with the characteristic necrotic lesions with a red halo. Ultimately, while EG is most commonly found in immunosuppressed patients with Pseudomonas as the causative agent, its definition should include immunocompetent patients and a broad spectrum of etiologies in order to diagnose and treat early in the disease process, thus mitigating the high risk of morbidity and mortality that comes with EG.
