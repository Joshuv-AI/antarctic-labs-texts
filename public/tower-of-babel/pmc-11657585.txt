Does scapular dyskinesia affect upper extremity performance, proprioception, and body image in kickboxers? Case-control study
Authors: Cetin Sayaca, Burak Erkan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScapular Dyskinesia (SD) is detected more frequently in athletes who do overhead activity sports. SD is more common in kickboxers who actively use their upper extremities. This study was conducted to compare upper extremity stabilization, proprioception and body image in kickboxers with and without SD.MethodsKickboxing athletes participating in the study were divided into two groups according to whether they had SD or not. Presence of SD with Lateral Scapular Slide Test; Shoulder proprioception sense with digital inclinometer (Dualer IQ Pro, JTECH Medical Industries) at 40, 70, 90 and 135 degrees; Shoulder joint stabilization was evaluated with the Closed Kinetic Chain Upper Extremity Stabilization Test, and body image was evaluated with the Body Assessment Scale.ResultsA total of 56 male kickboxing athletes with (n:27) and without (n:29) SD between the ages of 18–35 were included in the study. There was no difference between the groups regarding age, height, weight, body mass index, and 40° with 135° shoulder abduction proprioception sense (p > 0.05). There were differences between the groups in terms of sports history, 70° and 90° shoulder abduction proprioception sense, shoulder stabilization and body image (p: 0.001; p: 0.003; p: 0.003; p: 0.012, respectively).ConclusionShoulder proprioception, shoulder joint stabilization, and body image in kickboxers with SD were better than kickboxers without SD. In addition, Kickboxers with SD performed in this sport for a longer time than kickboxers without SD.Keywords: Kickboxing, Body image, Proprioception, Stabilization, Scapular dyskinesia

Introduction

Kickbox; it is a dynamic, high-intensity combat sport that aims to hit the opponent with full force with body parts, requiring complex skills and tactical perfection for success [1]. Nowadays, the popularity of kickboxing is gradually increasing [2]. The main purpose of kickboxing is to have influence upon the opponent with the upper and lower extremities [1]. Therefore, many physical factors such as skill, speed, agility, strength, balance, coordination, reaction speed, technique, power and aerobic condition of all parts of the body are necessary for performance [3]. Since it is a sport in which the aim is to hit the opponent directly, soft tissue injuries are encountered more frequently [4]. Instability in the shoulder joint, injuries such as rotator cuff tear or shoulder impingement syndrome may cause scapular dyskinesia (SD) [5].

SD is a cause of pain and dysfunction in the shoulder and is referred to as the improper motion of the scapula during movement [6]. It is a pathology characterized by symptoms such as scapular winging, pain, muscle weakness, loss of control and crepitation [5]. During shoulder movements, findings such as prominence of the medial edge and inferior angle of the scapula, early scapular elevation, and sudden downward rotation are observed [7]. While SD is seen in 33% of athletes who do not perform overhead activities, it has been reported to be seen in 61% of athletes who perform overhead activities [8]. It is twice as common in male kickboxers compared to sedentary individuals [9]. Pathologies such as rotator cuff syndrome, subacromial impingement syndrome, glenoid labrum problems, glenohumeral instability [10] and scapula-humeral rhythm disorder are common in individuals with SD [11]. It is known that rotator cuff and biceps muscle activations increase in individuals in order to compensate for the resulting SD [12]. Result of this increased activation, instability may develop and SD may continue [7]. After the resulting instability, proprioceptive sense may be negatively affected [13]. This condition, which is generally ignored in the treatment of shoulder problems, can cause related shoulder injuries to occur and recur if left untreated [5] or abnormal stabilization and loss of control [7]. Shoulder injuries may occur as a result of these negative changes in scapular movement patterns [14].

In addition to physical and physiological factors, the athlete’s psychological characteristics also affect performance and success directly [15]. Psychological skills, motivation, self-confidence, and mental endurance are among the psychological characteristics of the athlete. Therefore, it is important to collect objective information about the physical and physiological characteristics of athletes as well as their psychological characteristics, to create short and long-term training goals and to provide objective and accurate feedback [16]. SD is common in male kickboxing athletes and causes shoulder problems, negatively affects body image and self-esteem, and may cause the athlete to feel worthless, helpless and unsuccessful, resulting in a decrease in self-confidence [9]. Body image is explained as the individual’s idea of how his or her own body looks in his mind [17]. Body image is a concept that includes the individual’s feelings, thoughts and perceptions of his or her own body and varies according to age, gender, weight, health status, time and society [18]. Regular exercise has a positive effect on body image [18, 19]. It is stated that individuals who feel happy and have positive emotions about their body have a positive body image [20]. It has been reported that high body image perception in athletes has a positive effect on sports success and self-confidence, while consequences that negatively affect appearance, such as sports injury or disease, negatively affect body image [21]. In kickboxing athletes, performance is closely related to a high level of self-confidence and mental endurance [3].

In the literature, no study has been found investigating the upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception sense, and body image of kickboxing athletes with SD. The aim of the present study is to evaluate upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception sense, and body image of male kickboxers with and without scapular dyskinesia. Our hypothesis was that SD negatively impacts upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception sense, or body image in male kickboxers.

Methods

Patients and study design

The Noninvasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol. The kickboxing athletes were informed about the scope and procedures of the study. All kickboxing athletes provided written informed consent before participating in the present study. The study was conducted in Istanbul Bahcelievler Sports Club in 2022. The present study was designed and written according to The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

The sample size calculation was performed considering one of the study’s primary outcomes, Body Image, based on the results of the body image scale from the study by Sayaca et al. [9]. The effect size was calculated as 0.711. A power of 80% and a significance level of 5% were considered in the study. As a result of the software output, 26 kickboxers per group (total 52 kickboxers) were required. G×Power version 3.1 was used to calculate the sample size.

In the present study, 56 male licensed kickboxers whose ages were 18–35 years, and whose body mass indexes were between 18.5 and 25 kg/cm2 were included. Kickboxer who had chronic systemic disease, any surgery, pain in the upper extremity, and tumoral disease were not included. After the demographic informations (age, height, weight, time of performing kickbox) of all kickboxers participating in the study were recorded, SD, upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception sense, and body perception were evaluated by the same physiotherapist. The kickboxers included in this study were divided into two groups according to whether they had SD or not.

Instruments

All assessments except SD were made by the same researcher, and the researcher who made these assessments did not know whether the individuals had SD or not.

Evaluation of scapular dyskinesia

The Lateral Scapular Slide Test (LSST) was used to identify scapular dyskinesia. LSST was evaluated bilaterally in three different positions: (a) arms in neutral position and next to the body, (b) hands on the waist with thumbs pointing behind, and (c) arms in 90⁰ abduction and maximum internal rotation position. The distance between the inferior angle of the scapula and the spinous processes of the thoracic vertebrae was measured with a tape measure in all three positions and recorded. The test was considered positive if a difference greater than 1.5 cm was detected between bilateral measurements [7].

Evaluation of upper extremity performance

The Closed Kinetic Chain Upper Extremity Stability Test (CKCUES Test) was used to evaluate the upper extremity performance. The CKCUES test was performed from a push-up position. The hands at 36 inches apart and the weight-bearing upper extremities were positioned vertically to the floor. Two parallel and aligned lines were marked on the floor to determine the initial placement of the hands. Kickboxer gave his weight-bearing on one hand and lifted another hand to touch his supporting hands. Then he returned to the starting position again. The test consists of counting how many times he can touch his supporting hand with the other hand and then return to the starting position in fifteen seconds. Kickboxer performed a few repetitions to learn the task. After that, each kickboxer performed three trials of 15 s. The time counting started when the second physiotherapist said “GO” and stopped when he said “STOP” [22]. A resting time of 45 s was established between repetitions, because a work/rest ratio of 1:3 was suggested as appropriate for avoiding fatigue effects in the performance during a short duration and relatively high-intensity test, such as the CKCUES Test [23]. The test was accepted when he kept his back flat and his feet as in the initial position, did not touch the floor with the knee(s), and kept his weight-bearing upper extremity perpendicular to the floor. If the kickboxer was unable to perform properly the test, he was stopped, and after 45 s of rest another repetition was tried [22].

Evaluation of shoulder proprioception sense

Shoulder joint proprioception sense was evaluated by using the active reposition test in a standing upright position. Before the elevation, the eyes were closed with the help of a tape. A digital inclinometer (Dualer IQ Pro, JTECH Medical Industries) was used to evaluate shoulder proprioception sensation on the side with scapular dyskinesia. The digital inclinometer was placed at the insertion point of the deltoid muscle on the humerus. The athlete’s arm was brought to the target abduction angle to be measured from the 0-degree starting position by the physiotherapist who will perform the evaluation. The athlete was kept in the same position for 5 s to learn this target angle and then returned to the starting position again. For evaluation, the athlete was asked to actively move his arm to the taught target angle. The evaluation of proprioception sense was repeated at 40°, 70°, 90° and 135° abduction angles of the shoulder joint, respectively [24]. Since it was stated that the most reliable results in proprioception evaluation were measurements made with at least 6 repetitions, the test for each target angle was repeated 6 times and the average of the three highest angles measured during the evaluation was included in the study [25].

Evaluation of body image

The body perception scale was used to evaluate body image. The scale evaluates the individual’s satisfaction with forty different body parts and functions. Each question was evaluated between 1 and 5 points. On the scale, 1 point means “I like it very much” and 5 points mean “I don’t like it at all”. The total score that can be obtained from the scale was between 40 and 200 points. An increase in the total score expresses the individual’s dissatisfaction with body parts and functions [26]. Turkish validity and reliability of the scale used in the study was conducted [27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with the IBM-SPSS for Windows version 28 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were given as number and frequency distribution for categorical data, and given as mean ± standard deviation for numerical data. The normal distribution of the obtained numerical variables were determined by visual (histogram and probability graphs) and analytical methods (Kolmogorov–Smirnov/Shapiro–Wilk tests, variation coefficient analysis). Age, height, weight, body mass index, duration of doing sport, upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception, and body image were analyzed with independent sample t-tests between groups. The significance level was accepted p < 0.05.

Results

In the present study, 56 male individuals who were licensed kickboxing athletes were included. SD was seen in 27 (48.21%) of these kickboxers, SD was not seen in 29 (51.78%) of them. The mean age of the kickboxers was 25.03 ± 6 years, and the age range was 18–35 years. The mean of their body mass index was 23.39 ± 1.34 kg/cm2 and the mean duration of sports was 36.76 ± 44.16 months. 19 (70.4%) of the kickboxers with SD were found to have superior corner type, 7 (25.9%) had medial corner type SD, and 1 (3.7%) had inferior corner type SD. Discussion

This is the first study conducted to evaluate upper extremity performance, shoulder proprioception sense, and body image in male kickboxers with and without SD. At the end of the study, it was determined that kickboxers with SD had been doing this sport for longer time, also their upper extremity performance, body image, and shoulder proprioception sense at 70° and 90° abduction angles were better.

SD occurs due to many reasons and is called inappropriate movement of the scapula during shoulder movements [6]. It was stated that it has been especially seen more in athletes [28]. It has been reported that SD, seen in male kickboxing athletes, can negatively affect body image, causing the athlete to feel worthless, helpless and unsuccessful, and as a result, a decrease in self-confidence [9]. It is known that regular exercise has a positive effect on body image [18, 19]. Thanks to regular sports, athletes feel happy and have positive body image by gaining positive emotions about their bodies [20]. This positive body image perception achieved by the athlete increases the individual’s sports performance by positively affecting their self-confidence [3]. It was determined that the body images of male kickboxers with SD who participated in this study were higher than kickboxers without SD. SD is a cause of pain and dysfunction in the shoulder and is defined as improper movement of the scapula during movement. It may occur due to posture disorders as well as shoulder pathologies [6]. Consequences that negatively affect external appearance, such as sports injury or illness, negatively affect body image. It is known that this may have a negative effect on sports success and self-confidence [21]. It can be thought that the emergence of SD as a result of a posture or dysfunction may cause the body image to be negatively affected and the performance of athletes to decrease directly or indirectly. However, it was observed that the body images of the kickboxers with SD who participated in this study were better than the kickboxers without SD. It was thought that the main reason for this was that the kickboxers with SD who participated in this study had been performing kickbox for a longer time than the kickboxers without SD. Kickboxing for a long time might have caused the athletes to increase their self-confidence and develop their ability to more easily compensate for the negativities that occur in their bodies (posture disorder and/or shoulder pathologies). Another reason for this result might be that kikcboxers with SD, which are among the results we obtained from our study, had higher upper extremity performances and proprioception senses. The reasons for the high performance of the kickboxers with SD who participated in this study might be that they have a high level of self-confidence and mental endurance due to their body image and more time performing sports [3]. Because it is known that the athlete’s psychological characteristics are among the factors that directly affect his performance [15]. Although the average ages of kickboxers with and without SD participating in this study were the similar, it was determined that kickboxers with SD had been doing this sport for a longer time. A similar result was observed in Brazilian ju-jitsu athletes. Especially, the incidence of SD was higher in more experienced Brazilian ju-jitsu athletes [29]. Engaging in sports for a long time might have caused kickboxers to gain a higher level of willpower and personal maturity, and as a result, a better body image.

In a study conducted on patients with SD who underwent Bankart repair, it was found that SD did not affect the sense of proprioception at 40 and 100 degree shoulder elevation angles [30]. In this study, there was no difference in proprioception senses between kickboxers with and without SD at 40° and 135° shoulder abduction angles. However, it was determined that kickboxers with SD had better shoulder proprioception sense at 70° and 90° abduction angles. The main causes of SD were stated as reduced rotator cuff muscle strength, weak scapula muscles, reduced range of motion of the shoulder, soft tissue lesions in shoulder joints, inadequate movement of the scapula, increasing the risk of impact, and decreased strength of the rotator cuff [31]. Instability, which is one of the causes of SD and seen in individuals with SD, negatively affects the proprioceptive sense [13]. As is known, rotator cuff and biceps muscle activation also increases to compensate for SD [12]. As a result of this increased muscle activation, proprioceptive muscle receptors might have been stimulated more, positively affecting the proprioceptive sense in these angles where the stabilization abilities of the rotator cuff muscles were higher. Another reason for this result may be that the kickboxing athletes who participated in the study and had SD had been kickboxing for a longer time. In the literature, a study was found that athletes had better proprioception sense than age controls matched and it had been emphasized that might be due to the positive effect of sports on proprioception [32].

Another important result we obtained from this study was that kickboxers with SD had better upper extremity performance than kickboxers without SD. When the literature was examined, it was emphasized that there may be a relationship between scapular muscle endurance and SD [33]. It has been stated that the isometric muscle strength of the shoulder external rotators and scapular muscles is significantly lower in individuals with SD [34]. It has been reported that this decrease in shoulder girdle muscle strength also negatively affects scapular endurance [35]. Additionally, it has been stated that SD is related to the results of The Closed Kinetic Chain Upper Extremity Stability Test, which we used in our study [36]. Contrary to the literature, the upper extremity endurance of the kickboxers with SD who participated in our study was found to be better than the athletes without SD. We think that the main reason for this result is that kickboxers with SD have been kickboxing for a longer time. Regular exercise and training may have protected kickboxers from the negative effects of SD and increased upper extremity endurance.

While SD is seen in 33% of athletes who do not perform overhead activities, it has been reported to be seen in 61% of athletes who perform overhead activities [8]. SD is twice as common in male kickboxers compared to sedentary individuals [9]. The main reasons why SD is more common in kickboxers than in sedentary individuals may be that the risk of shoulder injury is higher due to the nature of kickboxing, it involves overhead activities, and there is asymmetric posture disorder. In the present study, similar to the results in the literature, it was determined that the kickboxers who participated in the study and had SD had been performing kickboxing for longer time than the kickboxers without SD. We think that rehabilitative exercise programs should be included in the training programs of kickboxers with SD, and preventive exercise programs should be started for kickboxers who have just started this sport and do not yet have SD.

There were some limitations of this study. Comparing the test results with athletes who do not have SD but have been kickboxing for similar periods of time will provide more accurate results. The use of 3-dimensional movement analysis to evaluate scapular dyskinesis and isokinetic testing to evaluate joint position sense would have provided more precise and objective results. SD may be graded the severity with a scapular dyskinesis test. Effect of severities of SD may be researched on proprioception and performance. Shoulder instability and SD should also be evaluated together.

Conclusion

As a result of the present study, it was determined that kickboxing athletes with SD had been interested in this sport for a longer time and were better in terms of shoulder proprioception, shoulder joint stabilization and body image than kickboxing athletes without SD. Although the incidence of SD increases in kickboxers who spend more time exercising, it is thought that the positive effect of doing sports compensates for the negative effects that may arise from SD.
