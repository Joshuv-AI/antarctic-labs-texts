The impact of high temperature, noise, benzene compounds, and shift work on renal function abnormalities in oil workers: a cohort study in China
Authors: Zheng Li, Zhikang Si, Shangmingzhu Zhang, Nan Wang, Haoruo Zhang, Xiaoming Li, Ling Xue, Jianhui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRenal dysfunction poses a significant global health burden, with occupational hazards in the petroleum industry potentially contributing to its development. This cohort study aimed to evaluate the impact of four occupational hazards—high temperature, noise, benzene compounds, and shift work—on renal dysfunction in a cohort of oil workers.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the “Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Occupational Population Health Effects Cohort.” A total of 2,292 petroleum workers without baseline renal dysfunction were followed from 2017 to 2021. Renal dysfunction was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2. Occupational exposures were assessed using national standards and weighted shift index (WSI) for shift work. Cox regression models, restricted cubic spline analysis, and interaction assessments were employed to analyze associations.ResultsOver four years, 24.52% of workers developed renal dysfunction. Multivariate Cox regression revealed significant risks for workers exposed to high temperature (P = 0.003, HR = 1.33, 95% CI:1.10–1.61), noise (P = 0.001, HR = 1.43, 1.18–1.72), benzene compounds (P = 0.001, HR = 1.70, 1.42–2.05), and high-intensity shift work (WSI > 1022.75; P = 0.001, HR = 1.96, 1.60–2.40). Additive interactions were observed between high-intensity shift work and high temperature (RERI = 1.89), noise (RERI = 1.40), and benzene compounds (RERI = 3.07). Moderate shift work intensity showed a protective effect.ConclusionsExposure to noise, benzene compounds, and high-intensity shift work independently increased renal dysfunction risk among petroleum workers. An association was also observed for high-temperature exposure, and interactions between these hazards amplified risks, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to mitigate occupational exposures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01165-x.

Introduction

Renal dysfunction is a key factor in the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Studies on the global disease burden show that CKD prevalence in China is as high as 10.8%, and the mortality rate related to CKD jumped by 41.5% from 1990 to 2017 [1, 2], which is a major public health issue. Workers in the oil industry face compounded exposure to complex occupational hazards [3], making it crucial to clarify the health risks these exposures pose to their renal function.

Although cross-sectional studies suggest associations between exposure to benzene, noise, and renal function indicators such as serum creatinine [4, 5], such cross-sectional studies cannot establish a temporal relationship between occupational exposure and renal dysfunction. Existing research has revealed the negative health effects of single exposures (such as benzene or high temperatures) [6–8], but it doesn’t explore how these factors might interact with each other. Furthermore, relevant literature often focuses on blood and liver function tests, with very limited and insufficiently detailed prospective assessment data on renal function [4], resulting in significant gaps in assessing kidney health risks for this high-risk group of oil workers.

Based on the “Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Occupational Population Health Impact Cohort,” this study employed a prospective cohort design to examine the independent and combined effects of four core occupational exposures (high temperatures, noise, benzene compounds, and shift work) on the risk of renal dysfunction among oil workers, and, for the first time, to investigate how these factors interact (especially synergistic interactions). This research aims to fill the gap in the long-term impact assessment of multifactorial occupational exposures and interaction analysis, providing important evidence to help develop targeted protective strategies for the kidney health of oil workers.

Methods

Study participants

This study, part of the National Key Research and Development Program on occupational health in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Region, involved North China Oilfield workers and utilized a cohort study method with baseline surveys in September 2017 and follow-ups from April 2019 to November 2021, collecting annual health check-up data. Inclusion criteria included formal employees with over a year of experience, informed consent, and no renal dysfunction; exclusion criteria involved incomplete questionnaires or health checks and medication affecting glomerular filtration rate; loss to follow-up included transfers, retirements, or unexpected deaths.

Formal employees: Refers to employees who have signed an indefinite or long-term labor contract (usually > 1 year) directly with the North China Oilfield Administration. They enjoy complete employee benefits (such as medical insurance, annual health check-ups, work injury insurance, etc.), and their job positions, exposure history, and health records are complete and stable. This study excludes temporary workers, seasonal workers, labor dispatch workers, and outsourced employees.

A total of 2802 study participants were included at baseline, with 426 cases of renal dysfunction excluded, followed by the exclusion of 84 participants who met the exclusion criteria during the two follow-ups, resulting in a final sample size of 2292. (see Fig. 1) This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of North China University of Science and Technology (number: 16040), and all study participants signed informed consent forms.

Flowchart of Participant Inclusion and Exclusion

Demographic information (e.g., age, gender, education level, and average monthly income), behavioral lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, and dietary habits), personal medical history (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hyperuricemia), and work-related information (e.g., employment nature, work experience, job type, shift work, and occupational hazard exposure) were collected via structured questionnaires. Physical examinations were conducted to collect information on height, weight, and blood pressure. For laboratory tests, we collected fasting venous blood samples from study participants in the morning, and the hospital laboratory doctors measured the levels of serum creatinine (Scr), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and uric acid (UA).

Renal dysfunction and occupational factors

This study applied the widely used Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) formula [9] to calculate estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}eGFR & =186\times\:(Scr{)}^{-1.154}\times\:(Age{)}^{-0.203}\\& \quad \times\:(female\times\:0.828)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

eGFR was assessed at baseline and subsequently re-measured at each annual follow-up examination. CKD progressing to stage G2 is an early sign of renal function damage [10]. According to the GFR standards for CKD stage G2, eGFR < 90 mL/min/(1.73 m2) is defined as renal dysfunction.

Shift work refers to a system where employees work in rotating shifts outside of regular working hours. Participants in the shift work group are those exposed to shift work factors. According to the results of the questionnaire survey, the shift types are divided into five shifts with three rotations (1 night shift in 5 days), four shifts with three rotations (1 night shift in 4 days), three shifts with two rotations (1 night shift in 3 days), and two shifts (1 night shift in 2 days). The weighted shift index (WSI) can be used to assess the cumulative exposure of study participants to shift work, calculated as WSI=∑T = T1 + T2…+Tn, where T is the average number of night shifts per year. Night shifts are defined as labor or work conducted between 0:00 and 6:00. For example, in the three shifts with two rotations, one work team has workers on the night shift, another work team has workers on the day shift, and a third work team is resting, rotating after 24 h. In other words, participants in the three shifts with two rotations have 1 night shift in 3 days, meaning that 1/3 of their working time is night shift time. Oil workers generally do not adjust their work schedules, and we assume that participants’ shift types remain unchanged. A higher WSI indicates stronger exposure to shift work among study participants.

This study assessed exposure to high temperatures, noise, dust, and benzene compounds based on relevant national standards [11–17], conducted by units qualified for occupational health evaluation and testing, and determined exposure based on the participants’ job positions. (see Appendix Table 1 in Additional File 1)

Covariates

Based on previous research experience, this study grouped the following factors for preliminary analysis: gender (male/female), age (≤ 45 years, > 45 years), education level (junior high school and below, high school, college and above), income (≤ 2000, > 2000 yuan/month), body mass index (BMI) (< 24.0, 24.0–, ≥ 28.0 kg/m2), smoking (non-smoker, former smoker, current smoker), drinking (non-drinker, former drinker, current drinker), blood pressure levels (normal blood pressure, normal high, hypertension) [18], blood glucose levels (normal blood glucose, prediabetes, diabetes) [19], blood lipid levels (normal blood lipids, hyperlipidemia) [20], uric acid levels (normal uric acid, hyperuricemia) [21], and dietary habits scored according to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet (8–40 points) [22], with higher scores indicating healthier diets.

Statistical analysis

An Excel database was created from baseline and follow-up data, with statistical analyses conducted using SPSS 23.0 and R-4.2.2. The handling of missing data was as follows: based on the pre-defined exclusion criteria, participants with any missing information in the exposure, outcome, or key covariates were excluded from the cohort. This complete-case analysis resulted in the exclusion of 84 participants, and no data imputation methods were applied. Non-normally distributed measurement data were described using median and interquartile range M (P25, P75), and count data were expressed as rates or composition ratios. Cox regression models were applied for univariate and multivariate analyses of the relationship between occupational hazards and renal dysfunction; a restricted cubic spline model was used to analyze the dose-response relationship between WSI and renal dysfunction, with four knots selected; in the Cox regression model, interaction terms between occupational hazards were included to evaluate whether multiplicative interactions existed. The interaction relative excess risk (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), synergy index (S), and their confidence intervals were calculated using the Andersson Excel table, and these three indicators were used to evaluate the potential additive interaction effects of shift work with other factors on the risk of renal dysfunction. If an additive interaction exists between two factors, the confidence intervals for RERI and AP should not include 0, and the confidence interval for S should not include 1. The significance level α was set at 0.05, and two-sided tests were used. A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons in the univariate Cox regression analyses. The significance level was adjusted to α = 0.05/n, where n is the number of independent variables tested (n = 19, resulting in a corrected α of 0.00263).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2292 study participants were included in this study, comprising 1520 males (66.3%) and 772 females (33.7%), with an age distribution of M (P25–P75) being 42 (37, 46) years, with the youngest being 19 years old and the oldest being 58 years old; among them, 562 individuals had renal dysfunction, with a cumulative incidence rate of 24.52% over four years. We calculated person-years at risk (PYAR) for each participant from the date of enrollment until the date of renal dysfunction diagnosis, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (November 2021), whichever came first. The total follow-up time was 6426.74 person-years, resulting in an incidence density of 87.44 per 1000 person-years. Due to eGFR < 60 mL/min/(1.73 m2) potentially indicating kidney disease, we performed calculations based on this criterion. There are 5 participants eGFR < 60 mL/min/(1.73 m2). The cumulative incidence is 0.22%, which corresponds to a PYAR of 6430.65 person-years and an incidence density of 0.78 per 1000 person-years.

Potential univariate risk factors for renal dysfunction

In univariate Cox regression analyses, several factors were associated with renal dysfunction at the nominal significance level (P < 0.05). After applying a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (significance threshold set at P < 0.00263), female gender, older age (> 45 years), lower monthly income (≤ 2000 yuan), and occupational exposures to noise and benzene compounds remained significant risk factors. The associations for high-temperature exposure, prediabetes, and current drinking were not robust to this strict correction. (see Appendix Table 2 in Additional File 1)

Analysis of oil workers showed 1396 (60.91%) were overweight or obese, with renal dysfunction rates of 25.24% and 25.17%, respectively, but no significant correlation with BMI (P = 0.706). Most had normal high blood pressure, with no renal dysfunction differences. Prediabetes was a risk factor for renal dysfunction (P < 0.05), while diabetes was not (P = 0.223). Drinkers had a higher incidence of renal dysfunction than non-drinkers, while blood lipids, uric acid, smoking, and diet did not increase risk. (see Appendix Table 2 in Additional File 1)

The factor with the highest number of exposed individuals was shift work, affecting 1855 oil workers with a higher renal dysfunction rate; noise exposure affected 1372 workers with an HR of 1.46; high temperatures and benzene compounds had lower exposure rates but higher incidence rates (HRs of 1.30 and 1.47, respectively) compared to non-exposed individuals. (see Appendix Table 2 in Additional File 1)

Multivariable independent risk effects of occupational hazards

We set the occurrence of renal dysfunction as the dependent variable and potential factors identified through literature review as independent variables. All independent variables underwent multicollinearity testing and did not exhibit collinearity characteristics. (see Appendix Table 3 in Additional File 1)

The study used multivariate Cox regression to analyze four occupational hazards as independent variables and renal dysfunction as the dependent variable, adjusting for factors’ influence. Results indicated that oil workers exposed to high temperatures had a 1.33 times higher risk of renal dysfunction, those exposed to noise had a 1.43 times higher risk, and benzene exposure increased risk 1.70 times, while shift work exposure did not significantly affect risk. (see Appendix Table 4 in Additional File 1)

Nonlinear dose-response relationship between shift work intensity and renal function

Due to significant discrepancies between the Cox regression analysis results for shift work and its univariate analysis results, and the complexity of shift work exposure related to both the duration and intensity of shifts, this study used WSI to calculate the cumulative exposure to shift work and quantitatively analyze the relationship between shift work and renal dysfunction in oil workers. After adjusting for high temperature, noise and benzene compounds, a non-linear relationship was found between WSI and the HR value for renal dysfunction (P non-linear < 0.05). Visual inspection of the RCS curve revealed a clear transition point at a WSI of approximately 1022.75, where the risk plateaued or slightly decreased before this point and increased steeply beyond it. Consequently, we defined this data-driven transition point as the cut-off value, categorizing WSI into two groups: ‘≤1022.75’ and ‘>1022.75’ for further analysis. (see Fig. 2)

After grouping WSI based on shift work conditions, other variables were gradually included to construct a Cox regression model. We first decided to include demographic variables such as gender, age, and monthly per capita income in Model 1 based on the univariate significance results. In Model 2, we further included health status variables such as diabetes and alcohol consumption. Finally, drawing from literature and our research experience, we added a few more variables in Model 3. The results showed that when WSI was above 1022.75, the risk of renal dysfunction among oil workers was 1.96 times that of those at or below 1022.75 (95% CI: 1.60–2.40), and this result was consistent across the three models. Synergistic effects of high-intensity shift work and other hazards

The results indicated that the interaction terms among the four occupational hazards were not statistically significant, suggesting an absence of multiplicative interactions; however, additive interactions were present in the high temperature × WSI, noise × WSI, and benzene compounds × WSI groups, meaning that when two occupational hazards coexisted, the risk of renal dysfunction was greater than the sum of the risks when each existed alone, with differences of 1.89, 1.40, and 3.07, respectively, and the proportion of risk attributable to the interaction being 0.36, 0.33, and 0.42. (see Table 2)

Stratified analysis of occupational hazard risks

Stratified analyses indicated that high temperature exposure increased renal dysfunction risk among oil workers across various subgroups, including gender, education, income, and health conditions, with similar results in age and uric acid groups compared to non-exposure. (see Appendix Table 5 in Additional File 1) Similarly, noise exposure also raised renal dysfunction risk among oil workers in several subgroups, including females and those with normal health metrics, though the small sample size of former drinkers limited quantitative conclusions. (see Appendix Table 6 in Additional File 1) Benzene exposure raised renal dysfunction risk among oil workers across various subgroups, including blood sugar, lipids, smoking, drinking, and demographic factors, compared to non-exposure. (see Appendix Table 7 in Additional File 1).

Stratified analyses for different levels of WSI and various subgroups showed that using WSI ≤ 1022.75 as a reference, stronger shift work exposure increased the risk of renal dysfunction among oil workers in the normal or low, overweight, non-drinking, and drinking subgroups; in other stratified analyses, stronger shift work exposure increased the risk of renal dysfunction compared to the reference group. (see Appendix Table 8 in Additional File 1).

Discussion

Currently, about 13.4% of people worldwide have chronic kidney disease, with 4.902 to 7.083 million patients requiring renal replacement therapy for ESRD [23]. In China, the overall prevalence of chronic kidney disease is 10.8%, with some regional differences; the prevalence of CKD in northern and southwestern regions is 16.9% and 18.3% [2], respectively, making them high-incidence areas for chronic kidney disease. According to the literature reviewed, most studies on chronic kidney disease are cross-sectional, with few studies on incidence rates, and most participants are from community populations, not considering the exposure to occupational hazards faced by working populations. This study employed a cohort study method to follow up on oil workers in northern China for four years, finding a cumulative incidence rate of 24.52% for abnormal renal function over four years and an incidence density of 87.44 per 1000 person-years, meaning that among 1000 healthy oil workers, approximately 87 develop abnormal renal function each year. This study used the Cox regression model to explore the risks of four occupational hazards associated with abnormal renal function and grouped the occupational hazards based on exposure and RCS curves to analyze the stratified effects and interactions among factors, investigating the associations between occupational hazards and abnormal renal function at certain exposure levels.

A global study by Xue Baode et al. [24] assessed the burden of CKD due to high or low temperatures in 204 countries from 1990 to 2019, showing a significant increasing trend in the mortality burden of CKD due to high temperatures during this period. An ecological survey in Adelaide, Australia, indicated that high-temperature environments increase the incidence of kidney diseases [25]; a meta-analysis by Liu J et al. [7] also supports this view, showing that for every 1 °C increase in temperature, the risk of kidney-related diseases increases by 1% (RR: 1.010; 95% CI: 1.009–1.011), and the risk of kidney-related mortality increases by 3% (RR: 1.031; 95% CI: 1.018–1.045). In this study, 28.05% of workers were exposed to high-temperature environments, with an incidence rate of 27.37% in the exposed group, showing a significant difference compared to the non-exposed group. Oil workers exposed to high temperatures had a 1.33 times higher risk of abnormal renal function than those not exposed in the multivariate model, however, this association did not remain statistically significant after strict correction for multiple testing in the univariate analysis. In more than half of the subgroups, high-temperature exposure did not increase the incidence risk, particularly among those with normal high blood pressure, hypertension, prediabetes, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, where no effect of high-temperature exposure on incidence risk was observed. This may be because these oil workers with metabolic syndrome had a high incidence rate in the non-exposed group [26], thereby diminishing the effect of high temperature as an occupational hazard.

Petrochemical enterprises generate high-intensity, continuous, and constant noise during various processes such as oil and gas field extraction, refining, and sales [27]. A cross-sectional study by KIM Y J et al. [5] identified “hearing discomfort” as an indicator of occupational noise exposure, which found that hearing discomfort is significantly linked to CKD (OR: 2.58; 95% CI: 2.18–3.06), with a stronger association between hearing discomfort in women and CKD (OR: 3.10; 95% CI: 2.42–3.96). However, they used a self-assessment questionnaire to check if study participants were exposed to noise, which is potentially subjective and could lead to biased results. In this study, 59.86% of oil workers were exposed to noise on the job. We used a cohort study to measure the noise exposure of oil workers on the job, concluding that oil workers exposed to noise are 1.43 times more likely to have abnormal renal function than those who aren’t exposed.

Petrochemical enterprises are the main producers of benzene compounds, and benzene can volatilize at room temperature, leading to frequent exposure of oil workers to benzene during operations [28]. Benzene compounds can cause oxidative stress in the body, damaging multiple organs [29, 30]. The kidneys are one of the main organs for excreting toxic substances, and even low-dose exposure can adversely affect renal function [31]. Cao Yanmei et al. [8] analyzed health check data from employees with long-term exposure to low concentrations of benzene in 71 enterprises, finding that in most workplaces, the benzene levels met national standards, yet the urea nitrogen levels in the exposed group were significantly higher than those in the non-exposed group, with higher rates of positive urine protein, urine glucose, urine red blood cells, and abnormal kidney ultrasound results, suggesting that benzene exposure leads to abnormal renal function. In this study, benzene compounds, as the only chemical factor among the four occupational hazards, had an incidence rate in the exposed group that was 1.70 times that of the non-exposed group and increased the incidence risk in most subgroups, acting as an independent risk factor for abnormal renal function.

Shift work primarily affects the body by disrupting normal circadian rhythms. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) in the kidneys is regulated by circadian rhythms, and long-term shift work leads to overactivation of RAAS, causing damage and remodeling of glomerular endothelial cells, which in turn leads to varying degrees of kidney damage [32]. It is evident that kidney damage is a long-term cumulative process, and merely categorizing shift work exposure as present or absent is insufficient. Therefore, this study chose to use WSI to quantify the exposure level of oil workers to shift work and employed restricted cubic spline analysis to investigate the dose-response relationship between shift work and abnormal renal function. The results indicated that moderate intensity shift work had a protective effect against the occurrence of abnormal renal function, suggesting a healthy worker effect in the shift work process of oil workers, where those who developed abnormal renal function or other diseases early on in shift work would choose to leave shift positions [33], thereby reducing the incidence of abnormal renal function among oil workers with moderate shift work exposure. This healthy worker effect has also been observed in other diseases [34]. In this study, the risk of abnormal renal function for those with WSI above 1022.75 was 1.96 times that of those with WSI at or below 1022.75, and in most subgroups, benzene exposure was a risk factor for abnormal renal function. It is noteworthy that there were additive interactions between WSI and exposure to high temperatures, noise, and benzene compounds, meaning that when high-intensity shift work coexists with exposure to high temperatures, noise, and benzene compounds, the risk of abnormal renal function is higher when both factors are present than when each is considered separately. This phenomenon indicates that multiple factors are not independent but may interact synergistically. When high-intensity shift work coexists with the other three occupational hazards, renal function is more adversely affected among oil workers.

There are a few limitations to this study. First, it did not consider the cumulative exposure to other occupational hazards aside from shift work. Second, the study participants were all active employees, which may introduce a healthy worker effect. Third, the study observed outcomes through regular health check-ups, which has a certain lag, and we only used eGFR to determine outcomes, lacking other auxiliary indicators for assessment. Fourth, this study excluded participants who had worked for less than one year and those who were lost to follow-up, since these could introduce bias, especially if the reasons for loss to follow-up are related to the exposure or outcome being studied. Fifth, this study did not conduct a more detailed comparative analysis of the types of shift work. Sixth, the analysis of smoking and alcohol consumption in this study lacks granularity, as data on the quantity and frequency of use were not available. Finally, the follow-up duration of this study was limited to four years; a longer observation period might be required to fully assess the long-term effects of these exposures.

Conclusion

Overall, we found that exposure to high temperatures, noise, benzene compounds, and high intensity shift work are all risk factors for renal dysfunction among oil workers. Among these, benzene compounds and high intensity shift work increase the risk of renal dysfunction across multiple subgroups, acting as independent risk factors for kidney problems. Notably, additive interactions exist between high intensity shift work and high temperatures, noise, and benzene compounds.
