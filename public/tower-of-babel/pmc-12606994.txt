Risk factors for incomplete excision of colorectal polyps: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhongxin Sun, Tengfei Cao, Wen Wei, Jiao Li, Jing Shan, Weidong Xi, Xiaobin Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe object of this study was to explore the risk factors for incomplete excision of colorectal polyps (CP) through a systematic review and meta-analysis.MethodsA comprehensive research of various databases, including CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, was carried out to confirm case-control studies investigating the risk factors for incomplete excision of CP. The search deadline was March 20, 2023. Analyses of the data were performed utilizing Stata 15.0.ResultsThe meta-analysis identified several factors significantly associated with the risk of residual or recurrent lesions. Polyps measuring ≥ 6 mm [OR = 1.494; 95%CI: 1.117–1.999; P = 0.007], flat polyps [OR = 1.81; 95%CI: 1.35, 2.41; P < 0.001], sessile serrated lesions [OR = 3.22; 95%CI: 1.93, 5.36; P < 0.001], high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia [OR = 2.34; 95%CI: 1.15, 4.76; P = 0.019], and polyps that underwent piecewise excision [OR = 3.10; 95%CI: 1.96, 4.90; P < 0.001] were all associated with increased risk. In contrast, adenomatous polyps [OR = 0.50; 95%CI: 0.26, 0.96; P = 0.036] were associated with a lower risk. Complete excision uncertainty was a protective factor for complete excision of CP [OR = 0.503, 95%CI (0.233, 1.087), P = 0.081]. In multivariate analysis, polyps ≥ 6 mm remained an independent predictor of residual or recurrent lesions [OR = 1.494, 95%CI (1.117, 1.999), P = 0.007].ConclusionsBased on existing evidence, CP ≥ 6 mm, flat morphology, sessile serrated lesions, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, and piecewise excision could be risk factors for incomplete excision of CP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04392-y.

Introduction

Colon cancer is the predominant tumor of the digestive system, characterized by its high incidence. Its incidence has risen from the fourth to the second most common type of malignant tumors in China [1]. In many underdeveloped regions in Eastern Europe, Asia, and South America, the incidence of colon cancer is also rising rapidly [2]. Early excision of colorectal polyps (CP) may effectively decrease the risk and mortality of colon cancer [3].

The endoscopic excision methods for CP include hot snare polypectomy (HSP), endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), cold snare polypectomy (CSP), and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD). In cases performing HP, EMR, and ESD, the cauterization effect may prevent local recurrence and the occurrence of intraoperative cancer (ICC). However, the risk of ICC after polypectomy still exists. In contrast to electrocautery, CSP does not have a cauterization effect. Notably, patients undergoing CSP might face a heightened risk of local recurrence and ICC due to incomplete polyp resection (IPR), compared to those undergoing HP or EMR [4, 5]. One of the primary factors contributing to ICC is IPR. However, there is currently limited research exploring the potential link between IPR and the endoscopic excision method of CP.

Recent studies have shown that the incomplete excision rate is influenced by the size of polyps, sessile serrated histology, and the experience of endoscopists. Some studies have even found that up to 31% of large, sessile, or flat polyps have recurred after incomplete excision [6, 7]. In addition, various factors such as female, the difficulty of colonoscopy operation, and the skills of assistants have, to some extent, increased the incomplete excision rate of CP [8, 9]. Incomplete polypectomy accounts for 19% −27% of the cancer incidence before the next colonoscopy (interval cancer) [6]. Therefore, this study explored the risk factors for incomplete excision of CP through a meta-analysis and systematic review. By identifying and preventing patient- or surgery-related risk factors, this study improved the complete excision rate of polyps.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) (https://www.prisma-statement.org/) guidelines and has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024568716, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024568716).

Literature search

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases, including CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, to determine the incidence and risk factors associated with incomplete excision of CP. The search deadline was March 20, 2024. Both Mesh and free-text terms were used, including intestinal polyps, CP, polypectomy, endoscopic excision, incomplete polypectomy, incomplete excision, risk factors, social risk factors, health-related, high-risk population, and risk factor score. The detailed strategy can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Criteria for inclusion were as follows: Adults who met the diagnostic criteria for CP excision [10]; Case-control research, cohort research, and randomized trials using incomplete polypectomy as an exposure factor. The primary outcome measure was the analysis of univariate and multivariate risk factors. While there were no restrictions on including both univariate and multivariate analyses, if both univariate and multivariate results were presented in one study, the univariate results were prioritized. When only univariate analysis was available, the univariate results were reported. The secondary outcome assessed was the rate of complete excision.

The following studies were excluded: conference abstracts, meta-analyses, protocols, letters, duplicates, systematic reviews, studies without full text, studies with unavailable data, and animal tests.

Literature screening and data extraction

The retrieved articles were independently screened by two reviewers. The titles, abstracts, and full texts were reviewed. Studies that were unequivocally eligible were directly screened. For those with discrepancies regarding inclusion, relevant experts were consulted, and their full texts were downloaded and reviewed to reach a consensus. Throughout the retrieval process, strict adherence to inclusion and exclusion criteria was maintained. Relevant indices were extracted and cross-verified to ensure consistency. Key extracted data encompassed the primary author’s name, year of publication, country, research design, sample size, gender, and age.

Quality assessment

Case-control/cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11], involving three aspects: selection of the research population (4 points), comparability between groups (2 points), and exposure factors or outcome measures (3 points). Based on an overall score of 9, ≤ 4 represented low quality, 5–6 represented moderate quality, and ≥ 7 represented high quality. The randomized experiments were evaluated using the Jadad scale [12], which assessed four aspects: random sequence generation (2 points), randomization concealment (2 points), blinding selection (2 points), and the presence of an exit description (1 point). The scale scored a maximum of 10 points, with scores of 1–3 indicating low quality and 4–7 indicating high quality. If two investigators have disagreements during the evaluation process, they can discuss or request a third party to make a decision.

Statistical analysis

Statistical assessment of the data was performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) 4.0. The risk values for all studies were represented by ORs, and the pooled OR values and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. The appropriate model was selected according to the results of the heterogeneity test (Q test). All outcomes were pooled using a random effects model to account for potential heterogeneity between studies [13–15]. Egger’s test and plot of funnel were used for analyzing publication bias, with the significance level set as α = 0.05. A P value below 0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant.

Results

Literature retrieval procedure and results

After a preliminary search of the databases CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, a total of 1,694 articles were found. After the elimination of duplicates, 1,066 articles were identified. After screening titles and abstracts, 104 studies were left. After a full-text review, ineligible studies were excluded, and 11 studies were eligible and included in this analysis. The retrieval procedure is detailed in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics of the enrolled studies

The 11 studies [7–9, 16–23] employed three research methodologies: case-control studies, cohort studies, and randomized trials. Notably, 5 of these articles were from Japan, reporting a total of 1,020 polyps partially removed and 17,248 polyps completely removed. The age of patients in the included studies varied from 56 to 70 years. The characteristics of these studies are detailed in Table 1. Two of the included articles were evaluated using the Jadad scale, with one article scoring 4 points and one scoring 6 points. The overall quality of the enrolled studies was high. Univariate meta-analysis

CP ≥ 6 mm

Ten studies mentioned CP ≥ 6 mm. The research findings revealed that CP ≥ 6 mm was a risk factor for incomplete excision of CP, and the difference was statistically marked [OR = 1.494, 95%CI (1.117, 1.999), P = 0.007]. However, this result should be interpreted with caution. The result exhibited significant heterogeneity (Q test, p < 0.1), with a prediction interval (0.715, 3.121). This indicates that while the average effect across existing studies supports CP ≥ 6 mm as a risk factor for incomplete resection, substantial inter-study variability suggests that future research in specific contexts may yield inconsistent conclusions. The details are illustrated in Fig. 2; Table 3.

Flat CP

Five studies mentioned the morphology of CP. The research findings revealed that flat CP was a risk factor for incomplete excision of CP, and the difference was statistically marked [OR = 1.81, 95%CI (1.35, 2.41), P = 0.00]. The details are illustrated in Fig. 3; Table 3.

Pathological results of adenoma

Seven studies mentioned adenoma. The research findings revealed that adenoma was a protective factor for complete excision of polyps, and the difference was statistically marked [OR = 0.5, 95%CI (0.26, 0.96), P = 0.036]. This result should be interpreted with caution. The result exhibited significant heterogeneity (Q test, p < 0.1), with a prediction interval (0.062, 4.047). This indicates that while the average effect across existing studies supports adenoma as a protective factor for incomplete resection, substantial inter-study variability suggests that future research in specific contexts may yield inconsistent conclusions. Other meta-analysis findings

No statistically significant association of incomplete excision of CP with age, female, polyps ≥ 1–5 mm, SSA/P, CSP, surgical experience of endoscopists, and the location of CP was observed. The details are illustrated in Supplementary Material 2 and Table 3.

Multivariate meta-analysis results

Combined multivariate analysis results from the studies indicated no statistically significant correlation of polypoid protuberant lesions, endoscopists’ experience, and polypectomy methods (CSP/EMR) with incomplete excision of CP. Discussion

The study explored the risk factors for incomplete excision of CP through a meta-analysis. CPs are protruding lesions from the surface of the colonic mucosa to the enteric cavity, with an incidence rate of 1% to 43% [24]. The carcinogenic process of CP mainly includes the adenoma pathway and serrated polyp pathway. The risk of carcinogenesis of inflammatory polyps and hyperplastic polyps is relatively low [25]. Early detection and excision of CP can significantly decrease the risk and the mortality of colorectal carcinoma [3]. However, there is still a high recurrence rate of CP after colonoscopy excision. It has been shown that the 5-year recurrence rate after surgery is as high as 60% [26]. The research findings have shown that the development of ICC is a potential complication after colon polypectomy, with IPR identified as a primary cause. While numerous studies have established correlations between IPR and factors such as gender, age, size of CP, difficulty of colonoscopy operation, and the operation experience of endoscopists, these findings remain controversial [7].

The results of univariate and multivariate analysis show that, in the future, for a similar population, CP ≥ 6 mm will be a risk factor for incomplete polypectomy. Consequently, patients with CP of this size will have a significantly higher incidence of incomplete polypectomy during colonoscopy. Regardless of HSP, CSP, or EMR methods, complete excision could be achieved. For polyps ≥ 6 mm, the incidence of incomplete excision was significantly higher with CSP (as high as 4%) compared to HSP and EMR [5, 27]. In contrast, some reports suggested that CSP showed no marked difference in incomplete excision rate for CP measuring 10–15 mm compared to those smaller than 5 mm [23]. This study also found that flat CP carried a high risk of incomplete excision, possibly due to the difficulty in adequately capturing them during CSP [28]. Furthermore, the presence of mucosal depressions is found to increase the probability of IPR. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen the preoperative observation and evaluation of the morphology of CP. For the treatment of flat CP, EMR or cold endoscopic mucosal resection (C-EMR) [10, 29] is often preferred due to their lower incidence of incomplete excision. The incidence of incomplete excision of CP was significantly higher in patients with pathological results of sessile serrated lesions and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia. The possible reason for this was that SSLs were easily overlooked during examination, which complicates the accurate assessment of residual polyps at the excision margin [30]. Prior studies have suggested a correlation between SSL and increased IPR, which may contribute to a higher incidence of interval cancer. In addition, serrated lesions exhibit pathological alterations in the basal crypts, including irregularly branched crypts and inverted T- and/or L-shaped crypts. In suspected cases of SSL, it is crucial to meticulously inspect polyp excision site to guarantee complete excision. If necessary, staining and magnifying endoscopy can be applied for observation [31, 32]. In cases of high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, the pathological tissue may breach the mucosal layer and extend towards the muscularis mucosae. The probability of developing cold snare defect protrusion (CSDP) during CSP is high, which will lead to an increased incidence of IPR. Studies have shown that endoscopists can achieve complete excision by using EMR or ESD, although a low probability of incomplete excision persists. These pathological characteristics may lead to incomplete excision of CP. The research findings revealed that piecewise excision was an independent risk factor for incomplete excision of CP. Current research suggests that complete excision of CP can decrease the residual or recurrent adenoma (ORA) rate of colon polypoid lesions [33]. Some studies have reported a notable incidence of ORA (20% −27%) following endoscopic piecewise excision, with a mean recurrence of 22% [34, 35]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis supports the significantly higher risk of local recurrence after piecewise EMR compared to complete EMR excision. Recurrence rates were 20% for piecewise EMR and 3% for complete EMR. Piecewise excision was the sole risk factor markedly correlated with recurrence. In a multi-center prospective study, Oka et al. showed that, irrespective of endoscopic excision methods applied, piecewise excision was the primary risk factor for local recurrence after endoscopic excision [36, 37]. These findings align with the outcomes found in this study. Therefore, clinicians treating CP patients must comprehensively evaluate and analyze patient information and strive for complete polyp removal to reduce the risk of both polyp recurrence and ICC. If complete polyp excision proves challenging, active pre- and post-operative evaluation of CP should be conducted through magnifying and staining colonoscopy, endoscopic ultrasonography, and imaging examinations. This comprehensive assessment, along with the application of appropriate treatment methods, aims to reduce the incidence of postoperative ICC and enhance patient prognosis.

The rate of incomplete excision did not exhibit a marked association with factors such as gender, age, operating experience of endoscopist, location of polyps, and surgical method, a finding that deviated from previous studies. This research has several limitations. Firstly, the number of enrolled articles was relatively small. Secondly, most of the studies originated from Asian populations. To reduce heterogeneity and bias, we will further investigate the incidence of incomplete excision of CP by expanding the sample size and adopting a multi-center study design.

Conclusions

Based on existing evidence, CP ≥ 6 mm, flat morphology, sessile serrated lesions, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, and piecewise excision could be risk factors for incomplete excision of CP. Although most included studies originated from Asian populations, these indicators are valuable for clinical workers to facilitate early detection, diagnosis, and evaluation of CP. By integrating comprehensive information on CP, clinicians can select appropriate treatment methods, thereby reducing the incidence of ICC and polyp recurrence rate, and ultimately improving the treatment quality and prognosis for these patients.
