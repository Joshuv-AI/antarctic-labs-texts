Professional Behavior Assessment During Residency Training
Authors: Ann Van Heest, Steven Frick, John Harrast, David Martin, Mona Saniei, Lisa Taitsman, April Armstrong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) Knowledge, Skills, and Behavior (KSB) project sets up a framework for competency-based medical education for orthopaedic surgery residency training. The Behavior aspect of KSB includes use of the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Behavior Tool (ABOSBT) to assess 5 domains of professional behavior. The purpose of this study was to analyze the results of 2 years of ABOSBT assessments and to characterize the low score ratings.Methods:All ABOSBT assessments from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2023, were reviewed totaling 51,678 completed assessments with 48,648 (94%) assessments from a 360° process and 3,030 (6%) as end-of-rotation assessments.Results:Distribution of 51,678 ABOSBT assessments with 258,390 scores demonstrated low scores (disagree or strongly disagree) in 0.9% of assessments. All 5 domains identified low scores in a small percentage at all years-in-training with the greatest number in second- and third-year residents. Comparison of scores from 360° vs. end-of-rotation requests demonstrated a higher percentage of low scores given during the 360° process (p < 0.0001). Three thousand seven hundred seven unique evaluators completed ABOSBT assessments, with attending physicians as the most frequent evaluator type (44%); inpatient nurses assessed residents with low ABOSBT scores more frequently (4.5%) than any other evaluator type. Residents with 2 or more low scores by 2 or more different evaluators were analyzed for each of the 5 domains; low scores were most frequently observed in the reliability domain.Discussion:The ABOSBT was originally validated 5 years ago in 18 programs with 9,892 assessments; this article updates results using the ABOSBT across 95 programs assessing 2,397 residents with 3,707 evaluators. The ABOSBT demonstrates that most residents demonstrate professional behavior across 5 domains of assessment; the ABOSBT identifies residents with low scores. A strength of KSB is the ability to identify professionalism deficiencies while residents are in training and can focus on individualized educational improvement. Tracking residents with low scores on the ABOSBT assessment over time will help determine its effectiveness in identifying unprofessional behavior.

Introduction

Competency-based medical education requires a framework of domains with clear goals for learners1,2. The American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) serves the public by maintaining and defining the educational standards for Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)-accredited orthopaedic surgery residency programs, and graduates of these programs may then be qualified to sit for the ABOS Part I Written (Computer-Based) Examination. After passing Part I, an orthopaedic surgeon becomes ABOS Board Eligible, and after a minimum of 17 months in the independent practice of operative orthopaedic surgery must pass the ABOS Part II Oral Examination to become ABOS Board Certified. To fulfill the mission of protecting the public through the Board Certification process, the ABOS has partnered with the ACGME, the American Orthopaedic Association Council of Orthopaedic Residency Directors (AOA/CORD), the Association of Residency Coordinators in Orthopaedic Surgery, and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons to establish the Knowledge, Skills, and Behavior (ABOS KSB) program for orthopaedic surgery resident education and training3. The ABOS KSB program focuses on the framework of 3 essential domains for resident education: medical knowledge, patient care skills, and professional behavior and is designed to transition orthopaedic residency education from time-based criteria and program director validation for graduation to criteria that combine time spent in residency with program director evaluation using validated competency-based assessments. Patient care skills have historically been delineated through ACGME milestones and ACGME case logs with surgical case minimums4. Recently, as a part of the movement in GME toward competency-based education, the ABOS developed a validated tool for assessment of resident performance during surgery, the ABOS Surgical Skills Assessment Tool3,5. Professional behavior has historically been viewed as subjective, “fuzzier,” and lacking assessment tools that can decrease the influence of bias and interpersonal conflicts on resident assessment. This lack of clarity of the definition of professionalism and professional behavior has gained attention in the lay press6,7 and the scientific press as a potential explanation for the higher rates of dismissal from residency programs of individuals who belong to groups that are underrepresented in medicine8. By creating a more formalized process, with defined parameters and multiple evaluators, this system attempts to provide greater accountability and objectivity for professionalism assessments.

To further clarify the components of professional behavior, in 2018, the ABOS collaborated with AOA/CORD on the development of the ABOS Professional Behavior Tool (ABOSBT) using securely requested assessments on the same online platform used for the ABOS Surgical Skills Assessment Tool5,9. The ABOSBT assesses 5 domains of professional behavior (Table I) and was validated by 18 participating residency programs with analysis of 9,892 behavior assessments. Based on the results of that pilot program, the ABOS recently approved adoption of a national professional behavior assessment program using both end-of-rotation and annual 360° evaluations of resident performance as part of its KSB program, with required participation starting July 1, 2025, for residents to be able to qualify for ABOS Board Certification processes beginning with the 2026 ABOS Part I Written Examination.

American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Behavior Tool—5 Domains of Professional Behavior

ABOSBT = American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery Behavior Tool.

Additional orthopaedic surgery residency programs have joined the original pilot programs in the ABOS KSB program, and more robust data on the ABOSBT are now available; thus, an update of the results is warranted. Unlike orthopaedic knowledge and surgical skills, which in most instances increase by year-in-training, results of the 2018 pilot study confirmed that most residents score highly on the ABOSBT, regardless of their year in training8. The ABOSBT identifies poor professional behavior in residents or “outlier” behavior compared with orthopaedic surgery resident peers. Identification, particularly while early in residency and still in the training environment, will hopefully allow interventions specific to the areas of deficiency.

The purpose of this study was to analyze the results of 2 years of ABOSBT assessments and to characterize the low score ratings.

Materials and Methods

All ABOSBT assessments from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2023, were reviewed totaling 51,678 completed assessments. The ABOSBT includes 5 domains with descriptors for each domain. For the individual completing the assessment of resident behavior, the assessor chooses whether they “strongly disagree,” “somewhat disagree,” “neutral,” “somewhat agree,” or “strongly agree” that the resident demonstrates the expected level in each domain of professional behavior (Table I).

The methods for assessment collection were through a request using the ABOS KSB platform with professional behavior assessments collected at the end of each residency rotation from faculty on the rotation, as well as 360° assessments collected in October from faculty, orthopaedic fellows, resident peers, and advance practice providers staff. The completed ABOSBT assessments were available for the program's education leadership to review and share with the residency Clinical Competency Committee and the resident as part of their annual review. Unlike the surgical skills tool5, which is available as real-time feedback, the professional behavior tool assessments are collated and blinded to the resident to preserve confidentiality9. If low scores of “strongly disagree” or “somewhat disagree” are recorded, a notification is sent immediately to the program director so that timely investigation and remediation actions can be pursued. For purposes of this study, all scores were deidentified before analysis.

Results of assessments were compared using a 360° process vs. an end-of-rotation process. The 360° evaluator type includes individuals submitted by the program, such as attending surgeons, orthopaedic fellows, resident peers, physician assistants, outpatient staff, operating room nurses, nurse practitioners, inpatient nurses, emergency department faculty, and other individuals identified by the program to be important for a 360° professional behavior assessment of the residents in their program. Residents are also able to place individuals on the 360° evaluation assessment list. The 360° evaluations are “pushed” by email to the evaluators. The 360° evaluators view a list of all residents in their program with resident pictures and choose those residents with whom they have worked sufficiently to complete a professional behavior assessment. Conversely, the end-of-rotation evaluations are initiated by the residents, who choose the faculty members with whom they have worked, to request assessment of their professional behavior during that rotation. Results of the assessments were compared by year in training and analyzed for each of the 5 domains for the entire group.

Results of the assessments were also analyzed for each individual resident. Residents were categorized as having “outlier” behavior assessments if they received (1) at least 7 assessments during that calendar year and (2) a “strongly disagree” or “somewhat disagree” rating by at least 2 different evaluators. This algorithm was established based on follow-up from our initial validation study9. The study design used these inclusion criteria to identify patterns of unprofessional behavior. Further analysis of outlier assessments included resident year-in-training and frequency of low-score domains. Descriptive statistics are presented. The χ2 test was used for contingency table analysis with p < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant, adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

Results

Analysis of Evaluations

Between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023, 2,397 residents were evaluated using the ABOSBT. Ninety-five residency programs used the behavioral skills assessment tool with the average number of completed assessments per program of 544 (range: 1-1,974). Of all the completed ABOSBT assessments, 25.2% evaluated residents in postgraduate year 5 (PGY5), 23.7% in PGY4, 21.8% in PGY3, 18.8% in PGY2, and 10.5% in PGY1. Residents in PGY1 had the lowest number of assessments in part because they completed only 6 months of orthopaedic surgery training during that academic year.

Overall, 51,678 assessments were analyzed with 258,390 scores across 5 domains. Results of ABOSBT assessment scores by domain are shown in Figure 1, confirming that most assessments reflect expected professional behavior irrespective of domain.

Discussion

Because the ABOSBT is an important component of the ABOS KSB program, this study was undertaken as a follow-up to the 2018 pilot study, to review the effectiveness of the ABOSBT across a broader range of programs with greater numbers of evaluators and residents. Competency-based medical education requires frequent and ongoing assessments of clinical performance to assess progress within the defined constructs of knowledge, patient care skills, and professional behavior. Although assessment of professionalism has often been felt to be subjective and “fuzzy,” this study demonstrates that professionalism can effectively be categorized and assessed in 5 domains with identification of “outlier” behavior. Professional behavior has been effectively assessed using the 5 domains: adherence to ethical principles, effective communication with patients/families, effective interactions with others in the health care system, reliability, and commitment to autonomous improvement in competence with ability to self-assess.

As unprofessional behavior can lead to patient harm and poor health outcomes10, the ABOS has an obligation to assess professional behavior and confirm orthopaedic surgeons practice in an ethical, professional manner to meet the mission of serving the public by certifying individuals as capable of the safe, independent, competent, and professional practice of orthopaedic surgery. This obligation necessitates defining professional behavior and developing tools to identify unprofessional behavior. In the residency environment, the responsibility of the educational program leadership and the faculty is to monitor resident performance and behavior to ensure the delivery of safe, ethical care to patients. ABOS developed the ABOSBT to assist program leaders and faculty in this critical job and to identify individuals exhibiting unprofessional behavior while they are in a supervised environment, with supportive educators available, to address identified professional behavior issues and offer opportunities for improvement. In addition, the 5-domain construct of this assessment tool allows for program directors to better identify focused areas for improvement for individualized resident professional development opportunities. All 5 domains identified deficiencies in a small percentage across all levels of residents; however, the domains of effective interaction with other people in the health care system, reliability, and autonomous improvement with ability for self-assessment showed higher overall deficiencies (Table IX). It would be prudent to consider new professional educational development training opportunities that focus initially on these 3 domains to have a greater potential early impact on the profession of orthopaedics.

Physicians are granted certain privileges as professionals and are parties to a “social contract” with society11. One requirement of this social contract is the obligation for physicians to “police ourselves,” including monitoring of professional conduct. Peer review is recognized as an important assessment methodology in professions and is widely used in medicine for certification, licensing, and credentialing12,13. The ABOS has determined that peer review is important for assessing professional behavior by residents, thus the inclusion of resident peers during the annual 360° evaluation. As modern medicine involves working in multidisciplinary teams for the best care of both straightforward and complex surgical patients, inclusion of staff, such as nurses, advanced practice providers, and clinic and operating room staff, is also viewed as essential14,15. The finding that inpatient nurses gave the highest number of assessments with low scores deserves further study and perhaps attention by residency education leaders regarding communication protocols and policies between residents and nursing staff in this high-stress environment where patient safety concerns are heightened. As hoped for, the results of this study demonstrate that a very small percentage of orthopaedic residents (12-48 residents, 0.9%-2.9%) were identified as exhibiting “outlier” unprofessional behaviors as assessed by at least 2 different evaluators in the same domain each year (Table VII). As participation in the KSB program becomes mandatory for taking Part 1 ABOS Board Examination starting July 1, 2025, all orthopaedic residents will be assessed using the ABOSBT; greater information will be available over time with future and ongoing analyses of the data needed.

The ABOS requires residency program directors to certify that an individual graduating from an accredited residency program has completed 60 months of orthopaedic surgery residency and is capable of the competent, independent practice of orthopaedic surgery; such practice requires making decisions and acting in a professional manner. Historically, this judgment has been made by the program director based on performance of the resident over a 5-year residency, comments and evaluations completed by faculty, and often a “gestalt” about the ethics, values, and communication skills of the resident. The ABOSBT provides program directors with contemporaneous and annual data about professional behavior in a validated, easy-to-use online format that hopefully will make determinations of an individual's readiness for independent practice from the standpoint of professional behavior data-driven and valid. In addition, although not studied and validated in residency populations other than orthopaedic surgery, the ABOSBT does not contain assessments specific to the practice of orthopaedic surgery and may be useful in other GME environments.

In conclusion, the ABOSBT, 5 years after being validated and with a larger cohort, continues to demonstrate that most orthopaedic residents demonstrate professional behavior across 5 domains of assessment. In addition, the ABOSBT continues to demonstrate its ability to identify residents with “outlier” assessment scores, indicating lack of expected professional behavior. The 360° assessment, which includes assessments from resident peers and other medical professionals, remains an important component of this assessment program. A strength of this assessment program is the ability to identify individualized domain-specific educational improvement opportunities. Longitudinal tracking of residents who demonstrate unprofessional behavior during training is planned to monitor further professional difficulties in passing Part I, Part II, or maintenance of ABOS Certification. There is an opportunity for broader application of this assessment program for other GME specialties.
