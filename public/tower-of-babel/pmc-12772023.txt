Multi-omics analysis reveals the role of tumor-infiltrating CD4+CCR7+ T cells in EGFR antibody resistance and prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Zizhong Yang, Lupeng Qiu, Guhe Jia, Zhuoya Sun, Yixin Gong, Yin Chen, Yu Wang, Lai Song, Xiao Zhao, Shunchang Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the crucial involvement of the EGFR pathway in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the clinical efficacy of EGFR antibodies in HCC remains uncertain. While existing evidence suggests that immune dysfunction and tumor microenvironment alterations may contribute to treatment resistance, the precise markers underlying this phenomenon in HCC warrant further investigation.MethodsIn this study, we employed patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models generated from 14 HCC patients enrolled in the REHOPE301 cohort to evaluate the sensitivity to nimotuzumab, a humanized anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA) were performed on tumor tissues and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) to elucidate the association between TIL characteristics and EGFR antibody response. In addition, immunofluorescence (IF) staining and flow cytometry were used to validate the findings from scRNA. A predictive risk score and nomogram were subsequently developed using LASSO regression analysis. The prognostic performance of this model was evaluated using 2 external datasets (ICGC-JP and GSE141202) through receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves and calibration curves analyses.ResultsNimotuzumab demonstrated a 50% response rate (7/14) in PDX models. Immune profiling revealed distinct TIL patterns between responders and non-responders. Notably, CD4+CCR7+ T cells were significantly enriched in resistant tumors (p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with the nimotuzumab response (r = -0.767 p = 0.02). IF analysis revealed a higher proportion of CD4+CCR7+ double-positive T cells in the non-responder group compared to responders (p = 0.012). In non-responsive tumors, CD4+CCR7+ T cells exhibited interactions with of macrophages and CD8+PDCD1+ T subsets. The proportion of CD4+CCR7+ T showed negative correlations with active CD8 T infiltrations. A reduced infiltration of CD4+CCR7+ T cells was associated with improved prognosis and enhanced EGFR antibody efficacy across multiple cancer types. Furthermore, a nine-gene signature related to CD4+CCR7+ T cells was identified as a strong prognostic factor in HCC (HR = 5.19, 95% CI: 3.18–8.46, P < 0.001), and was used to construct a nomogram. WES confirmed prognostic gene mutations (VCAN, CAMK4, and CD226) potentially influencing nimotuzumab response.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that elevated infiltration of central memory CD4+CCR7+ T cells in HCC correlates with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which may contribute to impaired efficacy of EGFR-targeted antibodies and worse clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15276-5.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Nimotuzumab, CC-chemokine receptor 7, EGFR-antibody, PDX-model

Background

Primary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], with approximately 529,000 new cases and 483,800 deaths reported annually [2]. HCC patients exhibit limited responsiveness to conventional chemotherapy, resulting in few effective systemic treatment options for patients with advanced-stage disease. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as sorafenib and lenvatinib [3], have demonstrated notable efficacy in HCC management [4]. Among the targets of TKI, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is overexpressed in 68–96% of HCC cases [5]. Although aberrant EGFR signaling is implicated in tumor initiation and progression, clinical trials evaluating various anti-EGFR agents have yielded inconsistent outcomes [6]. Beyond EGFR expression and mutation status [7], multiple additional factors may influence the efficacy of EGFR-targeted therapies and remain to be fully elucidated.

In addition to small-molecule EGFR TKIs, EGFR monoclonal antibodies [8, 9] are commonly used to treat solid tumors with EGFR expression. However, their efficacy in HCC also remains controversial [10]. Emerging evidence indicates that activated T cells [11], natural killer (NK) cells [12], and macrophages [13] may enhance the antitumor effect of EGFR antibodies through mechanisms such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Our previous studies on the EGFR antibody responses across HCC cell lines demonstrated that therapeutic efficacy was more closely associated with extracellular matrix characteristics and Hoshida subclass than EGFR pathway gene expression, suggesting a critical role of tumor microenvironment in modulating the treatment response [14]. Notably, in tumors such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC), EGFR antibodies have also been shown to modulate tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) infiltration [15] and alter intratumoral T cell clonality [16], further highlighting the importance of immune components in shaping treatment efficacy.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, which preserve the cellular heterogeneity of the original tumors, offer a valuable platform for predicting drug responses [17]. When combined with single-cell sequencing, these models enable detailed analysis of micro-environmental and molecular features. Prior studies have shown that inhibiting the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs) can enhance the efficacy of EGFR antibodies in PDX models [18, 19]. However, such investigations remain limited in HCC.

Nimotuzumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR, has been approved for the treatment of HNSCC and pancreatic cancer [20] in several countries [21]. Previous studies have also suggested potential therapeutic benefits of nimotuzumab in patients with HCC [14, 22]. According to Garrido et al.‘s research, nimotuzumab had an intermediate binding affinity for EGFR, enabling preferential accumulation in EGFR-overexpressing tissues [23, 24], thereby supporting its favorable safety profile and enabling us to investigate the mechanisms of EGFR antibody action in the context of HCC.

In this study, we sought to explore the responsiveness to anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies in HCC using PDX models. By integrating single-cell sequencing, we profiled the TIL composition and its association with nimotuzumab sensitivity [25]. Additionally, our findings were validated using through flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining of HCC tissues, together external datasets involving other anti-EGFR antibodies. Bioinformatics analysis revealed a negative association between CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cell infiltration and response to EGFR-targeted therapy. Based on genes associated with these cells, we developed a clinical predictive model, which was further supported by whole-exome sequencing (WES). Overall, this study provides new insights into the immune-mediated mechanisms underlying anti-EGFR resistance in HCC and offers potential avenues to optimize therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Data and sample collection

This study utilized patient specimens from the REHOPE301 cohort [26, 27], a prospective investigation designed to evaluate TIL characteristics in HCC via multi-omics sequencing and assess their prognostic relevance. HCC diagnoses were pathological confirmed and no enrolled patients received chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery. Details clinical characteristics and study design are illustrated in Fig. S1A. Following hepatectomy, a portion of tumor tissue was processed for pathological analysis, while the remaining samples were dissociated for downstream PDX model establishment and nimotuzumab sensitivity testing.

Tissue dissociation and PDX drug-sensitivity assay

To overcome the limitation of inadequate tumor tissue for traditional PDX models, we implemented hollow fiber capsule-based implantation (adapted from Wen et al. [28]) to achieve higher success rates in model generation and drug sensitivity assays. Fresh tumor samples were rinsed with Hank’s balanced salt solution to remove necrotic and non-solid material. Dissociation was performed using the gentleMACS Human Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), followed by centrifugation and filtration to eliminate blood and debris. Cellmax hollow-fiber cartridges (Spectrum, Netherlands) were sterilized after soaking in absolute ethanol for 30 min and rinsing with ultrapure water [29]. A tumor-cell suspension (2 × 105 cells/mL) was loaded into 10-mm fiber segments, which were heat-sealed at both ends. Three segments were implanted subcutaneously into each 6-week-old female NPG mouse (Charles River, China) to establish HCC PDX models. Prior to implantation, animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), and satisfactory anesthesia was achieved. After 24 h, successfully engrafted mice were randomly assigned to the treatment (nimotuzumab, 2.5 mg/mL; 20 mg/kg, i.v., Q4D; Biotech Pharma, China), or control (saline) group. On day 7, the fibers were removed from mice, the encapsulated cells were flushed out with saline, enriched using the Human Tumor Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany). Due to the encapsulation of tumors within fibrous tubes, direct comparison of mass/volume changes was impractical. Therefore, we assessed tumor cell viability by measuring ATP levels with the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, USA). Tumor growth inhibition (TGI) was calculated as .

CD45+TILs sorting and flow cytometry test

Remaining tumor were processed for CD45+ TIL isolation and analysis as previously described [26]. Briefly, cells were magnetically sorted using anti-human CD45 microbeads (130-118-780, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). After CD45⁺ cell enrichment, the cells were stained following the panel (Supplementary Table 1) and analyzed on an LSRFortessa (BD biosciences, USA) flow cytometry to confirm the sorting efficiency and quantify major lymphocyte subsets.

Single-cell sequencing and data processing

TILs were subsequently processed and sequenced using the 10x Chromium System (10x Genomics, USA). Raw fastq files were processed using Cell Ranger (version 7.1.0). Subsequent analyses were conducted with the “Seurat” R package (version 4.2.3). During quality control, genes expressed in > 3 cells and cells expressing > 200 genes were retained. Cells with > 20% mitochondrial or > 0.1% hemoglobin gene expression were excluded. Potential doublets were removed using “DoubletFinder” (version 2.0.4) [30]. The remaining cells underwent normalization, scaling, variable gene selection, principal component analysis (PCA), batch effect correction using “Harmony” (version 1.2.0), and clustering (dims = 30, resolution = 0.5). Cell clusters were annotated using canonical markers and top-ranked differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (adjusted p-values < 0.05 and log2FC > 1). A small fraction of non-immune cells included during magnetic bead–based CD45⁺ enrichment was also identified during clustering and excluded in most downstream annotations. Cell cycle states were inferred using “CellCycleScoring”.

T and NK cell subsets were further characterized through subclustering following the aforementioned pipeline. To assess their tissue distribution preferences between nimotuzumab-responsive (R) and non-responsive (NR) groups, odds ratios (ORs) were calculated as previously described by Zhang et al. [31]. DEGs within each subcluster between R and NR patients were identified using the “FindMarkers” and functional enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed with “clusterProfiler” (version 3.21). Additionally, to delineate the functional states of immune cells, we applied the “AddModuleScore” function to quantify 19 immune-related gene signatures defined by Chu et al. [32]. and compared scores between R and NR groups.

Analysis of cell-cell communication

Intercellular communication among TIL subclusters was inferred using the “CellChat” R package (version 1.5.0). Non-immune clusters (e.g., “Hepatocyte/Cancer Cell”, “Proliferating Cell”, “Endothelial Cell”, “Fibroblast”) were excluded to avoid bias. CellChat objects were grouped by treatment response, and the “rankNet” function was used to compare ligand–receptor interactions and signaling pathways between groups [33].

Immune infiltration and treatment correlation analyses

We used the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to quantify the enriching levels and relative abundances of immunocytes in samples from different HCC datasets using R package “GSVA” (version 1.48.3). The samples were stratified into high- and low- infiltration groups based on the median infiltration score. In addition, tumor microenvironment characteristics were assessed using CIBERSORT (version 0.1.0) or ESTIMATE (version 1.48.3) packages.

Prognostic gene identification and nomogram construction

Using TCGA-LIHC as the training dataset, we developed a prognostic risk score model and validated it in two independent HCC cohorts (ICGC-JP and GSE141202) [34]. DEGs were first identified between tumor and normal tissues within the TCGA-LIHC cohort. DEGs between tumor and normal tissues (|log₂FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05) were overlapped with CD4+CCR7+ T cell-related genes (adjusted p < 0.05, |correlation coefficient| > 0.4). Prognostic genes were identified via univariate Cox regression and refined using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method [35]. The risk scores were calculated via the following formula: (βi: regression coefficient; Expi: gene expression level). The predictive performance of the risk score was evaluated through Kaplan–Meier and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses across both the training and validation cohorts.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted incorporating the risk score and clinical parameters. Variables with p < 0.05 in multivariate analysis were used to construct a nomogram using the “rms” R package (version 6.4.0). Predictive accuracy was assessed using ROC curves and calibration plots in both the training and validation sets.

WES analysis

To confirm the mutations status of prognosis-related genes. DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) HCC tissues using the Maxwell 16 FFPE Plus LEV DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA) and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA).

After sequencing, quality control of the raw FASTQ files was preformed using “FastQC” (version 0.12.1). The sequence data were processed according to “GATK” (version 3.5) best practices. Somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels were detected using MuTect2, filtered via “VCFtools” (version 0.1.16) and annotated using “SnpEff” (version 5.1f). Mutation profiles were visualized using the R package “maftools” (version 2.16.0) [33].

Multicolor immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded HCC tissue sections as previously described [36]. Briefly, 4-µm sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking, sections were sequentially incubated with CD4 (AFRM0053 AiFang Bio, China), CCR7 (ab253187 Abcam, England) antibodies, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and TSA fluorophores (TYR-520, TYR-570). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were scanned using a KF-PRO multi-channel fluorescence scanner (KFBio, China). ImageJ was used for fluorescence quantification the number of CD4/CCR7 positive cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on data distribution. One-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Pearson and Spearman correlations were applied for continuous and ordinal variables, respectively. Survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test and visualized with Kaplan–Meier plots. All analyses were conducted in R software, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

TIL landscape in PDX models stratified by nimotuzumab response

We successfully established PDX models from 14 patients in the REHOPE cohort, with TIL single-cell RNA sequencing conducted on 10 of TILs these patients (Fig. 1A, S1A). In the PDX drug sensitivity assay, 7 samples exhibited reduced tumor cell viability following nimotuzumab treatment and were classified as responders, with maximum inhibition rate of 71.46%. The remaining 7 samples showed no significant response and were classified as non-responders, resulting in an overall nimotuzumab response rate of 50% (Fig. 1B, S1B). Baseline characteristics, including age and BMI, were comparable between responders and non-responders (Table 1). Notably, patients 001, 017, and 026 demonstrated over a two-fold increase in tumor cell viability post-treatment, indicating strong resistance to nimotuzumab.

Based on PDX response, scRNA-seq samples were stratified into two groups. After quality control, 86,828 single cells were retained. UMAP-based clustering identified major immune subsets (Fig. 1C, S1C) including lymphoid-derived: T cells, NK/NKT cells, dendritic cells (DCs), B cells, and plasma cells, as well as macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and neutrophils. The macrophages were further subdivided into three subsets: monocyte-derived macrophages (Mφ-Monocytes), liver-resident Kupffer cells (Mφ-Kupffer), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). A proliferating cell cluster characterized by high expression of cell cycle–related genes were identified (Fig. 1D). Despite CD45+ enrichment, non-immune cells—such as hepatocytes/HCC tumor cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells—were detected, primarily from non-responders (Fig. 1E-F), suggesting a potentially higher proportion of non-immune components in these tumors. Although the relative abundance of TIL subsets varied across samples, T cells, B cells, NK cells, and neutrophils were generally enriched in responder samples, indicating a greater immune infiltration (Fig. 1G). PCA-based unsupervised clustering stratified the samples into three distinct groups (Fig. 1H), suggesting that single-cell profiles can differentiate between response phenotypes. Of note, patients with mild resistance (011, 015, 016) and those with marked resistance (017, 026) were located distantly from each other in PCA space.

Distinct T and NK cell features between different response groups

Given the critical role of T and NK (TNK) cells in antitumor immunity, we performed subclustering of TNK populations. This analysis identified CD4⁺, CD8⁺, mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT), and three NK subsets, along with smaller populations of proliferating T cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and mast-like cells (Fig. 2A, E). Unlike the TIL composition, TNK subsets displayed more consistent distribution across samples but showed marked differences between response groups (Fig. 2B-C). Tissue preference analysis [31] revealed that activated T and NK subsets were predominantly enriched in responder samples (Fig. 2D), indicative of enhanced immune activation. Conversely, non-responders showed enrichment of the ILCs (in minimal numbers), CD4-FOXP3 (Tregs), and CD4-CCR7 (Central Memory T cell, Tcm) subsets. Among these, only the CD4+CCR7+ T proportion was significantly negatively correlated with nimotuzumab response (Fig. 2F), while CD4+FOXP3+ T cells were not (Fig. 2G)

To further validate our findings, we performed immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry on HCC tissues and TILs. Immunofluorescence results showed that the number of CD4⁺CCR7⁺ cells was increased in the tumor tissues of non-responder samples (Fig. 2H-I). Flow cytometry indicated that the proportion of CCR7⁺ cells in CD4⁺ T cells was higher in non-responders (Fig. 2J-L), whereas the total CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell counts did not differ significantly between responders and non-responders (Fig. S2). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in adjacent tissues between the two groups (Fig. 2K). These results provide additional protein-level evidence supporting that CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cells elevated in nimotuzumab non-responders.

Transcriptional and functional profiling reveals elevated TNK activation in responders

Differential gene expression analysis of TNK subsets (Fig. 3A) revealed three notable features: (1) cells from the responses exhibited high levels of activation markers (CD69, FOS, TOX) and effector genes (GZMK, CCL4, NKG7); (2) cells from non-response exhibited upregulation of stress-related proteins (HSP90AB1, DNAJB1, BAG3), suggestive of a stress/damage phenotype (Fig. 3B); and (3) certain T cell subsets (e.g., CD4-CCL5, CD8-PDCD1) expressed clonally enriched TCR VDJ genes (e.g., TRAV13-1, TRBV5-6), suggesting clonal expansion of specific T cell populations. Further analysis of the CD4+CCR7+ T subset showed higher enrichment in cytokine production, metabolic activity, and TCR signaling scores among responders (Fig. 3C-D), suggesting increased functional activation in this population.

Enhanced intercellular communication of CCR7+CD4+ T cells in non-responders

To investigate cell–cell communication among TILs, we applied CellChat. While the overall number of interactions was similar between groups, interaction strength was markedly higher in non-responders, especially among the CD4-CCR7, Mφ-Monocyte, and CD8-PDCD1 subsets (Fig. 4A and C). Mφ-Monocytes consistently showed the strongest interactions across groups, whereas CD4-CCR7 T cells exhibited strong interactions specifically in non-responders (Fig. 4B). Input-output (IO) analysis confirmed elevated signaling activity in CD4-CCR7 and CD8-PDCD1 subsets in the non-responsive group that surpassed the average levels observed in the other clusters (Fig. 4D).

Comparison of ligand–receptor interactions (Fig. 4E) revealed enrichment of inflammatory (TNF, Chemerin [37]) and immunosuppressive pathways (CD30, IL10 [38]) in non-responders (Fig. 4F), whereas responder samples were enriched in cytokine- and immune activation–related pathways, including CCL, CSF, and CLEC [39] signaling (Fig. 4G). Notably, in the CellChat signaling networks like CD45, although certain signaling ligand–receptor interactions were enriched in both responders and non-responders, the major cell populations involved in these pathways differed between the two groups (Fig. S3A-B).

Based on the CellChat results, we sought to further validate whether CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cells are correlated with other immune cell subsets. We utilized the flow cytometry assays of CD45+ TILs prior to single-cell sequencing to assess the relationship between the proportion of CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cells and two common markers of CD8⁺ T cells, CD69(activation) and CD28(co-sitimulatory). The results showed that the CCR7⁺ rate within CD4⁺ T cells was significantly negatively correlated with the proportion of CD69⁺ or CD28⁺ cells within the CD8⁺ subset (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, using the CIBERSORT algorithm and the TCGA-LIHC dataset, we compared the immune cell composition between high and low infiltration groups of CD4 memory resting cells (the main component of CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cells). The analysis revealed that M0 macrophages, activated memory CD4⁺ T cells, naïve CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and follicular helper T cells were significantly increased in the low CD4 memory resting infiltration group (Fig. 4I). In contrast, there was no significant difference in immune cell composition between the two groups in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. S3C). These findings suggest that elevated CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T cells, are associated with impaired immune infiltration within the tumor microenvironment.

Associations between CD4+CCR7+ T cells and EGFR antibodies response

To investigate the association between CD4+CCR7+ T cells and treatment response across different anti-EGFR regimens and tumor types, we performed ssGSEA using CD4+CCR7+ T-cell marker genes across the TCGA and GEO datasets. In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, tumor tissues exhibited significantly lower CD4+CCR7+ T-cell scores compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5A), and higher infiltration of these cells was negatively correlated with patient survival (Fig. 5B).

We next analyzed GSE102995 [40] dataset, which includes clinical data from panitumumab-treated patients. CD4+CCR7+ T cell scores were significantly negatively associated with progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 5C). To further assess whether these cells fluctuate during treatment, we examined GSE180480, which contains course gene expression data from patient-derived organoids. Compared to the baseline, CD4+CCR7+ T-cell scores significantly decreased at the first emergence of drug resistance, suggesting reduced infiltration during treatment response (Fig. 5D).

A similar result was found in the GSE108277 [41] dataset, which includes a PDX model treated with cetuximab, samples exhibiting partial responses showed significantly lower CD4+CCR7+ T cell scores than non-responders did (Fig. 5E). We further investigated whether these associations extended to other TKI. In the GSE109211 dataset [42], which studied sorafenib treatment in HCC, responders had significantly lower CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T-cell scores than non-responders (Fig. 5F), with the difference more pronounced than that seen in EGFR antibody datasets.

Construction of a CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated risk signature

To evaluate the prognostic value of CD4⁺CCR7⁺ T-cell infiltration in HCC, we developed a risk score based on gene expression associated with these cells. Differential expression analysis between 371 tumor and 51 normal samples in the TCGA-LIHC cohort identified 13,581 DEGs (Fig. 6A), comprising 1,354 upregulated and 11,227 downregulated genes. Among these, 410 were significant associations with the CD4+CCR7+ T cell cluster (Fig. 6B). Univariate Cox regression identified 43 prognostically relevant genes among them, with 37 associated with increased risk and 6 with protective effects.

Functional enrichment analysis of these 410 genes revealed significant involvement of GO terms including cell motility, angiogenesis, membrane structures and antigen binding (Fig. 6C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed associations with cytokine signaling, TNF signaling, the PI3K-Akt pathway, cytoskeletal regulation, and specific infection-related pathways, consistent with the KEGG pathways identified from single-cell CD4+CCR7+ T cell cluster DEGs (Fig. 3D).

We used LASSO regression to refine the model, selecting 9 key genes at log(λ) = − 0.31 (Fig. 6D-E), including four risk-associated genes (N4BP3, UAP1L1, MTHFD1L, MSC) and five protective genes (LTK, PGF, CAMK4, SPRED3, TNFRSF18). The final multivariate model was constructed using stepwise selection, resulting in the following risk score formula (Fig. 6F):

RISK Score = 1.4 Χ N4BP3 + 0.096 Χ UAP1L1 + 0.056 Χ MTHFD1L + 0.032 Χ MSC − 0.85 Χ SPRED3 − 0.22 Χ CAMK4 − 0.12 Χ LTK − 0.045 Χ PGF − 0.005 Χ TNFRSF18

Risk scores were calculated for each TCGA sample, and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. Survival analysis revealed significantly poorer survival outcomes in the high-risk group (Fig. 6G and I), with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.705, 0.700, and 0.730 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 6H). External validation using the ICGC-JP (Fig. 6J) and GSE141202 (Fig. 6K) cohorts confirmed the model’s predictive power, as high-risk patients consistently had poorer survival outcomes.

Construction and validation of a prognostic nomogram

To determine whether the CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor, we conducted both univariate (Fig. 7A) and multivariate (Fig. 7B) Cox regression analyses incorporating baseline characteristics sex, age, clinical stage, hepatitis B (HBV) infection, alcohol consumption, and tumor grade. HBV status, clinical stage, and the risk score were identified as independent predictors of overall HCC survival.

A prognostic nomogram integrating these variables was developed to predict overall survival (Fig. 7C). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly worse outcomes in the nomogram high-risk group (Fig. 7D). Calibration curves and ROC analysis indicated strong predictive performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.669 to 0.793 (Fig. 7E-F). The nomogram was further validated in the GSE141202 dataset, showing comparable AUCs (0.685–0.824) for 1- to 3-year survival predictions (Fig. 7G–H), supporting its robustness.

Association between the CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated risk score and immune infiltration

To investigate the immunological context of the risk score, we examined its correlation with immune infiltration in the GSE196576 dataset [43]. The CD4+CCR7+ T level were positively correlated with the expression of the 9-gene risk signature (Fig. 8A). Using the MCPcounter algorithm, we confirmed that higher risk scores were associated with increased CD4+CCR7+ T-cell infiltration in both the TCGA (Fig. 8B) and GSE196576 (Fig. 8C) cohorts. We next applied the ESTIMATE algorithm to assess stromal and immune infiltration. In GSE196576, the low-risk group had significantly higher ESTIMATE scores (p = 0.001; Fig. 8D), stromal scores (p = 0.003; Fig. 8E), and immune scores (p = 0.003; Fig. 8F), indicating a greater non-tumor and immune component in the tumor microenvironment. These findings suggest that patients with lower risk scores have higher immune cell infiltration.

WES analyses and validation of the CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated prognostic genes

To validate the presence of genetic alterations in CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated risk genes, we performed WES on 13 tumor samples corresponding to the PDX models. While no significant differences in variant types or SNV classes were observed between responders and non-responders, the non-responders exhibited a higher median mutation burden (Fig. 9A-B). We subsequently examined genetic mutations within the 43 risk signature genes. Among these genes, three (VCAN, CD226, and CAMK4) were mutated exclusively in non-responders (Fig. 9C), while seven genes (STRA6, NTM, GLP2R, ADAM12, CTHRC1, HTRA3, and SCG2) were mutated only in responders (Fig. 9D), suggesting distinct mutational profiles between the two groups. Notably, VCAN mutations were present in 60% (3/5) of non-responders (Fig. 9E). Survival analysis in the TCGA-LIHC cohort revealed that patients with VCAN mutations had significantly poorer survival (p = 0.015; Fig. 9F). All three VCAN-mutant patients harbored SNVs at distinct sites (Fig. 9G). In summary, our WES result confirmed the presence of mutations in CD4+CCR7+ T cell-related risk genes, with distinct mutational patterns between responders and non-responders to nimotuzumab.

Discussion

As a pivotal receptor orchestrating various proliferative and survival pathways, EGFR is associated with aggressive tumor behavior, metastasis, and poor patient survival. However, clinical trials evaluating TKI monotherapy in advanced HCC patients have not demonstrated clinical benefits. Recent trials focusing on combining EGFR antibodies with chemotherapy [44], ICIs [11, 45] or mTOR inhibitors [46], had shown promising results. Unlike small-molecule TKIs, EGFR antibodies also exert antitumor effects through ADCC and immune modulation [47]. Therefore, their efficacy is influenced by the infiltration and functional states of immune cells in the HCC microenvironment.

In this study, the PDX model demonstrated that nimotuzumab achieved a 50% response rate in HCC samples, outperforming our previous in vitro studies using HCC cell lines and clinical trials involving cetuximab. Moreover, the scRNA-seq analysis revealed elevated neutrophil and B-cell infiltration [48, 49], whereas T cells, NK cells, and macrophages remained relatively unchanged. These findings suggest that general variations in these immune subsets may not directly determine nimotuzumab efficacy. PCA further revealed distinct transcriptional patterns in highly resistant samples, indicating the presence of multiple resistance mechanisms.

Previous studies have demonstrated that EGFR antibodies exert antitumor effects through T- and NK cell–mediated mechanisms, particularly via ADCC [12, 50]. Compared with the overall TIL distribution, T and NK cells showed relatively uniform abundance across samples, while effector clusters such as NK-CCL3, CD4-GZMB, CD4-CCL5, and MAIT-GZMK were enriched in responders, suggesting that immune activation contributes to the efficacy of EGFR antibodies [51]. In contrast, non-responders were enriched in CD4+CCR7+ T cells, ILCs, and Tregs. Although Tregs have been reported to impair EGFR antibody efficacy [52], the roles of ILCs and CD4+CCR7+ T cells remain poorly defined. Only the number of CD4+CCR7+ subsets was directly and inversely correlated with nimotuzumab response. Further immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses supported this finding, suggesting that CD4+CCR7+ T cells represent a marker of EGFR antibody resistance in HCC, which was reported for the first time.

As a hallmark of central memory T cells, non-responders exhibited not only higher quantities of CD4+CCR7+ T subsets but also distinct functional characteristics. CD4+CCR7+ T cells from responders expressed higher levels of effector and activation markers such as CCL4, NKG7, and IL32, suggesting stronger activation and a higher likelihood of differentiation into effector phenotypes. In contrast, T cells from non-responders expressed higher levels of naïve, stress-response, and anti-apoptotic markers, indicating a dysfunctional or exhausted state that may impair ADCC [53]. Additionally, reduced CD4+CCR7+ T-cell abundance was observed in tumors compared to normal tissues, consistent with previous studies [54]. TCGA CIBERSORT results also indicated that groups with lower CD4-memory resting infiltration showed increased levels of 5 other immune cell subsets. Supporting this, elevated CD4+CCR7+ T-cell levels have been reported in relapsed breast cancer, and positive lymph nodes [55]. Thus, CD4+CCR7+ T-cell accumulation may indicate insufficient immune activation induced by EGFR antibodies.

Based on CellChat results, CD4+CCR7+ T-cell subsets in the NR group exhibited stronger intercellular communication, with significant ligand–receptor interactions differing from those in responders. These findings suggest that CD4+CCR7+ T cells may directly modulate immune function through crosstalk with other cells. In non-responders, communication occurred mainly through CD70–CD27 and TNF–TNFR ligand–receptor pairs between CD4+CCR7+ T cells, macrophages, and exhausted CD8+PDCD1+ subsets. The CD70 pathway has been shown to promote chronic stimulation–induced CD8+ T-cell exhaustion [56], while excessive TNF signaling is linked to inflammation and immune exhaustion. Previous studies have suggested a negative correlation between CD4+CCR7+ central memory T cells and CD8+PDCD1+ TEMRA cells [57]. Limited flow cytometry results showed that CD4+CCR7+ T-cell proportion was negatively correlated with the proportions of CD8+CD69+ (activated) and CD8+CD28+ (co-stimulatory [58]) subsets, further supporting this conclusion. Enhanced IL-10 and complement-related signaling was also detected in NR samples. Macrophages in non-responsive samples exhibited high IL-10 expression, a marker of TAM polarization [59], which has been shown to limit effector CD8+ T-cell proliferation after anti–PD-1 therapy [60]. The complement system in tumors can promote both inflammation and immunosuppression [61]. Furthermore, CCR7 expression itself may be influenced by EGFR family signaling; VEGF-C has been reported to activate lymphangiogenesis and recruit CCR7+ immune cells via the CCL21/CCR7 axis, possibly explaining elevated CCR7 levels. Anti-angiogenic TKI combined with PD-1 blockade markedly suppressed tumor progression [62]. However, our retrospective analysis could only establish correlations between CD4+CCR7+ T cells and immune activity; demonstrating a causal role will require functional studies such as CCR7 overexpression or knockdown experiments, which represent future research directions.

Given the significant difference in CD4+CCR7+ T cells between resistant and sensitive groups and their potential association with immune activation, ssGSEA analysis revealed that lower CD4+CCR7+ T-cell levels were consistently correlated with better outcomes in treatments involving cetuximab, panitumumab, and even sorafenib. This suggests that the relationship between CD4+CCR7+ T cells and EGFR inhibitors is not limited to nimotuzumab. Consequently, we constructed a clinical predictive model based on CD4+CCR7+ T cells and their related genes.

The risk model comprises nine genes: N4BP3, UAP1L1, MTHFD1L, MSC, LTK, PGF, CAMK4, SPRED3, and TNFRSF18. Among them, TNFRSF18 and MTHFD1L have been reported to associate with the immune microenvironment or ICI efficacy in HCC, while SPRED3 and LTK may suppress CD4+ T-cell responses through the MAPK and JAK–STAT pathways. N4BP3 promotes angiogenesis in HCC by binding with KAT2B [63]. Together, these genes reflect immune suppression and tumor adaptation.

Considering the limited feasibility of routine scRNA-seq or immune profiling in clinical settings, we identified CD4+CCR7+-associated genes in tumor tissues to construct a prognostic model. Using LASSO regression, we excluded low-correlation markers and retained 9 genes. In future applications, these genes can be measured using PCR, Nanostring, or bulk-RNA sequencing, thereby reducing implementation cost. To our knowledge, this is the first HCC prognostic framework based on CD4+CCR7+ T cells. Compared with previous models focusing on exhausted CD4+ T cells [64], our model shows superior prognostic performance. Ding et al. developed an AFP (Alpha-fetoprotein)- and TMB (Tumor Mutation Burden)-based model with an AUC of 0.70 (training set) [65], comparable to ours, while Lin et al. reported that AFP alone provided limited predictive value for postoperative recurrence or survival (median time-dependent AUC = 0.572 for OS, 0.587 for DFS) [66]. Notably, Classic prognostic markers like TMB and AFP have been extensively studied in prediction models for HCC recurrence, treatment response, complications, prognosis, and adverse events. Future research could explore integrating CD4+CCR7+ T cell-related gene risk scores with other indicators to expand their clinical applications.

Regarding clinical application, EGFR antibodies are not yet guideline-recommended for HCC and are mainly used in small-scale or exploratory settings, typically as later-line therapy [67]. In such cases, patients often have limited treatment options and may already possess RNA sequencing data. Therefore, our model could help identify patients who may benefit from experimental EGFR antibody therapy. Moreover, since this model is based on CD4+CCR7+ T -related genes, patients classified as high-risk for EGFR resistance might consider combining immunotherapies with EGFR-targeted therapy according to their prior treatment regimens. However, since our cohort did not include patients receiving ICI therapy, whether the combination of EGFR antibodies and ICIs confers clinical benefit in those patients remains to be validated. Future research should integrate other immunotherapy biomarkers (e.g., PD-1 expression, CPS score) with our gene signature to develop models predictive of response to combined immunotherapy.

WES analysis revealed no EGFR mutations in either group, indicating that resistance was not driven by EGFR alterations [7]. Higher TMB has been shown to favor cetuximab response in CRC patients; however, in this study, non-responders exhibited higher median mutation counts [68]. We hypothesize that elevated TMB may indicate increased malignancy or EGFR-independent signaling activation. Among the nine model genes, only CAMK4 was mutated. While CAMK4 inhibition suppresses tumor growth, it also regulates Th17 cell differentiation [69]. Notably, VCAN mutations were detected in 60% of resistant samples but were absent in responders. VCAN encodes an extracellular matrix component implicated in HCC progression and poor prognosis [70] and may influence EGFR via the ADAMTS1–VCAN–EGFR axis, which promote the tumor growth [71]. This study is the first to implicate VCAN mutations in anti-EGFR resistance in HCC.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, the use of fibrotic capsule–based PDX models improved engraftment efficiency from limited tumor tissue [28] but did not allow direct measurement of tumor size and volume as in traditional PDX systems. Future studies will optimize model construction. Second, scRNA-seq was performed only on pre-treatment samples, limiting assessment of dynamic CD4+CCR7+ T-cell changes during therapy. Third, although WES identified distinct mutational patterns in resistance-related genes, their direct roles in prognosis and EGFR resistance require further experimental validation.

Conclusion

Elevated proportions of CD4+CCR7+ T cells in HCC TILs are associated with resistance to nimotuzumab treatment. Increased CD4+CCR7+ T cells were associated with reduced effector immune cell infiltration and impaired immune activation. A prognostic model based on CD4+CCR7+ T cell-associated genes effectively predicts survival risk in HCC patients and inversely correlates with the extent of immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment.
