The influence of hydraulics and geometrical modifications on downstream scour in inclined drop structures
Authors: AmirHossein MohammadNezhad-Aghdam, Mirali Mohammadi, Amir Ghaderi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study experimentally investigates the hydraulic and geometric controls on local scour downstream of inclined drop structures (IDSs), emphasizing downstream face inclination and tailwater depth as key design variables. Twenty-seven physical model tests were conducted with systematically varied slope angles (21.8°, 26.6°, 33.7°), tailwater depths (free-flow, ×1.5, ×2), and multiple flow discharges. Results indicate that slope angle strongly governs scour morphology, steeper configurations concentrate jet momentum, increasing maximum scour depth by up to 32.4%, whereas shallower slopes produce longer scour cavities, with length increases exceeding 36%. Increased tailwater depth consistently mitigated scour, reducing depth and length by up to 16.5% and 14%, respectively, through decoupling of vortex activity from the bed. Temporal analyses showed that over 80% of equilibrium scour depth develops during the initial phase, underscoring the dominance of early-stage hydraulics. Novel multivariate regression models based on dimensionless parameters achieved prediction errors within ± 10% and demonstrated robustness in sensitivity analyses. Collectively, these findings provide quantitative benchmarks for design-oriented and performance-based optimization of IDS configurations, enabling the selection of scour-minimizing and hydraulically efficient designs that balance structural stability, hydraulic efficiency, and sediment transport management.

Introduction

Rivers commonly require structural measures to safely convey water from higher to lower elevations while maintaining channel stability. Grade-control structures (GCSs), also referred to as drop structures, are widely implemented to control longitudinal bed degradation, enhance energy dissipation, and regulate sediment transport in alluvial channels. Over the past decades, various configurations of GCSs have been developed, including vertical drops, sloped drops, baffled drops, pipe drops, stepped structures, and serrated geometries1. Among these alternatives, inclined drop structures (IDSs) have gained increasing attention due to their relatively high discharge capacity, improved compatibility with natural channel slopes, and reduced excavation and construction requirements compared with conventional vertical drops. Despite these advantages, the hydraulic conditions generated downstream of IDSs are often highly energetic and turbulent. Flow issuing from the downstream face typically accelerates into a supercritical jet (Fr > 1), which impinges on the erodible bed and induces intense sediment mobilization and local scour. Early investigations into inclined energy dissipators and drop structures in rivers and canals were conducted by Kindsvater2, Bradley and Peterka3, and later synthesized by Hager4. These studies collectively established that local scour downstream of inclined drops is largely unavoidable and constitutes a critical design concern. However, predicting the magnitude and geometry of scour remains challenging due to the complex interaction among jet trajectory, turbulence structure, sediment properties, and tailwater conditions. Consequently, many existing scour prediction formulas are empirical in nature and often limited to specific structural configurations or flow regimes5.

Extensive experimental and analytical research has examined scour downstream of hydraulic structures with various geometries. Melville and Sutherland6, highlighted the influence of approach flow and sediment characteristics on scour development, while Bormann and Julien7 proposed empirical relationships describing scour geometry downstream of plunging jets. Lenzi et al.8 investigated scour processes in steep streams equipped with grade-control elements, emphasizing the role of flow intensity and channel slope. Subsequent studies further clarified the effects of hydraulic loading, downstream conditions, and sediment properties on equilibrium scour morphology9,10.

In parallel with these structure-oriented investigations, recent studies have revisited jet-induced scour under submerged and free jet conditions, with a particular focus on vertical and circular jet configurations. Guguloth and Pandey11, evaluated the predictive accuracy of commonly used empirical scour-depth equations under submerged vertical jets and demonstrated that scour estimates are highly sensitive to jet submergence and flow parameters. Building on this work, Guguloth et al.12 employed hybrid artificial-intelligence models to predict scour depth beneath submerged circular vertical jets, reporting substantially improved accuracy relative to traditional empirical formulations. Furthermore, Guguloth and Pandey13, presented a comprehensive review of scour processes under different jet conditions, synthesizing recent advances and highlighting the governing roles of jet geometry, turbulence structure, and submergence ratio.

More recent investigations have focused on geometric modifications and the implementation of protective countermeasures to mitigate scour. Chen et al.14 demonstrated that upstream channel slope significantly alters turbulence characteristics and scour evolution, and proposed predictive equations for both transient and equilibrium scour phases. Wang et al.15 showed that downstream slope configurations influence jet impingement behavior and sediment entrainment mechanisms. Additional studies evaluated roughened beds, stepped aprons, and other structural adaptations as effective scour-mitigation strategies16–18. More specialized countermeasure investigations include Kurdistani et al.19, who reported that macro-roughness elements are more effective than aprons in reducing maximum scour depth in block ramps, whereas longer aprons primarily limit scour-hole extent and eroded volume. Bagherzadeh and Mohammadi20, demonstrated that gabion sills can substantially reduce both scour depth and length, with optimal performance depending on relative sill spacing, while increased tailwater depth further suppresses scour development. Similarly, Mohammadnezhad et al.21 showed that increasing apron length and adopting a cutoff wall height of Lc/h = 0.5 effectively minimizes scour downstream of vertical drops under free-flow conditions. Sediment characteristics and structural inclination have also been shown to play important roles in scour formation. Shariati and Hamidi22, reported that finer sediments produce deeper scour holes, and that reducing the structure inclination angle from 90° to 60° leads to deeper but shorter scour cavities. Ben Meftah and Mossa9, experimentally examined sloped grade-control structures and concluded that the influence of downstream face slope becomes significant only when it is smaller than the equilibrium slope associated with vertical-faced structures.

Although the general mechanisms of jet-induced scour and wall-jet erosion have been widely explored, inclined drop structures generate a fundamentally distinct hydraulic configuration. Unlike vertical drops and circular or vertical jet systems examined in most recent jet-scour studies, IDSs produce an inclined jet characterized by both normal and tangential momentum components relative to the bed. This dual-momentum structure modifies shear-stress redistribution, vortex formation, and sediment transport pathways in ways that cannot be directly inferred from classical wall-jet, vertical-drop, or vertical-jet-based scour formulations. Despite the substantial body of research on scour downstream of hydraulic structures, systematic investigations addressing the intrinsic hydraulic behavior of inclined drop structures particularly the coupled influence of downstream face inclination and tailwater submergence under comparable hydraulic conditions remain limited. Existing studies often consider slope angle, discharge intensity, or tailwater depth in isolation, without quantifying their integrated effects on jet momentum redistribution, submergence-controlled energy dissipation, and resulting scour morphology. As a result, generalized predictive relationships explicitly calibrated for inclined geometries are scarce.

Accordingly, the primary objective of the present study is not to redefine the general phenomenon of local scour, but to provide a configuration-specific and systematically quantified assessment of scour processes downstream of inclined drop structures. Through controlled laboratory experiments encompassing 27 test conditions, this study isolates and evaluates the coupled hydraulic and geometric effects of downstream face inclination and tailwater depth within a unified framework. Using dimensionless analysis based on the Buckingham–Π theorem, the study (i) identifies the dominant governing parameters controlling equilibrium scour depth and length, (ii) elucidates the physical linkage between jet trajectory modification and tailwater-mediated turbulence attenuation, and (iii) develops multivariate predictive relationships specifically calibrated for inclined drop structures.

Materials and methods

Experimental facilities and flume setup

A series of laboratory-scale experiments were carried out in an 18 m-long hydraulic flume with a rectangular cross-section of 0.30 m in width and 0.50 m in height. The channel walls were fabricated from 6 mm glass panels, while the bed consisted of 2 mm iron sheets. Water circulation was maintained by two centrifugal pumps, each capable of delivering up to 24 L/s. Flow rate was monitored with an ultrasonic flow meter with an accuracy of ± 1%. To minimize inlet turbulence, a perforated plastic mesh with uniform 1 cm² openings was positioned at the flume entrance23,24. The approaching flow passed through a 2.5 m straight section before ascending a 3 m glass ramp, which ensured a stable approach to the test section. The flow then crossed a weir and entered the inclined grade control structure located at the upstream boundary of the erodible bed. The sediment bed was 15 cm deep, 2 m long, and extended across the full channel width. Downstream water levels were regulated by an adjustable tailgate. Calibrated rulers were mounted along the glass walls to record scour depth, scour length, and sediment deposition height25. For end-of-test measurements, two perforated glass plates (2.5 × 2.5 cm grid) measuring 1 m × 0.4 m each were placed above the sediment bed. The inclined drop structure was fabricated from PVC sheet (length 0.50 m, width 0.30 m, height 0.20 m) and placed inside the flume. Three slope ratios 1:1.5, 1:2, and 1:2.5 (height: length) were tested. The corresponding structure lengths were approximately 35–52.5 cm, 35–70 cm, and 35–87.5 cm, respectively. A schematic of the experimental configuration is shown in Fig. 1.

Bed material and sediment properties

The movable bed consisted of uniform, non‑cohesive sand with a median grain size d50 =2.36 mm and a specific gravity G = 2.69, corresponding to a sediment density ρs = 2610 kg/m³ (water density ρ = 1000 kg/m³). The sediment exhibited a geometric standard deviation of σg = 1.4, indicating a well‑graded material (σg < 1.5), consistent with the criteria proposed by Chiew and Melville26 and Malik et al.27. In addition, a uniformity coefficient Cu=1.3 and a standard deviation of 1.25 satisfied the recommendations of Dey and Raikar28 and Lambe and Whitman29. Following Pagliara et al.30 and Yu et al.31, scale effects on local scour can be neglected for sediment sizes larger than 1.0 mm; therefore, scale distortion was not expected in the present experiments. A 2.0 m‑long sediment bed with a thickness of 0.15 m was placed immediately downstream of the IDS, extending across the full flume width. The maximum observed equilibrium scour depth was 0.135 m and occurred only under the most extreme hydraulic condition. In all experiments, the sediment layer thickness was sufficient to prevent interference from the rigid bed. Visual observations confirmed that no premature flattening of the scour hole occurred, indicating that the selected sediment thickness adequately represented the scour process.

Experimental program and test conditions

Each IDS configuration was tested under three different unit discharges and three tailwater depths, resulting in a total of 27 experimental runs. Unit discharges of 0.023, 0.033, and 0.043 m²/s produced critical depth ratios (yc/h) ranging from 0.253 to 0.386. To examine the effect of downstream submergence, three tailwater conditions were considered: free-flow conditions, 1.5 times the initial depth, and twice the initial depth. All experiments were conducted under clear-water scour conditions. Water temperature was maintained at approximately 20 °C throughout the tests. Each experiment began with gradual filling of the head tank and sediment bed until the IDS was fully submerged32. Once the target discharge was reached, the tailwater depth was adjusted to the prescribed level before data acquisition commenced. Scour evolution was monitored over time using sequential digital photography. At the end of each run, the bed topography was measured using a high-precision laser scanner with a vertical accuracy of ± 1 mm. Measurements were conducted with a grid spacing of 2.5 cm within the scour hole and 5.0 cm in the downstream reach. A summary of all experimental conditions is provided in Table 1.

Measurement accuracy and experimental uncertainty

The primary sources of experimental uncertainty in this study included discharge measurement, tailwater depth control, and bed elevation measurements. Discharge was regulated using a calibrated flow control system, and the associated uncertainty was maintained within ± 1% throughout the experiments. Tailwater depth was controlled using an adjustable downstream gate, with free‑surface fluctuations kept minimal under steady flow conditions. Bed elevation measurements were obtained using a laser scanner with a vertical accuracy of approximately ± 1 mm, which represents the dominant source of uncertainty in the determination of maximum scour depth. Considering the typical magnitude of the measured scour depths, this corresponds to a relative uncertainty of approximately 2–4%. To account for minor bed‑surface irregularities and reading repeatability, repeated scans were conducted at selected test conditions, confirming the consistency of the measured scour profiles. Minor variations in initial bed leveling and experimental duration were minimized through careful preparation, standardized test procedures, and uniform sediment placement prior to each run. The combined effect of these uncertainties remains within the acceptable range for laboratory‑scale scour experiments and does not alter the observed trends in scour depth or length. Therefore, the reported results reliably reflect the hydraulic and geometric influences investigated in this study rather than measurement artifacts.

Dimensional analysis

The initiation and evolution of scour downstream of an Inclined Drop Structure (IDS) are influenced by a combination of hydraulic and geometric factors, each affecting the intensity and spatial distribution of the phenomenon. These factors jointly govern the complex interaction between the impinging jet, the downstream bed material, and the surrounding flow field. The key variables considered in this study are schematically presented in Fig. 2. In Fig. 2, yu denotes the upstream flow depth prior to the structure, yc is the critical flow depth, and yt represents the tailwater depth. The structural height is indicated by h, while the unit discharge per unit width (q) is defined as the total flow rate (Q) divided by the channel width (B). To describe the scour geometry, two main metrics are used: the maximum scour depth (ds, max) and the maximum longitudinal length of the scour hole (ls, max). These parameters provide the primary means to assess scour development under varying hydraulic scenarios.

Experimental design justification

From a scaling perspective, the modeled grade-control structure height (h = 0.20 m) was selected to satisfy hydraulic similarity with typical prototype drop structures having heights on the order of 2–3 m, assuming geometric scale ratios between approximately 1:10 and 1:15. In laboratory modeling of free-surface hydraulic structures, Froude similarity is commonly adopted to preserve the balance between inertial and gravitational forces governing jet impingement and scour processes36,40. This scaling range is widely applied in experimental investigations of local scour downstream of grade-control and drop structures28,41. The structure length and channel width were selected to ensure fully developed approach flow, quasi-two-dimensional hydraulic conditions, and negligible boundary and scale effects. This is supported by the large width-to-sediment size ratio (B/d50 > 130) and the selected sediment characteristics, which fall within recommended ranges for laboratory scour studies26,30. According to Breusers and Raudkivi42, B/d50 ratios larger than 50–100 effectively suppress side‑wall interference in local‑scour experiments. Accordingly, the experimental configuration reproduces the essential hydraulic and sediment-transport mechanisms governing scour downstream of practical grade-control structures, ensuring that the obtained results are relevant for engineering design and field applications. The three downstream face slopes (1:1.5, 1:2, and 1:2.5) were selected to represent commonly adopted geometries in grade control and drop structures, covering a practical range from relatively steep to moderately mild configurations that are frequently used in river training and bed stabilization structures. Similar slope ranges have been adopted in previous experimental studies on drop and block-ramp structures to examine their influence on jet trajectory, energy dissipation, and downstream scour development9,15. The experiments were conducted using nearly uniform non‑cohesive sand to ensure clear‑water scour conditions and to minimize the influence of sediment heterogeneity and armoring on scour development. Such sediment characteristics are commonly used in laboratory studies of local scour to obtain reproducible and hydraulically representative results9,42.

Scaling implications

The experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions guided primarily by Froude similarity, which is the conventional basis for modeling free-surface hydraulic processes dominated by inertial and gravitational forces. The jet trajectory, hydraulic jump behavior, and downstream flow patterns of inclined drop structures (IDSs) are all governed predominantly by Froude dynamics; therefore, geometric, kinematic, and dynamic similarity were maintained through consistent reproduction of the relevant Froude numbers across tests. However, complete similitude between laboratory and field conditions is not achievable because sediment transport processes do not scale linearly under Froude similitude. The particle Reynolds number, Shields parameter, and relative sediment roughness cannot be matched simultaneously in reduced-scale physical models. As a result, sediment entrainment and local scour development may experience scale distortions. Such limitations are inherent to clear-water scour experiments and have been documented in classical scour modeling literature (e.g42,43). In the present study, uniform sediment of fixed diameter was used to eliminate heterogeneity effects and to isolate the intrinsic influence of jet hydraulics. While the physical mechanisms controlling scour beneath IDSs are expected to be preserved, the absolute magnitudes of scour depth and length should be interpreted within the limits of Froude-scaled hydraulic modeling. The predictive equations developed herein therefore represent geometry-specific trends consistent with IDS hydraulics, but their application to full-scale structures should incorporate appropriate engineering safety factors.

Results

Temporal evolution of scour and determination of equilibrium time of IDSs

The progression of scour downstream of hydraulic structures is inherently time-dependent, making accurate estimation of the equilibrium time a critical aspect of experimental and field-based hydraulic research. According to the classification proposed by Ben Meftah and Mossa9, scour development can be divided into three distinct stages. The initial stage is characterized by an abrupt and intense increase in scoured depth, resulting from high bed shear stresses and strong turbulence immediately following the onset of flow. This is followed by an intermediate stage, during which the rate of scour growth declines, and the deepening and elongation of the scour cavity occur more gradually as sediment entrainment and deposition approach a balanced rate. In the final stage, the system reaches a quasi-steady equilibrium, where the erosive action of the jet is counterbalanced by the resistance of the bed, leading to stabilization of both scour depth (ds) and length (ls). Throughout these phases, the velocity at which the jet impacts the bed governed by a combination of hydraulic boundary conditions and IDS geometry44 plays a decisive role in determining the magnitude and spatial extent of bed deformation. For the present investigation, an initial trial duration of 12 h was selected to determine the appropriate test period for all runs. Analysis of recorded measurements and sequential imagery revealed that, in most cases, sediment transport rates had dropped to near-zero value within approximately six hours, with negligible changes in scour hole geometry thereafter. Based on these observations, a standardized test duration of 10 h was adopted for all subsequent experiments. Figure 3 depicts the variation of relative scour depth (d/dsmax) as a function of dimensionless time (t/tu) for three selected experiments (R10, R11, and R12). Across all cases, a consistent relationship was identified between the discharge magnitude and the time required to attain equilibrium conditions. For the experiments with the lowest and highest discharges (q = 0.023 and 0.043 m2/s), about 79% and 84% of the ultimate scour depth developed within the first hour (t/tu≈0.2). By the time dimensionless time reached approximately t/tu≈0.6, nearly 98–99% of the maximum depth had already been established. Afterward, only negligible adjustments in the positioning of sediment grains within the scour hole were noted, with no significant modification in its overall geometry, demonstrating that the process had essentially stabilized. These results indicate that while minor sediment movement can continue, the scour progression tends toward a steady-state form, thereby enabling accurate determination of equilibrium conditions in laboratory-scale hydraulic testing.

In the early stage, sediment grains are primarily lifted and carried in suspension by the energetic jet flow. As the scour cavity grows and transitions into the developed stage, the erosion rate slows and eventually approaches equilibrium, indicating a transformation in sediment transport from a predominantly suspended load to a mixed regime of suspension and bed load. In the case of test R2, where the jet impinges on the bed surface at a steeper incidence angle, the scour depth develops more quickly in proximity to the structure due to the more concentrated momentum transfer of the flow. Conversely, in test R20, the flow travels a longer path over the gentler sloped face before contacting the bed. This extended flow path results in increased energy dissipation and complex shear interactions along the structure’s surface, causing a less intense impact on the bed and slower scour development. Furthermore, the delayed but more gradual scour evolution in R20 suggests that gentler slopes may promote a more distributed erosion pattern, potentially reduce localized stress concentrations and associate structural risks. Further analysis indicates that scour equilibrium is achieved relatively early in R20 at a normalized time of t/tu=0.20, whereas in R2 the scour process remains dynamically evolving beyond t/tu=0.50, showing no clear sign of stabilization within the observed timeframe.

Scour mechanisms downstream of inclined drop structures (IDSs)

When flow passes over an inclined drop structure (IDS) and enters an erodible downstream reach, the resulting scour is controlled by the interaction between jet momentum, turbulence generation, and sediment resistance. As water accelerates along the downstream face, gravitational acceleration and slope-induced shear produce a supercritical flow that issues at the toe as an inclined jet. The erosive capacity of this jet is primarily governed by the unit discharge (q = Q/B) and is commonly expressed by the densiometric Froude number (Frs), which reflects the combined effects of jet velocity, flow depth, and sediment density. Higher Frs values correspond to increased jet momentum and turbulence intensity, resulting in greater sediment entrainment and larger scour dimensions41,45. After entering the downstream reach, the jet impacts the bed at an angle determined by the downstream face inclination (λ), producing both longitudinal and downward velocity components. The downward component increases the bed-normal momentum flux and intensifies local shear stress and pressure fluctuations at the impact point. Consequently, increasing λ tends to concentrate the jet impact and deepen the scour hole, while limiting its downstream extension. This behavior explains the observed depth–length trade-off between maximum scour depth (ds) and scour length (ls). Tailwater depth also exerts an important hydraulic control through the relative submergence ratio (yt/h). Under low yt/h conditions, the jet interacts more directly with the bed and a free hydraulic jump develops farther downstream, allowing a larger fraction of the jet’s kinetic energy to contribute to erosion. Increasing yt/h promotes earlier jet submergence and hydraulic jump formation, which dissipates energy within the water column and reduces the effective erosive capacity of the flow. Accordingly, both ds and ls decrease with increasing tailwater depth. Immediately downstream of the impact region, a recirculation cell forms with upstream-directed near-bed flow and horseshoe-type vortices around the jet footprint. These coherent vortical structures enhance sediment pickup by repeatedly exposing bed particles to fluctuating lift and drag forces. The entrained sediment is transported downstream and deposited as a sediment mound, whose geometry reflects the balance between jet momentum decay and ambient flow resistance. Figure 5 illustrates these mechanisms for a representative test case (q = 0.034 m²/s, downstream slope 1:2, free-tailwater condition), where the jet impact region, recirculation zone, and downstream deposition area can be clearly identified. As scour progresses, ds and ls increase until a dynamic equilibrium is reached, at which sediment removal from the scour hole balances downstream deposition. Overall, scour downstream of IDSs results from the combined influence of jet hydraulics, structural geometry, and tailwater conditions. These mechanisms, consistent with previous jet-scour studies9,41, provide the physical basis for interpreting the observed scour patterns and for predicting erosion downstream of inclined drop structures.

The effect of downstream face inclination on scour cavity development beneath IDSs

Figure 6 depicts longitudinal bed profile variations downstream of inclined drop structures (IDSs) for tests i.e., (R1, R3); (R10, R13) and (R19, R21), representing downstream slope angles of λ = 33.7°, 26.6°, and 21.8°, respectively, under both low and high discharge conditions. The results clearly indicate that altering the downstream face inclination significantly influences scour morphology. Increasing the downstream face inclination enhances the vertical velocity component of the jet upon impact, concentrating energy on a smaller bed area, which intensifies local shear stresses and results in deeper scour holes. Specifically, steeper slopes (higher impact angles) correspond to increased scour cavity depths, whereas gentler slopes reduce cavity depth but cause a downstream elongation of the scour hole. This behavior can be explained by the modification of jet trajectory and momentum distribution as the inclination angle changes. As the downstream face becomes steeper, the jet leaves the structure with a sharper trajectory and a more concentrated momentum core, which increases the intensity of localized bed shear stresses at the impact point. The resulting concentration of kinetic energy enhances the development of horseshoe vortices and downward turbulent bursts, accelerating sediment entrainment and promoting deeper localized scour. On the other hand, decreasing the slope angle reduces the jet’s impact velocity and energy, promoting earlier sediment deposit near the impact zone and shifting erosion downstream. In test R19, representing the mildest slope (λ = 21.8°), the scour depth reached a minimum of 0.073 m, marking a 32.4% decrease compared to the steepest slope case in R1. This decrease is attributed to the reduced jet velocity upon impact, which encourages sediment deposition shortly after entrainment, shifting the scour cavity downstream and extending its length. Quantitatively, the scour hole length increased by 35.68%, from 0.482 m at λ = 33.7° to 0.654 m at λ = 21.8°. Under milder slopes, part of the jet energy is gradually dissipated along the inclined face before reaching the bed, which reduces the intensity of the direct jet impact while allowing turbulent structures to spread horizontally downstream. This redistribution of momentum weakens localized erosion but enlarges the spatial extent of the recirculation zone, resulting in longer yet shallower scour cavities.

Consequently, the interplay between energy dissipation and turbulence generation varies spatially along the scour hole, influencing the balance between erosion depth and length. At higher unit width discharges (q = 0.043 m2/s), the steepest slope (λ = 33.7°) consistently produced the deepest and most extensive scour cavities. However, while reducing the slope angle led to a notable decrease in scour depth, the relative trade-off between decreasing depth and increasing cavity length diminished. For example, in test R21 (λ = 21.8°), scour depth was 18.5% less than that observed in R3 (λ = 33.7°), measuring 0.11 m. These observations highlight that downstream face inclination controls not only the magnitude of scour but also its spatial distribution, indicating that slope selection in IDS design should consider both local structural safety and downstream sediment redistribution.

Figure 7 compares the maximum scour depth and length for varying unit discharges and downstream face inclinations of inclined drop structures (IDSs). The analysis demonstrates that altering the downstream slope angle of IDSs exerts both direct and inverse influences on scour cavity dimensions. Specifically, an increase in the downstream face inclination corresponds to a greater scour depth under identical discharge conditions. For instance, at a unit discharge of q = 0.034 m2/s, the steepest slope (λ = 33.7°) produces a maximum scour depth of 0.124 m, while the shallowest slope (λ = 21.8°) exhibits a 21.77% reduction, with a scour depth of 0.097 m. Conversely, the scour cavity length follows an opposite trend: as the impact angle decreases, the scour hole lengthens significantly. Although differences in scour length remain marginal at lower discharges, they become increasingly pronounced as discharge increases. For example, at q = 0.023 m2/s, the relative difference in scour length between the steepest and shallowest slopes is approximately 18.7%, whereas at q = 0.043 m2/s, this difference narrows to around 22.18%. Overall, steeper downstream face inclinations (e.g., λ = 33.7°) tend to generate deeper scour holes, which may threaten structural stability if not properly managed. However, gentler slopes (e.g., λ = 21.8°) produce longer but shallower scour cavities, potentially reducing localized bed degradation while spreading erosion downstream. Therefore, selecting an intermediate slope angle that balances scour depth and length is recommended to support performance-based design considerations for structural safety and controlled sediment displacement.

The effect of tailwater depth on inclined drop in reducing downstream scour

To better investigate the effect of tailwater depth on reducing the scour rate downstream of inclined drop structures, three different tailwater conditions were created downstream of these structures. Figure 8 illustrates these three conditions, namely free flow, 1.5 times, and 2 times the tailwater depth. As can be seen in Fig. 8, tailwater depth plays an important role in reducing the dimensions of the scour hole by creating a gap between the vortices generated by the incoming jet flow and the sediment bed. From a hydraulic perspective, increasing tailwater depth alters the downstream flow regime by promoting submerged jet conditions and earlier formation of hydraulic jumps, which significantly dissipate the kinetic energy of the incoming flow before it reaches the sediment bed. In Fig. 8(a), the free flow condition produces a larger scour hole compared to Figs. 8(b) and 8©. Moreover, the rate of sediment transport downstream is greater under this condition than in the other two cases. In contrast, Fig. 8© clearly demonstrates the effect of doubling the tailwater depth in reducing the scour hole as well as preventing sediment transport downstream. Higher tailwater levels reduce the effective interaction between jet-induced flow structures and the bed surface by enhancing energy dissipation within the water column before the jet reaches the sediment interface. As the water column becomes deeper, the intensity of near-bed shear stresses diminishes, thereby moderating sediment entrainment. The earlier literature discussing the influence of downstream flow depth on jet impact and scour behavior (e.g46,47), similarly highlights the role of increased submergence in weakening the erosive capacity of the incoming flow.

Recently, Esmaili Varaki et al.48 examined the effect of downstream tailwater depth of blocked drop structures and reported that increased flow depth contributes to a reduction in the downstream scour rate of these structures. Figure 9 presents the relative scour depth for two discharges, q = 0.023 m³/s·m and q = 0.043 m³/s·m, under three different tailwater depth conditions. The results clearly indicate that an increase in tailwater depth significantly reduces the scour depth compared to the free flow condition. The maximum scour depth was consistently observed under free flow, while the shallowest scour holes occurred when the tailwater depth was doubled. For the discharge of q = 0.043 m³/s·m, the maximum scour depth in the free-flow condition was 11% higher than that observed at 1.5 times tailwater depth, and 16.5% higher than at double tailwater depth. A similar pattern was found for the discharge of q = 0.023 m³/s·m, although with smaller differences: less than 10% and 13% when compared to the 1.5 and double tailwater conditions, respectively. These results confirm that increased tailwater depth acts as an effective hydraulic control parameter by attenuating jet momentum and reducing the strength of flow structures responsible for initiating sediment motion. Consequently, increasing tailwater depth not only reduces maximum scour depth but also limits the downstream propagation of sediment transport.

Discussion

The present investigation elucidates how hydraulic loading, downstream face inclination, and tailwater depth collectively govern the temporal evolution and equilibrium morphology of scour downstream of inclined drop structures (IDSs). By systematically varying these parameters and linking experimental observations with predictive modeling, the study provides a physically consistent and quantitatively supported framework for interpreting IDS scour behavior. Unlike classical wall-jet or vertical drop configurations, IDSs generate an inclined jet with simultaneous normal and tangential momentum components, leading to a distinct redistribution of bed shear stress and scour morphology. This distinction underpins the interpretation of the experimental trends discussed below. From a hydraulic standpoint, the inclined jet issuing from the downstream face undergoes rapid diffusion due to turbulent mixing with the surrounding tailwater before impinging on the bed. Following impact, the flow transitions through a reattachment region into a developing wall-jet, within which near-bed shear stress reaches its maximum values and governs the initiation and growth of local scour. Although direct measurements of jet diffusion, reattachment length, and shear stress distribution were beyond the scope of the present experimental program, their qualitative influence is reflected in the observed scour evolution and equilibrium morphology discussed in the following sections.

Temporal characteristics of scour development downstream of IDSs

The temporal analysis demonstrated that scour downstream of inclined drop structures developed rapidly during the early stages of flow. More than 79–84% of the ultimate scour depth occurred within the first hour of the experiment, regardless of discharge. This behavior reflects the high turbulence intensities and elevated bed shear stresses associated with the initial adjustment of the impinging jet before any morphological shielding of the bed takes place. Similar early-stage dominance has been reported by Ben Meftah and Mossa9 for plunging jets and by Pagliara and Palermo41 for stepped and drop structures, indicating that IDSs share comparable turbulence-driven erosion mechanisms. However, the inclined jet trajectory in IDSs accelerates the early concentration of momentum toward the bed, reinforcing the dominance of short-duration peak hydraulic conditions. As the scour cavity deepened, jet energy dissipated within the depression and sediment transport capacity decreased, causing a significant reduction in the rate of scour progression. The observed stabilization of scour at t/tu ≈ 0.6 supports the conclusion that peak scour beneath IDSs is governed primarily by short-duration peak flows rather than prolonged hydrographs, a finding also consistent with earlier work by Dey and Raikar28.

Effects of downstream face inclination on jet impact and scour geometry

The inclination of the downstream face was identified as a key geometric parameter controlling scour morphology. Increasing the slope angle enhanced the vertical momentum component of the jet, intensifying local energy concentration and resulting in deeper but more compact scour cavities. Conversely, milder slopes encouraged longer jet trajectories and a more distributed impact zone, producing elongated but shallower scour profiles. This depth–length trade-off is specific to inclined downstream geometries and cannot be directly inferred from classical wall-jet scour formulations, which typically assume horizontal jet issuance. The trade-off, observed here with up to 32.4% reduction in ds and 36% increase in ls when decreasing the inclination from 33.7° to 21.8°, aligns with the mechanisms described by Pagliara and Palermo50 and Hoffmans and Pilarczyk51. At higher discharges, these geometric effects remained evident but less pronounced due to the overriding influence of flow energy, indicating that structural slope and hydraulic loading interact to shape jet impact and erosion patterns. These results emphasize that selecting the downstream face inclination of IDSs is a balance between limiting maximum scour depth and managing downstream sediment redistribution.

Influence of tailwater depth on energy dissipation and sediment response

Tailwater depth played a clear moderating role in scour development by altering jet diffusion and hydraulic jump characteristics. Increasing yt/h promoted submerged jet behavior and enhanced energy dissipation in the water column before the flow interacted with the bed. As a result, both the intensity of near-bed erosive flow interaction and the capacity for sediment entrainment were reduced. In IDS configurations, tailwater depth not only controls submergence but also modifies the effective jet impact angle, reinforcing its coupled interaction with downstream face inclination. The experiments demonstrated reductions of 10–16% in maximum scour depth and 11–14% in scour length when the tailwater depth was doubled, especially at higher discharges where the hydraulic jump became more stable and effective in dissipating energy. These observations align with the mechanisms described by Pagliara and Palermo41,50, who showed that increased downstream submergence suppresses jet impact energy and reduces local scour downstream of block and drop structures. They are also consistent with the classical findings of Dey and Raikar28 and with the hydraulic jump stabilization theory of Rajaratnam52, both of which demonstrate that enhanced tailwater levels diminish near-bed shear stress and limit scour development. The observed patterns indicate that tailwater regulation is an effective hydraulic control for mitigating excessive scour downstream of IDSs, particularly in controlled river systems. The results confirm that tailwater regulation can be treated as an active hydraulic design variable for IDSs rather than a passive boundary condition.

Interpretation of experimental trends in light of the predictive models

The regression-based predictive equations developed in this study reflect the dominant hydraulic and geometric processes identified experimentally. The statistical significance of yc/h and λ in predicting scour depth is physically consistent with their control on jet thickness, impact momentum, and pressure distribution at the toe of the structure. Similar dependencies between relative flow depth and scour intensity have been reported in clear-water jet scour studies28,43. The inclusion of λ explicitly differentiates the present formulation from existing wall jet and vertical drop models, which do not account for inclined momentum redistribution. Likewise, the strong influence of the densiometric Froude number (Frs) and tailwater ratio (yt/h) on scour length is consistent with the well-established role of flow intensity and downstream submergence in determining sediment transport capacity and cavity elongation42,50. The incorporation of these dimensionless parameters follows classical similarity principles in local scour analysis, where flow intensity, relative depth, and sediment mobility are expressed through nondimensional groups43. The strong model performance (R² = 0.796 for ds/h and R² = 0.874 for ls/h) supports the adequacy of the selected variables and aligns with previously proposed multivariate formulations for scour downstream of grade-control and drop structures28,50. The close agreement between predicted and measured values indicates that the equations capture the primary hydraulic drivers of scour geometry and can be applied reliably within the tested parameter range.

Applicability, limitations, and design relevance

The applicability of the present findings is bounded by the experimental conditions under which the data were obtained, including uniform sediment, steady flow, and controlled downstream water levels. These constraints mean that natural river conditions such as sediment heterogeneity, unsteady hydrographs, debris interactions, or varying tailwater levels may influence scour behavior differently. Nevertheless, by isolating the intrinsic hydraulic response of IDSs, the experiments provide configuration-specific insight that is difficult to extract from field data or studies dominated by countermeasure effects. The controlled experiments establish reliable empirical tools for preliminary design and clarify how downstream slope and tailwater depth may be deliberately manipulated to mitigate scour. The trends identified here underscore the need for additional research under variable flow and sediment conditions to extend the applicability of the predictive equations to complex field scenarios. The uncertainty associated with measurement accuracy (± 1 mm), discharge regulation, and tailwater adjustment remains small compared to the observed scour magnitudes, and thus does not materially affect the interpretation of the hydraulic trends or the design-relevant conclusions presented.

Conclusions

This experimental study provides new insight into the mechanisms governing scour development downstream of inclined drop structures (IDSs) and identifies practical strategies for its control. The results demonstrate that scour evolution is rapid, with more than 80–85% of the final scour depth forming during the early stages of flow development (t/tu ≤ 0.2) in the majority of the experiments. This finding highlights the critical importance of early-stage hydraulic conditions in determining ultimate scour geometry and emphasizes the need for conservative design during initial flow exposure. The experiments confirm that both discharge intensity and jet impact angle strongly amplify scour, and their combined increase produces a nonlinear escalation in scour depth and extent. The downstream face inclination was shown to be a governing geometric parameter: steeper faces intensify jet momentum and promote deeper, more localized scour, whereas milder inclinations redistribute flow energy, resulting in longer but shallower scour profiles. This trade-off provides designers with a clear geometric lever to balance structural footprint against bed stability. Tailwater depth emerged as an effective and robust mitigation mechanism. Increasing the tailwater depth to 1.5–2.0 times the free-flow depth reduced maximum scour depth by up to 16.5% and scour length by up to 14%, primarily through enhanced energy dissipation and attenuation of near‑bed erosive flow intensity prior to jet impact. These results quantitatively confirm the stabilizing role of downstream submergence and support its deliberate use as a design variable rather than a boundary constraint. The regression-based predictive equations developed in this study integrate four key dimensionless parameters and reproduce measured scour depth and length with errors confined within ± 10%. The proposed predictive framework is specifically calibrated for inclined downstream geometries and therefore extends existing scour formulations derived primarily for vertical-drop and wall-jet configurations. Sensitivity analyses further clarify the governing controls, identifying relative critical depth and downstream face inclination as dominant predictors of scour depth, while the densimetric Froude number and tailwater ratio primarily regulate scour length. Together, these outcomes provide a physically interpretable and practically applicable framework for preliminary IDS design, performance evaluation, and scour risk assessment. From an engineering perspective, the findings offer quantitative guidance for performance-based selection and refinement of IDS geometry and operational conditions to minimize bed degradation while preserving hydraulic efficiency. The results are directly applicable to both the design of new structures and the retrofitting of existing installations, with implications for sustainable river training, grade control, and sediment management. The conclusions are based on controlled laboratory conditions with uniform sediment and limited geometric configurations. Extension of the present framework to heterogeneous sediment beds, broader geometric ranges, and the inclusion of bed roughness elements or impact-zone protection measures would further enhance its applicability to complex field environments.
