Enhancing Long Chain-of-Thought Reasoning through Multi-Path Plan Aggregation
Siheng Xiong, Ali Payani, Faramarz Fekri

Abstract
Inference-time scaling enhances the reasoning ability of a language model (LM) by extending its chain-of-thought (CoT). However, existing approaches typically generate the entire reasoning chain in a single forward pass, which often leads to CoT derailment, i.e., the reasoning trajectory drifting off course due to compounding errors. This problem is particularly severe for smaller LMs with long CoTs due to their limited capacity. To address this, we analyze raw long CoTs and uncover a reasoning hierarchy consisting of planning and execution steps. Our analysis reveals that most reasoning errors stem from incorrect planning. Motivated by this observation, we propose Multi-Path Plan Aggregation (MPPA), a framework that augments single-pass reasoning with plan exploration and aggregation. Following a variable interval schedule based on the token position, MPPA generates multiple candidate plans and aggregates them into a refined planning step. To maintain efficiency, we adopt a minimal design in which the base LM serves as the primary policy, while a lightweight LoRA module implements the plan aggregation policy. We further observe that outcome-reward RL is inefficient for long trajectories (e.g., exceeding 4K tokens). To overcome this, we introduce online Step-DPO, a process-level preference optimization scheme that leverages Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC) to provide scalable stepwise supervision using small LMs. This yields more efficient training, improved stability, and higher accuracy. Extensive experiments on challenging math, science, and logical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that, with only 10% SFT data and 5% of preference pairs, our method outperforms both the DeepSeek-R1 distillation baseline and the outcome-reward RL baseline across multiple base models and tasks 1 1 1 Code and data available at https://github.com/xiongsiheng/MPPA . .

Introduction

OpenAI’s o1 series
(
OpenAI 2024
)
introduce inference-time scaling by increasing the length of the Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
reasoning process.
This design yields significant improvements in complex reasoning tasks compared to non-reasoning models, marking a major step forward in language model (LM) capabilities.
Building on this idea, DeepSeek
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
proposes a large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline that directly incentivizes the generation of long CoTs through policy optimization.
Despite their empirical success, RL approaches that generate the entire reasoning chain in a single forward pass face notable limitations, including CoT derailment, where the reasoning trajectory drifts off course due to accumulated errors, and the inherent challenges of long-horizon RL with sparse outcome rewards.
These issues are especially pronounced for smaller LMs with long CoTs due to their limited capacity.

This sequential scaling strategy, i.e., simply extending the CoT length, can therefore be insufficient
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
.
It typically relies on self-correction from previous mistakes to refine reasoning.
However, once the model follows an incorrect path, recovery could become difficult, as subsequent reasoning remains influenced by earlier failed attempts.
To address this limitation, we begin by analyzing raw long CoTs from current large reasoning models (LRMs).
Our investigation reveals that the reasoning process inherently reflects the hierarchical structure of human cognition.
Due to pre-training priors and the inherent structure of reasoning tasks, reasoning steps are naturally segmented (by double newlines
\n\n
).
These steps can be further categorized into planning steps and execution steps.
An analysis of erroneous long-CoT trajectories shows that most errors originate from incorrect planning.
This observation aligns with the intuition that solving complex reasoning tasks often depends on identifying a key insight.
To improve planning quality, we introduce Multi-Path Plan Aggregation (MPPA).
For each planning step, the model generates multiple alternative plans and aggregates them into an improved plan before proceeding to the subsequent execution steps.
This mechanism enables exploration of diverse strategies before committing to a final plan, thereby enhancing robustness and accuracy.
Moreover, explicitly distinguishing between planning and execution steps allows us to preserve plan diversity while maintaining execution accuracy (reduce entropy) during optimization.

Beyond enhancing planning, we identify a fundamental challenge in credit assignment for long-horizon policy learning
(
Kaelbling et al. 1996
)
.
To tackle this, we propose an online Step-DPO pipeline for long CoT training, which significantly accelerates training and improves reasoning performance.
Existing RL fine-tuning frameworks struggle to provide effective process-level supervision
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
.
First, evaluating the correctness of intermediate steps is inherently difficult.
Automated annotation using LLM judges
(
Gu et al. 2024
)
often yield unreliable or noisy signals for long CoTs, while manual annotation
(
Lightman et al. 2023
)
is prohibitively expensive at scale.
Second, introducing a separate process reward model (PRM) adds complexity.
It is vulnerable to reward hacking
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
, requires substantial training data, and complicates the pipeline by necessitating repeated retraining.
To overcome these limitations, we repurpose Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC)
(
Doucet et al. 2001
;
Del Moral et al. 2006
;
Briers et al. 2010
)
as a process-level supervision signal for Step-DPO.
In the LM-based reasoning setting, the importance weight in TSMC estimates how much more likely a partial trajectory is to lead to a correct outcome under the target distribution compared to the current policy.
We then define the process preference between two candidate continuations at the same step by comparing their incremental log-weights.
This formulation has two key advantages:
1) it converts the multiplicative nature of sequential importance weights into an additive form, improving numerical stability;
2) it aligns naturally with the pairwise preference structure of DPO training.
We further observe that small LMs can be used to estimate survival probabilities efficiently.
Empirically, our approach provides stable and informative step-level preferences, leading to more efficient training and stronger performance on complex reasoning tasks.

We summarize our key contributions as follows:

•

We propose a novel Multi-Path Plan Aggregation (MPPA) framework that directly addresses the limitations of single-pass long-CoT approaches. Following a variable interval schedule based on the token position, the model generates multiple candidate plans and aggregates them into an improved planning step, enhancing robustness and accuracy in reasoning.

•

We repurpose Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC) to provide process-level preferences for online Step-DPO training. This approach removes the need for a separate process reward model, significantly reducing training overhead while delivering stable and informative supervision throughout long reasoning trajectories.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on challenging benchmarks in mathematics, science, and logical reasoning. Results show that our approach consistently outperforms both distillation-based long-CoT methods and RL methods that rely solely on outcome rewards.

Inference-time Scaling via Long Chain-of-Thought

Formulation.

Consider a query
q
, large reasoning models (LRMs) generate a CoT
c
before producing the final response
a
, where
q,c,a
are all sequences of tokens, i.e.,
c=(c[1],c[2],\cdots,c[T])
.
To improve model performance, these models extend the length of
c
by incorporating human-like reasoning behaviors such as exploration, self-verification and reflection.
The generation of long CoTs follows the standard autoregressive modeling: the probability of each token
c[t]
depends only on its preceding tokens
(c[1:t-1])
, which enables the factorization of the joint likelihood of the entire sequence as:

p_{\theta}(c[1:T])=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(c[t]\mid c[1:t-1]).

(1)

Note that, for notational simplicity, we omit the conditioning on
q
in Eq.
1
and in the following derivations.
Training the model
p_{\theta}
involves maximizing the likelihood of each token conditioned on its prefix, i.e., optimizing
p_{\theta}(c[t]\mid c[1:{t-1}])
over the training data.

Post-training.

Guo et al. 2025
detail how they incentivize the long CoT generation from a base model through large-scale RL without relying on SFT.
Specifically, they employ GRPO guided by rule-based outcome reward.
For each query
q
, GRPO samples a group of outputs
\{o_{1},o_{2},\cdots,o_{G}\}
from the old policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
, where each output is composed of a CoT followed by the final response, i.e.,
o_{i}=[c_{i},a_{i}]
, and then optimizes the policy
\pi_{\theta}
by maximizing the corresponding objective.

Discussion on the weakness of single-pass long CoT.

The above approach of using single-pass long CoT enables inference-time scaling with LRMs, but introduces several issues:

1)

CoT derailment.

In single-pass long CoT generation, the entire trajectory is produced in one uninterrupted forward pass.
Without guidance or structure, errors can accumulate and cause the reasoning path to drift off-course (see examples in
).

2)

Long-horizon RL with sparse outcome reward.

Long CoTs involve thousands of token-level actions before receiving a reward, which hinders effective credit assignment.
As shown in
Figure

1
, these trajectories can be extremely long, with errors occurring at widely varying positions, which undermines the effectiveness of outcome-based fine-tuning.
Moreover,
Figure

2
shows that latency and memory usage grow rapidly with trajectory length, while outcome-based supervision requires the entire trajectory to finish before feedback is provided.
Consequently, learning is slow and unstable, especially in the early stages when the model rarely produces correct trajectories.

Methodology

3.1
Enhancing inference-time scaling with multi-path plan aggregation

Reasoning hierarchy.

We begin by analyzing the raw long CoTs from current LRMs.
Due to pre-training priors and the inherent structure of reasoning tasks, reasoning steps can be naturally segmented by double newlines (
\n\n
).
During training, we treat each step as the optimization unit rather than individual tokens, since token-level optimization is computationally expensive and assigning credit to each step is more interpretable.
Formally, a long CoT can be expressed as
c=c^{(1:M)}
, where
c^{(m)}
denotes the
m
-th reasoning step separated by
\n\n
, and
M
is the total number of steps.
We further observe that these steps can be categorized into planning and execution steps (see
Figure

7
), forming a reasoning hierarchy analogous to human cognition.
In practice, we identify planning steps using indicative phrases, e.g., “Let me”, “Let’s”, “Wait”, and “Alternatively”.

Multi-path plan aggregation.

An analysis of erroneous long CoT trajectories reveals that most mistakes originate from incorrect planning.
This aligns with intuition that solving complex reasoning problems often requires finding a key insight.
Current LRMs typically rely on self-correction from previous failed attempts to refine their approach.
However, once the model follows an incorrect direction, it is often difficult to recover, as subsequent reasoning remains influenced by earlier errors.

To address this issue, we introduce Multi-Path Plan Aggregation (MPPA) to enhance long CoTs.
Specifically, for each planning step
c^{(m)}
, we prompt the model to generate multiple candidate plans and then aggregate them into an improved planning step (
Figure

3
).
This mechanism enables the model to explore diverse strategies before committing to a final plan, thereby improving robustness and accuracy.
Formally, the conditional probability is defined as:

p^{\text{MPPA}}_{\theta}(c^{(m)})=\begin{cases}p^{H}_{\theta}\left(c^{(m)}\mid c^{(1:m-1)},c_{1}^{(m:m+n)},\ldots,c_{l}^{(m:m+n)}\right)&\text{if }m\in S^{H}\\
p_{\theta}\left(c^{(m)}\mid c^{(1:m-1)}\right)&\text{if }m\notin S^{H}\end{cases}

(2)

where
c_{i}^{(m:m+n)}
represents the
i
-th candidate rollout covering steps
m
through
m+n
, the number of candidate plans is
l
, and
S^{H}\subseteq[1,M]
denotes the set of indices corresponding to planning steps.
Providing
n
future steps helps the model better assess the quality of each plan.
In practice, we generate 128 future tokens for each rollout instead of fixing
n
as a constant, which simplifies implementation.
We apply search only to planning steps, as they have the greatest influence on the reasoning process, while exploring all steps would be computationally intractable for long CoTs (which may contain dozens or even hundreds of steps).
To further manage computational cost, we introduce an interval constraint, performing search only for planning steps that appear after the specified interval.
To balance performance and efficiency, we adopt a variable interval schedule for planning-step search.
Let
t
denote the current token index.
The interval is set to 256 tokens for
t\leq 1024
, 512 tokens for
1024<t\leq 4096
, and 1024 tokens for
t>4096
.

Architecture.

The proposed model consists of two components: a base model and a plan aggregation module.
The base model follows the standard architecture of existing LRMs, while the plan aggregation module is implemented as a LoRA adapter
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, which offers parameter efficiency and has proven empirically effective.

3.2
Online Step-DPO with Process-Level Preferences

To effectively train our model, we adopt an online Step-DPO pipeline
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Lai et al. 2024
)
that integrates stepwise preference optimization with continual data refresh.
In each training round, we first fine-tune the base model on execution steps, then freeze it and optimize the plan aggregation module.
For the base model, we explicitly distinguish between planning and execution steps, as we aim to maintain high
diversity in planning
while ensuring
accuracy in execution
.

Supervised fine-tuning.

We collect long CoT examples from DeepSeek-R1 and construct a tailored dataset to train the plan aggregation module effectively.
Given a correct trajectory, we first identify several planning steps (treated as gold references) and then generate
l
alternative plan options for each selected step using a high-temperature sampling strategy.
For each option, we sample 128 future tokens to expose the downstream consequences of the chosen plan.
We consider two training settings:
1)
Select-best
: Given the gold step and its alternative options, the model learns to generate the gold step as the correct response.
2)
Refine
: Given only the alternative options, the model learns to refine them and generate the gold step as the improved outcome.
We design a structured prompt format as shown in
Figure

8
for this task.
In practice, we set
l=3
to achieve a favorable balance between reasoning accuracy and computational efficiency.

Process preference modeling.

A key component of our online Step-DPO pipeline is the process-level supervision applied to each reasoning step.
Since stepwise preferences must be continually refreshed during online training, manual annotation is infeasible due to both cost and latency.
We generate stepwise preferences with Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC)-based incremental log-survivability, which directly measures how a candidate step changes the probability of eventual success via fast rollouts.
Compared to alternatives, e.g., LLM-as-judge labeling, this yields on-policy, margin-calibrated, and style-agnostic supervision without training a separate reward model.

Formally, for a partial trajectory
c^{(1:m)}
, let
\hat{g}(\cdot)
denote the estimated
survival probability
, the chance that the partial trajectory can still lead to a correct final solution.
This is estimated via Monte-Carlo lookahead: from the current prefix, we generate
K
fast rollouts and compute the empirical success rate:

\hat{g}(c^{(1:m)})=\mathbb{E}_{(c^{(m+1:M)},a)\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,c^{(1:m)})}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\text{correct}}(a\mid q,c^{(1:m)})\right].

(3)

One key challenge of this approach is its computational cost. To address this, we design a strategy that is both efficient and reliable.
Specifically, we fine-tune a small LM on the SFT data and use it as the rollout policy.
This choice is justified for three reasons:
(i) the small model shares a similar distribution with the base model because it is trained on the same data;
(ii) unlike a process reward model that must predict precise scalar values, we only need to compare two options and capture the correct relative tendency;
and (iii) rollout with a smaller model is significantly more cost-effective.
As shown in
Figure

4
, the small model achieves comparable estimation accuracy while being much faster, supporting this design choice.

We then assign a utility to each candidate step by measuring the increment in log-survivability:

U(c^{(m)})\;=\;\log\big(\operatorname{clip}\big(\hat{g}(c^{(1:m)}),\ \epsilon,\ 1-\epsilon\big)\big)\;-\;\log\big(\operatorname{clip}\big(\hat{g}(c^{(1:m-1)})\ \epsilon,\ 1-\epsilon\big)\big).

(4)

where
U(c^{(m)})
measures how much the chosen step
c^{(m)}
improves (or harms) the chance of eventual success, relative to the prior prefix, and
\epsilon=10^{-6}
is a constant for numerical stability.
To create process preferences, we compare candidate steps under the same prefix.
Given multiple candidates with utilities, we form preference pairs
(c^{(m)+},c^{(m)-})
whenever
U(c^{(m)+})-U(c^{(m)-})>\delta
for margin threshold
\delta=0.1
.

This formulation produces
process-level preference labels
that are policy-free, interpretable, and directly grounded in estimated solvability.
By supervising the model to prefer steps that increase survivability, Step-DPO obtains fine-grained credit assignment at each reasoning step while avoiding the brittleness of handcrafted rules.

Experiments

Dataset

We evaluate our approach on a range of challenging reasoning benchmarks, including math (MATH500
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
, AIME24
(
MAA 2024
)
), science (GPQA-diamond
(
Rein et al. 2023
)
), and logical reasoning (BoardGameQA-hard
(
Kazemi et al. 2023
)
).
Detailed dataset statistics are provided in
Appendix

B
.
For training, we divide the datasets into two parts: SFT data and online preference data for Step-DPO.
The SFT set contains about 80K examples as described in
Section

3.2
.
In addition, we reserve 10K prompts for Step-DPO training.
These prompts are used in the online loop to generate candidate continuations at different reasoning steps, from which process-level preference pairs are derived.

Implementation details

We fine-tune three base models, Qwen-2.5-1.5B
(
Yang et al. 2024a
)
, Qwen-2.5-MATH-7B
(
Yang et al. 2024b
)
and LLaMA-3.1-8B
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
, on long CoT trajectories with full-parameter fine-tuning.
The resulting base models are then frozen, and we train the plan aggregation policy via LoRA fine-tuning
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
for parameter efficiency.
We set the number of candidate plans to
l=3
and the number of future tokens per rollout to 128.
Our online Step-DPO pipeline is implemented using the TRL framework
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
and executed over four training rounds.
In each round, we sample approximately 3K prompts, randomly select four planning/execution steps per prompt, and generate two candidate continuations for each selected step.
These candidates are scored using the utility (
Equation

4
).
In experiments, we use Qwen-2.5-1.5B SFT base model as the rollout policy, with
K=4
sampled rollouts per prefix.
From each prefix, we form one preference pair
(c^{(m)+},c^{(m)-})
, weighted by the utility margin.
Pairs are accumulated into a replay buffer that mixes 70% fresh and 30% replayed data.
Policy updates sequentially within each round: we first update the base policy with the collected execution step pairs, followed by the plan aggregation policy update.
Each update uses mini-batches of size 32 for
E=4
epochs, and applies the standard Step-DPO objective with
\beta=0.1
.
Generated continuations are capped at a maximum length of 8,192 tokens.
More implementation details, including ablation settings and hyperparameters, are provided in
Appendix

C
.

Related Work

Reasoning models
(
OpenAI 2024
;
Qwen-Team 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
represent a transformative advancement in the evolution of LMs, sparking substantial interest in replicating their strong performance.
Several subsequent works
(
HuggingFace 2025
;
OpenThoughts-Team 2025
;
Bespoke-Labs 2025
;
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
have primarily relied on supervised fine-tuning (SFT), i.e., distillation, using curated datasets composed of long CoTs generated by DeepSeek-R1 on math or coding problems.
These efforts focus on developing effective data collection and curation strategies to obtain high-quality reasoning traces.
While this approach has shown promising results, pure SFT is generally less efficient in utilizing training signals compared to RL, as it passively imitates given demonstrations, lacking both exploration and fine-grained credit assignment.

Follow-up works exploring RL have introduced enhancements along three main dimensions.

1) RL Algorithms.
Existing methods can be categorized by the underlying RL algorithm they employ:

•

PPO-based:
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
is an actor-critic method that utilizes a value network to estimate the expected return of generated responses.
VC-PPO
(
Yuan et al. 2025
)
introduces value pretraining and decoupled Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to mitigate value initialization bias and reward signal decay.
VAPO
(
Yue et al. 2025
)
further proposes length-adaptive GAE to handle variable-length sequences and incorporates additional techniques such as negative log-likelihood (NLL) penalty to improve training stability and performance.

•

GRPO-based:
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
removes the critic and GAE components from PPO, instead applying reward normalization within groups of sampled responses.
DAPO
(
Yu et al. 2025b
)
enhances GRPO with four techniques—Clip-Higher, Dynamic Sampling, Token-Level Policy Gradient Loss, and Overlong Reward Shaping.
Dr. GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
eliminates the normalization term entirely to reduce biases related to response length and question difficulty.

•

REINFORCE-based:
REINFORCE
(
Williams 1992
)
performs policy updates by directly maximizing expected rewards via gradient ascent on sampled trajectories.
REINFORCE++
(
Hu 2025
)
improves stability by introducing a token-level KL divergence penalty in the reward function.

Kimi-Team et al. 2025
further refine this direction by using centralized rewards and K2-style KL penalties to reduce variance and accelerate convergence.

Despite their effectiveness, these approaches rely primarily on sparse outcome rewards,
which are inefficient for training on long trajectories.

2) Reward Design.
Most existing approaches define rewards based on outcome accuracy, format compliance, and length constraints
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
.
Process rewards remain largely unexplored in current pipelines.

3) Data Sampling Strategies.
Several works
(
Hu et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
adopt curriculum learning techniques that gradually increase task difficulty over the course of training.
Rejection sampling
(
Wen et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2025b
)
has also been employed to improve sample quality and stabilize training dynamics.
In addition, epoch-level history resampling has been proposed
(
He et al. 2025c
)
, wherein easy examples are dropped from future epochs, allowing the model to focus on harder cases and improve learning efficiency.

To overcome sparse outcome rewards, recent work
(
Lightman et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
has explored Process Reward Models (PRM), but they struggle in practice
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
:
1) explicitly defining fine-grained reasoning steps is difficult;
2) reliably verifying the correctness of each intermediate step is non-trivial;
3) training a separate reward model introduces the risk of reward hacking and requires additional training.
MPPA alleviates these issues by providing process-level supervision through stepwise preferences derived via TSMC, eliminating the need for an explicit reward model.

A parallel line of research explores the integration of structured reasoning representations, such as graphs, programs, or symbolic traces, to enhance interpretability and compositional generalization.
Graph-based reasoning models
(
Xiong et al. 2024a
;
Xiong et al. 2024b
;
He et al. 2025b
;
He et al. 2025a
;
Yu et al. 2025a
)
represent intermediate reasoning states and dependencies as nodes and edges, enabling explicit tracking of logical relations or causal chains throughout multi-step reasoning.
Similarly, symbolic or program-guided approaches
(
Yang et al. 2024d
;
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Xiong et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
use formal structures (e.g., equations, function calls, tables, or logic programs) as intermediate supervision, promoting consistency and error recovery during long-chain reasoning.
These approaches provide stronger inductive biases than purely text-based reasoning and can be naturally combined with RL or process-level supervision.
On the other hand, agentic reasoning methods
(
He et al. 2024
;
Han et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
extend this direction by treating the model as an active problem-solving agent.
Such methods emphasize tool use and environment interaction, enabling more adaptive and self-corrective reasoning behaviors.
In our context, MPPA can be viewed as complementary to such paradigms, as it captures planning dependencies in a latent, text-based form.

6
Conclusion

We introduced Multi-Path Plan Aggregation (MPPA), a novel framework that enhances inference-time scaling by strengthening the planning capabilities of large reasoning models (LRMs).
MPPA enables the model to explore multiple candidate plans and aggregate them into a refined, higher-quality plan before execution.
By sidestepping the limitations of prior outcome supervision methods, MPPA provides a scalable and robust pathway for training LRMs.
Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent performance gains across math, science, and logical reasoning tasks, highlighting the potential of MPPA as a general-purpose framework for advanced reasoning.
Future directions include enabling adaptive allocation of reasoning steps to problem complexity, and developing self-improving fast rollout policies that enhance survival probability estimation through iterative distillation.

Limitations

Our method requires maintaining two separate policies and performing additional steps for process-level supervision, which increases overall training complexity.
To keep computation practical, we freeze the base model when training the plan aggregation policy, decouple supervision estimation from trajectory generation, and sequentially update the two policies.
These strategies help control training overhead; however, on resource-constrained hardware, further optimizations, such as quantization, activation checkpointing, or memory-efficient attention, may still be required.
The framework also introduces several additional hyperparameters, though most can be assigned reasonable default values that transfer well across tasks.
Empirically, we find that performance is robust to moderate variations in these settings, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning.

Appendix C
Experiment Details

Base model.

We construct training data for both the SFT and Step-DPO stages using all available training splits from the aforementioned datasets, following a 6:4 train–validation ratio.
During SFT, we employ the DeepSeek-R1 API to generate multiple reasoning trajectories per prompt via rejection sampling, retaining only those that yield correct final answers.
Each base model is trained on 80K examples with a batch size of 256, a peak learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, and a maximum sequence length of 8,192 tokens for 3 epochs.
Unless otherwise stated, all models are optimized using AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule and a linear warmup for 5% of total steps.
After SFT, the base model is frozen.

Plan aggregation module.

Given a verified reasoning trajectory, we identify several planning steps (treated as gold references) and generate
l
alternative plan candidates for each selected step
m
using high-temperature (
T=0.9
) sampling.
For each candidate, we sample 128 future tokens to expose the downstream consequences of the plan.
We consider two training modes:
(1)
Select-best
: given the gold step and its alternative candidates, the model learns to reproduce the gold step as the preferred response; and
(2)
Refine
: given only alternative candidates, the model learns to synthesize and refine them into the gold step.
The structured prompt format is illustrated in
Figure

8
.
In practice, we set
l=3
to balance reasoning accuracy and computational efficiency.
The plan aggregation module is trained with a higher peak learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
to encourage faster adaptation.

Step-DPO pipeline.

Our online Step-DPO training follows the TRL framework and runs for four rounds.
In each round, we sample approximately 3K prompts, randomly select four planning/execution steps per prompt, and generate two candidate continuations for each step using temperature 0.7 and top-
p=0.9
sampling.
Each continuation is truncated at 8,192 tokens and scored using the utility function (
Equation

4
).
We employ the Qwen-2.5-1.5B SFT model as the rollout policy, sampling
K=4
rollouts per prefix.
From each prefix, we form one preference pair
(c^{(m)+},c^{(m)-})
, weighted by the corresponding utility margin.
All pairs are stored in a replay buffer that mixes 70% newly generated and 30% replayed data, with a maximum capacity of 50K pairs (oldest entries discarded when full).
To improve sample efficiency, we apply a dynamic dropout strategy that removes “easy” prefixes whose candidates yield identical utilities; the dropout rate increases linearly from 0.1 to 0.9 during training.
Within each round, policy updates proceed sequentially: the base policy is first updated using execution-step pairs, followed by plan aggregation policy updates.
Each update uses a mini-batch size of 32 for
E=4
epochs under the standard Step-DPO objective with
\beta=0.1
.
The SFT checkpoint serves as the reference model for KL regularization.
All experiments are conducted on 4×A800 GPUs with
bf16
precision.

Evaluation.

During evaluation, we employ greedy decoding for both the base and instruction-tuned models to obtain coherent and consistent CoTs.
For our method, the base model adopts sampling-based decoding with temperature 0.6 and top-
p
0.95, while the plan aggregation module uses a lower temperature 0.2 with the same top-
p
to ensure stability during refinement.
For all other baselines, we follow the setup in
Guo et al. 2025
, using temperature 0.6 and top-
p
0.95.
For our method and all other baselines, we generate eight responses per prompt to reduce variance.
The maximum generation length is set to 16,384 tokens for all models.
Performance is reported using
pass@1
(average accuracy).
For AIME24, we additionally report consensus accuracy over 32 samples, denoted as
\text{cons}@32
.
