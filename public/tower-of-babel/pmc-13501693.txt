The economic burden of road trauma injuries in Canada: a comparison of road user types
Authors: Somayeh Momenyan, Herbert Chan, Lina Jae, John A. Taylor, John A. Staples, Devin R. Harris, Jeffrey R. Brubacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe incidence and severity of road trauma (RT) injuries differ based on road user type. This results in variability in the economic burden of RT injuries for different road users. This study aimed to estimate healthcare and lost productivity costs, in the year following RT injury by road user type, and to identify predictors of total costs.MethodsWe analyzed data from 1372 Canadian RT survivors between July 2018 and March 2020. Total costs, healthcare costs and lost productivity costs in the year following RT were estimated for each participant in 2023 Canadian dollars. Productivity loss was measured using the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment Productivity Cost Questionnaire at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury. A generalized linear model was applied to examine the relationship between patient characteristics and total costs.ResultsMean healthcare costs varied from $14,840 for cyclists to $27,369 for pedestrians. Mean lost productivity costs varied from $3,995 for pedestrians to $4,741 for motorcyclists. Mean total costs were highest for pedestrians ($30,958), followed by motorcyclists ($28,904), passengers ($22,513), drivers ($20,373), and cyclists ($18,836). Multivariable analyses showed that higher Injury Severity Score was associated with increased total costs across all road user types. Additionally, older age, poorer pre-injury health (lower health-related quality of life and more comorbidities), higher pre-injury pain catastrophizing, and lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores were significantly linked to higher total costs in certain road user types. Amongst drivers, students had lower mean total costs than employed patients.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates considerable variability in the economic costs of RT injuries among different road user types. It also identified factors associated with higher costs. This information is essential for policymakers to implement targeted interventions or policies tailored to specific road user groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27934-9.

Introduction

In Canada, road trauma (RT) accounts for approximately 1900 fatalities and 150,000 injuries annually [1]. The annual cost of RT injuries in Canada was estimated at $3.6 billion in 2018 [2]. According to previous studies, the incidence and severity of injuries differ based on road user type [3, 4]. Motorcyclists incur the most serious injuries, often leading to long-term disability and greater utilization of healthcare services [4, 5]. Cyclists, pedestrians, and motorcyclists are vulnerable road users as they are not protected by vehicle safety features. In motor vehicle collisions, they are more likely to sustain serious injuries that are associated with slower and less complete recovery. In Canada, the number of injured cyclists and pedestrians is increasing as active transportation becomes more common [6]. Based on available data from Transport Canada and health service estimates, cyclist injuries have risen by 20–25% from 2012 to 2022 [7]. In the United States, the National Highway Traffic Safety reported a 30% rise in cyclist injuries from 2012 to 2022, with annual injuries climbing from 38,000 to over 49,000, driven by increased urban cycling and inadequate infrastructure [8].

The reduction in non-fatal injuries has been less marked than the reduction in fatal injuries in recent years [9], indicating a need for greater attention to outcome following non-fatal injury. Comprehensive information on the costs of these injuries is helpful for translating the burden of non-fatal injuries into economic terms, aiding policymakers in decision-making and setting priorities for injury prevention [10]. The economic costs of non-fatal injuries include both direct healthcare costs and indirect cost such as costs due to lost productivity. Healthcare costs, such as emergency department (ED) visits, hospital admissions, medications, and rehabilitation costs, have grown over the last decades due to an increasing number of non-fatal injuries [11] and higher costs of healthcare in general [12]. Productivity costs encompass both absenteeism (time off work) and presenteeism (reduced productivity) in both paid and unpaid work (e.g., housework, caregiving, volunteer work) [13, 14].

There are only a few studies on the individual-level costs of RT injuries [15–27] and little information on their longer-term economic costs [16–18, 27]. Many studies focus exclusively on direct costs and neglect costs related to lost productivity [15, 16, 19, 20]. In addition, few studies investigated the healthcare and productivity costs of RT injuries by road user type [18, 28, 29]. The results from these studies revealed significant cost variations among different categories of road users. To our knowledge, no previous studies have used multivariable analysis to identify predictors of cost for each road user type. A key step in reducing the economic burden of RT injuries is prioritizing upstream policy solutions that prevent injuries from occurring, such as de-prioritization of motor vehicle use, reducing motor vehicle speeds, implementing traffic calming measures, enhancing cycling and walking infrastructure, removing parking minimums, increasing housing density, and improving public transportation. Identifying predictors of costs for each road user type complements these efforts by enabling healthcare providers to identify potentially costly road users for early intervention and medical care when injuries do occur.

This study aims to fill this gap by integrating cost estimation with a predictive analysis of economic burden across different road user types. By incorporating a multivariable approach, we move beyond descriptive statistics to develop a framework for understanding the determinants of RT injury costs. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the economic burden, in terms of direct healthcare costs and productivity costs, in the year following RT injury by five road user types: cyclists, pedestrians, motorcyclists, and motor vehicle drivers and passengers. We also aimed to identify the significant cost-drivers of RT injuries for different road user categories.

Methods

Participants and procedures

RT survivors, including cyclists, pedestrians, motorcyclists, and motor vehicle drivers and passengers, were enrolled from three British Columbia (BC) EDs (Vancouver General Hospital, Vancouver; Royal Columbian Hospital, New Westminster; and Kelowna General Hospital, Kelowna) between July 2018 and March 2020. Detailed methods have been previously outlined [30]. To be eligible, RT survivors needed to (i) be at least 16 years old and (ii) have presented to one of the participating EDs within 24 h of a collision involving a motorized vehicle. Non-residents of BC and those who died within 30 days of the injury were excluded. Data were gathered through interviews and review of medical records. Baseline interviews were conducted in-person by a research assistant during ED visits or hospital admissions, or by telephone for participants who were discharged from the ED when no research assistant was present. Interviews were completed within one week of injury except for in patients admitted to hospital with severe injuries who were not capable of being interviewed within this timeframe; over 98% of the interviews were conducted within two weeks of the injury and all interviews were completed within 35 days. Follow-up interviews were conducted by telephone, online survey, self-filled paper questionnaire or in-person (using a translator if required) depending on participant preference. For those unable to complete interviews independently (e.g., cognitive disability, language barrier), a proxy either assisted the participant or completed the questionnaire on the participant’s behalf. Injury type and severity was determined through medical chart reviews. For individuals who did not speak English, interviews were conducted in Cantonese, French, Korean, Mandarin, Punjabi, and Vietnamese by multilingual research assistants or through a translator. This study was approved by the research ethics board of the University of British Columbia. Participants provided informed written or verbal consent. For minors (16–18 years old), parental / guardian permission was obtained in addition to participant assent. For participants unable to provide consent (e.g., comatose), proxy consent was obtained from a designated caregiver.

Outcome variable

The outcome was total costs, including estimates of healthcare costs and productivity costs over a year following RT by five road user types. Data on healthcare resource use over a year following RT were obtained from Population Data BC (PopDataBC), a data repository containing individual-level longitudinal health records for all residents of BC [31]. De-identified patient data are compiled from several datasets linked by BC Personal Health Number. These include the Discharge Abstracts Database, which contains administrative and clinical data on patients admitted to hospital as well as data on day surgery, long-term care, rehabilitation and other types of care; the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, which contains ER visits and ambulance usage; the Medical Services Plan (MSP) Payment Information File, which contains data on medical services provided by practitioners in the province; and PharmaNet, which contains all prescriptions for drugs and medical supplies dispensed from community pharmacies in BC as well as prescriptions dispensed from hospital outpatient pharmacies for patient use at home. The Resource Intensity Weight (RIW) method was used to estimate hospital costs. RIWs are the relative case weights for Case Mix Groups (CMG) or Day Procedure Group (DPG)/Comprehensive Ambulatory Care Classification System (CASC) groups. RIWs are used to measure the intensity of resource use associated with different diagnostic, surgical procedure, and demographic characteristics of an individual [32, 33]. We calculated hospital costs per case by multiplying the case-specific RIW by the cost per weighted case provided by the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI).

Productivity costs were measured by the Productivity Cost Questionnaire (iPCQ) developed by the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment (iMTA) [34]. This validated questionnaire adopts a four-week recall period. To capture productivity loss over a year following RT injury, we collected this questionnaire from patients at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months post-injury. The iPCQ includes three modules measuring productivity losses of paid work due to (i) absenteeism, (ii) presenteeism and (iii) productivity losses related to unpaid work. This questionnaire also includes general questions regarding participants’ work status such as the weekly hours of paid work and number of working days per week. The absenteeism module identifies the occurrence and length of short-term and long-term absenteeism from paid work. Absences beginning before the recall period are considered long-term absences. The presenteeism module identifies reduced productivity at work. In the unpaid work module, participants are asked if they performed less unpaid work because of physical or psychological problems. The productivity losses for the recall period were extrapolated to adjust for the length of period between two consecutive measurements. The human capital approach (HCP) was used to calculate productivity costs for a year following RT injury [35]. The costs of productivity were calculated by multiplying the missed work or lost productivity hours by the average hourly wage rate obtained from Statistics Canada’s CANSIM database for a person in the same age group, sex, and interview date in BC [36]. The costs of unpaid productivity losses were calculated by multiplying the total hours of lost productivity by the average hourly wage rate for household care at the interview date in BC [37]. The missing numbers of days lost or lost productivity were assumed to be equal to zero. If the number of paid hours per week that a participant worked was missing, the national mean based on sex was used, 38 h per week for males and 34 h per week for females [38]. Participants were also asked about their productivity 4 weeks prior to the injury using the iPCQ. Absenteeism and presenteeism losses in paid work were considered to be zero for participants who reported pre-injury absence from work, were not in the working population (aged 15–64), or did not have paid employment. All costs were adjusted for inflation to 2023 CAD dollars using the Statistics Canada Consumer Price Index [39].

Predictor variables

Participants’ sociodemographic, psychological, medical, and trauma-related characteristics were collected at the baseline interview. Sociodemographic factors included age, sex, employment status, education level, living situation, and years living in Canada. Psychological factors included pre-injury general somatic symptom severity, pain catastrophizing, psychological distress, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The number of pre-injury comorbidities was the only medical factor measured at baseline. Trauma-related factors included Injury Severity Score (ISS) [40, 41], Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [42], and injury location. Pre-injury somatic symptom severity, pain catastrophizing, and psychological distress were measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-15 (PHQ-15) [43], the Pain Catastrophizing Scale [44], and the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) [45, 46], respectively. Higher scores on these scales indicate greater symptom severity, higher levels of catastrophic thinking, and more severe depression and/or anxiety, respectively. Pre-injury HRQoL was evaluated using the Short Form 12 survey (SF-12) [47], where higher scores indicate better health. Comorbidities encompassed eye disease, arthritis, diabetes, respiratory disease, heart disease, hypertension, stroke, epilepsy, kidney disease, psychiatric disorders, and other diseases self-reported by participants during the baseline interviews.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation (SD), number and percentage) were employed to summarize all variables. We used a generalized linear model (GLM) with a gamma distribution and log-link function to analyze total costs, addressing the skewness of its distribution (see Supplementary Figure S1). We conducted univariable analyses before multivariable analyses to assess the association between potential predictors and total costs. We included predictors that were univariately significant (p < 0.05) in the multivariable GLM. The final GLM was constructed using a backward elimination algorithm based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The results of multivariable GLM were presented as the relative difference in mean costs (exp(β)) with 95% confidence intervals.

The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method was used to impute missing values for baseline covariates. All baseline variables were considered in the imputation model, either as predictors or imputed variables. MCMC is a simulation-based approach that generates plausible values for missing data by iteratively sampling from the posterior distribution of the observed data. To ensure robustness, we performed diagnostics on the imputed dataset to assess distributional similarity to observed data. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the analysis of data with imputations (Table 4) to the analysis without imputations (Supplementary Table S2). The consistency of results between these analyses supports the validity of the imputation approach. Statistical significance was considered at the 0.05 level. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.5.

Results

Description of patients by road user type

The flow diagram showing enrolled participants for total costs outcome is depicted in Fig. 1. A description of patients’ characteristics by road user type is presented in Table 1. Of the 1372 participants, drivers represented the largest proportion at 45.8% (n = 629), followed by pedestrians at 19.3% (n = 265), passengers at 14.8% (n = 203), cyclists at 12.2% (n = 167), and motorcyclists at 7.9% (n = 108). The mean age varied among road users, from 39.8 years for cyclists to 46.6 years for pedestrians. The majority of motorcyclists were male (83.3%), while among road users, only passengers were predominantly female (67.0%). In terms of employment status, employed patients comprised the largest proportion within each road user type (drivers: 70.1%; passengers: 59.1%; motorcyclists: 78.7%; pedestrians: 54.7%; and cyclists: 74.3%). Motorcyclists and pedestrians had the highest injury severity, with a mean ISS of 10.0 and drivers had the lowest mean ISS of 5.6. Cyclists had fewer pre-existing comorbidities than other user types.

We report detailed costs by road user type for patients who were discharged home and those who were admitted to hospital in Table 2. Predictors of costs by road user type

The descriptive statistics (mean and SD) of total costs based on patients’ characteristics, along with the corresponding univariate analyses for each road user type, are presented in Table 3. According to univariate analyses, ISS, GCS, and the number of comorbidities were associated with total costs for all user types. In addition, results of univariate analysis in drivers showed that the mean total costs were significantly higher in the following groups: retired/unemployed patients ($24,148), patients with less than a university education ($27,658), patients with more than 10 years of residency in Canada ($21,686), patients with torso ($29,792), spine/back ($25,048), and lower extremity ($29,105) injuries. For passengers, the following groups had higher total costs: patients with more than 10 years of residency in Canada ($25,412), those with torso ($33,484), and lower extremity ($34,544) injuries. Among motorcyclists, patients with torso injuries incurred higher total costs ($48,648). In pedestrians, retired/unemployed patients ($47,355), patients with more than 10 years of residency in Canada ($35,618), and those with torso injury ($49,805) had higher total costs. In cyclists, patients with torso injury ($28,906) had higher total costs.

Table 4 shows the results of multivariable analyses of total costs for the five road user types. According to the final GLM results, higher ISS was significantly associated with higher mean total cost for all road user types. For instance, with each additional comorbid condition, mean total costs were estimated to increase by 17% (exp(β) = 1.17; 95% CI = 1.07–1.29). Discussion

This study prospectively enrolled a large inception cohort of road trauma survivors and followed them for one year to determine their healthcare and lost productivity costs. We found that pedestrians had the highest mean healthcare costs ($27,369), followed by motorcyclists ($24,163), passengers ($18,809), drivers ($16,363), and cyclists ($14,840). Motorcyclists had the highest mean productivity costs ($4,741), followed by drivers ($4,009), cyclists ($3,995), passengers ($3,704), and pedestrians ($3,589). Accounting for the frequencies of different road user types, drivers accounted for 40.2% of the total healthcare cost in this cohort, followed by pedestrians at 25.7%, and passengers at 14.3%, making them the top three identified categories.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the long-term costs including healthcare and lost productivity costs associated with RT injuries by road user type in Canada. We demonstrated that the total costs generated by RT injuries across all road users are significant. Since most medical costs were not paid by patients but by the public health insurance in BC, improving traffic safety is an opportunity to reduce a significant portion of government expenses. Moreover, our results demonstrated considerable variability in the economic burden among the different road user types. Therefore, our results offer a justification for prioritizing targeted interventions or policies based on road user type. This prioritization can be used in international contexts, especially in countries with traffic compositions similar to Canada. However, a systems approach to road safety, such as Vision Zero, suggests that built environment design and policies that lead to a safer system would be more effective than transport mode-targeted strategies. Furthermore, the most comprehensive and effective strategies to reduce road traffic injuries and their severity would likely involve speed reduction and de-prioritization of motor vehicle use in favor of alternative transportation modes such as bicycling, walking, and public transit.

In our study, motor vehicle occupants (drivers and passengers) had the lowest mean ISS (passengers = 6.1 and drivers = 5.6) of all road user groups and the lowest mean healthcare costs after cyclists. This is likely because motor vehicle occupants are less vulnerable to severe injury due to their higher level of protection in event of a collision. Conversely, pedestrians who are relatively unprotected, experienced the highest mean ISS (10.0), which translated to the highest healthcare costs in our sample. Pedestrians in our sample were also generally older than other road users which makes them more vulnerable to severe injuries and may contribute to slower recovery. Therefore, the costs associated with pedestrian injuries, especially involving older pedestrians, are significant and there should be increased public health focus on this group similar to that given to car occupants. Motorcyclists are also vulnerable road users and often travel at high speed making them susceptible to severe disabling injuries that often require long-term rehabilitative services [5]. Motorcyclists in our sample had the highest mean ISS (10.0) and the second highest healthcare costs, after pedestrians. Motorcyclists also incurred the highest mean productivity costs in our sample, while pedestrians had the lowest. This difference can be largely explained by the fact that a larger proportion of motorcyclists were employed (80.2%), while only 56.0% of pedestrians were employed. Cyclists had the lowest healthcare costs in our study. Although cyclists did not sustain the lowest mean ISS (7.2) compared to other road users in our study, they reported less pain catastrophizing, fewer comorbidities, less somatic symptoms, and higher HRQoL than other users. In a previous study, cyclists had significantly better psychological health, less psychological distress, and pain catastrophizing within 28 days and 6 months post-injury [48].

Our findings are largely in line with research from other countries. Van der Vlegel et al. (Netherlands) studied 1024 RT survivors who required hospital admission and reported that pedestrians had the highest healthcare cost, while bicyclists had the lowest costs [18]. Van der Vlegel et al. also reported that motorcyclists had the highest lost productivity costs, while cyclists had the lowest [18]. Olesen et al. (Denmark) analyzed a national sample of admitted RT survivors and showed that pedestrians and motorcyclists had the highest attributable hospital costs, whereas cyclists had the lowest costs [49]. Polinder et al. (Netherlands) used a national database to study costs of trauma patients and reported that, among RT survivors, healthcare costs were highest for pedestrians and motorcyclists and lowest for moped users and bicyclists [29]. Polinder et al. also reported that motorcyclist injuries incurred the highest lost productivity costs, while car occupants incurred the lowest (29). Nauman et al. (United States), used a national database to estimate total costs, including healthcare and productivity costs, for RT survivors hospitalized with non-fatal injuries; they reported that costs were highest for motorcyclists and lowest for pedal cyclists [28]. In contrast to our findings, Devos et al. (Belgium) used a national database to study costs of hospitalized RT survivors and reported that car occupants incurred the highest attributable costs and cyclists incurred the lowest [50]. This difference may be explained by variations in traffic composition, particularly the higher volume of traffic on highways. Other inconsistencies between studies is likely related to differences in the target population for cost estimation or varying proportions of employed patients across different road user types.

We found that higher ISS was significantly associated with higher total cost for all road user types. This is not surprising and is consistent with previous research [15, 18, 19, 21, 51]. We also found that lower GCS was associated with higher costs for drivers. Previous studies have also reported that healthcare costs were higher for patients with lower GCS scores [15, 16], likely because individuals with lower GCS typically require extended stays in hospitals offering higher levels of care. Our findings also indicate that pre-injury health is an important predictor of costs. We found that the number of comorbid conditions was linked to higher total costs for injured drivers, passengers, motorcyclists, and cyclists. This finding aligns with the adverse effects of poor pre-injury health on the recovery process and return-to-work post-injury [52]. Consistent with findings from a prior study including all adult trauma patients [53], our study also showed that lower pre-injury HRQoL was associated with higher healthcare costs among drivers. Our findings also align with previous research indicating higher healthcare costs [18, 19] and total costs [27] for older patients. This could be explained by age-related factors that exacerbate RT injury, leading to prolonged recovery times, extended hospital stays, and consequently, increased costs [54]. Pain catastrophizing is a negative emotional response to pain that includes worrying about pain, overestimate its severity or feel hopeless when feeling pain [55]. In our study, a pre-injury history of greater pain catastrophizing was linked to higher total costs of motorcyclist injuries. Therefore, motorcyclists with pain catastrophizing should be identified early post-injury, ideally at the time of hospital admission, for tailored intervention programs.

In light of our findings, targeted interventions tailored to specific road user groups could significantly reduce the economic burden of road trauma injuries. For pedestrians, who incurred the highest mean healthcare costs and injury severity, policies should prioritize collision prevention through infrastructure improvements, such as curb cuts, well-designed sidewalks, longer crossing signal times at intersections, leading pedestrian intervals, and traffic calming measures like speed bumps or reduced urban speed limits. Motorcyclists, with the highest productivity costs and second-highest healthcare costs, would benefit from policies promoting helmet use and protective gear, alongside advanced rider training programs to mitigate high-speed crash risks. Early-stage intervention programs for injured motorcyclists, particularly those identified with higher pain catastrophizing may also be beneficial. Cyclists, despite having the lowest healthcare costs, remain vulnerable (ISS = 7.2). Dedicated bike paths coupled with helmet use laws are cost-effective measures that may protect cyclists against severe injuries. Drivers and passengers, comprising 40.2% and 14.3% of total healthcare costs respectively, could be targeted with vehicle safety enhancements (e.g., automatic emergency braking) and public health initiatives to improve pre-injury health, given the link between comorbidities and higher costs. Implementing these interventions across diverse road user groups could leverage Canada’s public health insurance framework to reduce government expenditure, with applicability to international settings sharing similar traffic compositions. Also, since higher ISS was associated with higher costs in all road user groups, measures to reduce injury severity in event of a motor vehicle collision are indicated, particularly for pedestrians, motorcyclists and cyclists. One of the most effective ways of reducing the risk of severe injury for all road users, and especially for vulnerable road users, is to reduce traffic speed through lower urban speed limits, enhanced speed enforcement and/or traffic calming measures [56]. Our finding that worse pre-injury health was associated with higher costs suggests that costs could be reduced through health programs that aim to improve pre-injury health (especially for older individuals or those with comorbidities). Other possible interventions include targeted road safety campaigns for older road users, who face higher injury costs due to age-related factors.

This study has notable strengths, including its prospective design over one year with five rounds of data collection on lost productivity. We used validated scales to assess HRQoL, somatic symptom severity, psychological distress, and pain catastrophizing. The use of administrative data to estimate healthcare costs is another strength. Moreover, to improve the generalizability of the findings, no limitations were placed on injury severity level or language. We also acknowledge several limitations. First, although self-reported questionnaires are a common and convenient method in research, they do come with some drawbacks such as misinterpretation of questions. Also, self-reported pre-injury HRQoL, somatic symptom severity, and psychological distress may be subject to recall bias, although most baseline interviews were conducted within seven days post-injury to minimize this bias. Self-reported pre-injury variables, especially related to pre-injury health, are subject to the “good-old-days bias” whereby individuals, consciously or unconsciously, exaggerate their pre-injury health following injury. Future studies could mitigate this by incorporating prospective pre-injury data collection where feasible, such as leveraging existing health records or cohort studies with baseline assessments prior to injury. Second, the current estimate of costs may underestimate the actual economic burden of RT injury as private-pay healthcare costs, legal costs, property damage costs, life years lost costs, and compensation costs relating to pain, stress, and suffering have not been included. In addition, our study did not assess long-term healthcare costs beyond the first year following RT injury. This is a limitation because injured persons have increased usage of long-term healthcare services compared to the general population [57]. Older individuals and those experiencing significant pain, who likely utilize more healthcare resources, may have been less inclined to participate in the study. Third, we used national hourly wages instead of participants’ actual wages in the estimation of lost productivity costs. Finally, the findings related to the number of comorbidities should be interpreted with caution, as the scale used to assess this factor has not been validated.

Conclusions

This study estimated total costs including healthcare and productivity costs following RT injury by road user type and identified predictors of costs for each user type. Our results highlight significant long-term costs and their variability among the different road user types. Motorcyclists and pedestrians had the most severe injuries and motorcyclists had the highest cost per person. Over half of the total costs were attributed to driver and pedestrian injuries, emphasizing the importance of intervention programs targeting these road user groups. According to the multivariable analyses, older age, lower pre-injury HRQoL, higher pain catastrophizing, more comorbidities, higher ISS, and lower GCS were significantly linked to higher total costs among different user types. Our findings provide perspective on the economic impact of RT injuries, which cost billions of dollars each year, for policymakers and the general public. They are essential for policymakers to implement targeted interventions or policies for specific road user groups.
