How to Classically Verify a Quantum Cat without Killing it
Yael Tauman Kalai, Dakshita Khurana, Justin Raizes

Abstract
Existing protocols for classical verification of quantum computation ( 𝖢𝖵𝖰𝖢 \mathsf{CVQC} ) consume the prover’s witness state, requiring a new witness state for each invocation. Because 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} witnesses are not generally clonable, destroying the input witness means that amplifying soundness and completeness via repetition requires many copies of the witness. Building 𝖢𝖵𝖰𝖢 \mathsf{CVQC} with low soundness error that uses only one copy of the witness has remained an open problem so far. We resolve this problem by constructing a 𝖢𝖵𝖰𝖢 \mathsf{CVQC} that uses a single copy of the 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} witness, has negligible completeness and soundness errors, and does not destroy its witness. The soundness of our 𝖢𝖵𝖰𝖢 \mathsf{CVQC} is based on the post-quantum Learning With Errors ( 𝖫𝖶𝖤 {\sf LWE} ) assumption. To obtain this result, we define and construct two primitives (under the post-quantum 𝖫𝖶𝖤 {\sf LWE} assumption) for non-destructively handling superpositions of classical data, which we believe are of independent interest: 1. A state preserving classical argument for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} . 2. Dual-mode trapdoor functions with state recovery .

Introduction

Imagine a quantum prover that would like to demonstrate possession of Schrödinger’s proverbial cat without killing the cat.
Or picture a classical verifier seeking to assess whether a prover has sufficient quantum money to pay them.
In both cases, the challenge is: how can one prove the existence of a valuable quantum state without destroying it in the process?

It is well-known that quantum states are precious, consumable resources.
Some—like magic states—can be produced at a nontrivial but manageable cost, while others may be far harder to obtain. In particular, it is computationally infeasible to duplicate
\mathsf{QMA}
witnesses, relative to an oracle
[

Since only one copy of a witness may be available, it is crucial to avoid destroying this witness while proving statements about it.

And yet, despite this progress, a fundamental limitation remains: all existing protocols for classically verifying

In this work, we ask

Can a quantum prover convince a classical verifier of a

\mathsf{QMA}

Using a Single Witness.

In the setting where the verifier is
quantum
, non-destructive verification is exactly what enables deciding
\mathsf{QMA}
with negligible error using only one copy of a witness.
If a verifier can re-test the same witness repeatedly, completeness and soundness errors can be driven to
2^{-\lambda}

\mathsf{QMA}

[

Conversely, this near-perfect completeness also enables non-destructive
\mathsf{QMA}

quantum verifier

In contrast, existing protocols for

\mathsf{QMA}

\mathsf{QMA}
witness, a demand which is often unrealistic.
We ask:

Can a quantum prover convince a
classical
verifier of a

\mathsf{QMA}
statement using only one copy of the
\mathsf{QMA}
witness?

Witness Preservation Against Malicious Verifiers.

Finally, we mention the implications of our results to a related interesting scenario where the verifier is malicious and attempts to destroy the prover’s witness. In other words,

Can a quantum prover convince a verifier of a
\mathsf{QMA}
statement without losing their witness
even if the verifier deviates from the protocol?

It is not hard to see that any witness-preserving
\mathsf{CVQC}
(not necessarily non-adaptive) can be generically converted into one with
malicious-verifier
witness preservation, assuming time-lock puzzles and zero-knowledge arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
.
The verifier sends the prover a time-lock puzzle, containing its randomness; then for each message it sends in the
\mathsf{CVQC}
, it proves that this message is consistent with the time-locked randomness in zero-knowledge.
3
3

3

For soundness, the zero-knowledge argument and
\mathsf{CVQC}
must be together faster than the time to solve the time-lock puzzle.

If the verifier ever deviates from the protocol (e.g. by aborting), the prover can solve the time-lock puzzle and complete the protocol locally to recover its witness. On the other hand, the security of the time-lock puzzle and zero-knowledge argument ensure that a real prover has no information about the verifier’s randomness before the time-lock puzzle can be solved, so soundness is still guaranteed.

A conceptually even simpler approach uses classical witness encryption for
\mathsf{QMA}
(for which there are no plain-model constructions, but there are constructions in the classical oracle model e.g. due to
[
11
]
). The verifier encrypts a random message
m
under the
\mathsf{QMA}
statement to be proven, then the prover decrypts it and sends back
m^{\prime}
, whereupon the verifier checks that
m^{\prime}=m
.
If the witness encryption is correctly generated, then decryption is almost deterministic and disturbs the prover’s witness negligibly. Honest encryption can be guaranteed using a zero-knowledge argument.

1.2
Related Works

Classical Proofs of Quantum Knowledge
[
33
]
.

Vidick and Zhang observed that proofs of quantum knowledge with classical communication are generally destructive unless the witness can be (inefficiently) cloned, and so an honest prover in a sequentially repeated protocol needs to use multiple copies of the witness in order to succeed. Their analysis applies to perfectly non-destructive, statistically sound proofs and they provide some discussion about extending the results to protocols which only damage the witness by a small amount.
4
4

4

They also observed that in the specific case of QMA, an unbounded adversary could manufacture as many witness states as they wish. However, we note that the cloning implication still restricts QMA verification if there are at least two valid witnesses, since not all superpositions can be cloned.

On the other hand, in this work we build arguments of knowledge that are non-destructive.

Proofs of No Intrusion
[
17
]
.

Recently, Goyal and Raizes investigated a new primitive they called “proofs of no-intrusion” (PoNI). A PoNI for encryption is essentially a non-destructive classical argument that a ciphertext has not been “stolen”, in the sense that no external party can decrypt the ciphertext even given the key. Our results enable these by combining our state-preserving argument for NP with a public-key encryption scheme with publicly verifiable certified deletion (known from just public-key encryption
[
8
,
23
]
). See
Appendix

A
for more details.

However, their main new technical tool achieves something incomparable to our results. Goyal and Raizes introduce PoNIs for coset states, which is essentially a non-destructive classical argument of quantum knowledge specifically for coset states
[
16
]
. Although conceptually related, PoNIs for coset states are incomparable to our results. Our results allow non-destructive arguments for the general class of
\mathsf{QMA}
, where the statement being proven is public. On the other hand, PoNIs for coset states allow the prover to blindly prove a specific statement about coset states, where the prover
does not know the statement being proven.

Disambiguation.

[
26
]
also used the term “state preserving argument” in the context of arguments of knowledge. There, “state preserving” refers to the ability to extract some knowledge from an adversarial prover without noticeably disturbing the prover’s internal state. This takes place in a sandbox where the communications with the adversarial prover can be purified or rewound.
In contrast, our usage of state preserving refers to preserving an honest prover’s input even in a real execution.

1.3
Open Problems

We conclude this section with a set of open directions:

1.

Round Complexity.

Can we achieve single-copy
\mathsf{CVQC}
for
\mathsf{QMA}
with negligible errors in constant
(or even two)
rounds of interaction?
Since high-error protocols can only be composed sequentially when there is a single copy of the witness, addressing this question likely requires additional techniques beyond those developed in this work.

2.

Succinctness.

Can we achieve single-copy
\mathsf{CVQC}
for
\mathsf{QMA}
with negligible errors with smaller communication than the instance size?
Our compiler from
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
completeness to witness preservation can make each message succinct using techniques from
[
18
]
. However, our compiler for amplifying completeness using one copy of the witness uses a number of sequential repetitions which grows with the instance size, preventing it from being succinct even with short messages.

3.

Relationship between Witness Preservation and Zero-Knowledge.

Witness preservation in
\mathsf{CVQC}
seems to imply a form of witness hiding: intuitively, because the prover must keep the witness intact and quantum states cannot be cloned, an honest verifier cannot end the protocol holding the witness. Is there a more formal connection between witness preservation and zero-knowledge?

4.

Other Solutions for Malicious-Verifier Witness Preservation.

Can a quantum prover preserve its witness against a malicious verifier using weaker assumptions than time-lock puzzles and zero knowledge for
\mathsf{NP}
or witness encryption for
\mathsf{QMA}
? This question is even interesting using
quantum communication
, since the verifier is not guaranteed to cooperate with any request to uncompute the protocol.

5.

Further Applications.

Can witness-preserving
\mathsf{CVQC}
serve as a foundation for new unclonable cryptographic primitives, such as classically verifiable quantum money, copy-protected software, or quantum credentials?

Technical Overview

In what follows, we describe our compiler that compiles any non-adaptive
\mathsf{CVQC}
protocol, that uses a single copy of the
\mathsf{QMA}
witness (but may have poor completeness and soundness parameters and may destroy the witness), into a
\mathsf{CVQC}
protocol that uses a single copy of the
\mathsf{QMA}
witness and is witness-preserving and has nearly perfect completeness and soundness.

Our compiler works in three stages.

•

Stage 1:
\epsilon
-Non-Destruction and a Special Case.

First, we compile the
\mathsf{CVQC}
protocol to an
\epsilon
-non-destructive protocol with almost the same completeness
c-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
and soundness error
s+\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
. Roughly speaking, if the prover starts with a witness
\ket{w}
which would be accepted by the
\mathsf{QMA}
verifier with probability
p
(a “
p
-good witness”), then at the end of the protocol they have a witness
\ket{w^{\prime}}
which is at least
(p-\epsilon)
-good.
5
5

5

Our
\epsilon
-non-destructive protocol actually uses a special set of “repairable” witnesses that include all Marriot-Watrous witnesses and more generally any
(1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right))
-good witness, but for now we make the simplifying assumption that the
\epsilon
-non-destructive guarantee applies to any witness.

The runtime scales with
1/\epsilon
, so we are limited to
\epsilon=1/\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
.

As a special case of this compiler, if the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}

starts
with
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
completeness, then we achieve
witness preservation
: at the end of the protocol
\ket{w^{\prime}}
is negligibly far from
\ket{w}
. The runtime only blows up by a factor of the security parameter
\lambda
. This special case is used in Stage 3, below.

•

Stage 2: Sequential Amplification.

Next, we amplify completeness and soundness by repeating the protocol sequentially using the same witness. Since we can tune
\epsilon
to any
1/\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
, if we start with a witness just slightly better than the
\mathsf{QMA}
threshold, we can ensure that every intermediate witness still surpasses the
\mathsf{QMA}
threshold.

Thus, the
\mathsf{CVQC}
verifier accepts with probability
c-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
in each execution.

The parameters can also be tuned more aggressively to end up with a
(p-\epsilon^{\prime})
-good witness
\ket{w^{\prime}}
after all executions,

but we are still limited to
\epsilon^{\prime}=1/\mathsf{poly}\left(\lambda\right)
by the runtime blowup from stage 1.

•

Stage 3: Witness Preservation.

Finally, we re-compile the amplified protocol
again
, using the special case of the stage 1 compiler. Since the amplified protocol has
1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
completeness, the final compiled protocol is witness preserving.

The bulk of the technical ideas appear in stage 1, which is the focus of this overview.

2.1
Special Case: Almost-Perfect Completeness

We begin with the special case of
c=1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
. The main body of the compiler is the same as the general completeness version, but the witness recovery step is significantly simpler and illustrates the core ideas well.

Classical Verification in Superposition.

Ultimately, the goal in
\mathsf{CVQC}
is to convert a quantum statement into a classical one so that the classical verifier can check it.

The problem with existing approaches to
\mathsf{CVQC}
is that measuring said transcript collapses the prover’s state.
If the prover could keep the transcript “in superposition” throughout the protocol, then at the end of the protocol (after the verifier measures the verdict), the prover could uncompute the transcript and recover the witness.
This yields something like the following very high-level approach.

1.

Interactively, the prover
coherently
computes its answers
a_{i}
to each of the verifier’s
\ell
queries
q_{i}
during the
\mathsf{CVQC}
and does not measure them. At the end, the prover holds a superposition over answers
a_{[\ell]}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})
:

\sum_{a_{[\ell]}}\alpha_{a_{[\ell]}}\ket{a_{[\ell]}}

Since the verifier’s queries are non-adaptive, it doesn’t need to see the prover’s answers to send the next query.

2.

The verifier sends its (classical) secret state from the
\mathsf{CVQC}

to the prover
6
6

6

Sending the verifier’s internal
\mathsf{CVQC}
state is necessary so that the prover knows the
\mathsf{NP}
statement it is proving. If the
\mathsf{CVQC}
is publicly verifiable, this is not necessary, but publicly verifiable
\mathsf{CVQC}
remains an open problem.
and the prover uses a special argument for
\mathsf{NP}
to prove that it holds an answer sequence
a_{[\ell]}
which the verifier would accept, without collapsing its superposition over
a_{[\ell]}
.

Since the completeness is almost perfect, measuring whether
a_{[\ell]}
is an accepting transcript disturbs the state negligibly.

3.

Finally, the prover uncomputes its answers
\sum_{a_{[\ell]}}\alpha_{a_{[\ell]}}\ket{a_{[\ell]}}
to recover the witness.

Of course, this approach is not sound as written because the prover can change its answers
a_{[\ell]}
after seeing future queries or even the verifier’s internal state. So, the question is:

How do we bind the prover to
a_{[\ell]}
without collapsing it or otherwise preventing the prover from uncomputing its answers and recovering the witness?

Dual-Mode Trapdoor Functions with State Recovery.

Our solution to this conundrum is an observation of a novel property of
[
12
,
27
]
’s trapdoor claw-free functions which we abstract out as a new primitive: dual-mode trapdoor functions with state recovery.
This is a family of randomized functions and trapdoors
(f_{\mathsf{pp}},\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}})
. The tuples can be generated in two indistinguishable modes:
\mathsf{recovery}
or
\mathsf{injective}
.

In
\mathsf{injective}
mode, two different inputs
x\neq x^{\prime}
cannot collide:
f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x;r)\neq f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x^{\prime};r^{\prime})
for all randomness
r
and
r^{\prime}
. Given any
y=f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x;r)
, the trapdoor
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
can be used to find
x
.
This mode will be important for proving soundness of our protocol.

In
\mathsf{recovery}
mode,
f_{\mathsf{pp}}
is
2^{n}
-to-
1
, where
n
is the input size, and
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
can be used to invert a preimage
(x;r_{x})
of
y=f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x;r_{x})
for
any

x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
r_{x}
is the randomness. Similarly to Mahadev’s measurement protocol, a quantum party can “commit” to a computational basis measurement of a quantum state
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
by preparing a superposition over all randomness
r
then coherently evaluating
f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x;r)
and measuring the result
y
. This results in the state

\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x,r_{x}}

where
f_{\mathsf{pp}}(x,r_{x})=y
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Here is where the recovery comes in: if the trapdoor
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
were revealed, the quantum party can use it to coherently compute
r_{x}
controlled on
x
. By subtracting that from the randomness register, they disentangle their state and recover
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
.

Using the
\mathsf{recovery}
mode, we can update the protocol above as follows.

1.

The verifier generates a pair
(f_{\mathsf{pp}},\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}})
in the
\mathsf{recovery}
mode, and sends
\mathsf{pp}
to the prover. Interactively, the prover “commits” to each answer

by evaluating
f_{\mathsf{pp}}
as discussed previously. At the end, the verifier knows
y_{[\ell]}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell})
and the prover holds

\sum_{a_{[\ell]}}\alpha_{a_{[\ell]}}\ket{a_{[\ell]}}\otimes\ket{r_{a_{[\ell]}}}

where
y_{i}=f_{\mathsf{pp}}(a_{i};r_{a_{i}})
for each

.

2.

The verifier reveals its secret state for the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}
and the prover uses the “state-preserving” argument for
\mathsf{NP}
(which we elaborate on below) to prove that there exist preimages
(a_{i};r_{a_{i}})
of
y_{[\ell]}
such that the verifier would accept
a_{[\ell]}
in the
\mathsf{CVQC}
.

3.

The verifier sends
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
to the prover, who uses it to uncompute each
\ket{r_{a_{[\ell]}}}
. Since the superposition over answers
a_{[\ell]}
is no longer entangled with any external state, the prover can uncompute its answers to recover the witness.

If the state-preserving argument for
\mathsf{NP}
does not disturb the superposition
\sum_{a_{[\ell]}}\alpha_{a_{[\ell]}}\ket{a_{[\ell]}}\otimes\ket{r_{a_{[\ell]}}}
, then the
\mathsf{recovery}
mode trapdoor allows recovering the prover’s original witness up to negligible disturbance.

State-Preserving Arguments for
\mathsf{NP}
.

Roughly, we need the prover to be able to use a superposition of
\mathsf{NP}
witnesses
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\ket{w_{\mathsf{NP},i}}
to prove the
\mathsf{NP}
statement, and at the end of the argument they should have a state close to their original witness superposition.

A very simple construction is possible using witness encryption for
\mathsf{NP}
. The verifier encrypts a random message
m
under the statement to be proven. Then, the prover coherently decrypts
m^{\prime}
using their witness superposition. The verifier accepts if
m=m^{\prime}
. Since decryption is almost deterministic using a valid witness, measuring
m^{\prime}
disturbs the prover’s superposition negligibly.
Unfortunately, witness encryption is a relatively strong assumption, which we would like to avoid (and do avoid).

The next idea is to use a
statistically
witness-indistinguishable (
{\sf WI}
) argument. Intuitively, since the verifier’s view is statistically independent of which witness was used, the prover avoids measuring their state.
However, some care is needed here. During the argument, the prover may also entangle their witness with the randomness used. To recover the witness superposition, it is crucial that the prover be able to unentangle their state later.
7
7

7

It is not hard to come up with examples where unentangling is computationally hard, for example using a claw-free lossy function
without
a trapdoor.

We show that the well-known 3-coloring
{\sf WI}
argument, when instantiated using dual-mode trapdoor functions with state recovery, is a state-preserving argument for
\mathsf{NP}
.
The analysis requires a careful accounting of the prover’s randomness – both for the trapdoor function and for randomness inherent to the 3-coloring protocol – to show that it can be safely uncomputed at the end of the protocol.

Soundness.

The proof of soundness reduces to the soundness of the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}
by extracting an accepting transcript
a_{[\ell]}
from the prover.
As a result, the new
\mathsf{CVQC}
is a proof of knowledge if the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}
is.
If
(f_{\mathsf{pp}},\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}})
is generated in
\mathsf{injective}
mode, then there is only one preimage
a_{i}
of each
y_{i}
in step 1. The reduction can use
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
to extract each answer
a_{i}
from
y_{i}
as the prover sends it, then forward
a_{i}
to the
\mathsf{CVQC}
verifier.

The subtle part of the proof is in showing that the extracted
a_{[\ell]}
will be accepted by the verifier.
Intuitively, the indistinguishability of
\mathsf{injective}
and
\mathsf{recovery}
mode ensures that the adversarial prover cannot
knowingly
change its behavior between the extractor and a real execution. However, the adversarial prover does not know
\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
, so anything extracted using it might change when we switch modes.
8
8

8

As a concrete example, suppose that the function was specified by an Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) ciphertext
\mathsf{ct}=\mathsf{Enc}(b)
. If the mode is
\mathsf{recovery}
,
b=0
, and if the mode is
\mathsf{injective}
,
b=1
. An evaluation of
x
is just a homomorphic multiplication of
x\cdot b
. Although an adversarial evaluator might not be able to detect a switch from
b=0
to
b=1

To prevent this possibility, we break the proof into two parts. First, we observe that in
\mathsf{injective}
mode, the
existence
of a preimage
a_{[\ell]}
of
y_{[\ell]}
which the
\mathsf{CVQC}
verifier would accept, is sufficient to extract an accepting
a_{[\ell]}

Then, we use the soundness of the
\mathsf{NP}
argument to establish that this holds with almost the same probability that the adversarial prover would convince the non-destructive verifier.
If the
\mathsf{NP}
argument verifier would accept (in
\mathsf{recovery}
mode), there exists such a preimage, although it is not necessarily the unique preimage.
Since the
\mathsf{NP}
argument can be checked
without

\mathsf{td}_{\mathsf{pp}}
, we can rely on the indistinguishability of
\mathsf{recovery}
and
\mathsf{injective}
modes to show that the probability of the
\mathsf{NP}
argument verifier accepting is almost the same in
\mathsf{injective}
mode as in
\mathsf{recovery}
mode.

Dodging the Cloning Bullet.

As mentioned previously, at first it may seem that the ability to both extract and retain the prover’s witness implies the ability to clone it, which would violate the no-cloning theorem. Now that we have explained the core of the protocol, it becomes clearer why our approach does not imply cloning.

During our proof for (knowledge) soundness, we reduce to the knowledge soundness of the original
\mathsf{CVQC}
by using an
\mathsf{injective}
mode
f_{\mathsf{pp}}
to extract the prover’s answers for the original
\mathsf{CVQC}
. However,
\mathsf{injective}
mode does not have the nice recovery property we used to preserve the witness. In fact, measuring an evaluation of an injective mode
f_{\mathsf{pp}}

permanently
collapses the witness.
The only way to extract a witness is to take it away from the prover!

We also mention how our protocol avoids Vidick and Zhang’s more formal cloning implication for
proofs
of knowledge
[
33
]
.
Their technique relies on the non-destructivity to query the prover on every possible verifier message and learn its classical response. The resulting table of responses acts as an inefficient clone of the prover.

At that point, the extractor can be applied to each copy of the prover, extracting the prover’s witness twice.
In our case, the extraction and witness recovery modes have two disjoint, but indistinguishable sets of verifier messages. The prover’s behavior in the witness recovery mode could indeed be cloned, but it would not be possible to extract a witness from the cloned prover. In fact, the cloned prover would be able to distinguish between an
\mathsf{injective}
and
\mathsf{recovery}
mode
f_{\mathsf{pp}}
simply because it does not know how to respond when the verifier sends
f_{\mathsf{pp}}
in
\mathsf{injective}
mode.

2.2
\epsilon
-Repair with General Completeness.

In the more general setting of an arbitrary

Still, we have made some progress. In the original

\mathsf{CVQC}

Limiting to two possible outcomes enables a repair technique from

15

Suppose one had a state

Runtime Considerations.

CMSZ’s
\mathsf{Repair}
algorithm is extremely useful, but has a few important limitations.
First, the expected runtime scales with
N

N=2

Second,
\mathsf{Repair}

approximately

\epsilon

\epsilon

What Can CMSZ Repair?

The third and final limitation of
\mathsf{Repair}
is that it can only make strong repair guarantees for a particular set of “repairable” states.
If the original state
\ket{\psi}
was a superposition of two repairable states which were accepted with probability

As an example, imagine that
\ket{\psi}
was a superposition over a “good”
\mathsf{QMA}

The overall effect of repairing a state using CMSZ’s techniques can be thought of in three steps:

The primary factor determining the acceptance probability of the repaired state is the estimate in step 1. CMSZ requires a special estimation procedure that differs significantly from Marriot-Watrous’s

2.4
Amplifying Completeness and Soundness

Let us now focus on achieving negligible errors with a single copy of the witness (while potentially allowing the witness to eventually degrade by the end of the protocol).
To do this, we will have the prover and verifier sequentially repeat an
\epsilon
-non-destructive
\mathsf{CVQC}
, with completeness
c
and soundness
s
where
c-s\geq 1/{\sf poly}(|x|,{\lambda})
,
N
times, where
N
is a large enough polynomial in
({\lambda},|x|,{(c-s)}^{-1})
.
Such an
\epsilon
-non-destructive
\mathsf{CVQC}
can be obtained by applying the
\epsilon
-repair technique described in Section
2.2
to the base
\mathsf{CVQC}
described in Section
2.3
.

In our sequentially repeated protocol, the verifier will accept if and only if all the test rounds accept, and at least
\left(\frac{c+s}{2}\right)\cdot N
of the check rounds accept. If we start with an
\epsilon
“repairable” witness that is accepted with probability
p
, then after
N
executions, with overwhelming probability we are left with a witness that is accepted with probability
p-N\epsilon
, where we are able to set
N\epsilon
to be an arbitrarily small inverse polynomial value.
The degradation is small enough that an honest prover is still able to cause the verifier to accept in
\left(\frac{c+s}{2}\right)\cdot N
of the check rounds, except with negligible probability. This ensures negligible completeness error.

To argue that the protocol is a proof of knowledge, we will build an extractor that outputs a
\mathsf{QMA}
witness for
x
with oracle access to any prover that generates accepting transcripts for
x
with noticeable probability.
Our extractor will simply pick a random execution
j\leftarrow[N]
and run the extractor of the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}
on this execution: we will prove that this extractor succeeds with noticeable probability in finding a good
\mathsf{QMA}
witness for
x
.

To prove correctness of this extractor, we will rely on the extractability of the underlying
\mathsf{CVQC}
: namely, given any prover
P^{*}
that convinces
V_{\mathsf{T}}
to accept with probability close to
1
, and convinces
V_{\mathsf{C}}
to accept with probability noticeably larger than
s
, the underlying extractor outputs a
\mathsf{QMA}
witness with noticeable probability.

Then our goal is to simply prove that with noticeable probability, the session
j
picked by the outer extractor satisfies both the constraints above. We prove this in two parts: first, we

prove that for accepting transcripts, nearly all prefixes of the transcript satisfy the following: the prover, conditioned on this prefix, will pass
V_{\mathsf{T}}
with high probability in the upcoming round. We call such a transcript a
\mathsf{Good}
transcript.

Next, we prove that for nearly all
\mathsf{Good}
transcripts
\tau
, there is an
i
such that in session
i
, the prover convinces
V_{\mathsf{T}}
to accept with probability close to
1
as before, but additionally, passes
V_{\mathsf{C}}
with probability greater than
s
. This is proved by contradiction: suppose this were not the case, then we show that the number of check rounds that would accept will be smaller than
(c+s)/2
, and the transcript would be rejected by the verifier. This requires careful tail bounds for non-independent random variables, and in particular we are able to use Azuma’s inequality to obtain these bounds.

The proof of soundness proceeds similarly, by noting that for any prover that outputs accepting proofs with noticeable probability, there must exist a round where the prover passes the test round with probability close to
1
, and passes the check round with probability greater than
s
, which gives us a contradiction to the soundness of the base
\mathsf{CVQC}
.

This gives us a protocol that uses a single copy of the witness, and achieves negligible soundness and completeness errors, albeit at the cost of slightly degrading the witness. We can then apply the compiler described in Section
2.1

again
to the resulting protocol to make it witness preserving (i.e. with negligible disturbance to the witness state).

3.1
Quantum Computing

Let

A quantum algorithm
V

The probability of outcome

\|\Pi_{V}^{x}U_{V}(\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{0})\|^{2}

In the case where

V\coloneqq(I\otimes\bra{0})U_{V}^{\dagger}\Pi_{V}^{\mathsf{Accept}}U_{V}(I\otimes\ket{0})

Then the probability of accepting a state

is

\Tr[V\ket{\psi}]=\bra{\psi}V\ket{\psi}.

Any eigenvector of
V

V
with probability
p
. A general quantum channel may not be described in terms of a unitary, in which case we refer to a quantum algorithm that implements the channel, i.e. a unitary circuit
U_{\Phi}
and a measurement
\Pi_{\Phi}
, as a unitary dilation of the channel.

.

Lemma 1

(Mixed to Pure)
.

Let
V
be a binary-outcome measurement (not necessarily projective). Then any

\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\rho)]\geq 1-\gamma

can be decomposed as
\rho=q_{\mathsf{good}}\rho_{\mathsf{good}}+q_{\mathsf{bad}}\rho_{\mathsf{bad}}

q_{\mathsf{bad}}\leq\sqrt{\gamma}

and

\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\ket{\psi})]\geq 1-\sqrt{\gamma}

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may write

\rho=\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathinner{|{\psi_{i}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{i}}|}

for some orthogonal basis
\{\ket{\psi_{i}}\}_{i}
.
Let

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{good}}=\big\{i:~\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\ket{\psi_{i}}]\geq 1-\sqrt{\gamma}\big\}

and define
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bad}}
to be its complement.
Define

q_{\mathsf{good}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{good}}}q_{i}~~\mbox{ and }~~\rho_{\mathsf{good}}=q_{\mathsf{good}}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{good}}}q_{i}\mathinner{|{\psi_{i}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{i}}|}.

\rho_{\mathsf{bad}}

satisfies the second half of the claim.

Thus,

\displaystyle 1-\gamma\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i}q_{i}\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\ket{\psi_{i}})]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{good}}}q_{i}\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\ket{\psi_{i}})]+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bad}}}q_{i}\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow V(\ket{\psi_{i}})]\leq

Lemma 2

For any two Hermitian projectors

and in each two-dimensional subspace

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Pi_{A}
and
\Pi_{B}
be Hermitian projectors on a Hilbert space

\displaystyle\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\ket{\mathsf{jor}_{j,1}^{B}}

5
Dual-Mode Trapdoor Functions with State Recovery.

In this section we define the notion of a
dual-mode trapdoor function family with state recovery
. Intuitively, this is a family of (randomized) functions that have two modes: an
injective mode
and a
recovery mode
, and it is hard to distinguish between the two. Functions in the injective mode are injective and are associated with a trapdoor which can be used to invert the function efficiently. Functions in the recovery mode are also associated with a trapdoor, where this trapdoor is used to recover the state (this roughly corresponds to the lossy/two-to-one mode in the existing literature on dual-mode TCFs). More specifically, functions in recovery mode have the property that if we compute the function coherently on a quantum state and measure the output value then this does not disturb the state, and we can use the trapdoor to recover the quantum state.

Definition 11

(Dual-Mode Trapdoor Function Family with State Recovery.)
.

A

satisfying the following properties.

State-Preserving Arguments for
\mathsf{NP}

In this section, we construct an interactive argument for
\mathsf{NP}
with the property that if the (honest) prover starts with a state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{w}\alpha_{w}\ket{w}
which is a superposition over valid witnesses, then after running the protocol it is left with a state

. We call such an argument system a

Definition 12
.

A

for an
\mathsf{NP}
language
\mathcal{L}
is an interactive protocol between a
\mathsf{QPT}
prover
P
and a
{\sf PPT}

•

Completeness and State Preservation.

For every
x\in\mathcal{L}
and every superposition

over witnesses for
x
(encoded in the computational basis and potentially entangled with an external state
\ket{\phi_{w}}

\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
such that for every
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}

\Pr\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|}-\rho\big\|_{1}\leq\mu_{1}(\lambda)\right)~~\land~~\left(b=\mathsf{Accept}\right):~(\rho,b)\leftarrow\langle P(\ket{\psi}),V\rangle(1^{\lambda},x)\right]\geq 1-\mu_{2}(\lambda),

where
P
acts only on register
\mathcal{W}
and where the notation
(\rho,b)\leftarrow\langle P(\ket{\psi}),V\rangle(1^{\lambda},x)
means that
V
outputs
b\in\{\mathsf{Accept},\mathsf{Reject}\}
and
P
’s residual state is
\rho
.

When we wish to be more precise about the repair guarantees, we say that the argument is
\mu_{1}
-state-preserving.

•

Computational Soundness.

For every polynomial
\ell=\ell({\lambda})
, there exists a negligible function
\mu
such that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, every
x\notin\mathcal{L}
of size
\leq\ell(\lambda)
and every
\mathsf{QPT}
cheating prover
P^{*}
with auxiliary input
\ket{\psi}
consisting of at most
{\sf poly}(\ell(\lambda))

\Pr[\langle P^{*}(\ket{\psi},V\rangle(1^{\lambda},x)=\mathsf{Accept}]\leq\mu(\lambda)

We say a state-preserving argument for NP is an
\delta
-
argument of knowledge
if there exists a QPT extractor
\mathsf{Ext}
such that for every QPT adversarial prover
P^{*}
and every statement
x

\Pr[\mathsf{Accept}\leftarrow\langle P^{*},V\rangle(1^{\lambda},x)]\leq\Pr[(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}:w\leftarrow\mathsf{Ext}(P^{*})]+\delta

\sum_{w}\alpha_{w}\ket{w}^{\otimes n}

n

-state preserving.

Proof.

Since each repetition acts on disjoint registers from the others, the repetitions commute (for the purposes of determining the leftover state). Therefore we may consider running the repetitions one at a time. Let
\rho_{i}
be the leftover state after repetition

\rho_{0}=\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|}

By state-preservation and union bound,

for every

7.2
Completeness and Soundness

Claim 3
.

Let
(P,V)
be a
\mathsf{CVQC}
and let
(P^{\prime},V^{\prime})
be result of compiling
(P,V)
using
3
. If
(P,V)
has completeness
c
, then
(P^{\prime},V^{\prime})

.

Proof.

Let
\ket{w^{\prime}}
be the state after running
\sf ValEst
in step 1a. As noted previously, if
\ket{w}\in\mathsf{Repairable}_{p,\epsilon}(x)
, then with overwhelming probability

By the completeness of
(P,V)
, the prover’s measurement in step 5 outputs a verdict bit

with probability
\geq c
. Whenever this occurs, the prover’s state is supported on witnesses for

, so completeness of
\mathsf{SPA}_{\mathsf{NP}}
implies that the verifier accepts with overall probability

.
∎

.

Let
(P,V)
be a
\mathsf{CVQC}
and let
(P^{\prime},V^{\prime})
be result of compiling
(P,V)
using
3

\tau_{\lambda}\in\mathsf{Good}_{\lambda}
,

\sum_{i\in S}X_{i}>\left(s+\frac{{\lambda}^{d+1}}{n_{\mathsf{C}}}+\frac{{\lambda}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathsf{C}}}}\right)n_{\mathsf{C}}+\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)

Fix any transcript that satisfies the above equation.
By a Chernoff bound, with overwhelming probability,
n_{\mathsf{C}}\geq\frac{N}{3}
, and therefore for all but a negligible fraction of such transcripts,

\frac{\sum_{i\in n_{\mathsf{C}}}X_{i}}{n_{\mathsf{C}}}\geq\left(s+3\frac{{\lambda}^{d+1}}{N}+3\frac{{\lambda}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

Recall that
N=\left(\frac{{\lambda}^{d+2}}{(c-s)^{2}}\right)
, thus the fraction
\frac{\sum_{i\in n_{\mathsf{C}}}X_{i}}{n_{\mathsf{C}}}
is at most

\left(s+3\frac{{\lambda}^{d+1}(c-s)^{2}}{{\lambda}^{d+2}}+3\frac{{\lambda}(c-s)}{{\lambda}^{3/2}}\right)

which, for large enough
{\lambda}
, is at most

\left(s+\frac{c-s}{3}\right)\leq\left(\frac{c+s}{2}\right)

and therefore this noticeable fraction of
\mathsf{Good}
transcripts
\tau
are not accepted, a contradiction.
∎

Combining Claim
7
and Claim
9
, we have that with probability
(1-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right))
over
\tau_{{\lambda}}\in\mathsf{Good}_{{\lambda}}
, there exists
j\in[N]
such that:

\Pr\left[P^{*}_{j}(\rho_{j-1}),V_{j,\mathsf{T},\mathsf{NDCVQC}}(1^{\lambda},x)=(\cdot,1)\big|\tau_{j-1}\right]>\left(1-\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{d}}\right)

and

\Pr\left[P^{*}_{j}(\rho_{j-1}),V_{j,\mathsf{C},\mathsf{NDCVQC}}(1^{\lambda},x)=(\cdot,1)|\tau_{j-1}\right]>s+\frac{1}{p({\lambda})}

and furthermore, the event
\tau_{\lambda}\in\mathsf{Good}_{\lambda}
happens with probability
\frac{1}{p({\lambda})}-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
.

Thus, an extractor that randomly picks a sequential round
j\leftarrow[N]
and runs the extraction procedure of the underlying CVQC on round
j
will, with probability

\frac{\delta}{pN}({\lambda})-\mathsf{negl}\left(\lambda\right)
, where
\delta({\lambda})
is the (non-negligible) probability that the underlying extractor succeeds.
