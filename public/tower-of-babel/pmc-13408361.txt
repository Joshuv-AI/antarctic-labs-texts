Comparative Evaluation of Using Binaural Noise and Virtual Reality in Dental Management of Anxious Children: An In Vivo Study
Authors: Praveen K Bali, Tanya Sinha, Mallikarjuna Kenchappa, KP Bharath, Deeksha Sharma, Deepti V Balehosur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of virtual reality (VR) and binaural noise as distraction techniques for managing dental anxiety in pediatric patients.Subjects and methodsForty-four healthy children aged 5–8 years, with mild-to-moderate anxiety (Frankle's rating 2 and 3), participated. Each child was exposed to three different distraction methods on separate visits: (1) control (no aid), (2) binaural noise, and (3) VR. Anxiety levels were measured using Venham's picture test and pulse oximetry before and after local anesthesia (LA).Statistical analysisThe values were recorded in Microsoft Excel sheets, and the data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 software. Analysis of the data between groups was carried out using repeated measures analysis of variance (RANOVA) to analyze the effects of intervention, Chi-square test, and paired t-test for differences between groups.ResultsBoth VR and binaural noise significantly reduced anxiety compared to the control group. The VR group showed the most pronounced reduction in pulse rate and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, indicating lower anxiety and stress. The binaural noise group showed moderate anxiety reduction, whereas the control group experienced a significant increase in anxiety post-LA. Statistical analysis confirmed VR's superior effectiveness in reducing anxiety.ConclusionVR was the most effective in reducing dental anxiety, likely due to its immersive, multisensory experience. Binaural noise also reduced anxiety but to a lesser extent. The study suggests that nonpharmacological techniques, particularly VR, can significantly improve pediatric dental care by reducing anxiety and enhancing the treatment experience for young patients.Keywords: Anxiolysis, Binaural noise, Virtual reality

Introduction

McElroy (1895) defined behavior management, emphasizing the importance of a child's emotional state postdental appointment.1 Dental fear and anxiety (DFA) are common, affecting up to 20% of children and leading to avoidance of care.2,3 Behavior guidance techniques range from conventional methods to advanced options such as virtual reality (VR) and binaural beats. VR provides distraction by immersing patients in a virtual environment, reducing sensory input from the dental setting.3,4 Binaural beats create cognitive and behavioral responses through brainwave entrainment. Brainwaves, measured by electroencephalography (EEG), are classified into five groups based on frequency and influence human behavior.5

Subjects and Methods

The study design consists of 43 healthy children who rated 2 and 3 on Frankle's behavior rating scale and in the age-group of 5–8 years from the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, College of Dental Sciences, Davanagere, Karnataka, India. The study was conducted over a period of 0 yrs to 6 months after obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board (CODS/IEC/25/2023-24) and informed consent from the participants. A form of consent was also given to the parents, and the procedures were started only after taking their consent. Children who required local anesthesia (LA) for any procedure were included in the study. The study excluded all the children who had mental and developmental delays, Frankle's rating 1 or 4, children who completely refused to put VR device during the study.

There were three groups taken in the study: (1) control group, (2) binaural noise, and (3) VR, and all 43 children were included in all three groups. Each child was asked to visit three times and, on each visit, the child was exposed to a different distraction aid.

On the first visit, the child was taken up for LA without any aid. The scores from Venham's picture test and pulse oximeter were recorded both before and after the procedure was concluded. These scores were taken as V1, V1' and P1, P1', respectively.

On the second visit, the children were introduced to binaural noise and pulse, and Venham's score was similarly recorded as V2, V2' and P2, P2'.

On the third visit, the children were exposed to VR, and the scores were recorded as V3, V3' and P3, P3'.

The distraction aids were introduced 5 minutes before beginning the treatment procedure for the child to get accustomed to the gadgets. Figure 1 represents armamentarium used in the study.

Statistical Analysis

The values were recorded in Microsoft Excel sheets, and the data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 software. Analysis of the data between groups was carried out using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to analyze the effects of intervention, chi-square test to determine the difference between age and gender, and paired t-test to compare before and after values within groups.

Results

The study included 43 participants with an average age of 6.51 years [standard deviation (SD) = 1.261], ranging from 5 to 8 years. The gender distribution was nearly even, with 48.8% male (n = 21) and 51.1% female (n = 22) participants. The statistical analysis using the Chi-square test revealed no significant differences in gender distribution (p = 0.879) or age variability (p = 0.152), indicating a well-balanced demographic representation in the study as shown in Table 1.

Table 2 presents the mean pulse rates and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores before and after LA administration in three groups: (1) control, (2) binaural, and (3) VR. The pulse rates before LA were similar across groups, but after LA, a significant increase was observed in the control and binaural groups, with p-values < 0.001. The VR group showed a smaller increase in pulse rate. Regarding VAS scores, both the control and binaural groups exhibited an increase after LA, while the VR group showed a decrease. These findings, significant at p < 0.001, suggest that VR may be effective in reducing pain perception and stress during LA administration compared to control and binaural groups.

Table 3 presents paired comparisons of pulse rates and VAS scores before and after LA administration within each group. In the control and binaural groups, there was no statistically significant change in pulse rates after LA (p-values of 0.14 and 0.31, respectively), while the VR group showed a nonsignificant decrease in pulse rate (p = 0.48). However, VAS scores indicated a significant increase in pain perception post-LA in the control group (p < 0.05), whereas the binaural and VR groups experienced significant decreases in VAS scores, particularly in the VR group (p < 0.001). These results highlight VR's potential in effectively lowering pain perception and stress during dental procedures compared to other methods.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of VR and binaural noise as distraction techniques in reducing anxiety among pediatric dental patients. The findings revealed that both VR and binaural noise significantly lowered anxiety levels, with VR showing a more pronounced effect. The reduction in anxiety in the VR group was likely due to the immersive and engaging nature of VR,6 which effectively diverted the child's attention from the dental procedure by creating an interactive virtual environment. This aligns with existing research that supports the use of VR in medical settings, where it has been shown to effectively reduce anxiety by creating a strong sense of presence in a virtual world.7 Binaural noise also demonstrated an ability to reduce anxiety, though to a lesser extent compared to VR. This may be because binaural noise, relying solely on auditory stimulation, does not engage the child's senses as fully as the multisensory experience provided by VR.8 The effectiveness of VR in this study underscores its potential as a powerful tool for managing pediatric dental anxiety, whereas binaural noise offers a simpler alternative when VR may not be feasible.

This study utilized the Venham's picture test and pulse rate to determine the anxiety levels of a child, as the picture selection test is a useful tool for those interested in measuring young children's responses to stressful situations.9 Finger pulse volume (FPV) is used as measure of anxiety in response to threat of physical harm. According to Smith et al., FPV was sensitive to the stress manipulation during both periods and was correlated with self-reported anxiety during the anticipation period.10

In the present study, it was evaluated that there was a significant decrease in the anxiety after using binaural noise. The results of the study are in accordance with the studies done by Singh et al.,11 Sivakumar et al.,12 Lahmann et al.,13 Prabhakar et al.,14 and Marwah et al.15

A highly significant reduction in anxiety levels was also observed among children exposed to VR, which is consistent with the findings of studies conducted by Ram et al.,6 Gómez-Polo et al.,7 Perales et al.,8 and Custódio et al.7 The limitations of this study include the small sample size, limited age range, short-term evaluation period, and inclusion of participants from a single institution. In addition, anxiety assessment was based only on pulse rate and Venham's picture test/VAS scores, and possible carryover effects due to repeated exposure to interventions could not be eliminated. Further multicentric studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up are recommended.

Conclusion

The study concludes that both VR and binaural noise are effective nonpharmacological interventions for managing dental anxiety in pediatric patients, with VR demonstrating superior effectiveness. The significant reduction in both physiological and subjective measures of anxiety in the VR group suggests that VR could be a valuable tool in pediatric dental settings, enhancing patient cooperation and overall treatment experience. Binaural noise, while less effective than VR, still offers a viable alternative, particularly in settings where VR technology is unavailable. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of distraction techniques in pediatric dentistry to create a more positive and less stressful experience for young patients.
