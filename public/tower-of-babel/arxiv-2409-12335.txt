Bridging the Gap Between Approximation and Learning via Optimal Approximation by ReLU MLPs of Maximal Regularity
Ruiyang Hong, Anastasis Kratsios †

Abstract
The foundations of deep learning are supported by the seemingly opposing perspectives of approximation or learning theory. The former advocates for large/expressive models that need not generalize, while the latter considers classes that generalize but may be too small/constrained to be universal approximators. Motivated by real-world deep learning implementations that are both expressive and statistically reliable, we ask: “Is there a class of neural networks that is both large enough to be universal but structured enough to generalize?” This paper constructively provides a positive answer to this question by identifying a highly structured class of ReLU multilayer perceptions (MLPs), which are optimal function approximators and are statistically well-behaved. We show that any ( L , α ) (L,\alpha) -Hölder function from [ 0 , 1 ] d [0,1]^{d} to [ − n , n ] [-n,n] can be approximated to a uniform 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) error on [ 0 , 1 ] d [0,1]^{d} with a sparsely connected ReLU MLP with the same Hölder exponent α \alpha and coefficient L L , of width 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ n d / α ) \mathcal{O}(dn^{d/\alpha}) , depth 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( d ) ) \mathcal{O}(\log(d)) , with 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ n d / α ) \mathcal{O}(dn^{d/\alpha}) nonzero parameters, and whose weights and biases take values in { 0 , ± 1 / 2 } \{0,\pm 1/2\} except in the first and last layers which instead have magnitude at-most n n . Unlike previously known “large” classes of universal ReLU MLPs, the empirical Rademacher complexity of our class remains bounded even when its depth and width become arbitrarily large. Our class of MLPs achieves a near-optimal sample complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( N ) / N ) \mathcal{O}(\log(N)/\sqrt{N}) when given N N i.i.d. normalized sub-Gaussian training samples. We achieve this through a new construction that perfectly fits together linear pieces using Kuhn triangulations, along with a new proof technique which shows that our construction preserves the regularity of not only the Hölder functions, but also any uniformly continuous function. Our results imply that neural networks can solve the McShane extension problem on suitable finite sets.

Lipschitz Neural Networks, Optimal Approximation, Generalization Bounds, Optimal Interpolation, Kuhn Triangulation, Universal Approximation, McShane Extension Theorem, Lipschitz Extension.

(a)

Our Strategy - “Maximally Regular” Networks: The networks constructed in Theorem
1.1
interpolate a given grid linearly with the optimal slope between any two grid points. These ReLU MLPs constructed from finite sample values of the target function can never have Lipschitz constant (or, more generally, modulus of regularity, as shown in Lemma

(b)

Standard Approach - “Spiky” Networks: Following methods such as

Remark 1.1

(Approximation of Smooth Functions)
.

The approximate piecewise polynomial variant of this construction sketched in Figure

Remark 1.2

(Approximation in Lipschitz Norm)
.

Theorem
1.1
guarantees that any bounded
L
-Lipschitz function can be uniformly approximated by bounded ReLU MLPs whose Lipschitz constant
never exceeds

L

L
-Lipschitz functions in the Banach space of continuous functions on the
d

C([0,1]^{d})
.
Our result
does not
claim that one can

with ReLU MLPs with respect to the Lipschitz norm
\|f\|_{Lip}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}|f(\mathbf{x})|+\operatorname{Lip}(f)

Memorization/Interpolation with ReLU MLPs of Minimal Lipschitz Constant

The memorization (i.e. interpolation) problem for the MLP model dates back, at least, to

\mathbb{R}^{d}

The following is a shallow version of our main

Theorem 1.3

(Optimal Interpolation with ReLU MLPs of Maximal Regularity (Shallow Version))
.

Let
((x_{n},y_{n}))_{n=1}^{N}
be distinct pairs of training datapoints in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
(with
x_{n}<x_{n+1}
for
n=1,\dots,N-1
). There exists a ReLU MLP
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of width at most
2\lceil\sqrt{N}\rceil
, depth
2
, and with at most
2N+8\lceil\sqrt{N}\rceil
nonzero parameters such that

\Phi(x_{n})=y_{n}\qquad\mbox{ for }n=1,\dots,N

Furthermore,
\Phi
is linear on the intervals
[x_{n},x_{n+1}]
for
n=1,\dots,N-1
, and constant on
(-\infty,x_{1}]
and
[x_{N},\infty)
.

This (shallow) version of our main memorization/interpolation theorem matches the optimal (in the sense of VC-dimension) parameter usage of a ReLU interpolator, as in
[

]
. The construction of
[
88
]

It is worth noting that, in
[

, the authors recently constructed interpolating ReLU MLPs, which also obtain the optimal Lipschitz constant (with respect to the

norm) in the multi-dimensional case. Those networks, however, require depth

and width

Our main one-dimensional

55
,
29
,
78
]
.
Guarantees that networks can approximate a function while also approximating its (at least first) derivative date back to
[
39
]
with more recent quantitative guarantees being given by
[
33
,
34
,
11
,
63
]
(not all of which use the
\operatorname{ReLU}
activation function). Recall that, by the mean value theorem, every once continuously differentiable function is Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{d}
with its Lipschitz constant given by the maximum norm of its gradient thereon; and, as a partial converse, every Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is differentiable almost-everywhere on
[0,1]^{d}
; see
[
22
, Theorem 3.1.6]
.
Thus, for the subclass of Lipschitz functions which are once continuously differentiable, these results guarantee that MLPs can approximate these maps on
[0,1]^{p}
while also approximating their Lipschitz constant.

However, there is no guarantee that MLPs can approximate these functions while exactly implementing their Lipschitz constant even locally. More generally, there is no result that MLPs can approximate functions of lower regularity (e.g. Hölder of sub-Hölder functions) while also exactly matching their Lipschitz constant
globally
.

2.4
Global Universal Approximation

Theorem
4.1
provides exactly this guarantee showing, in particular, that
\operatorname{ReLU}
MLPs can
globally
implement concave moduli of continuity of any uniformly continuous functions (e.g. Hölder or Lipschitz functions) while locally approximating them in
[0,1]^{d}
. A global guarantee of the extrapolation rate for an approximation on
[0,1]^{d}
will also be provided in Corollary
5.2
, and this is possible due to our guarantee that we may exactly and globally match the modulus of continuity of the target function being approximated.

We note that there do exist
qualitative
global approximation theorems in the deep learning
[
16
]
, reservoir computing
[
32
]
, and Stone-Weirestrass type
[
27
]
approximation literature. However, although each of those results is qualitative, it is currently not known what the extrapolation rates are for deep learning models, which are only guaranteed to provide a uniform approximation on
[0,1]^{d}
.

2.5
Deficits of Parametric Generalization Bounds for Overparameterized MLPs

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, the Rademacher complexity bounds of
[
9
, Theorems 8 and 12]
and
[
7
]
, together with some result on random matrices with independent rows in
[
89
, Theorem 4.6.1]
(see Appendix
7.5.1
for details) can only imply the following much weaker bound: for each
0<\delta<1
the following holds

\sup_{\Phi\in\mathcal{NN}_{\Delta,W}^{L,n}}\big|\mathcal{R}(\Phi)-\mathcal{R}_{N}(\Phi)\big|\in\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)}}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\biggl(\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}+\frac{W^{3\Delta/2}\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)}}{\sqrt{N}}\biggr)

(2.2)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
We also note that, the nearly optimal VC-bounds derived in
[
8
]
for ReLU MLPs also diverge when
W
and
\Delta
tend to infinity.

3.2
Uniform Regularity

We first overview the standard notions of uniform continuity before introducing their discontinuous generalizations for which our approximation results hold.

3.2.1
Uniform, Lipschitz, and Hölder Continuity

A map
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is called a modulus of continuity if
\omega
is monotonically increasing and continuous (from the right) at
0
with
\omega(0)=0
.
Let
(X,\rho_{X})
and
(Y,\rho_{Y})
be metric spaces and
f:X\to Y
. We say that
\omega
is a modulus of continuity for
f
if: for each
x,\tilde{x}\in X

\rho_{Y}(f(x),f(\tilde{x}))\leq\omega(\rho_{X}(x,\tilde{x})).

We often consider uniformly continuous functions which are either Lipschitz and Hölder continuous.
If
L\geq 0
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
, and
\omega(x)=Lx^{\alpha}
, then we will say that
f
is
\alpha
-Hölder with constant
L
.
The class of all such
f:X\supseteq\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\operatorname{Hol}(L,\alpha,\Omega)
.
If
\alpha=1
, we say that
f
is
L
-Lipschitz. Given any real-valued function
f
on a subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use
\operatorname{Lip}(f)
to denote its optimal Lipschitz constant with respect to the restriction of the
\ell^{1}
-norm to
X
; i.e.

\operatorname{Lip}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X\\
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{1}}.

We are mostly interested in the case where
X
is a subset of the space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\ell_{1})
and
(Y,\rho_{Y})
is a Euclidean line; where, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\ell_{p}
indicates that we are equipping
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the metric induced by the
\ell^{p}
norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}^{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\,|x_{i}|^{p}
if
p
is finite and
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\max_{i=1,\dots,d}\,|x_{i}|
otherwise.

3.2.2
Uniform Regularity, Best Achievable Approximation Error, and Discontinuity

We consider the following broad class of (possibly discontinuous) target functions, including uniformly continuous functions.
The reader which is only interested in approximation guarantees for continuous functions,
where an asymptotic error of
0
is achievable by ReLU MLPs
, is encouraged to skip this section and swap the term “modulus of regularity” for “concave modulus of continuity” in the remainder of the paper.
We rely on the following weakened version of a concave modulus of continuity.

Definition 3.1

(Modulus of Regularity)
.

Let
I=[0,\infty)
or
I=[0,T]
for some
T\geq 0
.
A function
\omega:I\to[0,\infty)
is called a modulus of regularity if:

1.

\omega(0)=0
,

2.

\omega
is monotone increasing,

3.

\omega
is concave.

Moduli of regularity allow us to quantify the best achievable approximation error, when uniformly approximating discontinuous target functions.
Before formalizing this, we recall that concave functions on bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are continuous on their interior but need not be on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
. For example, the indicator function
I_{(0,\infty)}
of
(0,\infty)
is concave on
\Omega=[0,\infty)
, continuous on
(0,\infty)
, and has a discontinuity on
\partial\Omega=\{0\}
.
We will often consider the
best achievable approximation error

\omega^{\star}\in[0,\infty)
, when minimizing such a modulus, which is given by

\omega^{\star}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\,\omega(t).

Example 3.1

(Concave Moduli of Continuity are Moduli of Regularity)
.

If
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a concave modulus of continuity, then
\omega
is a modulus of regularity, with
\omega(0)=0
, and
\omega
is continuous at
0
; i.e.
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\,\omega(t)=0
and in particular
\omega^{\star}=0
.

The previous example illustrates the main difference between moduli of continuity and moduli of regularity; namely, the value of
0
need not be achievable by minimizing
\omega
from the right. Indeed, if
\omega
is a modulus of regularity, then it is non-negative and it fixes
0
; thus,
\omega(t)\geq\omega(0)=0
for all positive values of
t
. Consequentially,

\omega(0)=0\leq\omega^{\star}

(3.1)

for any modulus of regularity. This inequality can be strict for discontinuous functions.

Example 3.2

(Beyond Moduli of Continuity)
.

Fix
M>0
and
L\geq 0
.
The function
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
given for each
t\in[0,\infty)
by

\omega(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}M+L\,t&\mbox{ if }t>0\\
0&\mbox{ if }t=0\end{cases}

is a modulus of regularity but not a modulus of continuity, and the inequality in (
3.1
) is strict.

The class of continuous functions between metric spaces are precisely those functions for which: for every compact subset of their domain, there is a modulus of continuity bounding the distance between the images of all pairs of points therein, see e.g.
[
61
, Theorem 27.6]
, and such that
the images of arbitrarily close points are themselves arbitrarily close
.

If one relaxes the second condition, we are left with the following functions considered in our paper. Our interest in this class stems from (
3.1
), wherein our main result (Theorem
4.1
) shows that for
\omega
-regular function can be approximated by a ReLU MLP with at-most
\omega
-regularity, up to the best achievable approximation error
\omega^{\star}
. In the very special case where the target function is additionally continuous, we can guarantee the usual type of conclusion: our ReLU MLP approximators can achieve
zero approximation error
asymptotically.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\begin{aligned} h(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}1,\quad x\geq 0\\
0,\quad x<0\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Figure 3
:
The heaviside function
h
is
\omega
-regular with
\omega(t)=1
if
t>0
and
0
otherwise.

Definition 3.2

(
\omega
-Regular Functions)
.

Let
\omega
be a modulus of regularity,
(X,\rho)
and
(Y,\rho^{\prime})
be metric spaces. A map
f:X\to Y
is said to have
\omega
-regularity at a pair of points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
if

\rho^{\prime}\big(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\big)\leq\omega\big(\rho(x,x^{\prime})\big).

If, moreover,
f
has
\omega
-regularity at all pairs of points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
then, we way that
f
is
\omega
-regular (on
X
). The set of all
\omega
-regular functions from
(X,\rho)
to
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
is denoted by
\operatorname{Lip}_{\omega}(X)
.

We will say that a function is
regular
if it is
\omega
-regular for some modulus of regularity
\omega
.
Geometric examples of discontinuous regular functions include quasi-isometric
[
49
]
and certain coarse embeddings
[
66
,
58
]
. Discontinuous regular maps can also be constricted as additive perturbations of Lipschitz functions by some “deterministic bounded noise”, as follows.

Example 3.3

(Additive Perturbations of Lipschitz Functions)
.

Fix
L,M\geq 0
and fix any function
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to[-M/2,M/2]
. For any
L
-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
define the perturbed function
f_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as sending any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to

f_{\eta}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}f(\mathbf{x})+\eta(\mathbf{x}).

Then,
f_{\eta}
is
\omega
-regular with modulus of regularity as in Example
3.2
.

Regular functions need not be well-behaved measure-theoretically. For instance, in the context of Example
3.3
if
A
is the Vitali set in
\mathbb{R}
and
\eta=\frac{M}{2}\,I_{A}
then we see that

need not even be Lebesgue measurable even if

was Lipschitz.

Example 3.4

(Not all Functions are Regular)
.

Not all functions are regular. The following map is not regular:
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
given for each
x\in[0,1]
by

f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{x}&\mbox{ if }x>0\\
0&\mbox{ if }x=0.\end{cases}

However, any bounded function is regular. Nevertheless, their best achievable approximation error can be large.

Definition 3.3

.

Given a ReLU MLP

Let

4.2

Next, we investigate the learning theoretic implications of our main result.

\mathcal{R}(\Phi)

Assumption 4.1

Unlike the generalization bound in (

On the contrary, several recent results studying gradient dynamics of “infinitely wide” neural networks suggest that highly overparameterized neural networks (which may be extremely deep and wide) tend to generalize well and tend to converge to highly regular networks after being optimized by gradient descent on the training data. 5.1
Approximation Theory: Extrapolation Rates

Our main result guarantees that the approximating ReLU MLP

has a (modulus of) regularity which is no worse than that of the target function. This allows us to deduce a sharp extrapolation rate for our approximator outside of
[0,1]^{d}
.

We can thus directly obtain a version of a

. However, our result differs from that of
[
16
]

but weaker than the uniform topology thereon on suitable subspaces; e.g. the strict topology on continuous bounded functions studied by

,
27
,
84
]
. Instead, we do not discount/compress our error using weight functions outside the unit cube but rater we quantify how fast it grows, using the moduli of
both
the target function and its neural network approximator.

The first step towards this result is given by our first consequence of Theorem
4.1
. Namely, this first corollary shows that we can easily extend the domain of the interpolator
\Phi
in Theorem

to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

6.1
The Kuhn Triangulation is Unique for Regular Approximation

The multi-dimensional analogue of the cartoon of our one-dimensional regular ReLU approximator illustrated in Figure

, relies on a specific triangulation of the

. This triangular called the

, defined shortly afterwards, is the most “regular” triangulation in the sense that it is the only triangulation whose continuous piecewise linear approximators always preserve the regularity of the function that it approximates under some mild conditions; see
[

. This triangulation is used in one of the key technical steps in proving Theorem

Figure 4
:
Kuhn triangulations of a 2D (left) and a 3D (right) unit cube.

Definition 6.1

(Kuhn triangulation)

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, the
d!
different simplices

\operatorname{conv}\left(\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{\tau(i)}:0\leq k\leq d\right\}\right),\quad\tau\in S_{d}

It is natural to ask if another triangulation could have been used during the proof of our main result. Surprisingly, this is not the case as any other triangulation, other than reflections of the Kuhn triangulation, yield approximators with larger Lipschitz constants. This is the content of the following result which serves as a type of converse to Lemma
7.8
.

Theorem 6.1

(Uniqueness of the Kuhn Triangulation - up to Reflections)
.

The Kuhn triangulation is the only triangulation (up to reflections) that makes Lemma

.

6d+3
and depth
11d+3
.

Proof.

We label the nodes as
n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{2d+1}
, and let

D(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{2d+1}|x-n_{i}|

be the total distance from
x
to all the nodes. Then,
D
attains its minimum exactly at the median since there are odd number of nodes. We will use the following algorithm to compute the median:

1.

Compute
x_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\min\{n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{2d+1}\}

2.

Compute
s_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\min\{D(n_{1}),D(n_{2}),\cdots,D(n_{2d+1})\}

3.

For
i=1,2,\cdots,d
, let
\displaystyle x_{i}=x_{i-1}+\frac{D(x_{i-1})-s_{0}}{2d+1-2i}

4.

Output
x_{d}

We first show the correctness of this algorithm. We relabel the nodes as
m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\cdots\leq m_{2d+1}
and proceed by induction on
i
. For
i=0
,
x_{0}=m_{1}\in[m_{1},m_{d+1}]
. For
i=k
, suppose
x_{k}\in[m_{k+1},m_{d+1}]
for some
k\leq d-1
, we will show that
x_{k+1}\in[m_{k+2},m_{d+1}]
.

If
x_{k}\in[m_{k+1},m_{k+2}]
, then noting that for
r\leq d
,

D(m_{r})-D(m_{r+1})=(2(d-r)+1)(m_{r+1}-m_{r})

and the same holds when
m_{r+1}
changes to any
x\in[m_{r},m_{r+1}]
, in particular:

D(m_{k+1})-D(x_{k})=(2(d-r-1)+1)(x_{k}-m_{k+1})

so we have

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\frac{D(x_{k})-s_{0}}{2d-1-2k}=x_{k}+\frac{D(x_{k})-D(m_{d+1})}{2(d-k)-1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\frac{D(m_{k+1})-(2(d-k-1)+1)(x_{k}-m_{k+1})-D(m_{d+1})}{2(d-k)-1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-(x_{k}-m_{k+1})+\frac{D(m_{k+1})-D(m_{d+1})}{2(d-k)-1}

\displaystyle=m_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2(d-k)-1}\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}(2j-1)(m_{d+2-j}-m_{d+1-j})

then

x_{k+1}\leq m_{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}(m_{d+2-j}-m_{d+1-j})=m_{d+1}

x_{k+1}\geq m_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2(d-k)-1}\sum_{j=d-k}^{d-k}(2j-1)(m_{d+2-j}-m_{d+1-j})=m_{k+2}

hence
x_{k+1}\in[m_{k+2},m_{d+1}]
.

Otherwise,
x_{k}\in[m_{k+2},m_{d+1}]
, then suppose
x_{k}\in[m_{k^{\prime}+1},m_{k^{\prime}+2}]
for some
k<k^{\prime}\leq d-1
, so we know from above that

x_{k}+\frac{D(x_{k})-D(m_{d+1})}{2d-1-2k^{\prime}}\leq m_{d+1}

thus

m_{k+2}\leq x_{k}\leq x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\frac{D(x_{k})-D(m_{d+1})}{2d-1-2k}\leq x_{k}+\frac{D(x_{k})-D(m_{d+1})}{2d-1-2k^{\prime}}\leq m_{d+1}

hence
x_{k+1}\in[m_{k+2},m_{d+1}]
, so we completed the induction step.

Therefore,
x_{i}\in[m_{i+1},m_{d+1}]
for
i=1,2,\cdots,d
, so
x_{d}\in[m_{d+1},m_{d+1}]
,
x_{d}=m_{d+1}
, which is the median.

In the following, we will use this algorithm to construct our ReLU MLP. In the rest of this proof, column vectors represent layers of neurons, and functions apply to them entry-wise, whose outputs are still column vectors.

Step 1
: Compute
x_{0}
and
s_{0}

It is easy to see that

x=\sigma(x)-\sigma(-x),\quad|x|=\sigma(x)+\sigma(-x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}

then

\min\{x,y\}=\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{|x-y|}{2}=\frac{\sigma(x+y)}{2}-\frac{\sigma(-x-y)}{2}-\frac{\sigma(x-y)}{2}-\frac{\sigma(-x+y)}{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}

so we can use this formula to construct the following building block for computing
s_{0}
: denote the column vector
\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}&n_{2}&\cdots&n_{2d+1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
as
\mathbf{n}
, and let
\mathbf{n}_{k}
be the column vector obtained from
\mathbf{n}
by deleting its
k
th row, then

D(n_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}\cdot[|n_{k}-\mathbf{n}_{k}|]=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\sigma(n_{k}-\mathbf{n}_{k})\\
\sigma(\mathbf{n}_{k}-n_{k})\end{bmatrix}

Using these results, we can compute
x_{0}
and
d_{0}
via the following network:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{n_{i}}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{D(n_{i})}\end{bmatrix}\

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{n_{i}}\\
\sigma(n_{k}-\mathbf{n}_{k})\\
\sigma(\mathbf{n}_{k}-n_{k})\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{D(n_{i})}\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\sigma\left(\pm\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{n_{i}}\pm n_{k}\right)\\
D(n_{k})\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{D(n_{i})}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}{n_{i}}\\
\sigma\Big(\pm\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{D(n_{i})}\pm D(n_{k})\Big)\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}{n_{i}}\\
\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}{D(n_{i})}\end{bmatrix}

where
\sigma(\pm a\pm b)
abbreviates the four neurons
\sigma(a+b),\sigma(a-b),\sigma(-a+b)
and
\sigma(-a-b)
. Note that the terms without the
\sigma
are non-negative and thus are unaffected by the activation function
\sigma
. Therefore, by connecting
2d
such networks we can output
x_{0}=\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{n_{i}}
and
s_{0}=\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq k-1}{D(n_{i})}
. Its width is dominated by the second layer in the above, which is
(2d+1)+1+2d+2d+1=6d+3
, and its depth is
4(2d+1)=8d+4
(not counting the first layer, which is the input layer).

Step 2
: Compute
x_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,d

We use the following network:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
x_{k-1}\\
s_{0}\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\sigma(x_{k-1}-\mathbf{n})\\
\sigma(\mathbf{n}-x_{k-1})\\
x_{k-1}\\
s_{0}\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
D(x_{k-1})\\
x_{k-1}\\
s_{0}\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{n}\\
\displaystyle x_{k-1}+\frac{D(x_{k-1})-s_{0}}{2d+1-2k}=x_{k}\\
s_{0}\end{bmatrix}

Therefore, by connecting
d
such networks we can output
x_{d}
, which is the median as proven above. Its width is dominated by the second layer in the above, which is
(2d+1)+2d+2d+1+1=6d+3
, and its depth is
3d-1
(not counting the first and the last layer, since the first layer is shared with the network in the previous step, and the last layer is the output).

Connecting the two network in steps 1 and 2 gives the desired ReLU MLP for computing the median of non-negative inputs. In total, this network has width
6d+3
and depth
(8d+4)+(3d-1)=11d+3
.

∎

Lemma 7.3

(Median function preserves regularity)
.

If
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}\in\operatorname{Hol}(\nu,\alpha,X)
are Lipschitz functions from a metric space
X
to
\mathbb{R}
, then
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\operatorname{median}(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n})\in\operatorname{Hol}(\nu,\alpha,X)
.

Proof.

Take any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X
, then
|f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-f_{i}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\nu\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{X}^{\alpha}=:C
for all
i
. Let
y_{i}:[0,1]\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
be a linear function such that
y_{i}(0)=f_{i}(\mathbf{x}),y_{i}(1)=f_{i}(\mathbf{y})
for all
i
, then
y_{i}^{\prime}\leq C
for all
i
. For any
t\in[0,1]
, let
p(t)=(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n})
, where
s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n}
is a permutation of
1,2,\cdots,n
such that
f_{s_{1}}(t)\leq f_{s_{2}}(t)\leq\cdots\leq f_{s_{n}}(t)
, and
s_{i}\leq s_{j}
whenever
f_{s_{i}}(t)=f_{s_{j}}(t)
. Consider any
1\leq i<j\leq n
, there are two cases:

1.

If
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-f_{i}(\mathbf{y})=f_{j}(\mathbf{x})-f_{j}(\mathbf{y})
, then
y_{i}^{\prime}\equiv y_{j}^{\prime}
,
y_{i}(t)-y_{j}(t)
is a constant, so the relative position of
i
and
j
in
p(t)
does not change as
t
goes from
0
to 1;

2.

If
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})-f_{i}(\mathbf{y})\neq f_{j}(\mathbf{x})-f_{j}(\mathbf{y})
, then as
t
goes from
0
to 1,
y_{i}(t)
and
y_{j}(t)
coincide at most once, so the relative position of
i
and
j
in
p(t)
changes at most once.

Therefore,
p(t)
changes at most
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
times as
t
goes from
0
to 1, and the points where
p(t)
changes split
[0,1]
into finite intervals. Let
y(t)
be the median of
y_{1}(t),\cdots,y_{n}(t)
, then in each of these intervals:

1.

If
n
is odd, then
y(t)=y_{i}(t)
for some fixed
i
, so
|y^{\prime}(t)|=|y_{i}^{\prime}(t)|\leq C
;

2.

If
n
is even, then
y(t)=\frac{y_{i}(t)}{2}+\frac{y_{j}(t)}{2}
for some fixed
i
and
j
, so
|y^{\prime}(t)|\leq\left|\left(\frac{y_{i}(t)}{2}\right)^{\prime}\right|+\left|\left(\frac{y_{j}(t)}{2}\right)^{\prime}\right|\leq\frac{C}{2}+\frac{C}{2}=C
.

Therefore,
|y^{\prime}(t)|\leq C
in each of these intervals, then
|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|=|y(1)-y(0)|\leq C=\nu\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{X}^{\alpha}
.
∎

The next lemma shows that one dimensional continuous piecewise linear approximators have the same regularity as the function they approximate. See Lemma
7.8
for a generalization to higher dimensions.

Lemma 7.4

(Continuous piecewise linear approximators preserve regularity, and
L^{\infty}
error estimate)
.

Let
f
be a function from
[a,b]
to
\mathbb{R}
with modulus of regularity
\omega
, and let
\Phi
be a continuous piecewise linear function on
[a,b]
that passes through the points
\{(x_{i},f(x_{i}))\}_{i=0}^{n}
for
a=x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}=b
, and is linear on
[x_{0},x_{1}],[x_{1},x_{2}],\cdots,[x_{n-1},x_{n}]
. Then, we have the error estimate

\|f-\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}([a,b])}\leq\omega\left(\max\limits_{i=0,1,\cdots,n-1}{\frac{|x_{i+1}-x_{i}|}{2}}\right)

Moreover,
\omega
is a modulus of regularity of
\Phi
.

Proof.

We first show that
\Phi
has at least the same regularity as
f
. Take any
x,y\in[a,b]
, and assume without loss of generality that
x\leq y
. If
x,y
lie in the same interval in
[x_{0},x_{1}],[x_{1},x_{2}],\cdots,[x_{n-1},x_{n}]
, then
x,y\in[x_{m},x_{m+1}]
for some index
m
, by concavity of
\omega
,

\displaystyle\omega(y-x)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\left(1-\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}\right)\cdot 0+\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}\cdot(x_{m+1}-x_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}\right)\omega(0)+\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}\omega(x_{m+1}-x_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}\omega(x_{m+1}-x_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}|f(x_{m+1})-f(x_{m})|

\displaystyle=\frac{y-x}{x_{m+1}-x_{m}}|\Phi(x_{m+1})-\Phi(x_{m})|

\displaystyle=|\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)|

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\Phi
is linear on
[x_{m},x_{m+1}]
. Otherwise,
x,y
lie in different intervals in
[x_{0},x_{1}],[x_{1},x_{2}],\cdots,[x_{n-1},x_{n}]
, then
x_{p}\leq x\leq x_{p+1}\leq x_{q}\leq y\leq x_{q+1}
for some indices
p,q
, and let

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\frac{x-x_{p}}{x_{p+1}-x_{p}},\quad B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\frac{x_{q+1}-y}{x_{q+1}-x_{q}}

so
0\leq A,B\leq 1
. Without loss of generality, assume that
A\leq B
. Then,

\Phi(x)=(1-A)\Phi(x_{p})+A\Phi(x_{p+1}),\quad\Phi(y)=B\Phi(x_{q})+(1-B)\Phi(x_{q+1})

Since

\displaystyle y-x

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x_{q+1}-y}{x_{q+1}-x_{q}}-\frac{x-x_{p}}{x_{p+1}-x_{p}}\right)(x_{q}-x_{p})+\left(1-\frac{x_{q+1}-y}{x_{q+1}-x_{q}}\right)(x_{q+1}-x_{p})

\displaystyle+\frac{x-x_{p}}{x_{p+1}-x_{p}}(x_{q}-x_{p+1})

\displaystyle=(B-A)(x_{q}-x_{p})+(1-B)(x_{q+1}-x_{p})+A(x_{q}-x_{p+1})

with
B-A\geq 0,1-B\geq 0,A\geq 0
and
(B-A)+(1-B)+A=1
, by concavity of
\omega
, we have

\displaystyle\omega(y-x)

\displaystyle=\omega((B-A)(x_{q}-x_{p})+(1-B)(x_{q+1}-x_{p})+A(x_{q}-x_{p+1}))

\displaystyle\geq(B-A)\omega(x_{q}-x_{p})+(1-B)\omega(x_{q+1}-x_{p})+A\omega(x_{q}-x_{p+1})

\displaystyle\geq(B-A)|\Phi(x_{q})-\Phi(x_{p})|+(1-B)|\Phi(x_{q+1})-\Phi(x_{p})|+A|\Phi(x_{q})-\Phi(x_{p+1})|

\displaystyle\geq(B-A)(\Phi(x_{q})-\Phi(x_{p}))+(1-B)(\Phi(x_{q+1})-\Phi(x_{p}))+A(\Phi(x_{q})-\Phi(x_{p+1}))

\displaystyle=(B\Phi(x_{q})+(1-B)\Phi(x_{q+1}))-((1-A)\Phi(x_{p})+A\Phi(x_{p+1}))

\displaystyle=\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)

Similarly, we can show that
\omega(y-x)\geq\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)
, thus

\omega(|y-x|)=\omega(y-x)\geq|\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)|

Therefore, we always have
|\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)|\leq\omega(|y-x|)
for any
x,y\in[a,b]
, thus
\omega
is a modulus of regularity of
\Phi
.

Now we prove the upper bound for the
L^{\infty}
error. Take any
x\in[a,b]
, then
x\in[x_{k},x_{k+1}]
for some index
k
. For convenience, let
L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}x_{k+1}-x_{k}
, then we have

\displaystyle|f(x)-\Phi(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|f(x)-\left(\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}\Phi(x_{k})+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}\Phi(x_{k+1})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|f(x)-\left(\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}f(x_{k})+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}f(x_{k+1})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}(f(x)-f(x_{k}))+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}(f(x)-f(x_{k+1}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}|f(x)-f(x_{k})|+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}|f(x)-f(x_{k+1})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}\omega(x-x_{k})+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}\omega(x_{k+1}-x)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\left(\frac{x_{k+1}-x}{L}(x-x_{k})+\frac{x-x_{k}}{L}(x_{k+1}-x)\right)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\frac{(L-(x-x_{k}))(x-x_{k})}{L}+\frac{(x-x_{k})(L-(x-x_{k}))}{L}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\left(\frac{\left(\frac{L^{2}}{4}\right)}{L}+\frac{\left(\frac{L^{2}}{4}\right)}{L}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\omega\left(\max\limits_{i=0,1,\cdots,n-1}{\frac{|x_{i+1}-x_{i}|}{2}}\right)

Since
x\in[a,b]
was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that

\|f-\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}([a,b])}\leq\omega\left(\max\limits_{i=0,1,\cdots,n-1}{\frac{|x_{i+1}-x_{i}|}{2}}\right).

This completes our proof.
∎

7.2.2
One-Dimensional Memorizers with Optimal Regularity

Proposition 7.1

(Efficient Universal Memorization by Two-Hidden-Layer MLPs with Optimal Regularity)
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. For any set of
MN
samples
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{MN}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
(where
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{MN}
), there exists a ReLU MLP
\Phi
with
\operatorname{widthvec}=[M,4N-2]
that can memorize this sample set; i.e.

\Phi(x_{i})=y_{i}\qquad\mbox{for }i=1,\dots,MN.

Furthermore,
\Phi
is linear on the intervals
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
for
i=1,2,\cdots,MN-1
, and it is constant on each of the segment
(-\infty,x_{1}]
and
[x_{MN},\infty)
. The number of nonzero parameters in
\Phi
is at most
2MN+2M+8N-4
.

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the function to be implemented, i.e. it is the unique function on
\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

f(x_{i})=y_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,MN
;

2.

f
is linear on
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
for
i=1,2,\cdots,MN-1
;

3.

f
is constant on each of
(-\infty,x_{1}]
and
[x_{MN},\infty)
.

Then, it suffices to prove that
f
can be implemented by a ReLU MLP with
\operatorname{widthvec}=[M,4N-2]
.

Let’s relabel the samples as
(x_{ij},y_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{M,N}
such that
x_{ij}
increases as
(i,j)
increases lexicographically, i.e.

x_{11}<x_{12}<\cdots<x_{1N}<x_{21}<x_{22}<\cdots<x_{2N}<\cdots<x_{M1}<x_{M2}<\cdots<x_{MN}

For notational convenience, denote the
M+1
intervals
(-\infty,x_{11}],[x_{11},x_{21}],[x_{21},x_{31}],\cdots,[x_{M-1,1},x_{M,1}]
and
[x_{M1},\infty)
as
I_{0},I_{1},\cdots,I_{M}
, respectively. The notations
x_{ij}
and
x_{i,j}
have the same meaning.

The idea of the proof is matching the jumps in the derivative of
f
step by step (see Figure
5
): for
r=1,2
and
j=2,3,\cdots,N
, let
S_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\{I_{r+2k}:k\in\mathbb{N},1\leq r+2k\leq M\}=\{I_{r},I_{r+2},I_{r+4},\cdots\}
, and construct the continuous piecewise linear function
g_{j}^{(+r)}
in two steps:

1.

Matching upward derivative jumps at the
j
th sample point in intervals in
S_{r}
: let

X_{j}^{(+r)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\{x_{r+2k,j}:k\in\mathbb{N},f^{\prime}_{+}(x_{r+2k,j})-f^{\prime}_{-}(x_{r+2k,j})>0\}

be the set of the
j
th sample points in intervals in
S_{r}
where
f
has upward derivative jumps. Suppose the elements in
X_{j}^{(+r)}
are
x_{i_{1},j}<x_{i_{2},j}<\cdots<x_{i_{m},j}
, then for each
1\leq m^{\prime}\leq m
, let
g_{j}^{(+r)}
be linear on
I_{i_{m^{\prime}}}
with slope
(-1)^{m^{\prime}}\big(f^{\prime}_{+}(x_{i_{m^{\prime}},j})-f^{\prime}_{-}(x_{i_{m^{\prime}},j})\big)
, and
g_{j}^{(+r)}(x_{i_{m^{\prime}}})=0
;

2.

Smooth extension to the rest of
\mathbb{R}
: we have already defined
g_{j}^{(+r)}
on some distinct closed intervals in Step
1
(unless
X_{j}^{(+r)}
is empty, in which case simply let
g_{j}^{(+r)}
be the zero function on
\mathbb{R}
), which separate
\mathbb{R}
into some segments. For each of these segments, if it is infinite then we have already defined
g_{j}^{(+r)}
at its only endpoint, then let
g_{j}^{(+r)}
be constant on this segment matching its value at this endpoint; if it is finite then we have already defined
g_{j}^{(+r)}
at both of its endpoints, then let
g_{j}^{(+r)}
be linear on this segment such that it matches both of its values at the two endpoints.

Theorem 7.1

(Efficient Universal Memorization by Deep MLPs with Optimal Regularity)
.

Let

Given any set of

\Phi

, and it is constant on each of the segment

,

\displaystyle\geq\frac{K\operatorname{dim(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}{2K+23\sum_{b=1}^{L}n_{b}-121L}\cdot 100\%

7.3
ReLU Approximations on Polytopes via the Kuhn Triangulation

In this section, we generalize the concept of continuous piecewise linear approximators and the methods for constructing them to higher dimensions. As we will see, compared to the ones in Theorem
6.2
, these high dimensional continuous piecewise linear approximators require more width, but they can achieve about the same level of global error with much higher regularity (lower Lipschitz constant).

Definition 7.1

(Simplex)
.

A
d
-dimensional simplex (or
d
-simplex)
S
is the convex hull of
d+1
points
\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{d+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are affinely independent, i.e. the vectors

\mathbf{v}_{d+1}-\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{d+1}-\mathbf{v}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{d+1}-\mathbf{v}_{d}

are linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\operatorname{conv}(\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{d+1}\})=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}:\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d+1}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

Furthermore,
\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{d+1}
are called the vertices of the simplex
S
.

Definition 7.2

(Triangulation of polytopes)
.

A triangulation of a polytope
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite collection of
d
-simplices
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that

1.

S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}=P
;

2.

The set of all their vertices is the vertex set of
P

2
2

2

Some literatures allow the existence of additional vertices inside
P
, but here we require that there be no additional vertices other than those of
P
to simplify later arguments.
;

3.

Their interiors are pairwise disjoint.

Triangulations are generally not unique. For example, Figure
7
shows two different triangulations of a hexagon.

The next lemma shows how to triangulate all unit cubes
\{[i,i+1]:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}^{d}
of the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
simultaneously using hyperplanes. The triangulation of each of these unit cubes will be a Kuhn triangulation.

Lemma 7.5
.

The set of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\{x_{i}\pm x_{j}=2k:1\leq i<j\leq d,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{x_{i}=k:1\leq i\leq d,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

triangulates each of the unit cubes in
\{[i,i+1]:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\cup H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{h\in H}h
be the union of the hyperplanes (when considered as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) in
H
. By the definition of
H
,
\cup H
has the following two properties:

1.

\cup H
has period
2
along each axis:

\mathbf{z}\in\cup H\Longleftrightarrow(\mathbf{z}+2\mathbf{e}_{i})\in\cup H,\quad i=1,2,\cdots,d

2.

\cup H
is symmetric about each axis:

\mathbf{z}\in\cup H\Longleftrightarrow(-\mathbf{z})\in\cup H,\quad i=1,2,\cdots,d

By property
1
, we only need to show that
\cup H
triangulates
[-1,1]^{d}
. By property
2
, we only need to show that
\cup H
triangulates
[0,1]^{d}
.

By Definition
6.1
, the
d!
simplices

\operatorname{conv}\left(\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{\tau(i)}:0\leq k\leq d\right\}\right),\quad\tau\in S_{d}

(7.9)

form the Kuhn triangulation of

. Fix any permutation

\operatorname{conv}\left(\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{\tau(i)}:0\leq k\leq d\right\}\right)

For

in

. For

in

. For

. Therefore, all the faces of all

simplices are contained in some hyperplanes in

. It remains to show that the intersection point of any
d

Let

If one of these hyperplanes has the form

, and we also have

, so

.

If any two of these hyperplanes have the form

, and we also have
x_{i_{0}}^{\prime}=k+l\in\mathbb{Z},x_{j_{0}}^{\prime}=k-l\in\mathbb{Z}
, so

Finally, if

contain the following

By adding or subtracting these equations, we obtain an equation of the form

Without loss of generality, assume that the above equation is of the form

For the error estimate, fix any
\mathbf{x}\in[0,n]^{d}
. Suppose
\mathbf{x}\in Q_{\mathbf{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}[n_{1},n_{1}+1]\times[n_{2},n_{2}+1]\times\cdots\times[n_{d},n_{d}+1]

such that

\mathbf{x}=\lambda_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}\mathbf{u}_{d+1},\quad\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}=1

Since

is affine on this simplex,

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}\Phi(\mathbf{u}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}f(\mathbf{u}_{i})

thus

\displaystyle|\Phi(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=|\lambda_{1}(f(\mathbf{u}_{1})-f(\mathbf{x}))+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}(f(\mathbf{u}_{d+1})-f(\mathbf{x}))|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}|f(\mathbf{u}_{1})-f(\mathbf{x})|+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}|f(\mathbf{u}_{d+1})-f(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}\omega(\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1})+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}\omega(\|\mathbf{u}_{d+1}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\omega(\lambda_{1}\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d+1}\|\mathbf{u}_{d+1}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1})

(7.11)

where (
7.11
) follows by the concavity of the modulus
\omega
,
and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\|\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}-(\mathbf{x})_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}|(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}-(\mathbf{x})_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}|(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}-(\mathbf{x})_{i}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}|(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}-(\mathbf{x})_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}(n_{i}+1-(\mathbf{x})_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}+(1-((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\right)

for which we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}\right)-n_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}n_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}(n_{i}+1)\right)-n_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}

thus

((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}=\left(1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\leq\frac{1}{4}

(1-((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}=\left(1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\leq\frac{1}{4}

Combining the results above, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\|\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}+(1-((\mathbf{x})_{i}-n_{i}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\mathbf{u}_{j})_{i}=n_{i}+1\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}

Therefore,

|\Phi(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})|\leq\omega\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{j}\|\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{x}\|_{1}\right)\leq\omega\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)

Since

was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that

\|f-\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,n]^{d})}\leq\omega\left(\frac{d}{2}\right).

This concludes our proof.

j=0,1,2,\cdots,(n+1)^{d}-1
, let
y_{j}
be the value of
f
at the center of the cuboid that is mapped to
j
under
\pi_{k}
. Note that the range of
\pi_{k}
is
[0,(n+1)^{d}-1]
, so we don’t need to care about the behavior of our memorizer outside this interval.

Let
\{(j,y_{j}):j=0,1,2,\cdots,(n+1)^{d}-1\}
be a sample set of size
(n+1)^{d}
for our memorizer. Since

\left\lfloor\frac{2\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil+2}{4}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil+1}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil+1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{(n+1)^{d/2}}{2}

then by Proposition
7.1
(taking
M=2\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil
,

N=\big\lfloor\frac{2\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil+2}{4}\big\rfloor
),
there is a ReLU MLP
\phi_{k}
with width
2\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil
and depth 3 (counting the output layer, which is still a hidden layer of the final network) that memorizes this sample set, and
\phi_{k}
is linear on
[j,j+1]
for
j=0,1,\cdots,n^{d}-2
.

Step 3
:

Combine Step 1, Step 2 and Lemma
7.2
to construct the final network
\Phi

We denote the ReLU MLP implementing the median function on
2d+1
non-negative arguments as
\mathbf{M}_{2d+1}
, which has width
6d+3
and depth
11d+3
by Lemma
7.2
, then we construct
\Phi
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \cdots\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{2d}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{2d}(\mathbf{x})\\
\pi_{2d+1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{2d+1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\phi_{2}\circ\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\pi_{2d+1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\ \Longrightarrow\ \cdots\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\phi_{2}\circ\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{2d+1}\circ\pi_{2d+1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\ \mathbf{M}_{2d+1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\phi_{2}\circ\pi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{2d+1}\circ\pi_{2d+1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\right)\ \Longrightarrow\ \Phi(\mathbf{x})

Its width and depth are

\displaystyle\operatorname{width}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\operatorname{width}(\pi_{2d+1})+2d,\operatorname{width}(\phi_{2d+1})+2d,\operatorname{width}(M_{2d+1})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{(2n+3)d,6d+3,2\lceil(n+1)^{d/2}\rceil+2d\right\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2d+1}\operatorname{depth}(\pi_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{2d+1}\operatorname{depth}(\phi_{k})+\operatorname{depth}(M_{2d+1})

\displaystyle=3(2d+1)+3(2d+1)+(11d+3)

\displaystyle=23d+9

Step 4
:

Estimate the
L^{\infty}
error

We first show that the
L^{\infty}
error of
\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}
is controlled on
Q_{k}
for all
k
: pick any
\mathbf{w}\in Q_{k}
, then since
\pi_{k}
maps the cuboid in
Q_{k}
containing
\mathbf{w}
(whose center we denote by
\mathbf{w}_{0}
) to some constant
j
, which is further mapped by
\phi_{k}
to
y_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
, we have
\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(\mathbf{w})=f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
, so

|\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w})|=|f(\mathbf{w}_{0})-f(\mathbf{w})|\leq\nu\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}\|_{1}^{\alpha}

Since the edge length of any cuboid in
Q_{k}
is at most
l\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\frac{2d}{n(2d+1)}
(this can be seen from Equation
7.38
),
\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}\|_{1}\leq\frac{d}{2}l=\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}
, then

|\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w})|\leq\nu\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}\|_{1}^{\alpha}\leq\nu\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\right)^{\alpha}

Now, for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, we show that it belongs to at least
d+1
out of
2d+1

Q_{k}
’s: notice that in the definition of the trifling regions
\Omega_{k}
(Equation
7.37
), each
x_{i}
belongs to at most one of the following:

\bigcup_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{k-1}{2d+1}+j\right),\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{k}{2d+1}+j\right)\right),\quad\text{for }k=1,2,\cdots,2d+1

so
\mathbf{x}
belongs to at most
d
trifling regions, thus
\mathbf{x}
belongs to at least
d+1
of the
Q_{k}
’s.

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, at least
d+1
of
\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x})
(for
k=1,2,\cdots,2d+1
) belong to the interval
\Big[f(\mathbf{x})-\nu\big(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\big)^{\alpha},f(\mathbf{x})+\nu\big(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\big)^{\alpha}\Big]
, then by Lemma
7.1
,

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{median}\left\{\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x}):1\leq k\leq 2d+1\right\}\in\left[f(\mathbf{x})-\nu\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\Big)^{\alpha},f(\mathbf{x})+\nu\Big(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\Big)^{\alpha}\right]

hence

\|f-\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leq\nu\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n(2d+1)}\right)^{\alpha}

Step 5
:

Estimate the Lipschitz constants

To compute the Lipschitz constants, we first fix
2\leq k\leq 2d
, and compute the Lipschitz constant of
\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k}
. It is easy to see that
\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{1}
and
\phi_{2d+1}\circ\pi_{2d+1}
have at least the same regularity since there are less cuboids in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2d+1}
. Finally, by Lemma
7.3
, their median has the same regularity.

We first compute the Lipschitz constant of
\pi_{k}
. Let
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\Delta\mathbf{x}
small, we want to find an upper bound for
|\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x})|
. By definition of
p_{k}
, it only increases linearly by
1
inside intervals of length
\frac{1}{n(2d+1)}
, so
|p_{k}^{\prime}|\leq n(2d+1)
, thus

\|p_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}\leq n(2d+1)\|\Delta\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

where
p_{k}
applies to
\mathbf{x}
elementwise. Let
\mathbf{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}(n+1)^{0}&(n+1)^{1}&\cdots&(n+1)^{d-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, then

\displaystyle|\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-\pi_{k}(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{n}\cdot p_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{n}\cdot p_{k}(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{n}\cdot(p_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-p_{k}(\mathbf{x}))|

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)^{d-1}\cdot\|p_{k}(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\mathbf{x})-p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)^{d}(2d+1)\|\Delta\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

therefore

\operatorname{Lip}(\pi_{k})\leq(n+1)^{d}(2d+1)

Now we compute the Lipschitz constant of
\phi_{k}
. As mentioned above, let

(\overline{a_{d}a_{d-1}\cdots a_{1}})_{n+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(n+1)^{i-1}a_{i}

denote the number
\overline{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{d}}
in base
n+1
. Let
P_{(\overline{a_{d}a_{d-1}\cdots a_{1}})_{n+1}}^{(k)}
be the center of the unique cuboid in
Q_{k}
such that
p_{i}\left(P_{(\overline{a_{d}a_{d-1}\cdots a_{1}})_{n+1}}^{(k)}\right)=a_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,d
. Note that
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,n\}
for all
i
. Then,
\phi_{k}
is the unique continuous piecewise linear function that goes through

\{(j,f(P_{j}^{(k)})):j=0,1,2,\cdots,(n+1)^{d}-1\}
and is linear between adjacent points in this set. Then,

\operatorname{Lip}(\phi_{k})=\max\limits_{j=0,1,2,\cdots,(n+1)^{d}-2}{\left|f(P_{j+1}^{(k)})-f(P_{j}^{(k)})\right|}\leq\max\limits_{j=0,1,2,\cdots,(n+1)^{d}-2}{\nu\left\|P_{j+1}^{(k)}-P_{j}^{(k)}\right\|_{1}^{\alpha}}\leq\nu d^{\alpha}

Finally, by Lemma
7.3
, the median function preserves regularity, hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(\Phi)

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{k=1,2,\cdots,2d+1}{\operatorname{Lip}(\phi_{k}\circ\pi_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{k=1,2,\cdots,2d+1}{\operatorname{Lip}(\phi_{k})}\cdot{\operatorname{Lip}(\pi_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq\nu(n+1)^{d}(2d+1)d^{\alpha}.

This concludes our proof.
∎
