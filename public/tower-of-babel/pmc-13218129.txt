Child health implications of transitioning from charcoal to electric cooking in Uganda: a qualitative stakeholder study
Authors: Edda Weimann, Lydia Nandawula, Jeffrey Goldhagen, Peter Weimann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHousehold air pollution and cooking-related injuries from charcoal use remain major contributors to preventable child morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries, including Uganda. Electric cooking (e-cooking) could reduce children’s exposure to these risks, yet adoption remains limited and the policy conditions shaping this transition are poorly understood from a child health perspective.ObjectiveTo explore policy stakeholder perspectives on transitioning from charcoal to e-cooking in Uganda and to examine perceived implications for child health related to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative stakeholder study using structured interviews with 51 policy and energy-sector stakeholders involved in e-cooking promotion, regulation, research, financing and implementation in Uganda. Participants were purposively sampled from government, development partners, civil society organisations, the private sector, academic institutions and experienced end users. Data were collected in Kampala between September and November 2023 and analysed using Creswell’s six-step qualitative analysis approach. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research.ResultsStakeholders perceived e-cooking as safer than charcoal and associated with reduced smoke exposure and cooking-related hazards. Child health considerations were rarely described as explicit drivers of energy or clean cooking policy.ConclusionsE-cooking is viewed by policy stakeholders as a promising upstream child health intervention to reduce children’s exposure to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries in Uganda. Realising these child health benefits will require improved electricity reliability, greater affordability of e-cooking technologies and explicit integration of child health objectives into energy and clean cooking policy.Keywords: Health Policy, Low and Middle Income Countries, Child Health, Adolescent Health, Technology

Background

By late 2023, an estimated 2.3 billion people globally lacked access to clean cooking fuels, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing a disproportionate share of this burden.1 2 Widespread reliance on black and brown carbon energy sources, including diesel, kerosene, biomass and charcoal, contributes not only to environmental degradation and climate change but also to substantial adverse health effects, particularly for children and pregnant women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Cooking with wood fuels alone generates approximately one gigaton of CO₂-equivalent emissions annually, accounting for around 2% of global emissions and accelerating deforestation and loss of carbon sinks.3

In 2019, household air pollution from charcoal use accounted for an estimated 86 million lost healthy life years, underscoring its substantial public health burden.2 Charcoal cooking is also a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality, accounting for approximately 3.2 million deaths annually, including more than 237 000 among children under 5 years of age.2 Incomplete combustion produces harmful pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO) and fine particulate matter, which are strongly associated with acute and chronic respiratory disease. Due to prolonged exposure in poorly ventilated cooking environments, women and children are particularly vulnerable. Evidence links charcoal smoke exposure to childhood pneumonia, impaired lung function, acute respiratory infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including low birth weight and stillbirths.4,6

Beyond respiratory disease, charcoal cooking is associated with a significant household injury burden. Charcoal combustion poses a toxicological hazard through CO exposure, with children and pregnant women particularly susceptible to hypoxic injury.7 8 In Uganda, these household energy choices shape daily exposure to household air pollution, thermal injury risks and CO poisoning, positioning cooking energy policy as a structural determinant of child health.

Charcoal cooking-related injuries among women and children in LMICs

Charcoal remains a dominant household cooking fuel in many LMICs, particularly in urban and periurban settings where it is perceived as affordable and portable.9 However, its use in poorly ventilated and overcrowded domestic environments exposes women and children to a largely preventable injury burden linked to stove design, fuel combustion characteristics, household layout and gendered cooking roles.10 11

Thermal burns and scalds

Thermal injuries are the most frequently reported charcoal-related harms.12 Open braziers, unstable stoves and ground-level cooking increase the risk of flame and contact burns, with young children particularly affected due to proximity and limited risk awareness.10 Common mechanisms include stove or pot tipping, contact with hot surfaces and falls onto embers, often accompanied by scalds from spilled hot liquids.10 11 13

Women, as primary cooks, experience a distinct burn risk profile, including stove flare-ups, clothing ignition and injuries from handling heavy pots over open flames. Clothing-related ignition, particularly involving loose, flowing or synthetic materials, has been identified as a major contributor to burn severity.14 These injuries frequently result in prolonged disability, economic hardship and psychosocial consequences and are often compounded by smoke and heat inhalation in domestic fires.15

CO poisoning

Charcoal combustion generates high levels of CO, especially when used indoors or in poorly ventilated spaces.8 Acute CO poisoning has been reported in domestic and large-scale exposure events, with symptoms ranging from headache and dizziness to seizures, loss of consciousness and death.7 8 Children and pregnant women are particularly vulnerable due to higher metabolic oxygen demands and fetal susceptibility to hypoxic injury. Children with chronic lung diseases such as asthma are particularly vulnerable to CO, elevated CO₂ levels, NOx and small particle pollution deriving from incomplete combustion, charcoal and woodfire burning.16

Gendered and socioeconomic dimensions of injury risk

Charcoal-related injuries are patterned by socioeconomic status, housing quality and gender norms. Energy-poor households reliant on solid fuels experience higher injury rates, while women’s domestic roles and children’s routine presence in cooking spaces amplify exposure.11 Fuel stacking, using charcoal alongside kerosene, electricity or liquefied petroleum gas, further increases injury risk through unstable and hazardous cooking practices.10

Implications for prevention and policy

The injury burden associated with charcoal cooking highlights the need to frame household energy transitions not only as environmental or respiratory health interventions but also as injury-prevention strategies (figure 1). Improved stove stability, shielding of hot surfaces, ventilation and childcare-focused behavioural interventions can reduce risk, but sustained reductions require broader structural change, including access to safer cooking technologies, improved housing and gender-responsive energy policies.9

Modern cooking technologies offer safer, low-emission alternatives to traditional biomass fuels. Electric cooking (e-cooking) technologies such as electric pressure cookers (EPCs), induction stoves and slow cookers, substantially reduce indoor air pollution and improve household safety. In Uganda, EPCs have demonstrated benefits including time savings, cooking versatility, user satisfaction and reduced household energy costs.17 High energy efficiency and automation further enhance their potential contribution to health, gender equality and climate goals.18,21

Despite these advantages, e-cooking adoption in Uganda remains limited. Charcoal and firewood remain the primary cooking fuels for approximately 94% of households.22 Even in urban areas with relatively high electricity access, electricity is rarely used for primary cooking. Adoption of modern alternatives is constrained by affordability, electricity reliability, limited awareness, entrenched cooking practices and policy and institutional barriers. Evidence suggests that these constraints are shaped less by individual preferences than by systemic factors within the e-cooking ecosystem, including infrastructure, tariffs, regulation, standards and institutional coordination.

Recognising the role of policy and techno-policy interventions in shaping household energy transitions and child health risks, this study examines policy stakeholder perspectives on strategies for transitioning from charcoal to e-cooking in Uganda and their perceived implications for child health outcomes related to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative research design using structured interviews to explore policy stakeholder perspectives on electric cooking (e-cooking) in Uganda. A qualitative approach was used to examine policy, institutional and techno-policy factors shaping the transition from charcoal to e-cooking and their perceived implications for child health, household air pollution and cooking-related injuries.23 24

Study setting and context

Fieldwork was conducted in Kampala, Uganda’s capital city, selected for its relatively high electricity access, concentration of national policy institutions and relevance to ongoing e-cooking initiatives, including the government ban on commercial charcoal production.25 26 Despite urban–rural differences, Uganda continues to rely heavily on charcoal and firewood for cooking, exposing women and children to household air pollution and cooking-related hazards.22 Data were collected between September and November 2023.

Participant selection and recruitment

Participants were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling, approaches commonly used in qualitative policy research.23 27 Patients were not involved in the study. The study targeted stakeholders directly involved in e-cooking promotion, regulation, research, financing or implementation. Eligible participants included representatives from government agencies, civil society organisations, development partners, the private sector, academic institutions and end users with at least 2 years’ experience using e-cooking appliances.

Key informants were drawn from sector organisations (including the Uganda National Alliance for Clean Cooking, Centre for Research in Energy and Energy Conservation, Uganda National Bureau of Standards and National Renewable Energy Platform), as well as private energy providers and individual users identified through e-cooking promoters. In total, 51 stakeholders participated.

Data collection

Data were collected through structured interviews guided by a predefined interview guide (online supplemental appendix). Topics included the status of e-cooking in Uganda, electricity infrastructure and tariffs, policy and regulatory frameworks, technology availability, sociocultural factors and perceived barriers and enablers to adoption. Interviews also explored stakeholder views on how e-cooking could reduce health risks associated with charcoal use, including household air pollution and cooking-related injuries.

Interviews were conducted in person or remotely. Participants could provide written responses or audio recordings, while in-person interviews allowed clarification and follow-up where needed. Structured interviews ensured consistency across participants while allowing flexibility to explore emerging issues relevant to policy and child health implications.27

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Creswell’s six-step qualitative analysis approach.23 Interview data were organised, reviewed, and systematically coded to identify recurring concepts and patterns. Codes were grouped into analytic categories reflecting policy, infrastructure, technology, affordability and sociocultural dimensions of e-cooking, consistent with frameworks used in prior clean cooking research.28 29 These categories were analysed to identify key barriers and enabling factors influencing e-cooking adoption and its perceived implications for reducing children’s exposure to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries.

Interviews were transcribed verbatim by trained research assistants affiliated with NREP, reviewed for accuracy, and triangulated against field observations and programme documentation.

Document analysis of relevant policy and sector reports was conducted to contextualise stakeholder perspectives within Uganda’s broader energy and clean cooking policy landscape.30 31

Rigour and trustworthiness

Rigour was enhanced using a structured interview guide, purposive sampling of knowledgeable stakeholders and triangulation across stakeholder groups and document sources. An iterative analytic process supported refinement of categories and interpretation. Reflexivity was maintained by acknowledging the researchers’ backgrounds in energy policy, health and environmental research and their potential influence on analysis.7 8

Reporting standards

This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research.32

Results

Participant characteristics

The 51 stakeholders participating in the study had high educational attainment, with 40% holding a bachelor’s degree, 35% a master’s degree and 25% a PhD, indicating substantial sectoral expertise. Participants represented a broad range of roles within the energy and policy landscape, including government actors (25%), development partners and civil society organisations (20% each), private sector managers (20%) and research institutions (15%), providing a balanced mix of policy, practice and research perspectives.

Participants spanned all career stages, with 40% aged 20–35 years, 35% aged 36–50 years and 25% over 50 years. Gender representation was nearly equal (52% male, 48% female). Stakeholders reported varying degrees of personal experience with e-cooking technologies, including EPCs, rice cookers and hot plates, with use ranging from 1 to over 15 years. This diversity supported a broad range of perspectives on policy, technology and adoption dynamics relevant to household health and safety.

Status of e-cooking and implications for household health

Across stakeholder groups, e-cooking in Uganda was described as an emerging but still marginal practice. Although e-cooking technologies are increasingly visible in urban markets, their use remains limited relative to charcoal and firewood. Electricity was rarely reported as a primary cooking fuel, even in urban areas with improved access, resulting in continued exposure of women and children to smoke and combustion-related hazards.

Children were consistently described as being present in cooking spaces but were rarely identified as explicit beneficiaries of cooking energy policy. Several participants linked recent increases in e-cooking visibility to policy measures, including the introduction of a dedicated cooking tariff:

“The adoption of electric cooking in Uganda has experienced a slight increase… another contributing factor has been the introduction of a specialized cooking tariff on the main grid.” (Policy stakeholder)

E-cooking was most described as complementary to charcoal rather than a full replacement, reflecting widespread fuel stacking practices.

E-cooking technologies, affordability and perceived health benefits

Stakeholders reported increasing availability of e-cooking appliances, particularly EPCs and hot plates, and associated these technologies with reduced smoke exposure, improved household safety and time savings. EPCs were viewed especially favourably due to their efficiency and perceived ability to reduce indoor air pollution.

Affordability was consistently identified as a major barrier to adoption. High upfront appliance costs and concerns about electricity bills were described as more influential than health considerations in shaping cooking energy choices:

“Mostly affordability and availability of the cooking fuel are the guiding factors, which explains why charcoal and firewood are commonly used in Uganda. With the current governmental charcoal ban, there is a need to create a steady supply of alternative clean cooking fuels.” (Policy stakeholder)

As a result, continued reliance on charcoal was seen as sustaining household exposure to smoke and heat-related hazards, particularly for children who spend time in cooking environments.

Tariffs, regulation and integration of child health considerations

Policy stakeholders viewed the introduction of a dedicated electricity cooking tariff as a positive step towards encouraging e-cooking adoption. However, many emphasised that tariff reductions alone were insufficient without parallel measures addressing appliance affordability, electricity reliability and public awareness.

Participants highlighted gaps in policy and regulatory frameworks, including limited incentives and weak cross-sectoral coordination:

“The current government policies and regulations surrounding electric cooking in Uganda might not be fully conducive to promote this technology. There might be a need for more supportive policies, including subsidies or incentives for electric cooking appliances, and a more stable electricity supply.” (Policy stakeholder)

Clean cooking policies were primarily framed around energy access, environmental protection or climate mitigation, with limited explicit integration of child health objectives. This was perceived as constraining the ability of e-cooking initiatives to address household air pollution and cooking-related injury risks affecting children.

Sociocultural factors and child exposure in cooking environments

Sociocultural practices were described as strongly influencing cooking energy choices. Persistent preferences for charcoal, linked to taste, familiarity and perceived control over cooking, were reported to reinforce continued use even in households with electricity access:

“The belief that food cooked using electricity is not as tasty as that cooked with charcoal… Automation takes away the control many are used to with traditional cooking methods.” (Policy stakeholder)

Women were described as central to cooking decisions but often constrained by limited control over household finances. Children were consistently reported to be present in cooking spaces, increasing exposure to smoke, heat, and unstable cooking equipment. Despite this, child exposure and injury risks were rarely framed as explicit motivators in household decision-making or policy communication.

Summary of key findings

Across stakeholder groups, e-cooking in Uganda was described as a promising but constrained transition with potential implications for child health. Barriers related to electricity reliability, affordability, policy coherence and sociocultural practices were consistently identified. While stakeholders recognised e-cooking as a potential upstream intervention to reduce household air pollution and cooking-related injuries among children (figure 1), they emphasised that realising these benefits depends on coordinated techno-policy approaches linking energy planning with health and social objectives.

Discussion

This qualitative stakeholder study examined the implications of transitioning from charcoal to e-cooking in Uganda, with a focus on perceived child health outcomes related to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries. While stakeholders widely viewed e-cooking as a safer alternative to charcoal, its child health benefits were perceived as indirect and contingent on broader techno-policy and system-level conditions. Adoption was shaped less by health considerations than by infrastructure reliability, affordability, policy coherence and sociocultural practices.

E-cooking as an upstream child health intervention

Stakeholders linked continued reliance on charcoal cooking to persistent exposure of women and children to household air pollution and safety risks. E-cooking was perceived as a means of reducing smoke exposure, CO emissions and the risk of burns and fires, particularly for children who spend time in cooking environments. Our findings extend existing evidence by demonstrating that child health considerations are rarely explicit drivers of energy and clean cooking policy, revealing a disconnect between well-established health risks and policy framing.

As shown in the Results, fuel stacking was widely perceived as a transitional practice that limits the extent to which e-cooking can reduce children’s exposure to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries. From this perspective, e-cooking functions as an upstream intervention by modifying household environments rather than relying on individual behaviour change, reinforcing the role of energy policy as a determinant of child health.4

Policy and infrastructure constraints shaping health outcomes

A central finding was the role of electricity reliability in mediating the health-protective potential of e-cooking. Unreliable supply sustained charcoal use even among households aware of health risks, reinforcing evidence that clean cooking transitions depend on system reliability and affordability rather than technology availability alone.18 19

Affordability constraints including appliance costs and electricity tariffs were similarly perceived as limiting adoption, particularly among households where children face the highest exposure to household air pollution. Although targeted cooking tariffs were viewed positively, stakeholders emphasised that tariff reforms alone are insufficient without parallel measures addressing appliance access, financing and user confidence.20 21

Fragmented policy framing and missed child health co-benefits

Stakeholders described limited integration of child health considerations within clean cooking and energy policy frameworks, which were perceived to prioritise energy access, environmental protection or climate mitigation. From a paediatric perspective, this fragmentation reduces the visibility of preventable child health risks, constrains intersectoral collaboration and limits the salience of child health outcomes in both policy and household decision-making.2 3

Sociocultural practices and child exposure

Consistent with previous studies, sociocultural preferences for charcoal—linked to taste, familiarity and perceived control over cooking—continued to shape household behaviour.17 Women were described as central to cooking decisions but often constrained by limited control over household finances. Although children’s proximity to cooking activities was widely acknowledged, it was rarely foregrounded in policy discourse or programme design, suggesting scope for a more explicit child-centred framing of clean cooking initiatives.10 13

Strengths and limitations

This study provides novel insights into policy stakeholder perspectives on e-cooking and child health in Uganda by focusing on upstream techno-policy conditions shaping household exposure to health risks. However, findings are based on stakeholder perceptions rather than direct measurement of child health outcomes, and the urban focus may limit generalisability to rural contexts where infrastructure constraints differ.

Implications for research, practice and policy

This study positions cooking energy policy as a modifiable, upstream determinant of child health. Explicitly framing e-cooking as a child health intervention could enhance its relevance within health and social policy agendas. Future research integrating household-level perspectives with quantitative child health measures is needed to assess how e-cooking adoption translates into reductions in household air pollution and cooking-related injuries. For policy and practice, coordinated approaches aligning energy infrastructure development, appliance affordability and child health objectives are likely necessary to achieve meaningful health gains for children.2 21

Conclusions

This qualitative stakeholder study shows that transitioning from charcoal to e-cooking in Uganda has important implications for child health, particularly through the potential to reduce children’s exposure to household air pollution and cooking-related injuries. Stakeholders consistently perceived e-cooking as safer than charcoal; however, its health-protective potential for children remains indirect and dependent on broader techno-policy conditions.

Persistent barriers related to electricity reliability, appliance affordability, fragmented policy frameworks and sociocultural practices continue to sustain children’s exposure to smoke, CO and thermal injury risks. Consistent with prior clean cooking research, stakeholders emphasised that technology availability alone is insufficient to achieve sustained transitions without supportive infrastructure, financing mechanisms and policy alignment.18 20 21

Despite strong evidence linking household air pollution to adverse child health outcomes, child health considerations were rarely explicit drivers of energy or clean cooking policy. Positioning e-cooking more clearly as a child health intervention, rather than solely an energy or climate measure, could strengthen intersectoral collaboration and better leverage its potential child health co-benefits. Achieving meaningful gains will require coordinated techno-policy approaches that integrate energy planning, affordability measures and health-oriented policy objectives. Future research combining stakeholder perspectives with household-level data and quantitative health outcomes could further clarify how transitions to e-cooking translate into reductions in childhood respiratory illness and cooking-related injuries.
