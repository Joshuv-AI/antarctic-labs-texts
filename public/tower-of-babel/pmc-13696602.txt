Sequential emergence of FLT3-ITD and BCR::ABL1 during disease progression in relapsed acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Hidetoshi Irifune, Yuichiro Semba, Yasufumi Uehara, Takanori Ohta, Yasuhiro Sugio, Koichi Akashi, Takahiro Maeda, Yuju Ohno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergence of BCR::ABL1 during FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitor therapy is a rare resistance mechanism in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). We report a case of relapsed AML with sequential clonal evolution after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). A previously healthy man in his 30s was diagnosed with AML with maturation; initial cytogenetics were normal, and both FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) and BCR::ABL1 were absent, while targeted sequencing identified an FBXW7 mutation. After induction failure, he underwent allo-HSCT. At post-transplant relapse during venetoclax plus azacitidine maintenance, FLT3-ITD and WT1 mutations newly emerged. Quizartinib induced remission, but a second relapse occurred with emergence of BCR::ABL1 while FLT3-ITD persisted. Cytogenetics showed t(9;22)(q34;q11), and FISH confirmed BCR::ABL1 fusion in 73% of bone marrow cells, with the minor transcript (p190) confirmed by quantitative PCR. Despite no ABL1 kinase domain mutations, imatinib and ponatinib failed to control the disease. These findings suggest that BCR::ABL1 emergence may represent a bypass resistance mechanism arising under quizartinib-associated selective pressure.

Introduction

FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) mutations are among the most common molecular abnormalities in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and are associated with poor prognosis and high relapse risk [1, 2]. FLT3 inhibitors have improved outcomes in relapsed/refractory FLT3-mutated AML [3], but acquired resistance remains a major challenge, including secondary FLT3 kinase domain mutations, parallel pathway activation, and clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure [4, 5].

Activation of alternative tyrosine kinase pathways may serve as bypass mechanisms that reduce FLT3 dependence [4, 5]. Among these, BCR::ABL1 emergence during FLT3 inhibitor therapy is rarely reported in AML [5–7], and its clinical characteristics, clonal architecture, and optimal management remain poorly understood.

We report a rare case of relapsed AML with sequential emergence of FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) and BCR::ABL1 after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Serial molecular profiling demonstrated stepwise clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure, providing insight into FLT3 inhibitor resistance mechanisms.

Case presentation

A previously healthy man in his 30 s was diagnosed with AML after presenting with anemia, without lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly on physical examination. Bone marrow examination revealed 40% blasts that were positive for myeloperoxidase (MPO) and contained azurophilic granules, consistent with AML with maturation (FAB classification M2) (Fig. 1 A). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated positivity for CD13, CD33, CD34, CD117, and MPO (Fig. 1B). At diagnosis, conventional cytogenetic analysis showed a normal male karyotype. Screening for recurrent fusion transcripts including BCR::ABL1 was negative, and FLT3-ITD mutation was not detected. Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) using a 398-gene panel (DISCAVar panel) identified an FBXW7 mutation with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.34 without any additional pathogenic variants.

The patient received standard 7 + 3 induction chemotherapy with daunorubicin and cytarabine; however, complete remission was not achieved. Re-induction therapy with high-dose cytarabine was also unsuccessful. Therefore, approximately four months after diagnosis, he underwent allo-HSCT from an HLA-matched sibling donor using myeloablative conditioning consisting of cyclophosphamide and 12 Gy total body irradiation. Post-engraftment bone marrow examination demonstrated complete donor chimerism by short tandem repeat analysis.

Because the patient had refractory disease before allo-HSCT and was considered at high risk of post-transplant relapse, maintenance therapy with venetoclax plus azacitidine (VEN + AZA) was initiated one month after transplantation. However, hematological relapse occurred after the second cycle of VEN + AZA. Chimerism analysis at relapse revealed 94% recipient hematopoiesis, and conventional cytogenetic analysis showed a complex karyotype without t(9;22)(q34;q11) in all 20 metaphases. A newly emerged FLT3-ITD mutation with a high allelic ratio (0.96) was detected, whereas the mutation had not been identified at initial diagnosis. Repeat targeted sequencing demonstrated the persistent FBXW7 mutation together with an additional WT1 mutation. Flow cytometry at this relapse retained the original immunophenotype, with persistent positivity for CD13, CD33, CD34, CD117, and MPO.

Salvage chemotherapy with MEC therapy (mitoxantrone, etoposide, and cytarabine) was administered beginning the day after relapse was diagnosed; however, the disease remained refractory. Furthermore, the patient developed severe heart failure and acute pleuritis during MEC therapy, precluding further intensive chemotherapy. Bone marrow examination performed after MEC therapy and before quizartinib initiation showed persistent disease without remission; conventional cytogenetics did not detect t(9;22)(q34;q11). The FLT3 inhibitor quizartinib was therefore initiated as monotherapy 28 days after completion of MEC therapy, achieving CRi (complete remission with incomplete hematologic recovery, characterized by persistent thrombocytopenia) approximately one month later. Subsequently, approximately two months after quizartinib initiation the patient developed right-sided heart failure due to constrictive pericarditis of undetermined etiology, requiring surgical pericardiectomy via thoracotomy. Due to concerns regarding prolonged myelosuppression and perioperative bleeding risk, quizartinib was temporarily replaced with gilteritinib for two weeks before surgery. Following successful surgery, quizartinib was resumed immediately.

However, approximately five months after initiation of quizartinib-based therapy, blast cells reappeared in the peripheral blood with a concurrent rise in the white blood cell count. Bone marrow examination revealed marked hypercellularity with increased blast cells, consistent with a second relapse. Flow cytometry at this relapse confirmed persistent positivity for AML-associated markers, including CD13, CD33, CD34, CD117, and MPO, consistent with the previous immunophenotype. At the second relapse, cytogenetic analysis revealed a complex karyotype including t(9;22)(q34;q11) in all 20 analyzed metaphases (Fig. 2A). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated BCR::ABL1 positivity in 73% of cells (Fig. 2B). Quantitative PCR detected BCR::ABL1 minor transcript (p190) at 4.7 × 104 copies/µg RNA. Furthermore, FISH analysis of peripheral blood neutrophils revealed BCR::ABL1 positivity in 76% of mature neutrophils. As observed at the first relapse, the FLT3-ITD mutation remained detectable with a high allelic ratio (0.93). Serial molecular and genetic findings obtained by NGS are summarized in Table 1. Targeted sequencing again identified FBXW7 and WT1 mutations. These sequential molecular findings supported stepwise clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure, consistent with previous observations of dynamic clonal evolution in relapsed AML (Fig. 3) [8].

ABL1 kinase domain mutation analysis performed at the time of second relapse revealed no resistance-associated mutations. Subsequently, imatinib was administered as a BCR::ABL1-targeted therapy for one week, but it failed to control the disease. Ponatinib, which inhibits both BCR::ABL1 and FLT3-ITD, was then initiated as monotherapy for one month; however, the leukemia remained refractory. The patient subsequently received cytarabine followed by conditioning chemotherapy (fludarabine, busulfan, and melphalan), and ultimately underwent a second allo-HSCT using umbilical cord blood but died of sepsis on day 3 after transplantation.

Discussion

This case illustrates the emergence of distinct resistance mechanisms during sequential targeted therapies in relapsed AML. Reported FLT3 inhibitor resistance mechanisms include secondary kinase domain mutations, parallel pathway activation, and clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure [2, 4, 5]—consistent with the stepwise evolution observed here, with FLT3-ITD emerging at first relapse and BCR::ABL1 at second relapse during quizartinib.

Although FLT3-ITD was undetectable at diagnosis, it was found at a high allelic ratio at first relapse. The serial NGS findings (Table 1) show persistence of the ancestral FBXW7 mutation with subsequent acquisition of FLT3-ITD and WT1 mutations, indicating progressive molecular evolution rather than replacement by an unrelated clone; the favorable response to quizartinib further supported FLT3-dependence at this stage. FBXW7 mutations have also been implicated in acute lymphoblastic leukemia, suggesting broader relevance to leukemogenesis across myeloid and lymphoid lineages [9].

At the second relapse, the leukemia acquired BCR::ABL1 while retaining FLT3-ITD, FBXW7, and WT1 mutations. BCR::ABL1 positivity in mature neutrophils, not only blasts, suggests emergence at an early progenitor/stem cell level rather than in a committed blast clone. These findings support treatment-associated stepwise clonal evolution, with BCR::ABL1 emergence potentially functioning as a bypass resistance mechanism during disease progression.

To date, only limited case reports and small series have described BCR::ABL1 emergence during FLT3 inhibitor therapy, mostly during gilteritinib treatment [6, 7]; to our knowledge, this is the first reported case occurring during quizartinib-based therapy. Quizartinib has recently been established as effective not only in relapsed/refractory disease but also in combination with intensive chemotherapy for newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD-positive AML, as shown in the QuANTUM-First trial [10], underscoring the growing clinical relevance of resistance mechanisms emerging during quizartinib-based therapy. Although the patient briefly received gilteritinib for two weeks perioperatively, BCR::ABL1 emerged after approximately five months of predominantly quizartinib-based therapy, consistent with a possible contribution of quizartinib-associated selective pressure. Because BCR::ABL1-specific testing (FISH/PCR) was not performed at first relapse or immediately after MEC therapy, low-level emergence during this interval cannot be completely excluded; however, conventional cytogenetics at both timepoints did not detect t(9;22)(q34;q11), and BCR::ABL1 was undetectable at diagnosis with detection only after prolonged quizartinib exposure, supporting this hypothesis.

Ponatinib was considered rational given its dual activity against BCR::ABL1 and FLT3-ITD [11, 12], yet the disease remained refractory despite the absence of ABL1 kinase domain mutations. Several factors may explain this: the coexistence of persistent FLT3-ITD, BCR::ABL1, and WT1 mutations suggests dependence beyond a single kinase pathway; BCR::ABL1 positivity in mature neutrophils implies emergence at an early progenitor/stem cell level less susceptible to targeted therapy; and the complex karyotype at second relapse itself reflects broader genomic instability. Together, these findings suggest that once advanced clonal complexity develops, even multi-kinase inhibition may be insufficient for durable control.

This study has several limitations: it is a retrospective single-case observation of uncertain generalizability; single-cell sequencing was not performed, so whether FLT3-ITD and BCR::ABL1 coexisted within the same clone or independent subclones remains unclear; and functional validation of BCR::ABL1-mediated FLT3 inhibitor resistance was not performed.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of relapsed AML with sequential emergence of FLT3-ITD and BCR::ABL1 after allo-HSCT, illustrating that resistance mechanisms may dynamically shift under sequential targeted therapies. Repeated molecular evaluation at each relapse is essential, and BCR::ABL1 emergence should be recognized as a rare but potentially targetable bypass resistance mechanism during FLT3 inhibitor therapy in AML.
