Exponentially accurate Spectral Monte Carlo Method for linear PDEs and their error estimates Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Jiaying Feng, Changtao Sheng †,⋆, Chenglong Xu †,⋆

Abstract
This paper introduces a spectral Monte Carlo iterative method (SMC) for solving linear Poisson and parabolic equations driven by α \alpha -stable Lévy process with α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) \alpha\in(0,2) , which was initially proposed and developed by Gobet and Maire in their pioneering works (Monte Carlo Methods Appl 10(3-4), 275–285, 2004, and SIAM J Numer Anal 43(3), 1256–1275, 2005) for the case α = 2 \alpha=2 . The novel method effectively integrates multiple computational techniques, including the interpolation based on generalized Jacobi functions (GJFs), space-time spectral methods, control variates techniques, and a novel walk-on-sphere method (WOS). The exponential convergence of the error bounds is rigorously established through finite iterations for both Poisson and parabolic equations involving the integral fractional Laplacian operator. Remarkably, the proposed space-time spectral Monte Carlo method (ST-SMC) for the parabolic equation is unified for both α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) \alpha\in(0,2) and α = 2 \alpha=2 . Extensive numerical results are provided to demonstrate the spectral accuracy and efficiency of the proposed method, thereby validating the theoretical findings.

Gobet and Maire

The key aspect of those methods lies in the precise reconstruction of the numerical solution from rough data during the iterative process, as well as the terms with different order derivatives of numerical solutions in the governing equation. This accurate reconstruction is achieved by using the connection relationship between derivatives of orthogonal basis functions and their coefficients, thus enabling the SMC method to obtain appealing spectral accuracy. 2
Spectral Monte Carlo method for Poisson-type equation in 1D

In this section, we propose an exponentially accurate Monte Carlo algorithm for the approximation of the Poisson equation in both classical and fractional cases, which draws inspiration from the spectral Monte Carlo method (cf.
[
26
,
27
]
) for classical Poisson equations.
Before that, we introduce the Monte Carlo method and review some relevant properties of generalized Jacobi polynomials (GJFs), which will be an indispensable building block for the construction of our novel spectral Monte Carlo method.

2.1
WOS method

We consider the following Poisson equation involving IFL with the Dirichlet boundary condition and
\alpha\in(0,2)
:

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=f(x),\;&x\in\ \Omega,\\[4.0pt]
u(x)=g(x),&x\in\ \Omega^{c},\end{cases}

(2)

where
\Omega
is an open bounded domain and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function in a suitable space.
In what follows, we will introduce the WOS method for the solutions of Poisson equation (
2
) in
[
52
]
. To this end, we first recall the Feynman-Kac formula that the solution of (
2
) associated with
\alpha
-stable L
\acute{\text{e}}
vy process
X^{\alpha}_{t}
and can be rewritten in the form of path integral (cf.
[
36
]
)

u(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{\alpha}=\bm{x}}\Big[g(X_{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}^{\alpha})+\int_{0}^{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}f(X^{\alpha}_{t})\,{\rm d}t\Big]:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\big[\Phi_{\bm{x}}(f,g,X^{\alpha}_{t})\big],\;\;\bm{x}\in\Omega,

(3)

where
\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}
is the first exit to domain
\Omega
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}
is an abbreviation for
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{\alpha}=\bm{x}}
.
Instead of simulating the irregular trajectories of symmetric
\alpha
-stable L
\acute{\text{e}}
vy processes, the Monte Carlo method takes advantage of the explicit expressions of
P_{r}
as the probability density functions, which gives the transition probability of the symmetric
\alpha
-stable process trajectory inside the ball during its passage from the center to the boundary
\partial\Omega
or outside the domain
\Omega
(cf. Fig.
2
).
Since the Markov process will leave the given domain at a finite time, then we denote the stopping step for the random walk
X^{\alpha}_{m}
by
L=\text{inf}\{m:X^{\alpha}_{m}\notin\Omega\}.

Figure 2:
The path of walk on
2
-D irregular domain

According to
[
52
, Corollary 2.1]
, we can employ a walk-on-spheres based Monte Carlo method to approximate (
3
).
More precisely, let
r>0
and
\alpha\in(0,2]
, and assume that
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
,
g\in L^{1}_{\alpha}(\Omega^{c})\cap C(\Omega^{c})
, then the numerical solution
u_{\ast}(\bm{x})
for (
2
) can be evaluated by

\begin{split}&u_{\ast}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[g(X_{L}^{i})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X_{\ell}^{i})\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[f(Y_{\ell+1}^{i})]\Big]:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[\Phi^{\ast}_{\bm{x}}(f,g,X_{t}^{i})\Big],\end{split}

(4)

where
X_{\ell}^{i}:=X^{\alpha,i}_{\ell},

0\leq\ell\leq L
,
X_{0}=\bm{x}\in\Omega
, and

\zeta(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n}}Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y},\;\;\;\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[f(Y_{\ell+1}^{i})]=\frac{1}{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{1}}f(Y_{\ell+1}^{i,j}).

(5)

In the above,
\widetilde{Q}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n}}Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,{\rm d}\bm{y}}
, and
Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})
denote the Green’s function for
\bm{x}\neq\bm{y}
is given by

Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\begin{cases}\hat{C}_{n}^{\alpha}\big|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\big|^{\alpha-n}\int_{0}^{\rho(\bm{x},\bm{y})}\frac{t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}}{(t+1)^{2}}\,{\rm d}t,\;\;\;&\alpha\neq n,\\[6.0pt]
\hat{C}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\log\big(\frac{r^{2}-\bm{x}\bm{y}+\sqrt{(r^{2}-\bm{x}^{2})(r^{2}-\bm{y}^{2})}}{r|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\big),\;\;\;&\alpha=n,\end{cases}

(6)

with

\rho(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{(r^{2}-|\bm{x}|^{2})(r^{2}-|\bm{y}|^{2})}{r^{2}|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|^{2}},\;\;\;\hat{C}_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{2^{\alpha}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha/2)}.

Now it remains to simulate the trajectory of
\{X^{i}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. To accomplish this, it is sufficient to determine the position of the next random point, denoted as
X^{i}_{\ell+1}
, based on the current location
X^{i}_{\ell}
.
Indeed, as long as we can determine the position of the current random point
X^{i}_{\ell+1}
and its distance to the boundary
r_{\ell}
, we can utilize the Poisson kernel to determine the jump distance
J_{\ell}
, which is given by

\displaystyle J_{\ell}:=J_{\ell}(\omega;r_{\ell},\alpha)=\frac{r_{\ell}}{\sqrt{B(1-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})-B^{-1}(\frac{\pi\,\omega}{\sin(\pi\alpha/2)};1-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}},\;\;\;\omega\in(0,1),

(7)

where
B^{-1}(\cdot\,;a,b)
denote the inverse function of incomplete Beta function
B(\cdot\,;a,b)
.
With the jump distance
J_{\ell}
, we can obtain
X^{i}_{\ell+1}
by multiplying it with the uniformly distributed spherical direction, that is,

X^{i}_{\ell+1}=X^{i}_{\ell}+J_{\ell}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_{1}\\
\sin\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\\
\ \cdots\cdots\\
\sin\theta_{1}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-2}\sin\theta_{d-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

Note that we can generate another random variable
Y_{\ell+1}^{i}
for inside the ball
\mathbb{B}^{n}_{r_{\ell}}
centered at
X_{\ell}^{i}
associated with the density function
\widetilde{Q}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
. The method outlined above is applicable to the one-dimensional scenario as well, with the spherical directions replaced by left and right directions. However, for the sake of brevity and clarity, we will omit these straightforward details in our discussion.

2.2
Generalized Jacobi functions and spectral approximation

Let
\Omega=\Lambda:=(-1,1),
the classical Jacobi polynomials are mutually orthogonal with respect to the weight function
\omega^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
over the interval
\Omega:=(-1,1)
, i.e.

\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,\omega^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\gamma^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\delta_{nm},

(9)

where
\delta_{nm}
is the Kronecker delta and

\gamma^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)n!\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

For
\alpha,\beta>-1
, let
\{x_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\omega_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
be the set of Jacobi-Gauss (JG) quadrature nodes and weights, which enjoys the exactness

\int_{-1}^{1}\phi(x)\omega^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)dx=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{x}}\phi\big(x_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big)\omega^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j},\quad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2{N_{x}}+1},

where
\mathcal{P}_{N}
is the set of all polynomials of degree at most
N
. Let
I^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N_{x}}u
be the Jacobi-Gauss Lagrange polynomial interpolation of
u\in C(\Lambda)
defined by

I^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N_{x}}u(x)=\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}u(x^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j})h_{j}(x)\in\mathcal{P}_{2{N_{x}}+1},

(10)

where the interpolating basis polynomials
\{h_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
can be expressed by

h_{j}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}a_{nj}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x),\;\;\text{with}\;\,a_{nj}:=\frac{\omega_{j}}{\gamma_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x_{j}).

We infer from
[
29
, (7.12)]
that the solution of (
2
) is singular near the boundary
\partial\Omega
which behaves like
{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}v(x)
, where
{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
denotes the distance from
x\in\Omega
to
\partial\Omega
and
v
is a smooth function.
To match the singularity, we resort to the generalized Jacobi functions (cf.
[
13
,
14
]
) with index
(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})
as the basis function, that is,

\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)=\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\,P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x),\quad x\in\Lambda,

(11)

which together with (
9
) leads to

\int_{-1}^{1}\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x)\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{m}(x)\,\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\gamma^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}_{n}\delta_{nm}.

(12)

In the above, the notations
\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)
and
\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)
are short for
\omega^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)
and
\omega^{(-\frac{\alpha}{2},-\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)
, respectively.
The GJFs apparently cover the case of polynomials
\mathcal{J}_{n}^{1}(x)=(1-x^{2})P^{(1,1)}_{n}(x)=\frac{2(n+1)}{2n+3}(L_{n}(x)-L_{n+2}(x))
as
\alpha\rightarrow 2
.
Throughout this paper, we set
\alpha\in(0,2]
, and then we claim that our method is unified for classical and fractional PDEs.
We also define the finite-dimensional fractional-polynomial space:

\mathcal{F}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{N_{x}}=\big\{(1-x^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\psi:\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{N_{x}}\big\}={\rm span}\big\{\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x):0\leq n\leq N_{x}\big\}.

To simplify the notation, we write
x_{j}:=x_{j}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}
and
\omega_{j}:=\omega_{j}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}
the Jacobi-Gauss points and weights, respectively. We then define the following generalized Lagrange interpolation

\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}u(x_{j})l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x)\in\mathcal{F}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{N_{x}},

(13)

where the generalized Lagrange interpolating basis is defined by

l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x):=\Big(\frac{1-x^{2}}{1-x_{j}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{j}(x)=\Big(\frac{1-x^{2}}{1-x_{j}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq m\leq{N_{x}}\\
m\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{x-x_{m}}{x_{j}-x_{m}}.

(14)

One verifies readily that
l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x_{i})=\delta_{ij}
for
0\leq i,j\leq N.

Clearly,
l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x)
is a non-polynomials basis, we find their relation with generalized Jacobi functions, that is,

l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}c_{nj}\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x),\;\;\text{where}\;\,c_{nj}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}}(1-x_{j}^{2})^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x_{j})\omega_{j}.

(15)

According to
[
43
, Theorem 2]
, we have

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!}P^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}_{n}(x),

(16)

which together with (
13
) and (
15
) leads to

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)&=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{x}}u(x_{j})\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}c_{nj}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{J}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{n}(x)\Big)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{x}}u(x_{j})\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}c_{nj}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!}P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\Big)\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\tilde{u}_{n}P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x),\;\;\text{with}\;\,\tilde{u}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{x}}u(x_{j})c_{nj}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!}.\end{split}

(17)

2.3
Implementation of spectral Monte Carlo iteration algorithm (SMC)

In this section, we restrict our attention to the following fractional Poisson equation with
\alpha\in(0,2]
and homogeneous Dirichlet conditions in one dimension:

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=f(x),\;&x\in\ \Omega,\\[4.0pt]
u(x)=0,&x\in\ \Omega^{c}.\end{cases}

(18)

where the limiting case
\alpha\rightarrow 2
becomes the classical Poisson equation reads

\begin{cases}-\Delta u(x)=f(x),\;&x\in\ \Omega,\\[4.0pt]
u(x)=0,&x\in\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(19)

Note that for the Poisson equation with nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions (
18
), we set
u(x)
to define on
\mathbb{R}
and denote its restriction on the finite domain
\Omega
by
u_{\Omega}(x)=u(x)|_{x\in\Omega}.
Given
g(x)
defined on
\Omega^{c},
we look for

u(x)=\begin{cases}u_{\Omega}(x),\quad&x\in\Omega,\\
g(x),&x\in{\Omega}^{c},\end{cases}

i.e., the unknown
u_{\Omega}(x)
, such that
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=f(x)
in
\Omega.
It is clear that we can write the solution as

u(x)=\tilde{u}_{\Omega}(x)+\tilde{g}(x),\;x\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
\tilde{u}_{\Omega}
(resp.
\tilde{g}
) is the zero extension of
u_{\Omega}
(resp.
g
) on
\Omega
(resp.
\Omega^{c}
) to
\mathbb{R}.

Then the problem of interest becomes
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\tilde{u}_{\Omega}(x)=f(x)-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\tilde{g}(x).
While for
\alpha=2
, for the nonhomogeneous boundary conditions, we can direct split
u(x)=\tilde{u}(x)+\tilde{g}(x),
with
\tilde{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, then solve
\tilde{u}
by (
19
).

The traditional Monte Carlo method often faces limitations in achieving high accuracy. To address this, various variance reduction techniques combined with spectral methods have been developed
[
26
,
27
]
. More precisely, we obtain an initial value using the traditional Monte Carlo method and construct a residual equation based on this initial value. By iteratively solving the residual equation using Monte Carlo simulations and updating the initial value, we can progressively improve the accuracy of our computations.
Now, we describe the numerical implementation of the highly accurate stochastic iteration algorithm for (
18
). To summarize, the detailed implementation involves the following steps:

Step 1:
Using the walk-on-spheres method to compute the initial iteration solution of (
18
) denoted by
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)
at Jacobi-Gauss points
\{x_{j}:=x^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
:

u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X_{j,\ell}^{i})\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{Q}}\big[f(Y_{j,\ell+1}^{i})\big]\Big],\;\;\;0\leq j\leq N_{x},

(20)

where
\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{Q}}
is defined in (
5
),
M
denotes the number of paths and the initial position of random process
X^{i}_{j,0}=x_{j}
, with
1\leq i\leq M
.

Step 2:
Compute the fractional Laplacian of
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)
from initial data
\{u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})\}_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
, i.e.,
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)
.
More precisely, we employ the generalized Lagrange interpolation (
13
) and write
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)=\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})l^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}_{j}(x),
then by (
17
) the exact fractional Laplacian of
u^{(1)}_{\ast}
reads

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\tilde{u}_{n}P_{n}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x),\;\;\text{where}\;\,\tilde{u}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{x}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})c_{nj}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!}.\end{split}

Step 3:
Using the walk-on-spheres method to compute the residual function
\varepsilon=u-u^{(1)}_{\ast}
from the following

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\varepsilon=f^{(1)},&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\varepsilon=0,&\text{ on }\Omega^{c},\end{cases}

(21)

where the updated right hand side source function
f^{(1)}=f-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}.
Similarly, through the Feynman-Kac formula and walk-on-spheres method, we have the numerical solution
\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}
of (
21
)

\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X^{i}_{j,\ell})\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{Q}}\big[f^{(1)}(Y^{i}_{j,\ell+1})\big]\Big],\;\;\;0\leq j\leq N_{x}.

Step 4:
Update the solution by
u^{(2)}_{\ast}(x)=u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x)+\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}(x).
Clearly, we can expect that the updated solution
u^{(2)}_{\ast}
is more accurate than the previous stage
u^{(1)}_{\ast}
.

In actual computation, we repeat Steps 2-4 until the maximum absolute difference between the two
solutions
u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}
and
u^{(k)}_{\ast}
is less than the desired tolerance or the machine accuracy.

3

The error estimates for SMC

In this section, we shall analyze and characterize the convergence of the iteration Monte Carlo algorithm proposed in section
2.3
, where we aim to estimate the error bounds in the sense of weak convergence

E^{\infty}_{k}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N_{x}}|\mathbb{E}(u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{i})-u(x_{i}))|,

(22)

where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N_{x}}
denote the Jacobi-Gauss points.

Then, we can derive the following error estimate for interpolation operator in (
13
) under
L^{\infty}
-norm, which will be used later.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2]
and denote
\tilde{u}(x):=\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)u(x)
. Suppose that
\tilde{u}^{(r-1)}
is absolutely continuous on
\Lambda
for some
r\geq 1
and its
r
-th derivative
\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u}
is of bounded variation
{\rm Var}[\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u}]<\infty
, then we have

\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r},

(23)

where
c
is a positive constant independent of
r
and
N_{x}
.

Proof.

Recall the interpolation error
I^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N_{x}}
(cf.
[
58
]
): if
f(x)
has an absolutely continuous
(r-1)
-st
derivative
\partial_{x}^{r-1}f(x)
on
\Lambda
for some
r\geq 1
and its
r
-th derivative
\partial_{x}^{r}f(x)
is of bounded variation

{\rm Var}[\partial_{x}^{r}f]
, then

\|f-I^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N_{x}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cN^{\gamma-{\frac{1}{2}-r}}_{x},\;\;\;\text{with}\;\gamma=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}.

(24)

In view of (
10
) and (
13
), we can easily check that

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u&=\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}u(x_{j})l^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{j}(x)=\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}u(x_{j})\Big(\frac{1-x^{2}}{1-x_{j}^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h_{j}(x)\\
&=\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\sum^{N_{x}}_{j=0}\frac{u(x_{j})}{(1-x_{j}^{2})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}h_{j}(x)=\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\Big(I_{N_{x}}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}\big(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)u(x)\big)\Big).\end{split}

(25)

Then, we derive from (
24
) and (
25
) that

\begin{split}\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}&=\big\|\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(I_{N_{x}}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)-(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u))\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq\|\omega^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\big\|I_{N_{x}}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)-(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\leq c\big\|I_{N_{x}}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)-(\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cN^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}_{x}.\end{split}

This ends the proof.

To perform the error bound of
u(x)-u_{\ast}(x)
, we first introduce an auxiliary function

\widehat{\Phi}_{\bm{x}}(f,g,X_{t})=g(X_{L})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X_{\ell})\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{Q}}[f(Y_{\ell+1})],

(26)

which replaces
\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}
in
\Phi^{\ast}_{x}(\cdot)
(cf.(
4
)).
In view of (
4
) and (
26
), we define the error function

e(f,M_{1},x)=\Phi^{\ast}_{\bm{x}}(f,g,X_{t})-\widehat{\Phi}_{\bm{x}}(f,g,X_{t})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X_{\ell})\Big(\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[f(Y_{\ell+1})]-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[f(Y_{\ell+1})]\Big).

(27)

By virtue of the SMC in Section
2.3
, we can rewrite the
u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x)
in terms of
u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)
as

u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{j})=u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}),(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t}^{i}\big),

(28)

where the initial position of random process
X_{j,t}^{i}
is
X^{i}_{j,0}=x_{j}
, with
1\leq i\leq M
and
0\leq j\leq N_{x}.

For simplicity, we define the following energy norm

\|u\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Lambda)}:=\Big(\int_{\Lambda}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)\big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\bm{y}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now present the convergence result for the bias of the iterative algorithm.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
u(x)
be the solution of (
2
) and
u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x)
be the numerical solution of SMC described in section
2.3
, and denote
\tilde{u}(x):=\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)u(x)
. Suppose that
\partial_{x}^{r-1}\tilde{u}
is absolutely continuous on
\Lambda
for some
r\geq 1
and its
r
-th derivative
\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u}
is of bounded variation
{\rm Var}(\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u})<\infty
. Then, we have

E_{k}^{\infty}\leq\rho E_{k-1}^{\infty}+cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}+cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{H^{\alpha}},

(29)

where the constant
\rho=cN_{x}M_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\max_{0\leq j\leq N_{x}}\big\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{j}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big\|_{L^{2}}
, and the constant
c
is independent of parameters
k,N_{x},M_{1}
.
For any fixed
N_{x}
and sufficiently large
M_{1}
, we have
\rho<1
. Consequently, the sequence
\{E^{\infty}_{k}\}_{k}
converges geometrically at rate
\rho
, up to a threshold equal to

E_{k}^{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho}\big[cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}+cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{H^{\alpha}}\big].

(30)

Proof.

Clearly, (
30
) is a direct consequence of (
29
), so we will only prove (
29
).
Since
u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x)
is obtained by interpolating at the Jacobi-Gauss points
\{x_{j}\}_{0\leq j\leq N_{x}}
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)

=u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)
, then we get that

\displaystyle u(x)-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)

\displaystyle=\big(u(x)-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)\big)+\big(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)\big)

(31)

\displaystyle=\big(u(x)-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)\big)+\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big(u(x)-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)\big).

To distinguish between the simulations conducted before stage
k
and those at stage
k
, we define the following conditional expectations

\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\big[Y|\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{k-1}\big],

where
\sigma_{l}
represents the information for the
l
-th iteration, with
l=1,2,\cdots,k-1
.
Recall the Law of total expectation, we have

\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[Y]\big],\;\;\;k\geq 2.

(32)

Thus, we first consider the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\cdot]
of the above identity.
Substituting it into right-hand side of (
28
) and taking the conditional expectation on both sides leads to

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{j})]&=\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})]+\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{1}]+\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{2}]\\
&=u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})+\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{1}]+\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{2}],\end{split}

(33)

where

\begin{split}&B_{1}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X^{i}_{j,t}\big),\\
&B_{2}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}),(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})|_{\Omega^{c}},X^{i}_{j,t}\big).\end{split}

We first compute
\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{1}]
. Since
B_{1}
is independent of the previous stages
\{u_{\ast}^{(j)}\}_{j=0}^{k-1}
, implying
\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]
, we then deduce from the above equations that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X^{i}_{j,t}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X^{i}_{j,t}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt\quad+\mathbb{E}\Big[(\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}-\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}})\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\mathbb{E}\Big[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{t}\big)\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[e\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),M_{1},x_{j}\big)\Big],

which, together with the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)]=\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{x_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)]
, implies

\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]=[u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u](x_{j})+\mathbb{E}\Big[e\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),M_{1},x_{j}\big)\Big].

(34)

Then, we derive from (
27
) and (
33
) that

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[e\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u),M_{1},x_{j}\big)\Big]\Big|&\leq c\mathbb{E}[L]\Big|(\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{\widetilde{Q}}-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{Q}})\Big[(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\Big]\Big|\\
&\leq cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{Var}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big[(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\Big]\\
&\leq cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}}\Big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\Big)^{2}\widetilde{Q}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,{\rm d}\bm{y}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{B}_{r}}\Big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u)\Big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\bm{y}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

(35)

Taking the absolute value of both sides of (
34
), and combining with (
35
), and (
23
), we arrive at

\big|\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]\big|\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}+cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{H^{\alpha}}.

(36)

Next we estimate
\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{2}]
. It is evident that

\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x)=\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x).

(37)

Due to the mutual independence of the stochastic processes, we obtain from (
33
) and (
37
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{2}]=\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}),(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\big|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\big|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[(\Phi^{\ast}_{x_{j}}-\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}})((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\big|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)\big|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 49.0pt\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\big[e((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),M_{1},x_{j})\big].

Using the fact that
\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\widehat{\Phi}_{x_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)]=\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\Phi_{x_{j}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)]
and

\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\Phi_{x_{j}}((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)|_{\Omega^{c}},X_{j,t})]=l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij},

we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[B_{2}]=u(x_{j})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{j})+\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[e((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),M_{1},x_{j})\Big].

A combination of the above equation and (
33
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{j})-u(x_{j})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]+\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[e((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),M_{1},x_{j})\Big].

(38)

Taking the absolute value of both sides of (
38
), and using (
36
) and the triangle inequality, we derive that

\begin{split}&\big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{j})-u(x_{j})]\big|\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}+cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{H^{\alpha}}\\
&\quad+cN_{x}\max_{0\leq i\leq N_{x}}\Big|(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\Big|\Big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[e((-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}l_{i}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x),M_{1},x_{j})\Big]\Big|\\
&\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}+cM_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{H^{\alpha}}+cN_{x}M_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\max_{0\leq i\leq N_{x}}\Big|(u(x_{i})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i}))\Big|.\end{split}

(39)

Taking the expectation and pointwise maximum norm on both sides, and using the law
of total expectation (
32
) and the fact that
\big|\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\cdot]]\big|\leq\mathbb{E}\big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\cdot]\big|
leads to the desired result (
29
).
This completes the proof.

Remark 2
.

It is worthwhile to point out that the weak convergence error bound in (
29
) is independent of the number of simulations path
M
.

4

Space-time spectral Monte Carlo method for parabolic equation

The proposed spectral Monte Carlo method for Poisson equation could be straightforwardly extended to the solution of the parabolic equation.
We consider the following parabolic equation driven by
\alpha
-stable Lévy process in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(\bm{x},t)+(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(\bm{x},t)=f(\bm{x},t),\;&(\bm{x},t)\in\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
u(\bm{x},t)=g(\bm{x},t),\quad&(\bm{x},t)\in\ \Omega^{c}\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
u(\bm{x},0)=u_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad&\bm{x}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(40)

Thanks to the Feynman-Kac formula of (
40
) (cf.
[
54
]
), we can express the probability solution as

\begin{split}u(\bm{x},t)&=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{\alpha}=\bm{x}}\Big[u_{0}(X_{t}^{\alpha})\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}>t}+g(X_{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}^{\alpha},\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}})\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}\leq t}\Big]+\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{\alpha}=\bm{x}}\Big[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}f(X^{\alpha}_{s},s)\,{\rm d}s\Big]\\
&:=\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{\bm{x},t}(f,g,u_{0},X^{\alpha}_{t})\big],\end{split}

(41)

where
\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}
is the first exit time to domain
\Omega
and
X^{\alpha}_{\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}
is the first exit location on the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}]<\infty
, for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
.

4.1

Stochastic algorithm for parabolic equation

In this part, we will provide a detailed implementation of a stochastic method for computing the approximate solution
u(\bm{x}_{j},t_{n})
at given points
\{\bm{x}_{j}\}
and
\{t_{n}\}
. Note that the algorithm description provided in the part only focuses on the high-dimensional case, while the one-dimensional case can be easily recognized as a special case.
It is evident that, for a given time subdivision, there exists a correlation
between the expectation of the movement time of the stochastic process
X_{t}^{\alpha}
within the ball and the length of the time interval, which plays a crucial role in the construction of our algorithm. Thus, for any given grid points
t_{n}
, we divide the time interval
[0,t_{n}]
into
N
equal parts:

\mathcal{T}:=\big\{0=t_{n,0}<t_{n,1}<t_{n,2}<\cdots<t_{n,N-1}<t_{n,N}=t_{n}\big\},

(42)

where
\Delta t_{n}=t_{n,\ell}-t_{n,\ell-1}=\frac{t_{n}}{N}
, with
1\leq\ell\leq N
. To this ends, we define the exit time of the process
X_{t}^{\alpha}
from
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}(X_{t_{n,\ell-1}}^{\alpha})
as

\tau_{\ell}:=\inf\{s:X_{s}^{\alpha}\notin\mathbb{B}_{r}^{n}(X_{t_{n,\ell-1}}^{\alpha})\},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq N.

(43)

In each time interval
[t_{n,\ell-1},t_{n,\ell})
, the process
X_{t}^{\alpha}
starts from the location
X_{\tau_{n,\ell}}^{\alpha}
. Within this time interval, the trajectory of
X_{t}^{\alpha}
remains within a ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}(X_{t_{n,\ell-1}}^{\alpha})
, and the stochastic process
X_{t}^{\alpha}
takes a jump at time
t_{n,\ell}
and leaves ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}(X_{t_{n,\ell-1}}^{\alpha})
. Hence, we can directly set
\tau_{\ell}\approx\Delta t_{n}
.
The radius of the ball can be chosen as

with constant

In this setting, the motion path of the process
X_{t}^{\alpha}
can be approximated by the motion paths of the balls, and each ball, with a fixed radius, is not required to be tangent to the boundary, which differs slightly from the inscribed balls in
[
52
]
. Now we are ready to present the following result on the location of
X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell}}
from
X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell-1}}
, which states (cf.
[
53
]
)

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2]
and
X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell-1}}
be the location of
\alpha
-stable Lévy process at
t_{n,\ell-1}
,
then the location of
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell}}
can be computed by

X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell}}=X^{\alpha}_{t_{n,\ell-1}}+J_{\ell}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_{1}\\
\sin\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\\
\ \cdots\cdots\\
\sin\theta_{1}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-2}\sin\theta_{d-1}\end{bmatrix},

(44)

where the
\ell

J_{\ell}:=J_{\ell}(\omega;r,\alpha)=\frac{r}{\sqrt{B(1-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})-B^{-1}(\frac{\pi\,\omega}{\sin(\pi\alpha/2)};1-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}},\;\;\;\omega\in(0,1).

(45)

Different from the simulation for Poisson equation in (

\Delta t_{n}

For any given

Now, we only need to determine whether the location of the center of

p,\;\;&\text{if}\;X^{\alpha}_{n,\ell}\in\Omega,\;\forall 1\leq\ell\leq p<N\;\&\;X^{\alpha}_{n,p+1}\notin\Omega.\end{cases}

(46)

4.2

Space-time spectral interpolation

Let
L_{l}(x)
,
x\in(-1,1)
be the standard
Legendre polynomial of degree
l.
The shifted Legendre polynomial

\tilde{L}_{l}(t)
,
t\in(0,T)
is defined by

\tilde{L}_{l}(t)=L_{l}\Big(\frac{2t-T}{T}\Big),\quad l=0,1,2,\cdots.

The set of
\tilde{L}_{l}(t)
is a complete
L^{2}(0,T)
-orthogonal system,
namely,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{L}_{l}(t)\tilde{L}_{m}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\dfrac{T}{2l+1}\delta_{l,m}.

(51)

Denote the shifted Legendre-Gauss quadrature points and weights by
\{t_{k},\omega_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
. We define by
h_{j}(t)
the Lagrange interpolating basis polynomials associated with
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
.
Using (
51
), the Lagrange interpolating basis
h_{j}(t)
can be expressed by

h_{j}(t)=\frac{(t-t_{0})\cdots(t-t_{N_{t}})}{(t_{j}-t_{0})\cdots(t_{j}-t_{N_{t}})}=\sum_{q=0}^{N_{t}}b_{qj}\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\;\;\;\text{where}\;b_{qj}=\frac{2q+1}{2}\tilde{L}_{q}(t_{j})\omega_{j}.

(52)

Now we turn to the space-time interpolation. To this end, we denote
\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}
be the interpolation operator associated with the grids
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
. To match the singularity, we employ the generalized Lagrange interpolation
\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
in space and standard Lagrange interpolation
\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}
in time, that is,

\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u(x,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{t}}u(x_{i},t_{j})l_{i}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)h_{j}(t).

(53)

Using (
15
) and (
52
), we can rewrite (
53
) as

\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u(x,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{N_{t}}\hat{u}_{pq}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\;\;\text{where}\;\hat{u}_{pq}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{t}}u(x_{i},t_{j})c_{pi}b_{qj}.

(54)

with
c_{pi}
and
b_{qj}
are defined in (
15
) and (
52
), respectively.
We can derive from (
54
) and (
16
) that

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u(x,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{{N_{t}}}\hat{u}_{pq}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{{N_{t}}}\tilde{u}_{pq}P_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\end{split}

(55)

where the connection coefficients is given by

\tilde{u}_{pq}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{{N_{t}}}\frac{\Gamma(p+\alpha+1)}{p!}u(x_{i},t_{j})c_{pi}b_{qj}.

Recall the derivative of Legendre polynomials
[
51
, (3.176b)]
and use the chain rule, we arrive at

\partial_{t}\tilde{L}_{n}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=0\\
q+n\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{2(2q+1)}{T}\tilde{L}_{q}(t),

which together with (
54
) yields

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u(x,t)&=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{t}}}\hat{u}_{pn}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\partial_{t}\tilde{L}_{n}(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{{N_{t}-1}}\check{u}_{pq}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\end{split}

(56)

where

\check{u}_{pq}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=q\\
n+q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}^{N_{t}}\hat{u}_{pn}\frac{2(2q+1)}{T}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=q\\
n+q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}^{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{t}}u(x_{i},t_{j})c_{pi}b_{nj}\frac{2(2q+1)}{T}.

(57)

4.3

Implementation of space-time spectral Monte Carlo algorithm

In this section, we restrict our attention to the following Parabolic equation with
\alpha\in(0,2]
and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions in one dimension, i.e.,
\Omega=(-1,1)
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(x,t)+(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x,t)=f(x,t),\;&(x,t)\in\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
u(x,t)=0,\quad&(x,t)\in\ \Omega^{c}\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(58)

As with the Poisson equation in section
2
, the limiting case of (
58
) when
\alpha\rightarrow 2
reduces to the classical Parabolic equation, where the main difference lies in the global boundary condition
\Omega^{c}
replaced by the local one
\partial\Omega
.
Using an argument similar to that of Poisson equation, we can easily deal with nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Now, we describe the numerical implementation of the highly accurate stochastic iteration algorithm for (
58
). To summarize, the detailed implementation involves the following steps:

Step 1:
Using the modified walk-on-spheres method to compute the initial iteration solution of (
58
) denoted by
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)
at Jacobi-Gauss points
\{x_{j}:=x^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{x}}
and the shifted Legendre-Gauss points
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
over
(0,T)
.

u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j},t_{n})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\begin{cases}u_{0}(X^{i}_{t_{n,L}})+\mathcal{Q}^{i}_{L}[f],\quad&\text{if}\;X^{i}_{n,\ell}\in\Omega,\;\forall 1\leq\ell\leq N,\\[4.0pt]
g(X^{i}_{t_{n,L}},t_{L})+\mathcal{Q}^{i}_{L}[f],\quad&\text{if}\;X^{i}_{n,\ell}\in\Omega,\;\forall 1\leq\ell\leq p<N\;\&\;X^{i}_{n,p+1}\notin\Omega,\end{cases}

where
M
denotes the number of paths,
L
and
\mathcal{Q}^{i}_{L}[f]
are defined in
(
46
) and (
49
), respectively.

Step 2:
Compute the two derivatives of
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)
from initial data
\{u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j},t_{n})\}_{j=0,n=0}^{N_{x},N_{t}}
, i.e.,
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)
and
\partial_{t}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)
.
More precisely, we can construct the space-time interpolation (
53
) from initial data
\{u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j},t_{n})\}
, and write
u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{t}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})l_{i}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)h_{j}(t)
. Thus, by (
55
) and (
56
), the two derivative of
u^{(1)}
can be computed by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{{N_{t}}}\tilde{u}_{pq}^{(1)}P_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\;\;\;\partial_{t}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)=\sum_{p=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{q=0}^{{N_{t}-1}}\check{u}_{pq}^{(1)}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2})}(x)\tilde{L}_{q}(t),\end{split}

where the coefficients are given by

\tilde{u}^{(1)}_{pq}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{{N_{t}}}\frac{\Gamma(p+\alpha+1)}{p!}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})c_{pi}b_{qj},\;\;\;\check{u}_{pq}^{(1)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=q\\
n+q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}^{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{t}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})c_{pi}b_{nj}\frac{2(2q+1)}{T}.

Step 3:
Using the walk-on-spheres method to compute the residual function
\varepsilon=u-u^{(1)}_{\ast}
from the following

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varepsilon+(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\varepsilon=f^{(1)},&\text{ in }\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
\varepsilon=0,&\text{ on }\ \Omega^{c}\times(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
\varepsilon|_{t=0}=0,&\text{ in }\ \Omega,\end{cases}

(59)

where the updated right hand side source function
f^{(1)}=f-\partial_{t}u^{(1)}_{\ast}-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{(1)}_{\ast}.
Similarly, through the Feynman-Kac formula and walk-on-spheres method, we have the numerical solution
\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}
of (
59
)

\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}(x_{j},t_{n})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{Q}^{i}_{L}[f^{(1)}].

Step 4:
Update the solution by
u^{(2)}_{\ast}(x,t)=u^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t)+\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\ast}(x,t).
Clearly, we can expect that the updated solution
u^{(2)}_{\ast}
is more accurate than the previous one
u^{(1)}_{\ast}
.

In actual computation, we repeat Steps 2-4 until the maximum absolute difference between the two
solutions
u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}
and
u^{(k)}_{\ast}
is less than the desired tolerance or the machine accuracy.

4.4

The error estimate for ST-SMC

In this section, we shall analyze and characterize the convergence of the space-time spectral Monte Carlo iteration algorithm, where we aim to estimate the error bounds in the sense that

E^{\infty}_{k,T}:=\max_{0\leq i\leq N_{x},0\leq j\leq N_{t}}\big|\mathbb{E}(u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})-u(x_{i},t_{j}))\big|.

(60)

where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N_{x}}
denote the Jacobi-Gauss points,
\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{t}}
denote the Legendre-Gauss points.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2]
and denote
\tilde{u}(x,t):=\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)u(x,t)
. Suppose that
\partial_{x}^{r-1}\tilde{u}(x,t)
and
\partial_{t}^{s-1}\tilde{u}(x,t)
are absolutely continuous on
\Lambda\times I
for some
r,s\geq 1
, and the variations
{\rm Var}[\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u}]<\infty
and
{\rm Var}[\partial_{t}^{s}\tilde{u}]<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}N^{\frac{1}{2}}_{t}+cN_{t}^{-s},

(61)

where
c
is a positive constant independent of
r
,
s
,
N_{x}
, and
N_{t}
.

Proof.

Since the Legendre polynomials can be viewed as a special case of the Jacobi polynomials with
\alpha=\beta=0
, it follows that

\sum^{N_{t}}_{j=0}|h_{j}(t)|=\mathcal{O}(N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}).\\

Then, we obtain from (
23
), (
4.4
), and (
24
) with
\gamma=0
that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}(\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u)\big\|_{L^{\infty}}+\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}+\big\|\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u-u\big\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}+cN_{t}^{-s}.

This ends the proof.

Now we prove the error bound of
u(x,t)-u_{\ast}(x,t)
. As with (
28
), we can rewrite the
u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x,t)
in terms of
u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x,t)
as

\displaystyle u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})=u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\Psi}^{\ast}_{x_{i},t_{j},m}\big(\mathcal{A}(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}),(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})|_{\Omega^{c}},(u-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast})|_{t=0},X_{t}\big),

where the operator
\mathcal{A}=\partial_{t}+(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
. In view of (
41
) and (
50
), we further introduce the error functional

e(f,x,t)=\Psi_{x,t}(f,g,u_{0},X^{\alpha}_{t})-\Psi^{\ast}_{x,t}(f,g,u_{0},X^{i}_{t})=Q_{L}^{i}[f]-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{\Omega}^{\bm{x}}}f(X^{\alpha}_{s},s)\,{\rm d}s.

(62)

Now we state the convergence result for the bias.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
u(x,t)
be the solution of (
40
) and
u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x,t)
be the numerical solution of SMC described in section
4.3
.
Denote
\tilde{u}(x,t):=\omega^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)u(x,t)
, suppose that
\partial_{x}^{r-1}\tilde{u}(x,t)
and
\partial_{t}^{s-1}\tilde{u}(x,t)
are absolutely continuous on
\Omega\times I
for some
r,s\geq 1
, and the variations
{\rm Var}[\partial_{x}^{r}\tilde{u}]<\infty
and
{\rm Var}[\partial_{t}^{s}\tilde{u}]<\infty
, then we have

E_{k,T}^{\infty}\leq\widetilde{\rho}E_{k-1,T}^{\infty}+cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}+cN_{t}^{-s}+\max_{i,j}\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[e(\mathcal{A}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u),x_{i},t_{j})\big]\Big|,

(63)

where
0\leq i\leq N_{x},0\leq j\leq N_{t}
,
c
is a positive constant independent of
N_{x}
,
N_{t}
,
k
, and
\widetilde{\rho}
is given by

\widetilde{\rho}=N_{x}N_{t}\max_{i,j}\Big|\mathbb{E}[e(\mathcal{A}(l_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)h_{p}(t)),x_{i},t_{j})]\Big|.

For any fixed
N_{x},N_{t}
and sufficiently large
M
, one has
\widetilde{\rho}<1
. Thus, the convergence of
\{E^{\infty}_{k,T}\}_{k}
, is geometric at rate
\widetilde{\rho}
, up to a threshold equal to

E_{k,T}^{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\rho}}\Big[cN_{x}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-r}N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}+cN_{t}^{-s}+\max_{i,j}\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[e(\mathcal{A}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u),h,x_{i},t_{j})\big]\Big|\Big].

(64)

Proof.

Since the proof for parabolic-type equations can be derived in a similar manner to that of Poisson-type equations, as shown in (
38
) in the proof of Theorem
1
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})-u(x_{i},t_{j})]

\displaystyle=[u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u](x_{i},t_{j})+\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[e\big(\mathcal{A}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u),x_{i},t_{j}\big)\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\sum_{p=0}^{N_{t}}(u(x_{n},t_{p})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{n},t_{p}))\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}\Big[e(\mathcal{A}(l_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)h_{p}(t)),x_{i},t_{j})\Big].

Take absolute values on both sides of the above equation, we find that

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[u^{(k)}_{\ast}(x_{i},t_{j})-u(x_{i},t_{j})]\big|\leq\Big|[u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u](x_{i},t_{j})\Big|+\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[e\big(\mathcal{A}(u-\mathcal{I}_{N_{x}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}u),h,x_{i},t_{j}\big)\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle+cN_{x}N_{t}\max_{n,p}\big|u(x_{n},t_{p})-u^{(k-1)}_{\ast}(x_{n},t_{p})\big|\big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[e(\mathcal{A}(l_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)h_{p}(t)),h,x_{i},t_{j})]\big|.

Taking the expectation and pointwise maximum norm on both sides, and using the law
of total expectation (
32
) and the fact that
\big|\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\cdot]]\big|\leq\mathbb{E}\big|\mathbb{E}^{(k-1)}[\cdot]\big|
leads to desired result (
63
).
Clearly, (
64
) is a direct consequence of (
63
). This completes the proof.

Remark 2
.

In the above Theorem, the convergence factor
\widetilde{\rho}
depends on the error functional
\max_{i,j}\Big|\mathbb{E}[e(\mathcal{A}(l_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)h_{p}(t)),x_{i},t_{j})]\Big|
. Similar to the proof of (
35
), we can obtain

\max_{i,j}\Big|\mathbb{E}[e(\mathcal{A}(l_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x)h_{p}(t)),x_{i},t_{j})]\Big|\leq cM^{-\frac{1}{2}},

which implies that as
M\rightarrow\infty
with fixed
N_{x}
and
N_{t}
, we have
\widetilde{\rho}=cN_{x}N_{t}M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rightarrow 0
.

6

Conclusions

We have proposed a new spectral Monte Carlo method for solving the Poisson equation driven by
\alpha
-stable Lévy process and successfully extended it to solve the parabolic equation driven by
\alpha
-stable Lévy process using a space-time Monte Carlo method. Both of these methods are theoretically proven to exhibit exponential convergence, and the numerical results have further confirmed this. Unlike traditional methods, the proposed stochastic approaches bypass the need to solve linear systems and allow for parallel computations of numerical solutions at each grid point. This parallelization is particularly beneficial for parabolic equations, where simultaneous computations can be performed in both the space and time directions.
Moreover, compared to existing works, our method has a wider applicability range, and importantly, it includes the classical methods in the limit case with
\alpha=2
, so we claim it as the unified method. While this paper only provided numerical results in one dimension, it is worth noting that by replacing the generalized Jacobi functions with a suitable set of interpolation functions, one potential candidate is radial basis functions. The proposed methods have great potential for generalization to higher-dimensional cases, and we leave this extension as part of our future work.
