Insulin resistance spawns hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis and diabetic ketoacidosis: an evolving metabolic cascade
Authors: Chaoneng Wu, Apoorv Tiwari, Maxim Zlatopolsky, Omar Qaqish, William E. Harder, Sonela Blaceri, Chadi Saad, Sujata Kambhatla, Ashok Kondur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Insulin resistance (IR) is associated with uncontrolled lipolysis, leading to the release of free fatty acids, ectopic fat accumulation, and hypertriglyceridemia (HTG). Adipose IR is the primary factor contributing to severe HTG, which interacts with deregulated hepatic IR (Hep-IR), resulting in hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTGAP) and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). This differs from the traditional concept of ‘metabolic syndrome’, and demonstrates a developing metabolic cascade triggered by adipose IR that fosters pathological changes, creating a self-perpetuating cycle and establishing a two-way feedback loop with Hep-IR. This loop leads to lipotoxicity, causing self-hydrolysis of the pancreas and ultimately resulting in HTGAP. Severe HTG and HTGAP prompt the release of counter-regulatory hormones that exacerbate hyperglycemia and lipolysis, initiating a downward spiral from HTGAP to DKA.

Introduction

Insulin resistance (IR) affects 40% of U.S. adults aged 18 to 44 [1]. IR is associated with an increased risk of disability and all-cause mortality in both diabetic and nondiabetic populations, resulting in significant direct and indirect healthcare costs [2]. IR is characterized by defective insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in muscle tissue (muscle insulin resistance), impaired suppression of hepatic glucose output (hepatic insulin resistance, Hep-IR), and uninhibited adipose lipolysis (adipose insulin resistance, Adipo-IR). Specifically, Adipo-IR leads to uncontrolled hydrolysis of stored triglycerides, releasing free fatty acids (FFA) and glycerol. These substances then travel to hepatocytes and muscle cells, forming diacylglycerol (DAG) and triglycerides, resulting in ectopic fat deposition and hypertriglyceridemia (HTG).

HTG affects 10% of the general adult population and 42% of those aged 60 and older. Severe HTG is defined as triglycerides ≥ 1000 mg/dl, accounting for 5% of all HTG cases. While genetic defects can cause severe HTG, IR-associated secondary HTG has become common, significantly increasing the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease due to accompanying IR-related comorbidities like hyperglycemia and hypertension [3]. Additionally, severe HTG can lead to hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTGAP), with a 3% incidence when triglycerides range from 886 to 1772 mg/dl, increasing to 16 to 20% when triglycerides are ≥ 1000 mg/dl [4]. Severe HTG has been observed in patients with new-onset type 1 diabetes who develop diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) [5–7]. Among them, insulin deficiency is believed to contribute to severe HTG by over-activating lipolysis, increasing FFA levels, and suppressing lipoprotein lipase, leading to decreased clearance of very low density lipoprotein (VLDL) and chylomicrons. Currently, the connection between IR in type 2 diabetes (T2D) and the cascade of severe HTG-HTGAP and DKA remains unclear. This discussion will explore how IR in T2D triggers severe HTG, which in turn leads to acute pancreatitis and DKA.

A self-vicious cycle of pathological remodeling of white adipose tissue and adipose insulin resistance

The cycle of lipogenesis and lipolysis in white adipose tissue, regulated by insulin, maintains energy homeostasis [8]. Insulin surge after eating stimulates glucose uptake and glycogenesis, converting glucose into FFA through de novo lipogenesis while inhibiting lipolysis. During caloric restriction, insulin levels decrease, reducing the inhibition of lipolysis. Stored triglycerides are then broken down into glycerol and FFA, serving as the primary energy source. This shift between lipolysis and lipogenesis, known as metabolic plasticity, ensures that other organs receive adequate energy without excess [9]. However, chronic over-nutrition causes white adipose tissue to structurally expand, leading to hypertrophy and hyperplasia, trapping fat to minimize exposure of lean tissues to FFA and reducing cardiometabolic complications [10]. Continued caloric excess leads to pathological remodeling of white adipose tissue, including decreased adiponectin levels, accumulation of pro-inflammatory factors like M1 macrophages, and a shift in adipocytes from energy storage to extracellular matrix production [11]. This transformation leads to Adipo-IR, exacerbating pathological remodeling and forming a self-vicious cycle. Clinical evidence highlights the difference between individuals with ‘metabolically healthy obesity’ and ‘metabolically unhealthy normal weight’ [12], emphasizing the importance of distinguishing between metabolic adaption and pathological remodeling.

Adipose insulin resistance/hepatic insulin resistance as a two-way feedback loop

With Adipo-IR, excessive FFAs disperse to lean tissues, such as the liver, skeletal muscle, and pancreas, leading to lipotoxicity or ectopic deposition [13]. These accumulated FFAs are reesterified to DAG or triglycerides, or both which can be integrated into sphingolipid biosynthesis, such as ceramide [13]. Both DAG and ceramides disrupt hepatic insulin signaling and mitochondrial biogenesis [14]. Increased DAG inhibits insulin receptor tyrosine kinase by activating the epsilon isoform of protein kinase C, worsening Hep-IR and impairing hepatic glucose metabolism [13]. Hep-IR hinders hepatic glycogenesis but promotes gluconeogenesis, significantly increasing hepatic glucose production and hyperglycemia. To compensate, the pancreas produces excess insulin to counteract glucose intolerance and hyperglycemia. Adipo-IR and Hep-IR are interconnected, forming a self-perpetuating cycle that leads to uncontrolled insulin production.

Excessive insulin stimulates lipid synthesis, exacerbating HTG. Hep-IR worsens HTG by increasing hepatic triglyceride production, reducing chylomicron and VLDL breakdown, and enriching low-density lipoprotein and high-density lipoprotein with triglycerides due to upregulated cholesterol ester transfer protein. White adipose tissue lipolysis is the primary cause of hepatic glucose production, as uncontrolled lipolysis continuously supplies FFAs and other bioactive lipid metabolites to the liver, known as lipid ‘spillover’ or lipotoxicity [15]. This is particularly true for mesenteric and omental white adipose tissue, as the liver is directly exposed to releasing factors from visceral fat tissue through portal vein drainage. Diabetes mellitus has been referred to as ‘diabetes lipidus’, emphasizing the critical interplay of Adipo-IR/Hep-IR as a two-way feedback loop in HTG, T2D, and related metabolic disorders.

Adipose insulin resistance/hepatic insulin resistance axis spawns hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis

HTG is the third most common cause of acute pancreatitis, known as HTGAP. HTGAP accounts for approximately 5 to 25% of all cases, with this percentage potentially increasing to 56% during pregnancy [16]. Evidence suggests that triglycerides ranging from 201 to 500 mg/dl increase the risk of acute pancreatitis by five times, while levels >500 mg/dl increase the risk by eight times [17]. The Adipo-IR/Hep-IR lipotoxicity axis plays a crucial role in HTGAP development, ultimately leading to transient insulin insufficiency and potential DKA.

Excessive FFAs in white adipose tissue cause pancreatic lipotoxicity. Pancreatic lipase, especially pancreatic triglyceride lipase, is released in response to dietary triglycerides, hydrolyzing circulating and visceral fat triglycerides. This exacerbates FFA-mediated lipotoxicity, leading to mitochondrial impairment and necrosis. Additionally, FFAs stimulate pancreatic adipocytes to produce metabolites, chemokines, and cytokines, activating inflammatory cascades mediated by tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin (IL)-1β, while inhibiting the antiinflammatory cytokine IL-10, resulting in inflammatory injury [18]. Furthermore, FFAs taken up by the islets activate intracellular calcium release, inducing oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and damage to cytoprotective mechanisms, like autophagy and unfolded protein response, leading to pancreatic islet necrosis [19,20]. Elevated FFAs aggregate into micelles, causing thrombosis and ischemia, exerting cytotoxic effects on acinar and vascular endothelial cells, activating trypsinogen with pancreatic auto-digestion, ultimately resulting in necrotic pancreatitis. HTGAP is associated with a higher mortality rate compared to other causes of acute pancreatitis as well as a higher recurrence rate (16 to 32%) [21], potentially leading to pancreatic exocrine insufficiency or endocrine failure.

The severe hypertriglyceridemia-hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis-diabetic ketoacidosis downward spiral

DKA manifests as the triad of hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis. How does the Adipo-IR/Hep-IR axis with consequent severe HTG and HTGAP, trigger DKA? Chronic over-nutrition or metabolic inflammation serves as the initial cause, leading to white adipose tissue remodeling with the Adipo-IR self-vicious cycle. Adipo-IR causes lipotoxicity, impairing hepatic insulin signaling and causing Hep-IR worsening hyperglycemia and severe HTG, further exacerbating adipose tissue remodeling. Lipotoxicity from uncontrolled white adipose tissue lipolysis and pancreatic auto-digestion of dietary and visceral fat spawns HTGAP, resulting in impaired insulin production. Insulin deficiency worsens the Adipo-IR/Hep-IR axis, aggravating severe HTG. Meanwhile, HTGAP triggers counter-regulatory hormones, like glucagon, growth hormone, catecholamines, and glucocorticoids, further propagating lipolysis mediated by hormone-sensitive lipase and adipose triglyceride lipase. This pathogenesis culminates in insulin deficiency in T2D. Consequently, the liver utilizes FFA as an energy substrate to produce ketone bodies, including acetoacetyl-CoA, acetoacetate, and β-hydroxybutyrate, leading to metabolic acidosis and osmotic diuresis. Persistent HTGAP and DKA may result in macrophage-mediated inflammation and dysfunctional endothelial cells, causing systemic inflammatory response syndrome and ultimately, necrotic pancreatitis, renal tubular injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and multiorgan failure.

Assessing insulin resistance in human

Hyperglycemic and euglycemic clamps are considered the gold standards for measuring systemic IR, but both methods are nonphysiological, experimentally laborious, and expensive. The homeostasis model assessment of IR assesses IR based on fasting blood glucose and insulin levels. The Adipo-IR index measures the sensitivity of adipose tissue to insulin by multiplying fasting insulin by fasting FFA [22]. Hep-IR can be assessed using the isotope-labeled glucose dilution technique or the Hep-IR index based on an oral glucose tolerance test [23]. Hep-IR is associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, which are primary causes of end-stage liver disease and transplantation [24]. Further investigation involves developing clinically applicable assessments and stratification of the Adipo-IR/Hep-IR axis.

Summary and perspectives

We present a working model where Adipo-IR intertwines with Hep-IR, causing lipotoxicity, severe HTG, followed by HTGAP and DKA (Fig. 1). Further research should focus on clinically applicable assessment and stratification of the Adipo-IR/Hep-IR axis.

Working model. (1) Chronic over-nutrition or metabolic inflammation is the original culprit that causes remodeling of white adipose tissue Adipo-IR. (2) Adipo-IR leads to lipotoxicity, impairing hepatic insulin signaling and causing Hep-IR, which worsens hyperglycemia and severe HTG, further aggravating adipose tissue remodeling. (3) Lipotoxicity from white adipose tissue lipolysis and pancreatic auto-digestion of dietary and visceral fat leads to HTGAP with impaired insulin production. (4) Insulin deficiency exacerbates the Adipo-IR/Hep-IR axis, worsening severe HTG and initiating DKA. Counter-regulatory hormones, such as glucagon and catecholamines, worsen inappropriate lipolysis and DKA. (5) Excessive FFA is transported to the liver to form ketones and propagate DKA. (6) Chronic over-nutrition and metabolic inflammation worsen Hep-IR and exacerbate ketogenesis during DKA.
