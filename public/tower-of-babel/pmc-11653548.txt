Stakeholder analysis of banning unhealthy product advertisement in Iran
Authors: Marziyeh Najafi, Ali Mohammad Mosadeghrad, Mohammad Arab
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe advertising of unhealthy products, including unhealthy foods, drugs with abuse potential, certain cosmetic products and services, and tobacco, has raised significant public health concerns due to its role in increasing consumption and contributing to the rise of non-communicable diseases. Policy development and enforcement in this area necessitate an in-depth analysis of the relevant stakeholders. This study aims to examine the stakeholders involved in regulating the advertisement of unhealthy products in Iran, providing critical evidence for shaping advertising regulations and reducing the societal consumption of such products.MethodsThis qualitative study, conducted in 2022, involved 18 experts experienced in the area of unhealthy product advertisement in Iran. Participants were selected through purposive and snowball sampling methods. The stakeholders' roles, power, positions, and interests were analyzed using Schmeer's guidelines.Results17 stakeholders were identified as relevant to the ban on unhealthy product advertisements, of whom 14 were classified as active and three as inactive. Key stakeholders included radio and television broadcasters, the Ministries of Health, Culture, and Industry, as well as political institutions. The Ministry of Health (MOHME) emerged as a leading advocate for the ban, while the Ministries of Culture and Industry, along with political institutions, were identified as possessing significant power with moderate support for the policy. In contrast, broadcasters and social media platforms were powerful opponents of the ban.ConclusionsThe study underscores the pivotal role of stakeholders with high power and medium interest in the success of policies aimed at banning unhealthy product advertisements. Key challenges included conflicts of interest, insufficient penalties for organizations violating advertising regulations, organizational disputes, limited human and financial resources, lack of clear implementation guidelines, weak community engagement, and inadequate oversight. Engaging key stakeholders effectively could mitigate these obstacles and facilitate more effective health policy interventions in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12087-5.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a leading cause of mortality worldwide, primarily associated with risk factors such as alcohol abuse, smoking, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity [1]. Addressing these diseases requires a careful consideration of environmental influences and the various risk factors that threaten public health. The implementation of laws and regulations aimed at reducing the availability and promotion of health-threatening products has proven to be a common and effective strategy. Among the typical regulatory measures adopted across countries are the prohibition or restriction of advertising, the imposition of taxes and duties, restrictions on sales to children, and the establishment of age limits for purchasing these products [2].

Unhealthy eating habits can lead to a range of adverse health outcomes, including high blood pressure, elevated blood sugar levels, obesity, and malnutrition, both at the individual and community levels. These conditions significantly contribute to the increased prevalence of NCDs and associated mortality [3, 4].

The global tobacco epidemic is responsible for more than 8 million deaths annually, with over 7 million resulting from direct tobacco use and approximately 1.3 million due to exposure to second-hand smoke [5]. In response to this crisis, the World Health Organization (WHO) adopted the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) in 2003. This treaty includes key provisions such as a comprehensive ban on tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorship [6]. Research shows that tobacco advertising and promotion significantly influence young people's decisions to start using tobacco, often portraying smoking as attractive and increasing its appeal among adolescents [7]. Studies suggest that implementing advertising bans can reduce smoking rates among young people [8–10]. However, stronger enforcement of these bans is critically needed, especially in light of ongoing efforts by the tobacco industry to circumvent these regulations [11].

Pharmaceutical companies exert a significant influence on patient care through marketing strategies aimed at both consumers and clinicians. Clinicians are informed about treatment options, while patients gain awareness of pharmaceutical products through drug advertisements. These marketing efforts take various forms, including visits from medical representatives, sponsored trips, brochures, and ads on television, magazines, and the internet. A growing trend in this domain is direct-to-consumer advertising (DTCA) [12]. The main challenge in drug advertising is ensuring that the potential benefits outweigh the risks. Proponents argue that such advertisements educate the public and encourage the appropriate use of medications, while critics contend that they can be biased, inflate costs, and offer limited health benefits [13].

Cosmetics include a broad range of products designed to enhance appearance, such as makeup, skincare items, perfumes, and contact lenses. Media often perpetuates unrealistic beauty standards, contributing to the increased use of cosmetic products and services, including tattoos [14]. However, certain cosmetic ingredients have been shown to negatively impact health. Furthermore, beauty salons can harbor microbes, increasing the risk of infections through the use of makeup and other related products [15].

Addressing the advertising of unhealthy products is a critical public health concern, as it shapes preferences, knowledge, and attitudes, thereby influencing people's health [16]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has been at the forefront of efforts to mitigate the harm caused by commercial advertising. Its initiatives include not only the prohibition of tobacco advertising [17] but also comprehensive recommendations on the marketing of food and soft drinks, the Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2013–2020 [18], bans on the advertising of energy-dense, micronutrient-poor foods, restrictions on the promotion of beverages to children, and prohibitions on the commercial marketing of all foods to children [19]. WHO also advocates for a series of "best buys" to prevent and control NCDs, including the enactment and enforcement of comprehensive bans on tobacco advertising, limiting exposure to alcohol advertising across various media, implementing policies that shield children from harmful food marketing, and launching mass media campaigns to promote healthier diets. These campaigns encourage the reduction of energy intake, free sugars, sodium, and unhealthy fats, while promoting the consumption of legumes, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables [20]. These initiatives demonstrate WHO’s commitment to public health and its significant influence on global advertising policies.

In Iran, a comprehensive law has been enacted to ban the advertising of unhealthy products across all media platforms and for all age groups, with the goal of reducing consumption. The Iranian Ministry of Health, in collaboration with relevant stakeholders, is responsible for annually developing a list of unhealthy products and disseminating it to all media outlets. This updated list includes food items (such as processed foods, cooking oils, potato chips, and candies), beverages (e.g., carbonated drinks with or without sugar, and energy drinks), tobacco products (e.g., cigarettes), cosmetic products, harmful services (e.g., tattoos), and drugs with abuse potential (e.g., tramadol tablets, acetaminophen). According to this law, the Ministry of Health, together with other organizations, sets taxes for the production of these goods. A portion of the collected taxes is then allocated to relevant organizations, including the Ministry of Health [21, 22].

The ban on unhealthy product advertisements in Iran has faced numerous challenges. These include navigating political, social, and economic barriers, addressing conflicting stakeholder objectives, establishing effective communication channels, and managing various aspects of policy execution such as planning, organization, leadership, and control [23–25]. The successful implementation of this law requires extensive collaboration among a diverse group of stakeholders. These stakeholders, which include industry representatives, advertising agencies, and health regulatory organizations, have diverse roles and objectives, often creating distinct challenges. The differing goals of these stakeholders, particularly in the context of advertising bans, significantly affect their level of cooperation, further complicating the process [26].

Given the harmful impact of advertising unhealthy products—i.e., food, beverages, tobacco, cosmetic products, harmful services, and drugs with abuse potential—on public health [27–31], this study aims to analyze the power, interest, and positions of potential stakeholders involved in the effort to ban such advertisements in Iran.

Methods

This qualitative study explores the stakeholders involved in the ban on unhealthy product advertising. Conducted between July 2022 and December 2022, the research employs Schmeer’s model to guide its design and execution [32].

The study examines indicators related to stakeholders' roles, participation, active/non-active, interests, power, and position. The role of stakeholders refers to their level and type of activity in banning unhealthy product advertisements. The Level of Participation framework helps determine how to involve stakeholders based on their influence, interest, or role in a project. It assesses their degree of involvement in decision-making. The Active/Nonactive analysis identifies which stakeholders need to be engaged. Active stakeholders are directly involved in the project—attending meetings, providing resources, or lobbying. Nonactive stakeholders are not participating but may have an interest or potential influence if mobilized, such as community groups or organizations that are currently uninvolved. Interest reflects their motivation to influence relevant laws and their desire to implement them. Power indicates stakeholders' access to key actors, media ownership, financial and organizational resources, and significant influence on public opinion. A stakeholder's position reflects whether they support or oppose the ban on advertising unhealthy products.

Research process

This research is part of a larger study aimed at developing a model for intersectoral collaboration to address unhealthy product advertisements in Iran. The main study was conducted in five stages: a literature review of mechanisms for intersectoral collaboration in the health system, qualitative interviews to identify challenges and solutions to banning unhealthy product advertisements in Iran, stakeholder analysis, and the development and validation of a collaboration model [33]. This study is the stage forth of the main research and it is started from step 4 to step 7 of Fig. 1.

The process began with researchers planning and selecting a policy (Step 1 and 2) based on the Ministry of Health's priority to address common challenges related to public health risk factors. After reviewing documents [23, 34, 35] and analyzing existing evidence from the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME), it became evident that there is an urgent need to identify obstacles and propose solutions for better collaboration in banning unhealthy product advertisements. To identify the names of potential stakeholders (Step 3), semi-structured interviews were conducted with 24 experts using purposive and snowball sampling methods. The purpose was to identify the challenges and potential solutions to prohibiting unhealthy product advertisements in Iran. These experts represented a diverse group of policymakers and specialists involved in the country’s efforts to ban health-damaging product promotions [25]. Each interviewee was asked to assess how legislation influences or impacts these stakeholders. The researchers also investigated the missions of stakeholder organizations and conducted online research to confirm the roles of each stakeholder. Additionally, the 24 participants were asked to rate stakeholders based on their position, power, and interest.

The inclusion criteria for selecting participants for participating in stakeholder analysis included relevant work experience, knowledge of unhealthy product advertisements, and a willingness to participate in the research. A self-made checklist, designed based on the researchers' input, consists of potential stakeholders identified during the interviews in stage 3 of main study(Step 4). The checklist includes: stakeholders’ interest, power, and participation on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = low, 2 = moderate, and 3 = high). Additionally, stakeholders' positions on supporting or opposing a ban on advertising unhealthy products were rated on a 3-point Likert scale (3 = support, 0 = neutral, −3 = opposed). We asked if stakeholders are actively involved in banning unhealthy products or not, and we reviewed documents that were in the Ministry of Health (leader in banning unhealthy products) about active involvement in discussions or forums and interactions. Also, open-ended questions about adding any potential stakholders and each stakeholder’s role in banning unhealty products advertisement. The checklist also included questions about the challenges each stakeholder faced in participating in the ban on unhealthy product advertising, as well as possible solutions for enabling them to share their opinions (see Appendix 1).

The self-made checklist was sent to same 24 experts which are participated in third stage of main study (Step 5). The research objectives were clearly explained to all participants, and informed consent was obtained to ensure their comfort in answering the questions. Anonymity was guaranteed to all participants to respect their privacy and confidentiality, reinforcing the ethical integrity of the study. The average scores for participation, position, interest, and power were calculated and tabulated based on the experts' responses. We analyzed stakeholders' roles and challenges using MAXQDA V.20 and PolicyMaker V.4 software, which were instrumental in organizing stakeholder information, providing strategies for managing stakeholder relationships, and creating effective visual representations to inform policymakers (Step 6, 7) [36].

For analyzing quantitative data, average scores are categorized as follows: Scores between 1 and 1.66 are defined as low, scores from 1.67 to 2.33 are considered medium, and scores between 2.34 and 3 are classified as high for power, interest, or participation. Position averages are interpreted as opposed when they range from −3 to −1.01, neutral between −1.00 and + 1.00, and supportive from + 1.01 to + 3.

The qualitative data was analyzed using Braun and Clarke's six-step framework [37] for thematic analysis. These steps are: 1) becoming familiar with the data, 2) generating initial codes, 3) searching for themes, 4) reviewing themes, 5) defining themes, and 6) writing up the findings. The purpose of thematic analysis is to identify significant or intriguing patterns or themes within the data [38].

The data was reviewed multiple times to extract initial codes related to the status quo, roles, and challenges. These codes were then identified and grouped into themes and subthemes based on their similarities. The codes, themes, and subthemes were reviewed and, if necessary, combined, modified, or separated to create a thematic map that reflects their relationships. The final themes and subthemes were labeled, defined, and illustrated with quotations from the participants' responses. Participants are called "P," followed by their assigned number.

Finally, the findings and proposed solutions for enhanced collaboration were prepared to assist policymakers in implementing the ban on unhealthy product advertisements (Step 8).

Results

Planning and policy selection

Banning advertisements for unhealthy products was identified as a top priority for addressing common challenges related to public health risk factors (Steps 1 and 2).

Identifying potential stakeholders

In Stage 3, we identified 26 potential stakeholders responsible for regulating unhealthy products [25] These stakeholders were categorized into five groups: the advertising industry (3 stakeholders), supervision and legislation (13 stakeholders), production (2 stakeholders), enforcing organizations (5 stakeholders), and the public sector (3 stakeholders).

Participant characteristics

The stakeholder analysis checklist was sent to 24 experts, and 18 responded (Steps 4 and 5). These experts represented a range of organizations, including the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME), medical universities, the Islamic Republic of Iran Medical Council (IRIMC), the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance (MOCIG), Iran National Standards Organization (INSO), the Iranian National Tax Administration (INTA), the Ministry of Industry, Mines and Trade (MOIMT), media, and independent experts. Of the 18 respondents, 50% held an MSc, and 61% were male. Most participants worked in the University of Medical Sciences (6 experts), the Ministry of Health (3 experts), and the MOCIG (3 experts) (Table 1). The data was gathered from experts and organized in Step 6. In the following section, we outline the findings from the data analysis (Step 7).

Advertising industries

Radio and television, as key players in the advertising industry, promote products and shape public opinion. These industries often profit from advertising unhealthy products, which creates a conflict of interest. Social media and the press also play a significant role in determining which products are advertised and to whom. While they profit from promoting unhealthy products, they also play an essential role in educating the public. This dual influence highlights their complexity as stakeholders.

Government institutions and regulatory bodies

MOCIG and municipalities oversee environmental advertising by regulating and issuing licenses to advertising centers. These organizations take action against advertising centers that break the rules. MOIMT, which supports producers, is another key stakeholder. A ban on advertising unhealthy products may lead to reduced production, job losses, and decreased income for producers, resulting in dissatisfaction, which runs counter to the Ministry’s mission.

Producers

Producers of unhealthy products are also important stakeholders, having invested heavily in production and marketing. These businesses are likely to seek ways to circumvent the ban through lobbying or modifying their advertising strategies to continue promoting their products indirectly. Without their full cooperation, enforcing the ban may prove difficult.

Regulatory and political institutions

Iranian political institutions have enacted laws to regulate the advertising of unhealthy products, with support at national, provincial, and local levels. Collaboration between political institutions, medical universities, local media, and related organizations ensures enforcement. Regulatory bodies in Iran, such as MOHME, play a critical role in enforcing these laws, ensuring that non-compliance is addressed.

Health organizations

The MOHME leads the technical and executive organizations responsible for enforcing the advertising ban, aimed at protecting public health. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA), IRIMC, and INSO play crucial roles in overseeing this law. They annually compile a list of unhealthy items, share it with relevant entities, and monitor compliance. However, this often leads to overlapping responsibilities and redundancy between organizations.

Taxation and enforcement

INTA imposes taxes on unhealthy products, with the revenue allocated to enforcing the advertising ban. This taxation system plays a pivotal role in ensuring that there are resources available for implementing these health-focused regulations.

Public Sector

The public sector, including non-governmental organizations, local communities, and the education system, plays a key role in addressing the impact of unhealthy product advertising. Educating the public on the dangers of certain products can reduce their influence, prompting manufacturers to improve product formulations rather than focus on marketing. Increased public awareness about the risks of smoking, unhealthy foods, or unnecessary medications can reduce the effectiveness of advertisements and diminish the incentives for companies to invest in advertising such products.

Key organizations such as MOHME, IRIMC, and INSO play a vital role in enforcing the ban on advertising products harmful to health. MOHME leads efforts by implementing the law and creating an annual list of unhealthy products. This task is carried out by a dedicated working group that includes members from other organizations, such as INSO. This group compiles the list of harmful products and shares it with the media, manufacturing industries, and relevant departments to ensure its implementation. Additionally, the working group oversees the regulation of advertisements for these products.

While the FDA and INTA support the enforcement of the law, they have a more moderate level of participation since they are not directly involved in advertising. The FDA authorizes the production of unhealthy products, and INTA is responsible for collecting taxes on these products, which are then allocated to MOHME. On the other hand, MOCIG, MOIMT, and municipalities have the potential to greatly enhance their roles. IMT monitors producers, while MOCIG oversees environmental advertising. Political institutions, regulatory bodies, and educational institutions have shown moderate participation in banning advertisements for unhealthy products. Since these organizations depend on government support, they generally comply with the law. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and community groups also exhibit a medium level of participation; however, their awareness of the role of advertisements in reducing the consumption of unhealthy products and lowering the incidence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) needs to be improved.

In contrast, radio and television, social media platforms, and producers have demonstrated low participation in enforcing the ban on advertising health-damaging products.

Interest Level

Numerous stakeholders in Iran have a vested interest in banning unhealthy product advertisements, particularly MOHME, INSO, IRIMC, FDA, NGOs, and the community. Banning such advertisements can help reduce consumption of products that contribute to non-communicable diseases (NCDs). For example, a participant from a food production licensing organization mentioned, "There are significant benefits for MOHME and other governmental organizations involved in food production licensing, such as INSO and FDA, in reducing illness and improving public health" (P3). These organizations are eager to ban unhealthy product ads, monitor advertisements, and report violations, contributing to reducing morbidity and mortality.

However, regulatory agencies face challenges in enforcing these bans, primarily due to unclear penalties and ineffective fines. As one participant who worked at monitoring team in INSO noted, "The law does not clarify the penalties for those who violate it" (P4). On the other hand, ministries like MOCIG and MOIMT are less inclined to support the ban due to their direct involvement with advertising agencies and producers. Their obligation to support producers conflicts with their regulatory role, as highlighted by a participant who works in MOCIG said, "If advertising agencies and producers lose their income, their employees are at risk of losing their jobs" (P18).

Advertising stakeholders, such as radio, television, and social media, wield significant power due to their financial interests in advertising unhealthy products. Banning such ads would threaten their revenue, as noted by one expert in unhealthy products policy expert: " It is a major source of income for the advertising sector. By banning it, they would lose a significant amount of money, which is detrimental for them. " (P5).

Power Level

Advertising stakeholders, such as radio, television, and social media, wield significant power due to their direct role in advertising. As noted by one policy expert in unhealthy products: "Key stakeholders in the radio and television sectors serve as intermediaries between advertisers and consumers, approving and disseminating advertising content" (P5). These platforms, due to limited oversight, have substantial influence over the content and distribution of advertisements.

The Ministries of MOCIG, MOIMT, and political institutions are identified as powerful regulators. Political institutions, in particular, play a crucial role in compelling other departments to collaborate in enforcing the ban. As one participant from medical university’s NCD office remarked, "Political organizations hold significant executive power, and the Ministry of Guidance and Industry becomes more cooperative when political organizations support the ban" (P9). While MOHME, INSO, IRIMC, and FDA hold moderate power in compiling lists of unhealthy products, their lack of direct involvement in licensing and enforcement weakens their overall impact. As another participant who work at the medical university pointed out, "The lack of follow-up on reported violations undermines our monitoring efforts" (P7).

Position Level

Political institutions, though instrumental in legislation, provide only moderate support due to a lack of awareness regarding the health impact of unhealthy product advertisements. A professional working in the NCD office emphasized, "There is often a lack of understanding about health issues in governmental positions; appointing health experts in key roles would greatly improve public health" (P6).

The FDA's moderate support stems from its role in licensing products, where a key challenge lies in distinguishing between manufacturing and marketing licenses. As one participant who is expert in this area and work in a medical university noted, "FDA… moderate[ly] participate[s]… because they give licences to producing a specific product and they ban producers to marketing. If a product is harmful to health, why is its production permitted? Once we’ve invested money into production, we found a way to sell it." (P15).

Discussion

This study identified 26 key stakeholders involved in the effort to ban unhealthy product advertisements, including the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOHME), radio and television, social media, the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance (MOCIG), the Ministry of Industry, Mines and Trade (MOIMT), and political institutions. Consistent with previous studies, significant stakeholders in food, beverage, tobacco, and cosmetic marketing policies included the media, internet platforms, and the Ministry of Health [35, 39–41]. The main challenges identified by stakeholders in banning unhealthy product advertisements are conflicts of interest, low fines for violators, internal organizational disputes, lack of resources, unclear guidelines, insufficient community participation, and weak regulatory supervision.

Media entities, including radio, television, social media, and the press, derive substantial revenue from advertising, which influences their reluctance to support the ban. Given the influence of advertisements on consumer preferences and behavior [42], regulating content is essential. However, current regulatory bodies lack adequate authority to monitor and prohibit unhealthy product promotions. Studies have shown that when health authorities like the Ministry of Health oversee advertising, healthier options are more likely to be promoted [43]. In the UK, government and political stakeholder involvement has been crucial for the success of programs aimed at reducing obesity [44]. Therefore, implementing laws requiring media cooperation with health authorities could effectively advance the ban on unhealthy product advertisements. However, efforts must be made to increase their participation. The lack of familiarity with the law’s objectives and potential outcomes, as well as the low priority given to banning these advertisements, are some reasons for their limited involvement.

MOHME, in collaboration with Iran National Standards Organization (INSO), the Islamic Republic of Iran Medical Council (IRIMC), and the FDA, leads efforts to compile lists of unhealthy products, monitor advertising, and educate the public. However, the absence of clear executive guidelines and repeated stakeholder responsibilities due to a lack of coordination hinders progress. A conceptual framework could provide policymakers with a clearer understanding of factors affecting healthcare service quality [45]. Weak monitoring and insufficient fines for advertising unhealthy products further diminish the effectiveness of these regulatory efforts. Additionally, the separation of production licenses from advertising permissions poses challenges, as highlighted in Abachizadeh's (2022) study, which emphasized the need for legal clarity in industries where advertising bans apply [23]. Stronger collaboration and mutual understanding between stakeholders can improve relationships and streamline efforts [46]. However, to strengthen the enforcement of this law, a more robust leadership approach is necessary. With increased authority and power, these organizations could better manage and enforce the law, ultimately reducing the presence of unhealthy product advertisements.

Government entities play a critical role in enforcing laws. When penalties for violations are severe, compliance increases [47]. Imposing higher fines on organizations that promote unhealthy products can encourage adherence to advertising regulations. Conflicts of interest between MOCIG, MOIMT, and advertising agencies complicate the multi-stakeholder collaboration necessary for banning harmful product advertisements. MOCIG influences advertising centers, and banning advertisements would affect their revenue. Similarly, MOIMT is responsible for supporting producers, which creates a conflict of interest when regulating advertising [48]. The Grigsby-Duffy (2022) study found that opposition to price promotion policies stemmed from concerns about financial losses and competitive disadvantages [49]. In some countries, industries have responded to regulatory calls for reform, even before formal policies were implemented [50], underscoring the importance of industry collaboration in advancing nutrition laws [51]. Industries must also increase their participation in enforcing or revising the law to ensure its acceptability to all stakeholders. This highlights the urgent need to raise awareness among individuals and organizations about the negative impact of harmful products and the importance of regulating their advertisements.

To resolve these conflicts, ministries should adopt a neutral stance on the advertising ban and engage in thorough participation assessments. Involving ministries in the planning process and effectively communicating the public health significance of the ban could foster cooperation. Additionally, encouraging research to introduce healthier food and beverage options can reduce resistance from producers. Modifying product formulations is a complex and time-consuming process, but supporting manufacturers in their efforts to develop healthier products can ease the transition to advertising restrictions [52]. Increased public awareness and active involvement from NGOs and educational centers can further drive change, as public demand compels producers and advertisers to comply with laws [53]. Informed consumers are less influenced by unhealthy product advertisements, shifting the focus to product quality over marketing [54].

This study is one of the first to examine the impact of banning advertisements for harmful products from a multi-stakeholder perspective. One of the main limitations of this research is that banning various health-damaging products across all age groups and media platforms is rare in other countries, which made comparisons challenging. The research highlights the complexity of stakeholder involvement across sectors, each with its interests and challenges. Additionally, only some individuals within each organization are knowledgeable about the law. As a result, despite multiple follow-up visits and efforts to engage them, some individuals were reluctant to participate in the research due to concerns about potential consequences. Future research should explore how to engage key stakeholders, such as producers of unhealthy food and tobacco products, to facilitate the smooth implementation of these regulations.

Strategies for effective stakeholder engagement

The research results revealed several effective strategies for engaging stakeholders in efforts to ban advertisements for unhealthy products. One key approach is to establish clear processes for requesting information from the Ministry of Health regarding advertisements for unhealthy items such as food, beverages, tobacco, and drugs. This fosters stronger collaboration among administrative bodies, including the Ministry of Health (MOH), the Iranian National Standards Organization (INSO), and the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance (MCIG). This strategy aligns with WHO's emphasis on multisectoral collaboration for effective health policy implementation. [55].

Also, implementing legal amendments to clarify penalties and create more robust monitoring systems is essential for addressing violations and promoting healthier production practices. By clearly defining penalties, these eliminate uncertainties that may obstruct effective rule enforcement. In addition, implementing strong monitoring systems plays a crucial role in ensuring accountability among all parties involved while also serving as a deterrent against potential violations. This combination fosters a more transparent and reliable regulatory environment [56]. Effective enforcement mechanisms and clear legislative frameworks are essential for tackling health-related challenges and ensuring stakeholder compliance [32]. These measures have the potential to transform neutral stakeholders into supportive allies while reducing opposition from those who are currently resistant.

Additionally, establishing an executive guideline that clearly outlines the roles and responsibilities of each organization will be critical. These guidelines will minimize overlapping roles and confusion, enabling each organization to concentrate on its specific contributions [57]. A monitoring committee, led by influential figures from political organizations, can help evaluate stakeholder involvement and secure support from neutral parties. Studies have shown that political leadership can enhance stakeholder commitment and help mobilize neutral or hesitant parties by aligning their interests with broader public health objectives [58, 59].

Public education campaigns are an important strategy in this area. Evidence shows that targeted media campaigns can increase public demand for healthier policies, pressuring resistant stakeholders to adopt health-promoting practices [60]. Campaigning across all media platforms to promote healthy eating habits and reduce unhealthy product consumption will further engage NGOs and generate public demand. This can help transform indifferent stakeholders into active supporters.

Conclusion

It is essential to unite key stakeholders, including MOCIG and MOIMT, political institutions, and MOHME, to achieve a neutral stance with sufficient authority to implement the ban on unhealthy product advertisements. As the primary governing body, MOHME must enhance its monitoring capabilities and enforcement authority to ensure compliance. Establishing comprehensive management measures and engaging influential stakeholders in leadership roles is critical for achieving these objectives.
