Unlocking Enhanced Photoluminescence Potential: Zinc (Zn)-Doped Cadmium Oxide (CdO) Thin Films for Superior Structural and Optical Properties
Authors: Habtamu Fekadu Etefa, Francis Birhanu Dejene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study explores the impact of zinc (Zn) doping on the structural, morphological, optical, and photoluminescent properties of cadmium oxide (CdO) thin films synthesized via a cost-effective chemical bath deposition (CBD) method. Structural analysis using XRD reveals that Zn doping induces lattice strain, change crystallite size, and causes peak shifts, as confirmed by both Scherrer and Williamson–Hall methods. SEM images show that moderate Zn doping (3%) enhances grain connectivity, surface uniformity, and reduces porosity, which benefits charge transport. In contrast, excessive doping (4%) results in structural disorder. EDS analysis confirms the successful incorporation of Zn, with increasing Zn content without significant phase separation. The experimental and DFT optical characterization shows that Zn incorporation modifies the electronic structure and defect states of CdO, resulting in a bandgap narrowing to 1.95 eV at 3% Zn doping, which enhances visible-light absorption. Photoluminescence (PL) studies indicate enhanced emission at 550 nm due to Zn-induced defect modulation; however, higher doping levels introduce nonradiative centers that diminish emission intensity. The formation of a CdO/CdO–Zn heterojunction facilitates charge transfer mechanism, improving PL activity under visible light by enhancing the generation of reactive species such as •OH and H2O2. Optimal Zn doping significantly improves the structural, optical, and PL properties of CdO thin films, making them promising candidates for applications in PL, solar energy conversion, and environmental remediation technologies.

Introduction

Due to their cost-effectiveness and high-performance characteristics, semiconductor materials with high conductivity and transparency, such as indium oxide (In2O3), indium tin oxide (ITO), zinc oxide (ZnO), and cadmium oxide (CdO) hold significant importance in semiconductor technology. − Because of its many benefits, cadmium oxide (CdO), an n-type II–VI semiconductor, attracts much attention. These benefits include a straight bandgap range of 2.2–2.5 eV, strong carrier mobility, the ability to emit at the green wavelength, and reduced resistivity due to intrinsic oxygen vacancy defects. , CdO thin films can be employed as transparent conductive electrodes in solar cells, window layers in optoelectronic devices, gas detection sensors, and photodetectors’ active layers. − Their high electrical conductivity, optical transparency in the visible spectrum, and tunable bandgap suit these applications. Additionally, due to their favorable optical properties, , CdO thin films are used in photocatalytic processes for environmental remediation and photoluminescence systems. CdO thin films have garnered significant attention in recent years due to their excellent optical and electrical properties, making them promising candidates for optoelectronic and photovoltaic applications. , With a wide bandgap, high electrical conductivity, and good transparency in the visible region, CdO is particularly suited for use in solar cells, transparent conductive oxides, and light-emitting devices. , However, despite these advantages, the intrinsic limitations of pure CdO, such as moderate photoluminescence efficiency and suboptimal structural stability, hinder its full potential in advanced applications. These limitations underscore the need for innovative strategies to enhance the structural and optical properties of CdO thin films.

Doping CdO with suitable elements has emerged as a viable approach to address these challenges. , Among various dopants, zinc (Zn) stands out due to its ability to modify the band structure, improve crystallinity, and enhance photoluminescence properties. , Zinc doping can introduce defect states, alter the optical bandgap, and improve charge carrier dynamics, all of which are critical for optimizing the performance of CdO-based devices. Recent advancements in the doping of cadmium oxide (CdO) with transition metals have been pivotal in tailoring its properties for photovoltaic applications. Transition metal (TM) doping can significantly increase electron concentration in CdO. However, this often comes at the expense of electron mobility. For instance, studies have shown that while TM-doped CdO films achieve high electron concentrations, their mobility decreases rapidly with increased dopant concentration, leading to higher resistivity compared to CdO doped with conventional shallow dopants like indium (In). The interaction between localized d-levels of TM dopants and the conduction band of CdO leads to the formation of subbands, resulting in a flatter conduction band dispersion. This effect increases the effective mass of electrons, thereby reducing mobility and affecting optical transparency due to enhanced free-carrier absorption. Zn-doped CdO films exhibit a polycrystalline cubic structure with improved crystallinity. Optimal Zn doping levels (around 6%) have been associated with enhanced grain size and reduced lattice strain, contributing to better film quality. , Various transition metal dopants can modify the properties of CdO, Zn doping offers a balanced enhancement of structural, optical, and electrical characteristics, making it a compelling choice for photovoltaic applications.

However, the synthesis method plays a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of doping and the resulting properties of the thin films. Several techniques have been explored to fabricate CdO thin films. Several techniques include chemical bath deposition (CBD), pulsed laser deposition (PLD), sputtering, sol–gel method, electrochemical deposition, etc. ,,, Chemical bath deposition (CBD) is a widely used technique for fabricating thin films, including CdO thin films, due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to operate at low temperatures. , This method is particularly advantageous for large-scale production and allows for precise control over the film’s composition, thickness, and morphology. In the context of CdO thin films, CBD typically employs cadmium salts (e.g., cadmium sulfate or cadmium nitrate) as the source of Cd2+ ions, with complexing agents like ammonia or potassium hydroxide to modulate ion release. , For instance, a study demonstrated the deposition of CdO thin films using cadmium sulfate and potassium hydroxide, achieving uniform and adherent films suitable for optoelectronic applications. ,

Despite the potential of Zn-doped CdO thin films, a significant gap exists in understanding how the CBD process influences their structural, optical, and photoluminescence properties, particularly in photoluminescence applications. Previous studies have primarily focused on other deposition methods or undoped CdO, leaving the synergistic effects of Zn doping and CBD largely unexplored. This study aims to bridge this gap by systematically investigating the impact of zinc doping on CdO thin films synthesized via CBD. By optimizing the doping concentration and deposition parameters, we seek to enhance the photoluminescence efficiency and structural integrity of CdO thin films, paving the way for their integration into high-performance solar cells and other optoelectronic devices. Through this work, we propose a pathway to unlock the full potential of Zn-doped CdO thin films, offering a promising solution to the limitations of pure CdO and advancing photoluminescence and prediction for solar cell potential applicability in next-generation technologies.

Materials and Methodology

Materials

The precursor solution was prepared using cadmium chloride (CdCl2), deionized water (DI), zinc chloride (ZnCl2), and ammonia solution (NH3). Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and ethanol were also utilized in the process and kept overnight. Glass substrates measuring 76 mm × 25 mm with a thickness of 1 mm were employed as the base for thin film deposition. Key equipment included a hot plate, vacuum pump, furnace, and coated beakers for holding precursor solutions. Optical properties were analyzed using a Shimadzu UV-1700 Pharm spectrophotometer, with measurements taken across a 200–800 nm wavelength range. Photoluminescence (PL) studies were conducted using a Hitachi F-700 fluorescence spectrophotometer with a 150 W xenon lamp. The thin films’ surface morphology and elemental composition were examined using a JEOL JSM-7800F field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) coupled with an Oxford Aztec energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS). Structural analysis was performed using a Bruker D8 advance multipurpose X-ray diffractometer (XRD) at the University of Free State.

Methodology

Figure illustrates a straightforward single-step chemical bath deposition (CBD) method for fabricating CdO thin films on glass substrates. The glass slides are cleaned using a diluted hydrochloric acid solution (HCl, 1:5 ratio), followed by thorough rinsing with distilled water and ethanol. The slides are then dried using specialized cleaning sheets. A precursor solution is prepared by dissolving 1.803 g of cadmium chloride (CdCl2) in 300 mL of deionized water to achieve a 0.03 M concentration. The mixture is vigorously stirred at 600 rpm using a magnetic stirrer for 1 h to ensure homogeneity. At room temperature, the solution exhibits a clear blue color, with an initial pH of approximately 3.80, measured using a pH meter. To stabilize cadmium hydroxide (Cd (OH)2), the pH is adjusted to 10.0 by adding ammonia solution (NH3, 25% concentration).

Schematic of preparation of CdO and Zn doped CdO thin film.

The cleaned substrates are immersed in the solution and heated at 75 °C until boiling begins. During this process, the solution transitions to a white color, indicating the formation of CdO. This transformation is attributed to Cd (OH)2 dehydration, forming CdO crystalline nuclei. These nuclei gradually grow and precipitate onto the substrates and container walls at 75 °C. The entire deposition process takes approximately 10 min under the specified conditions. For zinc (Zn) doping, varying concentrations of zinc chloride (ZnCl2) are introduced into the solution to achieve desired doping levels, such as 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 M, concentration relative to CdO. Once the target pH is reached, the zinc chloride solution is slowly incorporated into the cadmium solution under continuous stirring to ensure uniform distribution of zinc ions. The cleaned substrates are then vertically immersed into the chemical bath, which is maintained at 80 °C for a deposition period of 10 min. This controlled heating promotes the growth of CdO and Zn-doped CdO films on the substrates, allowing sufficient time for zinc ions to integrate into the CdO lattice structure. Following the deposition process, the substrates are gently extracted from the chemical bath and thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to eliminate any unreacted residues. The films are subsequently dried in a furnace at 350 °C for 2 h to enhance crystallization and improve the structural quality of the thin films. After cooling, the zinc-doped CdO (Zn–CdO) and CdO thin films are prepared for detailed characterization. These analyses aim to optimize the material’s properties, further advancing its suitability for applications in solar cells and photoluminescence technologies.

Computational Methodology

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the quantum ESPRESSO package, employing the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) as proposed by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) for the exchange–correlation functional. The calculations aimed to investigate the electronic band structure, projected density of states (PDOS), and geometric optimization of pristine CdO and Zn-doped CdO systems. The self-consistent field (SCF) calculations were initiated using crystallographic parameters of experimentally determined cubic CdO. The initial lattice parameters were set to a = b = c = 4.695 Å, with interaxial angles α = β = γ = 90°, confirming the cubic symmetry of the rock-salt structure. The valence electron configurations considered were Cd (4d10 5s2), O (2s2 2p4), and Zn (3d10 4s2). A plane-wave cutoff energy of 60 Ry was chosen based on convergence tests, and a Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh of 8 × 8 × 8 was used for Brillouin zone sampling in the SCF and PDOS calculations. To account for relativistic effects, spin–orbit coupling (SOC) was included in both pristine and Zn-doped CdO calculations, as Cd is a heavy element with significant SOC effects. To examine the effect of Zn doping, a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell of CdO was constructed. Zn atoms were introduced substitutionally at Cd lattice sites to simulate doping concentrations of approximately 3% (Zn atoms replacing Cd atoms in the 64-atom supercell). Geometry optimizations were conducted to relax atomic positions and minimize the total energy until the forces on all atoms were less than 0.01 eV/Å. Band structure calculations were performed along high-symmetry directions in the Brillouin zone, typically covering the Γ-X-M-Γ-R-X-M-R path. The visualization and structural analysis of optimized configurations were carried out using XCrySDen and VESTA software tools.

Results and Discussion

Characterization

The SEM images presented in Figure offer significant insights into how zinc (Zn) doping influences the nanostructure of cadmium oxide (CdO) thin films. The undoped CdO thin film (Figure a) exhibits a uniform surface with distinct grain structures. These grains are irregularly shaped, a characteristic often attributed to intrinsic defects and natural segregation during synthesis. Such irregular grain morphology can impact the material’s electrical and thermal properties by creating variations in conductive pathways. Without Zn doping, the electrical conductivity of the CdO thin film is primarily governed by intrinsic defects, such as oxygen vacancies or interstitial copper atoms. These defects contribute a limited number of charge carriers, constraining overall electrical conductivity.

The incorporation of Zn doping modifies the morphology of CdO thin films, as demonstrated by the SEM images in Figure b,c for different Zn concentrations (0.1 and 0.2 M), respectively. However, at a Zn concentration of 0.3 M (seen in Figure d), the SEM image reveals significant improvements in grain connectivity compared to the undoped CdO. At a moderate Zn concentration (3%), there is a clear enhancement in the uniformity of grain sizes.

The grains exhibit more regular shapes and sizes, suggesting that Zn doping influences the growth process of CdO crystals. Decreased porosity indicates that the material becomes denser, which can improve its electrical properties by reducing defects that act as trapping sites for charges. The grains appear more interconnected, forming a more continuous and cohesive network. This enhanced connectivity facilitates more efficient charge transport, improving electrical conductivity by reducing the scattering of charge carriers.

As the concentration of Zn increased to 4%, the morphology of CdO became irregular, as seen in Figure e. Evidence of phase separation and clustering of Zn dopant atoms leads to agglomerated structures. While moderate levels of Zn improve surface properties by enhancing grain connectivity and reducing defects, excessive Zn doping causes the segregation of Zn atoms, forming secondary phases that degrade the material’s overall performance. As observed in Figure f,g, the images exhibit a smooth surface, with Zn uniformly covering the CdO. An energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum for Zn-doped CdO is displayed in Figure , which provides information on the elemental distribution and composition of components such as zinc (Zn), oxygen (O), and cadmium (Cd). The presence of Cd (∼3.4 keV) and O (∼2.2 keV) is confirmed by the big peak in Figure a, which is in line with the expected composition of CdO. A nearly stoichiometric CdO can be confirmed by examining the relative strengths of the Cd and O peaks, which are frequently close to a 2:1.5 ratio. The effective doping of Zn in the CdO matrix is confirmed by the presence of peaks for Zn at different energies (∼0.5 keV and ∼8–10 keV) in Zn-doped CdO (Figure b). These peaks indicate that the substance contains zinc. The Zn content is shown by the strength of the Zn peaks concerning Cd and O. The modest Zn signal in this instance indicates a discernible but not overwhelming amount of Zn, which may indicate regulated doping without phase segregation. As a result of doping, the EDS spectrum displays an enhanced profile with Zn peaks next to Cd, and O. The Zn peaks become more noticeable as the Zn concentration rises, which could change the material’s stoichiometry. Zn inclusion affects the material’s optical characteristics, and EDS makes it easier to monitor how the elemental composition changes as doping levels increase. Phase segregation or defect development may happen at greater Zn concentrations; however, the spectrum points to balanced doping in this case. This EDS spectrum offers convincing proof of effective Zn doping in CdO. By examining the intensity and distribution of the Cd, O, and Zn peaks, we can determine the doping level and any possible modifications to the material’s microstructure and electrical characteristics.

Figure b presents a strong correlation between crystallite size and optical band gap as a function of Zn doping. The crystallite size (D), shown in black, decreases from approximately 22 μm at 0% Zn to around 17.5 μm at 3% Zn, before slightly increasing at 4%. This reduction is indicative of grain boundary formation and increased lattice distortion due to Zn-substitution, which inhibits crystallite coalescence. The band gap energy, shown in blue, exhibits a similar trend, decreasing from ∼2.6 eV (undoped CdO) to a minimum near 1.97 eV at 3% doping. This reduction in band gap is attributed to increased structural disorder and the formation of localized defect states, which lead to band tailing (Urbach effect). The slight band gap widening at 4% Zn may be influenced by the Burstein–Moss effect, where increased carrier density shifts the Fermi level into the conduction band. Collectively, these results indicate that Zn doping significantly influences the microstructural and optical properties of CdO thin films, offering a tunable approach for optimizing material performance in optoelectronic and photovoltaic applications.

Optical Properties

A graph illustrating the optical characteristics of CdO thin films doped with varying concentrations of zinc (Zn) was generated based on the absorption spectra presented in Figure a. The doping process induces significant electronic structural modifications, influencing the material’s bandgap and optical properties. These changes impact energy levels, solar energy applications, photoluminescence behavior, and the role of Zn in energy level alignment. The absorption peak observed around 320 nm and at 524 nm correspond to the electronic transitions of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. These transitions are governed by the material’s bandgap energy (E g). Zn doping enhances CdO thin films’ electronic structure by modifying the states’ density in the conduction and valence bands. Through interactions between its orbitals and the CdO lattice, Zn introduces new energy states or adjusts existing ones, leading to beneficial changes in the material’s properties. When incorporated into the CdO lattice, Zn atoms contribute to lattice symmetry adjustments or serve as defect centers, effectively tuning the band edges and introducing localized states within the bandgap. These improve the electronic structure, improving charge transport and reducing recombination losses, key factors for enhancing solar cell performance following the photoluminescence. Furthermore, Zn doping refines defect states, facilitating efficient charge separation and transport. In photoluminescence applications, the material efficiently absorbs photons with energy equal to or greater than its bandgap, initiating desirable luminescence reactions.

The absorption edge at 524 nm marks the wavelength limit for photon absorption in Zn-doped CdO. Zn doping enhances light absorption and extends the lifetime of photogenerated carriers, which is crucial for photoluminescent processes like water splitting and pollutant degradation. The introduced energy levels act as intermediate states, promoting multiphoton absorption and improving photocatalysis efficiency under visible or near-ultraviolet light. In summary, Zn doping modifies the electronic structure and bandgap of CdO thin films, impacting their use in solar energy and photoluminescence. Shifts in absorption and transmittance spectra highlight the material’s tunable optical properties, enabling optimization for specific applications. By aligning energy levels to enhance charge separation and transport, these materials become versatile candidates for advanced renewable energy solutions, including solar cells and photoluminescence systems for environmental remediation.

The bandgap energy of Zn-doped CdO thin films can be approximately estimated using Tauc’s relation to the optical absorption spectra. Since the energy involved in these transitions is directly related to the bandgap, the bandgap energy defines the range of wavelengths (or photon energies) the material can absorb, making it a key factor in tuning optical and electronic performance.

Essentially, incorporating Zn into CdO modifies its optical properties, which is crucial for tailoring the material for specific optoelectronic applications. Comparison of Optical Properties of CdO and Zn-Doped CdO

Figure A presents a comparative analysis of the electronic band structures of pure cadmium oxide (CdO) and zinc-doped cadmium oxide (CdO–Zn) to evaluate the effects of Zn incorporation on the material’s electronic properties. Band structure calculations reveal that both CdO and CdO–Zn exhibit indirect band gaps, with the valence band maximum (V BM) and conduction band minimum (C BM) occurring at different high-symmetry points. In pure CdO, the V BM and C BM are located at approximately −0.532 and 2.021 eV, respectively, resulting in a band gap of 2.553 eV. Upon Zn doping, the V BM shifts to −0.146 eV and the C BM to 1.810 eV, reducing the band gap to 1.956 eV. The Fermi level in both cases remains positioned near the conduction band, indicating n-type semiconducting behavior. The observed band gap narrowing from 2.18 to 1.87 eV with Zn doping suggests enhanced electronic conductivity and potential for improved optical absorption. These findings demonstrate that Zn doping effectively tunes the electronic structure of CdO, making it a promising strategy for optimizing material performance in applications such as photocatalysis, optoelectronics, and transparent conducting oxides.

DFT simulation of (A) band structure of pure CdO and 3% Zn-doped CdO. (B) PDOS of pure CdO and 3% Zn-doped CdO.

The projected density of states (PDOS) plots in Figure B, for pristine CdO and Zn-doped CdO (CdO–Zn) with spin–orbit coupling (SOC) included, reveal insightful details on the influence of relativistic effects on the electronic structure of these materials. The inclusion of SOC results in the splitting of spin-up and spin-down states, leading to an asymmetric PDOS profile with respect to the horizontal axis. This spin splitting is prominently observed in the Cd-4d orbitals of pristine CdO, where the PDOS exhibits a clear separation between spin channels. This reflects the strong SOC impact on heavy elements. In the Zn-doped CdO system, SOC-induced splitting is also observed in the Zn-3d and O-2p orbitals, albeit to a lesser extent due to the relatively lighter mass of Zn. In CdO, the Cd-4d orbitals significantly contribute to the deep valence band, while the O-2p states are more dominant near the Fermi level. The strong spin splitting in Cd-4d states confirms the substantial SOC influence. Upon Zn doping, the Zn-3d and O-2p states exhibit hybridization, modifying the PDOS distribution. Although the Zn-3d states show moderate SOC splitting, the effect is enhanced through hybridization with the O-2p orbitals, especially in the valence band region. This implies, while the PDOS alone does not show the full band structure, the SOC-induced spin splitting in the PDOS implies; lifting of band degeneracies in the actual band dispersion curves, possible narrowing of the band gap, changes in band curvature that can affect effective mass and carrier mobility, modified optical and spin-dependent transition probabilities.

In summary, SOC is a vital parameter in determining the structural stability, optical transitions, and application-specific behavior of CdO-based materials. The observed spin–orbit splitting in PDOS underscores the influence of relativistic effects in Cd-rich systems and suggests design strategies for tuning material functionality in optoelectronic and spintronic technologies. Tailoring the SOC response through Zn doping offers a pathway for controlled modification of both electronic and optical properties, enabling a wide range of device applications.

The emission spectrum signifies the energy released as electrons return to their ground state from excited states. This enhancement may be attributed to introducing new energy levels or localized states facilitating more effective electron transitions within the bandgap. The emission spectrum exhibits shifts and alterations in shape as the Zn concentration increases. At elevated doping levels, the predominance of nonradiative recombination processes may be attributed to the formation of defect states or clustering of Zn atoms. Since nonradiative recombination does not yield light, this phenomenon results in decreased efficiency and diminished emission intensity.

The provided energy band diagram in Figure illustrates the photocatalytic mechanism and band structure alignment of pristine CdO and Zn-doped CdO (CdO–Zn) relative to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE), emphasizing the roles of photogenerated charge carriers in redox reactions critical for photocatalysis. The conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB) potentials of both CdO and CdO–Zn are shown with respect to the NHE, highlighting significant changes due to Zn doping. For pristine CdO, the conduction band minimum (CBM) is located at −2.42 V vs NHE, while the valence band maximum (VBM) lies at −4.972 V vs NHE. Upon light irradiation, electrons (e–) are excited from the VB to the CB, leaving behind holes (h+). These photogenerated electrons can migrate to the surface and participate in reduction reactions, particularly the conversion of molecular oxygen (O2) into superoxide radicals (•O2 –), as the CBM is more negative than the O2/•O2 – redox potential (∼−0.33 V vs NHE). Simultaneously, the holes in the VB possess strong oxidative power due to the highly negative potential (−4.972 V), enabling the oxidation of water molecules (H2O) to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH), which are potent oxidizing agents in photocatalysis.

Schematic illustration of optical properties and photocatalytic mechanism.

With Zn doping, the electronic band structure of CdO is modulated. The CBM of CdO–Zn shifts further negatively to −2.630 V vs NHE, while the VBM moves upward to −4.586 V vs NHE, effectively narrowing the bandgap (E g) and altering charge separation dynamics. The more negative CBM in CdO–Zn enhances the reduction potential of photogenerated electrons, improving the generation of superoxide radicals. Furthermore, although the VBM of CdO–Zn is less negative than that of pristine CdO, it still retains sufficient potential to oxidize H2O into •OH radicals. This band alignment indicates a more efficient spatial separation of charge carriers in CdO–Zn, facilitating both oxidative and reductive pathways crucial for photocatalytic processes.

Overall, the diagram underscores that Zn doping into CdO improves photocatalytic performance by tuning the band structure to enhance charge carrier activity and redox efficiency. The enhanced electron reduction ability and sufficient hole oxidation power in CdO–Zn highlight its superior potential for applications in photocatalytic degradation, water splitting, or environmental remediation.

Conclusions

This study successfully synthesized zinc-doped cadmium oxide (CdO/Zn) thin films using the chemical bath deposition (CBD) method, demonstrating that zinc incorporation significantly enhances the structural and optical properties of the films. XRD analysis revealed improved crystallinity and reduced defect density with increasing zinc concentration, with optimal performance observed at 3% Zn. Notably, zinc doping effectively narrowed the optical bandgap from 2.56 to 1.950 eV, enabling enhanced absorption in the visible to near-infrared spectrum. A strong photoluminescence emission peak at 550 nm further confirms the material’s potential for light-based applications. These findings highlight the promise of Zn-doped CdO thin films for use in photocatalysis and photovoltaic technologies. However, the current study is limited to structural and optical characterizations. Future work should focus on comprehensive electrical measurements such as resistivity, mobility, and carrier concentration to better understand the charge transport mechanisms. Additionally, practical integration into prototype devices will be essential to assess long-term stability, efficiency, and real-world performance. These next steps are critical to validating the full potential of Zn-doped CdO thin films in energy conversion and environmental applications.
