The impact of hyperthyroidism on the hematopoietic system
Authors: Wen Yang, Yanting Li, Li Ma, Yaxian Tan, Fan Zhou, Zeping Zhou, Jia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with hyperthyroidism frequently present with hematological abnormalities; however, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study enrolled 166 treatment-naïve hyperthyroidism patients and 56 age- and sex-matched healthy controls, comparing their complete blood count parameters. Additionally, a murine model of hyperthyroidism was established, and flow cytometry was employed to assess the quantity, proportion, cell cycle status, and apoptosis of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the bone marrow. Clinical data analysis revealed that, compared to the healthy control group, hyperthyroidism patients exhibited significant reductions in peripheral white blood cell counts—particularly neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils—as well as in red blood cell parameters, including hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (HCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC). These alterations were more pronounced in female patients. Animal experiments confirmed that hyperthyroid mice also developed leukopenia and neutropenia. Further investigation demonstrated a decreased number of HSPCs in the bone marrow of hyperthyroid mice. The primary cause was identified as cell cycle arrest rather than increased apoptosis. This study reveals that elevated thyroid hormone levels may lead to alterations in peripheral blood cell counts by inducing cell cycle arrest in bone marrow HSPCs, thereby impairing their proliferation and differentiation capabilities. These findings provide a novel cellular perspective for understanding the mechanisms underlying hematological abnormalities in hyperthyroidism and offer a theoretical foundation for potential clinical intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01999-z.Keywords: Hyperthyroidism, Complete blood cell count, Bone marrow, Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor cells

Introduction

Hyperthyroidism is a common endocrine and metabolic disorder characterized by excessive synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones, including total triiodothyronine (TT3) and total thyroxine (TT4). Biochemically, it typically presents as elevated serum levels of free T4 (FT4) and free T3 (FT3), accompanied by feedback suppression of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) [1]. Clinically, patients often exhibit symptoms of a hypermetabolic state—such as palpitations, excessive sweating, and weight loss—along with dysfunctions in the nervous, cardiovascular, and other systems, which collectively pose a substantial threat to the patient’s quality of life [2, 3].

Beyond these classical manifestations, a growing body of clinical observations and studies has highlighted the profound influence of thyroid hormones on the hematopoietic system, given their role as broad-acting physiological regulators [4, 5]. Thyroid hormone receptors are widely expressed on hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and various progenitor cells, suggesting that thyroid hormones may directly participate in the regulation of hematopoiesis [6]. Clinically, hyperthyroid patients often present with various degrees of hematological abnormalities, including leukopenia, anemia, and alterations in platelet counts [7, 8]. These changes may be attributed to direct effects of thyroid hormones on the bone marrow hematopoietic microenvironment, indirect influences of autoimmune factors, or nutrient depletion resulting from the hypermetabolic state. However, research into the mechanisms underlying hyperthyroidism-associated hematological abnormalities remains limited. Most existing studies have focused on descriptive analyses of peripheral blood parameters [9], with a lack of systematic investigation into the underlying cellular dynamics and alterations at the level of HSPCs.

In this study, we analyzed complete blood count parameters from hyperthyroid patients and healthy controls, combined with assessments in a murine model of hyperthyroidism, to evaluate the impact of hyperthyroidism on the hematopoietic system. Our work aims to provide new insights into the mechanisms of hematopoietic dysfunction induced by hyperthyroidism.

Methods

Patient

A total of 166 patients diagnosed with hyperthyroidism at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between January 2021 and January 2024 were enrolled in this study. Clinical data, including gender, age, thyroid hormone levels, and complete blood count parameters, were collected. All hyperthyroid patients were treatment-naïve at the time of enrollment. Additionally, 56 healthy individuals with normal thyroid function, matched for age and gender, were recruited from the health examination center of the same hospital to serve as the control group.

Laboratory analysis

During the initial examination following admission, parameters related to thyroid function and complete blood count were collected. Thyroid function assessments included: TSH, TT3, FT3, TT4, FT4, thyroglobulin antibody (TGAb), thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), thyroglobulin (TG), and TSH receptor antibody (TRAb). The normal reference ranges for these indices were provided by the hospital’s clinical laboratory and are as follows: TSH: 0.24–4.20 mIU/L, TT3: 0.76–2.20 µg/L, FT3: 3.39–7.14 pmol/L, TT4: 45–126 µg/L, FT4: 10.29–21.88 pmol/L, TGAb: 0–115 IU/mL, TPOAb: 0–34 IU/mL, TG: 0–25 ng/mL, TRAb: 0–14 IU/L. Complete blood count parameters included: white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), HGB, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, eosinophil count, basophil count, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, red cell distribution width-coefficient of variation (RDW-CV), red cell distribution width-standard deviation (RDW-SD), platelet distribution width (PDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), plateletcrit (PCT), and large platelet ratio (P-LCR). The normal reference ranges were: WBC: 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L; Neutrophils: 1.8–6.3 × 10⁹/L; Lymphocytes: 1.1–3.2 × 10⁹/L; Monocytes: 0.1–0.6 × 10⁹/L; Eosinophils: 0.02–0.52 × 10⁹/L; Basophils: 0–0.06 × 10⁹/L; RBC: 4.3–5.8 × 10¹²/L; HGB: 130–175 g/L; HCT: 0.4–0.5 L/L; MCV: 82–100 fL; MCH: 27–34 pg; MCHC: 316–354 g/L; RDW-CV: 11.5–14.5%; RDW-SD: 39–46 fL; PLT: 125–350 × 10⁹/L; PDW: 9–17%; MPV: 7.6–13.2 fL; PCT: 0.17–0.35%; P-LCR: 13–43%. The complete blood count parameters for mice were measured using a fully automated hematology analyzer, model BM830, manufactured by Tai’an Kangyu Medical Equipment Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China).

Experimental animals and design

Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. They were kept under controlled environmental conditions: temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 AM). Mice had ad libitum access to standard laboratory rodent chow and autoclaved drinking water. Cage bedding was changed regularly. In brief, a total of 28 male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to two groups (14 mice per group): the hyperthyroidism (T4) group and the control (CTRL) group. To validate the model, serum TT4 levels were measured weekly in a subset of 5 randomly selected mice per group. For the experimental endpoints, complete blood count analysis was performed on all 14 mice per group, while flow cytometric analyses were conducted on subsets of these animals: peripheral blood B cells, T cells, and myeloid cells were assessed in 6 mice per group, and bone marrow analyses (including hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell subtyping, cell cycle, and apoptosis) were carried out in 5 mice per group.

Hyperthyroid mouse model

To establish the hyperthyroid model, 6–8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were administered L-Thyroxine (MCE, #HY-18341) via drinking water for three consecutive weeks (The concentration of L-Thyroxine in drinking water is 1.55 µg/mL), supplemented with intraperitoneal injections of L-Thyroxine (1.0 mg/kg, every other day). Control mice received equivalent volumes of normal saline. Blood was collected weekly from the tail vein, and serum thyroid hormone levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (ORIGENE, #EA100835) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Changes in body weight and water consumption were also recorded.

Flow cytometry

Bone marrow cells were harvested from bilateral femurs and tibiae of mice to prepare single-cell suspensions. The following fluorescently labeled antibodies were used for surface and intracellular staining: anti-lineage (Invitrogen, #88–7772−72), anti-Sca1 (Invitrogen, #45–5981−82), anti-c-Kit (Invitrogen, #47–1171−82), anti-CD34 (Invitrogen, #11–0341−82), anti-Flk2 (Invitrogen, #17–1351−82), anti-CD127 (Invitrogen, #12–1271−82), anti-CD16/32 (Invitrogen, #48–0161−82), anti-Ki67 (Invitrogen, #25–5698−82). Hoechst 33,342 (Sigma, #B2261) and DAPI (Sigma, #D9542) were used for nuclear staining, and Annexin V (BioLegend, #640937) was employed for apoptosis detection.

Data analysis

Based on the normality of the data distribution, serum levels of TT3, TT4, FT3, and FT4 were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to their median or mean values. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (La Jolla, CA, USA) and SPSS 20.0 (IBM, Chicago, USA). Data conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test or Mann–Whitney U test. Considering the difference in sex composition between the groups, sex was included as a covariate in the statistical models to reduce potential confounding bias.

A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with levels of P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001 specifically indicated in the analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of hyperthyroid patients and healthy controls

A total of 166 patients with hyperthyroidism and 56 healthy controls were included in this study (Table 1). The hyperthyroid patient group had a median age of 53 years (49–60) and consisted of 119 females and 47 males. The healthy control group had a median age of 58.5 years (49–63), comprising 37 females and 19 males. No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding age or gender distribution. Thyroid function profiles were systematically compared between the groups. As expected, hyperthyroid patients exhibited significantly elevated levels of TT3 (2.99 (2.13–4.14) vs. 1.04 (0.96–1.09), p < 0.0001), FT3 (15.42 (10.13–21.76) vs. 4.79 ± 0.47, p < 0.0001), TT4 (172.2 ± 51.89 vs. 78.4 (74.31–86.3), p < 0.0001), and FT4 (45.75 (32.42–75.6) vs. 15.28 (14.58–17.93), p < 0.0001) compared to healthy controls. In contrast, TSH levels were markedly suppressed in the hyperthyroidism group (0.01 (0.01–0.01) vs. 2.25 ± 0.93, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, levels of thyroid-related autoantibodies were significantly higher in the hyperthyroidism group than in the control group, including TGAb (62.57 (18.54–415) vs. 9.26 (4.72–17.31), p = 0.0005), TPOAb (128.1(21.63–288.2) vs. 14.51(12.84–17.29), p < 0.0001), TG (35.29 (3.86–120.7) vs. 8.96 (6.5–13.27), p = 0.0008), and TRAb (10.81 (5.26–22.76) vs. 1.07 (0.8–1.37), p < 0.0001).

Hematological parameters stratified by thyroid hormone levels in hyperthyroid patients

Hyperthyroid patients were stratified into high- and low-level groups for TT3, FT3, TT4, and FT4, using the mean or median as the cutoff value based on the normality of the data distribution. Hematological parameters were subsequently compared across healthy controls (HC), the low hormone-level group (Low), and the high hormone-level group (High) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2). The results demonstrated that compared to the HC group, the high-TT3, high-FT3, and high-FT4 groups exhibited a more pronounced reduction in WBC count, which was primarily driven by decreases in neutrophil and eosinophil counts. Furthermore, HGB levels were consistently lower in all hyperthyroid patient groups, regardless of hormone level, compared to the HC group. However, the high hormone-level groups showed more substantial declines in MCV, MCH, and MCHC. Notably, across all four hormone stratification analyses, the low hormone-level groups generally exhibited lower HCT levels compared to their high hormone-level counterparts.

Bone marrow hematopoiesis in hyperthyroid mice

After successful establishment of the hyperthyroid mouse model, complete blood count analysis was performed. The results showed that, compared to the CTRL group, mice in the T4 group exhibited significant reductions in white blood cell, mainly neutrophils, while other parameters showed no marked alterations (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3). Further flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that the proportions of both T cells and myeloid cells in the peripheral blood of hyperthyroid mice were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. 2c). Within the bone marrow hematopoietic system, the absolute numbers of both HSCs and hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) were lower in the hyperthyroidism group compared to controls. Analysis of hematopoietic stem cell subpopulations revealed that, in the T4 group, the proportion of long-term hematopoietic stem cells (LT-HSC) increased, while that of short-term hematopoietic stem cells (ST-HSC) decreased, with no significant change observed in the proportion of multipotent progenitors (MPP). However, the absolute counts of both ST-HSC and MPP were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. 2a). Among the hematopoietic progenitor cell subpopulations, the absolute numbers of granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMP) and granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (CLP) were also reduced in hyperthyroid mice relative to controls (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, the observed reduction in the number of these HSPCs was primarily attributable to cell cycle arrest, rather than the activation of apoptotic pathways (Fig. 2d–e).

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the impact of hyperthyroidism on the hematopoietic system by integrating clinical data from hyperthyroid patients with experimental evidence from a murine model of the disease. Our principal findings reveal that hyperthyroid patients present with distinct hematological abnormalities characterized by leukopenia—particularly neutropenia—and a general reduction in red blood cell parameters, including HGB, HCT, MCV, MCH, and MCHC. These alterations were more pronounced in female patients and demonstrated a certain correlation with circulating thyroid hormone levels. More importantly, utilizing a hyperthyroid mouse model, we traced these clinical manifestations back to their origin in the bone marrow. We demonstrate for the first time that the reduction in HSPC numbers under high thyroid hormone conditions may be attributed to cell cycle arrest.

Our clinical observations are consistent with previous studies, confirming an association between hyperthyroidism and hematological abnormalities [10, 11]. However, our findings indicate that the observed leukopenia is not generalized but exhibits a specific cellular lineage predisposition, suggesting that high levels of thyroid hormones may selectively inhibit the differentiation and maturation of myeloid progenitors. Similarly, the comprehensive reduction in red blood cell parameters—including decreased MCV and MCH—implies that hyperthyroidism may induce a state analogous to “anemia of chronic disease” or “nutritional anemia” by impairing erythropoiesis or hemoglobin synthesis, potentially linked to increased nutrient consumption and disrupted iron metabolism under hypermetabolic conditions [12].

A notable finding is the sexual dimorphism in hematological manifestations. Female hyperthyroid patients appear more susceptible to alterations in blood parameters, whereas changes in male patients are considerably limited. This disparity may be attributed to the complex regulatory effects of sex hormones. Previous research has indicated that estrogen and its receptors are involved in the pathogenesis of hyperthyroidism by mediating immune dysregulation [13, 14]. This suggests that in the clinical management of hyperthyroidism, greater attention should be paid to hematological monitoring in female patients.

To further investigate the impact of elevated thyroid hormone levels on bone marrow hematopoiesis, we successfully established a murine model of hyperthyroidism. The observed changes in the peripheral blood profile of these mice—particularly the reduction in white blood cells and neutrophils—closely mirrored the clinical characteristics of hyperthyroidism patients. The absence of significant alterations in other parameters may be attributed to the lack of autoimmune antibodies in this pharmacological model.

Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant reduction in the absolute numbers of ST-HSC, MPP, and their downstream progenitors, including GMP and CLP. This shift in the cellular distribution strongly suggests that excessive thyroid hormone primarily disrupts the transition of HSCs from a quiescent state to active proliferation and differentiation, as well as subsequent lineage-commitment processes, ultimately leading to insufficient output of mature blood cells. Notably, the reduction in these hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells was not caused by increased apoptosis but rather by cell cycle arrest. This finding aligns with the recognized role of thyroid hormones as broad physiological regulators. It is well-established that thyroid hormones can directly regulate the transcription of various cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors via their nuclear receptors [15]. We propose that in hyperthyroidism, excessive thyroid hormone signaling may disrupt the precise regulatory network governing the cell cycle in hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells, leading to arrest at the G0/G1 phase checkpoint. Consequently, these cells fail to proliferate effectively, compromising the maintenance of downstream hematopoietic pools.

This study has several limitations. First, our clinical data were derived from a cross-sectional design, which did not allow for dynamic observation of hematological recovery following antithyroid treatment. Second, although the mouse model revealed a central role of cell cycle arrest in mediating the hematopoietic suppression, the specific downstream signaling pathways involved remain to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, the potential contribution of autoimmune antibodies to the destruction of specific blood cell lineages through mechanisms like antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity was not thoroughly investigated in this work.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals that elevated thyroid hormone levels may impair the proliferation and differentiation potential of bone marrow HSPCs by inducing cell cycle arrest, which could represent a pivotal mechanism underlying the alterations in peripheral blood cell counts. This finding provides a novel perspective for understanding the mechanisms of hyperthyroidism-associated cytopenia, thereby not only deepening the comprehension of the systemic effects of hyperthyroidism but also offering a new pathophysiological foundation for potential clinical interventions.
