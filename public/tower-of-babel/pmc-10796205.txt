Villoglandular Adenocarcinoma of the Uterine Cervix: A Case Report
Authors: Soyoung Park, Min Gyoung Pak, Jung-Woo Park
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical adenocarcinomas constitute for approximately 10%–20% of all invasive cervical cancers. Villoglandular adenocarcinomas (VGAs) are a rare subtype of cervical adenocarcinoma, representing approximately 5% of all cases of cervical adenocarcinomas. Herein, we report the case of a 49-year-old perimenopausal woman successfully treated for VGA. The patient presented to the hospital with a primary complaint of vaginal discharge persisting for 7 months with worsening symptoms. She had no underlying medical conditions or history of oral contraceptive use. A punch biopsy revealed an adenocarcinoma, and a human papillomavirus (HPV) test indicated positive for HPV-16. The patient underwent a radical hysterectomy with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection, and a pathological diagnosis of VGA was established. After surgery, the patient underwent a 6-week course of concurrent chemoradiotherapy with cisplatin. During the 42 months of follow-up, no signs of disease recurrence or metastasis were observed. Because of the limitations of specimen acquisition, achieving a precise diagnosis through cervicovaginal cytology and punch biopsy is challenging. Instead, conization should be considered to prevent misdiagnosis.Keywords: Case reports, Cervix uteri, Uterine cervical neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Cervical adenocarcinoma is the second most common type of cervical cancer worldwide, accounting for approximately 10%–20% of all invasive cervical cancers [1], and has shown an increase in incidence in recent years. Villoglandular adenocarcinomas (VGAs), which account for approximately 5% of all cervical adenocarcinomas [2], are a rare subtype of cervical adenocarcinomas, first reported by Young and Scully in 1989 [1]. Recently, the International Endocervical Adenocarcinoma Criteria and Classification system categorized cervical adenocarcinomas into human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated adenocarcinomas and non-HPV-associated adenocarcinomas [3]; VGA is an HPV-associated adenocarcinoma. While the prognosis of cervical adenocarcinoma is poor, that of VGA is generally favorable. Patients usually visit the hospital with complaints of vaginal bleeding and discharge. Diagnosis can be made through physical examination, imaging, and histology, and treatments include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Herein, we present the case of a 49-year-old perimenopausal woman with VGA and discuss the clinical and histological features of the disease.

CASE REPORT

A 49-year-old perimenopausal woman visited the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of Dong-A University Hospital with a complaint of vaginal discharge that had persisted for 7 months with aggravation and an abnormal punch biopsy result. The patient had no underlying diseases or history of oral contraceptive use. The patient’s last pap smear was performed in 2005, and the result was negative.

Physical examination revealed a 3 cm-sized cauliflower-like mass lesion in the cervix, and colposcopy revealed a papillary lesion. A punch biopsy was performed at a previous local medical center, and adenocarcinoma was confirmed. An HPV test indicated HPV-16 positivity. Tumor markers were checked, confirming an elevated CA125 (41.9 U/mL) level and a normal SCC (0.53 ng/mL) level. Hematological and biochemical tests revealed no other remarkable findings.

Transvaginal sonography was performed, and a 2.5 × 1.4 cm endocervical mass was detected. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a cervical mass larger than 4 cm with focal cervical stromal invasion. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed a hypermetabolic malignancy in the uterine cervix with no regional or distant metastasis.

The patient underwent radical hysterectomy with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection. The pathological diagnosis revealed stage IB3 (6.1 cm in the greatest dimension) VGA with a clear resection margin, no parametrial invasion, no lymphovascular invasion, and no pelvic lymph node metastasis. Microscopically, the surgical specimen revealed an endocervical exophytic mass with a villoglandular pattern (Fig. 1 and 2). Specific immunohistochemical staining was positive for p16 and estrogen receptor (ER) (Fig. 3 and 4). However, there was spillage of the cervical cancerous mass during surgery due to its bulkiness, leading to extensive suction and irrigation. Following consultation with the radiation oncology department, adjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) was deemed necessary due to the bulky mass and intraoperative cancer spillage. After surgery, the patient underwent CCRT with cisplatin for 6 weeks. After 42 months of follow-up, no signs of disease recurrence or metastasis were observed.

DISCUSSION

VGAs represent a very rare subtype of cervical adenocarcinoma. It is important to distinguish VGA from typical cervical adenocarcinoma [4], as the prognosis of cervical adenocarcinoma is typically poorer than that of VGA. The age range of patients with VGA is 22 to 64 years, with an average age of onset between 37 and 45 years [5]. Patients with VGA appear to be younger than those with cervical adenocarcinoma, whose average age of onset is 40 to 50 years [1]. Our patient was 49 years old at diagnosis.

Typically, cervical cancer is regarded as an estrogen-independent condition. Previously, oral contraceptive use was linked to the development of VGA [6]. Yet, recent studies have not demonstrated a significant relationship between oral contraceptive use and VGA development [7]. Conversely, HPV infection, particularly high-risk HPV, is considered a risk factor for VGA development [7]. A recent study suggests a potential influence of estrogen on carcinogenesis in cervical adenocarcinoma cases with high-risk HPV infection [8]. However, the study acknowledges limitations in confirming whether estrogen itself triggers carcinogenesis, necessitating further research to validate this aspect. Therefore, while VGA is a rare cancer, additional studies and data are imperative to comprehend its association with estrogen and disease occurrence.

Considering these recent findings, when contemplating hormone therapy for managing menopausal symptoms in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, it might be advisable to consider the potential of estrogen-induced carcinogenesis, particularly in cases with high-risk HPV infection. Caution and ongoing research are crucial in such contexts. The present patient had no history of oral contraceptive or hormone therapy use, but the HPV polymerase chain reaction results were positive for HPV-16.

Patients with VGA typically visit the hospital with the chief complaints of vaginal bleeding and discharge. VGAs usually present as an exophytic cervical mass, generally protruding from the endocervical canal, with polypoid or friable lesions [4]. An initial diagnosis can be made from patient history, physical examinations, cervicovaginal cytology, colposcopy, and imaging, including CT, MRI, and PET. However, diagnosing VGAs from cervicovaginal cytology is challenging due to the difficulty in differentiating between malignant and reactive glandular cells, which could result in VGA misdiagnosis [5]. Additionally, the accuracy of pretreatment VGA diagnosis is low owing to limited biopsy specimens [7,9]. In approximately 30% of VGA cases, the concurrent presence of other invasive cancers is possible [10]. Solely performing a punch biopsy on the area with VGA may miss other invasive cancers elsewhere in the cervix or neglect poor prognostic factors. Therefore, if malignancy is suspected, accurate diagnosis through conization, rather than a simple punch biopsy, is recommended to prevent misdiagnosis [9]. Subsequent treatment decisions should be based on its results. Our patient initially underwent a punch biopsy at a local medical center, which revealed adenocarcinoma; however, the post-operative pathological diagnosis was VGA.

The characteristic microscopic feature of VGA is the villoglandular architecture, with long and thin papillae with fibrovascular cores [11]. These papillae are lined with stratified columnar epithelium with mild-to-moderate nuclear atypia [2]. Immunohistochemical staining may be helpful in the pathological diagnosis of VGA. Positive staining results for Ki-67, p53, p16, carcinoembryonic antigen, vimentin, and ER have been obtained in VGAs; however, progesterone receptor (PR) positivity can be challenging to detect [10]. In our patient, tumor cells showed as an exophytic endocervical mass with a villoglandular pattern and, microscopically, the fibrovascular cores contained both acute and chronic inflammatory cells. Further, the patient's immunohistochemical staining was positive for p16 and ER but negative for PR.

Cone biopsy, trachelectomy, simple hysterectomy, and radical hysterectomy with or without lymph node dissection are all recognized surgical treatments for VGAs [12]. Pre- or post-operative radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy is also considered a VGA treatment option. Conization may be performed for diagnosis and initial treatment. If the resection margins of the cone biopsy are clear when the depth of tumor invasion is less than 3 mm and the absence of lymphovascular space invasion is confirmed, no additional treatment is needed [1].

The prognosis of VGA is favorable because invasion of the lymphovascular space and metastasis to the lymph nodes are both rare. VGAs usually lack or present only superficial stromal invasion of tumor cells [10]. Because VGAs are associated with a younger patient age at diagnosis and have a good prognosis, an accurate diagnosis may allow for fertility-preserving treatments or more conservative management in women of reproductive age [1,12]. However, several cases of recurrence and death have also been reported [9]. Therefore, before considering conservative treatment, the presence of poor prognostic factors, including involvement of the lymphovascular space, deep invasion of the stroma by the tumor, and the presence of high-grade tumor components and other histological features [5], should be thoroughly evaluated [13].

CONCLUSION

This case of VGA in a perimenopausal woman highlights the challenges associated with relying solely on punch biopsy for precise initial diagnoses. Our findings emphasize the potential advantages of considering conization to minimize misdiagnosis risks and facilitate subsequent treatment planning. While VGA commonly presents in younger women with a favorable prognosis, allowing for fertility-sparing approaches or conservative management, further studies and long-term follow-up would be necessary.
