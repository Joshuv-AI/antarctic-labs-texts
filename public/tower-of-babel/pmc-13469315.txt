Lactoferrin as a Novel Therapeutic Agent in Dermatology: Current Evidence and Future Perspectives
Authors: Hui Zhang, Xu He, Peisheng Chen, Shanhe Shen, Qibin Liu, Xinling Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLTF is a multifunctional natural glycoprotein with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties. These biological activities have led to growing interest in its potential applications in dermatology. This review summarizes current evidence on the role of LTF in melanoma, acne, psoriasis, wound healing, and related inflammatory skin disorders. Available studies suggest that LTF may regulate microbial colonization, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, immune responses, apoptosis, and tissue repair. However, current evidence is derived mainly from in vitro studies and animal models, while clinical evidence remains limited. Although LTF shows promise as a therapeutic or adjunctive agent, important challenges include incomplete mechanistic understanding, heterogeneity across studies, and the lack of standardized formulations and dosing strategies. Future research should prioritize mechanistic clarification and well-designed clinical trials to support the translational development of LTF in skin diseases.

Introduction

Skin diseases represent one of the most prevalent and socially disruptive global health burdens, affecting billions of individuals across all age groups and geographical regions.1 Current epidemiological data indicate that over 4.69 billion people worldwide suffer from diverse cutaneous disorders, ranking skin diseases as the fourth leading cause of global non-fatal disease burden in terms of disability-adjusted life years and years lived with disability.2 Common chronic inflammatory skin conditions, including acne vulgaris, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis, induce persistent erythema, pruritus, scaling, and recurrent skin lesions, which severely compromise patients’ psychological well-being, social interaction, and long-term quality of life. In addition, these disorders impose enormous socioeconomic pressure worldwide, characterized by continuous healthcare consumption, repeated outpatient interventions, and reduced social productivity, constituting a substantial public health challenge.3 Conventional first-line therapies, including topical glucocorticoids, antibiotics, and synthetic anti-inflammatory agents, have alleviated cutaneous symptoms to a certain extent. However, their clinical application is greatly restricted by inherent limitations, such as drug resistance, high recurrence rates, skin barrier damage, and undesirable systemic side effects,4 resulting in suboptimal long-term therapeutic outcomes and creating an urgent clinical demand for novel, safe, and multi-targeted therapeutic strategies for skin diseases.

Lactoferrin (LTF), a multifunctional non-heme iron-binding glycoprotein of the transferrin family, has emerged as a promising natural therapeutic candidate for cutaneous disorders.5 First isolated and purified from human milk in 1960, LTF has attracted extensive research attention in life sciences, medicine, and food science due to its versatile physiological functions. It is ubiquitously distributed in human breast milk and various mucosal secretions, participating in the maintenance of human physiological homeostasis.6 Structurally, LTF exhibits a typical single-chain bilobal configuration consisting of two homologous N-lobe and C-lobe domains, each possessing an independent high-affinity iron-binding site that reversibly chelates ferric iron.7 This unique iron-chelating property enables LTF to regulate local iron homeostasis, eliminate iron-dependent oxidative damage, and deprive pathogenic microorganisms of essential iron nutrients required for proliferation. Functionally, the biological activities of LTF are predominantly receptor-dependent. LTF specifically binds to membrane receptors including low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 and intelectin-1 on keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and multiple immune cells.8 After receptor-mediated internalization, LTF modulates core intracellular signaling cascades, principally inhibiting the pro-inflammatory NF-κB pathway and activating the antioxidant Nrf2 pathway.9 Through these structural and molecular regulatory mechanisms, LTF exerts comprehensive antibacterial, antiviral, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory bioactivities, which are closely and specifically correlated with the core pathogenic mechanisms of diverse skin diseases.

The multitargeted properties of LTF precisely target typical pathological abnormalities in dermatological disorders.7 Its antibacterial capacity improves cutaneous microbial dysbiosis, a critical initiating factor driving the occurrence and progression of acne vulgaris. The potent antioxidant activity of LTF scavenges excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), relieving oxidative stress injury, delaying skin aging, and mitigating ultraviolet-induced cutaneous carcinogenesis.10 Moreover, LTF modulates immune cell maturation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion, effectively correcting immune dysregulation and suppressing sustained chronic inflammatory cascades, which are the central pathological features of psoriasis, inflammatory acne, and other inflammatory dermatoses. In addition, LTF promotes keratinocyte migration and proliferation, repairs impaired cellular metabolism, and ameliorates defective skin tissue regeneration, showing great potential for cutaneous wound healing. Accumulating clinical evidence has validated the dermatological efficacy and safety of LTF.6,7 Oral supplementation of LTF combined with vitamin E and zinc can significantly reduce the number of comedones and inflammatory lesions in acne patients.11 Topical LTF ointment markedly improves erythema, scaling, and plaque elevation in patients with mild-to-moderate plaque psoriasis, serving as a reliable adjunctive therapeutic option for inflammatory skin diseases.12

Nevertheless, substantial unresolved knowledge gaps remain in current dermatological research regarding LTF, limiting its clinical transformation and popularization. Firstly, existing clinical studies are restricted by small sample sizes and insufficient long-term follow-up data, lacking systematic verification of dose–effect relationships and long-term safety profiles. Secondly, the molecular linkage between LTF’s unique structural characteristics and its cutaneous protective effects remains fragmented and poorly elucidated. Thirdly, the regulatory mechanisms of LTF on the immune-metabolic axis and gut–skin microbiota homeostasis in inflammatory skin disorders have not been comprehensively summarized and clarified.13 Currently, numerous published reviews have concluded the general biological functions of LTF as well as its research progress in tumor biology, systemic inflammation, and non-cutaneous tissue regeneration.14 However, these generalized reviews lack dermatological specificity and pertinence. They fail to elaborate the targeted regulatory effects of LTF on skin-specific pathological processes.

Considering the enormous global disease burden of skin diseases, the inherent deficiencies of conventional therapies, and the lack of systematic dermatology-focused summaries in existing literature, an updated and targeted review focusing on LTF in dermatology is urgently needed. It comprehensively consolidates the latest preclinical and clinical evidence, summarizes current research deficiencies and controversies, and proposes prospective research directions. This review aims to summarize current evidence regarding the molecular mechanisms, therapeutic applications, and translational potential of lactoferrin in major dermatological disorders, while identifying existing knowledge gaps and future research priorities.

Role in Melanoma

Skin cancer is typified into two primary classifications, namely melanoma and non-melanoma, based on its cellular origin. Non-melanoma cancers represent 95% of cases and are further subdivided into basal cell carcinoma, bowen disease and squamous cell carcinoma, etc.15 In view of the high malignancy of melanoma, the current treatment options for this condition remain limited.

Recent studies have indicated the occurrence of synergistic effects in synthesised lactoferrin-copper nanoparticles, suggesting their potential for the treatment of skin cancer. The cellular experimental findings demonstrate that the synthesised copper-LFNP displays augmented stability, water solubility, drug loading efficiency and sustained release performance. Copper-LFNPs demonstrated significant toxicity towards A375 melanoma cells, with a reduction in cell viability to 23.24±1.84% at a concentration of 40 μg/mL. Observations have revealed that LTF binds to LTF receptors, facilitating its entry into the cytoplasm. In this instance, the process of downregulation of Bcl-2 is initiated, which in turn results in the production of reactive oxygen species, otherwise known as ROS. The subsequent production of ROS then leads to further induction of DNA damage, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Consequently, this process ultimately triggers apoptosis by activating cytochrome C and caspases.16,17 Moreover, research suggests that the combination of LTF and extracellular vesicles, when used in conjunction with immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies, can enhance the efficacy of treatments for drug-resistant melanoma.17 LTF has been demonstrated to promote Microphthalmia-Associated Transcription Factor (MITF) phosphorylation and degradation by activating the Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK) signal pathway, whilst simultaneously downregulating MITF mRNA expression. This results in the inhibition of TYR at both the protein level and in its activity, consequently leading to a reduction in melanin production17,18 (Figure 1). This study has several limitations, as it was conducted exclusively in vitro using the A375 melanoma cell line. First, no additional melanoma cell lines or normal skin cells were included as controls, making it difficult to determine the broader applicability of the formulation or its cellular selectivity. The in vitro culture environment cannot adequately recapitulate the complex in vivo microenvironment, including the skin barrier and interference from serum proteins, and no transdermal permeation studies were performed to evaluate its potential for topical administration. The mechanistic investigation was limited to a preliminary assessment of apoptosis and melanogenesis-related pathways, without pathway inhibition or rescue experiments. Therefore, the molecular network underlying the synergistic regulation by copper and LTF remains unclear. In addition, the study lacked in vivo data from tumor-bearing animal models regarding antitumor efficacy, tissue distribution, and systemic or dermatological toxicity, precluding confirmation of therapeutic effects in vivo. Finally, in the absence of human clinical evidence, the in vitro findings cannot be directly translated into clinical application. Future studies should focus on formulation optimization, in vivo pharmacodynamic and toxicological evaluation, and more comprehensive mechanistic validation.

Role in Acne

Acne is the eighth most prevalent disease globally, with a global prevalence rate of approximately 9.4%. In the adolescent population, the prevalence rate has been reported to range from 50% to 95%.19 In severe cases, the condition can lead to psychological issues such as anxiety and depression. The core pathogenic factors of acne include excessive sebum secretion, abnormal follicular keratinization, proliferation of Propionibacterium acnes, and inflammatory responses.20,21 Current treatments are confronted with challenges including hormonal side effects,22 teratogenicity of medications, and bacterial resistance.23 This underscores the urgent need for the development of natural and effective ingredients.

LTF, a naturally occurring substance that is currently under investigation, has demonstrated, through in vitro and in vivo studies, that LTF at concentrations ranging from 10 to 500μg/mL significantly reduces lipid content in insulin-treated SZ95 cells. Concurrently, it has been demonstrated that the substance under investigation has the capacity to inhibit the expression of mRNA related to lipid metabolism (SREBP-1, FAS, SCD-1, and FADS2) and the inflammatory cytokine IL-8. In doses ranging from 1 to 50 milligrams per animal, LTF has been shown to effectively alleviate sebaceous gland inflammation in an acne mouse model. The findings of this study indicate that LTF reduces lipid production, inhibits sebaceous cell proliferation while promoting apoptosis, and decreases tissue levels of FADS2 and CXCL15 proteins. This finding underscores the potential value of LTF in counteracting sebum production and sebaceous gland inflammation associated with acne.19,24,25

A plethora of clinical studies have also demonstrated the efficacy of LTF in the treatment of acne vulgaris. An open-label, single-arm exploratory study was conducted in Germany, with 43 participants aged between 14 and 30 years with mild to moderate facial acne vulgaris enrolled. The participants were administered chewable tablets containing 100mg of bovine LTF twice daily for a period of eight weeks. The study was completed by 39 subjects, with a compliance rate exceeding 95%. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in non-inflammatory lesion counts and a substantial decrease of 22.5% in total lesion counts. A decline of 20.2% in inflammatory lesion counts was observed. The study found that 76.9% of subjects experienced a reduction in total lesion counts, and 66.7% reported an improvement in acne severity. During the course of the study, no adverse events related to LTF were observed, and the substance was found to be well tolerated26 (Figure 2). Although LTF has demonstrated sebum-suppressing, anti-inflammatory, and lipid metabolism-regulating effects in in vitro and animal studies, and has shown some clinical benefits in some patients with mild-to-moderate acne vulgaris, the current evidence supporting its use for treating acne remains significantly limited. Existing research is primarily based on cellular and animal studies. Clinical studies are few in number and have limited sample sizes, lacking support from large-scale, multicenter, randomized, double-blind controlled trials. The observation periods in most studies are relatively short, making it impossible to fully assess long-term efficacy, recurrence rates, and long-term safety. There are significant differences across studies regarding lactoferrin dosage, formulation, administration regimens, and whether it is used in combination with other active ingredients, leading to high heterogeneity in the evidence and limiting an accurate assessment of its efficacy. Furthermore, the existing evidence is primarily focused on mild-to-moderate acne vulgaris, and its efficacy for moderate-to-severe, refractory, or hormone-related acne remains unclear. Therefore, lactoferrin is currently more suitable as a potential adjunctive therapy and cannot yet replace existing standardized acne treatment regimens.

Role in Psoriasis

Psoriasis is defined as a long-term, immune-related skin disorder. The hallmark of psoriasis is a pathological process of excessive proliferation and abnormal keratinocyte differentiation, accompanied by immune cell infiltration.27 Clinically, the condition manifests as red plaques covered with silvery-white scales, often accompanied by itching. Affected areas may include the scalp, extensor surfaces of the limbs, and the trunk.28

There have also been some studies on the role of LTF in psoriasis. In an open-label, two-arm, four-week clinical trial, 30 patients diagnosed with mild to moderate plaque psoriasis were enrolled (15 males and 15 females, mean age 49.2 years). The inclusion criteria encompassed the following: Firstly, stable, symmetrical skin lesions that had persisted for a minimum of one month. Secondly, less than 10% of the body surface area affected, and thirdly, a Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score of 10 or below. Both groups received oral LTF at a dose of 100 mg twice daily. Group A (15 patients) applied 10% LTF ointment topically, twice daily, while Group B (15 patients) applied 20% LTF ointment topically, once daily. Following a period of four weeks, there was a 37.3% improvement in TLS in Group A and a 35.5% improvement in Group B. There was a significant improvement in skin thickness, erythema, and scaling in both groups in comparison to the control side. The severity of pruritus was significantly reduced12 (Figure 3). Small-sample clinical studies have suggested that LTF may provide some short-term improvement for mild-to-moderate plaque psoriasis, but the current evidence remains significantly limited. First, the study had a small sample size and employed an open-label design, resulting in high selection and observer bias. Second, the study did not include a strict placebo or standard treatment control group, and all participants received both oral and topical lactoferrin interventions simultaneously, making it difficult to determine the independent efficacy of LTF and the relative contributions of different routes of administration. Furthermore, the observation period was only 4 weeks, which is insufficient to assess long-term efficacy, recurrence rates, and safety. Additionally, the study primarily enrolled patients with mild-to-moderate, stable plaque psoriasis, so the results have limited generalizability to patients with moderate-to-severe or other types of psoriasis.

Role in Skin Wound Healing

The process of healing skin wounds is a complex and dynamic one, occurring within the organism as a result of the coordinated action of multiple cells, tissues, growth factors, and the extracellular matrix.29 The skin’s role as a protective barrier against external environmental threats is well-documented. However, the perpetual exposure to potential injury that this entails is a significant factor in wound healing, which is vital to the survival of all higher organisms.30

Research into LTF derivatives is currently experiencing a period of significant growth and development.31,32 Exosomes derived from colostrum encapsulating LTF have been shown to significantly enhance the survival and migration of mouse and human fibroblasts. Furthermore, EV-exoLF has been demonstrated to reduce the pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and phosphorylated JNK in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated fibroblasts. The findings demonstrate that exosomal extracellular vesicles (exo-EVs) from dermal fibroblasts promote wound healing through the mitigation of inflammation, underscoring their potential for therapeutic applications in skin regeneration.32 Recombinant rice-derived human lactoferrin (holo-rhLF) directly promotes wound re-epithelialisation by enhancing keratinocyte proliferation, increasing cell migration, and inhibiting apoptosis. It is noteworthy that holo-rhLF exhibits markedly superior efficacy in comparison to its partially iron-saturated variants, suggesting a potential therapeutic avenue for wound healing33,34 (Figure 4). Although LTF derivatives, such as lactoferrin-coated exosomes and recombinant human lactoferrin, have shown promising results in experimental studies regarding the promotion of fibroblast and keratinocyte proliferation and migration, the suppression of inflammatory responses, and the acceleration of re-epithelialization, their current application is still subject to various limitations. Existing evidence primarily stems from in vitro and animal studies, with a lack of high-quality, large-scale human clinical trials to support these findings. Although the relevant mechanisms of action involve anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and cell migration-promoting effects, the regulation of key processes in wound healing—such as angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and scar formation—has not yet been fully elucidated. Furthermore, there are currently no unified standards for the sourcing, extraction, purification, and quality control of exosome-based formulations, and variations in the structural consistency, iron saturation status, and biological activity of recombinant lactoferrin may also affect the stability of its therapeutic efficacy. At the same time, their long-term safety, formulation stability, delivery efficiency, and feasibility for industrial-scale production still require further validation. Therefore, although lactoferrin derivatives hold potential value for skin regeneration therapy, they remain generally in the early stages of translational research and require further validation through high-quality studies.

Role in Conditions Associated with Skin Disorders

Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic, autoimmune inflammatory disease that primarily affects the joints and the surrounding soft tissues. The condition has the potential to result in a range of debilitating consequences, including but not limited to joint dysfunction, deformity, and even permanent functional impairment.35,36 The global prevalence of RA is increasing, which is placing a significant burden on healthcare systems.36

The present study examined the inhibitory effect of orally administered LTF on the progression of RA. The results indicated that LTF exerts its effects by reducing joint swelling and bone destruction, inhibiting synovial inflammatory infiltration and vascular endothelium formation, and downregulating tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) expression. Its mechanisms of action include binding to TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) to inhibit NF-κB and MAPK signalling pathways, internalisation via the LRP1 receptor, and correction of spleen Th17/Treg cell imbalance. The 500 mg/kg/day dose exhibited more pronounced effects, suggesting potential as a dietary supplement for the prevention and treatment of RA.37 In a further study, preventive and therapeutic trials of LTF were conducted in mice with rheumatoid arthritis. In the preventive trial, LTF was administered orally on a daily basis from three hours prior to the injection of the adjuvant until day 18. The results demonstrated that BLF significantly inhibited acute and chronic inflammatory development in rat joints, reduced foot swelling, and lowered pain scores. In the therapeutic trial, the oral administration of 100mg/kg LTF on day 19 led to the effective halting of arthritis progression within a week, with analgesic effects evident from the first day of dosing. Concurrent single-dose studies have confirmed that LTF’s analgesic effect exhibits a dose-dependent relationship and is completely reversed by the opioid receptor antagonist naloxone. This indicates that its analgesic action is mediated via μ-opioid receptors. Further experimentation revealed that both single and daily oral administration of LTF suppressed LPS-induced production of the pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α, while simultaneously upregulating levels of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10.38

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is a complex group of disorders characterized by chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, primarily comprising Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, alongside IBD-undefined.39,40 The etiology of IBD is multifactorial, arising from a combination of genetic, immune, microbial, and environmental factors. The core mechanism of this process is abnormal intestinal immune responses, in which excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α) disrupts the intestinal barrier, activates NLRP3 inflammasomes, and exacerbates inflammation.41,42 IBD poses a considerable global health burden, markedly affecting patients’ quality of life and necessitating prolonged treatment. It is noteworthy that the prevalence of IBD is increased among patients with psoriasis, with a 1.6-fold elevated risk of ulcerative colitis compared to the general population.42,43

LTF has been demonstrated to promote cell growth in a dose-dependent manner, resulting in the arrest of the cell cycle at the G2/M phase and an increase in alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity. It has been demonstrated that this process enhances tight junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, ZO-1) at both the mRNA and protein levels, thereby enhancing intestinal epithelial barrier function. This research provides a foundation for the development of LTF-based therapeutic interventions for inflammatory bowel disease and related conditions.44 A further study established that recombinant human lactoferrin VEN-120 induces CD4+ T cells to undergo a phenotypic shift towards regulatory T cells (Tregs). This, in turn, results in the upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 while downregulating pro-inflammatory factors including IL-17 and IFN-γ. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that this process promotes Treg homing to the intestinal mucosal lamina propria and associated lymph nodes. This finding offers a novel immunomodulatory strategy for the treatment of IBD, by significantly alleviating intestinal inflammation and improving tissue damage45 (Figure 5). Although previous studies have shown that lactoferrin and its recombinant derivatives may exert potential anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects in IBD by enhancing intestinal epithelial barrier function, upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins, and inducing the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into regulatory T cells, current evidence remains significantly limited. Existing research has primarily focused on cellular and animal studies, with a lack of high-quality clinical trial evidence from large-scale studies. Furthermore, the pathological process of IBD involves multiple mechanisms, including intestinal barrier damage, immune dysregulation, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and chronic relapse. However, the comprehensive regulatory role of lactoferrin in these complex pathological processes has not yet been fully elucidated. Therefore, the application of lactoferrin in IBD remains in the exploratory stage and is more suitable as a potential adjunctive therapeutic strategy, requiring further validation through high-quality research.

Conclusion

In summary, LTF and its related preparations, as a class of functional molecules with multiple biological activities, have attracted increasing attention in recent years for their potential applications in the treatment of various skin diseases. A review of existing research reveals several interrelated core mechanisms. The anti-inflammatory effects of LTF are primarily manifested through the downregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17, as well as the regulation of key signaling pathways including NF-κB, MAPK, and JNK. At the same time, its immunomodulatory function involves the regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses, including correcting Th17/Treg imbalances, upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, and influencing the activation and migration of immune cells. In addition, LTF possesses antibacterial activity that can inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms; it exerts antioxidant effects that alleviate tissue damage associated with oxidative stress. And it promotes repair by facilitating epithelial repair, cell migration, collagen remodeling, and the restoration of barrier function. These overlapping mechanisms collectively form a reasonably coherent biological framework to explain the potential beneficial effects of LTF in inflammatory, infectious, hyperproliferative, and trauma-related skin diseases.

The research value of LTF lies not only in its broad biological activity but also in its unique advantages in clinical translation. As a naturally occurring iron-binding glycoprotein, LTF theoretically offers multiple advantages over traditional single-target therapeutic strategies, including multi-target regulation, good biocompatibility, and superior safety in nutritional and biomedical applications. Additionally, it can serve not only as a therapeutic molecule but also as a component of advanced delivery systems such as nanoparticles, hydrogels, and extracellular vesicles. These advantages may be key reasons for the continued interest in LTF within the field of dermatology; its application prospects are particularly promising in disease states characterized by the coexistence of inflammatory responses, microbial imbalance, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and impaired tissue repair.

However, it must be clearly noted that the evidence base for LTF across different indications is currently uneven. Regarding dermatological applications, acne has been supported by some human clinical studies, suggesting that LTF-containing interventions may help reduce inflammatory lesions and improve skin condition. In contrast, evidence related to psoriasis remains limited and is primarily derived from small-sample exploratory studies. For melanoma, wound healing, and various LTF-based engineered formulations, the current evidence remains predominantly preclinical, relying primarily on in vitro experiments and animal models. Therefore, although the existing research findings are promising, the exact clinical efficacy of LTF for most dermatological indications has not yet been fully established, and the current understanding is insufficient to support its widespread use in clinical treatment.

Currently, the clinical translation of LTF-based therapeutic strategies remains constrained by various factors. LTF formulations used in different studies vary significantly in terms of source, purity, iron saturation status, formulation type, dosage, and route of administration, resulting in limited comparability of research findings and complicating standardized application. Several key pharmaceutical and translational issues remain unresolved, including low oral bioavailability, susceptibility to protein degradation, limited tissue targeting efficiency, inconsistent transdermal delivery via topical application, and the need to improve large-scale production and quality control systems.

Overall, existing evidence suggests that lactoferrin possesses multiple biological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and tissue-repairing effects, which give it potential therapeutic value for various skin diseases. However, most of the current evidence remains at the preclinical stage, and numerous challenges still exist in terms of elucidating mechanisms, optimizing formulations, facilitating pharmaceutical translation, and conducting clinical validation. Therefore, at this stage, it is more reasonable to regard LTF as a promising but still-developing candidate strategy in the treatment of skin diseases. Its true clinical value remains contingent upon further mechanistic research, optimization of delivery systems, and systematic validation through high-quality clinical trials.
