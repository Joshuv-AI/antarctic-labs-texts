Deciphering senescence-associated mechanisms through cell painting & transcriptomics
Authors: Yuliana Tan, Anika Liu, Emily Larkin, Revati Wani, Alec Dick, Stefano Patassini, Patrik Erlmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Senescence is a key pathomechanism across chronic diseases but also highly heterogeneous. In this study, we demonstrate that high-content imaging and transcriptomics can jointly shed light on senescence in vitro, distinguishing replicative and chemically induced senescence, differentiating from quiescence and characterizing the senolytic response.

Introduction

Cellular senescence is defined as an irreversible cell cycle arrest linked to aging and aging-related diseases such as cancer, MASH, and IPF1–4. It can be triggered in vitro and in vivo by various inducers5 and known hallmarks include increased lysosomal β-galactosidase activity, nucleoli enlargement and chromatin reorganization, as well as a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and surfaceome. Nevertheless, senescence is highly heterogeneous, as demonstrated previously with transcriptomic and image-based profiling6–8, and phenotypes differ across cell types, states, and inducers. A better understanding of these differences, especially between normal and disease-associated aging and the differential senolytic response may aid in the development of more targeted senotherapeutics9.

Here, we demonstrate that insights on cellular senescence heterogeneity can be obtained in vitro by combining high-content imaging using Cell Painting (CP) and transcriptomics data. To do so, we induce senescence in primary normal human dermal fibroblast (DF) and primary normal human lung fibroblast (LF) either via chemical inducers or by passaging them close to the Hayflick limit (Fig. 1a). Senescence-like features were validated using live imaging to measure cell proliferation and the general biomarker senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-ß-GAL). In this experimental setup, senescence was defined based on low proliferative capacity after treatment with chemical inducers (Fig. 1b, Suppl. Fig. 1a, Suppl. Table 1), as well as increased SA-ß-GAL expression and cell area using late passage (LP) cells as benchmark (Fig. 1c-d, Suppl. Fig. 1b, Suppl. Table 2). LP cells were used to recapitulate the in vivo senescence phenotype in which a spectrum of replicative stress-associated states is present, largely consisting of cells in irreversible cell cycle arrest. In our initial setup, low serum (1% FBS) was used as a surrogate for LP in LF. These low serum cells recapitulate the low proliferative capacity and high SA-ß-GAL present in senescence but lack the biological significance of true LP cells. We selected three chemical inducers resembling LP senescence: 2 days bleomycin 5 ug/uL (bleo), 5 days palbociclib 4.5 uM (palbo) and 2 days nocodazole 50 ng/mL (noco). In contrast, 2 days 10 μM rapamycin (rapa) was selected for its quiescent-like phenotype (cell cycle arrest with immediate proliferation after compound removal). In total, we identified more than 1.4 million cells in the CP data and generated 4700-5500 features to characterize each cell. This dataset was used for downstream analysis including identification of cell clusters based on morphology, features and fraction analysis (Fig. 1f). We first performed cell-level quality control removing 12.4% of objects which belonged to clusters representing dead cells and technical artifacts (Suppl. Fig. 2a–c, Suppl. Tables 3, 4).

Next, well-level morphology was analyzed using PCA which separates senescence-like treatment groups from others along PC1 in both cell types and overall group conditions by chemical inducer rather than senolytic treatment (Fig. 2a). On the cell-level, we identified multiple clusters in each cell type with distinct morphology (Fig. 2b, Suppl. Fig. 2d, Suppl. Tables 5, 6). To gain a better overview of the biological role, we evaluated the distribution of cells across treatments in each cluster (Fig. 2c, Suppl. Fig. 3) and the morphological similarity between clusters (Fig. 2d, Suppl. Fig. 4a, b, Suppl. Table 6). We observed that in the repeated experiment cells treated with nocodazole remained in a proliferative phenotype reflected in nuclei counts and morphology (Suppl. Fig. 3, Suppl. Fig. 4c, Fig. 2a) in contrast to the initial experiment (Suppl. Fig. 1). Therefore, we did not regard nocodazole as senescence-like condition in the downstream analysis. We find four pairs of DF and LF clusters with matching morphology and abundance patterns indicating that these capture the same biological process across the two types of fibroblasts, although analyzed independently. These cluster pairs exhibited more extreme feature values (absolute mean normalized value > 2), indicating distinct morphologies, and were further investigated (Fig. 2d). DF4 and LF4 are primarily associated with senescent conditions and show senescence-typical increased cell size, as well as a larger nucleus with lower nuclear staining intensity (Fig. 2b, Suppl. Fig. 4, Suppl. Table 6). In contrast, DF1 and LF5 are enriched in the control conditions and are linked to larger nucleus size, increased nuclear staining intensity, but average cell size (Suppl. Fig. 4a, b, Suppl. Table 6). These CP features capture the cells containing two nuclei (Fig. 2b) representing overlapping cells frequently found in the control conditions given the larger cell counts per well (Suppl. Table 4). DF7 and LF6 are characterized by distinct mitochondrial features, such as increased maximal intensity in the cell (Fig. 2e, Suppl. Fig. 4a, b, Suppl. Table 6) and are distinctly enriched in rapamycin-treated conditions (Fig. 2f, Suppl. Table 7). The last cluster pair, DF6 and LF7, shows smaller nucleus size and increased nuclear staining intensity, indicating either active mitosis or fragmented nuclei from ongoing apoptosis (Fig. 2b, Suppl. Table 6). This cluster pair is rare, and their abundance increases with senolytic treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2g, Suppl. Table 8). Additionally, we see an increase in the number of nuclei in live imaging after senolytic addition for the chemically induced senescence groups which is also supportive of increased mitosis or apoptosis (Fig. 2h; Suppl. Table 9). This response, however, differs between cell type and chemical inducer (Suppl. Fig. 4c, Suppl. Table 9). For example, senolytic treatment does not shift nuclei count in palbociclib-induced senescence in DF. To map changes in morphology to biological processes, we generated transcriptomics data from technical replicates (Fig. 1e). Like the CP features, transcriptomics differentiates the senescence-like conditions from others along PC1 (Fig. 3a). Senescence-like conditions in this study were matched with the senescence signature by Hernandez-Segura et al.7, which describes in vitro fibroblast senescence (Fig. 3b, Suppl. Table 10). However, the SenMayo11 gene set, modelling in vivo senescence, was only partially enriched for chemically induced senescence-like conditions but not LP and was also enriched for LF upon senolytic treatment of the control (Suppl. Fig. 5a, Suppl. Table 11). This highlights differences between in vivo and in vitro systems and the need for a better understanding of senescence heterogeneity. Additionally, we found that differentially expressed cytokines vary between LP, chemically induced senescence, and quiescence (Suppl. Fig. 5b). Fibrosis markers linked to senescence12,13 were only partially increased in LP in our in vitro model, but not other conditions. We observed the expected expression pattern for the canonical cell cycle regulators CDKN1A and MKI67 but not CDKN2A in senescent conditions. As a next step, we integrated CP and transcriptomics data using MOFA per cell type, which returns latent factors capturing the variation across both modalities jointly in an unsupervised way (Suppl. Fig. 6a). For the five latent factors capturing the most variance (Suppl. Fig. 6b), we identified matching factors between DF and LF based on similar activity across conditions with an absolute Pearson correlation > 0.5 (Fig. 3c, Suppl. Fig. 6c). To characterize the biological function of the derived factors, we investigated their activation across experimental conditions and indeed recapitulated the relevant biological signals expected in this study (Fig. 3d). For instance, the senescence factor (SF) differentiates the broad senescent conditions (lower factor values) from other conditions (higher factor values). Similarly, we also identify factors characterizing quiescence (QF), replicative senescence specifically (RsF), inducer-specific differences between palbociclib and bleomycin (IsF), and senolytic response (SrF), which overall further define senescence heterogeneity (Fig. 3d). It should be noted that the contributing CP and gene expression features are shared between these cell types in some factors but not others (Suppl. Fig. 7). This indicates that the shared cellular behavior may be driven by partially distinct underlying processes across cell types.

We next investigated the difference between SF and RsF. Unlike SF, RsF is only activated in LP and morphologically correlates with a distinct cell population in DF but not in LF (Fig. 3e). This agrees with the shifts observed in cell density across conditions (Suppl. Fig. 3a) and is also reflected in the fact that general morphology features are enriched in SF of both cell types and in RsF of LF while they are not strongly represented for DF (Fig. 3f, Suppl. Fig. 6d, e, Suppl. Table 12). In addition, pathways enriched in RsF also differ between DF and LF, with an emphasis on reduction of chemokine signaling in DF and overall increase of mRNA processing in LF (Fig. 3g, Suppl. Table 13). Similarly, SrF in LF is correlated with a specific subpopulation, corresponding to the senolytic-responsive cluster LF7, and enriched in pathways relevant to cell cycle progression (Suppl. Fig. 6f, Suppl. Table 13), consistent with the previously noted increase in nuclei count. This observation emphasizes that senescence represents a complex biological response whichs is not fully understood, even in vitro. Taken together, this demonstrates that the combination of CP and transcriptomics uncovers subtle morphological and molecular changes specific to various senescence mechanisms and senolytic response.

In summary, our study demonstrates how integrating CP with transcriptomic profiling enables a nuanced view of senescence heterogeneity across dermal and lung fibroblasts. By analyzing the CP data at single-cell resolution we were able to morphologically characterize cell clusters corresponding to proliferation, senescence, quiescence and senolytic response in a cost-effective manner. Integration with transcriptomics captured relevant biological processes in a few MOFA factors and characterized senescence heterogeneity in vitro, for example by differentiating between senescence versus replicative senescence. This shows that while senescence is identifiable based on general morphology or transcriptomics alone, additional insights can be gained through combining both technologies. One limitation of this study, however, is that not the full spectrum of senescence is covered, such as complete irreversible cell cycle arrest or inflammation-induced senescence. While our findings lay important groundwork, broader experimental validation will be essential to develop robust experimental approaches, refine insights on the spectrum of senescence, and to predict therapeutic modulation by senolytics.

Methods

Cell culture

Primary Normal Human Dermal Fibroblast (DF) & Normal Human Lung Fibroblast (LF) were purchased from Lonza. NHDF were cultured in DMEM/F12 High Glucose (Gibco, 11995-065) with 10% FBS (Gibco, A5209402) and 1% P/S (Gibco, 15140-122). Similarly, NHLF were cultured with addition of NEAA (Gibco, 11140-050). Cells were passaged at 70-80% confluency during maintenance at 1:3 to 1:6 ratio. Late passage (LP) cells were obtained by passaging until cells close to reaching the Hayflick limit at passage 25 or above. LP cells were passaged once every week at 1:2 or 1:1 ratio for maintenance. Media was refreshed every 2-3 days.

Senescence inducer & treatment

Cells between passage 5–7 were used, except for the LP cells at passage 25 or above which were used as replicative senescence control. Tool compounds were used to induce quiescence or senescence. The following compounds were used: 10 μM Rapamycin (Sigma, 553211), 5–20 μg/mL Bleomycin (Sigma, b7216), 50 ng/mL Nocodazole (Sigma, sml1665), 4.5 μM Palbociclib (Cell Signaling Technology, 47284). As a Vehicle control, DMSO at 0.2% was used to mimic DMSO concentration of the palbociclib treatment. Tool compounds were added 24 h post-seeding. For cells treated with rapamycin, nocodazole, and bleomycin, the media was refreshed 48 hours after treatment. For palbociclib and the vehicle control, no media change was performed until the end of the experiment on day 7. On day 5, either media alone, 0.1 μM or 1 μM navitoclax was added to study the cell response to the senolytic compound. Technical triplicates were prepared for each assay, (1) Live imaging & Senescence-associated β-galactosidase, (2) CP, (3) RNA extraction. Randomization and automation were used to prevent technical errors.

Live imaging

Growth curves were observed by counting nuclei stained with Nuclight Rapid Red Dye (Incucyte, 4717) at 1:1000 dilution. Images were taken every 4 h for 7 days on an Incucyte S3. Nuclei counts were measured using Incucyte software (Incucyte 2022B Rev 2). Due to reduced adherence capacity and survival of LP cells, nuclei counts were normalized to the timepoint 4 h after treatment to measure proliferative capacity more accurately. Tabular data was exported and plotted using GraphPad PRISM (version 10) and ggplot2 in R.

Senescence associated β-Galactosidase imaging & measurement

Cells were stained with CellEvent™ Senescence Green Detection Kit (Invitrogen, C10851) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, staining solution (1X) was prepared by diluting CellEvent™ Senescence Green Probe into the pre-warmed CellEvent™ Senescence Buffer. Cells previously used for live imaging were washed with DPBS (Gibco, 14040-117) and fixed with Fixation Buffer (Biolegend, 420801) for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed with 1% BSA in DPBS and incubated with pre-warmed staining solution for 2 h at 37 °C without CO2. The plate was sealed with clear film to prevent evaporation during incubation. Cells were washed with DPBS thrice and then imaged with either an Incucyte S3 or a Revvity Operetta CLS at 10x magnification using the Alexa 488 filter for SA-β-Gal detection and Cy5 filter for nuclei counts. Tabular data was exported and plotted using ggplot2 in R.

Cell Painting (CP) sample preparation, image acquisition and pre-processing

Cells were stained with the PhenoVue Cell Painting Kit (Revvity, PING12) following the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. For 96‑well plates, the optimal dye concentration is fourfold higher than the recommended protocol concentration. Briefly, cells at endpoint (day 7) were stained with PhenoVue 641 – mitochondrial stain in diluent A and incubated for 30 minutes in the dark at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with Fixation Buffer (Biolegend, 420801) for 20 mins at room temperature protected from light. Then, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Sigma, 93443) for 15 minutes at room temperature. After permeabilization, cells were washed with DPBS once, then stained with Solution 2 containing PhenoVue Fluor 555 –WGA, PhenoVue Fluor 488 – Concanavalin A, PhenoVue Fluor 568 – Phalloidin, PhenoVue Hoechst 33342 nuclear stain, PhenoVue 512 nucleic acid stain in diluent A. Cells were incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark and washed thrice with DPBS. The plate was sealed with clear film to prevent evaporation for storage at 4 °C in the dark until ready to image. Image acquisition was performed with the Revvity Operetta CLS microscope at 10x magnification using manufacturer’s defined presets: Brightfield, Hoechst 33342, Alexa 488, Cy3 and Cy5 channels.

Data was exported and uploaded to Revvity Signals Image Artist™ (Version 1.3.29) for image pre-processing and analysis. Briefly, raw images were adjusted with flatfield and brightfield correction. Then, images on the Alexa 488 channel were processed with a 1px Gaussian Smooth filter. Nuclei were determined based on Hoechst 33342 staining using parameters available in SiMA. The Cy5 channel was multiplied by 10 to allow comparable intensity readouts with Alexa 488 and Cy3. Next, image calculation was performed to define “complete cytoplasm components” by adding the Alexa 488, Cy3 and Cy5 channels. Cytoplasm area was then determined based on complete cytoplasm components and proximity to nuclei previously defined. For each cell defined through previous analysis, SiMA calculates CP properties on each channel (Gaussian smoothed Alexa 488, Cy3, Cy5, and Hoechst 33342). This results in tabular data containing 4750 features either at cell-level or well-level (mean value of each feature across nine fields per well). Cells at image borders were removed as technical outliers. Tabular data was exported for downstream analysis.

CP data analysis

We compared the number of cells in each well to its technical replicates and removed four wells from the lung fibroblast dataset that were identified as technical outliers.

Next, we inspected the distribution of cell-level features to identify potential outlier cells. To do so, the cell-level features were first normalized per plate, features with at least 10% missing values were removed, and feature selection was performed with the pycytominer14 package using the default parameters. In this process, features with low variance or high frequency of missing values were excluded, as well as highly correlated features and outlier features. While all normalized features are kept to not miss out on potentially mechanistically interpretable features, only those identified in feature selection were used as the basis for dimensionality reduction and clustering based on their high redundancy.

For each cell type, dermal and lung fibroblasts, respectively, the single-cell morphology data was then loaded into an anndata object using the scanpy library15 Outlier clusters, e.g., representing dead cells, segmentation errors or dust, were removed by Leiden clustering and manual review of representative cell images (Suppl. Fig. 1a, b). To integrate data across two donors and plates, the scanpy implementation of the harmony16 R package was used which achieved good integration (Suppl. Fig. 2c). After integration, additional QC iterations were performed.

To identify marker CP features per cluster, we compared CP profiles for cells from each cluster to those from all other clusters from the respective cell type using a two-sided t-test as implemented in the rank_genes_groups function in scanpy (Suppl. Table 5-6) and used the t-statistic to rank the CP features. To characterize compositional changes, we fit the non-binomial GLMs with quasi-likelihood methods without trend using the edgeR implementation modelling cells per cluster instead of reads per gene as in previous differential abundance work17 Thereby, low-abundance clusters with less than 2 counts in the given contrast groups are removed. UMAP plots were generated with scanpy15 and all others with ggplot2 in R.

RNA extraction and NGS library preparation

RNA from each well was extracted using the RNeasy 96 Kit (QIAGEN, 74181) following the manufacturer’s protocol with slight modification to optimize total RNA yield. Technical duplicates (different plate, same well position) were combined into one sample during lysis as the starting materials. Briefly, supernatant was removed and 200 µL of RLT buffer supplemented with 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, M3148) were added to each well in the first technical plate to lyse the cells. After homogenization, the cell lysate was transferred to a second technical plate and homogenized. Cell lysates were kept at −80 °C for storage or immediately continued for RNA extraction. If stored, cell lysates were thawed on ice before RNA isolation and clean up with spin technology. Extracted total RNA was stored in −80 °C until further use.

For bulk RNA-seq, total RNA derived from human primary NHDF & NHLF cells was quantitatively and qualitatively assessed using the fluorescence Broad Range Quant-iT RNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Standard Sensitivity RNA Analysis DNF-471 Kit on a 96-channel Fragment Analyzer (Agilent), respectively. All total RNA samples had a RIN > 7.5. Total RNA input of 100 ng was employed for library preparation with the NEBNext UltraExpress RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, #E3330), together with the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, #E7490) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB, #E7600) as per manufacturer’s instructions on a Biomek i7 (Beckman Coulter). For double-stranded cDNA purification, Ampure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) were used instead of the recommended SPRIselect Beads. Libraries were amplified with 13 PCR cycles.

The final RNA-seq libraries were eluted in 16 µL EB Buffer (Qiagen) and were quantified by the High Sensitivity dsDNA Quanti-iT Assay Kit (ThermoFisher) on a Synergy HTX (BioTek). Libraries were assessed for size distribution and adapter dimer presence ( < 0.5%) by the High Sensitivity Small Fragment DNF-477 Kit on a 96-channel Fragment Analyzer (Agilent). Libraries were normalized on the MicroLab STAR (Hamilton), pooled and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with dual index, paired-end reads at 2 × 100 bp length (Rd1: 101 bp, Rd2: 8 bp, Rd3: 8 bp, Rd4:101 bp) with an average sequencing depth of ~32 million Pass-Filter reads per sample.

Transcriptomics data processing and analysis

Sequencing reads were processed with a Nextflow pipeline building upon the implementation of the ENCODE’ Long RNA-seq pipeline: Filtered reads were mapped against the Homo sapiens (human) genome hg38d1/GRCh38 (primary assembly, excluding alternate contigs) using the STAR (v2.5.2b) aligner allowing for soft clipping of adapter sequences. For quantification, transcript annotation files from Ensembl version 86 were used, which corresponds to GENCODE 25. Samples were quantified with the above annotations using featureCounts (v1.5.1). Quality controls were implemented using FastQC (v0.11.5), picardmetrics (v0.2.4), and dupRadar (v1.2.2) at the respective steps.

13,051 genes with a count ≥ 100 in at least one sample were retained and VST normalization was applied. To evaluate senescence levels induced across treatment conditions, we computed differential expression for each treatment condition to the no treatment control using edgeR17 Subsequently, we evaluated agreement with the core senescence signature from Hernadez-Segura7 which aims to characterize senescence across cell types and stressors and was also derived from fibroblast transcriptomics data. To do so, we correlated the observed logFCs in each contrast with the logFC observed in their fibroblast meta-analysis for the 55 genes in their core transcriptomic senescence signature. Next, we evaluated agreement with the SenMayo signature11 which aims to characterize senescence in vivo across tissues. To do so, we tested for positive enrichment of the 125 SenMayo signature genes in the observed contrasts using fgsea18.

Morphology & transcriptomics data integration

To identify shared signals between CP and transcriptomics, we applied MOFA19, as implemented in the MOFA2 package, using default parameters to integrate data from matching well positions (Suppl. Fig. 6a). This was done for dermal and lung fibroblasts, respectively, so that donor-specific batch effects were observed for transcriptomics and corrected per cell type using the removeBatchEffect function from the limma R package20 Next, we evaluated the top 5 factors explaining the most variance in each MOFA model and compared activities between both cell types per treatment group to identify corresponding pairs (Pearson correlation > 0.5). For these pairs, we then compared the genes and CP feature weights, respectively, to better understand if factor pairs with similar activity patterns are driven by the same features. From the positive and negative gene expression weights, respectively, we computed enrichment of Reactome pathways21, derived from MSigDB v2026.1.Hs, using the modified PCGSE22 version implemented in the MOFA2 package. Furthermore, we computed enriched dyes and regions among top 100 CP features by absolute weight using a one-sided Fisher’s Exact test and derived correlations between the CP weight signatures and the cell-level morphology profiles and plotted these on the UMAPs to identify associated cell populations.
