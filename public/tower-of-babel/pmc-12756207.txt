Safety, Tolerability, Pharmacokinetics, and Viral Pharmacodynamics of the Monoclonal Antibody Sotrovimab Administered via Intramuscular Injection in Participants with Early, Mild-to-Moderate COVID-19: A Randomized Clinical Trial

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesNew coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) therapeutics, including intramuscular (IM) formulations, may increase patient access. In COMET-TAIL, sotrovimab 500 mg IM was non-inferior to 500 mg intravenous (IV) in reducing the risk of COVID-19 progression; however, 250 mg IM was associated with more hospitalizations, despite similar viral load (VL) reductions to 500 mg IM.MethodsCOMET-PEAK was a randomized, three-part study to assess safety, tolerability, and viral pharmacodynamics of sotrovimab in adults with early, mild-to-moderate COVID-19. Parts B/C evaluated sotrovimab 500-mg IV infusion versus 500-mg or 250-mg IM injection. The primary objective was to compare virologic response of sotrovimab IM versus IV (mean area under the curve from day 1 to day 8 [AUCD1–8] of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 [SARS-CoV-2] VL); 90% confidence interval (CI) limits of 0.5–2.0 indicated equivalence.ResultsParts B/C included 167 and 157 participants, respectively. Median age range was 42–47 years, and approximately 50% of participants had one or more risk factor for severe disease. The primary objective was met: the ratio of geometric mean VL AUCD1–8 of sotrovimab IM versus IV was 1.04 (90% CI 0.98–1.09; Part B) and 1.02 (90% CI 0.94–1.11; Part C). No new safety signals emerged for sotrovimab; IM administration was well tolerated, with few injection-site reactions.ConclusionsBoth sotrovimab IM doses were equivalent to 500 mg IV with respect to SARS-CoV-2 VL change. IM administration was safe and well tolerated. The validity of VL as a biomarker for COVID-19 progression warrants further study.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT04779879 (date of first registration: March 3, 2021), https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04779879.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40268-025-00529-2.

Introduction

Since the World Health Organization declared coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) to be a global pandemic in March 2020, the COVID-19 landscape has continued to evolve, both in terms of viral variants and treatment options. There are multiple opportunities for therapeutics to mitigate the burden of COVID-19 disease, including treatments that limit progression of disease in mild-to-moderate cases, reduce mortality and long-term morbidity in severe cases, and protect individuals from severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection both before and after exposure. The COVID-19 pandemic also stimulated the development and authorization of several vaccines, although reported effectiveness has been variable, and uptake has been limited [1–4].

Sotrovimab is a dual-action engineered human IgG1κ monoclonal antibody (mAb) derived from the parental mAb S309, a potent neutralizing mAb directed against the receptor-binding domain of the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 [5–8]. In the pivotal COMET-ICE phase III clinical trial, a 500-mg intravenous (IV) infusion of sotrovimab resulted in a statistically significant 79% reduction in relative risk (0.21, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.09–0.50) compared with placebo of all-cause >24-h hospitalization or death due to any cause [9]. Sotrovimab (500 mg IV) received European Medicines Agency marketing authorization in December 2021 for use in adults and adolescents (aged ≥ 12 years and weighing ≥ 40 kg) with COVID-19 who do not require oxygen supplementation and who are at increased risk of progressing to severe COVID-19 [10, 11]. Emergency Use Authorization was granted by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in May 2021 for the treatment of mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in adult and pediatric patients (aged ≥ 12 years and weighing ≥ 40 kg) who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 and were at high risk of progression to severe COVID-19. Following the emergence of the Omicron BA.2 variant, the authorization was suspended on April 5, 2022 [12], and fully revoked on December 13, 2024 [13].

The COMET-TAIL trial in participants aged ≥ 12 years diagnosed with COVID-19 at high risk for progression demonstrated the non-inferiority of sotrovimab 500-mg intramuscular (IM) administration to IV administration in reducing the risk of COVID-19 disease progression [14]. A lower dose of 250 mg IM was discontinued early because of a greater proportion of hospitalizations compared with the 500-mg IM and 500-mg IV groups.

The objective of COMET-PEAK was to evaluate the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and viral pharmacodynamics (PD) of sotrovimab administered via IV infusion or IM injection as treatment for adults aged ≥ 18 years with early, mild-to-moderate COVID-19. The trial commenced in parallel with the COMET-TAIL trial, at a time when it was uncertain whether viral load would be a viable surrogate biomarker for COVID-19 therapeutic development. COMET-TAIL therefore provides important clinical outcome data against which the viral PD of sotrovimab administered via the IM route can be assessed in COMET-PEAK, to further inform the potential predictive value of viral load.

Methods

Study Design and Endpoints

COMET-PEAK (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04779879) was a randomized, parallel-group, multicenter, three-part (Part A, double-blind; Parts B and C, open-label) study in non-hospitalized adults aged ≥ 18 years with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 (Fig. 1). The study was conducted at 37 centers in the US, Spain, Canada, and South Korea. Participants were centrally randomized using an interactive voice response/web response system, with study sites contacting the system prior to the start of study intervention for each participant.Fig. 1Study design. In Part C, participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive a sotrovimab Gen2 500-mg IV infusion or 250-mg IM injection. Two versions of sotrovimab were tested in Part A: generation 1 (Gen1) and generation 2 (Gen2). Gen1 was manufactured using a pool of non-clonal cells stably transfected to express sotrovimab sequences, and was also used in the COMET-ICE trial [9]. Gen2 was produced using a similar manufacturing process involving a clonally derived cell bank, and was the intended commercial product. Full methods for Part A are described in the electronic supplementary material (ESM).

In Parts B and C, the safety, tolerability, PK, and viral PD of Gen2 sotrovimab administered as a single 500-mg IV infusion, or as a single 500-mg (Part B) or 250-mg (Part C) IM injection, were evaluated (Fig. 1). The primary objective in Parts B and C was to compare the virologic response of sotrovimab Gen2 (i.e. the intended commercial product) IV with that of sotrovimab Gen2 IM, with an endpoint of mean area under the curve (AUC) of SARS-CoV-2 viral load as measured by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) from day 1 to day 8 (AUCD1–8), with predefined 90% CI limits of 0.5–2.0 indicating equivalence. Secondary endpoints included day 1 to day 5 (AUCD1–5) and day 1 to day 11 (AUCD1–11) of SARS-CoV-2 viral load; safety and tolerability of sotrovimab Gen2 IV and sotrovimab Gen2 IM through day 29, week 24, and week 36 (end of study); and PK of sotrovimab through day 29 and week 24.

Study Participants

COMET-PEAK enrolled participants aged ≥ 18 years for Part A and ≥ 18 to < 70 years for Parts B and C (≥ 19 years in South Korea). Eligible participants had a positive SARS-CoV-2 test result via a validated molecular diagnostic test (e.g. qRT-PCR) on any respiratory sample type ≤ 7 days prior to enrollment, oxygen saturation ≥ 94% on room air, symptomatic COVID-19 disease (defined by ≥ 1 of the following: fever, chills, cough, sore throat, malaise, headache, joint/muscle pain, change in smell or taste, vomiting, diarrhea, shortness of breath on exertion), and were ≤ 7 days from onset of symptoms to enrollment. Screening for study eligibility was performed 24 h prior to dosing. Female participants had to agree to follow the contraceptive requirements provided.

Participants were excluded if they were currently hospitalized or judged by the investigator as likely to require hospitalization in the next 24 h, had symptoms consistent with severe COVID-19 (i.e. shortness of breath at rest, respiratory distress, or requiring supplemental oxygen), were likely to die in the next 7 days, or were severely immunocompromised. Patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who were hospitalized purely for public health/quarantine purposes in South Korea were not excluded. For Parts B and C, participants were also excluded if they had any condition, per the investigator’s judgment, that would prohibit receipt of IM injections, such as a coagulation disorder, bleeding diathesis, or thrombocytopenia.

Exclusion criteria around vaccination evolved in response to increased SARS-CoV-2 vaccine availability and rollout during the study period. For Parts A and B, prior receipt of an authorized or approved SARS-CoV-2 vaccine at any time was not permitted; however, this was not an exclusion criterion for Part C. For all study parts, receipt of any vaccine within 48 h prior to enrollment was not permitted, and administration of any vaccine was not allowed for 90 days after dosing.

Treatment Administration and Assessment

In Parts B and C, IV infusion of sotrovimab was administered over 15 minutes. In Part B, IM administration of the 500-mg dose was via two injections (2 × 4 mL), one in each dorsogluteal muscle. In Part C, the 250-mg IM dose was given either as a single 250-mg (4 mL) injection in the dorsogluteal muscle or as two injections (2 × 2 mL), one in each deltoid muscle. Nasopharyngeal samples using nylon-flocked swabs (one swab per nostril) were added to 3 mL of Viral Transport Medium (COPAN Diagnostics, Murrieta, CA, USA) and processed at a central laboratory (Cerba Research) for virologic assessments at days 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 11, 15, 22, and 29.

Safety assessments included the monitoring of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs), clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, electrocardiograms, physical examinations, collection of hospitalization and death data, active monitoring of COVID-19 progression by investigators, local injection-site tolerability assessments, and pregnancy tests. Events related to the expected progression, signs, or symptoms of COVID-19, unless more severe than expected for the participant’s presenting clinical status and medical comorbidities (and not determined to be related to study medication), were intended to be reported as a disease-related event (DRE) and not as an AE or SAE.

Statistical Analysis

Safety endpoints were summarized by descriptive statistics using the safety population, which comprised all randomized participants who were exposed to study treatment. The number and percentage of participants experiencing at least one AE of any type, AEs within each body system, and AEs within each Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) Preferred Term were presented for each treatment group.

Virology PD endpoints were assessed in the viral PD population, which comprised all participants from the safety population with a central laboratory-confirmed quantifiable SARS-CoV-2 viral load as assessed using qRT-PCR at baseline (day 1). Summaries of mean viral load and change from baseline were presented on the log10 scale for participants at each visit. For Parts B and C, the mean of log-transformed AUCD1–8, AUCD1–5, and AUCD1–11 in nasopharyngeal swab samples was assessed using an analysis of covariance model with treatment, prior exposure to an authorized or approved SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (for Part C only), and baseline viral load as covariates. The estimated geometric mean ratios and 90% CIs of 500 mg IV:500 mg IM in Part B and 500 mg IV:250 mg IM in Part C after back-transformation were presented.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction Assay

Total nucleic acids were isolated using the MagNA Pure 96 system and the MagNA Pure 96 DNA and Viral NA Small Volume kit (Roche Applied Science), to which a PDV internal control was added. Isolation was performed using the MagNA Pure 96 system using the Viral NA Plasma SV external lysis protocol. The amplification and detection of the SARS-CoV-2 N gene was performed with a real-time polymerase chain reaction system (Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System).

Sequence Analysis

Qualifying samples were analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) to evaluate the emergence of antiviral resistance. Nasal-mid turbinate swabs from Part A participants, and nasopharyngeal swabs from Part B and C participants, were used to perform NGS of the spike gene at baseline (day 1) and post-baseline (day 2 to day 29). Total nucleic acid was isolated and purified from nasopharyngeal swab samples using the MagMAX Viral Nucleic Isolation kit on the KingFisher Flex system according to manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Equine arteritis virus was spiked into the sample as an internal RNA isolation control. The SARS-CoV-2 spike gene outer amplification step was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum TaqHF DNA polymerase, including RNaseOUT (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). The full-length spike gene was amplified by nested RT-PCR using the primer sets developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention using the Q5 Hot Start High Fidelity DNA Polymerase kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) [15]. Deep sequencing was performed by two runs of three and two non-overlapping amplicon pools using the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at DDL Diagnostics Laboratory, Rijswijk, the Netherlands. The viral loads used during validation of the sequencing assay at DDL were quantified by qRT-PCR using the E-gene and N-gene [16]. Based on the Cerba DDL N-gene viral load assay, a viral load cut-off of ≥ 3.0 log10 copies/mL was applied.

Sequences were compared to a reference sequence (Wuhan-Hu-1, GenBank: MN908947.3) to determine amino acid substitutions, deletions, or gaps using DDL’s Athena bioinformatics pipeline, with the following minimum settings: coverage for mapping > 5000, read coverage for consensus extraction ≥ 500, base quality setting ≥ 20, read quality setting ≥ 20, and read length ≥ 50. A 5% allelic frequency detection threshold cut-off was used for data analysis. Amino acid substitutions detected at ≥ 5% allelic fraction were reported as minor substitutions, substitutions detected at > 1% and < 5% allelic fraction were shown to be nonspecific, and substitutions detected at < 1% allelic fraction were not reported. Amino acid substitutions at positions 333, 334, 335, 336, 337, 339, 340, 341, 343, 344, 345, 346, 354, 356, 357, 358, 359, 360, 361, 440, 441, and 509 in the spike were defined as the epitope positions. Treatment-emergent amino acid substitutions were defined as any substitution that was detected in a post-baseline sample at ≥ 5% but was not detected in the corresponding baseline sample at ≥ 5%.To determine the presence of circulating variants of concern and variants of interest (VOC/VOI), available sequences from any visit were analyzed for known variants (Supplementary Table 1, see ESM), as well as any additional amino acid substitutions of interest including K417N, K417T, L452R, S477N, E484K, S494P, or N501Y.

Pharmacokinetics

Blood samples for serum PK were collected on day 1 (pre-dose, end of infusion, and approximately 1, 2, 6, and 8 h following the end of infusion) and days 3, 8, 15, 29, 85, 141, and 169. A lead-in phase cohort (composed of approximately 10 patients per arm) included more intensive PK sampling, with additional collection timepoints on days 2, 5, and 57.

Results

Results for Part A of the study, which did not include an IM administration component, are primarily reported in the ESM.

Participant Disposition

Participants were enrolled from February 2021 to July 2021. Participant disposition for each study part is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. A total of 177 participants were screened in Part B, of which 167 (94%) entered the study and were randomized to receive sotrovimab 500 mg IV (n = 84) or sotrovimab 500 mg IM (n = 83). The Part B safety population included 166 participants (one participant was randomized to receive sotrovimab 500 mg IM but did not receive a dose and therefore was not included). The safety follow-up was extended mid-study from 24 to 36 weeks; therefore, only 12/166 participants in the Part B safety population had 36-week follow-up. The viral PD population included 134 participants.

A total of 162 participants were screened in Part C, of whom 157 participants (97%) were randomized (sotrovimab 500 mg IV: n = 79; sotrovimab 250 mg IM: n = 78) and five (3%) were considered as screen failures. All 157 randomized participants were included in the safety population and 127 participants were included in the viral PD population.

Participant Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Baseline participant demographics and clinical characteristics were similar across treatment groups in each study part (Supplementary Tables 2–4). The median (minimum, maximum) age of participants was 47.5 (18, 65) years in Part A, 47.0 (18, 69) years in Part B, and 42.0 (19, 71) years in Part C. The majority of participants in each study part were White (79–90%) and Hispanic or Latino (56–73%). Viral Resistance

Resistance surveillance was conducted on all randomized and treated participants. Of these, 282 participants qualified for sequencing at one or more visits; 253 at baseline and 251 post-baseline had sequencing results available (Supplementary Table 8, see ESM). All participants with baseline or post-baseline sequence available had substitutions in the spike protein at allelic frequencies of ≥ 5%. Substitutions in the epitope were detected at baseline in 16 of 253 (6%) participants (Supplementary Table 8, see ESM). Spike substitutions in the sotrovimab epitope were detected at P337H, R346I, R346K, and N440K (Supplementary Table 9, see ESM). These substitutions are not part of the changes in spike protein sequence associated with the principal variants detected in the study (e.g. Alpha, Beta, Delta).

Among the 222 participants with paired baseline and post-baseline sequencing results available, 152 (68%) had a treatment-emergent substitution detected in the spike protein, of which 27 (18% of 152) were in the epitope (Table 3). The frequency of treatment-emergent substitutions in the epitope across the 500-mg arms ranged from 0 to 17% and was 12 and 15% in the 500-mg and 250-mg IM arms, respectively. The frequency of treatment-emergent substitutions in the sotrovimab epitope was numerically lower in the 500-mg IM arm (7%) than in the 500-mg IV arm (15%) in Part B, and similar in the 500-mg IV (12%) and 250-mg IM (15%) arms in Part C. Treatment-emergent substitutions were detected in the epitope at P337L, G339D, E340A, E340K, E340V, R346I, R346K, N354S, R357G, and R509I. The E340K substitution was the most frequently observed treatment-emergent substitution in the epitope, detected in 15 of 222 participants (7%) (Table 3). Treatment-emergent substitutions associated with a loss of susceptibility in cell culture, P337L, E340A, E340K, and E340V, were detected in 20 of 222 (9%) participants with paired samples (Table 3) [17]. Safety and Tolerability

Safety data for Part A (Supplementary Table 10, see ESM) were consistent with those for the sotrovimab IV arms in Parts B and C through week 24.

A summary of all common (≥ 2%) AEs through week 36 by overall frequency in Parts B and C is shown in Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Table 12, respectively (see ESM). The most common AE in the sotrovimab 500-mg IM (Part B) and 250-mg IM (Part C) groups was injection-site reaction (ISR). A total of 10 (12%) participants in the 500-mg IM group experienced a Grade 1 ISR. In eight participants, this occurred at the 1-h post-dose assessment and resolved by the 2-h post-dose assessment; in two participants, a Grade 1 ISR occurred on day 1. Four participants (5%; two each with dorsogluteal and deltoid injection sites) in the 250-mg IM group experienced a Grade 1 ISR. In one participant, this occurred at the 1-h post-dose assessment and resolved by the 2-h post-dose assessment on day 1; in the remaining two participants, Grade 1 ISRs were reported on day 2.

There were few treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) in Parts B and C, and none were serious. Through week 36, 2 of 84 (2%) participants in the sotrovimab 500-mg IV group and 11 of 82 participants (13%) in the sotrovimab 500-mg IM group had TRAEs in Part B. Both TRAEs in the 500-mg IV group were leg restlessness. In the 500-mg IM group, the following TRAEs were reported: injection-site pain (12%), injection-site nodule (1%), and urticaria (1%). No participants in the sotrovimab 500-mg IV group and 4 of 78 (5%) participants in the sotrovimab 250-mg IM group had TRAEs through week 36 in Part C. The following treatment-related local ISRs were reported in the 250-mg IM group: injection-site pain (3%), injection-site discomfort (1%), and injection-site nodule (1%).

Pharmacokinetics

The exposure following a 500-mg IM administration of Gen2 sotrovimab was approximately half the exposure following a 500-mg IV administration of Gen2 sotrovimab, with mean AUCD1–29 of 671.3 and 1407.6 day μg/mL for IM and IV administration, respectively. The exposure following a 250-mg IM administration of Gen2 sotrovimab was approximately half the exposure following a 500-mg IM administration of Gen2 sotrovimab, with mean AUCD1–29 of 380.8 and 671.3 day μg/mL for 250-mg and 500-mg IM administration, respectively. Following a 500-mg IV dose of Gen2 sotrovimab, the mean maximum concentration (Cmax) (end of infusion) was around 140 μg/mL. Following a 500-mg or 250-mg IM dose of Gen2 sotrovimab, the median time to Cmax (Tmax) and mean Cmax were 6.9 days and 28.2 μg/mL, and 14 days and 11.4 μg/mL, respectively.

Discussion

The COMET-PEAK study assessed the safety, tolerability, PK, and viral PD of sotrovimab administered via IM injection for the treatment of early, mild-to-moderate COVID-19. During the period of enrollment of Parts A and B, Alpha was the main variant of SARS-CoV-2 globally, while in Part C, the Delta variant predominated. The study suggested the equivalence of sotrovimab 500 mg IM (Part B) and 250 mg IM (Part C) to 500 mg IV with respect to mean AUCD1–8 of SARS-CoV-2 viral load. In addition, sotrovimab IM injection was well tolerated, with a low overall rate of ISRs, all of which were mild. Across all doses and routes of administration, there was a very low frequency of TRAEs and no treatment-related SAEs.

Although the study was not designed to evaluate efficacy in terms of clinical outcomes, a higher percentage of participants had a COVID-19-related SAE of hospitalization (including one death) in the 250-mg IM arm of Part C than in any other arm. In the COMET-TAIL study, which was a powered non-inferiority trial, the 250-mg IM dose arm was discontinued early because of a greater proportion of hospitalizations in this group compared with the 500-mg IM and 500-mg IV groups [14]. However, the increase in progression could not be explained by differences in viral load, since the AUC values were similar across the groups. In this context, the demonstration in COMET-PEAK of equivalence in mean viral load AUCD1–8 between the sotrovimab 250-mg IM and sotrovimab 500-mg IV arms suggests that change in nasopharyngeal SARS-CoV-2 viral load is unlikely to be a robust surrogate measure for COVID-19 disease progression. Indeed, guidance from the FDA on developing COVID-19 therapies notes that, although virologic measures may be acceptable as a primary endpoint in phase II trials, they are not appropriate as primary endpoints in phase III trials [18]. There is currently no established predictive relationship between magnitude and timing of viral load reductions in response to a COVID-19 therapeutic and the extent of clinical benefit in patients. Further studies elucidating a robust surrogate measure for COVID-19 disease progression will be critical for accelerating future delivery of COVID-19 therapeutics, particularly given the increasing complexity of conducting COVID-19 efficacy studies.

Resistance analysis detected that 27 (12%) participants had treatment-emergent substitutions in the sotrovimab epitope. Rates were numerically similar across the study arms, with a slightly lower percentage observed in the 500-mg IM group. Given the range of treatment-emergent substitutions observed in the 500-mg IV arms (0–17%), this finding would not seem to be easily explicable by a PK/PD drug selection pressure hypothesis and could reflect a chance finding in a relatively small clinical study; in the larger phase III COMET-TAIL study, the sotrovimab 500-mg IV arm was observed to have a higher frequency of post-baseline substitutions in the sotrovimab epitope than the 500-mg or 250-mg IM arms [19]. In the present study, treatment-emergent substitutions at P337L, E340A, E340K, and E340V positions resulting in significant reduced in-vitro susceptibility to sotrovimab were found in 9% of those with paired samples. One limitation of the resistance profiling in COMET-PEAK is that samples were subjected to sequencing analysis at defined timepoints (baseline, day 8, day 29). Samples from additional timepoints were sequenced based on other data (e.g. viral rebound) or due to the inability to sequence a day 8 or day 29 sample. As the virus declines over time and most patients (> 80%) became negative for SARS-CoV-2 RNA by day 29, the majority of sequencing data came from attempts to sequence the baseline and day 8 samples. In the treatment-emergent substitution analysis, most substitutions in the sotrovimab epitope were observed at day 8.

Seven of 353 (2%) study participants were hospitalized for COVID-19. None of the seven had any mild or moderate immunocompromising conditions (severely immunocompromised patients were excluded); rather, these participants had comorbidities that are known risk factors for COVID-19, including overweight/obesity, diabetes mellitus, and older age. In six of the hospitalized participants, viral load declined overall during the study, and was below the lower limit of detection/quantification at day 29.

Furthermore, no participant who reported being hospitalized with COVID-19 had a treatment-emergent substitution detected, making the clinical relevance of this finding uncertain; participants with these substitutions also cleared the virus. While differences in study design, population, and viral landscape make comparison difficult, the 9% rate is apparently consistent with the rates of treatment-emergent resistance associated with substantial reduced in-vitro susceptibility observed for other single mAbs used for COVID-19 [20], and possibly higher than the rates observed for combination mAbs [21] or small molecules such as nirmatrelvir/ritonavir [22]. No new or unexpected safety concerns were identified in the current study for IV or IM sotrovimab. Results from Parts B and C indicate that IM administration of sotrovimab was generally well tolerated. ISRs were, as expected, the most commonly reported AEs in the sotrovimab IM arms; however, rates were low overall and all reported ISRs were considered to be mild (Grade 1). Results also suggest that IM administration of sotrovimab in either the deltoid (as 2 × 2-mL injections) or the gluteal muscle (2 × 4-mL or single 4-mL injection) is feasible from a tolerability perspective, which may be useful learnings for future pandemic therapeutic development.

The small sample size in some parts or arms of the study is a potential limitation. For Parts B and C, sample sizes were calculated to enable a comparison of log10 SARS-CoV-2 viral load AUCD1–8 based on the treatment ratio 90% CI falling within pharmacologic equivalence bounds of 0.5–2.0; the margin of 0.5–2.0, although broad, was deemed appropriate for the comparison of viral load due to the endpoint being a variable PD endpoint. For both Parts B and C, the 90% CI was contained within the equivalence bounds of 0.5–2.0 and also met the stricter bounds of 0.85–1.2. The authors are not aware of validated SARS-CoV-2 viral load equivalence criteria, but note that the actual AUCD1–8 ratio of IV to IM was within 4% across both doses in Parts B and C. In addition, in the absence of an untreated control arm, the effect of sotrovimab in either part cannot be differentiated from the natural history of viral load decline. Inconsistent reporting of DREs is another possible limitation. Guidance to report hospitalization with COVID-19 as a DRE unless more severe than expected was impacted by uncertainty around the expected severity of this newly emerging disease; some investigators therefore defaulted to reporting these hospitalizations as a SAE.

Conclusion

The COMET-PEAK study showed the equivalence of sotrovimab 500 mg IM and 250 mg IM to 500 mg IV with respect to SARS-CoV-2 viral load change. In addition, IM administration of sotrovimab appeared to be safe and well tolerated. The findings, in context with those from COMET-TAIL, suggest viral load may not be a robust biomarker for clinical outcomes in COVID-19, and further studies are warranted. By enabling faster administration in a wider range of healthcare settings, IM formulations may increase access compared with IV treatments; therefore, IM formulations have the potential to reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
