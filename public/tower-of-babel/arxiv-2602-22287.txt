Multi-Level Causal Embeddings
Willem Schooltink, Fabio Massimo Zennaro

Abstract
Abstractions of causal models allow for the coarsening of models such that relations of cause and effect are preserved. Whereas abstractions focus on the relation between two models, in this paper we study a framework for causal embeddings which enable multiple detailed models to be mapped into sub-systems of a coarser causal model. We define causal embeddings as a generalization of abstraction, and present a generalized notion of consistency. By defining a multi-resolution marginal problem , we showcase the relevance of causal embeddings for both the statistical marginal problem and the causal marginal problem; furthermore, we illustrate its practical use in merging datasets coming from models with different representations.



INTRODUCTION

Causality enables us to reason about real-world systems on a level beyond statistics, allowing us to answer questions on the effects of interventions and hypotheticals. Such queries naturally show in many fields, such as medicine, biology or economics, where studying correlation is not enough. The formalism of Structural Causal Models (SCMs)
[
19
]
rigorously captures reasoning about observations, interventions and counterfactuals (hypotheticals).

However, causal models of real-world systems often grow very large to a point where reasoning becomes impractical, as SCMs, based on Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), do not scale very well. One solution to the issue is to work with models at a coarser resolution. For example, when modeling the wildlife populations in a forest, we may have data on all subspecies of deer and rodents present, but for our purposes we may only care about the combined population of all deer subspecies. In such cases we can use
causal abstraction
to describe how a detailed (low-level) model maps to a coarser (high-level) model, whilst preserving causal relations when merging variables and the values they take. Such frameworks of causal abstraction
[
21
,
7
,
6
]
have rigorous mathematical foundations, and provide measures to evaluate consistency among the models.

Another solution to deal with large SCMs is to reason about sub-systems. In such cases we wish to map, or
embed
, the detailed models into sub-parts of a coarse model. Fig.
1
illustrates how embeddings compare against abstractions. While abstraction deals with one-to-one mappings of models, embeddings deal with the common challenge in the sciences of having a high-level model of a system, for example an overarching climate model, and a combination of low-level sub-models, each describing only a part of the global model.

Contribution.

In this paper, we extend the idea of abstractions: whereas abstractions describe how an entire high-level SCM can be described by a more detailed low-level SCM, we study
causal embeddings
, describing how sub-systems of a high-level SCM can be described by detailed low-level models. We will (i) illustrate how this new point of view allows us to define a high-level causal model as the combination of multiple low-level sub-system models, (ii) discuss the graphical and functional consistency of causal embeddings, (iii) discuss theoretical applications of causal embeddings in a multi-level version of the marginal problem, and (iv) show the use of causal embeddings as a tool to merge overlapping datasets with differing levels of detail.

RELATED WORKS

Causal abstractions can be described as SCM-to-SCM mappings; two frameworks have been proposed: the category theoretical
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
framework
[
21
,
20
]
and the
\tau
-
\omega
framework
[
22
,
8
,
6
]
. Alternatively, abstractions can be described as DAG-to-DAG mappings from one causal graph to another
[
1
]
. The relations between these abstraction frameworks have been studied by
23
. The closest work to our proposal is
16
, who adopts a non-surjective definition of abstraction in their proposed
\phi
framework. However, our definition is more flexible and extends to the case of embedding multiple low-level models into a single high-level model.

Embeddings have relation to both the statistical
[
12
]
and causal marginal problem
[
10
]
. Given multiple marginal SCMs
10
proposes a method to generate a family of compatible joint SCMs, and through falsification find those that are counterfactually consistent. We show that embeddings can be used to tackle a multi-resolution version of the causal marginal problem. Relatedly,
14
discusses how SCMs can be learned for overlapping statistical datasets under certain assumptions, while the Integration of Overlapping Datasets algorithm
[
24
]
proposes a sound method to merge causal datasets and learn an equivalence class of graphs representing the data generation. This work was extended to a more special case by
2
exploiting potential knowledge of variable ordering.

Finally, embeddings can be used to merge causal datasets. Causal abstractions has already been used before to merge datasets in order to improve statistical power
[
25
,
9
]
, although limited to transporting data from one model to another one. In the causal literature,
11
shows how causal models can help in merging overlapping statistical datasets, while exploiting data from different models has been studied in the context of data fusion and transportability
[
5
,
17
]
, including the case of multiple environments
[
4
]
; these approaches, however, do not establish an explicit relation between SCMs, but rely on graphical calculus to take best advantage of observational and interventional data from a source and target model.

CAUSALITY

Structural Causal Models.

SCMs are formal descriptions of causal models, specifying causal variables and effects among them
[
19
]
. We define an SCM as follows:

Definition 1

(Structural Causal Model)
.

An SCM is a 4-tuple
\mathcal{M}:\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathcal{F},P(\mathbf{U})\rangle

•

\mathbf{U}

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{U})

•

\mathbf{V}

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V})

•

•

: a probability distribution over the exogenous variables
\mathbf{U}
.

Causal models allow for reasoning about causality through interventions. In this paper we consider
hard interventions
:

Definition 2

(Intervention)
.

Given a causal model
\mathcal{M}:=\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U},\mathcal{F},P(\mathbf{U})\rangle
an intervention on a variable
X\in\mathbf{V}
, denoted as
do(X=x)
, is the replacement of the function
f_{X}\in\mathcal{F}
with a constant function
f^{\prime}_{X}=x
.

As is common, we will apply the
do
-operator over sets of variables
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
, implying the replacement of the functions
f_{X}\in\mathcal{F}
for all
X\in\mathbf{X}
. In essence, when intervening on a variable
X
its value is no longer dependent on its parents but forcibly set to some value
x
. Consequently, as the DAG implied by an SCM has edges determined by the functions
\mathcal{F}
, interventions have graphical implications: specifically, an intervention
do(X)
removes all edges coming into
X
in the DAG.

PEARL’S CAUSAL HIERARCHY.

In causal reasoning there are three distinct types of questions one may wish to answer: (i) given the observation
X
what can be said about
Y
, (ii) if
X
is set to
X=x
what can be said about
Y
, (iii) given observations
Y=y
and
X=x
what can be said about
Y
if
X
had been set to
X=\hat{x}
? These questions can be categorized as (i) observing
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, (ii) acting
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, and (iii) imagining
\mathcal{L}_{3}
. This defines the Pearl’s Causal Hierarchy (PCH)
[
19
,
3
]
, where each layer subsumes the previous layer, but cannot be reduced to the previous one: a query at layer
\mathcal{L}_{i}
can be answered at layer
\mathcal{L}_{i+1}
, but a query in layer
\mathcal{L}_{i+1}
cannot in general be reduced to layer
\mathcal{L}_{i}
. Fully specified SCMs allow for reasoning about counterfactuals
\mathcal{L}_{3}
.

Notation.

For generalization, we will use the
\mathcal{L}_{i}
operator in distributions: instead of
P(Y|X)
we write
P(Y|\mathcal{L}_{1}(X))
, and instead of
P(Y|do(X))
we write
P(Y|\mathcal{L}_{2}(X))
. We will not consider
\mathcal{L}_{3}
quantities in this work.

CAUSAL ABSTRACTIONS

Causal abstractions provide tools that allow us to map a detailed low-level causal model to a coarser high-level model. Similar to how causal models have a functional and graphical side (SCMs and DAGs, respectively), abstractions can be defined on both the functional and graphical level.

Functional.

We first consider the functional side through the framework of the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction
[
21
]
.

Definition 3

(
\alpha
-abstraction)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:\langle\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{M}},

P(\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{M}})\rangle
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:\langle\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}},\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}},P(\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}})\rangle
be two SCMs, then an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is given by a 3-tuple
\langle\mathbf{R},\varphi,\alpha_{V^{\prime}}\rangle
with:

1.

\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
is a subset of relevant variables in
\mathcal{M}
.

2.

\varphi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is a surjective map from the relevant variables to the variables of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

3.

\alpha_{V^{\prime}}:\mathcal{R}(\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime}))\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(V^{\prime})
, for each
V^{\prime}\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
, is a surjective function from the range of the variables in the pre-image
\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
in
\mathcal{M}
to the range of the variable
V^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Importantly, the application of
\alpha_{\mathbf{V}}
to a distribution is given by the pushforward
\alpha_{\mathbf{V}}\left[P(\mathbf{V})\right]={\alpha_{\mathbf{V}}}_{\#}(P)(\mathbf{V})
.

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstractions do not enforce any form of causal consistency between a base model and an abstracted model; any mapping, as long as surjective, is permissible. For this reason, an error measure is used to define how much an abstracted model agrees with a base model. For
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstractions the error measure is defined as follows:

Definition 4

(
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-Abstraction error)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction and
\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
, its
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-error is given by the distance or divergence
D
between the distribution obtained by first abstracting and then evaluating:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\>|\>\alpha_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))])},

and that obtained by first evaluating and then abstracting:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\alpha_{\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}}\left[P_{\mathcal{M}}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\>|\>\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})))\right]},

and taking the maximum over all
\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
as the error. Visually this corresponds to the maximum distance between the paths in blue and orange over all diagrams of the following form:
\varphi^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\right)
\varphi^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y^{\prime}}\right)\>|\>\mathcal{L}_{i}\left(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\right)
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}
\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\>|\>\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))
\mathcal{L}_{i}(X^{\prime})
\alpha_{\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}}
\alpha_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}

or formally by the following equation:

\hskip-4.0pte_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\max_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}D\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}|\alpha_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))])},\right.\\
\left.{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\alpha_{\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}}\left[P_{\mathcal{M}}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\>|\>\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})))\right]}\right)

(1)

An abstraction is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent
if its
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-error is zero.

While graphical models do not specify distributions and functions explicitly they do entail constraints on distributional (in)equalities. For example an edge
X\rightarrow Y
implies
P(Y|X)\neq P(Y)
. We denote
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(G)
the set of algebraic constraints on
\mathcal{L}_{i}
distributions implied by the causal graph
G
. We can then define a notion of consistency for graphical abstractions similar to that in functional abstractions.

Definition 6

(Graphical
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-Consistency)
.

Let
G_{\mathcal{M}}
be the causal graph induced by SCM
\mathcal{M}
, and
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
an abstraction of
G_{\mathcal{M}}
with the variable map
\varphi:\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
. Let us define
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}^{-1})
the set of all the constraints obtained from
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}})
substituting each variable
V^{\prime}\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
with the respective pre-image
\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime})
. Then
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is graphically
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent with
G_{\mathcal{M}}
iff

\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\left(G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}^{-1}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\left(G_{\mathcal{M}}\right).

(2)

Graphical consistency and functional consistency align as follows: a graphically
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent abstraction from
G_{\mathcal{M}}
to
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
implies the existence of an SCM
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
that is functionally
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent with a given
\mathcal{M}
; a functionally
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent abstraction implies graphical
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistency only if all causal dependencies are preserved (see for more details
23
).

(CAUSAL) MARGINAL PROBLEM

We will show applications of our work in the marginal problem: the challenge of finding a joint probability given two or more separate but overlapping datasets
[
12
]
, for example estimating the joint distribution
\hat{P}(X,Y,Z)
from the two distributions
\hat{P}(X,Y)
and
\hat{P}(Y,Z)
.

Definition 7

(Marginal Problem)
.

Given
marginal
datasets
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{V}_{1}},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{V}_{n}}
, defined respectively over variables
\mathbf{V}_{i}
, with possibly non-empty intersections of variables between datasets:
\mathbf{V}_{i}\cap\mathbf{V}_{j}\neq\emptyset
, find the joint distribution over the union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{V}_{i}
.

Overlap between the variables of the different datasets is a necessary condition in order to find a meaningful solution to the marginal problem. To illustrate this, consider the counter example where we have two datasets that do not overlap at all; this gives no information on dependencies and as such on the joint distribution of their variables.

The marginal problem per Def.
7
is a statistical problem: a question of combining observational distributions. We will focus on the causal extension of the marginal problem
[
10
]
: finding a joint SCM from multiple overlapping marginal SCMs. Whereas in the statistical setting the object of interest is distributions, in the causal setting it is SCMs. This distinction is required as observational distributions alone cannot fully describe causality.

Definition 8

(Causal Marginal Problem)
.

Given SCMs
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
, with possibly non-empty intersections of endogenous variables between models
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}\cap\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, find the space of joint causal models
\mathcal{M}^{*}
over the union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}
consistent with the models
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

Our proposed causal embeddings will tackle the more complicated setting of the causal marginal problem where the overlapping variables do not share the same level of detail.

SCM PROJECTIONS

In this section we will formalize a notion of projections for SCMs which will be instrumental in defining embeddings. First, recall that SCMs consist of a set of observable variables
\mathbf{V}
and a set of unobservable variables
\mathbf{U}
. One may want to move some variables
V\subset\mathbf{V}
to the set of exogenous variables
\mathbf{U}
, perhaps to simplify the model or since some variables have become unobservable.
Formally we can describe a projection of SCMs as follows:

Definition 9

(SCM Projection)
.

Let
G_{\mathcal{M}}
be the graph induced by an SCM
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
a set of relevant variables to be preserved,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
an SCM over the relevant variables s.t.
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{R}
, and
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
the graph induced by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is a projection of
G_{\mathcal{M}}
iff
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is graphically
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent with
G_{\mathcal{M}}
with
\varphi
the identity mapping.

Importantly, SCM projections enforce only graphical constraints. The SCM projection
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
of an SCM
\mathcal{M}
implies the compatibility of causal (in)dependencies; that is, if there is a causal arrow
X\rightarrow Y
in
\mathcal{M}
and both
X
and
Y
are preserved, then there must exist an arrow
X\rightarrow Y
in the graph induced by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
This definition follows as an extension of latent structure projections
[
18
]
(see App.
A.1
) to the domain of SCMs.

EMBEDDINGS

We will now define embeddings as a generalization of abstractions and similarly provide the notions of functional and graphical consistency. First, recall that an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction describes the relation of a causal model to a coarser causal model.
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstractions enforce surjectivity of both the mapping of variables
\varphi
and the mapping
\alpha_{\mathbf{V}}
of their ranges. The surjectivity requirement makes sure that all states of the abstracted model are represented in the base model. So a base model cannot represent only a sub-system of the coarse model. Dropping the requirement of surjectivity of
\varphi
allows for defining refinements of sub-systems of a larger system. To give more intuition to this idea, consider the following motivating example:

(Simplified Ecosystem Modeling)
.

Imagine we model the causal dynamics of some ecosystem of deer and squirrels, and the effects of predators and human hunting on their population, as in the causal model shown in Fig.

\mathcal{M}_{1}
, describing causal interactions between human hunting, squirrels, deer, and berry bush availability; and model
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, describing the causal interactions between wolves, eagles, red deer, fallow deer, and squirrels.

Formal Definition.

We define an embedding using a generalization of the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction framework by first expanding the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction definition to allow for non-surjective maps
\varphi
wrt
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
:

Definition 10

(Non-surjective
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction)
.

A non-surjective
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction is an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction with:

1.

\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
is a subset of relevant variables in
\mathcal{M}
.

2.

\mathbf{S}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is a subset of relevant variables in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

3.

\varphi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}
is a surjective map between relevant variables.

4.

\alpha_{V^{\prime}}:\mathcal{R}(\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime}))\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(V^{\prime})
, for each
V^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}
, is a surjective function from the range of the pre-image
\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbf{R}
in
\mathcal{M}
to the range of
V^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Similar to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction, Def.
10
does not enforce consistency or make any graphical guarantees. However, since embeddings are to encode a detailed description of a sub-system into a high-level model, it is important to ensure a compatible embedding. For this purpose we define embeddings as follows, by including graphical constraints:

Definition 11

(
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding)
.

Given SCMs
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and a non-surjective
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with
\varphi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding iff the projection of the graph
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
over
\mathbf{S}
is a CDAG of the projection of the graph
G_{\mathcal{M}}
over
\mathbf{R}
.

Let us illustrate
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings on our guiding example.

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding

\text{Deer}&\mapsto\text{Deer}\\

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding definition as the projection of
G_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
over

is a CDAG of the projection of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
over
\mathbf{R}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}=\{Humans,Squirrels,Deer\}
.

CAUSAL CONSISTENCY OF EMBEDDINGS

Similar to abstractions, it is important to assess the consistency of causal embeddings. Therefore, we will define a notion of consistency for embeddings, both in terms of a functional consistency, wrt the functional descriptions of SCMs, and graphical consistency, wrt to the causal graphs.

Functional Consistency.

First let us consider the functional side of the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding: the non-surjective
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction. We adapt the
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-abstraction error from Def.
4
:

Definition 15

(
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-Embedding error)
.

Given an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{S}
, the
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-error is given by the maximum distance or divergence between the distribution obtained by first embedding and then evaluating and the distribution obtained by first evaluating and then embedding, as given by the formula:

\hfill e_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\hfill\max_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{S}}D\left(P_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}\>|\>\alpha_{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}))]),\right.\\
\left.\alpha_{\mathbf{Y}^{\prime}}\left[P_{\mathcal{M}}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{Y}^{\prime})\>|\>\mathcal{L}_{i}(\varphi^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})))\right]\right)

(3)

An embedding with
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-error zero is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent. Functional consistency does not follow by construction, as this may be too limiting and some error may be tolerable, similar to abstractions. Notice that the embedding error subsumes the abstraction error and can be considered a generalization, mirroring the definition of the non-surjective
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction.

Remark 1
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be a set of
n
causal models and
\mathbf{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\}
be a set of
n

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
embeds into the same
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
i
.
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistency of all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbf{A}
solely does
not
imply uniqueness of the
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-distributions over the set of relevant variables
\mathbf{S}
for any permissible

, see App.

for a concrete example.
Instead, to guarantee uniqueness of all distributions stronger assumptions on the set of embeddings

are needed. We conjecture a sufficient condition would be that all causal and confounding edges are constrained by at least one of the embeddings
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbf{A}
.

Additional to the functional side, embeddings have a graphical restriction, so we will also consider graphical consistency by adjusting for unmapped variables in the high-level model. For this we introduce the following notation:

\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\left(G_{\left[\mathbf{S}\right]}^{-1}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\left(G_{\left[\mathbf{R}\right]}\right).

(4)

MULTI-RESOLUTION MARGINAL PROBLEM

We now show how
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings can be used to expand upon the causal marginal problem.
Recall the causal marginal problem (Def.
8
) where marginal models have sets of overlapping variables. We consider the case when this assumption does not hold: that is, marginal models have different representations of the overlapping variables, either by (i) having differing resolutions, or (ii) having the overlapping variables represented using multiple variables. We refer the reader again to Ex.
1
-
3
, where the detailed models have overlapping variables through the embedding.

We define the Multi-Resolution Causal Marginal Problem as an extension of the causal marginal problem where the resolution of the marginal models can differ and a common resolution must be found to represent all marginal models:

Definition 17

(Multi-Resolution Causal Marginal Problem)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}^{*}
be the set of high-level variables, and let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
be SCMs together with associated mappings
\varphi_{i}:\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}^{*}
, find the space of joint causal models
\mathcal{M}^{*}
over the variables
\mathbf{V}^{*}
consistent with
\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

The variable sets of the marginal models no longer need to overlap, but there must exist mappings from each marginal model to a collective set of high-level variables over which an SCM can be specified. This allows for different representations of the previously overlapping variables in each model; for example, a variable
V\in\mathbf{V}^{*}
may be represented by multiple variables in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and by only one variable in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Additionally, this framework allows for models to have different levels of resolution for each variable; for example, a variable
V\in\mathbf{V}^{*}
may be define on a discretized domain in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and on a continuous domain in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

Interestingly, there is a close connection between consistency in embeddings and the marginal problem in that a set of consistent embeddings define a solution to the multi-resolution marginal problem (see proof in App.
B.4
):

Theorem 5

(Consistent Embeddings as Solution to the Multi-Resolution Marginal Problem)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be a set of
n
SCMs and
\mathbf{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\}
be a set of
n

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}:\mathcal{M}_{j}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
embeds into the same
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
j
.
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a solution
\mathcal{M}^{*}
of the multi-resolution marginal problem if for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathbf{A}
: (i)
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent and (ii) the set of relevant variables contains all variables
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}
.

Notice that in the theorem we do not specify the level of consistency
\mathcal{L}_{i}
;
\mathcal{L}_{1}
-consistency provides a solution to a statistical marginal problem,
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-consistency a solution to a causal marginal problem as proposed by
10
, and an
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistency a solution to a causal marginal problem only up to
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.
For an example of the use of embeddings in the multi-resolution marginal problem see the following.

Example 4

(Multi-Resolution Marginal Problem)
.

Recall the motivating example for causal embeddings in Ex.

This does not permit for a causal marginal problem to be defined, instead this needs to be framed in the multi-resolution causal marginal problem.
Given the emdeddings

and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
consistent with
\mathcal{M}_{1}

of the embeddings have been defined earlier, while the maps

We now highlight further connections of our problem to previous marginal problems in the limit cases of reducing a multi-resolution problem to a single-resolution or using an identity embedding

Lemma 6

(Multi-resolution Marginal Problem Reduction)
.

Given a multi-resolution marginal problem, the application of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings reduces it to a single-resolution marginal problem.

Lemma 7

(Identity Embeddings as Solution to the Marginal Problem)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}

\boldsymbol{\alpha}

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all

.
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a solution

.

CONCLUSION

In this work we have introduced causal embeddings as a generalization of abstractions, and extended the notion of consistency. We illustrated with examples Ex.
4
and Ex.
5
that embeddings are not just a theoretical novelty, but serve a practical use: they express a multi-resolution marginal problem, they enable its reduction to the standard marginal problem in both the statistical and causal setting, and they allow for the merging of datasets or causal models with overlapping variables at different resolutions.

Future work might extend embedding from the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction framework to the
\tau
-abstraction (relying on the relations in
23
), and explore algorithms for learning embeddings, thus enabling solving multi-resolution marginal problems and merging datasets.

LATENT STRUCTURE PROJECTIONS

Here we will introduce latent structure projections
[
18
]
, a seminal work in graphical models with applications in graphical abstraction. In the main paper we show that through a combination of abstraction and the extension of latent structure projections to SCMs we obtain causal embeddings.

First we define latent structure models, as graphical causal models over a set of variables, some of which are unobservable:

Definition 18

(Latent Structure Model)
.

Given a set of causal variables
U
, a latent structure
L:=\langle D,O\rangle
is a tuple of a DAG
D
with vertices
U
, and a set
O\subseteq U
of observable variables.

Consider the case where some of the observable variables
O
have become unobservable, one may want to find a new model excluding that variable in the observable set whilst preserving all conditional dependencies implied by the original. Projections allow for reasoning about such cases:

Definition 19

(Latent Structure Projection)
.

A latent structure
L_{[O]}:=\langle D_{[O]},O\rangle
is a projection of another latent structure
L
if, and only if, the following holds:

1.

Every unobservable variable of
D_{[O]}
is a parentless common cause of exactly two non-adjacent observable variables.

2.

For every stable distribution
P
generated by
L
there exists a stable distribution
P^{\prime}
generated by
L_{[O]}
such that the independencies over the variables
O
implied by
P_{[O]}
equal to those implied by
P^{\prime}_{[O]}
.

It has been shown that latent projections are consistent in identifiablity and preserve topological ordering. Informally, given a model
L
and a its projection
L_{[\mathbf{V}^{\prime}]}
over a subset of variables
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
this implies that any causal quantities over
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
representable in
L_{[\mathbf{V}^{\prime}]}
are equally representable in
L
.

there exist a variable in the signature which carries its value (since all intermediate variables take cartesian products of the parents). Thus allowing the specification of the function
f_{V}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
to be equal to that in
\mathcal{M}_{[\mathbf{S}]}
.

Specifically, this construction additionally allows for preservation of mediated confounding, as exogenous parents are also preserved in intermediate variables. Thus it follows that this model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
can represent the exact same causal effects as
\mathcal{M}_{[\mathbf{S}]}
, and generate the same distributions.

Showing that given an SCM
\mathcal{M}
over the endogenous variables
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
, an SCM
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
over the endogenous variables
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
, and
\varphi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}
such that
\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbf{S}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
, we can always construct a specification for
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
such that there exists a functionally
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
∎

PROOF OF THEOREM
4

Theorem 4

(Functional
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent non-surjective abstraction
\not\Rightarrow
embedding)
.

A non-surjective
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction does not necessarily imply an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding.

Proof.

For this proof we will show that one can create a functionally
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent non-surjective abstraction, that is not graphically
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent. Thus by Thm.
2
the proposed abstraction is not an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding.

We will define a
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-abstraction
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be SCMs with the following specification:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle:=\{X,Y,Z\},\text{ with }\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}(X)&=\{0,2,4\}\\
\mathcal{R}(Y)&=\{0,1\}\\
\mathcal{R}(Z)&=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle:=\{U_{X},U_{Y}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}f_{X}(U_{X})&=U_{X}\\
f_{Y}(U_{Y})&=U_{Y}\\
f_{Z}(X,Y)&=X+Y\end{cases}

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}U_{X}&\sim\mathcal{U}\{0,2,4\}\\
U_{Y}&\sim B(0.5)\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle:=\{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\},\text{ with }\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}(X^{\prime})&=\{0,2,4\}\\
\mathcal{R}(Y)&=\{0,1\}\\
\mathcal{R}(Z)&=\{0,1\}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle:=\{U_{X^{\prime}},U_{Y^{\prime}}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}f_{X^{\prime}}(U_{X^{\prime}})&=U_{X^{\prime}}\\
f_{Y^{\prime}}(U_{Y^{\prime}})&=U_{Y^{\prime}}\\
f_{Z^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime})&=Y^{\prime}\end{cases}

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}U_{X^{\prime}}&\sim\mathcal{U}\{0,2,4\}\\
U_{Y^{\prime}}&\sim B(0.5)\end{cases}

Then define
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
by the maps:

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}X&\mapsto X^{\prime}\\
Y&\mapsto Y^{\prime}\\
Z&\mapsto Z^{\prime}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\alpha_{X^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}0&\mapsto 0\\
2&\mapsto 2\\
4&\mapsto 4\end{cases},

\displaystyle\alpha_{Y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}0&\mapsto 0\\
1&\mapsto 1\end{cases},

\displaystyle\alpha_{Z^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}0&\mapsto 0\\
1&\mapsto 1\\
2&\mapsto 0\\
3&\mapsto 1\\
4&\mapsto 0\\
5&\mapsto 1\end{cases}.

One can easily verify
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is functionally
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent. Now we draw the DAGs entailed by
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
:
\mathcal{M}:
X
Y
Z
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:
X^{\prime}
Y^{\prime}
Z^{\prime}

Notice that the edge
X^{\prime}\rightarrow Z^{\prime}
is absent as
X^{\prime}
is not in the signature of the function
f_{Z^{\prime}}
. Thus the algebraic constraint
P(Z^{\prime}|do(X^{\prime}))=P(Z^{\prime}|do(\emptyset))
is in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(G_{[\mathbf{S}]})
. In turn,
P(Z|do(X))=P(Z|do(\emptyset))
is in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(G^{-1}_{[\mathbf{S}]})
. However the graph of
\mathcal{M}
implies the constraint
P(Z|do(X))\neq P(Z|do(\emptyset))
, and thus

\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\left(G_{\left[\mathbf{S}\right]}^{-1}\right)\not\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\left(G_{\left[\mathbf{R}\right]}\right).

Showing a functional
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent non-surjective abstraction does not necessarily imply graphical
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistency, and consequently not an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding.
∎

PROOF OF THEOREM
5

Theorem 5

(Consistent Embeddings as Solution to the Multi-Resolution Causal Marginal Problem)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be a set of
n
SCMs and
\mathbf{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\}
be a set of
n

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}:\mathcal{M}_{j}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
embeds into the same
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
j
.
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a solution
\mathcal{M}^{*}
of the multi-resolution marginal problem if for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathbf{A}
: (i)
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent and (ii) the set of relevant variables contains all variables
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}
.

Proof.

A solution to the multi-resolution causal marginal problem requires

(i)

a mapping
\varphi_{j}:\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}^{*}
from the variables of the low-level models into a shared set of variables
\mathbf{V}^{*}
,

(ii)

a model
\mathcal{M}^{*}
over the set of shared variables
V^{*}
,

(iii)

the model
\mathcal{M}^{*}
to be consistent with all
\mathcal{M}_{j}\in\mathbf{M}
.

Requirement (i) is satisfied, since all model
\mathcal{M}_{j}\in\mathbf{M}
embed into
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and thus have a mapping
\varphi_{j}:\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
making
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
the set of shared variables
\mathbf{V}^{*}
. (ii) is then immediately satisfied as
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is the SCM over
\mathbf{V}^{*}
, thus
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Finally, (iii) is satisfied by all embeddings being consistent.

Therefore,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a solution
\mathcal{M}^{*}
to the multi-resolution marginal problem at the resolution of
\mathbf{V}^{*}
.
∎

PROOF OF LEMMA
6

Lemma 6

(Multi-resolution Marginal Problem Reduction)

Given a multi-resolution marginal problem, the application of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings reduce it to a single-resolution causal marginal problem.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be a set of
n
SCMs and
\mathbf{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\}
be a set of
n

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
embeds into the same
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
i
. For all
\mathcal{M}_{i}\in\mathbf{M}
the application
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(\mathcal{M}_{i})
applies the maps
\varphi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}
and
\alpha_{V^{\prime}}:\mathcal{R}(\varphi^{-1}(V^{\prime}))\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(V^{\prime})
for all
V^{\prime}\in\mathbf{S}
. Since
\mathbf{S}\subseteq\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
is the same for all embeddings
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
, the transformed models
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(\mathcal{M}_{i})
necessarily have equal representations and resolutions for any overlapping variables. Thus reducing to the single-resolution causal marginal problem of finding a model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
consistent with marginal models
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{1}),...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}(\mathcal{M}_{n})
with possible non-empty overlapping variables.
∎

PROOF OF LEMMA
7

Lemma 7

(Identity Embeddings as Solution to the Marginal Problem)

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}\}
be a set of
n
SCMs and
\mathbf{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\}
be a set of
n

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embeddings such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}:\mathcal{M}_{j}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
embeds into the same
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
for all
j
.
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a solution
\mathcal{M}^{*}
of the single-resolution marginal problem if for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathbf{A}
: (i)
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
-consistent, (ii)
\alpha_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}
and
\varphi
are identity maps, and (iii) the set of relevant variables contains all variables
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}
.

Proof.

First note that an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-embedding with
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}
,
\varphi
identity maps does not alter the model if applied to it:
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{M}
. Then as a consequence of Lem.
6
it follows that if all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\textbf{A}
are such identity embeddings the problem is the same as finding a specification
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
consistent with models
\mathcal{M}_{1},...,\mathcal{M}_{n}
as in the single-resolution marginal problem.
∎
