Sampling-based Continuous Optimization with Coupled Variables for RNA Design
Wei Yu Tang, Ning Dai, Tianshuo Zhou, David H. Mathews, Liang Huang

Abstract
The task of RNA design given a target structure aims to find a sequence that can fold into that structure. It is a computationally hard problem where some version(s) have been proven to be NP-hard. As a result, heuristic methods such as local search have been popular for this task, but by only exploring a fixed number of candidates. They can not keep up with the exponential growth of the design space, and often perform poorly on longer and harder-to-design structures. We instead formulate these discrete problems as continuous optimization, which starts with a distribution over all possible candidate sequences, and uses gradient descent to improve the expectation of an objective function. We define novel distributions based on coupled variables to rule out invalid sequences given the target structure and to model the correlation between nucleotides. To make it universally applicable to any objective function, we use sampling to approximate the expected objective function, to estimate the gradient, and to select the final candidate. Compared to the state-of-the-art methods, our work consistently outperforms them in key metrics such as Boltzmann probability, ensemble defect, and energy gap, especially on long and hard-to-design puzzles in the Eterna100 benchmark. Our code is available at: http://github.com/weiyutang1010/ncrna_design .

Its importance is also evidenced by

The problem of

RNA design
aims to find sequences that is capable of folding into a target structure
[
4
,

,

,

,
8
]
.
This process enables the creation of artificial RNA molecules with specific function, such as artificial ribozymes
[

, artificial miRNAs

, artificial RNA aptamers
[

, and artificial riboswitches

.

Computationally, the RNA design problem is extremely challenging
due to its exponentially large search space,
\{\text{\tt A},\text{\tt C},\text{\tt G},\text{\tt U}\}^{n}
, which has the size of
O(4^{n})
.
Indeed, it has been proved NP-hard at least for a simplified energy model
[
15
]
. Therefore probably the most popular approach for this problem uses
heuristic methods such as local search
[
6
,
4
]
,
which starts with a single sequence and tries to optimize an objective function by revising one or a few nucleotides in each step.
However, such methods are only capable of exploring a fixed number of candidate sequences, thus not able to keep up with the exponential growth of the design space.
As a result, they tend to perform poorly on longer and harder-to-design structures.

We instead cast the RNA design problem as
continuous optimization

[
16
,
17
]
.
The basic idea is to start with a distribution over
all possible
candidate sequences and use gradient descent to gradually sharpen the distribution,
with each step changing
all
positions
simultaneously in contrast to
local search methods.
In this work, we first define novel sequence distributions for any given RNA structure using coupled variables for paired and mismatch positions, which not only rules out invalid sequences but also models the positional correlations explicitly.
We then aim to optimize the

expectation
of an
arbitrary
objective function
over these distributions.
However, given the diversity of various objective functions in RNA design (such as Boltzmann probability or ensemble defect), it is often computationally prohibitive to
compute the exact values of
the expected objective function or its gradient
over the whole distribution of exponentially many sequences.
Therefore, in contrast to previous work, we use sampling
to approximate the expected objective function and its gradient.
At the end,
we return the best sample in terms of the objective function among all samples collected, which yields high-quality designs.

When tested on the Eterna100 benchmark,
our work consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art RNA design methods
[
6
,
4
]

in (almost) all metrics
such as Boltzmann probability, ensemble defect, and free energy gap.
The advantage of our work is especially salient
on longer and harder-to-design structures,
demonstrating the advantage of this distributional approach that models the whole design space over local search methods that performs local changes on a single sequence.

1
RNA Design as Discrete Optimization

An RNA sequence
\bm{{x}}
of length
n
is specified as a string of base nucleotides
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n},
where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
(
\mathcal{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{\text{\tt A},\text{\tt C},\text{\tt G},\text{\tt U}\}
is the set of nucleotides) for
i=1,2,...,n
.
A pseudoknot-free secondary structure for sequence
\bm{{x}}
is
a well-balanced dot-bracket string
\bm{{y}}=y_{1}y_{2}\dots y_{n}

where
y_{i}=``\text{\tt.}"
indicates that
x_{i}
is unpaired,
and
y_{i}=``\text{\tt(}"
indicates that
x_{i}
is paired with some downstream
x_{j}
and
y_{i}=``\text{\tt)}"
indicates that
x_{i}
is paired with some upstream
x_{j}
. The set of unpaired indices is denoted
\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})
and the set of paired indices

\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})
. For example,
if
\bm{{x}}=\verb|CCCAAAGGG|
and
\bm{{y}}=\verb|(((...)))|
,
we have
\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})=\{4,5,6\}
and
\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})=\{(1,9),(2,8),(3,7)\}
.
We assume each base-pair is a Watson-Crick-Franklin or wobble
pair, i.e.,
\forall(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}),x_{i}x_{j}\in\mathcal{P}
where

\mathcal{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{\verb|CG|,\verb|GC|,\verb|AU|,\verb|UA|,\verb|GU|,\verb|UG|\}
.

1.1
RNA Folding

The
ensemble
of an RNA sequence
\bm{{x}}
is the set of all possible secondary structures of
\bm{{x}}
, denoted as
\mathcal{Y}(x)
. In thermodynamic RNA folding models,
Gibbs free energy change

\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})
is used to characterize the stability of
\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})
. The lower the free energy
\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})
, the more stable the secondary structure
\bm{{y}}
for
\bm{{x}}
. The structure with the
minimum free energy
is the most stable structure in the ensemble, i.e.,

MFE
structure
,

\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}).

(1)

Note that for most methods for secondary structure prediction, ties for
\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}
are broken arbitrarily when there are multiple lowest free energy structures.
This issue was often neglected in the literature, but it deserves clarification here. To be precise, we define

\text{MFEs}(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{\bm{{y}}\mid\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})=\min_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\prime})\}

(2)

to be the
set of MFE structures
for
\bm{{x}}
. When it is a singleton set,
we say
\bm{{x}}
has a
unique MFE
(uMFE) structure.

The
partition function
sums the contribution of all structures in an ensemble:

Q(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sum_{\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}e^{-\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})/RT},

(3)

where
R
is the molar gas constant and
T
is the absolute temperature. Accordingly, the equilibrium probability of a sequence
\bm{{x}}
folding into a structure
\bm{{y}}
is defined as

p(\bm{{y}}\mid\bm{{x}})=\frac{e^{-\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})/RT}}{Q(\bm{{x}})}.

(4)

Figure 1:
RNA design criteria: (a) MFE vs. (b) Boltzmann probability. In (a), both designs
\bm{{x}}^{+}
and
\bm{{x}}^{-}
are MFE solutions
for the target structure
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
, but
\bm{{x}}^{-}

is not a good design
due to the competing structure
\bm{{y}}^{\prime}

having similar free energy, which results in low
probability of
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
in the ensemble (b).
By contrast,
\bm{{x}}^{+}
is a better design
with sharper energy landscape (less competition),
thus higher probability of
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
(i.e., it is more likely to fold into
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
).

1.2
RNA Design as Inverse Folding

Given a target structure
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
, RNA design aims to find a suitable RNA sequence
\bm{{x}}

that can naturally and easily fold into
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
,
within the design space
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

of all valid sequences for
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
:

\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{N}^{|\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}|}\mid\forall(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}),x_{i}x_{j}\in\mathcal{P}\}

(5)

But there are different ways to quantify how “naturally” or “easily”

\bm{{x}}
folds into
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
, which
we categorize into two broad groups:
(a) MFE-based and (b) ensemble-based criteria.

MFE criteria and uMFE criteria

Sequence
\bm{{x}}
is said to be an
MFE solution
of
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
if

\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
is one of the MFE structures of
\bm{{x}}
:

\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\in\text{MFEs}(\bm{{x}})

(6)

Or equivalently,

\forall\bm{{y}}\neq\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})\leq\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}).
As a stricter criteria,
sequence
\bm{{x}}
is
said to be a
uMFE solution
of
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
if
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
is the unique MFE structure of
\bm{{x}}
,
or equivalently:

\forall\bm{{y}}\neq\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})<\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(7)

To search for an MFE or uMFE solution,
we can start from a random sequence
\bm{{x}}
and gradually
update it to minimize one of the following metrics:

•

structural distance

d(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}))
,
where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a standard distance metric
between
two secondary structures, returning the number of differently folded nucleotides:

\displaystyle d(\bm{{y}},\bm{{y}}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}|\bm{{y}}|-2\cdot|\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})\cap\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\prime})|

(8)

\displaystyle-|\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})\cap\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\prime})|.

•

free energy gap

\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
, which is the difference between
the free energies of
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
and
\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}})
:

\displaystyle\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})-\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}))

(9)

\displaystyle=\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})-\min_{\bm{{y}}}\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})\geq 0

Clearly, when
d(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}))

or
\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
reaches
0
, we have an MFE solution.

Ensemble-based criteria: Boltzmann probability and ensemble defect

However, the above two criteria only consider MFE structures,
and neglect the other competing structures.
Even if
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
is the unique MFE structure, there could still be many highly competitive structures that are very close in energy to
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
;
see design
\bm{{x}}^{-}
in Fig.
1
(a) for an example.
As a result,
the Boltzmann probability
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
could still be arbitrarily small due to competition,
which means
\bm{{x}}
is highly unlikely to fold into
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
in equilibrium (see Fig.
1
(b)).
So a better criteria is to look at the whole Boltzmann ensemble to minimize the competition from alternative structures. We consider two such metrics:

•

conditional (i.e., Boltzmann) probability

p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
. Since each
\bm{{x}}
has exponentially many possible structures in the ensemble, this probability can be arbitrarily small. So for numerical stability, we
minimize
the negative log probability
-\log p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
instead.

•

ensemble defect

ED
(
\bm{{x}}
,
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
),
which is
the expected structural distance between
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
and all structures in the ensemble
[
18
]
.
This metric not only considers competition, but also how (dis)similar the competing structures are from
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
; we want to penalize highly competitive structures that are very different from
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
.
The value of ensemble defect can be normalized to between 0 and 1, known as
normalized ensemble defect
(
NED
):

\displaystyle\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\bm{{x}}|}~\mathbb{E}_{\bm{{y}}\sim p(\cdot\mid\bm{{x}})}~d(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\bm{{y}})

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bm{{x}}|}\sum_{\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}p(\bm{{y}}\mid\bm{{x}})\cdot d(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}},\bm{{y}}).

By plugging in Eq.
8
and some simplifications
[
18
,
19
]
, we get

\displaystyle\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\!=\!1\!-\!\frac{2}{|\bm{{x}}|}\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!\!p_{ij}\!-\!\frac{1}{|\bm{{x}}|}\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!\!q_{j},

(11)

where
p_{i}j
is the base-pairing probability of nucleotides
i
and
j
, while
q_{j}=1-\sum_{i}p_{ij}
is the probability of
j
being unpaired.
\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
can also be further decomposed into the sum of
positional defect
(
\epsilon_{i}
):

\displaystyle\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bm{{x}}|}\sum_{1\leq i\leq|\bm{{x}}|}\epsilon_{i}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(12)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=\!\begin{cases}1\!-\!q_{i}&\text{if }i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}});\\
1\!-\!p_{ij}&\text{if }(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\text{ for some }j>i;\\
1\!-\!p_{ji}&\text{if }(j,i)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\text{ for some }j<i.\end{cases}

(13)

Now we can formulate the RNA design problem as optimizing some
objective function

f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

over the design space
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
:

\bm{{x}}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(14)

where
the objective function can be one of these four:

f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})=\begin{cases}d(\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}),\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})&\text{structural distance}\\
\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})&\text{free energy gap}\\
-\log p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})&\text{conditional probability}\\
\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})&\text{ensemble defect}\end{cases}

(15)

2
RNA Design as Continuous Optimization

However, it is well known that the above discrete optimization formulation is hard to optimize. For any target structure, the RNA design space is exponentially large:

|\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})|=4^{|\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})|}\cdot 6^{|\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})|}

(16)

But most commonly used local search methods
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
considers only one (or a few) candidate sequence in each step and
only modifies one (or a few) nucleotides,
which seems highly inefficient in exploring the exponentially large design space.

Can we instead modify
all
positions of the candidate sequence in each step, or consider
all
candidate sequences simultaneously and promote the better ones?
Here we replace the discrete representation of a single candidate sequence
by a probability distribution
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})
over all possible sequences
\bm{{x}}
in
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
.
Essentially, we
propose the following continuous relaxation of the optimization problem minimizing the following objective function:

\mathcal{J}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)}\left[f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\right]=\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(17)

This new objective function is to find a
distribution
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)
of RNA candidates
whose expectation of the objective function
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
is minimized.
If the probability mass concentrates on only one sequence, then this new relaxed objective degenerates to the original discrete objective.

The way of modeling the probability distribution
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)
over the design space
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
could potentially affect the complexity of the optimization and the convergence of the final solution. We aim to find a method that can represent the design space efficiently while being easy to manage.

2.1
Independent distributions (v0)

The most obvious modeling of
p_{\bm{{y}}}(\bm{{x}})

is to use an independent distribution over
\mathcal{N}=\{\text{\tt A},\text{\tt C},\text{\tt G},\text{\tt U}\}
for each position, so that
the distribution over sequences is simply the product
of individual distributions:

p^{0}_{\bm{{y}}}(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\prod_{i}p_{i}(x_{i})

(18)

This is the same distribution in previous work
[
16
]
.
However, this distribution is simplistic and
overlooks the fact that
each base-pair
(i,j)\in\bm{{y}}

requires

x_{i}x_{j}
to be one of the 6 possible pairs in
\mathcal{P}
, which is impossible with independent variables (
4\times 4=16
choices for
x_{i}x_{j}
).
As a result, the domain of this
p^{0}_{\bm{{y}}}(\cdot)

distribution is
all possible sequences

\mathcal{N}^{|\bm{{y}}|}
(of size
4^{|\bm{{y}}|}
) rather than the set
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}})
of
valid sequences

(of size
4^{|\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})|}\cdot 6^{|\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})|}
). In other words, for any input structure
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}
(except for the trivial case of fully-unpaired), this naive distribution includes (exponentially many) invalid sequences.

2.2
Coupled variables for pairs (v1)

In order to model the dependencies between paired positions, we separate the positions into
two groups: the set of unpaired indices, denoted
\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})
, and the set of paired indices,
\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})
.
We now factorize the joint distribution of the entire sequence as

\displaystyle p^{1}_{\bm{{y}}}(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\prod_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(x_{i})\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(x_{i}x_{j})}}

where
p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\cdot)
is the local distribution (over
\mathcal{N}
) for unpaired position
i
,
and
p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(\cdot)
is the local distribution
(over 6 choices in
\mathcal{P}
) for paired positions
(i,j)
.
This is the first distribution
over the set of valid sequences
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}})
for
a given
\bm{{y}}
.

As an example, consider Fig.
2
(a). Here
\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}=\texttt{(.(...)...)}
, so
\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})=\{2,4,5,6,8,9,10\}
and
\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})=\{(1,11),(3,7)\}
.
The probability distribution of the design space is factorized as:

\displaystyle p^{1}_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})=\;

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{u}}_{2}(x_{2})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{4}(x_{4})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{5}(x_{5})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{6}(x_{6})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{8}(x_{8})

\displaystyle\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{9}(x_{9})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{10}(x_{10})\cdot\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{p^{\mathrm{p}}_{1,11}(x_{1}x_{11})}\cdot\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{p^{\mathrm{p}}_{3,7}(x_{3}x_{7})}

Therefore, for the particular design in
Fig.
2
(a),

\displaystyle p^{1}_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\texttt{CAUAAGACAUG})=\;

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{u}}_{2}(\text{\tt A})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{4}(\text{\tt A})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{5}(\text{\tt A})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{6}(\text{\tt G})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{8}(\text{\tt C})

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{u}}_{9}(\text{\tt A})\cdot p^{\mathrm{u}}_{10}(\text{\tt U})\cdot\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{p^{\mathrm{p}}_{1,11}(\text{\tt C}\text{\tt G})}\cdot\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{p^{\mathrm{p}}_{3,7}(\text{\tt U}\text{\tt A})}

2.3
Coupled variables for terminal mismatches (v2 and v3)

The next two versions (v2 and v3) are refinements
of the above v1.
First, we note that in the standard energy models (Turner rules
[
24
]
), there are terminal mismatches lookup tables. For example, for a hairpin loop defined by the pair
(i,j)
, the first and last nucleotide of the loop,
x_{i+1}
and
x_{j-1}
, are the terminal mismatch, and will be looked up together in the energy table (such as
x_{4}
and
x_{6}
in Fig.
2
(a)). Therefore, it is better to make a coupled variable over
\mathcal{N}^{2}
(
4\times 4=16
choices) for each terminal mismatch position-pair:

\displaystyle p^{2}_{\bm{{y}}}(\bm{{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(x_{i})\cdot\!\!\!\!\!\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(x_{i}x_{j})}\cdot\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(x_{i}x_{j})}

3
Sampling for Objective Evaluation, Gradient Estimation, and Design Space Exploration

Given the complexity and variety of RNA design problem settings, a method that can seamlessly switch between various objective functions
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
is desirable. Even though we factorize
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)
into many tractable local distributions, without making any assumptions or requirements about the structure of
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
, the exact calculation of the expectation
\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)}\left[f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\right]
is generally intractable.
Therefore, we employ gradient descent for optimization and adopt random sampling for estimating the objective and its gradients.

We approximate the expectation by averaging over a set of samples
\mathcal{S}
from the distribution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)}\left[f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\right]\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle\text{where\quad}\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{{x}}^{(l)}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)\}_{l=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}

Theoretically, as
|\mathcal{S}|\rightarrow\infty
, this approximation will converge to the true expectation.

3.2
Sampling for Gradient Estimation

Next, we derive the true gradient of
\mathcal{J}
with respect to
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)
as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)}\left[f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(def. of expectation)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(linearity of
\nabla
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})\frac{\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})}{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}\!\!\!p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

\displaystyle\!\!\!\left(\!(\log f(x))^{\prime}\!=\!\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}\!\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)}\left[\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\right]

(def. of expectation)

We again use sampling to estimate
the above expectation and derive the approximate gradient for step
t
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\mathcal{J}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\{\bm{{x}}^{(l)}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}^{(t)}(\cdot)\}_{l=1}^{N}

(20)

We then use the approximate gradient to update the distribution (see Sec.
4
for details):

\displaystyle p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\text{update}\left(p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}^{(t)},\nabla_{p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}}\mathcal{J}^{(t)}\right)

(21)

3.3
Sampling-based Design Space Exploration

At the end of this continuous optimization,
we still need to return a single sequence from the distribution, i.e., an “integral solution”.
This can be done by “rounding” if the distribution is close to one-hot, or more generally
by taking
the sequence with the highest probability in the final distribution

\displaystyle\bm{{x}}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\bm{{x}}}p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}})

(22)

For example,
for independent distributions (v0), since each position is isolated, we simply take the best nucleotide for each position:

x_{i}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmax}}_{a\in\mathcal{N}}p_{i}(a)
.
But for the coupled variable distribution (v1),
for each unpaired position
i\in\mathit{unpaired}(y^{*})
, we take
x_{i}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmax}}_{a\in\mathcal{N}}p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a)
same as in v0,
and for each paired position-pair
(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(y^{*})
, we take the best pair out of the six pair types:

x_{i}^{\star}x_{j}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmax}}_{ab\in\mathcal{P}}p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(ab).

However, this max-probability sequence
is not necessarily the best sequence in terms of the objective function, since the distribution is often not perfectly aligned with objective function.
Here we use an alternative approach that simply takes the best sample in terms of the objective function out of all samples collected in the optimization process:

\displaystyle\bm{{x}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}^{(t)}}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{{x}}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}_{t}\bm{{x}}^{(t)}

(24)

This method, which we call “sampling-based candidate exploration”,
outperforms the max-probability solution, because the samples offer much more diversity in the exploration of the distribution than a single sequence.

4
Parameterization and Optimization

Now we turn to the question of how to parameterize the factorized distribution
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot)

as
p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot;\bm{\Theta})
.
The first method (Sec.
4.1
) simply uses
\bm{\Theta}
as raw probabilities, but the update of
\bm{\Theta}
needs to result in probabilities, leading to a harder
constrained
optimization problem.
The second method (Sec.
4.2
) models the distribution implicitly by applying softmax on
\bm{\Theta}
, resulting in a simpler
unconstrained
optimization problem.

4.1
Method 1: Direct Parameterization and Constrained Optimization

The obvious way of parameterization is to
use
explicit
probabilities.
For each unpaired position
i
,
we use a non-negative parameter vector

\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt C}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt G}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt U}})
which sums to 1
as the probability distribution over nucleotides:

\forall a\in\mathcal{N},p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a}.

Similarly for each paired position
(i,j)
,
we use a non-negative parameter vector
\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,\text{\tt C}\text{\tt G}},\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,\text{\tt G}\text{\tt C}},\ldots,\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,\text{\tt U}\text{\tt G}})
which sums to 1,
and we have

\forall ab\in\mathcal{P},p^{\mathrm{p}}_{ij}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,ab}.

The cases for mismatches and trimismatches are also similar.
The whole parameter set
\bm{\Theta}
includes all parameter vectors:

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}=

\displaystyle\{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\mid i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\}

(25)

\displaystyle\cup\{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}\mid(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}\mid(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}\mid(i,j,k)\in\mathit{trimismatches}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\}

where each
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
is a distribution, i.e.,

\forall\theta_{a}\in\bm{\theta},\theta_{a}\in[0,1],\text{ and }\textstyle\sum_{\theta_{a}\in\bm{\theta}}\theta_{a}=1.

For example, for the structure
y^{*}
in Fig.
2
(a), we have its parameters as

\bm{\Theta}=\{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{5},\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{9},\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{1,11},\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{3,7},\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{4,6},\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{2,8,10}\}.

Now we can parameterize the whole distribution as

\displaystyle p^{3}_{\bm{{y}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})=

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(x_{i}x_{j};{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}}})

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(x_{i}x_{j};{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}}})

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{(i,j,k)\in\mathit{trimismatches}(\bm{{y}})}p^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}(x_{i}x_{j}x_{k};{\color[rgb]{0.71,0.4,0.11}{\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,x_{i}}\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}}\cdot

\displaystyle\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-14.22636pt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}}\cdot\hskip-8.5359pt\prod_{(i,j,k)\in\mathit{trimismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-19.91684pt{\color[rgb]{0.71,0.4,0.11}{\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}}}

Now we adopt a parameterized version for our objective function:

\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})=\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\cdot;\bm{\Theta})}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

The optimization problem can then be formulated as a
constrained optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Theta}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})

(26)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\text{each $\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}$ is a distribution.}

To solve this constrained optimization problem, we use the Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) method
[
25
]
.
At each step
t
,
we first perform a gradient descent (with learning rate
\alpha
):

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\leftarrow\bm{\Theta}-\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})

(27)

where the gradient components are computed individually for each parameter vector:

\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})=\{\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\mid\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}

For example, for an unpaired position
i
, we have:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}}=(\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}},\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt C}}},\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt G}}},\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt U}}})

The first component can be estimated using Eq.
19
as follows:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

(28)

Expanding the term
\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})
, the gradient can be simplified (details provided in Sec.
S1.A
) as:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}=A]\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=A\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

(29)

After the gradient update (Eq.
27
), we then project
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
back onto the set of valid distributions.
For each
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
in
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
,
we project it back
to the probability simplex by finding the vector in the simplex that is closest (in
\ell_{2}
norm) to
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}^{\prime}\leftarrow

\displaystyle\{\mathit{proj}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\mid\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\in\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\}

\displaystyle\mathit{proj}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}}\quad\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\textrm{s.t.}\quad\text{$\bm{\theta}$ is a distribution}.

4.2
Method 2: Softmax Parameterization and Unconstrained Optimization

An alternative approach to the optimization problem is to introduce a parametrization that naturally enforces the required normalization for a valid distribution, thus converting the problem into an unconstrained optimization problem. This approach eliminates the need for performing gradient projection at each step. A common choice for achieving this normalization is the
softmax
function, which inherently converts a set of real numbers into a valid probability distribution.

Instead of using a parameter vector as a distribution explicitly, now we model a distribution implicitly using softmax and our new parameter vector

\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt C}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt G}},\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt U}})
no longer sums to 1; instead we have:

\forall a\in\mathcal{N},p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a^{\prime}})}

(30)

where
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a}
can be any real number. The softmax function ensures that each
p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})
forms a valid distribution without explicitly imposing this as a constraint. This definition can be extended for other parameter vectors
\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}
,
\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}
, and
\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall ab\in\mathcal{P},p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,ab})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}}\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\forall ab\in\mathcal{N}^{2},p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,ab})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{2}}\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle\forall abc\in\mathcal{N}^{3},p^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}(abc;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,abc})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{3}}\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}})}

With this new parametrization, the optimization problem becomes an unconstrained problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Theta}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})

(31)

Since the constraints have been naturally embedded into the problem formulation through the softmax function, we can directly apply the vanilla gradient descent algorithm to solve this optimization problem. The gradient can be updated by:

\bm{\Theta}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{\Theta}-\alpha\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})

where
\alpha
is the learning rate.

Due to the softmax parametrization, the specific form of the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})
differs from that in the constrained optimization problem. Using the chain rule, we express it as:

\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{N}}\underbrace{\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}}_{\text{same as Eq.~\ref{eq:grad-partial}}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}}_{\text{softmax; Eq.~\ref{eq:softmax-grad}}}

(32)

where the first partial derivative on the right hand side
is identical to the case of direct parameterization above (Sec.
4.1
; Eq.
29
),
but the second partial derivative, which used to be 1 in direct parameterization, is now the
gradient of the softmax function
(see Fig.
4

and Sec.
S1.B

for details):

\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}=p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[a=\text{\tt A}]-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))

(33)

So the gradient for the softmax parameterization is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

\displaystyle\approx\!\sum_{a\in\mathcal{N}}\!\!\left(\!\!\!\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=a\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a}}\!\!\right)\!\!\cdot\!\left[p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\!\cdot\!(\mathbbm{1}[a=\text{\tt A}]-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))\right]

We run the gradient decent step until the changes in the value of the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})
become sufficiently small (see Sec.
6
), indicating that the solution has converged. Algorithm
1
outlines the procedure of both constraint and unconstraint optimization approach.

Related Work

Although Matthies et al.
[
16
]
also used continuous optimization for RNA design, our approach is vastly different from and substantially outperforms theirs
in both scalability and design quality (by all metrics).

•

First, their sequence distribution is a simple product of independent distributions for each position
(same as our distribution v0 in Sec.
2.1
)
which is ill-suited for the RNA design problem
for two reasons:
(a) that distribution
includes exponentially many
illegal sequences for any input structure
due to pair violations and
(b) that distribution does not explicitly model the covariance between paired positions.
Instead,
we use coupled variables for paired and mismatch positions (our distributions v1, v2, and v3 in Secs.
2.2
–
2.3
), which rules out invalid sequences and
explicitly models the dependencies between
correlated positions.

•

Second, our sampling framework can work with arbitrary objective functions while their work is specifically designed for one such function, the Boltzmann probability.

•

Third, our unbiased sampling yields an
unbiased
approximation to the expectation of an arbitrary objective function over the distribution of sequences.
For example, for the case of Boltzmann probability,
our sampling results in an unbiased approximation
of the expected Boltzmann probability,
which converges to the true expectation as the sample size increases:

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}{\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})}\approx\E_{\bm{{x}}}[p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})]

(34)

By contrast, they optimize a different objective (in red below) that
deviates from the true expectation
of Boltzmann probability with a bias (
\E[X/Y]\neq\E[X]/\E[Y]
):

{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\E_{\bm{{x}}}[e^{-\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})/RT}]}{\E_{\bm{{x}}}[Q(\bm{{x}})]}\!\neq}\E_{\bm{{x}}}\!\!{\Big[\frac{e^{-\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})/RT}}{Q(\bm{{x}})}\Big]}\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\E_{\bm{{x}}}[p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})]}

(35)

•

Fourth, our sampling-based approach is much more efficient: it scales to the longest structures in the Eterna100 benchmark (400
nt
) while their work only scaled to structures up to 50
nt
long.

•

Last, our results substantially outperform theirs in all metrics (see Table
S1
).

Sampling-based Continuous Optimization (This Work)

By default, our method samples 2500 sequences at each step. The number of steps is adaptive to each puzzle, specifically the run stops after 50 steps in which the objective function does not improved and the total number of steps is limited to 2000. Our main program is implemented in C++ and utilizes OpenMP for parallelization.

The default initial learning rate is set to 0.01, which works well for all puzzles under the softmax parameterization. However, we observe that the direct parameterization (
projection
) requires smaller learning rates as puzzle lengths increases; otherwise, the objective value does not improve. Therefore, we apply adaptive learning rate decay for the projection method. Additionally, we implement momentum-based optimizers: Adam
[
29
]
for softmax and Nesterov accelerate gradient
[
30
]
for projection.

We adopt three types of initialization:

•

Uniform
: Each parameter is set to uniform distribution.

•

Targeted
: Assigns 100% A for unpaired, 50% CG and GC for base pairs, and uniform distribution for mismatches and trimismatches.

•

\epsilon
-
Targeted
: A combination of targeted and uniform distribution defined by
\epsilon\cdot\text{targeted}+(1-\epsilon)\cdot\text{uniform}
.

For the projection method, we use both uniform and targeted initializations. For the softmax method, we use uniform and
\epsilon
-targeted initializations with
\epsilon=0.75
. The final solutions are selected from the best out of both initializations.

We use our previous work, LinearPartition
[
19
]
, to compute the partition function and base-pairing probability in linear time with beam pruning. We use beam size
b=250
for optimizing
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
, and
b=100
for optimizing
\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
. It is only when optimizing for
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
, we use a larger beam size because LinearPartition tends to underapproximate the partition function, resulting in
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})>1
. For optimizing
d(\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}),\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
and
\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
, we fold the sequences using LinearFold
[
31
]
with
b=100
.

At each step, we record the best sample among the 2,500 samples. After completing all steps, we reevaluate the recorded samples using ViennaRNA 2.0
[
32
]
, selecting the best sequence for each metric. The values reported in this paper are thus, unaffected by the approximation error from beam search.

Baseline 1: SAMFEO

SAMFEO is an iterative approach that selects a few nucleotides to mutate by (a) sampling positions based on positional defects and (b) utilizing structural information
[
4
]
. Similar to our work, SAMFEO is a general approach that can work with any objective function
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
. In their paper, they optimize for two ensemble objectives:
1-p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
and
\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
. SAMFEO is run five times on each puzzle under their default settings with 5000 steps, and we report the best solution obtained from these five runs.

Baseline 2: NEMO

NEMO uses Nested Monte Carlo Search with domain-specific knowledge to solve puzzles
[
6
]
. It maximizes a scoring function defined by:

\text{score}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})=K(1+\Delta\Delta\>\!\!G^{\scriptsize\circ\!}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}))^{-\mathrm{sign}(K)}

(36)

where

K=1-\frac{\text{BPD}(\text{MFE}(\bm{{x}}),\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{2\lvert\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})\rvert}

and
\text{BPD}(\bm{{y}},\bm{{y}}^{\prime})
is the base pairing distances,

\text{BPD}(\bm{{y}},\bm{{y}}^{\prime})=\lvert\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})\cup\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\prime})\rvert-\lvert\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})\cap\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}}^{\prime})\rvert.

We ran NEMO five times with the parameters of ViennaRNA 2.5.1 and take the best solutions out of the five runs.

Time Analysis

Figure
S7
(b) shows the total number of steps to solve each puzzle based on the stopping criteria: 50 steps since the the objective function improved and a maximum of 2000 steps. Generally, as the puzzle length increases, it takes more steps to find the best solution. However, for puzzles between 100
nt
to 192
nt
, most puzzles terminated earlier than others, suggesting that these puzzles are easier to design.

Figure
S7
(c) shows the time taken to solve each puzzle when ran on a server with 28 physical cores. The whole evaluation on the Eterna100 dataset takes about 10 days, with the longest puzzle taking up to 20 hours.

The primary time bottleneck in our method is computing the objective function
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
for all 2500 samples. For example, computing
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
takes up to more than 99% of total time due to cubic time complexity. However, this issue is mitigated to some extent by using beam search from LinearPartition. Additionally, computing the objective function for each sample is parallelizable. With 28 physical cores, we observed a
27.4\times
speedup for the longest puzzles with 400 nucleotides, reducing the time from 1233 seconds to 45 seconds per step.

Other speedup options include using a smaller sample size, but it tends to result in longer convergence times and greater difficulty in finding a better solution. Speed improvements can also be achieved by caching the
f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
of each sample, which avoids redundant computations for repeated samples. This approach is effective when the puzzle is short and targeted initialization is used, which tends to produce more repeated samples. For example, puzzle
#36
with 151 nucleotides obtained a 1.95
\times
speedup from caching, reducing the time from 4.2 seconds to 2.15 seconds per step.

Learning Curves

Figure

presents the learning curves for puzzles

and

. At each step, the plots illustrate
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
for the best sample and the integral solution, along with the arithmetic and geometric means of
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
computed from 2500 samples. Overall, the learning curves show that the values for the best sample, as well as the arithmetic and geometric means of
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})

than the integral solution. In our findings, the highest quality solutions are consistently derived from the best sample rather than the integral solution.

Ablation Studies

We run two additional experiments with targeted initializations to assess the effectiveness of coupling variables on mismatches and trimismatches. First, we ablated the coupling variables for trimismatches. Then, we ablated the coupling variables for both mismatches and trimismatches. The results of these ablation studies are presented in Table
S3
.

As more coupling variables are removed, the results deteriorate across all metrics. The arithmetic mean of
p(\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}\mid\bm{{x}})
decreased from
0.589
to
0.578
after ablating trimismatches and further declines to
0.566
when mismatches are also ablated. Similarly, the average
\text{NED}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})
increases from 0.035 to 0.039 and then to 0.042. This pattern continues across other metrics, suggesting that the coupled variables approach is useful.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

We described a general framework for sampling-based continuous optimization with coupled-variable distributions that is applicable to optimizing any objective function for RNA design. Our method consistently outperformed other state-of-the-art methods across nearly all metrics, such as Boltzmann probability and ensemble defect, particularly on the long and hard-to-design puzzles in the Eterna100 benchmark.

In the future, our work can be improved in ways such as:

1.

Our method evaluates the objective for each sample, which takes up the majority of runtime. Currently we cache full sequences (unique samples),
but to speed up even further, we can cache subsequences shared among the samples. We can also use importance sampling
[
33
]
to reduce the number of samples in each step.

2.

Our sequence distributions are products of independent distributions of coupled variables, which are both expressive and easy to sample from. But we can also consider more complex distributions using discriminative models
[
34
]

and neural models
[
35
]
, although sampling would be more difficult.

3.

We can extend this framework to protein design which should in principle work for arbitrary objective functions. The challenge is scalability due to much slower objective evaluation using protein folding engines
[
36
,
37
]
.

S1.A
Direct Parameterization

In the example of direct parameterization (Sec.
4.1
), our objective is to compute the gradient of the objective function with respect to the parameter associated with an unpaired position
i
and nucleotide
A
. As noted in Eq.
28
, this gradient can be approximated as follows:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})

Here,
\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\prod_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,x_{i}}\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}}\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-14.22636pt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}}\cdot\hskip-8.5359pt\prod_{(i,j,k)\in\mathit{trimismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-19.91684pt{\color[rgb]{0.71,0.4,0.11}{\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathit{unpaired}(\bm{{y}})}\log(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,x_{i}})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathit{pairs}(\bm{{y}})}\log({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathit{mismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-14.22636pt\log({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,x_{i}x_{j}}}})+\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\mathit{trimismatches}(\bm{{y}})}\hskip-19.91684pt\log({\color[rgb]{0.71,0.4,0.11}{\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}}})

Since we are interested in the gradient with respect to a particular position
i
and nucleotide
A
, the other terms are constants. This simplifies the equation to:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log p_{\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}}}(\bm{{x}};\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}(\log\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,x_{i}})=\frac{1}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,x_{i}}}\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}=\text{\tt A}]

Finally, the gradient approximation can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbbm{1}[x_{i}=A]\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=A\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

We can also extend the gradient to other cases, including pairs, mismatches, and trimismatches. The gradients are listed below:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,ab}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=a\\
x_{j}=b\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j,ab}},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,ab}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=a\\
x_{j}=b\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}^{\!\!\;\text{$\star$}})}{\theta^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j,ab}},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}(\bm{\Theta})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k,abc}}\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{S}\\
x_{i}=a\\

S1.B
Softmax Parameterization

In Section
4.2
, we introduce the softmax parameterization as an alternative approach to solving the optimization problem. The probability of a nucleotide
a\in\mathcal{N}
at an unpaired position
i
is defined in Eq.
30
as:

p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a})}{Z},\quad\text{where }Z=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a^{\prime}})

To compute the gradient of the objective function under the softmax parameterization (Eq.
32
), we need to derive the derivative of the softmax function. Suppose that we are interested in the derivative of
p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})
with respect to the parameter
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}
. For the case where
a=\text{\tt A}
, applying the quotient rule yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})S-\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})}{Z^{2}}=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})}{Z}\cdot\frac{Z-\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})}{Z}=p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\cdot(1-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))

Similarly, for the case where
a\neq\text{\tt A}
, the derivative is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{0-\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a})}{Z^{2}}=-\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}})}{Z}\cdot\frac{\exp(\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,a})}{Z}=-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})=p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})(0-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))

Combining these results, we can express the derivative of the softmax function with respect to
\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}
as:

\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,\text{\tt A}}}=p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(a;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i})\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[a=\text{\tt A}]-p^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\text{\tt A};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))

For other cases, such as pairs, mismatches, and trimismatches, the derivatives of the softmax function are provided below:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,j,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}}=p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j})\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[ab=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}]-p^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}(a^{\prime}b^{\prime};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j})),\qquad\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,j,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}}=p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(ab;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j})\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[ab=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}]-p^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j}(a^{\prime}b^{\prime};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{m}}_{i,j})),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}(abc;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k})}{\partial\theta^{\mathrm{u}}_{i,j,k,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}}=p^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}(abc;\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k})\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[abc=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}]-p^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}(a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime};\bm{\theta}^{\mathrm{tm}}_{i,j,k}))
