High-frequency ultrasound examination of acute ankle joint ligament injury and concomitant lesions
Authors: Qirui Yu, Wenxian Yang, Dan Li, Yuhao Wang, Xueting Tang, Yunyun Shan, Weiyong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimTo determine the diagnostic value of high-frequency ultrasonography in the assessment of anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injury and concomitant lesions.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the data of 1005 patients with acute, unilateral ATFL injuries diagnosed using high-frequency ultrasonography in our hospital between January 2021 and December 2024. We analyzed ATFL and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) thickness in patients with right vs. left ankle injuries and those with complete vs. incomplete ATFL rupture. We also analyzed the damage to other supporting structures of the ankle to provide an imaging basis for clinical diagnosis and treatment.ResultsIn patients with incomplete ATFL injury with concomitant CFL injury, the thickness of the left and right CFL was 1.75 ± 0.47 mm and 1.90 ± 0.58 mm, respectively (P < 0.01). The CFL thickness was 1.83 ± 0.54 mm and 2.13 ± 0.54 mm in patients with incomplete and complete ATFL injury, respectively (P < 0.001). The incidence of concomitant CFL and superior extensor retinaculum (SER) injuries significantly differed between patients with incomplete and complete ATFL rupture (P < 0.05).ConclusionHigh-frequency ultrasonography is valuable for diagnosing acute ankle ligament injuries. CFL thickness was greater in patients with complete ATFL injury than in patients with incomplete ATFL injury. A trend toward increase CFL thickness on the right was noted, possibly due to right-sided dominance, though this requires further validation. ATFL injuries are frequently associated with concomitant CFL and SER injuries, and these structures should be carefully assessed during ultrasound examinations of acute ankle injuries.

Background

Ankle sprains are the most frequent musculoskeletal injuries in the general population as well as in athletes [1]. In the ankle joint, the lateral ligament complex is relatively weaker in biomechanical strength than the medial deltoid ligament (DL), and its 3 main components are anatomically distributed as independent bundles, rather than existing as a continuous structure [2]. The complex of ligaments on the lateral aspect of the ankle joint includes the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL), and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). Among these, the fragile ATFL is prone to sprain or tear in the setting of ankle joint varus or metatarsal flexion [3]. Indeed, the ATFL is the most frequently damaged ligament in lateral ankle sprains, and accounts for 80% of all ankle joint injuries; additionally, combined ATFL and CFL injuries account for approximately 20% of all ankle joint injuries [4]. The ATFL connects the anterior border of the lateral malleolus of the fibula to the neck of the talus. Its flat, band-like structure is essential for ankle joint stability [5]. Without effective treatment and rehabilitation, ankle ligament injuries can potentially progress to chronic instability and osteoarthritis of the ankle joint [6].

Currently, acute injuries of the ankle and foot are increasingly being assessed using high-frequency ultrasonography, which provides high-resolution images that clearly depict soft-tissue structures [3]. Studies have demonstrated the high sensitivity and specificity of high-frequency ultrasonography in the assessment of ATFL injuries [7]. However, the concomitant damage to the surrounding ligaments and support structures in cases of ATFL injuries has not been widely reported.

Therefore, this retrospective study was designed to analyze the impact of ATFL injuries on the peripheral ligaments and support structures of the ankle joint. Specifically, we determined the changes in the thickness of both the ATFL and CFL in patients with varying degrees of ATFL injuries, and observed the condition of the surrounding ligaments and support structures to assess their involvement. We aim to provide a comprehensive imaging analysis of ATFL injuries in order to improve their diagnosis and treatment outcomes.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively enrolled subjects who were diagnosed with ATFL injuries by means of ultrasonography in our hospital between January 2021 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were acute and unilateral ankle joint sprain resulting in an ATFL injury. The diagnosis of ATFL injury was based on high-frequency ultrasonography. Patients with chronic ankle injuries, those with a history of ankle surgery, and those with bilateral injuries were excluded from this study. The medical history and ultrasound data of the patients, including age, gender, injured side, and ligament thickness, were obtained and analyzed. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (Anhui Provincial Hospital; approval no.: 2025-RE-053). Due to the retrospective study paradigm the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Ultrasound imaging

Ultrasound examinations were performed using a Mindray Resona R9S color ultrasound diagnostic machine, equipped with an L20-5U linear array probe operating at a frequency of 5 to 20 MHz. The musculoskeletal mode was selected for the examination. All ultrasound evaluations were performed by a single sonographer with 10 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasonography.

The examination was performed with the patients in a seated position, and the knee flexed at a 90° angle and slightly medially rotated. The sole was placed flat on the examination bed, creating an approximately 120° angle between the foot and the bed, ensuring that the ATFL was stretched. The transducer should be placed directly on the lateral aspect of the ankle after the application of a coupling gel. The probe should be swept from the proximal to distal direction, while maintaining a sweep angle nearly parallel to the examination bed to locate the long axis of the ATFL.

We measured the thickness of the proximal and distal ends of the ATFL on the fibular and talar sides of the ligament, respectively, and calculated the average value of ATFL. In cases where the ligament was fully ruptured in the midsection, the thickness of the fibular and talar remnants was measured and averaged. If the rupture occurred near the talar or fibular attachment, the thickest portion of the remaining ligament was measured and recorded.

The probe was then rotated by 90°, and positioned between the fibula and the anterior process of the calcaneus, perpendicular to the examination bed. The thickness of the CFL was measured at the ligament’s midpoint, corresponding to the central region between the calcaneus and the fibula, and calculated the average value of CFL. The measurements were recorded immediately during the ultrasound examination using the built-in data-logging function of the ultrasound device. All ligament thickness measurements were performed directly using the built-in electronic calipers of the Mindray ultrasound device. Subsequently, the probe was adjusted to examine the peripheral ligaments, such as the DL, anterior-inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL), and superior extensor retinaculum (SER) on the lateral and medial aspects of the foot, for the assessment of additional injuries.

Standard of judgment

ATFL injuries can be classified into 3 subtypes based on their ultrasound imaging features: [8] type I, ligament thickening, hypoechogenicity, and loss of fibrillar pattern; type II, swollen ligament with local thinning or interruption of continuity, and decreased ligament tension on dynamic scanning; and type III (complete rupture), complete interruption of ligament continuity, with the severed ends showing a contracture-like appearance, and disappearance of ligament tension on dynamic scanning. In this study, type I and type II ATFL injuries were collectively termed incomplete injuries, while type III ATFL injuries were referred to as complete ruptures [9, 10].

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 29.0) was used to perform all statistical analyses in this study. Normally distributed measurement data were presented as mean ± SD. Ligament thickness was compared between patients with left and right ankle injuries, and between patients with incomplete injuries and complete ligament rupture by using the independent-samples t-test. Count data were reported as numbers and percentages. The rates of concomitant peripheral ligament injuries were compared between patients with incomplete injuries and complete rupture of the ATFL by using the χ² test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

General information

We enrolled a total of 1005 subjects consisting of 500 male patients and 505 female patients, with a mean age of 34.3 ± 14.78 years (range, 4–83 years). Among them, 528 patients had right ankle injuries, while the remaining patients had left ankle injuries. All patients had unilateral lesions. Among the 1005 patients with ATFL injuries, 171 (17.0%), 739 (73.5%), and 95 (9.5%) patients had type I, type II, and type III injuries, respectively, and 24 of the 95 type III ATFL injuries were surgically confirmed. The ATFL injury was accompanied by a CFL injury in 632 (62.8%) patients, a talonavicular ligament (TNL) injury in 272 (27.0%) patients, a DL injury in 246 (24.4%) patients, a SER injury in 133 (13.2%) patients, and an AITFL injury in 91 (9.0%) patients (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Comparison of ATFL and CFL thickness between right and left ankle injuries

Among patients with incomplete injuries of the ATFL, the thickness of the ATFL did not significantly differ between those with left ankle injuries and those with right ankle injuries (P = 0.211); however, the thickness of the CFL, when an accompanying CFL injury was present, was significantly lesser on the left than on the right (P < 0.001). Among patients with complete rupture of the ATFL, the thickness of neither the ATFL (P = 0.158) nor the CFL (P = 0.133) significantly differed between left-sided and right-sided injuries (Table 2).

Comparison of ligament thickness between patients with incomplete and complete ATFL injuries

The thickness of the ATFL was 2.48 ± 0.59 mm in patients with incomplete injury and 2.54 ± 0.74 mm in patients with complete ATFL rupture; the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.416). Among patients with concomitant CFL injury, the thickness of the CFL was significantly lesser among those with incomplete ATFL injury than among those with complete ATFL rupture (1.83 ± 0.54 mm vs. 2.13 ± 0.54 mm, P < 0.001).

Incidence of concomitant injuries of peripheral ligaments and supporting structures

The incidence of concomitant CFL and SER injuries significantly differed between patients with incomplete and complete rupture of the ATFL (P < 0.01, Table 3), whereas the incidence of concomitant injuries of the TNL, AITFL, and DL did not significantly differ between patients with incomplete and complete ATFL injuries (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The key findings of this study are as follows: (1) in patients with incomplete ATFL injury and concurrent CFL damage, the left CFL was significantly thinner than the right CFL, and (2) CFL thickness was notably greater in patients with complete ATFL injury than in those with incomplete ATFL injury. These results suggest potential links between the laterality and severity of ATFL injury, and structural CFL changes. ATFL injuries were more likely to be accompanied by injuries of the CFL and SER than by injuries of the DL, TNL, and AITFL.

Imaging is commonly used to diagnose joint and ligament injuries. While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior tissue resolution, it is time-consuming and expensive. Unlike MRI, high-frequency ultrasonography is mainly indicated for soft-tissue assessment, less expensive, widely available, portable, and allows for high spatial resolution, dynamic evaluation, and easy comparison with the contralateral side. Additionally, ultrasound findings can be directly correlated with the painful area. Notably, MRI typically visualizes the ATFL in only 1 or 2 slices, and small lesions are difficult to detect using MRI [11]. In contrast, ultrasonography can clearly show the internal structure of the ligament and accurately assess the degree of injury.

Studies have shown that ultrasonography has high sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing ATFL injuries; additionally, it allows for the visualization of other ligaments and supporting structures [1, 7, 12]. Retrospective analyses indicate that the accuracy of ultrasonography in diagnosing ankle ligament injuries is approximately 96%, with 93% accuracy for acute ATFL injuries and 97% accuracy for chronic ATFL injuries [10]. Previous reviews have demonstrated that ultrasound (US) can offer higher sensitivity than magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [7, 13]. While the mechanisms and treatment options for acute ATFL injuries have been extensively researched, reports on the associated concomitant lesions remain limited.

In our study, the prevalence of ATFL injuries on the left and right sides was 47.5% and 52.5%, respectively, which is in line with the findings of Li et al. [14]. Ankle ligament laxity is a risk factor for ankle joint injuries, and it is more commonly observed in female subjects. As a result, female athletes are generally more prone to ankle ligament injuries than their male counterparts [15, 16]. In our study, however, the incidence of ankle ligament injuries was similar between the genders. This could be because our study only included patients with acute injuries, excluding those with chronic ankle conditions. Ankle injuries are most frequent among young individuals, and the 1005 patients in our study had a mean age of 34.3 ± 14.8 years, which is consistent with previous research [17].

The thickness of the injured ATFL and CFL has been demonstrated to increase as compared to healthy ligaments [18]. The ATFL connects the talus to the fibula, forming an inverted triangle, with its midsection suspended. This anatomical configuration makes the ATFL particularly susceptible to swelling after injury, which contributes to the increased thickness observed in damaged ligaments. Both the ATFL and CFL originate from the lateral malleolus of the distal fibula, with the CFL positioned below the ATFL [19]. The peroneal tendon sheath covers the CFL, which makes it less likely to swell and thicken after injury [20]. In this study, there was no significant change in ATFL thickness following injury. However, a significant difference in CFL thickness was observed between the left and right sides in patients with incomplete ATFL tears. A significant difference in CFL thickness was also noted between patients with incomplete and complete ATFL ruptures. When an ATFL injury occurs, ankle joint instability increases. In cases of incomplete rupture, the injured but not ruptured ligament will still be exposed to normal joint loading. One potential explanation for the observed right-left difference in CFL thickness could be right-sided dominance, a phenomenon where dominant limbs may exhibit adaptive structural changes in stabilizing ligaments. However, this remains a speculative hypothesis. As our study did not directly test limb dominance nor its mechanistic relationship with ligament thickness, alternative explanations cannot be excluded.

A previous study has reported that 11.4% of ATFL injuries are accompanied by a CFL injury, which is often missed clinically [21]. Therefore, when an ATFL injury is detected, clinicians should carefully assess the patient for a concomitant CFL injury. In this study, the incidence of CFL injury was 60.3% (549 of 910) among patients with incomplete ATFL rupture and 87.4% (83 of 95) among patients with complete ATFL rupture; these results are consistent with previously published findings [22]. Studies have suggested that the SER is connected to the fibular end of the ATFL in 58.7% of the normal population [23]. Thus, it is believed that ATFL injuries can lead to SER injuries. Damaged ligaments typically become edematous, resulting in increased ligament thickness. Therefore, when ATFL injuries are detected using ultrasonography, it is important to carefully examine the CFL and SER to fully assess the lateral collateral ligament damage and associated ligamentous injuries, to avoid any missed diagnoses.

The DL is a fibrous complex that stabilizes the medial ankle and helps maintain its normal anatomical position [24, 25]. Although this structure is strong and complex, its injuries are often underdiagnosed after ATFL injury. DL injuries are relatively uncommon [24]. However, in this study, the prevalence of ATFL injuries accompanied by DL injury was approximately 24.5%, potentially due to the fact that ankle inversion injuries rarely damage the DL, whereas more complex injuries involving external rotation can affect both the lateral collateral ligament and DL simultaneously [24].

It has been suggested that ultrasonography may overdiagnose type I ATFL injuries and underdiagnose type II ATFL injuries [8]. Additionally, the quality of ultrasound images and accuracy of diagnosis can vary, and greatly rely on operator experience and skill [13]. Therefore, specialized training in musculoskeletal ultrasonography is essential for sonographers to minimize missed diagnoses. All ultrasound evaluations were performed by a single sonographer with 10 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasonography, a choice intended to minimize intra-operator variability in landmark identification and injury categorization. However, this approach limits the generalizability of our ultrasound protocol, as it does not account for variability in technique or interpretation across operators with different levels of experience.

There are some limitations to this study. First, we did not compare the injured and uninjured sides in patients with ATFL injury. Second, we did not include MRI correlation, despite MRI being the accepted reference standard. In future studies, universal MRI correlation for all cases would validate ultrasonography as a surrogate for the gold standard. Third, ultrasonography was performed by a single experienced sonographer in our study. While this minimized intra-operator bias and improved the reliability of our measurements, it also means that the reproducibility of our methodology is untested, which may introduce potential measurement bias. Future prospective studies should address operator dependence and report both intra- and inter-observer reliability across operators with varying experience levels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, complete ATFL rupture can cause significant thickening of the CFL. Additionally, a trend toward increased CFL thickness on the right side was noted, which may be related to right-sided dominance, though this association requires further validation. ATFL injuries are more likely to be accompanied by CFL and SER injuries than by injuries of other ankle ligaments. Therefore, ultrasonography should be used to assess the severity of ankle ligament injuries and detect damage to the surrounding ligaments, in order to minimize missed diagnoses and support clinicians in treatment planning. However, the lack of gold standard confirmation and the limited generalizability from single-operator ultrasound evaluations are significant drawbacks. Future research needs to develop a more rigorous gold standard assessment system and multi-operator collaborative ultrasound evaluation models.
