The multinomial dimer model
Richard Kenyon and Catherine Wolfram

Abstract
. An N N -dimer cover of a graph is a collection of edges (with multiplicity) such that each vertex is contained in exactly N N edges in the collection. The multinomial dimer model is a natural probability measure on N N -dimer covers. We study the behavior of these measures on periodic bipartite graphs in ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} , in the scaling limit as the multiplicity N N and then the size of the graph go to infinity. In this iterated limit, we prove a large deviation principle, where the rate function is the integral of an explicit surface tension, and show that random configurations concentrate on a limit shape which is the unique solution to an associated Euler-Lagrange equation. We further show that the associated critical gauge functions, which exist in the N → ∞ N\to\infty limit on each finite graph, converge in the scaling limit to a limiting gauge function which solves a dual Euler-Lagrange equation. We use our techniques to compute explicit limit shapes in some two and three dimensional examples, such as the Aztec diamond and “Aztec cuboid”. These 3 ​ d 3d examples are the first stat mech models in dimensions d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 where limit shapes can be computed explicitly.

1.1.
Solvability of the model and broader context

Figure 2.
The surface tension functions for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

In general, statistical mechanics models in dimensions
d\geq 3
are much less well-understood than their two-dimensional counterparts. Among other things, the higher dimensional analogs of classical statistical mechanics models are rarely exactly solvable in any sense,
and it is unknown how to compute anything explicitly.

One of the reasons that the two-dimensional dimer model is so tractable is that it is exactly solvable in a strong sense. Determinantal formulas for dimer partition functions on planar graphs discovered in the 1960s by Kasteleyn
[
Kas61
]
and Temperley Fisher
[
TF61
]
can be leveraged to compute many key statistical quantities explicitly, including the surface tension
[
CKP01
]
. The surface tension for dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(s_{1},s_{2})=-\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg(L(\pi p_{1})+L(\pi p_{2})+L(\pi p_{3})+L(\pi p_{4})\bigg)

where
L(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}\log|2\sin t|\,\mathrm{d}t
is the
Lobachevsky function
, and
p,s
are related by:
2(p_{1}-p_{3})=s_{1},2(p_{2}-p_{4})=s_{2}
,
p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}+p_{4}=1
, and
\sin(\pi p_{1})\sin(\pi p_{3})=\sin(\pi p_{2})\sin(\pi p_{4})
. See Figure
2
.

The determinant formulas for dimers in two dimensions do not extend to any dimension
d\geq 3
, and correspondingly explicit formulas for key statistical quantities are not known. (The failure of determinants formulas is related to well-studied and subtle problems in combinatorics about “local moves” for dimers in higher dimensions. For a small subset of works on this, see e.g.
[
FHNQ11
,
MS14
,
FKMS22
,
KS22
,
dM25
]
.)

Limit shapes are known to exist for dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}

[
CSW23
]
but since
there is no formula for the surface tension, nothing is known rigorously about what the limit shapes look like.

In contrast with the standard dimer model, we show that the large
N
limit of the multinomial dimer model is exactly solvable in a strong sense in any dimension
d
: we can give explicit formulas for the surface tension and compute limit shapes. These formulas do not involve determinants. Instead, they come the fact that the partition functions for multinomial dimer measures have a generating function in
N
which can be expanded around infinity, as observed in
[
KP22a
]
. For
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the formula for the multinomial surface tension in the large
N
limit is

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sigma(s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+2s_{1}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1+2s_{1}}{2}\bigg)+\frac{1-2s_{1}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1-2s_{1}}{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\frac{1+2s_{2}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1+2s_{2}}{2}\bigg)+\frac{1-2s_{2}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1-2s_{2}}{2}\bigg),

see Figure
2
.
The Euler-Lagrange PDE for the limiting height function
h=h(x,y)
is

\frac{4h_{xx}}{1-4h_{x}^{2}}+\frac{4h_{yy}}{1-4h_{y}^{2}}=0.

We compute
\sigma
by showing that it is the Legendre dual of an explicit
free energy
function on the torus, see Section
4
(see also more informal description in Section
1.3
).

Despite a smaller set of available tools, there are a number of works on statistical mechanics in higher dimensions, with approaches very different from ours. See e.g.
[
Tag22
,
QT23a
,
CSW23
]
for works on the dimer model,
[

,
WMDB02
,

]
for a generalization of rhombus tilings to

[

]
for a survey on very successful approaches involving the random currents expansion, applied for example to the 3D Ising model
[

]
, and
[

,

,
Koz07
]
for a very small selection of works on other related statistical mechanics models and their scaling limits in dimensions

We remark that solvability, and examples of solvable models, can be an extremely powerful tool, and has potential to be useful beyond the limit shape theory we establish in this paper. In part due to its solvability, the dimer model in two dimensions has played a central role in the development of random conformal geometry, which studies the interplay between lattice models in statistical mechanics and complex analysis. It was predicted in physics that (critical) statistical mechanics models should exhibit conformal invariance in their scaling limits; for one early reference, see e.g.
[
BPZ84
]
. The 2D dimer model was one of the first statistical mechanics models where conformal invariance was established rigorously
[
Ken00
]
. It is also connected to the Gaussian free field, Schramm-Loewner evolutions, and conformal field theory, see e.g.
[

,

,

,

,

1.2.
Scaling limits in general dimension

In two dimensions, the question about scaling limits of dimer covers can be made precise using the correspondence with height functions. In dimensions
d\geq 3
, there is no height function correspondence. Instead, in any dimension, there is a correspondence between dimer covers and
discrete flows.
Recall that all the graphs we consider are bipartite, with bipartition into “white” and “black” vertices. Given an
N
-dimer cover
M
, the corresponding
discrete flow

\omega_{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{M}(e)=M_{e}/N\in[0,1]

if
e
is oriented from its white vertex to its black vertex (and
\omega_{M}(-e)=-\omega_{M}(e)
if
-e
denotes
e
with reversed orientation). Here
M_{e}
denotes the multiplicity of
e
in
M
, so if
M
is a single dimer cover, then
\omega_{M}(e)\in\{0,1\}
. By construction, the divergences
\sum_{e\ni v}\omega_{M}(e)
of
\omega_{M}
are
\pm 1
for all vertices
v
. If
M,M^{\prime}
are dimer covers on the same graph, then we can compare them using the sup norm on the edges of the graph.

We now specialize to the case of dimer covers of subgraphs of lattices. Throughout, we will assume that the bipartite lattices
\Lambda
that we consider have various symmetries that simplify our exposition. In particular we assume that
\Lambda
has a “bipartite transitive”
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
action by translations, that is, transitive on white vertices and transitive on black vertices. We let

D
denote the common degree, and incident edge directions at white vertices are denoted
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
. See Section
2.2
.

To compare dimer covers of a sequence of graphs
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use the
weak topology on flows
, which is described in Section
3
. This is the same topology used in
[
CSW23
]
to compare dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
(and obtain a large deviations principle), but we streamline its description slightly in adapting it from
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
to general lattices. In this topology, the
n\to\infty
scaling limits of discrete flows on
R_{n}
are
asymptotic flows
(see Theorem
3.9
), namely, they are measurable divergence-free vector fields supported in
R
and valued in the
Newton polytope

\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, defined in our setting by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)=\text{convex hull}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{D}\}.

We call a point
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
a
slope.
See Section
2.5
.

This flow description makes sense in any dimension, in particular it still makes sense in two dimensions where the height function does exist. In this case, the relationship between divergence-free flows and height functions is simple: if
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is divergence-free, then its curl-free dual is
(-\omega_{2},\omega_{1})=\nabla h
, where
h
is the height function. In order to give a unified description, we use the discrete flow correspondence to formulate the large deviation principle for multinomial dimers in any dimensions
d
.

1.3.
Large deviations, free energy, and surface tension

Fix a lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as above. Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact region which is the closure of its interior and has piecewise smooth boundary, and fix a boundary condition
b
on
\partial R
(e.g., the boundary value of an asymptotic flow). Finally, let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
be a sequence of graphs which approximate
R
in Hausdorff distance
and whose boundaries approximate
b
in the appropriate sense.

Let
\rho_{n,N}
be the multinomial dimer measure on
R_{n}
.
We then have

Theorem 1.1

(Rough statement; see Theorem
6.1
)
.

Let
(R,b)
,
R_{n},\rho_{n,N}
be as above. In the iterated limit as
N
and then
n
go to infinity, the measures
\rho_{n,N}
satisfy a large deviation principle in the weak topology on flows with good rate function
I_{b}(\cdot)
which up to an additive constant is

\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,

if
\omega
is an asymptotic flow. If
\omega
is not an asymptotic flow, then
I_{b}(\omega)=\infty
. Here
C_{b}
is a constant and
\sigma
is the surface tension determined by
\Lambda
.

For general background on the theory of large deviations, see e.g.
[
Var16
,
DZ09
]
. This statement is analogous to the large deviation principles of
[
CKP01
,
CSW23
]
for the standard dimer model on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.

We show that
\sigma
can be computed explicitly for any
\Lambda
, and is strictly convex (Theorem
4.16
). The strict convexity implies that the rate function has a unique minimizer. This minimizer is the
limit shape
for
(R,b)
.

Corollary 1.2

(Rough statement; see Corollary
6.4
)
.

Fix
(R,b)
and
R_{n}
as above. As
n,N
go to infinity, random
N
-dimer covers of
R_{n}
sampled from
\rho_{n,N}
concentrate exponentially fast on the limit shape, i.e., the unique asymptotic flow
\omega
which minimizes
I
with boundary condition
b
.

See Figure

The proof of Theorem
1.1
uses a variety of different tools, which make use of a mixture of techniques coming from explicit formulas, general combinatorics that holds for the dimer model in any dimension, and substantial simplifications made possible by the large
N
structure. In particular:

•

We use the generating function for multinomial dimer partition functions, found in
[
KP22a
]
, to explicitly compute the free energy
F
for multinomial dimer measures on the torus in the large
N
limit (see Theorem
4.5
).

•

We show that the surface tension
\sigma
is the Legendre dual of
F
(see Theorem
4.14
). The general outline of this argument mimics the one in
[
CKP01
]
, but the key variance estimate (Lemma
4.9
) instead uses the large
N
structure.

•

We prove a version of the
patching theorem
for the large
N
limit. Analogous to the patching theorem of
[
CSW23
]
, the main tool is Hall’s matching theorem. However, the proof is substantially simpler because of the large
N
structure. See Section
5
.

•

We prove that any asymptotic flow can be approximated by a discrete flow corresponding to an
N
-dimer cover with a new construction, which makes use of the large
N
structure (see Theorem
6.11
).

The formulas for the free energy
F
are especially simple and explicit. Namely, if
\Lambda
has edges in directions
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
at white vertices, then
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is

(1.3)

\displaystyle F(\alpha)=\log\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)\bigg).

The
amoeba

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the largest subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on which
F
is strictly convex. Unlike for the standard dimer model, where
\mathcal{A}
is a strict subset, here
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
, and
F
is analytic. The surface tension
\sigma
is the Legendre dual of
F
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma(s)=\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}-F(\alpha)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\alpha_{i}.

The domain of the surface tension is the Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. The surface tension has a simple closed formula in some cases, see e.g. the surface tension for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
above in (
1.2
). The surface tension for the three-dimensional body-centered cubic (
BCC
) lattice is also simple, its free energy and surface tension are:

\displaystyle F(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})

\displaystyle=\log\bigg((\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{1}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{1}/2})(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{2}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{2}/2})(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{3}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{3}/2})\bigg)

\displaystyle\sigma(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1+2s_{i}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1+2s_{i}}{2}\bigg)+\frac{1-2s_{i}}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{1-2s_{i}}{2}\bigg).

For
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, the free energy is

\displaystyle F(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=\log(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{1}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{1}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{2}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{2}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\alpha_{3}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha_{3}/2}).

In this case the Legendre dual
\sigma
is valued in the Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|s_{1}|+|s_{2}|+|s_{3}|\leq 1/2\}
, which is an octahedron. Restricted to any triangular face of the octahedron, it is equal to the multinomial surface tension for the honeycomb lattice. However, the formula on all of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
is computationally complicated, involving the root of a degree 8 polynomial. For a numerically-generated discrete approximation of the multinomial limit shape for the
Aztec octahedron
graph in
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, see Figure
5
.

1.4.
Limit shape regularity

The limit shape
\omega
, which exists by Corollary
1.2
, is a measurable divergence-free vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In two dimensions, where the height function is defined, the limit shape height function
h
is related to
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
by
\nabla h=(-\omega_{2},\omega_{1})
.

One of the iconic properties of limit shapes for standard dimers is the existence of
facets
. In terms of slopes, a facet corresponds to a region where the limit shape divergence-free flow
\omega
takes values in
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, the boundary of the set of allowed slopes
(and/or certain integer interior points). In Figure
1
, the facets are the four regions, one at each corner, where the height function is linear. Single dimer covers of regions like the Aztec diamond in
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
also appear to have facets in simulations, but it is not known how to prove this rigorously (see
[
CSW23
, Problem 9.0.12]
). In contrast, we show the following for the multinomial model in the large
N
limit:

Theorem 1.3

(See Theorem
7.8
)
.

For any
(R,b)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
flexible (a mild regularity condition, see Definition
7.1
), the
N\to\infty
multinomial dimer limit shape does not have facets.

This result holds in any dimension. The proof of this theorem comes from a perturbative argument, and uses the fact that the surface tension gradient blows up at
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda).
It would be interesting to study this in the regimes between
N=1
and
N=\infty
, see Question
10.4
. In two dimensions, elliptic regularity allows us to conclude the following.

Corollary 1.4

(See Corollary
7.9
)
.

In two dimensions,
N\to\infty
multinomial dimer limit shapes are smooth.

Note that for the standard dimer model, limit shapes are often not smooth, and are not (or are not expected to be) smooth in interesting examples such as the Aztec diamond (or its three-dimensional analogs). Indeed, if the limit shape has facets then it has a singular locus (along the boundary of the facet) where
\omega
and
\nabla h
need not even be continuous. For discussion of this, see e.g.
[
ADPZ23
, Section 1.3]
. We expect
N\to\infty
multinomial limit shapes to be smooth in higher dimensions as well, see Question
10.2
.

1.5.
Euler-Lagrange equations

Subject to boundary conditions, the limit shape divergence-free flow
\omega
on
(R,b)
is the unique minimizer of

\displaystyle\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

and so can also be described as the solution to a system of Euler-Lagrange equations. For a divergence-free flow in dimension
d
, the Euler-Lagrange equations have the following general form.

Theorem 1.5

(See Theorem
7.3
)
.

The limit shape flow
\omega
for
(R,b)
is the solution to the following system of differential equations:

•

the divergence equation,

\displaystyle\text{div}(\omega(x))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)=0;

•

a collection of
{d\choose 2}
equations:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{j}}\sigma(\omega(x))-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{i}}\sigma(\omega(x))=0

for all
i\neq j
between
1
and
d
.

Since
\sigma
has a known formula, these PDEs are completely explicit. For example, for the
BCC
lattice, they are

\displaystyle\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{1}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{1}}{\partial y}-\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{2}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{2}}{\partial x}=0,\quad\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{1}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{1}}{\partial z}-\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{3}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial x}=0,\quad\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{2}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{2}}{\partial z}-\frac{4}{1-4\omega_{3}^{2}}\frac{\partial\omega_{3}}{\partial y}=0.

When
d=2
, we can immediately derive the familiar Euler-Lagrange equations for a height function
h
from Theorem
7.3
using the relationship
\nabla h=(-\omega_{2},\omega_{1})
. A recent work of the first author
[
KP22b
]
gives a general method for describing the solutions to gradient variational problems in terms of their tangent planes; we apply this to 2D multinomial dimers in Section
9.5
.

We also observe a general principle, stemming from Legendre duality, which transforms the equations in Theorem
1.5
into a new gradient variational problem in any dimension. Generally, Legendre duality implies that
\nabla F
and
\nabla\sigma
are inverses of each other, and we have the relationships:

\displaystyle\nabla F(a)=s\iff\nabla\sigma(s)=a.

Hence if we define
\alpha:=\nabla\sigma(\omega)
, where
\omega
is the limit shape divergence-free flow, then the equations in (
1.4
) imply that
\alpha=\nabla H
is the gradient of a function. We call
\alpha
the
gauge flow
. The divergence free equation for
\omega
then corresponds to a PDE for
H
.

Theorem 1.6

(See Theorem
7.10
, Corollary
7.12
)
.

Fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a suitable boundary function
H_{b}
on
\partial R
. If
H
is a weak solution to the differential equation

(1.5)

\displaystyle\text{div}(\nabla F(\nabla H))=0

with
H\mid_{\partial R}=H_{b}
, the limiting gauge flow is
\alpha=\nabla H
.

This relationship between divergence-free and gradient variational problems seems to be well-known to experts in PDEs. The relationship between these equations is purely formal, and also holds for other Legendre dual pairs.

The gauge PDE for the
BCC
lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{e}^{H_{x}}H_{xx}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{H_{x}})^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{H_{y}}H_{yy}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{H_{y}})^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{H_{z}}H_{zz}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{H_{z}})^{2}}=0.

A particular benefit of the gauge PDE is that it is in terms of the free energy
F
(which has a simple expression (
1.3
)), so it is simple even if the surface tension is not. Unlike the two dimensional height function
h
, which is Lipschitz, the limiting gauge function
H
does not have any a priori regularity (it follows, however, from Corollary
1.4
that
H
is smooth in two dimensions). The limiting gauge function
H
for the Aztec cuboids
\mathrm{AC}(a,b,c)
, with
(a/n,b/n,c/n)\to(A,B,C)
as
n\to\infty
, is

\displaystyle H(x,y,z)=\;\;

\displaystyle A\log A-x\log x-(A-x)\log(A-x)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle B\log B+y\log y+(B-y)\log(B-y)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle C\log C+z\log z+(C-z)\log(C-z).

The limit shape for a sequence of Aztec cuboids is shown in Figure
3
. See Section
9.3
for more details on this example.

1.6.
Critical gauge

For the large
N
limit on a fixed finite graph, Pohoata and the first author showed that the behavior of the multinomial dimer model is captured by the
critical gauge

[
KP22a
]
.

To describe our last main result, the version of this we need is the following; see Section
2.3
for more detail. Suppose that
G=(V=B\cup W,E)
is a (nice) bipartite graph, with all vertex multiplicities
N
. There exist
critical gauge functions

f:W\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
g:B\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that as
N\to\infty
, random
N
-dimer covers sampled from multinomial dimer measures concentrate on the discrete flow proportional to

\displaystyle\{c(e)=f(u)g(v)\}_{e=(u,v)\in E}.

These are called the
critical edge weights
. The gauge functions
f,g
are unique up to the transformation
f\mapsto Cf,g\mapsto g/C
for some constant
C>0
, and are the unique solutions (up to this transformation) to the
critical gauge equations
:

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in W:(u,v)\in E}f(u)g(v)=1\qquad\forall v\in B

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in B:(u,v)\in E}f(u)g(v)=1\qquad\forall u\in W.

The critical gauge can be efficiently computed numerically using Sinkhorn’s algorithm
[
Sin64
]
. To see this, note that if
G
is a finite graph that admits
N
-dimer covers, then it has the same number of white and black vertices. We let
A
denote the corresponding bipartite adjacency matrix of
G
. Solving the critical gauge equations is then the same as finding diagonal matrices
D_{1},D_{2}
such that
D_{1}AD_{2}
is bistochastic, i.e., has both row and column sums which are
1
.

Given
A
, Sinkhorn’s algorithm finds these matrices
D_{1},D_{2}
by alternately rescaling all rows of
A
(i.e., changing all the
f(u)
’s), so that the critical gauge equations are solved at all white vertices, and then rescaling columns of
A
(i.e., changing all the
g(v)
’s), breaking the critical gauge equations at white vertices but solving them at the black ones. Viewing these two updates as open step, each step produces a matrix that is closer to being bistochastic, see
[
KP22a
]
.

See Figures
4
and
5
for applications of Sinkhorn’s algorithm. Empirically, critical gauge functions are almost always algebraic of high degree. We do however know of a small number of examples where they take rational values, see Section
9
. It would be interesting to understand more generally which graphs have simple descriptions of their critical gauge functions or critical edge weights, see Question
10.1
.

We show that given a sequence of graphs
R_{n}
as in the large deviation principle, the scaling limit of the critical gauge functions
g_{n}
(resp.
f_{n}
) is the limiting gauge function
H
(resp.
-H
), i.e., a solution to (
1.5
).

Theorem 1.7

(See Theorem
8.1
)
.

Fix a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
and
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as before. Suppose that
H
is
C^{1}
and solves the gauge PDE:
\text{div}(\nabla F(\nabla H))=0
, and that
g_{n}
is the critical gauge function on black vertices of
R_{n}
with limiting multiplicity
1
everywhere on

. Further suppose that

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in B\cap\partial R_{n}}|(1/n)\log g_{n}(u)-H(u)|=O(1/n).

We equate

with its linear interpolation to a continuous function. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(1/n)\log g_{n}

exists in the topology induced by the supremum norm on continuous functions and is equal to
H
. Similarly,
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1/n)\log f_{n}=-H.

This theorem establishes rigorously the intuitive result that the scaling limit of the critical edge weights (which capture the large
N
limiting behavior on each fixed graph) should be the limit shape divergence-free flow. We apply this in Section

to compute limit shapes in some examples. See Figures

The proof of this theorem uses a collection of new ideas, in particular a version of the maximum principle for solutions to the critical gauge equations (which are discrete difference equations), and an expansion which relates the critical gauge equations to the gauge PDE. See Section

.

Since
F
is analytic for this model, the condition that

is
C^{1}
is equivalent to requiring that the limit shape divergence-free flow
\omega
(that is,
\nabla h
) is continuous, and this holds for the multinomial dimer model in two dimensions by Corollary
1.4
. While we believe the
C^{1}
condition holds in general dimension for the large
N

.

2.
Background and preliminaries

The multinomial dimer model is a generalization of the dimer model and a special case of the multinomial tiling model introduced in
[
KP22a
]
.

2.1.
Definition of the model

2.1.1.
The dimer model.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite bipartite graph. A
dimer cover
or
perfect matching

\tau
of
G
is a collection of edges
\tau\subset E
so that every vertex
v\in V
is contained in exactly one edge
e\in\tau
. We let
\Omega_{1}(G)

denote the set of dimer covers of
G
.

Given a collection of positive edge weights
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
, the
Boltzmann measure
is the
associated probability measure on
\Omega_{1}(G)
where the probability of a
dimer cover
\tau
is proportional to the product of
its edge weights:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{e\in\tau}w_{e}.

The normalizing constant
Z=\sum_{\tau}\prod_{e\in\tau}w_{e}
is called the
partition function
.

2.1.2.
Blow-up graph.

Let
{\mathbf{N}}=(N_{v})_{v\in V}
be a collection of positive integer
vertex multiplicities
on the vertices of
G
.

The
{\mathbf{N}}
-fold blow-up
of
G
, denoted
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
, is the graph where each vertex
v\in V
is replaced by
N_{v}
vertices, called lifts of
v
, and each edge
e=(u,v)\in E
is replaced by the complete bipartite graph on lifts of
u,v
.
We denote the vertices and edges of
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
by
(V_{{\mathbf{N}}},E_{{\mathbf{N}}})
.
If
{\mathbf{N}}\equiv 1
, then the blow-up graph is just the original graph
G
.
Let
\pi:G_{{\mathbf{N}}}\to G
be the projection map.

2.1.3.
The multinomial dimer model.

The multinomial tiling model, introduced in
[
KP22a
]
, studies the behavior of tilings of blow-up graphs
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
. The
multinomial dimer model
is the special case where tiles are dimers, that is, the multinomial dimer model is the dimer model on
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
.

An
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover
of
G
is a function
M:E\to\{0,1,2,\dots\}
summing to
N_{v}
at each vertex
v
,
that is, such that
\sum_{u\sim v}M_{(u,v)}=N_{v}
. We let
\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}
denote the set of
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers of
G
.
Note that when
{\mathbf{N}}\equiv 1
, then
\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}(G)=\Omega_{1}(G)
is the set of dimer covers of
G
.
If
\tau
is a dimer cover of
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
, let
M:=\pi(\tau)\in\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}
be its projection: for each edge
e\in E
, the multiplicity
M_{e}
is the number of lifts of
e
in
\tau
. We fix the notational convention throughout that
\tau
is a dimer cover of a blow-up graph
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
, and

M\in\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}
is its projection
M=\pi(\tau)
. Note that while
\tau
determines
M
,
M
does not determine
\tau
.

Given positive edge weights
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
, we endow
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
with edge weights such that all lifts of
e
have weight
w_{e}
. This defines the Boltzmann measure on dimer covers of
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
where the probability of a tiling
\tau
is given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{e\in E}w_{e}^{M_{e}}

where
Z
is the partition function.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with
vertex multiplicities
{\mathbf{N}}=(N_{v})_{v\in V}
. Given edge weights
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
, the corresponding
multinomial dimer measure
is the probability measure on
\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}
where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M)=\frac{\left|\pi^{-1}(M)\right|}{Z}\prod_{e\in M}w_{e}^{M_{e}}

where
Z=Z_{G,{\mathbf{N}}}
is the same partition function, given below. In other words, the multinomial dimer measure is the measure on
\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}
which is the projection of the Boltzmann measure on dimer covers of
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
defined in (
2.1
). We also refer to the measure in (
2.1
) as the
lifted
version of the multinomial dimer measure.

The partition function of either measure is

(2.2)

Z_{G,{\mathbf{N}}}({w})=\sum_{M\in\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}(G)}|\pi^{-1}(M)|\prod_{e\in E}w_{e}^{M_{e}}.

If
{\mathbf{N}}\equiv 1
, this measures is just the Boltzmann measure on
\Omega_{1}(G)
, the dimer covers
G
.
For any
{\mathbf{N}}
, if
w_{e}\equiv 1
, then the lifted measure is the uniform measure on dimer covers of
G_{{\mathbf{N}}}
. However it is important to note that in this case the projection is
not
the
uniform measure on
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers of
G
. The probability of an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover
M
is proportional to

|\pi^{-1}(M)|
, which depends on the edge multiplicities
M_{e}
.
In general, the number of lifts of
M
is given by

\displaystyle|\pi^{-1}(M)|=\frac{\prod_{v\in V}N_{v}!}{\prod_{e\in E}M_{e}!}.

See Figure
6
for an example.
(To see this, note that at each vertex
v
with edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
we must split the
N_{v}
vertices over
v
into subsets of sizes
M_{e_{1}},\dots,M_{e_{d}}
, and then for each edge
e
pick a pairing of the
M_{e}
vertices on each side.
This results in, for each vertex, a multinomial coefficient
\binom{N_{v}}{M_{e_{1}},\dots,M_{e_{d}}}
then for each edge
e

a set of
M_{e}!
choices.)

{\mathbf{N}}\equiv 2

\frac{1}{24}\{4,4,16\}=\{\frac{1}{6},\frac{1}{6},\frac{2}{3}\}
of occurring.

2.2.
Lattices and multinomial dimers on subgraphs

Let
\Lambda=(V=B\cup W,E)
be a
D
-regular bipartite graph embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
acting by translation isomorphisms, transitively on
W
and on
B
. In other words we assume
W={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
B={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}+v_{0}
where
v_{0}
is some vector in
[0,1]^{d}
minus its vertices. Edges connect
w
to
w+e_{1},\dots,w+e_{D}
if
w\in W
, and therefore connect
b
to
b-e_{1},\dots,b-e_{D}
. We call such a graph
\Lambda
a
lattice
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. An edge
e
connecting
w
to
w+e_{i}
is said to be
of type
e_{i}
.

We assume that
\Lambda
is connected (which implies that
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{D}\}
span
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) and
harmonically embedded
, that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{D}e_{i}=0
. We call
W
the “white vertices” and
B
the “black vertices” of the lattice. We also consider lattices
\Lambda
which are a linear image of ones of the form described above. As a technical point, for simplicity, we assume that the edges of
\Lambda
are embedded so that they are monotone and don’t contain other vertices of
\Lambda
in their interior (for the examples we consider in Section
9
this simply holds when all the edges are embedded as straight lines.) We also assume that
\Lambda
is symmetric under reflection. This condition is not necessary, but simplifies one part of our argument, see Remark
6.9
.

Our results hold in any dimension
d
. They should also hold more generally for periodic graphs with larger fundamental domains, but we restrict to the family of lattices above to simplify the exposition. Examples of lattices satisfying the above conditions are
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for any
d
and the body-centered cubic lattice (
BCC
) in three dimensions. (See Section
9.3
for an example in the
BCC
lattice.)

If
G\subset\Lambda
is a finite subgraph, we define the
boundary

\partial G
of
G
in
\Lambda
to be those vertices which have neighbors in
\Lambda\setminus G
:

\displaystyle\partial G:=\{v\in G:\exists\,u\in\Lambda\setminus G,(u,v)\in E(\Lambda)\}.

We call
G^{\circ}=G\setminus\partial G
the
interior
of
G
. In this paper we study multinomial dimers on subgraphs
G\subset\Lambda
where the multiplicity function
{\mathbf{N}}
is constant in
G^{\circ}
but allowed to vary on the boundary:

•

for all
v\in G^{\circ}
,
N_{v}=N
;

•

for
v\in\partial G
,
N_{v}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
;

•

the collection of multiplicities
{\mathbf{N}}=(N_{v})_{v\in V}
is
feasible
, i.e. (i)
G
has an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover and (ii) for every each
e\in E
, there exists an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover
M
of
G
with
M_{e}>0
.

Note that the feasible condition depends only on
(G,{\mathbf{N}})
, and is independent of the edge weights. See Figure
7
for an example of
(G,{\mathbf{N}})
which is not feasible.

G\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where for any

there exists an
{\mathbf{N}}\equiv N

is not feasible because not all edges can occur.

2.3.
Asymptotics as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
on a fixed graph

2.3.1.
Limiting boundary conditions

Fixing
G\subset\Lambda
and letting
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
,
the
limiting covering multiplicity

\beta_{v}
of a vertex
v\in G
is defined to be

\beta_{v}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}N_{v}/N

which we assume exists (we only take limits
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
where this limit exists).
This multiplicity is
1
for all
v\in G^{\circ}
and has
\beta_{v}\in[0,1]
for
v\in\partial G
. We refer to
(\beta_{v})_{v\in\partial G}
as the
limiting boundary condition
. We say that
(\beta_{v})_{v\in G}
is feasible if it can be approximated by
(N_{v})_{v\in V}
feasible.

2.3.2.
Asymptotics of multinomial tilings

Here we summarize some key definitions and results from
[
KP22a
]
about the multinomial dimer model as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
on a fixed finite graph.

The
tiling polynomial

P
with edge weights
w=(w_{e})_{e\in E}
is a function of vertex weights
\mathbf{x}=(x_{v})_{v\in V}
given by

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{(u,v)=e\in E}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}.

As a function of
\mathbf{x}
, we can use this to write a generating series for the multinomial partition functions.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
KP22a
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}^{{\mathbf{N}}}=\prod_{v\in V}x_{v}^{N_{v}}
and let
{\mathbf{N}}!=\prod_{v\in V}N_{v}!
. Then

\displaystyle\exp(P(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{{\mathbf{N}}\geq 0}Z_{G,{\mathbf{N}}}(w)\frac{\mathbf{x}^{{\mathbf{N}}}}{{\mathbf{N}}!}.

Two weight functions
w,w^{\prime}
are
gauge equivalent
if there exists a function
\mathbf{x}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for all edges
(u,v)=e\in E
, we have

\displaystyle w_{e}^{\prime}=w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}.

Gauge equivalence is a central topic of
[
KP22a
]
, and will play an important role in this paper as well. If
G
and
{\mathbf{N}}
are fixed and
w,w^{\prime}
are gauge equivalent, then they induce the same probability measure on
\Omega_{{\mathbf{N}}}(G)
.

To study asymptotics, we must fix how
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
. Using the normalization in
[
KP22a
]
, we let
K=\sum_{v\in V}N_{v}/2
be the total number of dimers in an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover. We take the limit
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
in such a way
that
\lim N_{v}/K
exists for each
v
; we denote

\alpha_{v}:=\lim N_{v}/K\in[0,1]
. Here
\alpha_{v}
is the asymptotic fraction of dimers covering
v
. We say that
(\alpha_{v})_{v\in V}
is
feasible
if it is approximated by feasible
(N_{v})_{v\in V}
. This differs from the limiting covering multiplicity
\beta_{v}
defined in the previous section by a multiplicative constant; see Remark
2.4
below.

Theorem 2.3

(See
[
KP22a
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Fix a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, a weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\alpha_{v}\in[0,1]
feasible and a sequence of multiplicites
{\mathbf{N}}
with
N_{v}/K\to\alpha_{v}
as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
for all
v\in V
. Then there exist a critical weight function
\mathbf{x}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for each
v\in V
,

(2.3)

\sum_{u:(u,v)=e\in E}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}=\alpha_{v}P(\mathbf{x}).

Further, the critical weights
\mathbf{x}
are unique up to the operation of multiplying by a constant on all black vertices,
and the inverse of this constant on white vertices. In particular, the critical edge weights
w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}
for
(u,v)\in E
are unique.

Remark 2.4
.

When
G
is a lattice subgraph, and all interior vertices have the same multiplicity
N
, then
\alpha_{v}=\lim N_{v}/K
and
\beta_{v}=\lim N_{v}/N
differ by a constant which is independent of
v
(approximately
|V|
). As such, the critical gauge equations all change by a global multiplicative constant. This can be absorbed as changing the edge weights by a gauge, and therefore does not change the critical gauge.

Since
G=(V,E)
is bipartite with
V=W\cup B
, we can view the critical gauge function
\mathbf{x}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
as a pair of functions
f:W\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
g:B\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
f,g
are determined up to multiplying
f
by a constant
C>0
and
g
by
1/C
. These functions

are the
critical gauge functions
on white and black vertices respectively. These will be a tool in Section
4
, and we study their scaling limits on a growing sequence of graphs in Section
8
.

By dividing
M_{e}
by
K
, we can view multinomial dimer measures as probability measures on the compact space
[0,1]^{E}
for any
{\mathbf{N}}
. As a corollary of Theorem
2.3
, the function
(M_{e}/K)_{e\in E}
satisfies a “limit shape theorem” as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
, where the limit shape is proportional to the critical edge weights.

Theorem 2.5

(See
[
KP22a
, Corollary 3.2]
)
.

Fix a finite graph
G=(V,E)
and edge weights
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
. Suppose that
{\mathbf{N}}=(N_{v})_{v\in V}
are feasible vertex multiplicities such that
N_{v}/K\to\alpha_{v}
as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
, and let
(c_{e})_{e\in E}
denote the critical edge weights. The configuration
\{\frac{M_{e}}{K}\}_{e\in E}
concentrates on its expectation as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
and the limit is proportional to the fractional matching
\{c_{e}\}_{e\in E}\subset[0,1]^{E}
.

2.4.
Discrete vector fields

In two dimensions, there is a correspondence between single dimer covers and Lipschitz height functions, up to an additive constant
[
Thu90
]
. In higher dimensions, there is no longer a correspondence between single dimer covers and height functions, but there is a related correspondence with
discrete flows

\omega
which works in any dimension, as used in
[
CSW23
]
. There are analogous correspondences for
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers. We use the correspondence with discrete flows to compare dimer covers of different graphs; this is explained in Section
3
.

A
(discrete) flow

\omega
is a function on the oriented edges of a graph such that
\omega(-e)=-\omega(e)
, where
-e
denotes
e
with reversed orientation. The
divergence
of
\omega
at a vertex
v\in V
is
\text{div}\,\omega(v)=\sum_{e\ni v}\omega(e)
, where the edges
e
in the sum are all oriented out of
v
.

As in Section
2.2
, let
G=(V=W\cup B,E)\subset\Lambda
be a subgraph of the lattice
\Lambda
and let
{\mathbf{N}}
be a feasible multiplicity function equal to
N
for all
v\in G^{\circ}
and
N_{v}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
for
v\in\partial G
.

Given an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover
M
, we define the flow
\omega_{M}(e)
as a function of the oriented edge
e
by rescaling

M
by
N
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\omega_{M}(e)=\pm\frac{M_{e}}{N}

where the sign is positive if
e
is oriented white-to-black and negative otherwise. The divergences of
\omega_{M}
are:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\text{div}\,\omega_{M}(v)=\sum_{e\ni v}\omega_{M}(e)=\begin{cases}\pm 1\qquad&\forall\;v\in G^{\circ}\\
\pm N_{v}/N\qquad&\forall\;v\in\partial G\end{cases}

where the sign is positive if
v\in W
and negative if
v\in B
. In particular, after fixing the vertex multiplicities
{\mathbf{N}}
, the divergences of
\omega_{M}
depend only on the parity of the vertex.

Remark 2.6
.

A single dimer cover
\tau
corresponds to a flow
\omega_{\tau}
which takes values
\{0,1\}
on all white-to-black edges, and has
\pm 1
divergences. Taking
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
can be seen as “weakening” the constraints of the dimer model by allowing the discrete flow to take continuous values in
[0,1]
on edges, but still requiring it to have fixed divergences.

For any two
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers
M_{1},M_{2}
, the flow
\omega_{M_{1}}-\omega_{M_{2}}
is divergence-free. Alternatively, subtracting just a constant, we notice the flow
\overline{\omega}_{M}(e)=\omega_{M}(e)-1/D
is divergence-free. In Section
3
, we will see that the
scaling limits
as
n\to\infty
of all of these discrete flows on
\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
are measurable divergence-free vector fields (Theorem
3.9
).

Remark 2.7

(Relationship to height functions)
.

In two dimensions, we can derive the correspondence (up to an additive constant) between an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover
M
and a height function
h_{M}
from the correspondence with flows. This construction can be thought of as using
\overline{\omega}_{M}
. Let
\Lambda
be a planar bipartite lattice, and let
\Lambda^{*}
denote the dual lattice. The height function
h_{M}:\Lambda^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is determined up to an additive constant by its differences across dual edges: for a neighbor
w^{*}
connected to
v^{*}
by a dual edge
e^{*}
, the height difference is

(2.6)

h_{M}(w^{*})-h_{M}(v^{*})=\pm\bigg[\frac{M_{e}}{N}-\frac{1}{D}\bigg],

with positive sign if the dual edge
e^{*}=(v^{*},w^{*})
oriented from
v^{*}
to
w^{*}
has a white vertex on the left and negative sign otherwise. We note that this defines a function since summing these differences around any loop in
\Lambda^{*}
gives
0
.

The height function exists only in two dimensions for the topological reason that in two dimensions a divergence-free vector field (i.e.,
\overline{\omega}_{M}(e):=\omega_{M}(e)-1/D
) is dual—by rotating by
90^{\circ}
—to a curl-free vector field, and any curl-free vector field on the plane is the gradient of a function, hence of the form
\nabla h
.

2.5.
Slope and Newton polytope

The main setting of this paper will be an iterated limit, where we take both the multiplicity
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
and the lattice spacing
{1}/{n}\to 0
. Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact connected domain with piecewise smooth boundary (domain means that
R
is the closure of its interior, denoted
R^{\circ}
). Fix a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
, and consider any sequence of regions
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
approximating
R
in Hausdorff distance. We define the boundary of
R_{n}
to be

\displaystyle\partial R_{n}=\{v\in R_{n}:\exists\,u\in\tfrac{1}{n}\Lambda\setminus R_{n}\text{ such that }(u,v)\in E(\tfrac{1}{n}\Lambda)\}.

We refer to
R_{n}
as a
lattice approximation
of
R
. We only work with regions
R_{n}
that are feasible (i.e., tileable with the given multiplicities).

We compare dimer covers of the graphs
R_{n}
via the corresponding flows. For each

, we set feasible boundary conditions
(\beta_{v}^{n})_{v\in\partial R_{n}}
as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
, and study the behavior of a sequence of multinomial measures
(R_{n},{\mathbf{N}})
which are feasible and converge to
\beta_{v}^{n}
as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
.

For a dimer cover
M
, we define the
slope at white vertices
or just
slope

s_{M}:W\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

(2.7)

\displaystyle s_{M}(u)=\sum_{e\ni u}\omega_{M}(e)e,

where
e
is oriented white-to-black and interpreted as a vector.
At all white vertices, the incident edge directions are
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
. As such,
s_{M}
is valued in the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)=\text{convex hull}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{D}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This is the
Newton polytope
for the lattice
\Lambda
1
1

1

This differs from the usual definition of the Newton polytope using the characteristic polynomial, but these definitions are equivalent for the lattices
\Lambda
we consider.
. We call a point
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
a
slope
. The
slope at black vertices
can be defined similarly, but the vector directions incident to a black vertex are
-e_{1},\dots,-e_{D}
.

Remark 2.8
.

It is more standard in the 2D dimer literature for slope to mean the gradient of the height function. This differs from our convention by a rotation. If
s=(s_{1},s_{2})
is the slope at white vertices, the gradient of the height function is
(-s_{2},s_{1})
. Our definition of slope is called
mean current
in
[
CSW23
]
.

3.
Comparing dimer covers via vector fields

To compare
{\mathbf{N}}
- and
{\mathbf{N}}^{\prime}
-dimer covers
M,M^{\prime}
of the same finite graph
G=(V,E)
, we can use the sup norm on
the edges
E
of
G
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle|M-M^{\prime}|_{\infty,E}:=\sup_{e\in E}|\omega_{M}(e)-\omega_{M^{\prime}}(e)|=\sup_{e\in E}\left|\frac{M_{e}}{N}-\frac{M^{\prime}_{e}}{N^{\prime}}\right|.

This is an extremely natural way to compare dimer covers and is what is used in
[
KP22a
]
.

However, in this paper we will also be interested in comparing
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers of different graphs, in particular dimers covers of a growing sequence of rescaled graphs
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
approximating a compact region in
R\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as above. To do this we use the
weak topology on flows
, which is a topology on the flows corresponding to dimer covers described in Section
2.4
. This is the same topology used to study dimer covers of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
described in
[
CSW23
, Section 5]
. In adapting this to lattices other than
{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
, we also streamline its description and provide more intuition for why this topology is natural.

3.1.
Scaling limits of dimer covers

Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact, connected domain with piecewise smooth boundary and let
\Lambda
be a
d
-dimensional lattice as in Section
2.2
. Let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
be a sequence of lattice approximation of
R
as above. Recall that by convention, we fix the initial scaling of
\Lambda
so that the Voronoi tile of each vertex in
\Lambda
has volume
1/2
.

If
M
is an
N
-dimer cover of
R_{n}
, it has an associated flow
\omega_{M}:E\to[0,1]
, where we view
E
as the set of edges oriented white-to-black. We study the asymptotics of the corresponding discrete vector field

\displaystyle\omega_{M}(e)e,

where
e
is viewed as the vector oriented white-to-black.
Further associated to
M
is its slope per white vertex function

s_{M}(u)=\sum_{e\ni u}\omega_{M}(e)e:R_{n}\cap W\to\mathcal{N}(\Lambda).

We also consider the slope per black vertex function, denoted
\tilde{s}_{M}
, which is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{M}(v)=\sum_{e\ni v}\omega_{M}(e)(-e)\qquad v\in R_{n}\cap B.

Here we still view
e
as an edge oriented white-to-black, hence the minus sign comes from the fact that the edge directions at a black vertex are
-e_{1},\dots,-e_{D}
.

The objects
w_{M}(e)e,s_{M},\tilde{s}_{M}
are
discrete vector fields
, in the sense that they are vector-valued functions defined on lattice points or edges.
However for convenience we henceforth refer to them as
discrete flows
.

The
Wasserstein metric on flows
that we discuss next metrizes the weak convergence of the above discrete flows in the
n\to\infty
limit. This topology was introduced in
[
CSW23
]
to study dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. In this topology, we will show (see Theorem
3.9
) that the
n\to\infty
scaling limits of
\omega_{M}(e)e
,
s_{M}
and
-\tilde{s}_{M}
are the same and are
asymptotic flows
for
\Lambda
.

Definition 3.1
.

An
asymptotic flow
on
R
for
\Lambda
is a measurable vector field
\omega
which is (i) supported in
R
, (ii) divergence-free as a distribution, and (iii) valued in the Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. We denote the space of asymptotic flows on
R
for
\Lambda
by
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

3.2.
Weak topology on flows

The key aspect of the topology we define here is that it is a metrizable topology that captures weak convergence of the slope functions in the scaling limit. We can view the slope functions
s_{n}
or
\tilde{s}_{n}
, discrete flows
\omega_{n}(e)e
, and asymptotic flows
\omega
as elements of the same space by viewing them as vector-valued distributions. Through this, there is a natural correspondence between these and their dual measures. Let
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d}
denote unit basis vectors for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}x_{d}
. The dual measures are as follows:

•

If
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d})
is an asymptotic flow, then the dual measures are
\mu_{i}(x)=\omega_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.

•

If
\omega_{n}(e)e
is a discrete flow, on edges
e\in E\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
, then its dual measures are

\mu_{i}(x)=\sum_{e\in E}\omega_{n}(e)\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{i}_{e}(x)}{n^{d}},

where
\mathrm{d}\lambda^{i}_{e}
is supported on
e
and projects to Lebesgue measure on the projection of
e
to the
i
th coordinate.

•

If
s_{n}:V\cap W\to\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
is a slope function on white vertices, then its dual measures
are

\mu_{i}(x)=\sum_{v\in V\cap W}\langle s_{n}(v),\eta_{i}\rangle\frac{\mathds{1}_{v}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{n^{d}},

where
\mathds{1}_{v}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
denotes the unit point mass at
v
. The slope function on black vertices has an analogous dual which is a sum of point masses at black vertices.

Remark 3.2
.

Recall that as a technicality, we assumed that the edges of
\Lambda
are monotone and embedded so that they don’t cross other edges, and don’t pass though other vertices of the lattice outside of their endpoints. This doesn’t change the limiting slope, and the reason we make this convention is that it means the measures
\vec{\mu}
corresponding to a discrete flow
\omega_{n}
determine
\omega_{n}
. This means that our use of
e
in some places to denote the corresponding vector (which is just determined by the endpoints of the edge) is a slight abuse of notation.

We refer to the measures that can occur above (as a coordinate component of an asymptotic flow, discrete flow, or slope function) as
component measures
. A sequence of vector-valued measures
\vec{\mu}^{k}=(\mu_{1}^{k},\dots,\mu_{d}^{k})
converges weakly to
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})
if
\mu_{i}^{k}
converges weakly for each
i=1,\dots,d
. We will see below (Proposition
3.3
) that the
weak topology on flows
is the same as the metric topology coming from the

Wasserstein metric on flows

d_{W}
given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle d_{W}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime}),

where
\mu_{i},\mu_{i}^{\prime}
are the
i^{th}
component dual measures of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
respectively, and where
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}
is the
generalized Wasserstein distance
which we describe in the next subsection. In other words,
d_{W}
metrizes weak convergence for component measures.

3.3.
Wasserstein metric

The standard
L^{1}
Wasserstein distance, denoted
W_{1}
, is a metric on probability measures on a metric space
(X,d)
given by:

(3.3)

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}d(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the collection of all couplings of
\mu,\nu
. Intuitively
W_{1}
measures the minimum “cost” of transforming
\mu
into
\nu
, where cost is the amount of mass moved times the distance moved to transform
\mu
into
\nu
. Wasserstein metrics have been studied extensively in analysis and optimal transport, see e.g.
[
Vil09
]
for a general introduction. The generalized Wasserstein distance
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}
extends this notion to (i) measures of different total mass by adding an
L^{1}
cost for adding and deleting mass
[
PR14
,
PR16
]
, and (ii) to signed measures
[
AMS11
,
PRT19
]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of positive Borel measures with finite total mass. If
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|+|\nu-\tilde{\nu}|+W_{1}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu}).

If
\mu=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}
and
\nu=\nu_{+}-\nu_{-}
are signed measures, then
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu,\nu)=\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu_{+}+\nu_{-},\nu_{+}+\mu_{-})
.

For positive measures, it is shown in
[
PR14
, Theorem 3]
that
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}
metrizes weak convergence for tight sequences. While the component measures we use are signed, they have bounded mass and we infer the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

The Wasserstein metric on flows
d_{W}
metrizes weak convergence of the component measures.

We further have that:

Theorem 3.4

(
[
CSW23
, Theorem 5.3.4]
)
.

The metric space
(\text{AF}(\Lambda,R),d_{W})
is compact.

Remark 3.5
.

The only adaptation of this result is that
\text{AF}(R)=\text{AF}(\mathbb{Z}^{3},R)
is replaced with
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
. This just changes the values asymptotic flows can take from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
to the general
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

The next three results are basic lemmas about
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}
which are useful for deriving bounds and proving convergence results in
d_{W}
. These are proved in
[
CSW23
, Section 5]
.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
CSW23
, Lemma 5.2.7]
)
.

Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are component measures and are supported on a common compact set
R
of dimension
d
. Suppose there is a partition of
R
into sets
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},...,B_{K}\}
of diameter at most
\varepsilon
such that
\bigg|\mu(B)-\nu(B)\bigg|<\delta
for all

. If one of the measures corresponds to a discrete flow on

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{1,1}_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq K(10\varepsilon^{d+1}+\delta).

Lemma 3.7

(

)
.

Suppose that

is a connected region with piecewise smooth boundary. If

Lemma 3.8

(

Let

3.4.
Convergence theorems

As above, fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be a compact, connected domain with piecewise smooth boundary and a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
. Suppose that
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
is a sequence of lattice regions approximating
R
as discussed above. Let
\omega_{n}:E(R_{n})\to[0,1]
be any sequence of discrete flows that could correspond to dimer covers, i.e., which have the specified divergences at all vertices.

Theorem 3.9
.

Any subsequential limit
\omega_{*}
of
\{\omega_{n}(e)e\}_{e\in E}
as
n\to\infty
in the weak topology on flows is an asymptotic flow, i.e.,
\omega_{*}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

To prove the theorem, we first note that it is equivalent to work with the associated slope functions.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\omega_{n}
be a sequence of discrete flows as above and
s_{n},\tilde{s}_{n}
their slope functions on white and black vertices respectively. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\{\omega_{n}(e)e\}_{e\in E}=\omega_{*}
in the weak topology on flows, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\omega_{*}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{s}_{n}=-\omega_{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu^{n}_{i},\nu^{n}_{i},\mu_{i}
denote the
i^{th}
component measures corresponding to
\omega_{n},s_{n},\omega_{*}
respectively. Let
A_{1},\cdots,A_{M}
be the Voronoi cells of the white vertices in
R_{n}\cap W
. Note that
M=Cn^{d}
for some constant
C>0
. These sets have diameter
C^{\prime}/n
for some other constant
C^{\prime}
, and
\mu_{i}^{n}(A_{k})=\nu_{i}^{n}(A_{k})
for all
i,k
. Hence by Lemma
3.6
,
d_{W}(\omega_{n},s_{n})=O(1/n)
. Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\omega_{*}
.

For
\tilde{s}_{n}
, instead sum over the Voronoi cells of black vertices. On these,
-\tilde{s}_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
have the same total flow and we repeat the argument above.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.9
.

Take a convergent subsequence
\{\omega_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
and let
\omega_{*}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\omega_{n_{k}}
. By Lemma
3.10
,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{W}(s_{n_{k}},\omega_{*})=0.

Again let
A_{1},\dots,A_{M}
denote the Voronoi cells of the white vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{M}
in
W\cap R_{n}
. Note that these all have volume
1/n^{d}
. Define the piecewise-constant flow
\gamma_{n}:R\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is constant on each Voronoi cell
A_{m}
, with value (for
x\in A_{m}
)

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(x):=s_{n}(u_{m})\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda).

By Lemma
3.6
,
d_{W}(\gamma_{n},s_{n})=O(1/n)
, and hence

\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{W}(\gamma_{n_{k}},\omega_{*})=0.

Since
\gamma_{n_{k}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure for all
k
, by Lemma
3.8
,
\omega_{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Further since
\gamma_{n_{k}}
is valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
for all
k
, since convergence in
d_{W}
implies weak convergence,
\omega_{*}
is also valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
as a distribution.

To see that
\omega_{*}
is divergence-free, we look at another approximating sequence. Recall that
D
is the degree of a vertex in
\Lambda
and define
\overline{\omega}_{n}(e)=\omega_{n}(e)-1/D
for all edges
e
oriented white-to-black. Then we also have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{W}(\overline{\omega}_{n_{k}},\omega_{*})=0.

The measures
\overline{\mu}_{n}^{i}
corresponding to
\overline{\omega}_{n}
are divergence-free as distributions on
R^{\circ}
for any
n
, i.e., for any smooth function
\phi
compactly supported in
R^{\circ}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{R}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\overline{\mu}_{i}^{n}=0.
(See
[
CSW23
, Proposition 5.2.6]
; this can be seen by applying the fundamental theorem of calculus along each edge and then using that
\sum_{e\ni u}\overline{\omega}_{n}(e)=0
for all
u\in R_{n}\cap R^{\circ}
.) Since convergence in
d_{W}
implies weak convergence of the component measures, (
3.5
) implies that
\omega_{*}
is divergence free in the interior of
R
.

Therefore for any subsequential limit
\omega_{*}
we have
\omega_{*}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

∎

Boundary conditions play an important role in the large deviation principle. If
\omega_{n}\to\omega
in the weak topology on flows, then the corresponding boundary values must converge to the boundary value of
\omega
. We use the notion of

boundary value operator

T
from
[
CSW23
, Section 5]
to define boundary values, but streamline the description.

The boundary value
T(\omega)
of
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
on
\partial R
is the flux of
\omega
through
\partial R
. Let
\text{AF}^{\infty}(\Lambda,R)\subset\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
denote smooth asymptotic flows and let
\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote signed Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 3.11

(See
[
CSW23
, Definition 5.4.1]
)
.

Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact region with piecewise smooth boundary
\partial R
. We define the
boundary value operator

T:(\text{AF}^{\infty}(\Lambda,R),d_{W})\to(\mathcal{M}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1})
by

\displaystyle T(\omega):=\langle\omega(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathds{1}_{\partial R}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle\omega(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\partial R}(x),

where
\xi
denotes the
L^{2}
unit normal vector to
\partial R
and
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\partial R}
is the surface area measure on
\partial R
.

By
[
CSW23
, Proposition 5.4.6]
,
T(\cdot)
extends to a uniformly continuous map on
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
, and further for any
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
,
T(\omega)
is a signed measure absolutely continuous with the surface area measure
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\partial R}
with density taking bounded values determined by
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. In fact
[
CSW23
, Proposition 5.4.6]
shows that for any piecewise-smooth
(d-1)
-dimensional surface
S\subset R
, the analogously defined operator
T(\omega,S):=\langle\omega(x),\xi(x)\rangle\mathds{1}_{S}(x)\mathbb{\mathrm{d}}x
extends to a continuous map on
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

We say that
b
is a
boundary asymptotic flow
for
\Lambda,R
if there exists
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
such that
T(\omega)=b
. Uniform continuity of
T
and the compactness of
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
then give the following corollary.

Corollary 3.12
.

If
b
is a boundary asymptotic flow for
\Lambda,R
, then we define
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b):=T^{-1}(b)\subset\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
. The metric space
(\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b),d_{W})
is compact.

The natural notion of boundary condition that we consider on a graph
R_{n}
consists of specifying the vertex multiplicity
\beta_{n}(u)=\sum_{e\ni u}\omega_{n}(u)\in(0,1]
at each
u\in\partial R_{n}
. As a measure, after rescaling this is:

(3.6)

\displaystyle b_{n}=\sum_{u\in\partial R_{n}}\text{sign}(u)\beta_{n}(u)\frac{\mathds{1}_{u}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{2n^{d-1}},

where
\text{sign}(u)=1
if
u\in W
and
\text{sign}(u)=-1
if
u\in B
. The factor of
1/2
comes from the fact that we are summing over white and black vertices. We show that the notion of boundary value operator for asymptotic flows above can be extended by continuity to this case. The main idea is to bound the
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}
distance between the discrete boundary value in (
3.6
) and
T(\omega)
using the
d_{W}
distance between
\omega_{n}
and
\omega
in a thin neighborhood of
\partial R
. To simplify the proof (and since this assumption is also needed in Section

) we assume that the continuum boundary condition
b
is
extendable outside
, i.e. that there exists
\lambda>0
such that
b
extends to a divergence-free flow in a
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact with piecewise smooth boundary, and let
b
be a boundary asymptotic flow which is extendable outside. Let
\omega
be an asymptotic flow with
T(\omega)=b
and suppose that
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
approximates
R
as above, and
\omega_{n}
is a discrete flow on
R_{n}
. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\omega_{n}=\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
in the weak topology on flows, then

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(b_{n},b)=0

with
b_{n}
from (
3.6
) and
\beta_{n}(u)=\sum_{e\ni u}\omega_{n}(e)
as above.

Proof.

Assume that
b
is extendable outside to a
\lambda
neighborhood of
R.

By a mollification argument, we can without loss of generality assume that
\omega
is continuous. To see this, first let
\omega
also denote a divergence-free extension of itself to the
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
. Then for any
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
we can define

\displaystyle\omega_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{1}{|B_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)|}\int_{B_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

for
x\in R
. This is divergence-free and continuous for any
0<\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
, and converges to
\omega
as
\lambda^{\prime}
goes to
0
. Since
T
is uniformly continuous on
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
,
\lim_{\lambda^{\prime}\to 0}\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(T(\omega_{\lambda^{\prime}}),T(\omega))=0.
For the remainder of the proof we assume that
\omega
is continuous.

Since
\partial R
is compact and piecewise smooth, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can partition it into
K=O(\varepsilon^{d-1})
smooth patches with diameter of order
\varepsilon
, and perimeter of order
\varepsilon^{d-2}
. We denote these by
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K}
. Let
\xi(x)
denote the normal vector to
\partial R
at
x
. Since
\partial R
is piecewise smooth and compact, by Taylor expansion it follows that there is a constant
c_{0}>0
independent of
i
such that
|\xi(x)-\xi(y)|<{c_{0}\varepsilon^{2}}
for all
x,y\in\pi_{i}
. For
\varepsilon
small enough, we can therefore find hyperplane patches
P_{1},\dots,P_{K}
such that (i)
P_{i}
approximates
\pi_{i}
in Hausdorff distance and (ii)
P_{i}
is contained in the
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
. Without loss of generality, we let
\omega
denote a divergence-free extension of
\omega
to the
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
. We define

(3.8)

\displaystyle b^{i}(x)=\langle\omega(x),\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\mathds{1}_{P_{i}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the surface area measure on
P_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
denotes the normal vector to
P_{i}
(with the same orientation as
\pi_{i}\subset\partial R
, which is oriented outward). Since the perimeter of
\pi_{i}
is of order
\varepsilon^{d-2}
, and since the distance between
P_{i}
and
\pi_{i}
is at most a constant times
\varepsilon^{2}
, the divergence theorem implies that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{P_{i}}b^{i}-\int_{\pi_{i}}b\bigg|<(c_{0}\varepsilon^{2})(c_{1}\varepsilon^{d-2})={c\varepsilon^{d}}

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
for some constant
c>0
. The order
\varepsilon^{d}
comes from the surface area of the “band” needed to make
P_{i}\cup\pi_{i}
into a closed surface, which has volume proportional to the perimeter of
\pi_{i}
(order
\varepsilon^{d-2}
) times the distance between
\pi_{i}
and
P_{i}
(order
\varepsilon^{2}
).

We further let
\pi_{i}^{n}\subset\partial R_{n}
be discrete patches of
\partial R_{n}
which approximate
\pi_{i}
(and hence also
P_{i}
) in Hausdorff distance. By Lemma
3.6
, if there is
\delta>0
such that for all
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}}b_{n}-\int_{\pi_{i}}b\bigg|<\delta,

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(b_{n},b)<K(10\varepsilon^{d}+\delta).

Using (
3.9
), to show (
3.10
), we can replace the surface
\pi_{i}
with the plane
P_{i}
, and it suffices to show that for all
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and some
\delta>0
(smaller than
1/K=O(\varepsilon^{d-1})
), we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}}b_{n}-\int_{P_{i}}b^{i}\bigg|<\delta.

We now fix
i
, and achieve (
3.11
) by relating it to the distance between
\omega_{n}
and
\omega
. Fix a small constant
t_{0}>0
. For any
t
, we define
P_{i}(t)=P_{i}-t\xi_{i}
to be this plane translated distance
t
inward by its normal vector. We can choose
t_{0}
small enough so that all these planes are still contained in the
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
. We then define
U_{i}=\cup_{t=0}^{t_{0}}P_{i}(t)
, and
U_{i}(t)=\cup_{s=0}^{t}P_{i}(s)
. Since
\omega
is divergence free, for all
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
the divergence theorem implies that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{P_{i}}b^{i}-\int_{P_{i}(t)}\langle\omega(x),\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}(t)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|=\bigg|\int_{P_{i}}\langle\omega(x),\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{P_{i}(t)}\langle\omega(x),\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}(t)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|<Ct_{0}.

Here we use that
\xi_{i}
on
P_{i}
, and
-\xi_{i}
on
P_{i}(t)
, are outward unit normal vectors to
\partial U_{i}(t)
, and
C
is a constant such that
|\partial U_{i}\setminus(P_{i}\cup P_{i}(t))|<Ct_{0}
for all
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
.
Averaging (
3.12
) over
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
gives that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{P_{i}}b^{i}-\frac{1}{t_{0}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{P_{i}(t)}\langle\omega,\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}(t)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|<Ct_{0}.

Next we derive a similar bound for the discrete flows. Let
U_{i}^{n}\subset R_{n}
be lattice sets approximating
U_{i}
in Hausdorff distance, where
U_{i}^{n}\cap\partial R_{n}=\pi_{i}^{n}
. We can then partition
U_{i}^{n}
into slices, where each slice is a discrete approximation of one of the plans
P_{i}(t)
for some
t
. We take values
t_{1},\dots,t_{L}=t_{0}
, for
L
approximately equal to
t_{0}n
, and define
\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
to be a discrete approximation of the plane
P_{i}(t_{k})
, such that all vertices in
U_{i}^{n}
are contained in
\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
for exactly one value of
k
. We further define
U_{i}^{n}(t_{k})=\cup_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k^{\prime}})
. The boundary value of
\omega_{n}
restricted to
\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
is

\displaystyle b_{n}(t)=\sum_{u\in\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})}\frac{1}{2n^{d-1}}\text{sign}(u)\beta_{n}^{t_{k}}(u)\mathds{1}_{u}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where (i)
\text{sign}(u)
is
+1
if
u\in W
and
-1
if
u\in B
and (ii)
\beta_{n}^{t_{k}}(u)
is the sum of
\omega_{n}(e)
over edges
e=(u,v)
incident to
u\in\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
with
v\not\in U_{i}^{n}(t_{k})\setminus\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
.

The total divergence of
\omega_{n}
on
U_{i}^{n}
is
O(1/n)
. We note that since all vertices have the same degree
D
, and same collection of incident edge directions
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
, there is an upper bound on the Euclidean distance between vertices connected by a single edge. As such, for any
k
, the number of edges which cross laterally (i.e., out the sides of
\partial U_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
which are not one of the slices) is at most of order
t_{k}n^{d-1}
(to be precise these are the edges through
\partial U_{i}^{n}(t_{k})\setminus(\pi_{i}^{n}\cup\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k}))
). Therefore taking
C
possibly larger, the divergence theorem implies that for all
k
,
0\leq t_{k}\leq t_{0}
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}}b_{n}-\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})}b_{n}(t_{k})\bigg|<Ct_{k}n^{d-1}/n^{d-1}<Ct_{k}<Ct_{0}.

Therefore

(3.15)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}}b_{n}-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})}b_{n}(t_{k})\bigg|<Ct_{0}+O(1/n).

Combining (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) gives

(3.16)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{P_{i}}b^{i}-\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}}b_{n}\bigg|<2Ct_{0}+t_{0}^{-1}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{P_{i}(t)}\langle\omega,\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}(t)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})}b_{n}(t_{k})\bigg|.

Let
\mu=(\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{d})
and
\mu_{n}=(\mu_{n}^{1},\dots,\mu_{n}^{d})
be the measures corresponding to
\omega,\omega_{n}
respectively (recall that
\mu^{m}=\omega_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
). Then splitting the integral into components, we have

(3.17)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{P_{i}(t)}\langle\omega,\xi_{i}\rangle\mathds{1}_{P_{i}(t)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\xi_{i}^{m}\int_{U_{i}}\mu^{m}.

Recall that
\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})
is a discrete approximation of a hyperplane
P_{i}(t_{k})
. The divergence theorem implies that any two approximations have the same flux, up to an error of order
O(\varepsilon^{d})+O(1/n)
(analogous to the bound in (
3.9
)). Therefore

(3.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\int_{\pi_{i}^{n}(t_{k})}b_{n}(t_{k})=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\xi_{i}^{m}\int_{U_{i}}\mu_{n}^{m}+O(\varepsilon^{d})+O(1/n).

Combining (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) with (
3.16
) and applying the fact that
d_{W}(\omega_{n},\omega)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
gives the bound (
3.11
) with
\delta=O(\varepsilon^{d})
, and completes the proof.
∎

3.5.
Comparison with sup norm topology

Here we prove a straightforward comparison result that for discrete vector fields corresponding to
N
-dimer covers of the same finite graph
G=(V,E)
, the weak topology on flows is equivalent to the sup norm topology on edges.

Proposition 3.14
.

Suppose that
G=(V,E)\subset\Lambda
is a finite subgraph. Then a sequence of discrete flows
(\omega_{k}(e))_{e\in E}
converges to a discrete flow
(\omega(e))_{e\in E}
as
k\to\infty
in the sup norm topology on
E
if and only if it converges to
(\omega(e))_{e\in E}
in the weak topology on flows.

Proof.

First suppose that
\omega_{k}\to\omega
in the sup norm topology on edges. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
k
large enough such that

\displaystyle|\omega_{k}(e)-\omega(e)|<\varepsilon\qquad\forall e\in E.

To bound the Wasserstein distance
d_{W}
, it suffices to show that there is a way to redistribute, add, or delete flow to transform
\omega_{k}
into
\omega
. In particular one way to redistribute would be to just add or delete the total flow difference on every edge
e\in E
. This gives the bound:

\displaystyle d_{W}(\omega_{k},\omega)<d|E|\varepsilon,

where
d
is the dimension of
\Lambda
. Since
|E|
is finite this completes the first direction.

Next we assume that
d_{W}(\omega_{k},\omega)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Let
(\mu_{1}^{k},\dots,\mu_{d}^{k})
be the measures corresponding to
\omega_{k}
and let
(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{d})
be the measures corresponding to
\omega
. For any
\varepsilon>0
there is
k
large enough such that
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu_{i}^{k},\mu_{i})<\varepsilon/d=\delta
for all
i=1,\dots,d
.

For all
i
the support of
\mu_{i}^{k},\mu_{i}
is contained in the edges
E
. Since
E
are embedded so that they intersect only at the vertices of
\Lambda
, we can partition the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
covered by
G
into cells
B_{e}
, each of which contains one edge
e\in E
. Each cell contains one edge
e
, on which
\mu_{i}^{k},\mu_{i}
both take constant values. Therefore
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(\mu_{i}^{k}\mid_{B_{e}},\mu_{i}\mid_{B_{e}})\leq 1/\ell_{i}(e)|\omega_{k}(e)-\omega(e)|
. Summing over
i
and applying Lemma
3.7
to add together the contributions from each cell completes the proof.
∎

4.
Free energy and surface tension

4.1.
Measures on the torus

Let
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{T}}(n,\Lambda)=(V_{n},E_{n})
denote the cubic torus in the
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
:
{\mathbb{T}}=\Lambda/n\Lambda
.
It contains
2n^{d}
vertices,
n^{d}
white and
n^{d}
black. Recall that
\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})
is the set of single
dimer covers of the
N
-fold blow-up
{\mathbb{T}}_{N}
of
{\mathbb{T}}
. (And recall that this is not the same as
\Omega_{N}({\mathbb{T}})
, the set of
N
-dimer covers of
{\mathbb{T}}
.)

The graph
{\mathbb{T}}
has degree
D
at all vertices, with edges connecting
v
to
v+e_{i}
for
i=1\dots,D
for each white vertex
v
. We fix edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}>0
for edge types
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

We let
\mu_{n,N,w}:=\mu_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})
denote the associated Boltzmann measure on
\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})
.
Its partition function is

(4.1)

Z_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})=\sum_{\tau\in\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})}\prod_{e\in\pi(\tau)=M}w_{e}^{M_{e}}=\sum_{M\in\Omega_{N}({\mathbb{T}})}|\pi^{-1}(M)|\prod_{e\in{\mathbb{T}}}w_{e}^{M_{e}},

In order to study the asymptotics as
n,N\to\infty
, we need to renormalize the partition function. This does not change the corresponding probability measures
\mu_{n,N,w}
. In fact we study the asymptotics of

(4.2)

\frac{Z_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})}{(N!)^{n^{d}}}.

This normalization is analogous to ones used for random permutations, see e.g.
[
KKRW20
]
,
[
BDMW23
]
.

Remark 4.1
.

To see that (
4.2
) is the correct normalization, consider the weight function where
w_{1}=1
and
w_{i}=0
for
i\neq 1
. Projected to a measure on
\Omega_{N}(\mathbb{T})
,
\mu_{n,N}(1,0,\dots,0)
samples only one configuration with probability
1
, namely the
N
-dimer cover where every edge in the
w_{1}
direction is in the collection with multiplicity
N
, and all other edges are not occupied. On the other hand, there are
(N!)^{n^{d}}
ways to lift this configuration to a dimer cover of the blow-up graph, corresponding to choosing a permutation of
N
for each edge
e
in the collection.

The critical edge weights for these measures are very simple.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
n
and
\Lambda
. As
N\to\infty
, the critical edges weights on
\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
are precisely
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
.

Proof.

Recall that the tiling polynomial with weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
is
P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E_{n}}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}
. The critical gauge function
(x_{v})_{v\in V_{n}}
solves the family of equations:

\displaystyle\sum_{u:e=(u,v)\in E_{n}}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}=\frac{1}{n^{d}}P(\mathbf{x})\qquad\forall v\in V.

Since the graph is transitive (on white vertices or black vertices),
x_{v}\equiv 1
for all
v\in V
solves the critical gauge equations.
∎

It follows that the asymptotic behavior of samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
is determined by
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
p_{i}:=\frac{w_{i}}{w_{1}+\dots w_{D}}
. As
N\to\infty
with
n
fixed, samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
concentrate on the discrete flow where
\omega_{n}(e)=p_{i}
for all edges
e
of type
e_{i}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.5
, samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
concentrate as
N\to\infty
on a discrete vector field
\omega_{n}
proportional to the critical edge weights, where the constant is determined by the constraint that the divergences are
\pm 1
(if white, black respectively) at all vertices. Hence by Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(e)=p_{i}\qquad\text{ for all }e\text{ of type }e_{i}.

∎

Remark 4.4
.

By Proposition
3.14
, this is equivalent to samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
converging to
\omega_{n}(e)
in the weak topology on flows as
N\to\infty
, so if we further take the scaling limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\omega_{n}
, we see that samples concentrate on the constant flow
s:=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}e_{i}\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

4.2.
Free energy

Here we explicitly compute the free energy per dimer of the torus measures
\mu_{n,N,w}
in the iterated limit as
N
and then
n
go to infinity. This computation is remarkably simple and depends minimally on the lattice
\Lambda
. Note that
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{T}}(n,\Lambda)
has
2n^{d}
vertices, so an
N
-dimer cover of
{\mathbb{T}}
contains
Nn^{d}
edges, counted with multiplicity.

Theorem 4.5
.

The free energy per dimer of
\mu_{n,N,w}=\mu_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})
is

F(w_{1},...,w_{D}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})}{(N!)^{n^{d}}}=\log(w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}).

Proof.

This proof is an application of the results of
[
KP22a
]
stated in Section
2.3
. Let
P_{n}(\mathbf{x})
denote the tiling polynomial for
\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
with periodic weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
, namely

P_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E_{n}}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}.

By Theorem
2.2
,

(4.3)

\frac{Z_{n,N}(w_{1},\dots,w_{D})}{(N!)^{n^{d}}}=\frac{[\mathbf{x}^{N}]P_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{Nn^{d}}}{(Nn^{d})!}

where the notation
[\mathbf{x}^{N}]Q
denotes the coefficient of
\mathbf{x}^{N}=\prod_{v\in V}x_{v}^{N}
in
Q
. We can extract the
\mathbf{x}^{N}
coefficient in
P_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{Nn^{d}}
using a contour integral:

[\mathbf{x}^{N}]P_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{Nn^{d}}=\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\bigg)^{n^{d}}\int_{(S^{1})^{n^{d}}}\frac{P_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{Nn^{d}}}{\prod_{v\in V_{n}}x_{v}^{N}}\,\prod_{v\in V_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{v}}{x_{v}}.

As
N\to\infty
with
n
fixed, the method of steepest descent says that this integral is approximated by the value at the extreme points of the integrand. In other words, by its value at
(x_{v})_{v\in V_{n}}
satisfying:

(4.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{v}}\bigg(Nn^{d}\log P_{n}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{v\in V_{n}}N\log x_{v}\bigg)=0\qquad\forall\;v\in V_{n}.

Note that for any
v\in V_{n}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{v}}P_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{u:e=(u,v)\in E_{n}}w_{e}x_{u}.

Since
\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
has homogeneous vertex degree
D
, this is a sum over
D
terms for any
n
and
v\in V_{n}
. Hence Equation (
4.4
) can be rewritten:

(4.5)

\sum_{u:e=(u,v)\in E_{n}}w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}=\frac{1}{n^{d}}P_{n}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\forall\;v\in V_{n}.

These are exactly the critical gauge equations (
2.3
). By Lemma
4.2
, for any
n
,
\mathbf{x}\equiv 1
solves the family of equations in (
4.5
), and determines the unique critical edge weight solution
w_{e}x_{u}x_{v}=w_{e}
for all
e\in E_{n}
. Therefore

(4.6)

[\mathbf{x}^{N}]P_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{Nn^{d}}\approx P_{n}(\text{{1}})^{Nn^{d}}\qquad\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Further, since the graph is transitive,

(4.7)

P_{n}(\text{{1}})=\sum_{e\in E_{n}}w_{e}=n^{d}(w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}).

We can now compute the desired limit. Combining Equations (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) with Equation (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle F(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\bigg[(N!)^{n^{d}}\frac{P_{n}(\text{{1}})^{Nn^{d}}}{(Nn^{d})!}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[n^{d}N\log N-n^{d}N-Nn^{d}\log Nn^{d}+Nn^{d}+Nn^{d}\log P_{n}(\text{{1}})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigg[-\log n^{d}+\log\bigg(n^{d}(w_{1}+\dots+w_{D})\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\log(w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}).

∎

4.3.
Surface tension

Let
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional unit cubic torus. If
\Lambda
is a
d
-dimensional lattice,
then the
graphs
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{T}}(n,\Lambda)
are lattice approximations of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. For
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, the surface tension
\sigma(s)
is defined (below) to be the asymptotic growth rate of the normalized partition function of
N
-dimer covers of
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
which approximate flow
s
in the
n,N\to\infty
limit.

To isolate the set of
N
-dimer covers
M
of
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
such that
\omega_{M}
is close to
s
as
n,N\to\infty
,
we use the fact that the the torus has non-trivial (co)homology
H_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{d-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. A divergence-free discrete flow
\omega
corresponds to a closed
(d-1)
-form, so it has a homology class
[\omega]\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The discrete flow
\omega_{M}
has divergence
\pm 1
at all white, black vertices respectively. The modified flow, where we subtract a constant from all edges,
\overline{\omega}_{M}(e)=\omega_{M}(e)-1/D
, is divergence free and therefore has a homology class. On the other hand, since
\Lambda
is harmonically embedded, this subtraction does not change the slope per white vertex function:

(4.8)

\displaystyle s_{M}(u)=\sum_{e\ni u}\omega_{M}(e)e=\sum_{e\ni u}\overline{\omega}_{M}(e)e.

Definition 4.6
.

Fix a slope
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Recall that
\pi
is the projection map from dimer covers of the
N
-fold blow-up of a graph
G
to
N
-dimer covers of
G
. We define the set of dimer covers
\tau
such that the projection
\pi(\tau)
(i.e., an
N
-dimer cover of
G
) has a particular homology class approximating the slope
s
:

\Omega_{N,s}=\{\tau\in\Omega_{N}:[N\overline{\omega}_{\pi(\tau)}]=[\lfloor n^{d-1}Ns_{1}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor n^{d-1}Ns_{d}\rfloor]\}.

Equivalently, by (
4.8
),
s
is the slope per white vertex over all of
\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
.

From this, we can define probability measures
\mu_{n,N,w}^{s}
restricted to a homology class
s
. The (unnormalized) partition function of this measure is

Z_{n,N,w}^{s}=\sum_{\tau\in\Omega_{N,s}}\prod_{e\in\pi(\tau)=M}w_{e}^{M_{e}}.

When
w_{e}\equiv 1
, we denote this by just
Z_{n,N}^{s}
. The surface tension is the asymptotic growth rate of these homology-class-restricted partition functions as
n,N\to\infty
, suitably normalized like the free energy.

Definition 4.7
.

The
surface tension

\sigma:\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is given by

(4.9)

\sigma(s):=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N}^{s}}{(N!)^{n^{d}}}.

It is straightforward to check that
\log(Z_{n,N}^{s}/(N!)^{n^{d}})
forms as subadditive sequence in
n,N
, so
\sigma(s)
exists for any fixed
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

Remark 4.8
.

Note that the edge weights in the surface tension definition are all equal to one. The surface tension can also be defined analogously with non-constant weights.

4.4.
Entropy

To relate the surface tension to the free energy, we first relate the free energy to entropy. This will allow us to infer some key properties of
\sigma
and provide a method to compute it explicitly. The arguments here mirror the ones for standard two-dimensional dimers in
[
CKP01
]
, but the variance bound on the number of tiles of each type is replaced by Lemma
4.9
.

For finite
n,N
,
\mu_{n,N,w}
is a probability measure on the finite set
\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})
of dimer covers of the
N
-fold blow-up of
\mathbb{T}(n,\Lambda)
. The Shannon entropy of
{\mu}_{n,N,w}
is

H(\mu_{n,N,w})=-\sum_{\tau\in\Omega(n,N)}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\log{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau).

The key lemma is the following strengthening of Proposition
4.3
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
k_{i}
denote the number of tiles on edges of type
e_{i}
, and let
\overline{k}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n,N,w}}[k_{i}]
. There exists a constant
c=c(w)>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
N
large enough given
n,\varepsilon
,

(4.10)

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n,N,w}}(|k_{i}-\overline{k}_{i}|>\varepsilon\overline{k}_{i})\leq\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}N/cn^{d}).

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
, for any fixed
n
, samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
concentrate on the discrete vector field where
\omega_{n}(e)=p_{i}=\frac{w_{i}}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}
if
e
is of type
e_{i}
and hence has weight
w_{i}
.

Fix
n
and
\varepsilon>0
. By
[
KP22a
, Theorem 5.1]
, for
n
fixed and
M
sampled from
\mu_{n,N,w}
, the fluctuations of
(M_{e})_{e\in E}
around the limiting configuration as
N\to\infty
are jointly Gaussian with covariances that can be computed explicitly in terms of the edge weights and are proportional to
Nn^{d}
. Hence there is
c=c(w)>0
such that for all edges
e
,

\displaystyle\text{Var}(M_{e})\leq cNn^{d}

Thus,
\omega_{M}(e)=M_{e}/N
is approximately a Gaussian with variances bounded by
cn^{d}/N
for
N
large. Hence if
e
has edge weight
w_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n,N,w}}\bigg(\bigg|\omega_{M}(e)-p_{i}\bigg|>\varepsilon\bigg)<\exp\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}N}{2cn^{d}}\bigg).

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n,N,w}}(|k_{i}-\overline{k}_{i}|>\varepsilon\overline{k}_{i})\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n,N,w}}\bigg(\bigg|\omega_{M}(e)-p_{i}\bigg|>\varepsilon\bigg)

the result follows.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

The limiting entropy per dimer for
\mu_{n,N,w}
exists as
n,N\to\infty
and is given by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[H(\mu_{n,N,w})-n^{d}\log N!\bigg]=F(w)-\frac{1}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}\log w_{i}.

Remark 4.11
.

The additive correction term on the left hand side is the same normalization as in the free energy and surface tension, and is necessary for the limit to be finite.

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
4.5
that
F(w)=\lim_{n,N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}[\log Z_{n,N,w}-n^{d}\log N!]
. Here
Z_{n,N,w}
is the unnormalized partition function of
\mu_{n,N,w}
. We can rewrite

Z_{n,N,w}=\sum_{\tau\in\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})}\prod_{e\in\pi(\tau)=M}w_{e}^{M_{e}}=\sum_{\tau\in\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})}\prod_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{k_{i}(\tau)},

where
k_{i}(\tau)
is the number of tiles of type
e_{i}
in
\tau
. Letting
C(k_{1},\dots,k_{D})
be the number of dimer covers in

\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N})
with
k_{1},\dots,k_{D}
tiles of types
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
respectively, we have that

Z_{n,N,w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{D}\\
\sum k_{i}=Nn^{d}\end{subarray}}C(k_{1},\dots,k_{D})\prod_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{k_{i}}.

By Lemma
4.9
, for
N
large enough given
\varepsilon,n
, the set

U_{\varepsilon}=\{k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{D}):|k_{i}-\overline{k}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon\overline{k}_{i},\;i=1,\dots,D\}

has
\mu_{n,N,w}(U_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}N/cn^{d})
for some constant
c>0
. We can take this to be arbitrarily close to
1
by taking
N
larger with
\varepsilon,n
fixed. Therefore given
n
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, for
N
large enough we have

\sum_{k\in U_{\varepsilon}}C(k_{1},\dots,k_{D})\prod_{i-1}^{N}w_{i}^{k_{i}}=(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})Z_{n,N,w}.

Let
V_{\varepsilon}=\{\tau\in\Omega_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{N}):(k_{1}(\tau),\dots,k_{D}(\tau))\in U_{\varepsilon}\}
. Then

\displaystyle H(\mu_{n,N,w})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\log{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)+\sum_{\tau\not\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\log{\mu}_{n,N}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\log\bigg[\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{k_{i}}}{Z_{n,N,w}}\bigg]+O(Nn^{d}{\varepsilon^{\prime}\log\varepsilon^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\bigg[\log Z_{n,N,w}-\log\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{\overline{k}_{i}}w_{i}^{k_{i}-\overline{k}_{i}}\bigg)\bigg]+O(Nn^{d}{\varepsilon^{\prime}\log\varepsilon^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\bigg[\log Z_{n,N,w}-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\overline{k}_{i}\log w_{i}+(k_{i}-\overline{k_{i}})\log w_{i}\bigg]+O(Nn^{d}{\varepsilon^{\prime}\log\varepsilon^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\log Z_{n,N,w}-\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N,w}(\tau)\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{D}{Nn^{d}}p_{i}(n,N)\log w_{i}+(k_{i}-\overline{k_{i}})\log w_{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\;\;+O(Nn^{d}{\varepsilon^{\prime}\log\varepsilon^{\prime}}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[H(\mu_{n,N,w})-n^{d}\log N!\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigg[(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N,w}}{(N!)^{n^{d}}}-\sum_{\tau\in V_{\varepsilon}}{\mu}_{n,N}(\tau)\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(n,N)\log w_{i}+\varepsilon p_{i}(n,N)\log w_{i}\bigg)

\displaystyle+O(\varepsilon^{\prime}\log\varepsilon^{\prime})+\varepsilon^{\prime}\log N\bigg].

Since
\varepsilon^{\prime}\sim\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}N/cn^{d})
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}\log N\to 0
as
N\to\infty
with
n
fixed. Thus applying Theorem
4.5
and Lemma
4.9
gives

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[H(\mu_{n,N,w})-n^{d}\log N!\bigg]=F(w)-\frac{1}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}\log w_{i}+O(\varepsilon).

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
completes the proof.
∎

4.5.
Gauge equivalence and Legendre duality

To relate
F
to the Legendre dual of
\sigma
, we have to write
F
as a function of
d
“magnetic field” variables
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
.
Recall that
e_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the vector representing the edge of type
e_{j}
. For
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
, we define the weight function
on edges of type
e_{j}
to be:

(4.11)

\displaystyle w_{j}(\alpha):=\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha).

We slightly abuse notation by writing
F(\alpha):=F(w_{1}(\alpha),\dots,w_{D}(\alpha))
.

Recall from the background section that two edge weight functions
w_{e},w_{e}^{\prime}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
are gauge equivalent if there exists a function
\mathbf{x}:V\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle w_{e}^{\prime}=x_{u}x_{v}w_{e}\qquad\forall e=(u,v)\in E.

The weights in (
4.11
) are not gauge equivalent to the uniform weights on the whole torus. This is a natural choice, however, because they are gauge equivalent to the uniform edge weights if we condition on a choice of homology class. Both of these claims can be seen from the following characterization of edge weights gauge equivalent to the uniform weights.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite bipartite graph. The weight function
\mathbf{w}:E\to\mathbb{R}
is gauge equivalent to the uniform weight function if and only if for every loop
\gamma
of
G
, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle w_{e_{1}}w_{e_{3}}\dots w_{e_{2k-1}}=w_{e_{2}}w_{e_{4}}\dots w_{e_{2k}}

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{2k}
are the edges around
\gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
are gauge equivalent to the uniform weights, and let
\mathbf{x}:V\to\mathbb{R}
denote the change of gauge. Then both sides of (
4.12
) are equal to
\prod_{v\in V}x_{v}
.

Conversely suppose (
4.12
) holds. First assume that
G=\gamma
is a single cycle, and label its vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{2k}
in cyclic order, where
e_{i}=(v_{i},v_{i+1})
. We define a function
\mathbf{x}
such that
x_{v_{1}}=1
, and

\displaystyle x_{v_{i+1}}=w_{e_{i}}x_{v_{i}}^{-1}

for
i=1,\dots,2k-1
. This changes the weights on
e_{1},\dots,e_{2k-1}
to
w_{e_{1}},\dots,w_{e_{2k-1}}
and it suffices to check the corresponding weight on
e_{2k}
. This is

\displaystyle x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2k}}=1\cdot w_{e_{2k-1}}x_{v_{2k-1}}^{-1}=w_{e_{2k-1}}w_{e_{2k-2}}^{-1}x_{v_{2k-2}}=\frac{w_{e_{2k-1}}\dots w_{e_{1}}}{w_{e_{2k-2}}\dots w_{e_{2}}}

which is equal to
w_{e_{2k}}
by (
4.12
). If
G
is not just a single loop, then we iterate this construction.
∎

The weights in (
4.11
) satisfy the condition in Lemma
4.12
on contractible loops, but not on loops with non-trivial homology class. As such, the probability measures
\mu_{n,N}
and
\mu_{n,N,w}
are different (as one could infer e.g. from Proposition
4.3
). Instead, let
\mu_{n,N}^{s},\mu_{n,N,w}^{s}
denote these measures restricted to dimer covers of homology class
s
. Then since any two dimer covers of the same homology class differ by only contractible loops, Lemma
4.12
implies that
\mu_{n,N}^{s}
and
\mu_{n,N,w}^{s}
are the same probability measure.

Theorem 4.13
.

Fix
\Lambda
. Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{D}
be periodic edge weights satisfying (
4.12
) for contractible loops, and suppose that
s=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}e_{i}
, where
p_{i}=\frac{w_{i}}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}
. Then the surface tension is minus the entropy of
\mu_{n,N,w}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma(s)=\frac{1}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}\log w_{i}-F(w).

Proof.

Recall that
\sigma(s)
is given by:

\displaystyle\sigma(s)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[\log{Z_{n,N}^{s}}-n^{d}\log N!\bigg].

By Lemma
4.12
, the weights
w
are gauge equivalent to the uniform weights if we restrict to a homology class. Therefore there exist constants
C_{n,N}(w)
such that

\displaystyle Z_{n,N,w}^{s}=C_{n,N}(w)Z_{n,N}^{s}.

Since
s
satisfies
s=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}e_{i}
, by Proposition
4.3
, the constant
C_{n,N}(w)
are asymptotic to
\prod_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}^{p_{i}Nn^{d}}
, and
Z_{n,N,w}^{s}/Z_{n,N,w}\to 1
in the iterated limit as
N\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sigma(s)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[\log\frac{Z_{n,N,w}}{C_{n,N}(w)}-n^{d}\log N!\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\bigg[\log{Z_{n,N,w}}-Nn^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}\log w_{i}-n^{d}\log N!\bigg]

\displaystyle=-F(w)+\frac{1}{w_{1}+\dots+w_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}\log w_{i}.

∎

Using the magnetic field weights of (
4.11
), we show that the surface tension is the Legendre dual of the free energy.

Theorem 4.14
.

Fix
\Lambda
. Then
\sigma(s)
is the Legendre dual of
F(\alpha)
, i.e. for all
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
,

(4.13)

\sigma(s)=\max_{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}-F(\alpha)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\alpha_{i}.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.5
and the prescription in (
4.11
),

\displaystyle F(\alpha)=\log\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)\bigg).

Since
F
is strictly convex and differentiable, the maximum value in (
4.13
) is realized at
\alpha
such that
s=\nabla F(\alpha)
. We compute:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha_{i}}(\alpha)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{D}\langle e_{j},\eta_{i}\rangle\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)}{\sum_{k=1}^{D}\exp(e_{k}\cdot\alpha)}\implies\nabla F(\alpha)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)e_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{D}\exp(e_{k}\cdot\alpha)}.

On the other hand plugging the weights
w_{j}=\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)
in to the expression in Corollary
4.13
, where
s=\sum_{j=1}^{D}p_{j}e_{j}
,
p_{j}=w_{j}/(w_{1}+\dots+w_{d})
, gives

\displaystyle\sigma(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)}{\sum_{k=1}^{D}\exp(e_{k}\cdot\alpha)}(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)-F(\alpha)=\nabla F(\alpha)\cdot\alpha-F(\alpha),

as desired.
∎

We summarize some properties of
F(\alpha)
and
\sigma(s)
in the following corollaries. We define the
amoeba

\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the largest domain where
F(\alpha)
is strictly convex.

Corollary 4.15
.

The free energy
F(\alpha):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex and real analytic on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in particular,
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Corollary 4.16
.

The surface tension
\sigma:\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex on the entire closed Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

Remark 4.17
.

These results are somewhat different from their analogs for the standard dimer model. In that setting, the surface tension is convex everywhere but is piecewise linear on the one-dimensional edges of
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, hence strictly convex only on the complement of the edges of
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Similarly, for the standard dimer model the amoeba is a strict subset of the plane. We leverage these corollaries in Section
7.2
.

5.
Patching configurations for large
N

Here we prove a “local interchangeability” property for
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer covers as
{\mathbf{N}},n\to\infty
. This result will apply to samples from multinomial dimer measures because of
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
concentration (Theorem
2.5
). This can be seen as a simplification of the patching theorem for dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
in
[
CSW23
]
, where the proof here is simpler because of the control we have as
{\mathbf{N}}\to\infty
. The main tool will be Hall’s matching theorem, described below in Section
5.2
.

5.1.
Statement and set up

Take a compact domain
R\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with piecewise smooth boundary and let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
be a lattice approximation of
R
as before. We prove that two
N
-dimer covers of
R_{n}
can be “patched” together, one on each side of the boundaries of an annulus of lattice width
O(n)
, in the iterated limit as
N
and then
n
go to infinity, if their
N\to\infty
limiting boundary conditions are within
O(1/n)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix a constant
\delta>0
. Let
R,R_{n}
be as above. Let
\mu_{n,N}
and
\nu_{n,N}
be sequences of measures on
N
-dimer covers of
R_{n}
with boundary vertex multiplicities converging to
(\alpha_{v}^{n})_{v\in\partial R_{n}},(\beta_{v}^{n})_{v\in\partial R_{n}}
respectively as
N\to\infty
. Assume that

•

The
N\to\infty
limiting boundary values satisfy
\sup_{v\in\partial R_{n}}|\alpha_{v}^{n}-\beta_{v}^{n}|=O({1}/{n})
.

•

For any
n
fixed, samples from
\nu_{n,N}
concentrate on a discrete limiting flow
\omega_{n}
as
N\to\infty
which is (i) for all
v\in R_{n}^{\circ}
,
\sum_{e\ni v}\omega_{n}(e)=1
and for all
v\in\partial R_{n}
,
\sum_{e\ni v}\omega_{n}(e)=\beta_{v}^{n}
, and (ii) strictly non-degenerate, i.e. there is
a>0
independent of
n
such that
\omega_{n}(e)>a
for all edges
e\in E(R_{n})
.

Let
R_{n}^{\delta}
be a lattice approximation of
R\setminus N_{\delta}(\partial R)=\{x\in R:d(x,\partial R)>\delta\}
. If
M,M^{\prime}
are sampled from
\mu_{N,n},\nu_{N,n}
respectively, the joint probability that there exists a dimer cover
M^{\prime\prime}
of
R_{n}
such that
M^{\prime\prime}\mid_{R_{n}^{\delta}}=M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
has the same vertex multiplicities as
M
at all
v\in\partial R_{n}
goes to
1
in the iterated limit as
N\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
.

Remark 5.2
.

The notation
M^{\prime\prime}\mid_{R_{n}^{\delta}}=M^{\prime}
means that
M^{\prime\prime}_{e}=M^{\prime}_{e}
for all edges
e
with an endpoint in
(R_{n}^{\delta})^{\circ}
.

Remark 5.3
.

If the limiting boundary conditions
\beta_{v}^{n}
are feasible for each
n
and
\nu_{n,N}
are
multinomial
dimer measures with these limiting multiplicities, then by Theorem
2.5
samples from
\nu_{n,N}
will concentrate on a limiting flow
\omega_{n}
satisfying (i) and which is non-degenerate, i.e.
\omega_{n}(e)>0
for all
e\in R_{n}
. The theorem applies to any sequence of multinomial measures
\nu_{n,N}
as long as their limiting flows
\omega_{n}
together satisfy the strictly non-degenerate condition.

Theorem
5.1
is equivalent to saying that if
n,N
are large enough, and
M,M^{\prime}
are sampled as described, then the annular region
A_{n}=R_{n}\setminus R_{n}^{\delta}
has an
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover with multiplicity
N
on its interior and vertex multiplicities on
\partial A_{n}
determined by
M,M^{\prime}
respectively. The key tool in the proof is a version of
Hall’s matching theorem
which we describe in the next section.

5.2.
Hall’s matching theorem and counterexamples

An
{\mathbf{N}}
-dimer cover of a graph
G
can be lifted to a standard dimer cover of the
\mathbf{N}
-fold blow-up graph of
G
, so it suffices to show that the associated region in the blow-up graph has a dimer cover. Hall’s matching theorem
[
Hal35
]
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a bipartite graph to have a dimer cover.

Theorem 5.4

(Hall’s matching theorem)
.

Suppose that
G
is a finite bipartite graph with bipartition
(B,W)
such that
|B|=|W|
. There exists a dimer cover of
G
if and only if there is no set
C\subset W
such that

N(C)=\{v\in B:\exists\,c\in C,(v,c)\in E\}

satisfies
|N(C)|<|C|
. We call
U:=C\cup N(C)
satisfying these conditions a counterexample in
G
.

It is straightforward to see that Hall’s matching theorem applied in the blow-up graph of
G
projects to the following equivalent condition in
G
.

Proposition 5.5

(Hall’s matching theorem projected from the blow-up graph)
.

Let
G
be a finite bipartite graph with bipartition
(B,W)
. Label every vertex
v\in G
with a multiplicity
N_{v}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in W}N_{v}=\sum_{v\in B}N_{v}.

There exists an
{\mathbf{N}}=(N_{v})_{v}
-dimer cover of
G
if and only if there is no set
C\subset W
such that

N(C)=\{v\in B:\exists\,c\in C,(c,v)\in E\}

satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in N(C)}N_{v}<\sum_{v\in C}N_{v}.

We call
U:=C\cup N(C)
satisfying these conditions an
{\mathbf{N}}
-counterexample
in
G
.

We say that the
{\mathbf{N}}
-
imbalance
of a set
U\subset G
is

(5.1)

\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U):=\sum_{v\in W\cap U}N_{v}-\sum_{v\in B\cap U}N_{v}

Recall that we work with graphs
G
which are a subset of a lattice. Any counterexample set
U
must satisfy the condition that its
interior boundary

\partial_{0}U
is contained in
B
:

(5.2)

\partial_{0}U:=\{u\in U:\exists\,v\in G\setminus U,(u,v)\in E\}\subset B.

More generally, we call a set
U
with the property in (
5.2
) a
potential counterexample
(note that this definition does not depend on
{\mathbf{N}}
). If
U
is a potential counterexample, it is an
{\mathbf{N}}
-counterexample if and only if
\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U)>0
.

In our setting, the multiplicities at
v\in G^{\circ}
are
N
, and the multiplicities along
v\in\partial G
are between
1
and
N
. We relate the
{\mathbf{N}}
-imbalance of a potential counterexample
U
to the maximum multiplicities of
edges incident to vertices
\partial U=\{u\in U:\exists\,v\in\Lambda\setminus U,(u,v)\in E\}
. Note that this is the boundary of
U
in
\Lambda
, not in
G
. Correspondingly, we split these edges into two groups, “interior” boundary edges
I_{E}(U)
and “exterior” boundary edges
X_{E}(U)
:

\displaystyle I_{E}(U)

\displaystyle=\{e=(u,v):u\in\partial U,v\in G\setminus U\}

\displaystyle X_{E}(U)

\displaystyle=\{e=(u,v):u\in\partial U,v\in\Lambda\setminus G\}.

Recall that
D
denotes the degree of any vertex in
\Lambda
. The divergence theorem (applied in the blow up graph) gives the following.

Proposition 5.6
.

If
U
is a potential counterexample in
G\subset\Lambda
with multiplicities
N_{v}=N
at all
v\in G^{\circ}
and multiplicities
N_{v}
between
1
and
N
at
v\in\partial G
, then

ND\cdot\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U)=-N|I_{E}(U)|+\sum_{u\in\partial U}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}\,\text{sign}(u),

where
\text{sign}(u)=+1
if
u\in W
and
-1
if
u\in B
and
X_{E}(u)
is the set of
e\in X_{E}(U)
incident to the specified vertex
u\in\partial U
(this can be empty).

Proof.

Let
\tilde{U}\subset\Lambda_{{\mathbf{N}}}
denote a lift of
U
to the blow-up graph.
Let
(\tilde{W},\tilde{B})
denote the bipartition in
\Lambda_{{\mathbf{N}}}
. On one hand it is clear that

\displaystyle\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U)=|\tilde{W}\cap\tilde{U}|-|\tilde{B}\cap\tilde{U}|.

On the other hand, consider a discrete vector field
r
on the edges of
\Lambda_{{\mathbf{N}}}
defined by
r(\tilde{e})=1/(ND)
for all
\tilde{e}\in E(\Lambda_{{\mathbf{N}}})
oriented from
\tilde{W}
to
\tilde{B}
. Note that this has divergence
+1
at every
v\in\tilde{W}
and
-1
at every
v\in\tilde{B}
. By the divergence theorem,

\displaystyle\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U)=\text{div}_{\tilde{U}}(r)=\frac{1}{ND}\sum_{\tilde{e}=(x,y):x\in\tilde{U},y\not\in\tilde{U}}{\text{sign}(x)}.

Let
\pi
denote the projection to
\Lambda_{{\mathbf{N}}}\to\Lambda
. For each
\tilde{e}
in the sum,
\pi(\tilde{e})
is incident to
\partial U
. If
\pi(\tilde{e})\in I_{E}(U)
, then
\text{sign}(x)=-1
. Otherwise it can have either sign. Every
e\in I_{E}(U)
has
N
lifts in the sum, and every
e\in X_{E}(U)
has
N_{u}
lifts if
e=(u,v)
,
u\in U
. Putting this together gives the result.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we apply Hall’s matching theorem to an annular region of lattice width
O(n)
. We use the
O(n)
width to get a lower bound on the number of interior boundary edges along a potential counterexample set
U
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose that
A=R_{n}\setminus R_{n}^{\delta}
is as in Theorem
5.1
, and suppose that
U\subset A
is a potential counterexample which intersects both boundary components of
A
. Then there exists a constant
\kappa=\kappa(\delta)
such that

\displaystyle|I_{E}(U)|\geq\kappa n^{d-1}.

The lemma follows from a discrete version of the isoperimetric inequality. For
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{3}
it is the same as
[
CSW23
, Proposition 6.5.1]
but restated in our terminology.

5.3.
Proof of the patching theorem

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
A_{n}=R_{n}\setminus R_{n}^{\delta}
and suppose for contradiction that
U_{n}
is a counterexample with boundary conditions on
A_{n}
coming from
M_{1},M_{2}
sampled from
\mu_{n,N},\nu_{n,N}
respectively. Note that since
M,M^{\prime}
alone extend to tilings of all of
R_{n}
,
U_{n}
must connect inner and outer boundaries of
A_{n}
. It suffices to show that
A_{n}
is tileable with the following multiplicities on its boundary.

•

For
a\in\partial R_{n}
, the multiplicity is
N_{a}(M,A_{n})=\sum_{e=(a,v),v\in A_{n}}{M}_{e}=\sum_{e=(a,v)}M_{e}
.

•

For
a\in\partial R_{n}^{\delta}
, the multiplicity is
N_{a}(M^{\prime},A_{n})=\sum_{e=(a,v),v\in A_{n}}M_{e}^{\prime}
. Note that
M_{e}^{\prime}
summed over all edges
e
in
R_{n}
incident to
a
would be
N
since
a\in R_{n}^{\circ}
, but this is the multiplicity needed for a tiling of
A_{n}
that agrees with
M^{\prime}
in the interior of
R_{n}^{\delta}
.

By Proposition
5.6
we have:

\displaystyle ND\cdot\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U_{n})=-N|I_{E}(U_{n})|+\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}\,\text{sign}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-N|I_{E}(U_{n})|+\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M,A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u)+\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}^{\delta}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M^{\prime},A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u).

Since
M^{\prime}
extends to an
N
-dimer cover of
A_{n}
with its own boundary condition on
\partial R_{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}^{\delta}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M^{\prime},A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u)<N\sum_{e\in I_{E}(U_{n})}M_{e}^{\prime}-\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M^{\prime},A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u)

and we use this to replace the third term in the sum above. Along the outer boundary
\partial R_{n}
, the limiting boundary multiplicities
\alpha_{v}^{n},\beta_{v}^{n}
differ by
O(1/{n})
. On the other hand the total area of the boundary of is order
n^{d-1}
. Thus for any
\varepsilon>0
, for
N
large enough, we have with probability
1-\varepsilon
,

\bigg|\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M,A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u)-\sum_{u\in\partial U_{n}\cap\partial R_{n}}|X_{E}(u)|N_{u}(M^{\prime},A_{n})\,\text{sign}(u)\bigg|<\varepsilon Nn^{d-1}+O(Nn^{d-2}).

Combining these gives the bound

(5.3)

\displaystyle D\cdot\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-|I_{E}(U_{n})|+\sum_{e\in I_{E}(U_{n})}\frac{M_{e}^{\prime}}{N}+\varepsilon n^{d-1}+O(n^{d-2}).

For
n
fixed, as
N\to\infty
samples from
\nu_{n,N}
concentrate on a limiting flow
\omega_{n}
which has unit divergences in
R_{n}^{\circ}
. Further, this family of limiting flows is strictly non-degenerate in
n
. Combined with the fact that
U_{n}
connects the inner and outer boundaries of
A_{n}
, Lemma
5.7
implies there is a constant
c=c(\delta)
such that for
N
large enough, with probability
1-\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle N|I_{E}(U_{n})|-\sum_{e\in I_{E}(U_{n})}M_{e}^{\prime}>cNn^{d-1}.

Plugging this in to (
5.3
) gives that for
N
large enough, we have with probability
1-\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle D\cdot\text{imbalance}_{{\mathbf{N}}}(U_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-cn^{d-1}+\varepsilon n^{d-1}+O(n^{d-2}).

Since
c>0
is fixed and
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, taking
\varepsilon<c
and then
N,n
large enough would make the imbalance non-positive. Therefore for
n,N
large enough,
U_{n}
is not an
{\mathbf{N}}
counterexample, which completes the proof.
∎

6.
Large deviations

6.1.
Set up, statement, and corollaries

Fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is which compact, connected domain with piecewise smooth boundary and a boundary asymptotic flow
b
on
\partial R
as in Section
3
. Let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
be a lattice approximation of
R
, i.e. a sequence of lattice regions approximating it in Hausdorff distance. Here we prove a large deviation principle in the iterated limit as
N\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
, for samples from multinomial measures on
R_{n}
with discrete boundary conditions converging to
b
as
n,N\to\infty
in the way specified below.

We assume that
b
is
extendable outside
, i.e. there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
b
extends to an asymptotic flow on an
\varepsilon
neighborhood of
R
(versions of this condition are also present in the large deviation principles for the standard dimer model in 2D
[
CKP01
]
and 3D
[
CSW23
]
).

On the discrete side, for each
n
fixed, we specify boundary conditions on
R_{n}
as
N\to\infty
by choosing the limiting vertex multiplicities
\beta_{n}(v)\in(0,1]
for all
v\in\partial R_{n}
. In terms of a discrete flow
\omega_{n}
corresponding to a dimer cover of
R_{n}
, this is fixing the divergences of
\omega_{n}
along
\partial R_{n}
. The rescaled boundary conditions on
R_{n}
viewed as a dual measure as in Section
3.4
are thus

\displaystyle b_{n}:=\sum_{v\in\partial R_{n}}\text{sign}(v)\beta_{n}(v)\frac{\mathds{1}_{v}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{2n^{d-1}},

where
\text{sign}(v)=1
if
v\in W
and
-1
if
v\in B
. We say that
\beta_{n}
converges to
b
as
n\to\infty
if
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(b_{n},b)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

For each
n
, we consider only feasible limiting boundary conditions
\beta_{n}
on
\partial R_{n}
. To avoid some technicalities, instead of fixing one limiting boundary value for each
n
, we fix
thresholds

\theta_{n}
such that
\theta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, but sufficiently slowly (the existence of these thresholds follows from Remark
6.12
). We define
\rho_{n,N}
to be the multinomial dimer measure with vertex multiplicities
N
at all interior points of
R_{n}
, and any multiplicities
(N_{v})_{v\in\partial R}
between
1
and
N
which are feasible and such that the limiting boundary value
b_{n}
as
N\to\infty
with these choices has
\mathbb{W}_{1}^{1,1}(b,b_{n})<\theta_{n}
.

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 6.1

(Large deviation principle)
.

Fix
\Lambda
,
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact, connected with piecewise smooth boundary, and a boundary asymptotic flow
b
on
\partial R
which is extendable outside. Let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
a lattice approximation of
R
, fix thresholds
\theta_{n}
going to zero sufficiently slowly, and probability measures
\rho_{n,N}
as above.

The measures
(\rho_{n,N})_{n,N\geq 1}
satisfy a large deviation principle in the iterated limit as
N
and then
n
go to infinity in the weak topology on flows with good rate function
I:\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)\to\mathbb{R}
. That is, for any Borel set
B
in the weak topology on flows,

\displaystyle-\inf_{\omega\in{B}^{\circ}}I(\omega)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\rho_{n,N}(B)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\rho_{n,N}(B)\leq-\inf_{\omega\in\overline{B}}I(\omega)

where
\overline{B},B^{\circ}
denote the closure and interior of
B
respectively. Further, if
\omega
is an asymptotic flow, the rate function
I
is given explicitly by

(6.1)

\displaystyle I(\omega)=C_{b}+\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
C_{b}\geq 0
is an additive constant and
\sigma
is the surface tension for
\Lambda
from Theorem
4.14
, i.e., it is the Legendre dual of the free energy on the torus
F(\alpha)=\log\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp(e_{j}\cdot\alpha)
. In particular
C_{b}=-\min_{\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)}\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x
. If
\omega
is not an asymptotic flow, then
I(\omega)=\infty.

Remark 6.2
.

The integral
\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x
is nonpositive for all asymptotic flows. Shifting by the additive constant
C_{b}
makes
0
the minimum value of
I(\cdot)
on
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
. To simplify some of the later statements, we define the
total entropy

\mathrm{Ent}
by

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}(\omega):=-\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.

We call this total entropy because of Corollary
4.13
, where we saw that the surface tension and entropy are related by a minus sign. Note that the choice of sign here means that
\mathrm{Ent}
is a non-negative, strictly
concave
function.

Remark 6.3
.

The thresholds
\theta_{n}
only enter in to one step of the proof (Theorem
6.11
), and could be avoided by a proving a stronger and more technical patching theorem (replacing Theorem
5.1
). For
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, a stronger patching theorem is implied by the generalized patching theorem for single dimer covers of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
proved in
[
CSW23
]
, and hence it would follow that Theorem
1.1
holds without the thresholds under the mild regularity condition that
(R,b)
is
flexible
, see Definition
7.1
. However, we choose to give more direct, simplified proofs that make use of the large
N
structure, instead of relying on the substantial tools developed specifically for
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.

As a corollary of the large deviation principle and properties of the surface tension
\sigma
, we see that samples from
\rho_{n,N}
concentrate on a unique
limit shape
, which is the minimizer of
I
subject to the boundary conditions.

Corollary 6.4
.

(Limit shape.)
Fix
\Lambda
and
(R,b)
as in Theorem
6.1
. First, the rate function
I
has a unique minimizer
\omega_{\min}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
. Second, given
\varepsilon>0
define the event

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}=\{\omega:d_{W}(\omega_{\min},\omega)>\varepsilon\}.

For any sequence of multinomial measures
\rho_{n,N}
as in Theorem
6.1
, dimer covers sampled from
\rho_{n,N}
concentrate exponentially on
\omega_{\min}
in the iterated limit as
N
and then
n
go to infinity. In other words there is a constant
C>0
determined by
(R,b)
such for
n,N
sufficiently large,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\rho_{n,N}(V_{\varepsilon})<C^{-Nn^{d}}.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.12
,
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
is compact. By Corollary
4.16
,
\sigma
is strictly convex on the closed Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Combined this shows that
I
has a unique minimum
\omega_{\min}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
.

Concentration on the minimizer follows from Theorem
6.1
by a standard argument. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and cover
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
by open neighborhoods
B_{\omega}
such that:

•

B_{\omega_{\min}}=\{\omega:d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\min})<\varepsilon\}
.

•

If
\omega\neq\omega_{\min}
, then
I(\omega^{\prime})>I(\omega_{\min})
for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\overline{B}_{\omega}
.

By compactness this has a finite subcover which we can denote
B_{\omega_{\min}}=B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{k}
. Let
\omega_{i}
be a minimizer of
I
over
\overline{B_{i}}
. By Theorem
6.1
,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\rho_{n,N}(V_{\varepsilon})\leq\sum_{i=2}^{k}\rho_{n,N}(B_{i})\leq\sum_{i=2}^{k}\exp(Nn^{d}(I(\omega_{\min})-I(\omega_{i}))).

Since
I(\omega_{\min})-I(\omega_{i})<0
for all
i=2,\dots,k
this completes the proof.
∎

The rest of this section is dedicated to proving Theorem
6.1
. We first prove here that
I
is a good rate function.

Lemma 6.5
.

Fix
(R,b)
as above. The function
I:\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)\to[0,\infty)
given by

\displaystyle I(\omega)=C_{b}+\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

is a good rate function, i.e. it is lower semicontinuous in the weak topology on flows and the level sets
\{\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b):I(\omega)\leq c\}
are compact.

Remark 6.6
.

Since
\mathrm{Ent},I
have opposite signs, it follows that
\mathrm{Ent}
is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

We extend
I
(which is defined to be finite only on
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
) to
\tilde{I}(\omega)=C_{b}+\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x
, which is defined for any measurable flow valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
(i.e., we have removed the divergence-free requirement).

Suppose that
(\omega_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
is a sequence with
d_{W}(\omega_{k},\omega)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. We replace
\omega_{k}
with
\omega_{k,\varepsilon}
defined by

\displaystyle\omega_{k,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{|B_{\varepsilon}(x)|}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\omega_{k}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where we set
\omega_{k}(y)=0
if
y\not\in R
. We define
\omega_{\varepsilon}
similarly by averaging
\omega
. These mollified flows may not be divergence-free, but they are valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
and hence
\tilde{I}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon}),\tilde{I}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
are still defined and finite. By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem
\omega_{k,\varepsilon},\omega_{\varepsilon}
converge a.e. to
\omega_{k},\omega
respectively. Since the surface tension
\sigma:\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, this implies that
I(\omega_{k})=\tilde{I}(\omega_{k})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{I}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon})
and
I(\omega)=\tilde{I}(\omega)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{I}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
. For
\varepsilon
fixed these flows are continuous and
d_{W}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon},\omega_{\varepsilon})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Since convergence in
d_{W}
implies weak convergence (Proposition
3.3
),
\omega_{k,\varepsilon}
converges pointwise to
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, and hence
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{I}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon})=\tilde{I}(\omega_{\varepsilon}).
On the other hand since
\sigma
is convex,

\displaystyle\sigma(\omega_{k,\varepsilon}(x))=\sigma\bigg(\frac{1}{|B_{\varepsilon}(x)|}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\omega_{k}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{|B_{\varepsilon}(x)|}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\sigma(\omega_{k}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon})\leq I(\omega_{k})+C\varepsilon.

Therefore

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}I(\omega_{k})\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\tilde{I}(\omega_{k,\varepsilon})-C\varepsilon=\tilde{I}(\omega_{\varepsilon})-C\varepsilon.

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
and using that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{I}(\omega_{\varepsilon})=I(\omega)
shows that
I
is lower semicontinuous. Since
\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
is compact (Corollary
3.12
) it follows that the level sets are compact.
∎

Standard arguments (see e.g.
[
Var16
]
) imply that the large deviation principle of Theorem
6.1
is equivalent to upper and lower bound statements for open balls in
AF(\Lambda,R,b)
, plus the
exponential tightness
property, that is: for any
c<\infty
there exists a compact set

such that for any closed set

with

,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\rho_{n,N}(K)\leq-c.

Exponential tightness follows by taking

, the level set of the rate function, which is compact by Lemma

.

For the upper and lower bound statements, it suffices to consider neighborhoods in the topology. For
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
we define:

(6.5)

\displaystyle U_{r}(\omega)=\{\omega^{\prime}:d_{W}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})<r\}.

We let
Z_{n,N}
denote the unnormalized partition function of
\rho_{n,N}
, and further for any set

To prove Theorem

it remains to prove the following two bounds for the asymptotics of the normalized partition functions. Recall that

differs from

by a sign and an additive constant, see (

).

Lemma 6.7

(Lower bound)
.

For any
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\bigg[\frac{Z_{n,N}(U_{r}(\omega))}{(N!)^{|R|n^{d}}}\bigg]\geq\mathrm{Ent}(\omega).

Lemma 6.8

(Upper bound)
.

For any
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\bigg[\frac{Z_{n,N}(U_{r}(\omega))}{(N!)^{|R|n^{d}}}\bigg]\leq\mathrm{Ent}(\omega).

In Section

, we prove two approximation theorems which are needed for the proof of the lower bound. After that, in Section

we prove the lower bound and in Section

we prove the upper bound. The other key tool is the

), which we use to patch samples from the torus measures defined in Section

.

Remark 6.9
.

Recall from Section

) were established for general lattices. A sufficiently robust patching theorem has been established, for example, for dimer covers of

. (This step can also be achieved easily for the honeycomb lattice using height function extension results.)

6.2.
Approximation theorems

6.2.1.
Discrete approximation

The main result is of this section is that any asymptotic flow
\omega
can be approximated by discrete flows corresponding to
N
-dimer covers. This is a converse to Theorem
3.9
.

From the critical gauge equations, it is straightforward to show that any discrete flow with the right divergences can be approximated by flows corresponding to
N
-dimer covers.

Lemma 6.10
.

Fix
G=(V,E)\subset\Lambda
and
(\beta_{v})_{v\in\partial G}
a feasible limiting boundary condition. Suppose that
\omega
is a discrete flow on

with divergences
\pm 1
at interior vertices and

at white vertices and

.

Proof.

For each

is a feasible boundary condition and

as

with these multiplicities and edge weights

; this is a valid edge weight function since

Then

∎

Theorem 6.11
.

Fix
\delta>0
and any
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
with
b
which is extendable outside. Let
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
be a lattice approximation of
R
as before. For all

large enough, there exists an
N
-dimer cover
M
of

, with boundary vertex multiplicities
(N_{v})_{v\in\partial R_{n}}
between
1
and
N
, such that

Remark 6.12
.

In particular, since the boundary value operator is uniformly continuous, this theorem shows that any
\omega
can be approximated by an
N
-dimer cover in the support of
\rho_{n,N}
, as long as the thresholds
\theta_{n}
used to define

go to zero sufficiently slowly.

We note any
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
can be approximated by a smooth flow, and then give a construction for the discrete approximation when
\omega
is
C^{2}
.

Lemma 6.13
.

Suppose that
(R,b)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that
b
is extendable outside and fix
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Any asymptotic flow
\omega\in\mathrm{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
can be approximated in
d_{W}
by a smooth flow
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}\in\mathrm{AF}(\Lambda,R)
, where
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
is constructed by mollifying
\omega
with a bump function
\phi
supported in
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
, and has
d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{sm}})<C\varepsilon^{1/2}
for some constant
C>0
. Further we can assume that
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
is valued strictly in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

Remark 6.14
.

Note that the boundary conditions of
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
are allowed to be different from those of
\omega
. However, since the boundary value operator
T
is uniformly continuous, the bound
d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{sm}})<C\varepsilon^{1/2}
implies a bound on the difference in their boundary conditions.

Proof.

Since
b
is extendable outside there exists
\lambda>0
such that
b
can be extended to an asymptotic flow on an
\lambda
neighborhood of
R
(i.e., divergence-free and valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
). Let
\phi
be a smooth bump function supported on
B_{\varepsilon}(0)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which integrates to
1
, with
\varepsilon<\lambda
. We define a smooth approximation
\omega_{\text{sm}}:=\omega\ast\phi
restricted to
R
. Since
\varepsilon<\lambda
,
\omega_{\text{sm}}
is divergence-free on
R^{\circ}
. Since
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
is convex,
\omega_{\text{sm}}
is valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

To bound
d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\text{sm}})
, first note there is a constant
c_{0}
such that the total flow of
\omega,\omega_{\text{sm}}
may differ by at most
c_{0}\varepsilon
. Next for
\delta>0
fixed, there is a constant
c_{1}
such that for any box
B
of side length
\delta
contained in
R
,

d_{W}(\omega\mid_{B},\omega_{\text{sm}}\mid_{B})<c_{1}\varepsilon\delta^{d-1},

since the averaging procedure to construct
\omega_{\text{sm}}
moves flow by distance at most
\varepsilon
. We can cover
R
with
O(\delta^{-d})=c_{2}\delta^{-d}
boxes of side length
\delta
. Hence by Lemma
3.6
,

\displaystyle d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\text{sm}})<c_{0}\varepsilon+c_{2}d\delta^{-d}(10\delta^{d+1}+c_{1}\varepsilon\delta^{d-1}).

Since
\delta
is arbitrary, we can take it to be on the order of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
, which gives
d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{sm}})<C\varepsilon^{1/2}
for some constant
C>0
. Finally, if
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
itself is not valued strictly in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, then
(1-\kappa)\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
is for
\kappa
sufficiently small. If we take
\kappa\leq O(\varepsilon^{1/2})
, then the distance between
\omega
and
(1-\kappa)\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
is still of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
, and without loss of generality we can replace
\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
with
(1-\kappa)\omega_{\mathrm{sm}}
.
∎

Suppose that
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
is
C^{2}
. Recall that
e_{1},\dots,e_{D}
are the edge vectors of edges incident to a white vertex. Recall from Section
4
that given a slope
s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, there are probabilities
p_{1}(s),\dots,p_{D}(s)
corresponding to the
D
edge types which as edge weights are gauge equivalent to the uniform weights, and have the property that

(6.6)

\displaystyle s=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(s)\,e_{i}.

We define a discrete flow
\omega_{\text{{white}},n}
on
R_{n}
such that for each edge
e=(u,v)
in
R_{n}
of type
e_{i}
and with
v\in W\cap R_{n}
, we set

(6.7)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}(e)=p_{i}(\omega(v)),

for
e
oriented white-to-black. If
e=(u,v)
is not contained in
R_{n}
, then
\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}(e)=0
. We similarly define
\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}
where the role of black and white vertices is reversed. These approximations have the following properties.

Lemma 6.15
.

Fix
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
which is
C^{2}
. Then

(a)

For all interior black vertices
u
,
\mathrm{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}(u)=-1
, and for all interior white vertices
v
,
\mathrm{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}(v)=1
.

(b)

For all interior white vertices
v
,
\mathrm{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}(v)=1+O(1/n^{2})
, and for all interior black vertices
u
,
\mathrm{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}(u)=-1+O(1/n^{2})
.

(c)

For all edges
e\in E(R_{n})
,
|\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}(e)-\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}(e)|=O(1/n)
.

(d)

The distances
d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}),d_{W}(\omega,\omega_{\mathrm{white},n})
are
O(1/n)
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows immediately from (
6.7
) and the fact that
\sum_{i}p_{i}(s)=1
for any slope
s
. Part (c) follows since
\omega:R\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
C^{2}
and
p_{i}(s)
is a continuous function of
s
.

For (b) consider just
\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}
; the proof for
\omega_{\mathrm{white},n}
is analogous. For all
v\in R_{n}^{\circ}\cap W
, we have

(6.8)

\displaystyle\text{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(\omega(v+{\frac{1}{n}}e_{i})).

Let
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d}
be the basis vectors for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define
a_{i}^{j}
so that
e_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{i}^{j}\eta_{j}
. We expand (
6.8
) in
{1}/{n}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(\omega(v+\frac{1}{n}e_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(\omega(v))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial(p_{i}\circ\omega)}{\partial e_{i}}(v)+O(1/n^{2})=1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{i}^{j}\frac{\partial(p_{i}\circ\omega)}{\partial\eta_{i}}(v)+O(1/n^{2}).

On the other hand by (
6.6
), the
j^{th}
component
\omega_{j}
of
\omega
is

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}a_{i}^{j}\,p_{i}(\omega(x)).

Thus for any
v\in\partial R_{n}^{\circ}\cap W
,

\displaystyle\text{div}\,\omega_{\mathrm{black},n}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}(\omega(v+\frac{1}{n}e_{i}))=1+\frac{1}{n}\,\text{div}\,\omega(v)+O(1/n^{2})=1+O(1/n^{2}).

The second equality uses that
\omega
is divergence-free in
R^{\circ}
.

Finally part (d) follows from the continuity of
\omega
, a simple averaging argument and Lemma
3.6
.

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

By Lemma

, it suffices to show that
\omega

, for any

small enough, we can assume that

by

It follows that

6.2.2.
Piecewise-constant approximation

Fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a scale
\delta>0
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a collection of
d
-dimensional simplices (i.e. triangles or tetrahedra in two or three dimensions) with diameters of order
\delta
and disjoint interiors, all of which intersect
R
, and such that
R\subset\cup_{X\in\mathcal{X}}X
. We assume that the simplices
X\in\mathcal{X}
are all translations of a finite collection of shapes (e.g., those with vertices in an appropriate lattice).

We say that an asymptotic flow
\omega
is
piecewise constant
on
\mathcal{X}
if for each
X\in\mathcal{X}
,
\omega\mid_{X}
is constant. We show that any asymptotic flow can be approximated by a piecewise constant one (with slightly perturbed boundary conditions).

Proposition 6.17
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
(R,b)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
b
extendable outside.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a mesh of simplices as above, with
\delta>0
small enough. For any
\omega\in\mathrm{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
, there exists
\widetilde{\omega}
which is piecewise constant on
\mathcal{X}
, has
d_{W}(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})<\varepsilon
, is valued strictly in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, and which has
\widetilde{\omega}\in\mathrm{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

The proof of Proposition
6.17
is analogous to that of
[
CSW23
, Proposition 8.3.1]
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.13
, it suffices to prove this result for
\omega
smooth. Note that since
b
is extendable outside, we can take
\delta>0
small enough so that
\omega
is smooth and divergence free on all
X\in\mathcal{X}
. For any fixed simplex
X\in\mathcal{X}
,
\partial X
consists of
(d+1)
faces. Let
F_{1},\dots,F_{d+1}
denote the faces of
\partial X
and let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d+1}
denote their outward-pointing unit normal vectors. We define the vector
s_{X}
by the condition that

\displaystyle s_{X}\cdot\xi_{i}=\frac{1}{|F_{i}|}\int_{F_{i}}\langle\omega,\xi_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\text{ for }i\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}.

Since
\omega
is divergence free on
X
, the divergence theorem gives

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{F_{i}}\langle\omega,\xi_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Moreover since
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}|F_{i}|=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle s_{X}\cdot\xi_{d}=\frac{1}{|F_{d}|}\int_{F_{d}}\langle\omega,\xi_{d}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x.

In particular,
s_{X}
does not depend on the order of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d}
. We define
\widetilde{\omega}(x):=s_{X}
for all
x\in X
. This is valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, continuous and divergence-free on
R
, hence its restriction to
R
is in
\mathrm{AF}(\Lambda,R)
.

It remains to bound
d_{W}(\widetilde{\omega},\omega)
. Let
m
denote the modulus of continuity of
\omega
on
\widetilde{R}\supset R
. Since we assume that all simplices
X\in\mathcal{X}
are translations of a finite collection, there are finitely many normal vectors
\xi_{i}
ranging over all faces of
\partial X
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
. Thus there is a constant
K
such that for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
,

(6.9)

\displaystyle|s_{X}-\text{avg}_{X}(\omega)|\leq Kdm\delta,

where
s_{X}=\widetilde{\omega}\mid_{X}
. Hence by Lemma
3.6
, for any
\delta
small enough given
\omega,\varepsilon
we have
d_{W}(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})<\varepsilon
. We can replace
\widetilde{\omega}
with itself times
1
minus a small constant (of order at most
\varepsilon
); this modification will satisfy the same properties as above and be valued strictly in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

∎

The piecewise constant approximations of Proposition
6.17
have the following useful property.

Proposition 6.18
.

Fix
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R)
. If
\tilde{\omega}_{\delta}
is a piecewise constant approximation of
\omega
on
\delta
simplices as in Proposition
6.17
, then
\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega}_{\delta})=\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)+o(1)
as
\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.5
,
\mathrm{Ent}
is upper semicontinuous, so
\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega}_{\delta})\leq\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)
, and hence
\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega}_{\delta})\leq\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)+o(1).

For the other direction, we use the convexity of
\sigma
. Let
\mathcal{X}^{\delta}
denote the collection of simplices at scale
\delta
on which
\widetilde{\omega}_{\delta}
is constant (we assume that for all
\delta
,
\mathcal{X}^{\delta}
consists of translations of rescaled versions of the same collection of simplices, so that the collection of normal vectors remains the same for all
\delta
). Define another flow
\omega_{\mathrm{avg},\delta}
by

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{avg},\delta}(x):=\frac{1}{|X|}\int_{X}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\qquad\text{ for all }x\in X,\text{ for all }X\in\mathcal{X}^{\delta},

where we set
\omega(y)=0
for
y\not\in R
. While this may not be an asymptotic flow (it may not be divergence-free), it is still valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Since
\sigma
is convex,

\displaystyle\sigma\bigg(\frac{1}{|X|}\int_{X}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{|X|}\int_{X}\sigma(\omega(y))\,\mathrm{d}y.

Since
\sigma
is continuous, (
6.9
) implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}(\tilde{\omega}_{\delta})+o(1)=-\int_{R}\sigma(\omega_{\text{avg},\delta}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)=-\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-\int_{R}\sigma(\omega_{\text{avg},\delta}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{Ent}(\tilde{\omega}_{\delta})+o(1),

which completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
Proof of the lower bound

Proof of Lemma
6.7
.

Fix
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
. By Proposition
6.17
, we can approximate
\omega
by
\widetilde{\omega}
which is piecewise-constant on a mesh
\mathcal{X}
of simplices with diameters of order
\delta
and valued in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Let
s_{X}:=\widetilde{\omega}\mid_{X}\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
for each

. By Proposition
6.18
,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)=\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega})+o(1)=-\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}|X|\sigma(s_{X})+o(1)

as
\delta\to 0
. Further, for
\delta
small enough, we have
U_{r/2}(\widetilde{\omega})\subset U_{r}(\omega)
. It therefore suffices to prove the lower bound for
\widetilde{\omega}
.

By Theorem
6.11
, for
n,N
large enough there exists an
N
-dimer cover
M
of
R_{n}
(with boundary conditions allowed by
\rho_{n,N}
) as in Theorem
6.11
such that
d_{W}(\omega_{M},\widetilde{\omega})<r/2
. In fact we can find

which has a particular form, which is a modification of
\omega_{\text{black},n}
approximating
\widetilde{\omega}
as in the previous section.

As a technical point,
\widetilde{\omega}
is not
C^{2}
, and thus we cannot immediately construct
\omega_{\text{black},n}
from it. However, we can mollify it by convolving it with a bump function
\phi
supported in
B_{\delta^{\prime}}(0)
for
\delta^{\prime}\ll\delta
. The flow
\widetilde{\omega}\ast\phi
will be smooth (and still close to
\widetilde{\omega}
by Lemma

), and in fact it will be equal to
\widetilde{\omega}
outside a

neighborhood of the set of boundaries

. We construction
\omega_{M}
from
\omega_{\text{black},n}
from
\widetilde{\omega}\ast\phi
.

Recall that a slope
s_{X}
determines a collection of edge probabilities,
\{p_{i}(s_{X})\}_{i=1}^{D}
. Since
s_{X}\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
,
p_{i}(s_{X})\neq 0
for all

. (Concretely,
p_{i}(s_{X})=\exp(e_{i}\cdot\nabla\sigma(s_{X}))/\sum_{k=1}^{D}\exp(e_{k}\cdot\nabla\sigma(s_{X}))
, see Theorem

.)

Let
X_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\Lambda\cap X
and let
N_{2\delta^{\prime}}(X)
be the width-

tubular neighborhood of
\partial X
. Since
\widetilde{\omega}\ast\phi
is equal to
\widetilde{\omega}
on

,
\omega_{\text{black},n}(e)=p_{i}(s_{X})
for all edges

of type
e_{i}
in
X_{n}^{2\delta^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n}\Lambda\cap(X\setminus N_{2\delta^{\prime}}(\partial X))
. By Corollary
6.16
,
\sup_{e\in E_{n}}|\omega_{M}(e)-\omega_{\text{black},n}(e)|=O(1/n)
, so
\omega_{M}(e)=p_{i}(s_{X})+O(1/n)
for all edges
e
of type
e_{i}
in
X_{n}^{2\delta^{\prime}}
.

On the other hand, by Proposition
4.3
, the discrete flow
p(e)=p_{i}(s_{X})
for all edges
e
of type
e_{i}
is the limit shape for the torus measure with periodic edge weights
w_{i}=p_{i}(s_{X})
on each edge of type
e_{i}
. Let
Z_{X_{n},N,w}^{\text{torus}}
denote the (unnormalized) partition function of the torus measure with these weights on
X_{n}
and let
\mu_{n,N,w}
denote the corresponding probability measure.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Proposition
4.3
and Corollary
6.16
respectively,
\omega_{M}
, and samples from
\mu_{n,N,w}
for
N
large enough given

, have boundary conditions on
\partial X_{n}^{2\delta^{\prime}}
within
O(1/n)
of each other with probability

(they are both close to the discrete flow

.) Further, since

,

for all edges

in

, so

satisfy the strictly non-degenerate condition. In summary, on

, the dimer cover
\omega_{M}
(outside) and a sample from

(inside), satisfy the conditions of the patching theorem (Theorem

), on an annulus of width

.

As such, for

large enough, the dimer cover
\omega_{M}
outside
X_{n}^{2\delta^{\prime}}
can be patched with a sample from
\mu_{n,N,w}
restricted to
X_{n}^{3\delta^{\prime}}
with probability
1-\varepsilon
. We apply this on all
X\in\mathcal{X}
. By Proposition
4.3
, for
n,N
large enough we can assume that all but
\varepsilon
proportion of these dimer covers constructed by patching are in
U_{r/2}(\widetilde{\omega})
.

To compute a lower bound on the number of dimer covers in
U_{r/2}(\widetilde{\omega})
from this patching construction, recall as in Theorem
4.13
that restricted to the homology class
s_{X}
, the weights
w
are gauge equivalent to the uniform edge weights, and hence there are constants
C_{n,N}(w)
such that
Z_{n,N,w}^{s_{X}}=C_{n,N}(w)Z_{n,N}^{s_{X}}
. Therefore for
r^{\prime}
determined by
r
, for
n,N
sufficiently large, we have for all
X_{n}
,

\displaystyle\log Z_{X_{n},N}^{\text{torus}}(U_{r^{\prime}}(s_{X}))\geq\log Z_{X_{n}^{\prime},N}^{s_{X}}+O(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon),

where
X_{n}^{\prime}=X_{n}^{3\delta^{\prime}}
(the region we patched into). Therefore for
n,N
large enough,

\displaystyle\log Z_{n,N}(U_{r}({\omega}))\geq\log Z_{n,N}(U_{r/2}(\widetilde{\omega}))\geq\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\log Z_{X_{n}^{\prime},N}^{s_{X}}+O(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon),

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sigma(s_{X})=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}|X|}\log\frac{Z_{X_{n},N}^{s_{X}}}{(N!)^{n^{d}|X|}}

Hence

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N}(U_{r}({\omega}))}{(N!)^{n^{d}|R|}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\sigma(s_{X})|X|(1-O(\delta^{\prime}))+O(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ent}({\omega})(1-O(\delta^{\prime}))+o_{\delta}(1)+O(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon).

Here
\varepsilon,\delta,\delta^{\prime}
are small constants which go to
0
as

. This completes the proof.
∎

6.4.
Proof of the upper bound

Proof of Lemma
6.8
.

Let
\widetilde{\omega}
be a piecewise constant approximation of
\omega
as in Proposition
6.17
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be the mesh of simplices on which
\widetilde{\omega}
takes constant values,
s_{X}:=\widetilde{\omega}\mid_{X}\,\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
. By Proposition
6.18
,
\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega})=\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)+O(\delta)
, where
\delta
is the scale of the simplices in
\mathcal{X}
. It suffices to prove the upper bound for
\widetilde{\omega}
.

For each
X\in\mathcal{X}
, let
\rho_{X_{n},N}^{\text{free}}
be the multinomial measure on
N
-dimer covers of
X_{n}=X\cap\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
with
free
boundary conditions. Let
Z_{X_{n},N}^{\text{free}}
be the corresponding unnormalized partition function. Recall that
U_{r}(\cdot)
denotes a ball of radius
r
in the Wasserstein metric on flows. We have the bound:

(6.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N}(U_{r}(\widetilde{\omega}))}{(N!)^{n^{d}|R|}}\leq\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\log\frac{Z_{X_{n},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))}{(N!)^{n^{d}|X|}}.

It therefore suffices to understand the asymptotics of
Z_{X_{n},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))
for one
X\in\mathcal{X}
. We can further cover
X_{n}
with boxes
B_{m}
with
(2m)^{d}
vertices (i.e.,
B_{m}\cap W
is a translation of
[0,m-1]^{d}
. We can take

to be of the order
n^{1/2}
, so that only a lower-order proportion of these boxes intersect the boundary of
X_{n}
. Hence it suffices to understand the asymptotics of
Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))
, where
B_{m}
is a box. Let
\rho_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}
be the corresponding probability measure. We split this by boundary condition, i.e. consider

\displaystyle Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))=\sum_{b}\mathbb{P}(b)Z_{B_{m},N}^{b}(U_{r}(s_{X})),

where
b
is a choice of multiplicities for vertices in
\partial B_{m}
, and
\mathbb{P}(b)
is the probability of

under
\rho_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}
.

Since
\Lambda
is reflection symmetric, we can split the torus
\mathbb{T}_{2m}
with
(4m)^{d}
sites into
2^{d}
copies of
B_{m}
. We put the boundary condition
b
on one copy of
B_{m}
, and then reflected boundary condition
b^{\prime}
on all neighboring copies of
B_{m}
in
\mathbb{T}_{2m}
. We repeat this until all copies have a boundary condition, and sample
2^{d}
independent dimer covers with boundary condition
b
(or
b^{\prime}
) in each copy. We then add multiplicity to the remaining edges of
\mathbb{T}_{2m}
, which go between the reflected copies of
B_{m}
, so that all vertices have total multiplicity
N
. For any

this gives us the bound

\displaystyle(Z_{B_{m},N}^{b}(U_{r}(s_{X})))^{2^{d}}\leq\sum_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}Z_{2m,N}^{s},

where
Z_{2m,N}^{s}
is the partition function of
N
-dimer covers of homology class

\mathbb{T}_{2m}
with uniform edge weights. Recall that for

fixed, there are of order

\displaystyle Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))=\sum_{b}\mathbb{P}(b)Z_{B_{m},N}^{b}(U_{r}(s_{X}))\leq\bigg(\sum_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}Z_{2m,N}^{s}\bigg)^{1/2^{d}}

\displaystyle(Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X})))^{2^{d}}\leq\sum_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}Z_{2m,N}^{s}\leq N(4mr)^{d}\max_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}Z_{2m,N}^{s}.

Therefore taking

and applying the definition of

(Definition

), we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nm^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))}{(N!)^{m^{d}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N(4m)^{d}}\log\frac{N(4mr)^{d}\max_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}Z_{2m,N}^{s}}{(N!)^{(2m)^{d}}}

\displaystyle\leq-\max_{s:|s-s_{X}|<r}\sigma(s)

\displaystyle\leq-\sigma(s_{X})+o_{r}(1),

where in the last inequality we use that

is continuous. We can now add together the contributions from all the boxes

used to cover

. Since

, there are

of them intersect

. So for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{X_{n},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))}{(N!)^{n^{d}|X|}}\leq\frac{1}{|X|m^{d}}\bigg(\frac{1}{Nm^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{B_{m},N}^{\text{free}}(U_{r}(s_{X}))}{(N!)^{m^{d}}}\bigg)\leq-|X|(1+O(n^{-1/2}))(\sigma(s_{X})+o_{r}(1)).

Combined with the inequality in (
6.11

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nn^{d}}\log\frac{Z_{n,N}(U_{r}(\omega))}{(N!)^{n^{d}|R|}}\leq\mathrm{Ent}(\widetilde{\omega})+o_{r}(1)=\mathrm{Ent}(\omega)+o_{r}(1)+o_{\delta}(1).

Since

∎

7.
Euler-Lagrange equations and limit shape regularity

In this section we study the regularity of limit shapes for the multinomial dimer model. We saw that limit shapes
exist and are unique in Corollary
6.4
. To avoid degenerate cases, we impose a mild regularity condition on
(R,b)
.

Definition 7.1

(Flexibility)
.

A region and boundary condition
(R,b)
is
flexible
(for
\Lambda
) if for every point
x\in R^{\circ}
, there exists a neighborhood
U\ni x
in
R
and an asymptotic flow
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
such that
\omega\!\mid_{U}\subset\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
almost everywhere.

The flexible condition rules out the possibility that there are subsets
A\subset R
where every extension
\omega
of
b
is valued in
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
on
A
. Intuitively, it can be viewed as the continuum version of the feasible condition. One would expect a condition of this form to be needed for a large deviation principle if we used fixed boundary conditions on the discrete side, instead of allowing them to lie in a shrinking interval, as is the case e.g. for the standard 3D dimer model
[
CSW23
]
. It is straightforward to see by an averaging argument that the flexible condition is equivalent to the following.

Lemma 7.2
.

The pair
(R,b)
is flexible if there exists
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
such that
\omega\!\mid_{R^{\circ}}\subset\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
almost everywhere.

7.1.
Euler-Lagrange equations for a divergence free flow

From the large deviation principle, we saw that the limit shape divergence-free flow exists and is the unique minimizer of

\displaystyle\int_{R}\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

over
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
. This can be rephrased as saying that
\omega
is the unique solution to a system of Euler-Lagrange equations.

Theorem 7.3

(Euler-Lagrange equations for a divergence free flow)
.

The limit shape flow
\omega
for
(R,b)
is the solution to the following system of differential equations:

•

the divergence equation,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\text{div}(\omega(x))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)=0;

•

a collection of
{d\choose 2}
equations:

(7.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{j}}\sigma(\omega(x))-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{i}}\sigma(\omega(x))=0

for all
i\neq j
between
1
and
d
.

Proof.

Since
\omega
is an asymptotic flow it is divergence-free and therefore must solve the first equation. To derive the others, we use that a divergence-free vector field is dual to a closed
(d-1)
form. Explicitly
\omega
is dual to the differential form

\displaystyle A=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(-1)^{k-1}\omega_{k}\,\widehat{\mathrm{d}x_{k}},

where
\widehat{\mathrm{d}x_{k}}=\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{k-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{d}
. Let
\Upsilon^{m}
denote the set of
m
forms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote the differential by
\mathrm{d}:\Upsilon^{d-1}\to\Upsilon^{d}
. Since
\omega
is divergence-free,
\mathrm{d}A=0
. Thus there exists
B\in\Upsilon^{d-2}
such that
\mathrm{d}B=A
. We can write
B
using
{d\choose 2}
functions
B_{i,j}=B_{j,i}
,
i\neq j
:

\displaystyle B=\sum_{i<j}B_{i,j}\widehat{\mathrm{d}x_{i,j}}.

where
\widehat{\mathrm{d}x_{i,j}}
means both
\mathrm{d}x_{i}
and
\mathrm{d}x_{j}
are omitted. Thus

\displaystyle A=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigg[\sum_{i<k}\frac{\partial B_{i,k}}{\partial x_{i}}(-1)^{i-1}+\sum_{j>k}\frac{\partial B_{k,j}}{\partial x_{j}}(-1)^{j}\bigg]\widehat{\mathrm{d}x_{k}}

Hence

\displaystyle\omega_{k}=\sum_{i<k}(-1)^{i-k}\frac{\partial B_{i,k}}{\partial x_{i}}+\sum_{j>k}(-1)^{j-k+1}\frac{\partial B_{k,j}}{\partial x_{j}}

We now use calculus of variations to derive the
{d\choose 2}
equations. Pick
k<\ell
and consider the perturbation
B_{k,\ell}\mapsto B_{k,\ell}+\delta\eta(x)
, where
\eta
is a a smooth function with
\eta\mid_{\partial R}=0
.
This perturbation appears only in
\omega_{k}
and
\omega_{\ell}
. If
\omega
is a minimum, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\delta}\bigg\lvert_{\delta=0}\int_{R}\sigma\bigg(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}+(-1)^{\ell-k+1}\delta\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_{\ell}},\dots,\omega_{\ell}+(-1)^{k-\ell}\delta\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_{k}},\dots,\omega_{d}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\pm\int_{R}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{k}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_{\ell}}-\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{\ell}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mp\int_{R}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\ell}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{k}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{\ell}}\bigg)\eta\,\mathrm{d}x.

In the last equality we use integration by parts, and note that the boundary term vanishes because
\eta
is
0
on
\partial R
. By the fundamental lemma of calculus of variations, since
\eta
is arbitrary, for all pairs
k<\ell
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\ell}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{k}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{\ell}}=0.

∎

Remark 7.4
.

When
d=3
we can instead phrase this in terms of vector calculus. In that case,
\omega
divergence free implies it has a vector potential
V
(dual to the form
B
) such that
\text{curl}(V)=\omega
. The
{3\choose 2}=3
equations each come from perturbing a component of
V=(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3})
.

In dimension
d=2
, the equations above can be rewritten as a gradient variational problem using the height function
h
. The limit shape height function is related to the limit shape flow
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
by
\ast\omega=(-\omega_{2},\omega_{1})=\nabla h
.

Corollary 7.5

(Euler-Lagrange equations for the height function in 2D)
.

Suppose that
h
is the limit shape height function for
(R,b)
. Then
h
is the unique solution to the PDE

\displaystyle\text{div}(\nabla\sigma(\nabla h))=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{1}}(\nabla h)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial s_{2}}(\nabla h)=0.

7.2.
Multinomial limit shapes do not have facets

One of the well-known properties of limit shapes for the standard dimer model (e.g., on the Aztec diamond) is the existence of
facets
in the limit shape
\omega
, i.e., regions where
\omega
takes values in
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Here we show that the limit shapes for the
N\to\infty
multinomial dimer model do not have facets (as long as
(R,b)
is flexible).

Recall that the
amoeba

\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)
is the closure of the set where the multinomial free energy
F(\alpha)
is convex, and that since
F
depends analytically on
\alpha
,
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
(Corollary
4.15
). The fact that
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
implies certain blow-up behavior of
\nabla\sigma(s)
as
s\to s_{0}\in\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
.

Lemma 7.6

(surface tension blow-up)
.

Fix
s_{0}\in\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
and choose any direction
v
such that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough,
s_{0}+\varepsilon v\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
. Then

(7.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\sigma(s_{0}+\varepsilon v)-\sigma(s_{0})}{\varepsilon}=-\infty.

Proof.

Note that
\sigma
is continuous and convex, achieving its minimum value at
0
, and is negative except at the vertices of
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
where it is zero. Hence
\sigma(s_{0}+\varepsilon v)-\sigma(s_{0})<0
for all
\varepsilon>0
. The gradient
\nabla\sigma
is a one-to-one correspondence from
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
to
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
. Since
\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
while
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
is compact, the result follows.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

For the standard dimer model (in two or three dimensions, with all edge weights equal to one), the surface tension does not blow-up if
s_{0}
is a vertex of
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, but it does blow-up if
s_{0}
is along an edge of
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
. Correspondingly, if
(R,b)
is flexible, limit shapes for the standard dimer model can have regions where they are valued at one of the vertices of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, but not along an edge.

As a consequence of the blow-up property for all points on
\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, we now show that the
N\to\infty
multinomial limit shapes have no facets.

Theorem 7.8

(No facets)
.

For any
\Lambda
, if
(R,b)
is flexible, then its limit shape is valued in the interior of
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
almost everywhere on the interior of
R
.

Proof.

Let
\omega
denote the limit shape for
\Lambda,(R,b)
, which is unique by Corollary
6.4
. Suppose for contradiction that there is a positive measure set
A\subset R
such that
\omega\mid_{A}\subset\partial\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
almost everywhere (throughout this proof, we use this restriction notation to mean almost everywhere). Since
(R,b)
is flexible, there exists a flow
\omega_{0}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
such that
\omega_{0}\mid_{R^{\circ}}\subset\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
. We then define:

\displaystyle\omega_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)\omega+\varepsilon\omega_{0}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
\omega_{\varepsilon}\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
and is valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
on
R^{\circ}
. We split
I(\omega_{\varepsilon})
into an integral over
R\setminus A
and an integral over
A
.

Let
M=-\min_{s\in\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)}\sigma(s)>0
, this is finite since
\sigma
is continuous and
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
is compact. Since
\sigma
is convex (Theorem
4.16
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{R\setminus A}\sigma((1-\varepsilon)\omega(x)+\varepsilon\omega_{0}(x))-\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\int_{R\setminus A}\sigma(\omega_{0}(x))-\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\varepsilon M|R\setminus A|.

On the other hand, by Lemma
7.6
, there is a function
K(\varepsilon)>0
such that
\varepsilon K(\varepsilon)\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and for
\varepsilon
small enough given
s_{0},v
as in the statement,

\displaystyle\sigma(s_{0}+\varepsilon v)-\sigma(s_{0})\leq-\varepsilon K(\varepsilon).

Hence there is a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{A}\sigma((1-\varepsilon)\omega(x)+\varepsilon\omega_{0}(x))-\sigma(\omega(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-c\varepsilon K(\varepsilon).

Therefore

\displaystyle I(\omega_{\varepsilon})-I(\omega)\leq\varepsilon M|R\setminus A|-\varepsilon cK(\varepsilon).

For
\varepsilon
small enough this would be negative, which contradicts the claim that
\omega
minimizes
I(\cdot)
. Therefore
A
has measure zero and hence the limit shape
\omega
has no facets.
∎

Theorem
7.8
allows us to leverage classical elliptic regularity results to show that
N\to\infty
multinomial limit shapes in two dimensions are smooth. See e.g.
[
FRRO22
, Chapter 3]
for an overview of regularity theorems for solutions to gradient variational problems. Extending this to higher dimensions would require other ad hoc estimates; see Question
10.2
.

Corollary 7.9

(Smoothness of limit shapes in 2D)
.

If
\Lambda
is a two dimensional lattice and
(R,b)
is flexible, its limit shape height function
h
is a smooth function, and as a consequence the limit shape divergence-free flow
\omega
is a smooth vector field.

Proof.

By Theorem
7.8
, for any
x\in R^{\circ}
there exists a neighborhood
U\ni x
with
\overline{U}\subset R^{\circ}
, and where the limit shape height function
h
has
\nabla h
taking values in contained in a closed set
C\subset\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
. By Corollary
4.16
,
\sigma
is strictly convex on the closed Newton polytope
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
, so on
C
it is smooth and uniformly convex. Hence by elliptic regularity,
h
is smooth on
U
. Since we can apply this for any interior point
x\in R^{\circ}
, it follows that
h
and hence
\omega=\ast\nabla h
are smooth on
R^{\circ}
.
∎

7.3.
Dual Euler-Lagrange equations for the gauge

There is also a system of Euler-Lagrange equations for minimizers of the free energy
F
. Since
\sigma
and
F
are Legendre duals, the gradients
\nabla\sigma:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{A}
and
\nabla F:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{N}
are inverses of each other and any flow
\omega\in\text{AF}(\Lambda,R,b)
has a dual
gauge flow

\alpha:R\to\mathcal{A}
related to it by:

(7.4)

\displaystyle\omega=\nabla F(\alpha)\iff\alpha=\nabla\sigma(\omega).

Using (
7.4
) one can see that the Euler-Lagrange equations for limit shape flow
\omega
in Theorem
7.3
are equivalent to the following Euler-Lagrange equations for
\alpha
.

Theorem 7.10

(Euler-Lagrange equations for the gauge flow)
.

Fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and boundary value for the gauge flow
b^{\prime}
. The limiting gauge flow
\alpha:R\to\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unique solution to the following system of differential equations with boundary value
b^{\prime}
:

•

the equation

(7.5)

\displaystyle\text{div}(\nabla F(\alpha))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}}F(\alpha)=0;

•

a collection of
{d\choose 2}
equations of the form

(7.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=0,

for all
i\neq j
between
1
and
d
.

Remark 7.11
.

There are some technicalities in relating the boundary conditions
b^{\prime}
for the gauge to boundary conditions
b
of the divergence free flow. If
b
has an extension
\omega
such that
\omega\mid_{\partial R}
is a.e. valued in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)^{\circ}
, then we can define
b^{\prime}=T(\nabla\sigma(\omega))
(recall that
T
is the boundary value operator). However, if
\omega
takes values in
\mathcal{N}(\Lambda)
on
\partial R
(which is the case e.g. for the Aztec diamond), then
\nabla\sigma
will not be defined. We do not address this rigorously, but note that, for example, one could approximate the solution by replacing
b
with
(1-\delta)b
and taking
\delta
to zero.

The equations (
7.6
) imply that we can rephrase the Euler-Lagrange equations for the gauge as a gradient variational problem.

Corollary 7.12
.

Fix
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a suitable boundary function
H_{b}
on
\partial R
. If
H
is a weak solution to the differential equation

(7.7)

\displaystyle\text{div}(\nabla F(\nabla H))=0

with
H\mid_{\partial R}=H_{b}
, the limiting gauge flow is
\alpha=\nabla H
.

Remark 7.13
.

Note that we do not derive an explicit description for the suitable boundary conditions
H_{b}
of
H
(where “suitable” means e.g. that there exists a unique solution to the PDE). We note however that
H_{b}
can be well-defined and finite even when
b^{\prime}
(the boundary condition for
\alpha=\nabla H
) is infinite, as is the case e.g. for the Aztec diamond (see Section
9
).

This
gauge function limit shape

H
exists (at least weakly) in any dimension. Unlike the height function, which is Lipschitz and therefore continuous and differentiable almost everywhere, in general dimension,
H
does not a priori have any additional regularity.

8.
Scaling limit of critical gauge functions

Here we show that the limit shape gauge function
H
arises as the scaling limit of critical gauge functions on graphs
R_{n}\subset\frac{1}{n}\Lambda
approximating
R
. Let
g_{n},f_{n}
denote the critical gauge function on the black, white vertices of
R_{n}
respectively. Here we assume that all limiting vertex multiplicities are
1
, even on
\partial R_{n}
. Then these satisfy the
critical gauge equations
:

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\sim v}g_{n}(u)f_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\sum_{v:u\sim v}g_{n}(u)f_{n}(v)

\displaystyle=1

for all
u\in B\cap R_{n}
and
v\in W\cap R_{n}
. By solving for

in the first equation, we can combine these as a single equation in terms of only

. Recall that

are the edge vectors incident to a white vertex in

, and correspondingly the edge vectors incident to a black vertex are
-e_{1},\dots,-e_{D}
.
The equation is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g_{n}(u)}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}g_{n}(u-\frac{1}{n}e_{i}+\frac{1}{n}e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}\qquad\forall\,u\in B\cap R_{n}.

Theorem 8.1
.

Fix a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda
and
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as before. Suppose that
H
is
C^{1}
and solves the gauge PDE:
\text{div}(\nabla F(\nabla H))=0
, and that
g_{n}
is the critical gauge function on black vertices of
R_{n}
with limiting multiplicity

everywhere on
R_{n}
. Further suppose that

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in B\cap\partial R_{n}}|(1/n)\log g_{n}(u)-H(u)|=O(1/n).

We equate
(1/n)\log g_{n}
defined on
B\cap R_{n}
with its linear interpolation to a continuous function. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(1/n)\log g_{n}

exists in the topology induced by the supremum norm on continuous functions and is equal to
H
.

Remark 8.2
.

The limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1/n)\log f_{n}
also exists, but differs by a sign (since all the edge directions per vertex have the opposite sign, etc). Analogous arguments show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1/n)\log f_{n}=-{H}
.

Remark 8.3
.

If

is

, then standard perturbative estimates can be used to show that

is real analytic. The technical issue here is that while

is strictly convex, is not uniformly convex on all of

, and thus e.g. the classical elliptic regularity results of
[

Nas58

do not apply. In 2D, by Corollary

We use in the proof that the solution

is real analytic. To simplify the next statement, we introduce the following “operator” notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(q):=\text{div}\nabla F(\nabla q)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}(q(u)):={q(u)}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}q(u-(1/n)e_{i}+(1/n)e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}

for

.

Definition 8.4
.

We say that

is a subsolution (resp. supersolution) to the equation

if
\mathcal{D}(q)<0
(resp.
\mathcal{D}(q)>0

(resp.

The proof of Theorem

has three main steps: (1) we show that sub/supersolutions to critical gauge equations satisfy a version of the maximum principle (Proposition

), (2) we relate

to

by expanding the critical gauge equations as

(Proposition

), (3) we partition

into small balls and construct perturbations of

which are sub/supersolutions to the critical gauge equations on each ball, and iteratively apply the maximum principle to show that

being close to

Proposition 8.5

(Maximum principle for the critical gauge equations)
.

Suppose that
q,q_{+},q_{-}
are a solution, supersolution, and subsolution respectively to the critical gauge equation
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\cdot)=1
on a region
U\subset B\cap\varepsilon\Lambda
. Then

(i)

the minimum value of
q/q_{-}
over
U
is realized on
\partial U
.

(ii)

the maximum value of
q/q_{+}
over
U
is realized on
\partial U
;

Proof.

We show (i), as the proof of (ii) is analogous. Since
q,q_{-}
are a solution and subsolution respectively we have that for
u\in U
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q(u)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}q(u-(1/n)e_{i}+(1/n)e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q_{-}(u)}

\displaystyle>\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}q_{-}(u-(1/n)e_{i}+(1/n)e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}.

Hence, dividing the second by the first,

\displaystyle\frac{q(u)}{q_{-}(u)}>\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}q_{-}(u-(1/n)e_{i}+(1/n)e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{D}q(u-(1/n)e_{i}+(1/n)e_{j})\bigg)^{-1}}.

On the other hand note that if
a,b,c,d,e,f>0
then

\displaystyle\frac{a}{b}=\frac{c+d}{e+f}\quad\implies\quad\max\bigg\{\frac{c}{e},\frac{d}{f}\bigg\}\geq\frac{a}{b}\geq\min\bigg\{\frac{c}{e},\frac{d}{f}\bigg\}.

We apply this twice to show that

\displaystyle\frac{q(u)}{q_{-}(u)}>\min_{i,j=1,\dots,D}\frac{q(u+(1/n)e_{i}-(1/n)e_{j})}{q_{-}(u+(1/n)e_{i}-(1/n)e_{j})},

in other words,
q(u)/q_{-}(u)
is bounded below by its value at at least one of its neighbors. Iterating this until we reach
\partial U
proves (i).
∎

9.
Computing limit shapes

From the results in previous sections, we have a few approaches for computing limit shapes:

•

solve the Euler-Lagrange equations from Section
7
, either for the flow or gauge;

•

compute the critical gauge functions on a sequence of graphs and take the scaling limit, applying Theorem
8.1
;

•

in two dimensions, solve the Euler-Lagrange equations for the limiting height function.

Here we compute examples where the critical gauge functions turn out have nice closed form expressions in
n
, and use this to explicitly compute all of the equivalent data of the limit shape by applying Theorem
8.1
. We compute: the limiting gauge function, the limit shape divergence-free flow, the limit shape gauge flow, and (in two dimensions) the limit shape height function.

These examples are in fact the only examples we know of families of growing graphs where the critical gauge has a closed form expression in terms of
n
. Finding other examples, and understanding in general the properties a graph must have for its critical weights to have a closed form expression, is an interesting open question; see Question
10.1
.

In Section
9.5
, for two-dimensional lattices where there is a height function, we use the tangent plane method of
[
KP22b
]
to describe the limit shape height function as an envelope of
\kappa
-harmonically moving planes, where
\kappa
is determined by the surface tension.

9.1.
Aztec diamond

The first example we consider is the Aztec diamond, where the critical gauge functions for fixed

[
KP22a
]
. The Aztec diamond limit shape height function is displayed in the Introduction, see Figure

9.4.
Truncated orthants

For any dimension

This is a generalization of an example from

Here we take a linear image of the standard honeycomb lattice so that black vertices are at integer points, and the white vertices at

\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}(u)+\bigg(\lambda-\frac{3}{4\sin^{2}(u)}\bigg)a(u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}(v)+\bigg(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda-\frac{3}{4\sin^{2}(v)}\bigg)b(v)

\displaystyle=0.

The solutions for arbitrary
\lambda
can be computed in terms of
{}_{2}F_{1}
hypergeometric functions. Varying
\lambda
would lead to more solutions, but we note that when
\lambda=1/4
,
a,b
satisfy the same equation whose solution is

\displaystyle a(u)=\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}\cos(u)}{\sqrt{\sin(u)}}.

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants. From this solution we get a family of
\kappa
-harmonic functions
w=\tilde{w}/\kappa^{1/2}
which can be written

\displaystyle w=c_{1}+c_{2}\cos(u)+c_{3}\cos(v)+c_{4}\cos(u)\cos(v)

for arbitrary constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
. In particular we verify that
s=\cos(u)/2
and
t=\cos(v)/2
are
\kappa
-harmonic.

If we specialize to the case of the Aztec diamond, the limiting height function is
h(x,y)={1}/{4}\cdot(2x-1)(2y-1)
. So then
\cos(u)=2s=2h_{x}=2y-1
and
\cos(v)=2t=2h_{y}=2x-1
, and we get

\displaystyle c=h(x,y)-(sx+ty)=st-sx-ty=-\frac{1}{4}(\cos(u)+\cos(v)+\cos(u)\cos(v))

which is indeed also
\kappa
-harmonic, as expected. The graph of the height function
(x,y,h(x,y))
is hence the envelope of the planes

\displaystyle P_{z}=\{x_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\cos(u)x+\frac{1}{2}\cos(v)y-\frac{1}{4}(\cos(u)+\cos(v)+\cos(u)\cos(v)\}.

for
u,v\in[0,\pi]
.

9.5.2.
Honeycomb lattice

Here the surface tension is

\displaystyle\sigma(s,t)=s\log s+t\log t+(1-s-t)\log(1-s-t)

and hence

\displaystyle\text{Hess}_{\sigma}=\bigg(\begin{array}[]{cc}1/s+1/(1-s-t)&1/(1-s-t)\\
1/(1-s-t)&1/t+1/(1-s-t)\end{array}\bigg).

The Euler-Lagrange equation for the height function
h=h(x,y)
,
\nabla h(x,y)=(s,t)
, is

(9.14)

(h_{y}-h_{y}^{2})h_{xx}+2h_{x}h_{y}h_{xy}+(h_{x}-h_{x}^{2})h_{yy}=0.

We find a conformal coordinate
z=u+iv
in the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}=\{\Im(z)>0\}
to be given by:

\displaystyle u(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{s-t(1-s-t)}{(1-t)^{2}}

\displaystyle v(s,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{st(1-s-t})}{(1-t)^{2}}.

This gives

\psi=(\det H)^{1/4}=\frac{1+\sqrt{(u-1)^{2}+v^{2}}+\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}}{\sqrt{2v}}=\frac{1+|z-1|+|z|}{\sqrt{2v}}

from which we find

(9.15)

q=\frac{\Delta\psi}{\psi}=\frac{3}{4v^{2}}.

We need to solve

(-\Delta+\frac{3}{4v^{2}})\tilde{w}(u,v)=0.

Looking for a solution of the form
\tilde{w}(w,u)=a(u)b(v)
leads to, for a constant
C
,

\displaystyle-a^{\prime\prime}(u)+Ca(u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle-b^{\prime\prime}(v)+(-C+\frac{3}{4v^{2}})b(v)

\displaystyle=0.

Here
a
is an exponential function and
b(v)
is a Bessel function:

b(v)=c_{1}\sqrt{v}J_{1}(v\sqrt{C})+c_{2}\sqrt{v}Y_{1}(v\sqrt{C})

where
J_{1},Y_{1}
are the Bessel functions of the first and second kind, resp.
In the special case
C=0
, we have

b(v)=\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}v^{2}}{\sqrt{v}}
and
a(u)=c_{3}+c_{4}u
. This leads to “elementary”
\kappa
-harmonic functions

w=\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}u+c_{3}v^{2}+c_{4}uv^{2}}{1+|z-1|+|z|}

for constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
.
Another family of
\kappa
-harmonic functions are those of the form

\frac{|z-a|}{1+|z|+|z-1|}

for constant
a
(and linear combinations of such).

The function
s,t
are the
\kappa
-harmonic functions

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{1-|z-1|+|z|}{1+|z-1|+|z|}

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\frac{-1+|z-1|+|z|}{1+|z-1|+|z|}.

As an example limit shape, take
c=-\frac{u}{1+|z-1|+|z|}
.
Then solving the linear system

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=sx+ty+c

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=s_{u}x+t_{u}y+c_{u}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=s_{v}x+t_{v}y+c_{v}

for
x,y
and
x_{3}=h
gives

(9.16)

(x,y,h)=\left(\frac{|z|+|z-1|}{2},\frac{|z|+u-1}{2},\frac{|z-1|+u-1}{2}\right).

As
z=u+iv
runs over
\mathbb{H}
the upper half plane, this parameterizes the limit
shape for the truncated orthant (See section
9.4.1
) with vertices
(\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},0)
and
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0)
.
On this region in (
9.16
) we can solve for
h
as a function of
x,y
:

h(x,y)=\frac{(2x-1)(2y+1)}{2(2x+1)},

which indeed satisfies (
9.14
).

10.
Open questions

Question 10.1
.

In Section
9
we saw various examples of growing families of graphs where the critical gauge has an explicit (in fact rational) closed form in terms of
n
. These are the only such examples we know. What are other families of graphs where the critical gauge are rational functions of
n
, or otherwise have a closed form in terms of
n
? What are the properties of these graphs?

Question 10.2
.

Using the height function, we saw that multinomial limit shapes in 2D are smooth from the fact that they do not have facets (Corollary
7.9
). Is it true that multinomial limit shapes are smooth in any dimension? A positive answer to this question would mean that the
C^{1}
condition in Theorem
8.1
is satisfied in any dimension.

Question 10.3
.

On a finite graph as the multiplicity
N
goes to infinity, the fluctuations of the critical edge weights are a Gaussian process, with covariances determined by a version of the graph Laplacian
[
KP22a
]
. What are the scaling limits of these processes?

Question 10.4
.

Here we have studied the
N\to\infty
limit of the multinomial dimer model; the
N=1
case is the standard dimer model. Can anything be said for small
N
, e.g.
N=2
or
N=3
? For example, do the
N
multinomial limit shapes for the Aztec diamond have facets, which decrease in size as
N
grows?

Question 10.5
.

Overlaying two single dimer covers gives a collection of non-intersecting loops and infinite paths, built by alternating between the two dimer covers. Overlaying two dimer covers of the
N
-fold blow up graph, what can about “geography” of the infinite paths, either in the blow up graph or in their projection? Can this be used to say anything about ergodic Gibbs measures for this model?

Question 10.6
.

The multinomial tiling model, of which the multinomial dimer model is a special case, allows for any collection of tiles. What can be said about the “large
N
limits” of other statistical mechanics models, e.g. the square ice model, using the multinomial framework?
