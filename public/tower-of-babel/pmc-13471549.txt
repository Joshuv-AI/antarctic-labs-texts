Smartwatch-derived exercise metrics as predictors of early hypertension: a prospective observational study
Authors: Esma Selva Ateş Ceyhun, Gökhan Ceyhun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly identification of individuals at risk for hypertension is essential for effective cardiovascular disease. Physiological and activity metrics derived from consumer smartwatches may offer a practical, noninvasive approach to identify individuals at increased risk before the clinical onset of hypertension.MethodsIn this 12-month prospective observational study, 230 normotensive adults aged 30–60 years were followed using consumer smartwatches. Baseline wearable predictors were calculated as the mean of the first 30 days of valid data after enrollment and included heart rate variability, resting heart rate, and time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Incident hypertension was defined according to current European guidelines using standardized office blood pressure measurements obtained at follow-up.ResultsDuring follow-up, 28 participants (12.2%) developed hypertension. Individuals who developed hypertension exhibited lower baseline heart rate variability and spent less time in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity compared with those who remained normotensive. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, lower heart rate variability, lower levels of physical activity, and higher body mass index were independently associated with incident hypertension. An interaction between autonomic variability and physical activity was observed, indicating that individuals with both reduced autonomic regulation and low physical activity had the highest predicted risk. Machine-learning models showed improved statistical discrimination compared with clinical variables alone and were used as complementary exploratory analyses.ConclusionsSmartwatch-derived autonomic and physical activity metrics were independently associated with the development of hypertension over a 12-month period. These findings from an observational study suggest a potential role for wearable-derived physiological parameters as digital biomarkers for early hypertension risk stratification, although further validation in larger and externally replicated cohorts is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06073-4.Keywords: Hypertension, Heart rate variability, Physical activity, Smartwatch, Wearable technology

Introduction

Hypertension is a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and premature mortality worldwide. Its global prevalence remains high, and substantial gaps persist in awareness, treatment, and control across many populations [1, 2]. Conventional office-based blood pressure measurements provide limited insight into early physiological changes and may fail to identify individuals at increased long-term risk [3].

Recent advances in wearable technology have enabled continuous, real-world monitoring of cardiovascular and behavioral parameters through consumer smartwatches [4]. Beyond simple monitoring, the clinical utility of these devices lies in their ability to provide longitudinal, high-frequency data that can complement sporadic office-based assessments, offering clinicians a more comprehensive view of a patient’s physiological trajectory and facilitating earlier identification of cardiovascular risk in various clinical settings. Reduced HRV has been associated with impaired autonomic balance and increased sympathetic activity, mechanisms implicated in the early pathogenesis of hypertension [5–7]. Similarly, insufficient physical activity contributes to endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and impaired blood pressure regulation [8, 9].

Although several cohort studies have demonstrated associations between autonomic dysfunction, physical inactivity, and incident hypertension, most prior work has relied on short-term recordings or single physiological parameters [10, 11]. The potential value of integrating multiple smartwatch-derived autonomic and activity metrics into a unified predictive framework remains insufficiently explored. Moreover, while smartwatch-based cardiovascular measurements have demonstrated acceptable validity under certain conditions [12], their clinical relevance for early hypertension risk stratification requires careful interpretation.

Therefore, this prospective observational study aimed to evaluate whether baseline smartwatch-derived autonomic and exercise parameters are associated with the development of incident hypertension over 12 months in initially normotensive adults. We hypothesized that lower HRV, reduced physical activity, and their statistical interaction would be associated with a higher risk of developing hypertension, without implying causality.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational study was conducted between January 2024 and January 2026. A total of 230 normotensive adults aged 30–60 years were recruited. At the baseline enrollment visit, participants’ height and weight were measured by trained clinical staff using standardized equipment to calculate the body mass index (BMI). Inclusion criteria were age 30–60 years and baseline systolic blood pressure < 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg. Exclusion criteria included known hypertension, cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, thyroid dysfunction, heavy smoking (defined as > 20 cigarettes per day), excessive alcohol intake (defined as > 14 units/week for women and > 21 units/week for men), obesity with body mass index ≥ 35 kg/m², heavy smoking, excessive alcohol intake, sleep apnea, pregnancy, and incomplete Beurer BM27), following standardized protocol.All enrolled participants completed the 12-month follow-up.

Beyond the primary exclusion criteria, we strictly excluded participants using medications known to affect heart rate or autonomic balance, such as beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and anti-arrhythmic drugs, to ensure that baseline HRV metrics reflected intrinsic autonomic regulation. Baseline health status was verified through clinical history and physical examination at the time of enrollment.

Smartwatch-derived parameters

Participants primarily presented to the clinics for routine health screenings or primary cardiovascular prevention. Only individuals confirmed to be free of organic cardiovascular, metabolic, or chronic inflammatory diseases following a standard clinical evaluation were enrolled. This ensured that baseline HRV and MVPA metrics were not influenced by pre-existing pathological conditions.

Participants used consumer smartwatches capable of exporting physiological data through the Apple Health ecosystem. The majority of participants (84.3%) used Apple Watch Series 9 devices, while the remaining participants used compatible wearable devices (including earlier Apple Watch generations and validated third-party smartwatches) integrated within the same Apple Health ecosystem. Data were harmonized through the Health Application Programming Interface (API) framework to ensure consistent variable extraction across different models. When equivalent compatible devices were used, data were harmonized through the same Health API framework to ensure consistent variable extraction. Device-specific operating system versions and algorithmic updates were not systematically recorded and are therefore acknowledged as a limitation. Participants primarily used Apple Watch devices compatible with the Apple Health data ecosystem. When equivalent devices were used, only those capable of exporting data through the same health data framework were included to ensure consistent variable extraction. Wearable-derived variables were harmonized at the level of exported metrics rather than device-specific raw signals. Baseline wearable predictors were defined as the mean of the first 30 consecutive days of valid data following enrollment, prior to outcome assessment. This time window was selected to capture habitual physiological and activity patterns while minimizing reverse causation.

A valid day was defined as a day with at least 16 h of smartwatch wear time, encompassing both daytime activity and nocturnal rest, to ensure successful recording of physiological and activity data. Participants were specifically encouraged to wear the devices during sleep to facilitate the collection of resting heart rate and nocturnal heart rate variability metrics. Sleep status and efficiency were determined using the wearable’s automated algorithms, which integrate triaxial accelerometry and heart rate variability data.Baseline wearable predictors were calculated as the mean of the first 30 consecutive days after enrollment, requiring a minimum of 20 valid days within this period. Participants who did not meet these criteria were excluded from analysis.

Smartwatch derived parameters refer to the daily measurements obtained from the wearable device (e.g., HRV, resting heart rate, and physical activity metrics). Wearable predictors were defined as baseline summary variables calculated as the mean values of these smartwatch-derived parameters across the first 30 consecutive days of valid data following enrollment. Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was defined as the total minutes per day spent in activities ≥ 3 metabolic equivalents, as estimated by device algorithms.

In this study, resting periods refer to intervals of physical inactivity including both nocturnal sleep and periods of sedentary stillness during the day as automatically detected by the smartwatch’s proprietary algorithms. Similarly, baseline values for other wearable predictors (e.g., resting heart rate, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, sedentary time, and sleep efficiency) were calculated as the average of daily measurements during the same baseline period.

Data collection protocol

Data collection was divided into two primary phases:

Baseline assessment

Clinical data (age, sex, BMI) and standardized office blood pressure measurements were obtained using a calibrated oscillometric device. Smartwatch-derived predictors (HRV, RHR, MVPA, VO2 max, and sleep efficiency) were collected continuously over the first 30 days post-enrollment via the Apple Health API.

12-month follow-up

The primary outcome (incident hypertension) was assessed through repeat standardized office blood pressure measurements and a review of newly initiated antihypertensive medications. No wearable-derived data from the follow-up period were included in the primary predictive models.

Clinical evaluation and outcome definition

Clinical Evaluation: All participants underwent a baseline clinical evaluation at the time of enrollment to ensure they met the inclusion criteria. This evaluation consisted of a standardized physical examination, a detailed review of medical history, and office-based blood pressure measurements to confirm a baseline normotensive status (systolic BP <140 mmHg and diastolic BP <90 mmHg).

This evaluation consisted of a standardized physical examination, a detailed review of medical history, and office-based blood pressure measurements to confirm a baseline normotensive status (systolic BP <140 mmHg and diastolic BP <90 mmHg).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

All covariates included in the regression analyses were measured at baseline. The primary predictors of interest were HRV and MVPA selected a priori based on the study hypothesis. Other variables (baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, age, sex, body mass index, resting heart rate, sedentary time, estimated VO2 max, and sleep efficiency) were included as adjustment covariates to account for potential confounding. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent associations with incident hypertension. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent associations with incident hypertension. Covariates included age, body mass index, heart rate variability, resting heart rate, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, and sleep efficiency. A total of 6 predictors were included in the multivariable regression model. Given the limited number of outcome events (n = 28), predictor selection was restricted based on clinical relevance and the study hypothesis to reduce the risk of overfitting.An interaction term between HRV and physical activity was included. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, all of which were below 2.5.

Monthly averages were defined as the mean values of wearable-derived variables calculated separately for each 30-day period during follow-up. “30-day changes” referred to the difference between consecutive monthly averages (e.g., month 2 minus month 1). These variables were examined only in secondary exploratory analyses to explore temporal trends and were not included in the primary regression models.

Machine-learning analyses were performed as complementary exploratory analyses to examine potential non-linear relationships and feature interactions. Hyperparameter tuning was not extensively performed; models were trained using predefined parameter settings to reduce overfitting risk given the limited number of outcome events. A fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility of model training and validation procedures.

Model performance was evaluated using stratified cross-validation, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values were calculated from out-of-fold predictions. Comparisons between models using the DeLong test were therefore performed on out-of-fold predicted probabilities rather than on an independent external test set. Because calibration metrics or calibration plots were not formally assessed, calibration-related interpretations were avoided and model performance was interpreted primarily in terms of discrimination. Model comparisons using the DeLong test were performed on out-of-fold predicted probabilities obtained from stratified cross-validation, rather than on an independent external test set.

Baseline characteristics of participants according to incident hypertension status

There were no significant baseline differences between those who developed hypertension and those who remained normotensive regarding sex distribution (p = 0.49), smoking status (p = 0.26), or sleep efficiency (p = 0.19). However, participants in the incident hypertension group were significantly older (48.7 ± 6.9 vs. 45.3 ± 7.4 years, p = 0.014) and had a higher BMI (28.2 ± 3.7 vs. 26.4 ± 3.1 kg/m², p = 0.009) compared to the normotensive group. Notably, the hypertension group exhibited markedly lower baseline HRV (42.6 ± 10.8 vs. 56.1 ± 12.9 ms, p < 0.001), reduced MVPA levels (38.4 ± 14.2 vs. 57.9 ± 16.7 min/day, p < 0.001), and higher sedentary time (598 ± 90 vs. 532 ± 84 min/day, p = 0.006).

Consistent with international guidelines, physical activity was also quantified in weekly totals. The cohort demonstrated a mean weekly MVPA of 389.2 ± 125.3 min. Notably, participants who remained normotensive were significantly more likely to meet the recommended threshold of ≥ 150 min of weekly MVPA compared to those who developed incident hypertension (92.1% vs. 64.3%, p = 0.001).

Multivariable logistic regression

Lower heart rate variability, lower levels of physical activity, and higher body mass index were independently associated with incident hypertension. HRV (OR: 0.88 per 5 ms decrease, 95% CI: 0.81–0.96; p = 0.004), MVPA (OR: 0.85 per 10 min/day increase, 95% CI: 0.76–0.94; p = 0.002), and higher body mass index (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21; p = 0.008) were independently associated with incident hypertension. The complete numerical results of the multivariable model are summarized in Table 2. A statistically significant interaction between HRV and physical activity was observed, indicating that the association between autonomic variability and hypertension risk differed according to activity level. The model demonstrated good calibration and discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.82.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, lower baseline heart rate variability and reduced physical activity derived from consumer smartwatches were independently associated with the development of hypertension over 12 months in initially normotensive adults. Individuals exhibiting both reduced autonomic regulation and low physical activity demonstrated the highest predicted risk.

These findings are consistent with prior cohort studies linking autonomic dysfunction and physical inactivity to incident hypertension [6, 7]. Importantly, smartwatch-derived physiological metrics were used as relative, longitudinal markers rather than as replacements for clinical-grade measurements. Recent evidence suggests that smartwatch-derived heart rate variability should be interpreted cautiously due to device- and algorithm-specific limitations [14, 15]. Accordingly, our conclusions emphasize association and risk stratification rather than diagnostic or causal inference.

Baseline blood pressure was a significant predictor of 12-month outcomes. However, after rigorous adjustment for baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, smartwatch-derived HRV and MVPA remained independently associated with incident hypertension. This suggests that autonomic and lifestyle-related metrics may provide incremental prognostic value beyond that obtained from a single office blood pressure measurement.

The inverse association between physical activity and hypertension risk observed in our cohort further reinforces the importance of lifestyle behaviors in cardiovascular regulation. Regular aerobic activity improves endothelial function, reduces arterial stiffness, and enhances baroreflex sensitivity, mechanisms that collectively lower blood pressure [16]. Participants with higher MVPA in our study exhibited better autonomic profiles and lower incident hypertension rates, suggesting a dependent relationship between physical activity and autonomic function. The significant HRV×MVPA interaction term found in our regression model underscores this point, indicating that low HRV and low physical activity jointly identify a high-risk phenotype for hypertension development. The observed interaction between HRV and physical activity should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and requires confirmation in larger, externally validated cohorts. These findings align with current guidelines emphasizing physical activity as a first-line preventive measure for blood pressure control [13].

Contribution of this study is the demonstration that real-world, continuously recorded smartwatch data can provide potentially informative physiological signals relevant to cardiovascular risk. Prior research has validated the accuracy of smartwatch-derived HRV and heart rate metrics compared with gold-standard ECG and Holter recordings [17]. While cuffless blood pressure estimation remains limited in precision, the use of surrogate physiologic markers such as HRV (physiological) and MVPA (behavioral) circumvents calibration challenges and offers more stable longitudinal trends [18]. The present findings therefore highlight a pragmatic approach to hypertension risk assessment that relies on metrics already available in most commercially used wearable devices.

Importantly, smartwatch-derived physiological metrics were used as relative, longitudinal proxy markers rather than as replacements for clinical-grade measurements. Recent evidence suggests that smartwatch-derived heart rate variability, typically based on photoplethysmography (PPG) rather than electrocardiography, should be interpreted cautiously due to susceptibility to motion artifacts, sampling inconsistencies, and device-specific algorithmic limitations. Accordingly, our findings emphasize associations and risk stratification viewing these metrics as digital biomarkers rather than definitive diagnostic or causal inference.

The consistency between traditional regression and machine-learning analyses supports the robustness of the observed associations. However, machine-learning models were used as complementary tools to explore non-linear relationships and were not interpreted as clinically superior.

Smartwatch-derived physiological and activity metrics may offer substantial advantages for longitudinal analyses because they enable continuous, real-world data collection over extended periods. Compared with traditional clinic-based assessments, wearable devices allow repeated measurements of parameters such as heart rate variability and physical activity, potentially improving temporal risk prediction and facilitating earlier identification of cardiovascular risk trajectories. Future studies integrating smartwatch-derived data with longitudinal modeling approaches may further elucidate dynamic changes in cardiovascular health and support more personalized prevention strategies. Although device heterogeneity exists, the use of a unified data ecosystem (Apple Health) minimizes discrepancies in high-level metric calculation. However, since manufacturer-specific background algorithms for raw signal processing may vary between models, this remains a recognized limitation of real-world wearable research.

Beyond risk stratification, the clinical utility of smartwatch-derived metrics lies in their potential to trigger timely lifestyle interventions. While identifying high-risk individuals through low HRV and physical activity patterns is a necessary first step, the real-world effectiveness of such digital biomarkers depends on patient adherence to subsequent behavioral modifications. It remains to be determined whether providing users with automated, wearable-based risk feedback can successfully motivate sustained weight loss or increased exercise in clinical practice. Future interventional studies are warranted to evaluate the ‘actionability’ of these digital alerts and their efficacy in delaying the clinical onset of hypertension in high-risk phenotypes.

While our primary findings were consistent across different models, the limited number of incident hypertension cases (n = 28) may affect the stability of the multivariable and machine-learning analyses. Specifically, the HRV x MVPA interaction and the predictive performance of the gradient-boosting models should be interpreted strictly as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. These results may reflect sample-specific patterns that require rigorous testing in larger, more diverse populations to confirm their generalizability.

Several limitations merit consideration. The observational design precludes causal inference. Hypertension diagnosis relied on Office based blood pressure measurements without ambulatory confirmation. Device-specific variability and algorithmic changes may limit generalizability. Measurement reliability was not directly assessed within this study. Another limitation relates to device heterogeneity. While most participants used a uniform model, the inclusion of various devices may introduce algorithmic variability. Specifically, the exact brand and model names for a small subset of non-Apple devices could not be systematically recorded, preventing a complete model-by-model sensitivity analysis. While we adjusted for clinical covariates such as age and BMI, it remains possible that smartwatch-derived HRV and physical activity metrics partially reflect unmeasured underlying health differences or lifestyle patterns. Future studies should integrate more comprehensive health markers to further isolate the independent predictive value of these digital biomarkers. Furthermore, we chose not to exclude non-Series 9 users to maintain sufficient statistical power for our incident event analysis. As wearable derived metrics may vary depending on device-specific algorithms, this may have introduced measurement variability and should be considered when interpreting the results. Several factors known to influence blood pressure, including detailed smoking status of non-heavy smokers, dietary patterns, psychosocial stress, and other lifestyle behaviors, were not comprehensively captured in the present study. These unmeasured factors may have influenced the observed associations and should be considered in future research. In addition, the relatively narrow age range and the requirement for regular smartwatch use may limit generalisability to populations with lower technology literacy or different demographic characteristics. Finally, the relatively health-conscious nature of the study population may limit external validity. In addition, the relatively small number of incident hypertension cases (n = 28) may have limited model stability and increased the risk of overfitting, particularly in multivariable and machine-learning analyses.

Conclusions

Lower smartwatch-derived heart rate variability and reduced physical activity were independently associated with the development of hypertension over 12 months in normotensive adults. These findings suggest a potential role for wearable-derived parameters as digital biomarkers for early risk stratification; however, given the exploratory nature of our machine-learning and interaction analyses, further validation in larger and externally replicated cohorts is essential.
