Sheffer polynomials and the s-ordering of exponential boson operators
Robert S. Maier

Abstract
The s s -ordered form of any product of single-mode boson creation and annihilation operators, containing only a single annihilator, is computed explicitly. The s s -ordering concept originated in quantum optics, and subsumes normal, symmetric (Weyl), and anti-normal ordering for any two operators satisfying a canonical commutation relation. Because the s s -ordering map can be viewed as producing a function of a complex variable, its inverse is a quantization map that takes such “classical” functions to quantum operators. The explicit s s -ordered expressions are derived with the aid of a parametric family of Sheffer polynomial sequences (or equivalently an exponential Riordan array of polynomial coefficients), called the Hsu–Shiue family. To yield orderings interpolating between normal and anti-normal, this family is extended in an intricate way.

&\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{{\rm e}^{-\lambda}\exp\left[\left(1-{\rm e}^{\lambda}\right){a^{\dagger}}a\right]}\,\mathclose{:}_{A}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

2.1
Definitions

The single-mode Heisenberg–Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
is an
associative algebra over the scalar field
\mathbb{C}
generated by
noncommuting indeterminates
a,{a^{\dagger}}
. Any element
of
\mathcal{W}
can be written as a polynomial in
a,{a^{\dagger}}
, i.e.,
a linear combination over
\mathbb{C}
of finite products
of
a,{a^{\dagger}}
.
\mathcal{W}
has a unit (multiplicative identity),
written
1
, which is the empty product multiplied by the scalar
1
.

The representation as polynomials in
a,{a^{\dagger}}
is not unique, as the
product on
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
[a,{a^{\dagger}}]=1
.
\mathcal{W}
can
be identified with
\mathbb{C}\langle a,{a^{\dagger}}\rangle/\allowbreak(a{a^{\dagger}}-\penalty{a^{\dagger}}a-\penalty 1)
, the
quotient of the
free
algebra
\mathbb{C}\langle a,{a^{\dagger}}\rangle

by the two-sided ideal generated by
a{a^{\dagger}}-\penalty\penalty{a^{\dagger}}a-\penalty 1
. Thus each element of
\mathcal{W}
is an equivalence
class of polynomials in
a,{a^{\dagger}}
, though a distinguished
representative can be singled out by imposing an ordering criterion.

One can also view
\mathcal{W}
as
\mathbb{C}[{a^{\dagger}}][a]
, the ring of
polynomials in
a
with coefficients that are polynomials in
{a^{\dagger}}
,
equipped with a product operation that comes from
[a,{a^{\dagger}}]=1
. In
effect, this imposes normal ordering. Alternatively, one can view

\mathcal{W}
as the isomorphic algebra
\mathbb{C}[a][{a^{\dagger}}]
, which
imposes anti-normal ordering. The isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[{a^{\dagger}}][a]\leftrightarrow\mathbb{C}[a][{a^{\dagger}}]
is also based on
[a,{a^{\dagger}}]=1
:
any polynomial can be written in either normal or anti-normal ordered
form.

So, any
G\in\mathcal{W}
can be viewed as a finite sum
over
\mathbb{C}
of terms of the type
{a^{\dagger}}^{n}a^{m}
, or equivalently
one of terms of the type
a^{m}{a^{\dagger}}^{n}
. To avoid the problem of
convergence when dealing with
{\rm e}^{G}
, which is formally an
infinite sum
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G^{n}/n!
and is not an element
of
\mathcal{W}
, one can embed
{\rm e}^{G}
in the one-parameter
family
{\rm e}^{\lambda G}\in\mathcal{W}[[\lambda]]
, a power series
in an indeterminate
\lambda
with coefficients in
\mathcal{W}
.

It is useful to define an extension algebra

\overline{\mathcal{W}}{(a)}
of
\mathcal{W}
, each element of which
is a sum of
{a^{\dagger}}^{n}a^{m}
terms, finite or infinite, but with the
exponent of
a
taking only a finite number of values in all. (The
product on
\overline{\mathcal{W}}{(a)}
comes from
[a,{a^{\dagger}}]=1
.)
One can equally well say that each element is a sum of
a^{m}{a^{\dagger}}^{n}

terms, finite or infinite, with the same condition imposed.
Similarly, one defines the extension algebra

\overline{\mathcal{W}}{({a^{\dagger}})}
of
\mathcal{W}
, where it is the
exponent of
{a^{\dagger}}
that can take only a finite number of values.

\overline{\mathcal{W}}{(a)}
,
\overline{\mathcal{W}}{({a^{\dagger}})}
could
be denoted by
\mathbb{C}[[{a^{\dagger}}]][a]
,
\mathbb{C}[[a]][{a^{\dagger}}]
. The
power-series algebras
\mathbb{C}[[{a^{\dagger}}]][a][[\lambda]]
,

\mathbb{C}[[a]][{a^{\dagger}}][[\lambda]]
will also appear.

2.2

The following defines

2

\displaystyle G(a,{a^{\dagger}})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{F^{(N)}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})}\,\mathclose{:}_{N},

(5a)

\displaystyle G(a,{a^{\dagger}})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{F^{(A)}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})}\,\mathclose{:}_{A},

3
Riordan, Sheffer, and Hsu–Shiue polynomials

The following definitions and facts are standard. For the theory of
(exponential) Riordan arrays and polynomials, introduced under that
name by Shapiro et al. to derive combinatorial identities, see

[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. For the equivalent theory of
Sheffer polynomial sequences (originally called Sheffer of
A

zero), which are used in approximation theory and elsewhere, see

[
9
,
10
]
. In their present form, both emerged
from the work of G.-C. Rota and collaborators on the refashioning of
the classical umbral calculus as a finite operator
calculus
[
32
,
33
]
.

Hsu and Shiue
[
34
]
defined a parametric family of generalized
Stirling numbers, which now have their own literature

[
35
, Chapter 4]
. For any choice of parameters, they can
be viewed as the elements of a exponential Riordan array, the “row

3.1
Exponential Riordan arrays and polynomials

Let
d,h\in\mathbb{C}[[z]]
be formal power series in
z
of order
0

and
1
respectively, meaning that their first nonzero terms are the

z^{0}
and
z^{1}
terms; so
1/d
and the compositional inverse
\bar{h}

both exist as formal power series. (For instance,
d,h
may be
Maclaurin expansions of functions
d(z),h(z)
analytic at
z=0
.) One
usually requires that
d
be monic,
d=1\cdot z^{0}+\penalty O(z^{1})
,
and often that
h=1\cdot z^{1}+\penalty O(z^{2})
. Then, a polynomial
sequence
s_{n}(t)
,
n\geqslant 0
, where
\deg s_{n}=n
, with EGF

\mathcal{S}(t,z)=d(z){\rm e}^{th(z)}
that is an element of

\mathbb{C}[t][[z]]
, is defined by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}(t)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\mathcal{S}(t,z),

(25)

with
s_{0}(t)\equiv 1
. The sequence
s_{n}(t)
is said to be
exponential Riordan for the ordered pair
[d,h]
, and is denoted
briefly by
\mathbf{R}[d,h]
. Such notations as

s_{n}(t)=\mathbf{R}[d,h]_{n}(t)
and

\mathcal{S}(t,z)=\mathbf{R}[d,h](t,z)
are also used.

Equivalently, one can define a triangular array of
coefficients
s_{n,k}
,
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{n,k}t^{k}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\mathcal{S}(t,z),

(26)

so that

s_{n,k}=\frac{n!}{k!}\,[z^{n}]\left[d(z)h(z)^{k}\right],

(27)

where
[z^{n}]
extracts the coefficient of
z^{n}
from the element of

\mathbb{C}[[z]]
that follows. Then
s_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{n,k}t^{k}
, so the
s_{n}(t)
are the row polynomials of the array.
The array
s_{n,k}
can be viewed as the lower-triangular part of an
infinite matrix, and is called an exponential Riordan array. The
notation
s_{n,k}=\mathbf{R}[d,h]_{n,k}
is also used, with

\mathbf{R}[d,h]
alternatively signifying the matrix as a whole.

Row-finite lower-triangular matrices form a group under matrix
multiplication, and exponential Riordan arrays turn out to form a
subgroup, which will be denoted by
\mathfrak{R}
. The following is a
fundamental fact.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

In the exponential Riordan group
\mathfrak{R}
the product operation
is
\mathbf{R}[d_{1},h_{1}]\,\mathbf{R}[d_{2},h_{2}]=\allowbreak\mathbf{R}[(d_{2}\circ\penalty h_{1})d_{1},\allowbreak h_{2}\circ\penalty h_{1}]
, and the inversion operation is
\mathbf{R}[d,h]^{-1}=\allowbreak\mathbf{R}[1/(d\circ\penalty\bar{h}),\bar{h}]
, where

\bar{h}
is the compositional inverse of
h
, i.e.,
\bar{h}(h(z))=\penalty z
. The multiplicative identity is
[1,z]
, which
signifies the infinite identity matrix.

For matrix inverses, the notation

\mathbf{R}[d,h]^{-1}\mathrel{=:}\mathbf{R}\overline{[d,h]}=\mathbf{R}[\bar{d},\bar{h}],

(28)

where
\bar{d}=1/(d\circ\bar{h})
, can be used. Note that
\bar{d},\bar{h}
, like
d,h
, are formal power series of orders
0
and
1
.

The generating functions of the theory facilitate the computation of
matrix–vector products.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

For any column vector

with

, an element

\mathbb{C}[[t]]
, and for any exponential Riordan array

equaling

by

(29)

or symbolically by

. Then, its EGF

, an element of

equals

, where

\cdot
and

are the binary operations of product and
composition on formal power series.

3.2
Sheffer polynomials

The theories of Sheffer and exponential Riordan polynomials are
equivalent, being related by the inversion operation

[
36
,
37
]
. A polynomial sequence
s_{n}(t)
,
n\geqslant 0
,
where
\deg s_{n}=n
and usually
s_{0}(t)\equiv 1
, is said to be Sheffer
for the ordered pair
[g,f]
if and only if it is exponential Riordan
for the pair
[d,h]
equal to
\overline{[g,f]}
, the inverse of

[g,f]
in the group
\mathfrak{R}
, which will be written as
[\bar{g},\bar{f}]
. The sequence as a whole will be denoted briefly by

\mathbf{S}[g,f]
, or equivalently by
\mathbf{R}[d,h]=\mathbf{R}[\bar{g},\bar{f}]
.

It is convenient in the Sheffer theory to use the indeterminate
D
in
the series
g,f
, and
z
in the series
\bar{g},\bar{f}
. That is, to
reduce ambiguity one adopts the convention that

g,f\in\mathbb{C}[[D]]
and
\bar{g},\bar{f}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]]
. This
makes it possible to write both
z=f(D)
and
D=\bar{f}(z)
, related
by series reversion.

So a polynomial sequence
s_{n}
is Sheffer for
[g,f]
, where

g,f\in\mathbb{C}[[D]]
are of order
0
and
1
respectively, and

\bar{g},\bar{f}\in\mathbb{C}[[z]]
are likewise, if

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}(t)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\bar{g}(z){\rm e}^{t\bar{f}(z)}=\frac{1}{g(\bar{f}(z))}{\rm e}^{t\bar{f}(z)},

(30)

and one writes
s_{n}=\mathbf{S}[g,f]_{n}=\mathbf{R}[\bar{g},\bar{f}]_{n}
and

s_{n,k}=\mathbf{S}[g,f]_{n,k}=\mathbf{R}[\bar{g},\bar{f}]_{n,k}
, etc.

Many well-known polynomial sequences can be viewed as Sheffer
sequences
[
9
,
38
]
, and there is some overlap with the
orthogonal polynomials of theoretical physics. An example is the
sequence of Hermite polynomials
\mathop{{}\it He}\nolimits_{n}(t)
, which is

\mathbf{S}[{\rm e}^{D^{2}/2},D]
or equivalently
\mathbf{R}[{\rm e}^{-z^{2}/2},z]
. The
\mathop{{}\it He}\nolimits_{n}(t)
are orthogonal with
respect to an appropriate measure (namely,
{\rm e}^{-t^{2}/2}\,{\rm d}t
), as they happen to satisfy a three-term recurrence relation.
The squared Hermite polynomials
[\mathop{{}\it He}\nolimits_{n}(\sqrt{t})]^{2}

turn out to be
\mathbf{S}[\sqrt{1-\penalty 2D}/\allowbreak(1-\penalty D),\allowbreak D/(1-\penalty D)]
or
\mathbf{R}[1/\sqrt{1-\penalty z^{2}},\allowbreak z/\allowbreak(1+\penalty z)]
, but do not
satisfy such a relation.

For a Sheffer sequence
\mathbf{S}[g,f]
, the formal power series

z=f(D)
may be the Maclaurin expansion of an elementary function
of
D
, without the compositional inverse
D=\bar{f}(z)
appearing in
the EGF (
30
) having the same property. An example
is the sequence of Abel polynomials
A_{n}(t)=t(t-\penalty n)^{n-1}
,
which turns out to be
{\bf S}[1,D{\rm e}^{D}]
. (See
[
9
]
.)
But the compositional inverse of
z=f(D)=D{\rm e}^{D}
is the Lambert

W
function
D=\bar{f}(z)=\allowbreak W_{L}(z)=\allowbreak z-\penalty z^{2}+\penalty\frac{3}{2}z^{3}+\penalty\cdots
, which is not an elementary
function. It is more convenient to refer to the Abel sequence as
{\bf S}[1,D{\rm e}^{D}]
than as
{\bf R}[1,W_{L}(z)]
. The same phenomenon
will appear in Sec.
5
.

The following reveals the deep connection between the Sheffer theory
and the Heisenberg–Weyl algebra. It also clarifies why the seemingly
arbitrary symbol ‘
D
’ is used here as a power-series variable.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
9
,
17
,
39
]
)
.

For any pair

of orders

Here,

The operators

3.3
Hsu–Shiue arrays and polynomials

Hsu–Shiue arrays
\mathcal{HS}(A,B;r)
, where
A,B,r\in\mathbb{C}
,
are a parametric family of exponential Riordan arrays, i.e., a family
of elements of the group
\mathfrak{R}
, with associated row
polynomials and EGF’s. They can be introduced as follows.

The classical Stirling numbers (of the second kind), denoted by

\left\{{n\atop k}\right\}
or
S(n,k)
, count the partitions of an

n
-set into
k
disjoint, nonempty subsets. They make up
a triangular array indexed by
n,k
with
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, and satisfy

x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\{{n\atop k}\right\}x^{\underline{k}},

(33)

where
x^{\underline{k}}=x(x-1)\dots(x-k+1)
is the falling factorial
with unit “stride.” So, they are coefficients of connection between
bases of
\mathbb{C}[x]
. The sequence of row polynomials

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\{{n\atop k}\right\}t^{k}
,
n\geqslant 0
, which could be
called Stirling polynomials (of the second kind), but are more often
called Touchard, Bell, or exponential polynomials, is the
exponential Riordan sequence
{\bf R}[1,\allowbreak{\rm e}^{z}-\penalty 1]
or equivalently the Sheffer sequence
{\bf S}[1,\allowbreak\log(1+\penalty D)]
.

The generalized Stirling numbers of Hsu–Shiue
[
34
]
are a
family
\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n,k}=\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n,k}(A,B,r)
that subsumes the classical
Stirling numbers and many previous generalizations. For

A,B,r\in\mathbb{C}
, they are defined by

(x+r)^{\underline{n},A}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}HS_{n,k}(A,B,r)\,x^{\underline{k},B},

(34)

where
(x+r)^{\underline{n},A}
,
x^{\underline{k},B}
are falling
factorials with stride not necessarily equal to unity, e.g.,

x^{\underline{k},B}=x(x-\penalty B)\dots[x-\penalty(k-\penalty 1)B]
. The
\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n,k}
reduce to
\left\{{n\atop k}\right\}
if

(A,B,r)=(0,1,0)
. The parametric row polynomials

\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n}(t;\,A,B,r)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n,k}(A,B,r)t^{k},\qquad n\geqslant 0,

(35)

will be called Hsu–Shiue polynomials. (Note that
\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{0}(t)\equiv 1

and
\deg\mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n}=n
for all
n
.)

By examination, this
n
-indexed sequence of polynomials is
exponential Riordan for the pair

[d_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;A,r),h_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;A,B)]
, where if

A,B\neq 0
,

\displaystyle d_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,r)\mathrel{:=}(1+Az)^{r/A},

(36)

\displaystyle h_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,B)\mathrel{:=}\frac{(1+Az)^{B/A}-1}{B},

the right-hand sides being elements of
\mathbb{C}[[z]]
. (The cases

A=0
,
B=0
are handled by taking limits.) One way of confirming
this is to verify that eq. (
34
), viewed as an equality
between a column vector indexed by
n
and a matrix–vector product,
is consistent with Proposition
3.2
.

It is easily checked that

\overline{[d_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;A,r),h_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;A,B)]}={[d_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;B,-r),h_{\textit{HS}}(\cdot;B,A)]},

(37)

which is a statement of
duality
: as infinite lower-triangular
matrices and as elements of the exponential Riordan
group
\mathfrak{R}
, Hsu–Shiue arrays
\mathcal{HS}(A,B,r)
and

\mathcal{HS}(B,A,-r)
are inverses of each other.
(Cf.
[
34
]
.) So, one can write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{HS}(A,B,r)&={\bf R}[d_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,r),h_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,B)]\\
&={\bf S}[d_{\textit{HS}}(D;B,-r),h_{\textit{HS}}(D;B,A)]\end{aligned}

(38)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathop{{}\it HS}\nolimits_{n}(t;\,A,B,r)&={\bf R}[d_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,r),h_{\textit{HS}}(z;A,B)]_{n}(t)\\

Theorem 5.3
.

For all integer

with

has for all

the

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\lambda({a^{\dagger}}^{L}a{a^{\dagger}}^{R})}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{\mathcal{T}({a^{\dagger}}a,\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e};\,e,1,-L,R;\,s)}\,\mathclose{:}_{s}

(61)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{\bar{g}_{T}(\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e};\,s)\exp\left[({a^{\dagger}}a)\bar{f}_{T}(\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e};\,s)\right]}\,\mathclose{:}_{s},

,

\displaystyle\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{(1-e\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e})^{-R/e}\exp\left\{\left[\bigl(1-e\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e}\bigr)^{-1/e}-1\right]{a^{\dagger}}a\right\}}\,\mathclose{:}_{N}

\displaystyle\textrm{if }s=-1

\displaystyle\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{\exp\left\{-a{a^{\dagger}}\left[\bigl(1+e\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e}\bigr)^{-1/e}-1\right]\right\}(1+e\lambda{a^{\dagger}}^{e})^{-L/e}}\,\mathclose{:}_{A}

Proof.

The idea is this:
G={\rm e}^{\lambda({a^{\dagger}}^{L}a{a^{\dagger}}^{R})}
equals

\mathopen{:}\,\mathrel{F^{(s)}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})}\,\mathclose{:}_{s}
, where

F^{(s)}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is some function that satisfies the
diffusion equation (
23
), a PDE that evolves

F^{(s)}
toward smaller values of
s
. (In this proof, for clarity
the commuting arguments of
F^{(s)}
will be written as

\alpha,\alpha^{*}
and not as
a,{a^{\dagger}}
. The notational convention
in quantum optics that dependence on
\alpha,\alpha^{*}
really means
dependence on a single complex variable

\alpha=\allowbreak\alpha_{r}+\penalty{\rm i}\alpha_{i}
will
briefly be followed.)

In principle, one could start with

and integrate
the diffusion equation backward in
s
toward

, as
one does in quantum optics when smoothing the Glauber–Sudarshan

P
-function (
s=\penalty+1

s=\penalty-1
)
[
2
,
3
]
.
The differential operator

in the PDE is to be
interpreted as
\Delta/4
, with

\Delta=\partial^{2}/\partial\alpha_{r}^{2}+\partial^{2}/\partial\alpha_{i}^{2}

\alpha
-plane.

However, to avoid convergence questions the exponential operator

is viewed in this paper as an element of
\mathcal{W}[[\lambda]]
,
and

is accordingly an element
of
\mathcal{W}_{c}[[\lambda]]
. So

is not a single
function on the complex
\alpha
-plane (i.e., a function
of
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{i}
or informally of
\alpha,\alpha^{*}
), and the

be used. Instead,

at each value of

is a power series
in
\lambda

in
\alpha,\alpha^{*}
, and the series is formal: it is never summed.
The following proof should be read with this in mind.

The statement of the theorem incorporates the
s=\pm 1

for

from Theorem
4.4
. To match their

(63)

where
\lambda\neq 0
and the function

is to be
determined. Changing the independent variables
\alpha,\alpha^{*}

(really
\alpha_{r},\alpha_{i}
) to
t=\alpha^{*}\alpha
,

z=\lambda{\alpha^{*}}^{e}
converts the diffusion
equation (
23
) to an equation of diffusion type
on the
(t,z)
-plane,

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial s}+\mathcal{X}\right]V(t,z;\,s)=0,\qquad\mathcal{X}\mathrel{:=}\frac{1}{2}\left(t\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+ez\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\,\partial z}\right),

(64)

irrespective of
\lambda
; so that formally,

V(\cdot,\cdot;\,s)=\exp\left[(s_{0}-s)\mathcal{X}\right]\,V(\cdot,\cdot;\,s_{0}),\qquad s<s_{0}.

(65)

There is an obvious

V(t,z;\,s)\varpropto(s_{0}-s)^{-1}{\rm e}^{-2t/(s_{0}-s)},\qquad s<s_{0},

(66)

for any real
s_{0}
. Depending on
t=\alpha^{*}\alpha
but not on

, for

s
-ordered quasiprobability density on the complex

\alpha
-plane coming from the density operator of the
vacuum state
[
2
, §12.4.4]
, showing how the

P
-function (
s=\penalty+1
), which is

\delta
-function located at
\alpha=0
, broadens into
a Gaussian as

is decreased and the

-function
(
s=\penalty-1
) is approached. But it is qualitatively different from the
expressions
V(t=\alpha^{*}\alpha,\allowbreak z=\lambda{\alpha^{*}}^{e};\pm 1)
appearing in
Theorem
4.4
.

To incorporate those functions, make the further Riordan/Sheffer
Ansatz

V(t,z;\,s)=\mathbf{R}[\bar{g}(\cdot;s),\bar{f}(\cdot;s)](t,z)=\mathbf{S}[g(\cdot;s),f(\cdot;s)](t,z),

(67)

which says that

(68)

where as usual,
\bar{g}(z;s)=1\cdot z^{0}+O(z^{1})
and

, with

and

and

gives

\displaystyle 2\bar{g}_{s}+ez\bar{f}\bar{g}_{z}+(\bar{f}+ez\bar{f}_{z})g=0,

(69a)

\displaystyle 2\bar{f}_{s}+ez\bar{f}\bar{f}_{z}+\bar{f}^{2}=0,

(69b)

where subscripts denote partial derivatives. These are coupled
nonlinear PDEs for
\bar{g}(z;s)
,
\bar{f}(z;s)
. The two-point
boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bar{g}(z;\,s=-1)=(1-ez)^{-R/e},

\displaystyle\bar{g}(z;\,s=+1)=(1+ez)^{-L/e},

(70a)

\displaystyle\bar{f}(z;\,s=-1)=(1-ez)^{-1/e}-1,

\displaystyle\bar{f}(z;\,s=+1)=1-(1+ez)^{-1/e}

4.4
.

The function (or more accurately formal infinite series)

With its aid, the independent variables

, and the dependent

\displaystyle-2f_{D}\,g_{s}+D^{2}f_{D}\,g_{D}+(Df_{D}+ef)g=0,

and

\displaystyle g(D;\,s=-1)=(1+D)^{-R},

\displaystyle g(D;\,s=+1)=(1-D)^{-L},

\displaystyle f(D;\,s=-1)=\left[1-(1+D)^{-e}\right]/e,

\displaystyle f(D;\,s=+1)=\left[(1-D)^{-e}-1\right]/e.

(72b)

\displaystyle F(\eta)=\frac{\left(\frac{-1-\eta}{2}\right)^{-e}-\left(\frac{1-\eta}{2}\right)^{-e}}{e},

so that

\displaystyle z=f(D;\,s)=\frac{\left[1-(\frac{1+s}{2})D\right]^{-e}-{\left[1+(\frac{1-s}{2})D\right]^{-e}}}{e},

(75)

with expansion

. With this choice of

It has the similarity solution

g(D;\,s)=\left[\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\eta+1}{\eta-1}\right)^{e+1}\right]G(\eta),

(76)

where the function

To match the boundary conditions (
72a
), choose

(77)

so that

g(D;\,s)=\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)\left[\frac{1+(\frac{1-s}{2})D}{1-(\frac{1+s}{2})D}\right]^{L}+\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)\left[\frac{1-(\frac{1+s}{2})D}{1+(\frac{1-s}{2})D}\right]^{R},

(78)

with

. Comparing
(

given in (

(79)

Therefore

(80)

∎
