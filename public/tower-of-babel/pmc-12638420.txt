Research trends and clustering analysis of postoperative infections following lumbar interbody fusion: a bibliometric study
Authors: Yanxiao Liu, Hua Wang, Lei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a cluster analysis of research on postoperative infection following lumbar interbody fusion. It aims to identify major research themes, reveal development trends, and explore collaborative patterns in this field. Publications on postoperative infection and lumbar interbody fusion from 1998 to2024 were analyzed using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R-bibliometrix to visualize collaboration, keyword co-occurrences, and research trends. We analyzed a total of 349 publications, observing a general upward trend in research output. The USA emerged as a leader in both the number of publications and citations, with significant contributions from the University of California System and the Mayo Clinic. Zhou Yue was noted for his considerable scholarly influence. Among high-impact journals, Spine was identified as a prominent publication outlet. The keyword co-occurrence analysis highlighted the hotspots, such as types of postoperative infections, risk factors of postoperative infections, clinical outcomes and drainage treatment, and minimally invasive spinal surgery. Additionally, keyword burst analysis indicated a growing research interest in areas such as “risk factors”, “indirect decompression” and “follow up.” This bibliometric analysis provides a comprehensive overview of the research landscape concerning postoperative infection in lumbar interbody fusion. Current studies focused on research hotspots included types of postoperative infections, risk factors of postoperative infections, clinical outcomes and drainage treatment, and minimally invasive spinal surgery. Introduction

Lumbar interbody fusion is a well-established surgical intervention for the treatment of various spinal disorders, including degenerative disc disease, spondylolisthesis, and spinal instability [1, 2]. This technique entails the excision of a compromised intervertebral disc, which is subsequently replaced with a bone graft or cage to facilitate spinal fusion, thereby alleviating pain and restoring stability [2]. However, despite its therapeutic advantages, the procedure is not devoid of risks, particularly with regard to the potential for postoperative complications [3, 4]. Among these complications, postoperative infection emerges as a significant concern due to its detrimental impact on patient recovery, the necessity for additional interventions, and its association with increased morbidity and healthcare costs [5].

Postoperative infection represents a serious complication following lumbar interbody fusion surgery, manifesting through symptoms such as fever, localized pain, swelling, and wound discharge, and significantly affecting patient recovery and healthcare resource utilization [5]. Recent studies have elucidated the incidence, risk factors, and management strategies pertinent to this critical issue. Lee et al.. reported a substantial contamination rate of local bone autografts employed in posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), with a positive culture result being significantly associated with postoperative spinal infection, thus emphasizing the necessity for aseptic techniques during graft preparation [6]. Pei et al. identified several independent risk factors for surgical site infection following PLIF and instrumentation, which include advanced American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, length of preoperative stay, presence of heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and renal insufficiency. This underscores the necessity for optimized surgical conditions and postoperative antibiotic management [7]. Furthermore, Liu et al.. developed a nomogram model that incorporates factors such as smoking, diabetes, intraoperative blood loss, and serum biomarkers to predict the risk of surgical site infection following transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, thereby demonstrating its potential as a clinically useful tool [5]. Collectively, these studies highlight the complexity of postoperative infections following spinal surgery, and the prevalence of such occurrences necessitates a comprehensive and aggregated analysis to enhance understanding and address this critical issue within patient care.

Bibliometrics employs statistical methodologies to analyze scholarly literature, quantifying research output and impact [8]. This research approach measures publications, citations, and other metrics to evaluate the dissemination and influence of scholarly ideas, proving valuable for identifying trends, tracking scientific evolution, and assessing the impact of studies and researchers within the field [8, 9]. Bibliometric studies offer a broad perspective on the scholarly landscape, thereby enhancing our understanding of its structure and dynamics. Given the dearth of bibliometric analyses focusing on postoperative infection following lumbar interbody fusion in the existing literature, the objective of this study is to address this gap by conducting a comprehensive bibliometric analysis. This study systematically evaluates the current body of knowledge regarding postoperative infections following lumbar interbody fusion to identify trends and guide future research.

Methods

Search strategies and data collection

We performed a systematic literature search within the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) due to its focus on high-quality, globally peer-reviewed academic publications, primarily traditional academic literature, including journal articles, conference proceedings, and books [10]. Besides, the WoSCC database is multidisciplinary and comprehensive, offering a complete citation network and key bibliometric indices (i.e., JCR, IF, and H-index) [11]. Therefore, we selected it to obtain global academic information for bibliometric analysis according to previous studies [12–14]. In the present study, the search terms were as follows: (TS=(postoperative infection* OR Post-operative infection* OR infection after surgery OR surgical site infection* OR surgical incision infection*)) [15, 16] AND TS=(“lumbar interbody fusion”) [17]. The search was limited to English-language articles to ensure consistency in linguistic analysis. To reduce the influence of database updates, we completed the search and retrieval process on September 12, 2024. Data extraction was standardized in textual format and included publication and citation metrics, titles, author information, institutional affiliations, geographical locations, and keywords. Additionally, journal names were recorded to support subsequent bibliometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we extracted relevant data from the retrieved bibliographic records and used Microsoft Excel to identify and calculate bibliometric indicators. For visualization analysis, we leveraged three robust bibliometric tools to perform a comprehensive examination of the scholarly data: VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), CiteSpace (version 6.3.R3), and the R package “bibliometrix” (version 4.3.3).

VOSviewer, a versatile software tool, played a pivotal role in mapping institutional collaborations, author co-authorships, co-publications, citations, and co-citations [18]. By using VOSviewer, we were able to visualize and explore complex networks of collaboration and relationships within the academic domain, providing insights into the interconnections among authors, institutions, and publications. To identify emerging trends and research hotspots in our field of study, we employed VOSviewer for keyword co-occurrence analysis and used CiteSpace to detect keyword bursts. For the keyword burst analysis, we used CiteSpace (version 6.1.R3) with parameters set for time slicing from January 2000 to August 2024. The time slicing was configured to span one-year intervals, and node types were set to keywords. When nodes were designated as keywords, the threshold was set to the top N per slice (N = 4), and pruning was conducted using the Pathfinder network merging algorithm. These settings allowed us to perform a visual analysis that generated a timeline map of keywords within the field of postoperative infection in lumbar interbody fusion, enabling us to trace the evolution of research focus and identify key concepts over time. The R package “bibliometrix” was used to conduct a comprehensive bibliometric analysis. In bibliometrics, the h-index is a measure of both the productivity and citation impact of an author or a journal, representing the number of publications that have been cited at least as many times as the index value [19]. The impact factor (IF) reflects the average number of citations a journal’s articles receive over a specific period, indicating its influence within the academic community. Journal Citation Reports (JCR) further classify journals into quartiles based on their IF rankings within subject categories, providing a relative measure of a journal’s citation performance.

Results

Overview of publications

In this research, we analyzed a corpus of 349 scholarly articles (Fig. 1A). These articles were authored by 1,972 individuals affiliated with 927 institutions across 168 countries and regions. Altogether, these contributions generated 349 manuscripts, which were published in 88 peer-reviewed journals and collectively cited a total of 7,188 references. Our analysis (Fig. 1B) shows an overall upward trend in the number of publications, with a notable inflection point between 2017 and 2018, when the number of publications dropped significantly from 25 to 14. After this decline, the growth resumed, peaking in 2021 with 34 publications. In 2022 and 2023, publication numbers have remained steady in the high thirties.

China’s research garnered 1,138 citations, South Korea’s articles were cited 779 times, and Japan’s received 640 citations. The USA not only led in total publications (TP) and TC but also had an average of 37.4 citations per publication. China’s publications averaged 11.5 citations. Among the 36 nations involved in international research collaborations (Fig. 2B), the USA led with the highest number of cooperative efforts, with a total link strength of 39. Germany and Italy followed with 22 and 18 link strengths, respectively.

Highly cited literatures

In this study, we identified the most highly cited articles within the domain of lumbar interbody fusion research. The most cited article, “The Bagby and Kuslich method of lumbar interbody fusion: History, techniques, and 2-year follow-up results of a United States prospective, multicenter trial,” published in Spine (IF = 2.6) in 1998, had a significant impact, with a total of 347 citations [20]. The second most influential article, “Intraoperative and early postoperative complications in extreme lateral interbody fusion: an analysis of 600 cases,” published in Spine in 2011, received 317 citations [21]. Rounding out the top three, “Rates of infection after spine surgery based on 108,419 procedures: a report from the Scoliosis Research Society Morbidity and Mortality Committee,” published in Spine in 2011, amassed an impressive 386 citations [22].

Keyword analysis

We identified a total of 119 keywords that occurred at least three times across the articles. Based on the results of the analysis (Table S4), which ranked the keywords by their total link strength in co-occurrence networks, we identified several key terms that significantly contributed to the research landscape. The keyword “complications,” with a frequency of 45 and a link strength of 133, emerged as a prominent term. “Minimally invasive” also appeared with substantial link strength of 84. “Spondylolisthesis” was another keyword with significant link strength of 52. “Outcomes” (38) highlighted the emphasis on patient outcomes in spinal research.

Finally, four possible study directions were primarily identified: Cluster 1: types of postoperative infections, with terms like “sepsis”, “spondylodiscitis” and “urinary tract infection”; Cluster 2: risk factors of postoperative infections, including “risk factors” and “obese”; Cluster 3: clinical outcomes and drainage treatment, featuring keywords such as “clinical outcome” and “reoperation”; Cluster 4: minimally invasive spinal surgery to improve the postoperative infections with keywords, such as “minimally invasive spinal surgery” and “minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar”.

The timeline, visualized through the keyword co-occurrence network analysis, was depicted with color-coded nodes representing different periods. Purple nodes, indicating the year 2014, included keywords such as “spondylolisthesis,” “degenerative spondylolisthesis,” and “BMP deformity,” which were central to research discussions during that period. Green nodes, which likely represented the period between 2016 and 2018, encompassed terms like “minimally invasive surgery” and “spinal surgery,” reflecting a growing interest in less invasive procedures and their broader applications in treating spinal pathologies. Yellow nodes, corresponding to 2020, featured keywords such as “infection,” “surgical site infection,” and “outcomes,” indicating a more recent focus on postoperative complications and the importance of patient outcomes in spinal surgery research (Fig. 5A).

Discussion

General information

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of a substantial body of literature on postoperative infection in lumbar interbody fusion, highlighting the growing interest and expanding research efforts in the field. The publication trend mirrored the dynamic evolution of the research focus, with a marked downturn in 2017–2018, followed by a robust recovery and a peak in 2021, suggesting a resurgence of interest and activity in the area. The sustained high level of publications in 2022 and 2023 indicated continued momentum in research output, reflecting a vibrant and active research community. Notably, our analysis of keywords revealed four hotspots, such as types of postoperative infections, risk factors of postoperative infections, clinical outcomes and drainage treatment, and minimally invasive spinal surgery. Besides, “risk factors,” “indirect decompression,” and “follow up” were prominent terms, indicating an increasing emphasis on meticulous postoperative surveillance and the ongoing advancement of minimally invasive techniques within the field of spinal surgery.

Research hotspots

Keywords provide a critical lens to uncover shifting research priorities and emerging themes. The co-occurrence network revealed four major clusters:

Cluster 1: Types of Postoperative InfectionsCluster 1 delved into various types of postoperative infections after lumbar interbody fusion. Overall, urinary tract infection (UTI), surgical site infection (SSI), pneumonia and sepsis were the most common morbidity events [23]. Kelly et al. reported that the incidence of postoperative UTI in patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis is 10.9% [24]. SSI, a common complication but can lead to serious consequence after spinal surgeries, varied in incidence rate, from 0% to 20.0% [7]. As was estimated, SSI was associated with 11 additional days of hospitalisation per patient and 20% increased risk of readmission within 30 days of surgery [25]. For those patients older than 65 had a significant increase in risk of pneumonia and sepsis undergoing anterior lumbar interbody fusion [26]. These infections sequentially affect the quality of life, prolong rehabilitation and hospitalisation time and increase the medical expenses of patients [27].

Cluster 2: Risk FactorsCluster 2 focused on identification of risk factors of postoperative infections following lumbar interbody fusion. Although advances have been made in infection control practices, including improved operating room ventilation, sterilization methods, barriers, surgical technique, and availability of antimicrobial prophylaxis, the infection rates are still high. Thus, understanding and preventing the risk factors for postoperative infections are paramount for optimizing patient outcomes, minimizing morbidity rate, and containing health care costs [28]. Several studies have investigated its risk factors, such as male, obesity, smoking, increased age, diabetes, etc [29–31]. However, the results of these studies were inconsistent. A recent meta-analysis including 26 studies with 41,624 patients showed that fusion approach (anterior vs. posterior; anterior vs. combined), osteotomy, transfusion, a history of diabetes and surgery, hypertension, surgical location (cervical vs. thoracic; lumbar vs. thoracic), osteoporosis and the number of fusion levels were associated with postoperative infections after spinal surgery, rather than age, sex, a history of smoking, body mass index, fusion approach (posterior vs. combined), surgical location (cervical vs. lumbar), duration of surgery, blood loss, using steroid, dural tear and albumin [31]. This article not only guides spinal surgeons during clinical practice, but also shares decision-making and communication with patients undergoing spinal surgery because some of these factors, such as diabetes, can be adapted during workup. Enhanced management for high-risk population may be a more efficient prevention strategy.

Cluster 3: Clinical Outcomes and Drainage TreatmentCluster 3 highlighted the adverse clinical outcomes by postoperative infections following lumbar interbody fusion. Infection after spinal surgery is a serious postoperative complication, which increases the incidences of patient morbidity and mortality, length of hospital stay, hospital readmission rate, and a staggering additional cost of approximately $200,000 per case [28, 32]. A NSQIP study of 410,930 patients showed that unplanned readmission (47.3% vs. 3.8%), reoperation (43.5% vs. 2.0%), and 30-day mortality (0.9% vs. 0.4%) were all greater in the postoperative infection group when compared to the group without postoperative infection [32]. Hematoma is commonly considered as an excellent culture medium for infections and wound complications [33]. Although negative pressure drainage is theoretically more effective in the prevention of postoperative hematomas compared with natural pressure drain [34], it may result in excessive drainage and blood loss [35]. Additionally, drains remain controversial due to no clear guidelines, and there is little evidence of drain use in spinal procedures, including drain types, indication, depth of placement, duration, and the time of removal based on fixed time or drain volume [34]. Thus, it is urgent to establish the standard management for postoperative infection following lumbar interbody fusion.

Cluster 4: Minimally Invasive Spinal SurgeryCluster 4 indicated minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) may minimize postoperative infections. A larger surgical bed predisposes to the risk of postoperative infection and increased pain, resulting in longer hospital stays [36]. Theoretically, MISS has garnered interest as a viable alternative to open surgery with added benefits, including decreased blood loss, lower surgical site infection rates, and improved cosmesis due to much smaller wound bed [37]. Specifically, MISS markedly reduces (2- to 16-fold) the risk of postoperative infection (0.29%) when compared with other large open spine surgery series [38]. On the contrary, Wu et al. showed that no significantly lower incidence of infections with MISS compared to incidences in the literature, suggesting that the increased rates of postoperative infections may not be best addressed by MISS alone [39]. Further investigation and the development of a multidisciplinary approach that addresses other factors, such as a prolonged recumbent state, compromised immunological state and non-healing wound, may bring better patients’ prognosis.

Specifically, MISS markedly reduces (2- to 16-fold) the risk of postoperative infection (0.29%) when compared with other large open spine surgery series [38]. On the contrary, Wu et al. showed that no significantly lower incidence of infections with MISS compared to incidences in the literature, suggesting that the increased rates of postoperative infections may not be best addressed by MISS alone [39]. Further investigation and the development of a multidisciplinary approach that addresses other factors, such as a prolonged recumbent state, compromised immunological state and non-healing wound, may bring better patients’ prognosis.

Research trends

The identification of “bone morphogenetic protein” as a keyword with a significant citation burst between 2009 and 2012 in our study is particularly intriguing. This period of intense research interest may suggest a pivotal moment in the evolution of spinal surgery practices, where the focus on bone growth and regeneration within the spinal column became a critical area of exploration [40]. The surge in citations during this timeframe likely reflects the growing body of evidence supporting the use of bone morphogenetic proteins in enhancing fusion rates and potentially reducing the need for autografts or allografts [41, 42]. Although bone morphogenetic protein may reduce the need for autogenous bone grafts during surgery and may increase fusion rates, their use is also associated with a number of complications, especially a possible increased risk of postoperative infection. This increased risk may be related to the local inflammatory response caused by bone morphogenetic protein, the carrier material becoming a breeding ground for bacteria, or the body’s immune response to bone morphogenetic protein. To reduce the risk of infection, physicians need to carefully select the indication for bone morphogenetic protein, use the appropriate dose and carrier, strictly adhere to aseptic surgical techniques, and use antibiotics to prevent infection after surgery, while closely monitoring the patient’s recovery to detect and manage the infection in a timely manner [41, 42].

The substantial citation burst for “follow up” from 2012 to 2018 underscores the growing recognition of the importance of long-term patient monitoring and outcomes assessment in spinal research [43, 44]. Through regular follow-up, doctors can detect the abnormal symptoms and signs of patients after surgery, such as redness, pain, fever or exudation and other signs of infection, and quickly take appropriate treatment measures, such as antibiotic treatment or surgical intervention if necessary. In addition, follow-up can help to assess the patient’s overall recovery process, including pain control, improved mobility, and fusion results, thereby optimizing treatment options and improving surgical success. Therefore, systematic follow-up is not only crucial for early identification and treatment of postoperative infection, but also an important link to ensure postoperative recovery and surgical results.

The identification of “instrumentation” as a keyword with a citation burst between 2014 and 2016. This surge in citations suggests that the role of surgical instrumentation in spinal procedures became a focal point of scientific inquiry during this timeframe [45]. The emphasis on instrumentation likely reflects advancements in spinal stabilization technologies, as well as a growing body of research aimed at evaluating the efficacy and safety of various implantable devices used in spinal surgeries [45]. High-precision instruments used during surgery, such as navigation systems and minimally invasive surgical tools, can help surgeons precisely place implants and perform fusion operations, reducing damage to surrounding tissue and thus reducing the risk of postoperative infection. Therefore, instrumentation not only plays a role in surgical operations, but is also an important part of postoperative infection management and patient safety. Through precise surgical procedures and effective postoperative monitoring, instrumentation helps to improve the success rate of lumbar interbody fusion and reduce postoperative complications.

The emergence of “risk factors” with a significant citation burst in 2020 to 2022 points to a more recent intensification of research aimed at identifying and mitigating factors that may complicate spinal procedures or affect patient outcomes [46]. Infection after lumbar interbody fusion is associated with multiple risk factors, including the patient’s own conditions such as age, diabetes, obesity, smoking habits, and immune status, which may impair the patient’s ability to heal and fight infection. Surgery-related factors, such as duration of surgery, technique, aseptic conditions, and sterilization of the implant, also have a significant impact on the risk of infection. In addition, postoperative management measures, including the appropriate use of antibiotics, wound care, and patient education, are equally critical. This focus on risk stratification may be indicative of a maturing field, where the emphasis is not only on performing surgeries but also on optimizing patient selection, preoperative planning, and postoperative management to minimize adverse outcomes [47]. The heightened interest in risk factors suggests a shift towards a more personalized and precision medicine approach in spinal surgery, aiming to tailor treatments to individual patient profiles and to enhance the predictability and reliability of surgical outcomes.

The observation that “indirect decompression” experienced a significant surge in citations from 2020 to 2022, as identified in our study, suggests a concentrated period of scientific focus on this minimally invasive surgical approach within the field of spinal surgery [48]. The primary purpose of indirect decompression is to relieve symptoms by reducing the compression of the spinal structure on the nerve elements, rather than directly manipulating the soft tissues where the risk of infection is higher. This technique may indirectly reduce the risk of postoperative infection by reducing surgical trauma and soft tissue damage. This surge likely reflects an increasing recognition of the potential benefits of indirect decompression, such as reduced tissue disruption, faster recovery times, and equivalent or superior clinical outcomes compared to traditional direct decompression techniques [49]. The significant citation increase for “indirect decompression” reflects the evolving landscape of spinal surgery, emphasizing the need for ongoing research into minimally invasive techniques that enhance patient outcomes and reduce surgical trauma. The surge in citations for these keywords signifies the shifting research priorities and the advancement of novel approaches within the field of spinal surgery. These terms encapsulate the growing interest in minimally invasive techniques, patient-centered outcomes, and the pursuit of safer, more effective surgical methods.

Limitations

However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our analysis. The findings were constrained by the data available within the WoSCC and the specific time frame of our search, which may not have encompassed the full spectrum of global research contributions. Additionally, by focusing on English-language publications, our study may have inadvertently excluded relevant research from non-English-speaking regions, potentially limiting the breadth of our analysis. Despite these constraints, the insights gleaned from this study provided a valuable foundation for further investigation into postoperative infections in lumbar interbody fusion procedures.

Conclusion

This study employed a bibliometric analysis of the literature on postoperative infection following lumbar interbody fusion. The research hotspots were primarily focused on types of postoperative infections, risk factors of postoperative infections, clinical outcomes and drainage treatment, and minimally invasive spinal surgery. Future studies on deeper understanding of risk factors and the adoption of indirect decompression strategies will facilitate early diagnosis and early intervention for decreased postoperative infections. The significant findings of this study, particularly the identification of key risk factors and emerging techniques, possess clinical significance, as they can inform surgical practices and potentially improve patient outcomes by mitigating the risks associated with postoperative infections.
