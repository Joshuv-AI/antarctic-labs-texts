Exploring the drivers of hesitancy toward coronavirus disease 2019 vaccine among Syrian refugees residing in Mount Lebanon: A qualitative study
Authors: Masa Al Fakih, Lara Obeid, Emile Whaibeh, Nivine Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Refugee populations, vulnerable to coronavirus disease 2019, face low vaccine uptake, partly because of hesitancy.•The study aimed to understand the reasons behind vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees in Mount Lebanon.•Hesitancy is driven by limited knowledge, trust, and access to medical information.•Non-mandatory vaccination and socio-cultural factors also contribute to hesitancy.•Implementing responsive educational interventions are means to combat vaccine misinformation.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccine hesitancy, Refugee populations, Qualitative study, Lebanon

Introduction

Vaccination is considered one of the most cost-effective measures to control and eradicate numerous infectious diseases, reducing preventable deaths worldwide [1]. More specifically, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines have been shown to reduce COVID-19–related morbidity and mortality, with emerging evidence suggesting reduced transmission in some populations, including vulnerable groups such as refugees [2,3]. Globally, refugees’ vulnerability to poverty, health problems, and social inequalities has been exacerbated during COVID-19 pandemic [4,5]. Their situation can be further worsened in resource-limited countries like Lebanon which hosts around 1.5 million Syrian refugees, representing approximately 25% of its population and one of the highest refugee-to-population ratios globally [6]. This exacerbates strain on Lebanon’s fragmented health care system amid economic, political, and security crises, along with shortages of food and medication.

To address global vaccine inequities during COVID-19 pandemic, the World Health Organization (WHO), France, and The European Commission launched the COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access initiative to ensure vaccine access for all, including vulnerable groups [7]. The WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts’ (SAGE) framework for the prioritization and allocation of COVID-19 vaccine advises countries to prioritize these groups, such as refugees, to promote equity and help control pandemics, as excluding them increases transmission risk [8,9].

Vaccine availability alone does not ensure protection, as vaccine hesitancy can hinder COVID-19 containment efforts [10]. Vaccine hesitancy is defined as the delay in rejecting or accepting vaccine uptake despite the availability and accessibility of vaccination services [11]. Listed among WHO’s top 10 global health threats 2019, vaccine hesitancy affects over 90% of the countries [12]. It has been associated with outbreaks and increased mortality [13]. In pandemics, it poses significant barrier to achieving herd immunity [7].

WHO’s SAGE has established an explanatory model of vaccine hesitancy constituting of a multi-level influence: (1) contextual factors (e.g. politics, media, religion), (2) group/individual factors (e.g. peer influence, attitudes, beliefs, and trust in health care system), and (3) vaccination-specific determinants (e.g. administration mode, vaccination schedule, and vaccine cost) [14,15].

The WHO SAGE framework informed the conceptual framing of this study by guiding the exploration of contextual, individual, and vaccine-specific factors influencing COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees.

Lebanon’s COVID-19 vaccine rollout was complicated by severe supply shortage [6] and a phased, age-based prioritization strategy. Pfizer-BioNTech vaccines were in limited supply and allocated primarily to older adults and high-risk groups, whereas AstraZeneca, the most widely available vaccine in the country, was initially restricted to adults aged ≥40 years, with walk-in eligibility later extended to those aged ≥30 years during specific campaigns [16,17]. As a result, younger populations were effectively excluded from vaccination at the time, not because of hesitancy, but because of eligibility constraints. Meanwhile, refugee populations showed low COVID-19 vaccine uptake despite being disproportionately impacted by the pandemic. According to the statistics of COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access–Inter-Ministerial and Municipal Platform for Assessment, Coordination and Tracking (IMPACT) platform between February and August 2021, only 1.9% of the Syrian refugees have registered for vaccination [18]. This low uptake can be partly attributed to vaccine hesitancy [8]. Addressing this hesitancy is crucial for improving vaccination rates and mitigating the pandemic’s impact [19]. Understanding the diverse attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors within refugee populations is essential for effectively addressing the issue [8,15].

The purpose of this study is to fill the gap in the literature on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees in Lebanon through an in-depth exploration of its drivers, with the aim of informing vaccination strategies and health communication efforts targeting refugee populations.

Methods

Study design

The current study is qualitative and exploratory in nature, because it aimed to understand the phenomenon of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy as experienced and understood by Syrian refugees in Mount Lebanon.

Sampling and recruitment

Using a non-probability convenience sampling, the study targeted middle-aged adults of Syrian refugees residing in Mount Lebanon. The attainable population was the middle-aged adults of Syrian refugees in Mount Lebanon visiting two primary health care centers (PHCCs): Souk El Gharb PHCC in Aley district and Health and Social Center of Aley in Aley city.

The inclusion criteria included: (1) being a Syrian refugee, (2) residing in Mount Lebanon, (3) being middle-aged (36-55 years), as this was the age group with meaningful access to both available vaccine options at the participating PHCCs at the time of data collection, making it the most appropriate group in which to study vaccine hesitancy as a genuine decisional phenomenon rather than a structural access barrier); (4) being unvaccinated and self-reporting unwillingness or hesitancy (including individuals who were undecided about vaccination) at the time of recruitment, as assessed through a screening question prior to the interviews, and (5) using the services of the two selected PHCCs.

The exclusion criteria encompass (1) refugees of other nationalities, (2) individuals willing but unable to take the vaccine, (3) those outside the age group, (4) individuals with impairments affecting interview ability (5) those unable to give informed consent, and (6) individuals refusing consent.

Ethical considerations and human subjects

Prior to data collection, the researchers visited the PHCCs and briefed the staff on the research purpose, objectives, ethical considerations, and the importance of tackling COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees. They received approval from the institutional Review Board approval of St. George Hospital University Medical Center to start data collection. Researchers approached potential participants in the waiting rooms, informing them about the study’s purpose, benefits, risks, and obtaining informed consent. To ensure safety, participants received a prevention kit containing hand sanitizer and a mask. Participation was voluntary, with participants free to withdraw at any time. Although the risk of emotional distress was low, provisions were made to refer any distressed participants to the PHCC’s psychologist; however, none required referral.

Data collection was conducted in a private room in the PHCC to ensure privacy and comply with COVID-19 preventive measures. Arabic versions of the consent form and topic guide facilitated communication and understanding among participants. Interviews were audiotaped with participant consent and anonymized for confidentiality. Access to records was limited to the researchers to ensure data security.

Data collection and analysis

Semi-structured interviews were conducted for data collection, using a topic guide developed based on literature and the WHO SAGE framework’s conceptual domains (contextual, individual/group, and vaccine-specific factors), which guided the scope of exploration. The topic guide was then translated to Arabic and piloted for validity. Each interview lasted for 20-30 minutes. The audiotaped interviews were transcribed verbatim, translated, audited for accuracy, and anonymized. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached [20].

Data were analyzed using thematic content analysis. Although the WHO SAGE framework informed the development of the topic guide and the overarching conceptual framing of the study, the coding process itself was conducted inductively, with codes generated directly from participants’ accounts without imposing the framework’s categories as predetermined themes. This hybrid approach allowed findings to emerge from the data while remaining anchored in established conceptual domains. Two researchers jointly conducted the coding process, familiarizing themselves with the transcripts and generating codes iteratively through ongoing discussion until consensus was reached. Sub-themes and final themes were subsequently developed through an iterative review involving two senior academic researchers (a senior lecturer and an associate professor in public health), providing an additional layer of analytical oversight. Microsoft Excel was used to organize and manage codes and themes throughout the process.

Research rigor and reflexivity

To ensure research rigor, data triangulation was achieved by collecting data from two PHCCs, and investigator triangulation was applied across multiple levels of the research team. At the data collection level, two Master of Public Health researchers used the same topic guide independently. At the analytical level, the development of sub-themes and final themes involved an iterative review process with two senior academic researchers whose engagement added another layer of triangulation and ensured that the interpretations were grounded in the data and aligned with the broader public health literature. Member checking was conducted through follow-up calls or return visits. Reflexivity was maintained through ongoing awareness of the researchers’ backgrounds and their potential influence on data collection and interpretation. The primary researchers were Master of Public Health students with academic training in public health and development sciences, fluent in Arabic, which facilitated communication with participants. They had no prior relationship with the PHCCs or participants.

Results

A total of 18 interviews were conducted between February 13 and April 12, 2022, with seven interviews at Souk El Gharb PHCC and 11 at Health and Social Center of Aley. The participants had a mean age of 40 years (±4), with 72.2% being women. Additional socio-demographic characteristics (e.g. education level, employment status, and duration of residence in Lebanon) were not systematically collected.

Our analysis identified five major drivers for COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy: (1) limited knowledge about the vaccine, (2) the non-mandatory nature of COVID-19 vaccine, (3) socio-cultural beliefs surrounding vaccination, (4) limited access to informed medical and health professionals, and (5) negative perceptions of pharmaceutical companies which developed the vaccines (Figure 1).

Theme 1: Limited knowledge about the vaccine

Sub-theme 1: Limited knowledge about the mode of action and types of existing vaccines

The interviewees displayed varying levels of knowledge regarding the role, mode of action, and types of available COVID-19 vaccines. Although some recognized the vaccine’s general protective purpose, their understanding remained limited. For instance, one participant reported: “I hear that vaccine protects from this disease. The vaccine stops the spread of the virus, but I do not know more about it because I did not read much about this topic” (Participant AM12). However, other participants (5/18) showed limited or inaccurate knowledge about how the vaccine functions. As participant AF16 explains: “I do not know anything about this vaccine, honestly. People are scared from the vaccine because the vaccine inserts the virus in the body.”

Furthermore, most participants reported limited awareness of the different vaccine types available (14/18) and expressed uncertainty regarding which vaccine was appropriate for their age group (17/18). For example, participant SF1 mentioned: “I do not know which types of vaccine are available for me to take. I have never heard that there is a type that anyone cannot take. I do not want to take any already.” In contrast, only a few participants were knowledgeable about the types of COVID-19 vaccines. Participants AM17 mentioned: “I know that there is Pfizer, AstraZeneca and a third type I forgot what it is called now. Some people say that Pfizer is the best among them all. If people recommended a specific vaccine type that I am supposed to take, I might take it, but as for now, I am hesitant to take all types.”

Sub-theme 2: Limited knowledge about vaccine safety

Lack of knowledge about vaccine safety was reported among many participants. For instance, two female participants demonstrated hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccine because of breastfeeding. For instance, participant SF2 stated: “Breastfeeding my baby makes me very hesitant to take the vaccine now. I might register to take it when I stop breastfeeding.” For other participants, having a health condition and a history of COVID-19 infection were primary drivers for their vaccine hesitancy, as participant AF14 said: “I am hesitant to take the vaccine mainly because I am sick, I have inflammation in the tonsils, and I am scared to take it personally.”

Sub-theme 3: Misinformation about the vaccine

Participants had varied sources of information about COVID-19 vaccine, including mass media (13/18), vaccine receivers (2/18), neighbors (4/18), and health care providers (1/18). Methods for verifying information varied, with some relying on personal beliefs (4/18), public opinion (2/18), and medical opinions (3/18), whereas nine participants did not distinguish between true and false information.

Misinformation about vaccine types was also reported by some participants, where participant AM13 mentioned: “I hear from people that all vaccine types have complications and our immunity does not respond to all of them, and I hear that people die after 2 years of taking any type of vaccine.” Furthermore, the detected misinformation revolved around the vaccine’s role, mode of action and complications. For instance, participant AM15 stated: “This vaccine did not take enough time to be developed and delivered. Each time the virus has a new variant, you should take a new dose. I am a football fan, and I am updated with their news. Since a year ago and till now, three football players died because they have taken the vaccine; and I know that sudden deaths are not common among sports’ players.” Add to that, some participants (3/18) had their hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccine fueled by the fear of exaggerated COVID-19 vaccine complications. As participant AF16 stated: “I am hesitant to take the vaccine mainly because I know someone who have taken the vaccine and then his heart muscle got diseased. Also, people say that the vaccine negatively impacts the senses.” These findings suggest that limited knowledge about COVID-19 vaccines contributed to the spread and acceptance of misinformation, which in turn amplified fear and uncertainty among participants.

Theme 2: Non-mandatory nature of COVID-19 vaccine

Most (16/18) participants indicated that the non-mandatory status of the COVID-19 vaccine contributed to their vaccine hesitancy. They suggested that making the vaccine mandatory for everyone, regardless of ethical considerations, could reduce this hesitancy, as being required to receive the vaccine would eliminate the option to refuse. Participant AM15 emphasized: “If taking COVID-19 vaccine became mandatory, I will take it for sure. meaning ‘one is compelled rather than heroic.’ I would need to complete my daily tasks without restrictions.” However, for some participants (2/18), making the vaccine mandatory will not affect their vaccine uptake and therefore, their hesitancy will prevail.

Theme 3: Limited access to medical and expert opinion

Limited access to medical and health professionals’ opinions was identified as a factor contributing to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among many participants (13/18), suggesting that the primary driver in this theme was structural rather than attitudinal. Additionally, a minority (5/18) expressed mistrust toward health care professionals, believing that experts were promoting “unsafe and harmful vaccines.” However, this perception was shaped by the media narratives rather than direct experiences with providers. This was conveyed in participant SF7’s quote: “I knew about COVID-19 vaccine from the media. This vaccine can make people die. The doctors say that the vaccine causes death. I am hesitant to take the vaccine because it causes death.” Conversely, many participants (12/18) did not link their vaccine hesitancy to their perceptions of health care workers or the Lebanese health care system. Some indicated they might be persuaded to get vaccinated if encouraged by their providers. This was illustrated by a quote from participant AF10, who explained: “If my medical doctor encourages me to take the vaccine, I take the vaccine. I will not stay hesitant. Although there might still be little fear, I will get encouraged to take it for sure.” This distinction is important: rather than reflecting a breakdown in trust, hesitancy in this context appears to reflect an unmet need for accessible, personalized patient-provider communication.

Theme 4 Negative perception of pharmaceutical companies and how they developed the vaccines

A number of participants (7/18) demonstrated negative perceptions toward the way pharmaceutical companies rapidly developed the COVID-19 vaccines. This was revealed by participant AM18 who said: “The way that COVID-19 vaccine companies developed and delivered the vaccination is gambling human souls. They invented the virus then its vaccine in such a short duration to make money and profits. This vaccine does not conform to the standards.” However, for the others, their hesitancy was not related to the mistrust of pharmaceutical companies but was rather fueled by either lack of knowledge about the vaccine safety or the misinformation they were exposed to about potential vaccine complications. Negative perceptions appeared to stem mainly from media exposure and community narratives rather than direct personal experiences.

Theme 5: Socio-cultural factors

Hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccine was also driven by socio-cultural factors among a large group of participants (11/18). Most of the participants related their hesitancy to family-driven decisions, as reflected by participant AF8: “If any of my family members or friends take COVID-19 vaccine, I get encouraged to take it. It will not be dangerous anymore.” In contrast, social factors were not considered as a driver for vaccine hesitancy among some participants (7/18). This was emphasized by participant AM17 who said: “If my family members or friends take COVID-19 vaccine, my hesitancy will not be diminished. I do not trust the vaccination implementation procedures. It is all a big lie. It causes a lot of harm to all people who take it. I am physiologically and mentally not encouraged yet to take the COVID-19 vaccine.” This variation suggests that while social influences played a significant role for some participants, others relied more on individual beliefs and personal judgments when making vaccination decisions.

The results revealed no link between religious beliefs and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. Most participants (17/18) agreed that religious values did not contribute to their hesitancy, nor were there negative perceptions of how the Lebanese health care system manages vaccination.

Discussion

The purpose of the study was to explore the reasons behind COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among middle-aged Syrian refugees in Mount Lebanon, revealing five major, interrelated drivers of hesitancy. The findings provide context-specific insights into how knowledge gaps, structural conditions, and socio-cultural influences shape vaccination decisions within a marginalized refugee population.

Limited knowledge about COVID-19 vaccines emerged as a central driver of hesitancy, influencing participants’ perceptions of vaccine safety, effectiveness, and necessity. Participants demonstrated uncertainty regarding vaccines’ mode of action, types, and safety, which contributed to fear and susceptibility to misinformation. These findings are consistent with previous studies among refugee and migrant populations, which have shown that limited health literacy and reliance on informal information sources can exacerbate misinformation and mistrust during public health emergencies. For instance, a quantitative study on older Syrian refugees in Lebanon, identified the novelty of the vaccine and a lack of knowledge about potential complications as reasons for hesitancy [21]. Another mixed-methods study on refugee populations in the United States highlighted those rumors and misinformation fueled vaccine hesitancy [22]. Limited knowledge among Syrian refugees also suggests that commonly used information dissemination channels—such as unofficial sources, word of mouth, and social media—may be ineffective because of language barriers and limited access to reliable information [23].

This lack of knowledge can foster conspiracy theories and misinformation, creating confusion about the vaccine’s importance, efficacy, and potential side effects [24]. Misinformation disseminated through mass media or community networks can be particularly difficult to control during public health emergencies. Evidence from studies conducted in Australia, the United States, Italy, Switzerland, France, and the United Kingdom indicates that poor health literacy and language barriers increase susceptibility to misinformation among migrant and refugee populations, contributing to mistrust in COVID-19 vaccines and lower vaccine uptake [23,25]. In the present study, health-related concerns—including breastfeeding, comorbidities, and prior COVID-19 infection—were similarly shaped by conflicting or inaccurate information. Misinformation may bias perceptions of what constitutes credible evidence [26], whereas selective trust in information sources can be influenced by personal beliefs grounded in inaccurate or incomplete knowledge [27].

The second explored driver of hesitancy was the non-mandatory nature of COVID-19 vaccine, with several participants indicating that they would consider vaccination only if it were compulsory. In Lebanon, this trend is consistent with high compliance for mandatory vaccines and intermediate to low compliance for non-mandatory ones among both Lebanese citizens and Syrian refugees [28]. Although non-mandatory vaccines are often associated with lower uptake because of high costs not covered by the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH), this explanation does not apply to the COVID-19 vaccine, which was provided free of charge. The expressed reluctance toward vaccination aligns with findings from a cross-sectional analysis of a five-wave longitudinal study among older Syrian refugees in Lebanon (n = 2906), which reported that more than half of participants had not received their first dose, with 38% citing “not wanting the vaccine” as the primary reason [29]. Such attitudes may reflect a combination of perceived low risk, fear of side effects, mistrust, or a strong emphasis on personal autonomy. Although mandatory vaccination policies may increase uptake, they may also risk undermining trust in public health institutions if not accompanied by transparent and context-sensitive communication. As highlighted by the WHO, clear communication surrounding vaccine mandates is essential to balance public health objectives with ethical considerations and trust-building [30].

The third theme is limited access to medical opinion on the COVID-19 vaccine. A qualitative study among migrants and refugees in the United Kingdom identified distrust in the health care system as a contributor to vaccine hesitancy [23]. In contrast, participants in the present study did not express overt distrust in the health care system’s management of vaccination. This finding may be influenced by several contextual factors, including refugees’ reliance on United Nations Higher Commissioner for Refugees rather than MoPH for health care services, limited awareness of vaccination governance, or reluctance to voice critical opinions. Additionally, recruitment from PHCCs may have influenced participants’ responses, as regular service users may feel hesitant to criticize the health care system within clinical settings. Importantly, many participants reported limited opportunities to receive direct medical advice about vaccination and indicated that encouragement from health care providers could positively influence their decision-making. These findings suggest that vaccine hesitancy was driven more by constrained access to professional guidance than by generalized mistrust, with levels of trust in health care providers varying across participants. Structural constraints within PHCCs, including high patient volumes, COVID-19 restrictions, and digital or health literacy barriers, may further limit meaningful communication. Moreover, “unspoken vaccine hesitancy” among health care workers, particularly during the early phases of the pandemic, may have indirectly affected public confidence and vaccine uptake [31].

The fourth theme explored the mistrust and the negative perceptions refugee populations hold toward pharmaceutical companies and the rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines. A systematic review examining vaccine hesitancy among ethnic minority groups identified institutional mistrust—particularly toward pharmaceutical companies and medical systems—as a key driver [32]. In the present study, some participants perceived the vaccine rollout as financially motivated and expressed concerns that vaccines were developed too quickly to ensure safety. These perceptions appeared to be shaped largely by media exposure and community narratives rather than direct personal experiences. Similar concerns have been documented globally. An online survey across six continents (n = 26,852) found that approximately one-third of respondents did not trust pharmaceutical companies to develop safe and effective vaccines, with perceived safety varying by the vaccine’s country of origin [14]. Likewise, a cross-sectional study among Syrian refugees in Jordan reported that 27% preferred vaccines manufactured in the United States or Europe [33]. Such findings underscore the importance of transparent communication regarding vaccine development processes, particularly in populations with limited trust in global institutions.

The fifth explored driver related to socio-cultural pressures, including social norms, peer influence, and family-driven decision-making. Consistent with studies conducted among refugee populations in European countries, social environments were found to shape both vaccine hesitancy and acceptance [34]. Participants often relied on family members and peers when interpreting vaccine-related risks and benefits, reinforcing either hesitancy or reassurance depending on collective experiences.

Notably, the study found no evident role of religion in contributing to vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees. This contrasts with previous research indicating that religious prohibitions have influenced vaccine hesitancy in certain refugee contexts [22]. Other studies have highlighted how religious or political narratives have shaped vaccine attitudes, such as among religious minorities or in settings where COVID-19 vaccination was highly politicized [35]. The absence of religious influence in this study may be explained by the relative religious homogeneity of Syrian refugees in Lebanon, most of whom share similar Islamic beliefs [36], as well as the comparatively limited politicization of COVID-19 vaccination in the Lebanese context.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The use of a convenience sample from two PHCCs in Mount Lebanon may limit the diversity of perspectives captured, as participants accessing health care services may differ from those who do not. The predominance of female participants reflects utilization patterns of PHCCs, as women are more likely to seek preventive health services [37], but limits representation of male perspectives. Furthermore, additional socio-demographic variables (e.g. education level, employment status, and duration of residence in Lebanon) were not systematically collected, which may restrict contextual interpretation of the findings. The age-specific inclusion criteria, while contextually necessary and informed by vaccine eligibility policies at the time of data collection, may affect transferability of these findings to younger or older Syrian refugee populations. Future studies may benefit from incorporating a broader range of socio-demographic characteristics and age groups to further contextualize vaccine hesitancy patterns among refugees. Despite these limitations, this qualitative study provides in-depth insights into the complex interplay of personal, institutional, and contextual factors shaping COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among Syrian refugees in Lebanon, a population that remains underrepresented in the literature.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifaceted drivers of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among middle-aged Syrian refugees in Mount Lebanon, emphasizing the roles of limited knowledge, misinformation, structural barriers, and socio-cultural influences. Vaccine hesitancy in this context appears to be shaped less by outright refusal and more by uncertainty, constrained access to reliable information, and limited opportunities for engagement with trusted health care professionals. Improving access to accurate, accessible, and culturally appropriate information about COVID-19 vaccines, particularly regarding vaccine types, safety, and side effects—may help address knowledge gaps and counter misinformation among refugee populations [20]. Such efforts should be supported by health care professionals and other trusted sources to facilitate informed decision-making [21]. Although improving knowledge and addressing misinformation may contribute to vaccine acceptance without reliance on mandatory approaches [38], the effectiveness of such strategies was not evaluated in this study. Moreover, although mandatory vaccination policies have been discussed as mechanisms to increase vaccine uptake, they raise ethical considerations related to autonomy and human rights, particularly within vulnerable populations, and were beyond the scope of the present research [39].

These findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s scope, which focused on middle-aged Syrian refugees attending PHCCs in Mount Lebanon, and may not fully reflect the perspectives of other age groups or refugee populations. The convenience sample and predominance of female participants may further limit the breadth of perspectives captured.

Future research should explore vaccine hesitancy across broader age groups and geographic regions in Lebanon and examine how different communication strategies and health care delivery models may support vaccine acceptance among refugee populations. Involving refugees in research remains essential for understanding their lived experiences and informing evidence-based, context-sensitive public health responses [22,23]. As refugee populations remain vulnerable to COVID-19 and future public health threats, research and policy efforts should prioritize inclusive approaches that strengthen access to health services, promote trust, and support equitable health outcomes [40].
