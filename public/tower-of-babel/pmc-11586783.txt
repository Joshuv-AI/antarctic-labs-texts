Targetoid skin lesions in a newborn: a case of erythema multiforme
Authors: Ameer Awashra, Fathi Milhem, Noor Nabresi, Shatha Matar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractErythema multiforme (EM) is an immune-mediated condition that manifests as targetoid skin lesions and can be triggered by various factors, including infections and vaccinations. This case report describes a 41-day-old full-term male infant who developed widespread annular, bullseye-shaped erythematous skin lesions one week after receiving the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination. The infant, exclusively breastfed and without a significant past medical history, presented with these lesions but no associated systemic symptoms. Physical examination revealed characteristic targetoid lesions, sparing the face, palms, soles, and mucous membranes. Laboratory tests, including a complete blood count and infection markers, were within normal ranges except for a mildly elevated C-reactive protein. The differential diagnosis ruled out other conditions and EM diagnosis is confirmed. The infant was managed conservatively with supportive care, and the lesions resolved without the need for antibiotics or any additional therapy. The patient remained stable and was discharged with instructions for monitoring and follow-up. So this case highlights the importance of distinguishing EM from other similar conditions such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, and urticaria.Keywords: erythema multiforme, neonate, BCG vaccination, targetoid lesions

Introduction

Erythema multiforme (EM) is an immune-mediated condition affecting the skin and mucous membranes. It is characterized by bull’s-eye lesions, which start as annular macules and later develop into papules, sometimes merging to form plaques. It is often associated with infections, vaccinations, and occasionally with medications or other triggers. The incidence of EM following vaccination, although rare, has been documented in medical literature. Vaccines, such as the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine, are recognized as potential triggers for EM, likely due to their ability to activate the immune system [1, 2].

While EM is relatively rare in neonates, it is crucial for clinicians to recognize its presentation and differentiate it from other serious dermatological conditions. In this case, we present a 41-day-old male full-term infant who developed widespread annular bullseye-shaped erythematous skin lesions one week after receiving the BCG vaccination. The infant had no associated systemic symptoms and improved with supportive care, leading to discharge without additional therapy.

Case presentation

A 41-day-old full-term male infant, born at 38 weeks of gestation via cesarean section to a 40-year-old mother, weighing 2500 grams at birth, was admitted. The pregnancy was uneventful, with no significant family or medical history. He received the first dose of the hepatitis B vaccine at birth without any adverse reactions. He is exclusively breastfed and currently weighs 4000 grams.

The infant developed widespread skin lesions one week following the administration of the BCG vaccination. These lesions rapidly disseminated across the body within two days, yet notably, the infant did not exhibit any systemic symptoms such as high-grade fever, vomiting, diarrhea, decreased oral intake, or alterations in bowel habits or urine color.

Upon admission, the infant exhibited a low-grade fever (37.8°C) but otherwise appeared well. Physical examination revealed characteristic targetoid erythematous patches of varying sizes, with a darker center compared to the periphery (see Fig. 1). These annular, bull’s-eye-shaped lesions were noted, sparing the face, palms, soles, and mucous membranes. The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable, except for mild inflammation observed at the circumcision site, which was performed four days earlier.

Symmetrical distribution of annular, target-like erythematous patches was observed in both the upper and lower limbs.

A series of laboratory tests and imaging studies were conducted upon admission. The initial laboratory results are summarized in Table 1. Additional serologic tests for HSV IgM and IgG antibodies and HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) were non-reactive, indicating no active infection. Maternal serologic tests for anti-SSA (Ro) and anti-SSB (La) antibodies were also non-reactive, reducing the likelihood of neonatal lupus. An ECG was performed to rule out cardiac involvement, with normal results observed.

The infant’s clinical presentation required a thorough differential diagnosis to exclude conditions such as EM or a vaccine-related adverse reaction. Following comprehensive diagnostic evaluations and laboratory tests, which confirmed the EM diagnosis, the management plan focused on careful monitoring of vital signs and necessary follow-up investigations. Consequently, the infant continued breastfeeding as tolerated, with acetaminophen prescribed for low-grade fever. Since there were no signs of infection, antibiotic therapy was withheld.

Over the course of the hospital stay, the bullseye rash improved, and the infant remained active and well. There were no additional findings on repeat examinations, and no murmurs or abnormal abdominal findings were noted. The infant’s condition was consistently stable, with vital signs within normal limits. Nutritional status remained adequate with exclusive breastfeeding.

Five days after admission and with an improvement in skin lesions and stable condition (see Fig. 2), the infant was discharged home with instructions for the parents to monitor for any signs of fever or worsening of symptoms and to return for follow-up as needed. The discharge plan included maintaining regular feeding, monitoring skin changes, and ensuring prompt medical consultation if new symptoms arose.

Discussion

EM is an acute hypersensitivity reaction affecting both the skin and mucous membranes. It can be triggered by various factors, including infections such as Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1), specific medications (like antibiotics, antiepileptic drugs, and Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs), and underlying conditions (such as inflammatory bowel disease, hepatitis C, leukemia, and lymphoma) [2, 3].

EM is categorized into two main types: EM minor, primarily affecting the skin, and EM major, which also involves mucous membranes and can be associated with more severe systemic symptoms. The pathophysiology of EM is characterized by a hypersensitivity reaction, leading to the deposition of immune complexes in the superficial microvasculature of the skin and mucous membranes. This process results in inflammation and the characteristic lesions associated with EM [4].

EM is primarily a type IV hypersensitivity reaction, also known as delayed-type hypersensitivity. This reaction is mediated by T-cells responding to an antigenic stimulus provided by the vaccine components. The vaccines introduce antigens or adjuvants into the body, which are processed and presented by antigen-presenting cells. This leads to the activation of T-cells, which then target the epidermis and dermo-epidermal junction, causing keratinocyte apoptosis and the characteristic skin lesions [5, 6]. Activated T-cells release various cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, which contribute to the inflammatory cascade. This leads to the recruitment of additional immune cells, further amplifying the immune response and resulting in the clinical manifestations of EM [7]. Histologically, EM shows keratinocyte necrosis, spongiosis, and a predominantly perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate. The presence of subepidermal bullae and basal cell vacuolar degeneration are also noted, which confirm the diagnosis of EM following vaccination [8]. Specific components of vaccines, such as adjuvants (e.g. polysorbate 80, aluminum hydroxide) or viral proteins, are considered potential triggers for the hypersensitivity reactions leading to EM. These components can initiate an immune response in susceptible individuals, leading to the development of EM [9].

EM must be carefully distinguished from other immune-mediated epidermal disorders, notably Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and urticaria. Although SJS and TEN share similarities, such as target lesions on the skin or mucous membranes that rapidly progress to distinguishable blistering lesions, their clinical presentations differ significantly from EM. The latter, particularly in children, often manifests with target lesions on the extremities (acral), but lesions can also appear on the trunk. A distinguishing feature of EM is the presence of raised typical target lesions. Furthermore, the duration of individual lesions helps differentiate EM from urticaria; EM lesions typically persist for at least a week, whereas urticarial lesions generally resolve within 24 h [3, 10].

Case studies provide evidence of EM following vaccination. For instance, a 19-year-old female developed EM following the HPV vaccine, suggesting a temporal relationship between vaccination and EM onset [11]. Similarly, a 2.5-year-old boy developed EM following pneumococcal vaccination, indicating the immune-mediated reaction to vaccine components as a potential cause [9]. Moreover, several cases of EM have been reported following COVID-19 vaccinations, including mRNA vaccines, which further supports the common mechanism of immune response to the vaccine’s antigenic components [8].

Finally, supportive care and careful monitoring are the primary approaches in managing children with EM. Topical treatments, such as steroids or antihistamines, may offer relief to some patients. The use of systemic steroids is a topic of debate and should be reserved for critical cases where reducing inflammation by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, suppressing immune cell activation and migration, and decreasing vascular permeability is necessary. It is worth noting that if HSV-1 infection is suspected, early administration of antiviral therapy with acyclovir might help reduce the severity and duration of EM lesions, though the evidence supporting this approach is limited. Hospitalization for hydration and monitoring should be considered in critical cases characterized either by the toxic appearance of the child or the significant distribution of lesions affecting mucous membranes, especially the oral mucosa [3].

Conclusion

In summary, this report underscores the clinical significance of erythema multiforme (EM) as an immune-mediated reaction potentially triggered by vaccination, especially in pediatric patients. It emphasizes the necessity of distinguishing EM from other dermatological conditions and highlights the critical role of supportive care in its management, particularly when systemic involvement is absent. The case provides valuable insights into the immune mechanisms associated with vaccinations, stressing the importance of recognizing and managing vaccine-induced hypersensitivity reactions through careful observation and timely intervention.
