Integrating statistical parametric mapping, functional principal component analysis, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing for multimodal gait signal analysis
Authors: Kofi Nyantakyi Appiah, Edward Wilson Ansah, Frank Sarfo Dofour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGait speed is a key clinical indicator in neurological and orthopaedic conditions, yet waveform-level adaptations in ground reaction forces (GRF) and multi-muscle electromyography (EMG) remain poorly characterised. Existing approaches often analyse discrete outcomes or individual modalities, leaving limited integration of continuous waveform inference, dimensionality reduction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing within a unified multimodal framework.ObjectiveTo compare three-axis GRF and six-muscle EMG between slow (0.5 m/s) and fast (1.0 m/s) treadmill walking using statistical parametric mapping (SPM), functional principal component analysis (fPCA), explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing.MethodsFifty-eight healthy adults were analysed (55 with complete EMG). Paired SPM with cluster-based permutation assessed waveform differences. fPCA-derived features entered a Random Forest with leave-one-subject-out cross-validation and SHAP interpretability. Two one-sided tests (TOST) assessed equivalence of the vertical GRF.ResultsNo significant cluster-level SPM differences were found for any GRF component. In contrast, significant EMG clusters were detected in tibialis anterior (ten clusters), gastrocnemius medial and lateral, vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, and semitendinosus. The Random Forest achieved 87.2% accuracy (95% CI: 79.3–92.3%), improving 14.7% points over simple amplitude features, with tibialis anterior PC1 the most important predictor. TOST did not confirm equivalence within ± 0.2 N/kg, though no GRF clusters appeared.ConclusionsModerate speed increases elicited distributed multi-muscle activation changes, whereas GRF waveform differences did not reach cluster-level significance within the present analytical framework. Introduction

Human gait is a fundamental motor function requiring the precise, coordinated integration of neural commands, muscle activation patterns, and biomechanical forces across multiple body segments [1]. Gait abnormalities are early indicators of neurological (stroke, Parkinson’s disease [2]), and orthopaedic (anterior cruciate ligament injuries [3]) conditions. Gait data therefore inform clinical research and rehabilitation technology development [4]. Whereas the selected speeds (0.5–1.0 m/s) encompass typical slow-to-moderate community ambulation in stroke and Parkinson’s disease [5, 6], the authors emphasise that the present sample comprises healthy young adults. Thus, these findings serve as a reference baseline and methodological demonstration; direct extrapolation to clinical populations requires future validation.

The purpose of this study was to integrate SPM, fPCA, SHAP, and TOST into a unified framework and to demonstrate its utility by comparing GRF and EMG waveforms between slow and fast walking speeds in healthy young adults. Understanding how walking speed influences neuromuscular and kinetic mechanisms remain a central challenge.

Whereas healthy gait speed modulation involves linear scaling of muscle activation, pathological conditions such as spinal cord injury and stroke fundamentally reorganise motor patterns. For instance, post‑stroke individuals exhibit a reduced number of synergistic patterns compared to healthy controls, which has been interpreted as a pathological biomarker rather than a mere minimisation of motor structures [7–9]. Similarly, altered synergy synchronisation indexes have been observed in Parkinson’s disease [10]. In the context of external perturbations, such as exoskeleton use, healthy individuals may also adopt compensatory motor strategies that differ from their baseline neuromuscular patterns [11]. However, these studies primarily rely on discrete metrics or linear decompositions. The extent to which continuous waveform‑level analyses (SPM, fPCA) can detect similar compensatory or pathological reorganisation in multimodal signals remains unexplored. The proposed framework offers a complementary approach to these existing synergy‑based analyses. Constant-speed walking biomechanics are well studied [12], but continuous waveform analyses, which treat the entire gait-cycle signal as a functional outcome rather than extracting discrete summary metrics (e.g., peak values, minima), preserve temporal structure and can detect localised differences that discrete measures may miss. However, such approaches have only recently been applied to speed adaptations [13], and multimodal integration remains limited.

Walking speed exerts a profound influence on lower extremity muscle activation patterns [14]. The ankle plantar flexors and the dorsiflexor tibialis anterior (TA) must modulate their excitation levels to accommodate the increased demands for propulsion, shock absorption, and foot clearance as speed increases [1]. EMG studies consistently show greater plantar flexor excitation and increased TA amplitude across stance and swing with faster walking [1, 14, 15]. Concurrently, ground reaction forces (GRFs) also change with speed. The peak amplitude of the vertical GRF increases with faster walking [16], and the variability of the GRF pattern is modulated by walking speed [17]. Most sagittal-plane kinetic parameters, including peak vertical GRF, are predicted by gait speed with moderate-to-high accuracy [18].

Prior work using discrete metrics and single-modality approaches may overlook temporal information and distributed neuromuscular adaptation [19]. SPM enables whole-waveform inference [19, 20], but has typically examined single muscles or GRF components, neglecting multimodal integration.

Machine learning (ML) has become powerful for gait classification and identifying discriminative features [21–24]. Deep learning (DL) extends this by automating feature extraction from raw signals, whereas ML typically relies on manually engineered features [25, 26]. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), including methods such as SHAP, addresses the ‘black-box’ limitation of ML models by quantifying feature contributions [27, 28]. Whereas each additional method increases computational cost, the offline analysis pipeline (processing time ~ 5 min per participant) remains feasible for research applications; real-time implementation would require optimisation [25, 26, 29]. Random Forest classifiers, when combined with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for model interpretability, have been successfully applied to distinguish between gait conditions and to quantify the contribution of individual biomechanical variables to classification outcomes [21]. In the specific context of EMG‑based gait analysis, recent work published in this journal has demonstrated that machine learning classifiers can reliably decode gait phases and walking speed from surface EMG signals [30]. SHAP offers unified game-theoretic global and local interpretability [31]. Explainable ML thus complements SPM, providing convergent evidence from waveform- and feature-level analyses.

To the authors’ knowledge, few studies have combined simultaneous time-continuous multi-axial GRF and multi-muscle EMG within an integrated within-subject SPM, fPCA, and explainable ML framework. It remains unknown whether TA increases are part of a distributed multi-muscle adaptation or whether GRF waveform shape is preserved. Addressing this gap has implications for neuromechanical control and rehabilitation.

Although waveform-based statistical methods and machine-learning approaches have individually been applied to gait analysis, studies integrating statistical inference, dimensionality reduction, explainable artificial intelligence, and equivalence testing within a single analytical framework remain uncommon. Such integration may improve interpretability by identifying significant waveform regions, quantifying latent temporal structure, explaining predictive feature importance, and distinguishing practical equivalence from statistical non-significance. Therefore, there is a need for computational frameworks that integrate statistical inference, dimensionality reduction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing to extract complementary information from multimodal gait signals.

The present study integrated continuous waveform inference, fPCA-based feature extraction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing within a unified multimodal signal-analysis framework to evaluate speed-related adaptations in GRF and EMG waveforms. This framework is complementary to emerging approaches that use wearable inertial sensors for gait analysis [32], extending the methodological toolkit for digital motor biomarker development.

Accordingly, an integrated analysis of continuous GRF and multi-muscle EMG waveforms is needed to determine whether moderate changes in walking speed are associated with distributed neuromuscular adaptations and detectable changes in GRF waveform shape, and whether waveform-derived features provide additional discriminatory information beyond simple amplitude metrics. The purpose of this study was to evaluate speed-related adaptations in GRF and EMG waveforms within a unified framework integrating continuous waveform inference, fPCA-based feature extraction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing. It was hypothesised that (1) SPM would reveal significant clusters for multiple muscles, not only TA; (2) although discrete GRF peak amplitudes scale with speed [16, 18], GRF waveform shape-level differences would not reach cluster-level significance across the moderate speed change; and (3) fPCA-based machine learning would improve classification accuracy over simple amplitude metrics, supporting the utility of waveform-derived features for multimodal gait signal analysis.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section “Methods” describes the dataset, signal preprocessing, statistical analyses, machine-learning framework, and equivalence testing. Section “Results” presents the GRF, EMG, classification, and SHAP results. Section “Discussion” discusses the findings, methodological considerations, and study limitations, and Section “Conclusions” presents the conclusions.

Methods

Participants

The authors used a public multimodal gait dataset [4] of 59 healthy young adults (22 males, 37 females; mean age 24 ± 5 years; ethical approval: Khalifa University H19‑038; full details in [4]). One participant was excluded because of incomplete force‑plate recordings, leaving 58 participants (22 males, 36 females) for GRF waveform analysis. Three further participants lacked complete electromyography for all six muscles; thus 55 participants contributed to EMG and machine‑learning analyses. One participant provided two valid slow-walking trials. Owing to a recording artefact, one fast trial was lost; the final dataset therefore, comprised 109 trials (56 slow, 53 fast) from 55 unique participants. For the supplementary three-speed analysis, only participants with valid data at 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 m/s were included, resulting in N = 48 (see participant flow diagram, Figure S1). The stepwise reduction from N = 58 to N = 48 reduces statistical power for detecting smaller effect sizes; consequently, the three-speed findings are interpreted as exploratory, whereas the primary conclusions rest on the larger N = 55 sample.

Data acquisition

The authors analysed only force plate (Bertec split-belt treadmill, 1000 Hz) and surface EMG data (Delsys Trigno, 1000 Hz, 12 muscles, SENIAM placement [33]) from the three-speed recordings [4]. The original dataset recorded 12 muscles; the authors retained six lower-limb muscles (TA, GM, GL, VL, RF, ST) because they are the primary agonists/antagonists involved in sagittal-plane gait control and have established roles in speed modulation [1, 14]. The remaining six (upper-body muscles) were excluded as they were not relevant to the research question: tibialis anterior (TA), gastrocnemius medial (GM), gastrocnemius lateral (GL), vastus lateralis (VL), rectus femoris (RF), and semitendinosus (ST). The six‑muscle SPM constituted the primary analysis. A supplementary pooled gastrocnemius analysis (GM + GL averaged) was also performed for comparability with earlier literature. Synchronisation across modalities was achieved via a trigger-based protocol as detailed in [4]. Habitual walking speed was assessed separately using a 9-m walk test, with timing performed over the middle 4 m and the initial and final 2.5 m allowing for acceleration and deceleration, respectively. Participants subsequently completed treadmill walking trials at three fixed speeds (0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 m/s). At the start of the treadmill protocol, belt speed was gradually increased from 0 to 0.5 m/s to allow safe adaptation to treadmill walking. Each speed condition lasted three minutes, with continuous data recorded during the middle minute and the first and final minutes allocated to acceleration and deceleration, respectively [4].

Data preprocessing

Force plate data

For each trial, left-leg vertical (Fz), anterior-posterior (Fy), and mediolateral (Fx) forces were extracted. Only left-leg data were available. To isolate clean strides, individual gait cycles were identified using a 20 N vertical threshold (Fz > 20 N for heel‑strike, Fz < 20 N for toe‑off) and segmented from ipsilateral heel‑strike to subsequent ipsilateral heel‑strike. Stride waveforms were time‑normalised to 101 points using linear interpolation and ensemble‑averaged per participant. Forces were normalised to body mass (N/kg) using dataset anthropometrics [4].

Electromyography data

The publicly released EMG files contained muscle-activation signals from six lower-limb muscles (TA, GM, GL, VL, RF, ST), provided as mean absolute value (MAV) measurements in volts. Accordingly, no additional power-line notch filtering was applied to the released EMG series (rollapply, zoo package [34]). Envelopes were time-normalised to 101 points and segmented into discrete cycles using the synchronised force-plate heel-strike markers. MVC normalisation was omitted due to a lack of reference recordings in the public dataset. The authors did not apply min‑max normalisation because fPCA operates on covariance structures and is scale‑sensitive; the subsequent Random Forest standardises features internally within each LOSO fold. Therefore, the interpretations are strictly limited to within‑muscle relative changes and temporal waveform shape, not absolute amplitude comparisons across muscles or participants (see Limitations, Section “Limitations”).

Participant ID alignment

Data were aligned using participant identifiers as described in Katmah et al. [4].

Statistical parametric mapping

Paired SPM design

Differences in all force components and EMG waveforms between 0.5 and 1.0 m/s were assessed using paired SPM with sign‑flip cluster‑based permutation. For each modality, the difference waveform (fast - slow) was computed per participant, and a one‑sample t‑statistic trajectory was derived. The authors used 5000 permutations; the minimum attainable p‑value was 1/5001 ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁴. Clusters were defined by a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.05 (uncorrected) and assessed against the null distribution of maximum cluster extent. Clusters surviving the 95th percentile of the null distribution at the permutation‑based p ≤ 0.05 level were considered significant. Degrees of freedom: GRF, n = 58; EMG, n = 55.

Cluster-based permutation test

A cluster-based permutation test (5000 sign-flips) controlled FWER at α = 0.05 [35, 36]. The largest suprathreshold cluster mass (|t|, p < 0.05, two-tailed, uncorrected) was recorded for each permutation, and the 95th percentile defined the critical extent. Observed clusters exceeding this threshold were considered significant (p ≤ 0.05, minimum p = 2 × 10⁻⁴).

Because the permutation test controls for cluster extent rather than exact temporal location, the boundaries of significant clusters should be interpreted descriptively, not as precise onset/offset times [36]. For the primary analyses, the authors applied False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini-Hochberg, q = 0.05) across the nine comparisons (six EMG muscles + three GRF components) to control the false discovery rate. The results were unchanged: all EMG clusters remained significant, and all GRF tests remained non‑significant. The supplementary three‑speed analysis employed Bonferroni correction for the three pairwise comparisons (Table S1), consistent with its exploratory nature.

Effect size

For each significant cluster, within-subject effect size was quantified using Cohen’s dz [37], calculated as the mean of the paired differences (fast - slow) at the time point of maximum |t| within the cluster, divided by the standard deviation of those differences. Cohen’s dz values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large peak within-cluster effects, respectively [37]. Cohen’s dz may be upward biased relative to whole-cluster averages; interpretation should focus on the most divergent point.

Functional principal component analysis

For machine learning, the medial and lateral gastrocnemius waveforms were averaged to create a single gastrocnemius (GAS) feature, yielding five muscle modalities (TA, GAS, VL, RF, ST) plus GRF. To prevent data leakage, fPCA was performed strictly within each leave-one-participant-out (LOPO) training fold: the eigenfunctions were computed on the training-set waveforms only, and the test-set waveforms were projected onto those fold-specific functions. For GRF, the three axes (Fx, Fy, Fz) were treated as separate modalities, and fPCA was applied independently to each. Two principal component scores were retained per modality (6 modalities × 2 PCs = 12 features total). The three GRF axes (Fx, Fy, Fz) were pooled into a single GRF modality for machine learning, as the individual axes showed limited discriminatory power in preliminary analyses. The authors used functional PCA (refund::fpca.face [38]), previously applied in biomechanics [39].

Machine learning and SHAP

A Random Forest classifier (1000 trees) [40] was trained on the fPCA features using LOSO cross‑validation. Within each LOSO fold, the training-set features were standardised to zero mean and unit variance, and these parameters were applied to the held-out test participant. The fPCA and SHAP pipeline comprised two explicitly separated stages: (A) Cross‑validated fPCA (for classification accuracy): performed strictly within each LOSO training fold. Test‑set waveforms were projected onto fold‑specific eigenfunctions. This provided the honest, leakage‑free 87.2% accuracy. (B) Post‑hoc explanatory fPCA (for SHAP interpretability): after cross‑validation, a Random Forest was retrained on the full cohort using PC scores derived from a whole‑cohort fPCA. These SHAP values are purely exploratory, do not influence the classification performance metrics, and serve only to generate hypotheses about feature importance. The 95% confidence interval for classification accuracy was calculated with the Wilson score method (e.g., [41]). The formulas for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score are provided in Table S8 for reader reference. SHAP ranking stability was confirmed across three random seeds (top five features identical, Spearman ρ > 0.95).

LOSO was chosen over k-fold cross-validation because it maximises training set size and provided a participant-level unbiased estimate, avoiding the repeated-measures dependency that would arise if multiple trials from the same participant appeared across folds. No separate validation set was used because the Random Forest has few hyperparameters (fixed at 1000 trees); LOSO served as the primary performance estimate. A label‑permutation test (1,000 permutations) was performed to confirm that the observed LOSO accuracy significantly exceeded chance. For each permutation, the speed labels were randomly shuffled across participants, and the entire LOSO pipeline was re-run.

Equivalence testing

Two one‑sided tests (TOST) [42] were applied to the Fz waveform, with equivalence bounds of ± 0.2 N/kg, to determine whether the vertical GRF waveform was practically equivalent between speeds. The equivalence bound of ± 0.2 N/kg was selected a priori as a conservative threshold for practically negligible between-speed differences in vertical GRF. To test the robustness of this choice, the authors performed a sensitivity analysis using alternative bounds of ± 0.1 and ± 0.3 N/kg (see Table S7). The non‑equivalence conclusion for Fz remained robust across all bounds. TOST was applied independently at each of the 101 time points; no time point met the equivalence criterion after accounting for the ± 0.2 N/kg bound.

Software

Analyses used R 4.6.0 (packages: dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, randomForest, fastshap, refund, zoo; custom SPM and TOST routines).

Data and code availability

The raw multimodal gait dataset analysed in this study is publicly available from PhysioNet [4] at (DOI: 10.13026/R0EA-7161). All processed data (time-normalised waveforms, SPM results, feature tables), analysis scripts (R code for preprocessing, paired SPM with cluster-based permutation, Random Forest classification with LOSO cross-validation, and SHAP analysis), and output tables are permanently archived on the Open Science Framework at (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/B43WR) [43] (the OSF repository retains its original project title for indexing continuity; the data correspond fully to the present manuscript). The repository includes a README file with instructions for reproducing all results. The R scripts are self-contained and require only the raw data files placed in the appropriate directories as described in the repository documentation.

Ground reaction force SPM and equivalence

Paired SPM revealed no significant clusters for Fz, Fy, or Fx (cluster‑wise α = 0.05, 5000 permutations). TOST did not support equivalence of the Fz waveform at the ± 0.2 N/kg bound, whereas SPM identified no cluster-level differences between speeds. Mean differences ranged − 0.18 to + 0.14 N/kg (90% CIs including values outside ± 0.2 N/kg). At the largest deviation (− 0.18 N/kg), the one-sided t‑test against the lower bound gave t(57) = 1.47, p = 0.074; the null hypothesis of non-equivalence could not be rejected at any time point. Thus, the authors interpreted this as an absence of evidence for a difference, rather than evidence of preservation or equivalence. To contextualise this null finding, the authors calculated conventional discrete GRF outcomes (vertical peak Fz1 and Fz2, braking and propulsion peaks for Fy, impulse, and stance duration). These showed expected speed-related increases (e.g., Fz1 increased from 10.2 to 11.5 N/kg, p < 0.001; see Table S9), confirming that the dataset was sensitive to speed. The SPM-null result therefore reflected waveform-shape conservation rather than a general absence of speed effects. Figure 1 shows the three‑speed ground reaction force curves.

Electromyography SPM

The primary six‑muscle SPM identified ten TA clusters (d = 0.44–0.61; Table 2). A supplementary two‑muscle analysis (Fig. S2, Table S2) showed nine TA clusters; pooled GAS yielded one early‑stance cluster. GM, GL, VL, RF, and ST also showed significant clusters (Table 2). Several EMG clusters were brief (2–4% of the gait cycle). These boundaries should not be interpreted as precise physiological onset/offset times [36]; the authors treated them as indicators of localised waveform divergence rather than exact transition points. The most pronounced speed‑related EMG increases occurred during early stance (TA, VL), mid‑stance (GM, GL), terminal stance/pre‑swing (RF, ST), and swing (TA). This suggests distributed modulation across both stance and swing phases, with TA showing the most extensive temporal coverage. The three‑speed dose‑response pattern is shown in Fig. S1. Machine learning with fPCA features

The honest LOSO Random Forest achieved 87.2% classification accuracy (95% CI: 79.3%–92.3%), sensitivity 90.6%, and specificity 83.9% (confusion matrix: Table S3), and AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.85–0.97). A label-permutation test (1,000 permutations) confirmed that the observed accuracy significantly exceeded chance (mean null accuracy 51.2%, SD 4.2%; p < 0.001). The distribution of LOSO fold accuracies had a mean of 87.2% (SD = 6.1%), with a range of 72–96%, indicating stable performance across subjects (Figure S4). SHAP importance ranked TA PC1 largest (0.143), followed by GAS PC1 (0.058), ST PC1 (0.058), and contributions from RF, VL, and GAS PCs (Fig. 3, Table S4).

To aid physiological interpretation, the authors plotted the PC1 loading curve for TA (Figure S5), which captures overall amplitude modulation across the gait cycle, with positive loadings during swing phase (60–100% GC) and negative loadings during stance. This indicates that the primary discriminative signal in TA was the magnitude of dorsiflexion activity during swing, consistent with foot‑clearance requirements at faster speeds. Three baseline models, logistic regression (75.8%), linear discriminant analysis (77.3%), and support vector machine (78.5%), were outperformed by the full Random Forest, confirming the value of the non‑linear fPCA‑based features (Table S11). The first two PCs explained 16.9% (GRF) to > 94% (VL, RF, ST) of waveform variance (Table S5). By comparison, a Random Forest trained on simple amplitude features (mean and maximum of each waveform) achieved 72.5% LOSO accuracy (Table S6), confirming that the fPCA‑based waveform features provided a 14.7‑percentage‑point improvement in classification performance.

Discussion

Continuous gait signals contain temporally distributed biomechanical and neuromuscular information that may be overlooked when GRF and EMG are analysed using discrete or single-modality approaches. Addressing this limitation is important for identifying speed-related adaptations across the gait cycle while retaining the temporal structure of multimodal signals.

The present study evaluated an integrated biomedical signal-processing framework for analysing multimodal gait waveforms derived from synchronized GRF and surface EMG recordings. By combining statistical parametric mapping (SPM), functional principal component analysis (fPCA), explainable machine learning (SHAP), and equivalence testing (TOST), the framework provided complementary perspectives on waveform-level differences, latent temporal structure, feature attribution, and practical equivalence. Three principal observations emerged. First, the framework identified distributed speed-dependent neuromuscular adaptations across multiple muscles, with particularly strong contributions from tibialis anterior activity. Second, no ground reaction force component demonstrated statistically significant waveform-level differences within the sensitivity limits of the present analysis. Third, fPCA-derived waveform features enabled accurate speed discrimination and highlighted interpretable multi-muscle coordination patterns. Collectively, these findings demonstrated the value of integrating statistical inference and explainable machine learning within a unified gait-analysis workflow.

Absence of detectable GRF waveform changes

No statistically significant cluster‑level waveform differences were detected for any GRF component, and pointwise TOST indicated that Fz differences were time‑distributed and below the threshold for contiguous effects. The authors interpreted this as an absence of evidence for waveform-level differences, rather than evidence of preservation or practical equivalence. The narrow speed range and left-leg-only analysis may have limited sensitivity. Notably, conventional discrete GRF outcomes (e.g., vertical peaks, impulses) showed expected speed-related increases (Table S9), confirming that the null SPM finding reflected waveform-shape conservation rather than a general insensitivity to speed, even though discrete peak values may scale [18, 44]. However, because equivalence was not demonstrated at the pre‑specified ± 0.2 N/kg bound, these findings should be interpreted as indicating relative robustness of vertical loading rather than definitive preservation of GRF waveform shape. The narrow speed range and left‑leg‑only analysis may have limited sensitivity.

Multi-muscle speed adaptation

TA remained the dominant speed-modulated muscle, but VL, RF, and ST also showed significant clusters, particularly during stance and swing transitions. This indicated that speed adaptation may not be confined to a single muscle but involves distinct temporal activation patterns across multiple muscles. However, because EMG amplitudes were not normalised to MVC, the authors refrained from comparing absolute activation magnitudes across muscles; the interpretations focus on within-muscle relative changes and waveform timing. In the pooled two-muscle analysis, gastrocnemius modulation appeared limited (one early-stance cluster); however, the six-muscle SPM revealed 8 clusters for gastrocnemius medial and 4 for lateral, indicating that speed-dependent gastrocnemius modulation was more extensive when the heads were separated. This finding was consistent with prior work showing that gastrocnemius activity scales with walking speed [14]. For comprehensive EMG assessment, recommended practices include standardised protocols for electrode placement and signal processing [45]. Whereas the present study adhered to SENIAM guidelines [33] for electrode placement, future work should adopt the full methodological framework described in Studnicki et al. [45] to enhance reproducibility and cross-study comparability.

fPCA captures temporal structure

The Random Forest classifier, used as a complementary interpretive tool to SPM, achieved 87.2% LOSO accuracy (95% CI: 79.3%–92.3%). This substantial improvement over simple amplitude features (72.5%) indicated that waveform-derived features contain discriminative information beyond amplitude scaling. SHAP analysis identified TA PC1 as the most influential feature within the trained model (0.143), followed by GAS PC1 (0.058), ST PC1 (0.058), and contributions from RF and VL PCs. The presence of ST, RF, and VL PCs among the top features suggests that speed‑dependent coordination involves multi‑muscle timing patterns. Note that the SHAP values were derived from a model trained on whole‑cohort fPCA scores and are therefore exploratory; they should not be conflated with the cross‑validated accuracy, which was derived from the leakage‑free LOSO pipeline. Whereas the integrated framework is computationally intensive (~ 5 min per participant on a standard desktop), this is acceptable for offline research analyses. Real-time applications, such as wearable biofeedback, would require algorithmic optimisation or reduced feature sets [25, 26, 29].

The machine-learning component was incorporated to quantify discriminative information within multimodal gait signals and to provide interpretable feature attribution complementary to waveform-level statistical inference. Rather than functioning as a black-box classifier, the Random Forest-SHAP framework was used to identify which waveform-derived features contributed most strongly to speed discrimination and to facilitate biological interpretation of the underlying neuromuscular adaptations [46]. This approach aligns with a growing body of work in biomedical signal processing that combines feature extraction from EMG waveforms with interpretable classifiers to study human movement [47]. Because the primary goal was interpretability and convergent validation with SPM, the authors prioritised within-participant leave-one-participant-out (LOSO) cross-validation and SHAP‑based feature importance over maximal classification accuracy. The feature-to-participant ratio (12 features for 55 participants) remained within recommended limits to reduce overfitting risk [48], which was further minimised by the LOSO procedure. Alternative classifiers (e.g., elastic net, support vector machine) may yield comparable performance but were not pursued because SHAP importance from tree‑based models is more computationally tractable and easier to interpret. The stability of the SHAP rankings was verified by re-running the full pipeline with different seeds, which produced a consistent top-feature order. Accordingly, the SHAP results were interpreted directly in conjunction with, rather than independently of, the continuous SPM findings.

Methodological considerations

The primary contribution of this study was the demonstration of an interpretable multimodal biomedical signal-processing workflow that integrates statistical inference, dimensionality reduction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing within a single analytical framework. Statistical parametric mapping provided waveform-level inference whereas preserving temporal structure. Functional principal component analysis reduced high-dimensional waveform data into compact representations suitable for machine learning. Random Forest classification quantified discriminative information content, SHAP improved model interpretability through feature attribution, and TOST enabled evaluation of practical equivalence rather than merely the absence of statistical significance. Together, these complementary techniques provided an interpretable framework for extracting clinically relevant information from multimodal gait signals, where statistical inference and machine-learning approaches are frequently applied in isolation. Conceptual schematic of the multimodal analytical framework. Walking speed (0.5 m/s → 1.0 m/s) is used as the input to a multimodal dataset comprising three‑axis ground reaction forces (GRF) and six lower‑limb surface electromyography (EMG) signals. Continuous waveform processing includes time‑normalisation to 101 points and body‑weight normalisation of forces. The processed waveforms are analysed via two complementary branches: (1) paired statistical parametric mapping (SPM) with cluster‑based permutation, which reveals no detectable GRF waveform changes and multi‑muscle EMG clusters; and (2) functional principal component analysis (fPCA), which extracts latent temporal features dominated by tibialis anterior PC1, with contributions from other muscles. Both branches feed into an explainable machine‑learning module (Random Forest with SHAP), which achieves 87% classification accuracy and confirms TA as the top predictor whereas highlighting the role of multi‑muscle timing features. The combined findings suggest that walking-speed adaptation involves distributed multi-muscle activation, whereas no GRF component showed cluster‑level waveform differences within the present sensitivity and equivalence was not demonstrated

Limitations

The paired SPM-TOST-ML framework of this study provided a comprehensive toolkit, but several limitations apply. The healthy young adult sample limits generalisability to older adults [49] and clinical populations. Second, EMG amplitudes were not normalised to MVC or submaximal reference contractions, which precludes direct comparison of absolute activation levels across muscles and participants [50, 51]. This limitation has differential consequences across the analytical branches: (a) SPM effect sizes (Cohen’s dz) are not comparable between muscles; the authors therefore restrict between‑muscle comparisons to qualitative temporal patterns; (b) fPCA PC loadings reflect waveform shape rather than amplitude, partially mitigating the impact; and (c) SHAP feature importance may be influenced by signal scaling, so the dominance of TA PC1 should be interpreted as waveform discriminability rather than neuromuscular dominance. The authors performed a sensitivity analysis using per‑participant z-scoring, which yielded qualitatively unchanged SPM results (Table S10), supporting the robustness of the temporal waveform findings. Because the publicly released EMG data were provided as mean absolute value (MAV) muscle-activation signals rather than high-frequency raw EMG waveforms, additional power-line notch filtering could not be applied to the released signals. This lack of normalisation may have contributed to the weak GAS results and reduced the signal-to-noise ratio in the machine learning features. Whereas LOSO cross-validation provided a participant-level unbiased performance estimate, the authors did not use a separate validation set for hyperparameter tuning because the Random Forest hyperparameters were fixed. Future work could explore hyperparameter optimisation with nested cross‑validation.

Third, the narrow speed range (0.5–1.0 m/s) may have been insufficient to elicit robust plantar flexor modulation or GRF waveform changes, as more pronounced EMG speed effects emerge at higher speeds [52]; future studies should include a broader range of speeds (e.g., up to 1.6 m/s). Fourth, the IMU-based knee kinematics were not analysed due to data incompleteness, precluding a joint-level interpretation of the TA and GAS activation patterns. Fifth, the EEG data were not used, so potential cortico‑muscular interactions that are known to occur during walking [53] could not be assessed. Finally, whereas LOSO cross validation provided an unbiased estimate of classification performance, the absolute accuracy (872%) may still be limited by the available feature set and the absence of kinematic variables that could further improve speed discrimination.

Surface EMG from TA may be contaminated by cross‑talk from adjacent muscles, especially the extensor digitorum longus and peroneus tertius [14, 54]. This could affect the precision of TA onset/offset timing and may contribute to the observed widespread modulation. The GRF analysis was limited to the left leg; potential bilateral asymmetries, which are present even in able‑bodied gait [55], could not be assessed.

Conclusions

This study evaluated an integrated computational framework for analysing continuous multimodal gait waveforms derived from ground reaction forces and multi-muscle surface electromyography. First, SPM revealed significant clusters for multiple muscles beyond TA, confirming hypothesis (1). Second, no GRF component showed cluster-level waveform differences within the present framework, partially supporting hypothesis (2). Third, fPCA-based machine learning improved speed classification accuracy over simple amplitude metrics, supporting hypothesis (3). These findings suggest that neuromuscular adaptation to moderate speed changes may involve distributed multi-muscle activation patterns detectable through waveform-based analyses, whereas GRF differences did not reach cluster-level significance.

The integrated SPM-fPCA-SHAP-TOST pipeline demonstrated how statistical inference, dimensionality reduction, explainable machine learning, and equivalence testing could be combined within a unified biomedical signal-processing workflow for multimodal gait analysis. Beyond the present walking-speed application, the framework could be adaptable to a broad range of biomedical signal-analysis problems; however, clinical utility in rehabilitation engineering, wearable sensing, digital biomarker development, and movement-disorder assessment remains to be tested in patient cohorts, real-world walking conditions, and longitudinal outcome studies. The present analysis should be viewed primarily as a methodological demonstration of an integrated analytical pipeline in a controlled public dataset.
