Association between lumbar disc degeneration at L4-L5 level and atrophy of paraspinal muscles and gluteus medius: a cross-sectional study using 3T quantitative magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Qun Wen, Jiaoyan Wang, Guang Tan, Yanwen Huang, Hui Wang, Xiaoqing Ding, Kaoqiang Liu, Yujie Zhang, Wenli Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore alterations and correlations of multifidus (MF), erector spinae (ES), psoas major (PM), and gluteus medius (Gmed) in intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) using multi-echo Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) based water-fat separation.MethodsWe retrospectively collected data from patients who presented to our hospital with low back pain. Proton density fat fraction (PDFF) was measured using the multi-echo Dixon VIBE sequence. IVDD at the L4-L5 level was assessed on T2-weighted sagittal images according to the Pfirrmann grading system. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the relationships between MF, ES, PM, and Gmed cross-sectional area (CSA), PDFF, and IVDD. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to determine independent associations of gender, age, and Pfirrmann grade with CSA and PDFF of the MF, ES, PM and Gmed.ResultsA total of 506 patients with a mean age of 44.43 ± 13.49 years were included. As Pfirrmann grade increased, the CSA of the MF, ES, and PM progressively decreased. With advancing Pfirrmann grade, the PDFF of the MF, ES, and PM demonstrated a progressive increase. The Pfirrmann grade showed a moderate negative correlation with MF and ES CSA (Rho = -0.395, Rho = -0.348, p < 0.001), and a weak negative correlation with PM CSA (Rho = -0.293, p < 0.05). Conversely, Pfirrmann grade demonstrated a strong positive correlation with MF and ES PDFF (Rho = 0.595, Rho = 0.610, p < 0.001), and a moderate positive correlation with PM PDFF (Rho = 0.415, p < 0.001). Multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that gender, age, and IVDD were independently associated with CSA and PDFF of the MF and ES muscles (P < 0.001). However, for the PM muscle, only its PDFF showed independent correlations with IVDD, gender, and age, while PM CSA was independently linked to gender and age but not to Pfirrmann grade. With an increase in the Pfirrmann grade of intervertebral discs, Gmed CSA shows a tendency to increase. The Pfirrmann grade demonstrated weak positive correlations with both the Gmed CSA and PDFF (Rho = 0.160, Rho = 0.264, p < 0.001). After adjusting for the confounding effects of sex and age, the Pfirrmann grade remained an independent factor associated with Gmed CSA (β = 0.136, p = 0.001).ConclusionsMRI reliably evaluates paraspinal muscles and Gmed atrophy, particularly in quantifying fat content. PDFF emerges as a valuable tool for assessing fat infiltration in paraspinal muscles.

Introduction

With the aging population, low back pain (LBP) has become a common and frequent condition threatening human health, currently ranking as the second most common reason for medical visits [1]. This symptom not only causes physical and psychological suffering, reducing patients’ quality of life, but also imposes a significant economic burden on both individuals and healthcare systems. The precise causes of most LBP cases remain unclear. It is generally believed that LBP may be associated with intervertebral discs, paraspinal muscles, facet joints, vertebral bodies, or nerve root compression. Initially, intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) was considered the primary contributor to LBP [2]. IVDD arises from multifactorial causes, including aging, trauma, poor posture, abnormal loading, environmental factors, and genetic predisposition [3]. IVDD serves as the pathological foundation for various spinal disorders, such as annular tears, spinal instability, facet joint degeneration, spinal stenosis, and chronic LBP, particularly disc herniation [4]. Recent advancements in spinal stability theory and imaging technologies have underscored the critical role of paraspinal muscles in maintaining spinal integrity. A growing body of evidence highlights the frequent coexistence of IVDD and paraspinal muscles pathology, suggesting their synergistic contribution to spinal dysfunction [5, 6]. The paraspinal muscles are divided into anterior and posterior groups. The posterior group primarily includes the multifidus (MF) and erector spinae (ES), while the anterior group mainly comprises the psoas major (PM), which plays a vital role in maintaining spinal stability and controlling lumbar motion [5]. Panjabi [7] defined the paraspinal muscles and ligaments as a subsystem of the spinal stabilization system, proposing that they sustain normal trunk posture, ensure joint stability, and protect intervertebral discs and facet joints during movement. Reduced paraspinal muscles function can alter biomechanical relationships, increase disc loading, and thereby accelerate IVDD. Conversely, IVDD may trigger compensatory adaptations in paraspinal muscles, leading to load imbalance and atrophy [8]. Studies [6] have indicated that spinal segments affected by IVDD exhibit lower Computed Tomography(CT) density values in adjacent paraspinal muscles, which may degenerate further due to a “vicious cycle”. The relationship between IVDD and paraspinal muscles degeneration is bidirectional. On one hand, paraspinal muscles atrophy can induce lumbar instability, exacerbating IVDD and disc herniation. On the other hand, IVDD at specific segments increases compensatory stress on adjacent muscles, potentially hastening their degenerative changes. However, the quantitative relationship between paraspinal muscles alterations and IVDD in patients with LBP remains poorly understood. Thus, further research into quantifiable changes in paraspinal muscles associated with IVDD and LBP is warranted. The gluteus medius (Gmed) is a critical muscle in the hip region, serving as a core stabilizer for maintaining an upright and balanced posture under both static and dynamic conditions. However, its alterations in the context of lumbar degenerative changes have long been overlooked.

The Dixon technique exploits the difference in precessional frequencies of hydrogen protons in water and fat within a magnetic field to effectively separate water and fat signals, enabling quantitative assessment of fat content across body regions [9]. The VIBE-q-Dixon method utilizes six echoes in a single acquisition to generate six image sets: water phase, fat phase, water fraction (WF), fat fraction (FF), R2*, and T2*, directly measuring the proton density fat fraction (PDFF) of tissues. While existing Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) studies have explored paraspinal muscle changes in IVDD patients, most assessments of muscular fat infiltration rely on semi-quantitative methods [10, 11], which are prone to subjective biases.

In this study, we employ VIBE-q-Dixon to quantitatively measure PDFF of paraspinal muscles and Gmed in IVDD patients and evaluate their cross-sectional areas (CSA) on axial T2-weighted images, aiming to determine whether IVDD correlates with paraspinal muscles and Gmed atrophy. Additionally, we also explore whether gender and age are related to the atrophy of the paraspinal muscles and Gmed.

Materials and methods

Study design

A total of 546 subjects with LBP were retrospectively enrolled from the Orthopedics Department of Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between March 2018 and December 2023. After excluding 40 patients due to poor image quality, 506 participants were finally included (200 males, 306 females; age range: 20–87 years, mean age: 44.43 ± 13.49 years). Exclusion criteria comprised a history of lumbar surgery, vertebral fractures, spondylolisthesis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, spinal infections, malignancy, or pregnancy. All participants in this study signed the informed consent form and provided consent for publication. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The study was approved by the local institutional review board (No. 2020-815-22-01).

Magnetic resonance imaging parameters

All participants underwent spinal MRI using a 3.0 T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with an 18-channel body surface coil and a table-mounted spine coil. The imaging protocol included axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), axial and sagittal T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), as well as axial multi-echo Dixon sequences. Assessment of paraspinal muscles and IVDD

All MRI images were processed using a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Two experienced radiologists independently evaluated the degree of degeneration at the L4/5 level on sagittal T2WI according to the Pfirrmann grading system. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. The Pfirrmann scoring system categorizes disc degeneration into five grades (Fig. 1):

Fig. 1illustrates the Pfirrmann grading system for the assessment of lumbar disc degeneration

illustrates the Pfirrmann grading system for the assessment of lumbar disc degeneration

Grade I: Normal disc structure and height, bright hyperintense signal intensity, distinct nucleus pulposus-annulus fibrosus interface.

Grade II: Heterogeneous disc structure with preserved height, hyperintense signal intensity, clear demarcation between nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus.

Grade III: Heterogeneous structure, normal or slightly reduced height, intermediate (gray) signal intensity, loss of nucleus pulposus-annulus fibrosus demarcation.

Grade IV: Structural disorganization, normal or moderately reduced height, hypointense (dark gray) signal intensity, complete loss of nucleus pulposus-annulus fibrosus distinction.

Grade V: Structural collapse, severely hypointense signal, and indistinct nuclear-annular boundaries.

Based on previous studies, the L4–5 level was selected for paraspinal muscles assessment because it provides the largest CSA of the paraspinal muscles at this level, and the FF measured here is highly representative of overall muscle composition [12]. Axial T2WI was used to measure the CSA of the paraspinal muscles, and the PDFF map were utilized to quantify PDFF values (Fig. 2a-b). At the level of the anterior superior iliac spine, the CSA of the Gmed is measured on axial T1WI, and the PDFF of the Gmed is measured on the PDFF map (Fig. 2c-d). The CSA and PDFF of the bilateral paraspinal muscles(MF, ES, PM) as well as Gmed were measured separately, and the mean values from both sides were calculated for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Chi-square test was used to compare sex differences across IVDD grades. The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to compare whether the differences in CSA and PDFF of the paraspinal muscles and Gmed across different Pfirrmann grades were statistically significant, and post-hoc analysis of continuous variables was conducted using the Bonferroni correction method. We used Spearman correlation analysis to evaluate the relationships between the CSA and PDFF of the MF, ES, PM and Gmed with IVDD and sex (since Pfirrmann grading is an ordinal variable). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to evaluate associations with age. Correlation strength was interpreted as follows: weak (< 0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), strong (0.5–0.7), or very strong (> 0.7) [13]. Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to determine independent associations of sex, age, and IVDD grade with CSA and PDFF of the MF, ES, PM and Gmed. The results of the multiple linear regression are presented as standardized regression coefficients (β). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In clinical studies, muscle atrophy in patients is influenced by numerous factors, including age, gender, BMI, and physical activity [14]. To analyze paraspinal muscles atrophy, we measured the CSA and PDFF of the MF and ES across different Pfirrmann grade. Our findings revealed that as Pfirrmann grade increased, the CSA of both MF and ES progressively decreased. Only the CSA of MF had no statistical significance between grades II and III (P > 0.05), though significant differences existed between all other adjacent grades. The CSA of ES had little difference between different grades. We further validated the relationship between paraspinal muscles CSA and IVDD through Spearman correlation analysis. The results demonstrated that Pfirrmann grade exhibited a moderate negative correlation with the CSA of both the MF and ES, while showing a weak negative correlation with PM CSA. In a separate study [15], which employed similar imaging methodologies to ours, the CSA of MF and ES at the L4/5 and L5/S1 levels was examined and analyzed for associations with IVDD. Notably, their findings diverged from ours: the previous study reported only a weak correlation between MF CSA and Pfirrmann grade, and no significant correlation for ES CSA, which was inconsistent with our results. Yaltirik et al. [16] observed that the MF and ES CSA were significantly reduced in patients with lumbar disc herniation (LDH) compared to controls. D’Hooge et al. [17] reported that fatty degeneration of muscles does not necessarily alter their CSA. Similarly, Yazici et al. [11] found that in LDH patients, the ES area was unexpectedly lower in the non-nerve root compression group than in the nerve root compression group, suggesting the presence of pseudohypertrophy in the latter. Pseudohypertrophy implies that CSA increases due to fat infiltration despite muscle atrophy. Although numerous studies have investigated paraspinal muscles CSA, the interpretation of muscle atrophy remains controversial. Khil et al. [18] measured the CSA of paraspinal muscles across spinal segments using both CT and MRI. Their results indicated no statistically significant differences in paraspinal muscles CSA between lumbar spinal levels on CT imaging, whereas MRI revealed a significant reduction in CSA at lower spinal segments. Given the inconsistency in CSA findings across studies, some researchers propose that fat infiltration, rather than CSA itself, may be more strongly associated with IVDD [19]. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that, alterations in paraspinal muscles CSA are influenced by multiple confounding factors, including the severity of IVDD, nerve root compression status, and duration of low back symptoms, which could explain the variability in study results. Compared to CSA, fat infiltration may more directly impair lumbar functional mobility and better reflect the pathophysiological progression of paraspinal muscle atrophy.

In recent years, the relationship between paraspinal muscles and IVDD has become a highly debated topic. Located posterior to the spine, the MF and ES contribute to spinal stability through their contractions and enhance the integrity of the posterior annulus fibrosus. Together, they resist shear forces, thereby delaying rupture of the posterior annulus fibrosus and mitigating IVDD progression [6]. The MF primarily aids in spinal extension and rotation. Notably, the CSA of the MF progressively increases from the upper to lower lumbar spine, whereas the CSA of the ES decreases caudally [5]. In contrast, the PM, situated anterolateral to the spine, functions primarily to prevent excessive spinal hyperextension and anterior disc protrusion. However, its role in delaying IVDD is relatively limited compared to the MF and ES [6]. Multiple metrics are used to assess muscle atrophy, with CSA and fatty infiltration being common imaging-based indicators for evaluating paraspinal muscles degeneration.

In this study, we aimed to utilize quantitative MRI to measure PDFF within paraspinal muscles, investigating whether IVDD in patients with LBP correlates with paraspinal muscles atrophy. The findings revealed that as the Pfirrmann grade increased, PDFF values in the MF, ES, and PM significantly increased. For the MF, no statistically significant differences in PDFF were observed between grades II and III or between grades IV and V, while other grade comparisons were statistically significant. Similarly, the ES showed no significant PDFF differences between grades II and III, while other grade comparisons were statistically significant. Notably, Pfirrmann grade exhibited a strong positive correlation with PDFF in both MF and ES. The lack of significant differences in the PDFF of the MF and ES between grades II and III, and in the PDFF of the MF between grades IV and V, may be attributed to the classification of grades II–III as mild-to-moderate IVDD and grades IV–V as severe IVDD [20]. The PDFF of paraspinal muscles is positively correlated with IVDD, suggesting that increased fatty infiltration in these muscles may serve as an independent risk factor for IVDD. Previous studies have explored the relationship between paraspinal muscles atrophy and IVDD. A study on patients with chronic LBP revealed significant correlations between PDFF in the MF and ES and the severity of IVDD, aligning with the findings of this research [15]. Cheng et al. demonstrated that, after adjusting for factors such as gender, age, weight, BMI, and facet joint degeneration, the degree of fatty degeneration in the MF and ES of chronic LBP patients remained strongly associated with IVDD [21]. Huang et al. [22] utilized quantitative MRI to assess fatty infiltration in paraspinal muscles within a novel discogenic LBP model, involving both human patients and rats. Their findings demonstrated fatty infiltration in paraspinal muscles across both groups, establishing a causal link between fatty infiltration and IVDD. Interestingly, another study reported a poor correlation between the severity of MF fatty atrophy and IVDD in LBP patients [1], highlighting potential variability in pathophysiological mechanisms. Stevens et al. [23] propose that atrophy of the MF is caused by denervation due to nerve compression. Following muscle denervation, tissue edema occurs initially, and prolonged denervation leads to progressive muscle atrophy [24]. Other studies [5, 25] suggest that the degree of MF atrophy is significantly correlated with the duration of LBP, with longer clinical courses and symptom persistence associated with more severe MF atrophy. However, experimental animal studies have shown that even in cases of anterolateral disc lesions that do not compromise the posterior annulus fibrosus, local degenerative changes can still develop in paraspinal muscles. This indicates that paraspinal muscles degeneration may occur in the absence of direct nerve compression [26]. Hence, some researchers argue that spinal degeneration typically disrupts spinal function, subsequently modifying MF quality, and the relationship among spinal degeneration, MF atrophy, and LBP-related outcomes represents a complex interplay [27].

Only a limited number of studies have analyzed changes in the PM in the context of IVDD, yielding inconsistent findings. Özcan-Ekşi et al. [20] employed semi-quantitative methods to compare fatty infiltration in paraspinal muscles at corresponding disc levels between patients with severe IVDD and those with mild-to-moderate IVDD. They reported that MF and ES at affected levels exhibited higher fatty infiltration in severe IVDD patients, but no significant association was observed between IVDD severity and fatty infiltration in the corresponding PM. Another study [28] verified that the Pfirrmann grade, as the first weighting factor, was independently associated with fatty infiltration of MF and ES, and as the second weighting factor in PM. Yaltirik et al. [16] found no significant difference in PM CSA between patients with LDH and the control group. However, Kim et al. [29] observed a reduction in PM CSA among LDH patients with symptoms persisting for over three months. Shen et al. [6] compared fatty infiltration levels in the MF, ES, and PM between patients with single-level and multilevel IVDD. They reported that single-level IVDD was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of fatty infiltration in MF and ES compared to multilevel IVDD, while PM fatty infiltration showed no statistically significant difference. Furthermore, the study emphasized that increased fatty infiltration in MF and ES correlated with a higher risk of degeneration. Arbanas et al. [30] demonstrated that patients with IVDD accompanied by Modic changes exhibited smaller PM CSA compared to healthy individuals, a finding corroborated by Cui et al. [31]. Similarly, Dangaria et al. [32] documented ipsilateral PM CSA reduction in patients with unilateral LDH. Additionally, several authors have described ipsilateral PM atrophy in individuals with unilateral chronic back pain [33]. Animal studies [34] have demonstrated significant differences in the PM when comparing varying degrees of IVDD. Our findings indicate correlation between IVDD and PM atrophy: the CSA of the PM consistently diminished with increasing IVDD severity, exhibiting a weak negative correlation. Conversely, the PDFF of the PM escalated alongside IVDD progression, with fatty infiltration severity showing a moderate positive correlation with IVDD stages. Synthesizing these results with prior literature, conflicting evidence persists regarding PM atrophy in IVDD. For instance, Arbanas et al. [30] compared the PM CSA between patients with LBP and a control group, found that the PM CSA in patients with LBP was larger than that in the control group. More than 80% of control group as well as patients showed no detectable fat infiltration in both PM. As IVDD severity increases, the likelihood of LBP rises. To alleviate pain, patients may reduce physical activity, potentially leading to PM atrophy and subsequent fatty infiltration. Notably, neither the reduction in CSA nor the progression of fatty infiltration in the PM was as pronounced as observed in the MF or ES, aligning with their distinct functional roles.

To further investigate the independent effects and relative contributions of gender, age, and the severity of IVDD on the CSA and PDFF of the MF, ES, and PM, we performed a multivariate linear regression analysis. The results revealed that these three factors exhibited distinct patterns of influence on different muscles and metrics. This study found that gender was the strongest factor affecting MF and ES CSA, even surpassing the effects of Pfirrmann grade and age. This suggests that, after controlling for the effects of age and degeneration severity, gender itself is a key determinant of muscle CSA. Yazici et al. also reported that males have larger MF and ES CSA than females, with age demonstrating weak- moderate association with muscle CSA [11]. However, studies in the literature present divergent findings regarding age-related muscle changes. For instance, Khil et al. [18] measured CSA of lumbar paraspinal muscles and the PM across vertebral levels via CT and MRI, concluding that CSA was strongly correlated with gender (males > females) but showed no significant age-related differences. There result inconsistent with our findings. This discrepancy may arise from differences in study populations: their cohort comprised relatively young, asymptomatic adults, whereas our participants spanned a broader age range (20–87 years). Additionally, Khil et al. [18] observed that paraspinal muscle density and fat fraction were linked to both age and gender, with females exhibiting lower muscle density and higher fat fractions. Notably, they identified gender as the strongest determinant of these parameters. Some researchers have proposed that the CSA of the MF and ES is unrelated to age. Alikemal Yazici et al. [11] analyzed fatty infiltration in paraspinal muscles among patients with herniated discs, comparing those with and without nerve root compression. Their findings revealed that females consistently demonstrated higher fat infiltration rates than males in both groups, and fat infiltration increased steadily with age. Interestingly, a separate study [10] reported that in women with chronic LBP, IVDD showed no significant association with paraspinal muscles fatty infiltration at any lumbar spinal level. Özcan-Ekşi et al. [20] investigated the association between IVDD and paraspinal muscles fatty infiltration by comparing lumbar discs and paraspinal muscles in 50 symptomatic females and 50 symptomatic males. They found that females exhibited greater fatty infiltration in the MF and ES at the L4-5 and L5-S1 levels. The more pronounced fatty infiltration in female paraspinal muscles may be attributed to higher estrogen levels and greater body fat percentage.

In our study, Pfirrmann grade showed an independent negative correlation with the CSA of the MF and ES, confirming that the severity of IVDD is significantly associated with atrophy of the paraspinal muscles, particularly the dorsal muscle groups. Notably, for the CSA of the PM, only gender and age were independent correlates, while Pfirrmann grade did not demonstrate a statistically significant association. This finding suggests that, compared to the dorsally located MF and ES, the atrophy of the ventrally situated PM may be less sensitive to IVDD itself, and its dimensional changes are likely influenced more significantly by gender and systemic aging processes. In terms of fatty infiltration, the analysis revealed a pattern distinct from that of CSA. Age emerged as the strongest predictor of PDFF for all three muscles (MF, ES, and PM), indicating that advancing age is the primary independent risk factor for fatty infiltration in the paraspinal muscles. Importantly, Pfirrmann grade demonstrated independent positive correlations with PDFF across all muscles, indicating that even after adjusting for the effects of age and gender, more severe IVDD remains a significant predictor of higher muscular fatty infiltration. This finding strengthens the pathological link between IVDD and fatty infiltration of the paraspinal muscles. Therefore, based on our results, the atrophy and fatty infiltration of paraspinal muscles may be regulated by distinct physiological and pathological mechanisms. When evaluating paraspinal muscles degeneration, it is essential to consider both gender, age and IVDD severity, and to analyze muscles at different locations separately.

This study also investigated the relationship between IVDD and the morphology and composition of the Gmed. The key finding is that although Pfirrmann grade showed weak correlations with Gmed CSA and fatty infiltration, multivariate linear regression analysis revealed that this association was not independent but rather driven by the strong confounding factors of age and gender. The observed tendency of increased Gmed CSA with higher degeneration grades in this study may reflect compensatory hypertrophy. As lumbar degeneration progresses and spinal stability decreases, the Gmed, a key hip abductor and stabilizer,​ may need to bear increased loads to maintain pelvic and gait stability, potentially leading to compensatory hypertrophy. These alterations in the Gmed may be a protective or compensatory response for the intervertebral discs. However, after controlling for the confounding effects of gender and age, IVDD itself does not directly cause fatty infiltration of the muscle. This indicates that IVDD primarily affects the size of the Gmed, while fatty infiltration is more strongly driven by systemic factors such as age and gender. This suggests that changes in the Gmed may not be a specific marker of IVDD. However, the hypotheses of compensatory hypertrophy and the protective mechanism for intervertebral discs warrant further validation to deepen our understanding of the compensatory mechanisms of pelvic girdle muscles under lumbar pathological conditions. Lee et al. [35] found that in patients with degenerative lumbar scoliosis (DLS), degeneration of the hip muscles was associated with more severe back pain and poorer functional status. Compared to paraspinal muscles, hip muscles demonstrated a more pronounced correlation with global spinal imbalance.

This study has several limitations. First, given the cross-sectional design of our study, we recommend that future research employs longitudinal cohorts to further investigate and confirm the relationship between IVDD and paraspinal muscles fatty infiltration. Second, all participants were of Asian ethnicity from Shanghai, China. Given that IVDD correlates with lifestyle, physical activity, and occupational intensity, the findings may not generalize to the global population of IVDD patients. Third, while the L4/5 level was selected as the optimal measurement plane, potential changes in paraspinal muscles at L1-4 and L5-S1 levels remain unexamined. Fourth, this study only demonstrated correlations between paraspinal muscles CSA, PDFF, and IVDD. Without histopathological biopsies, the causal relationship between paraspinal muscles metrics (CSA, PDFF) and IVDD remains unestablished. Fourth, this study only evaluated changes in the Gmed at the level of the anterior superior iliac spine but did not assess alterations in the gluteus maximus and gluteus minimus. Further investigation is needed in subsequent research.

Conclusions

At the L4/5 level, as IVDD severity increases, the CSA of the MF, ES, and PM progressively decrease, while their PDFF values correspondingly increase. The severity of IVDD is significantly associated with paraspinal muscles area, particularly exerting a notable impact on the MF and ES. Pfirrmann grade demonstrates an independent positive correlation with the PDFF of the MF, ES, and PM, indicating that severe IVDD is a significant predictor of increased fatty infiltration in these muscles. The CSA of the Gmed shows a tendency to enlarge with advancing degeneration grades. MRI can reliably assess paraspinal muscles atrophy, with particular accuracy in detecting fat content. PDFF serves as an optimal tool for evaluating fatty infiltration in paraspinal muscles.
