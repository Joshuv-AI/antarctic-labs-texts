Clinical Impact of Target‐Mediated Drug Disposition of Small Molecules with Focus on Early Phase I Study Designs
Authors: Francesca Tessaro, Neil John Parrott, Matthias B Wittwer, Franziska Schaedeli Stark, Andrés Olivares‐Morales, Michael Derks, Stefan Sturm
Published: 2026

Target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) presents a significant challenge in small molecule drug development and may only become evident during first‐in‐human (FIH) trials. Characterized by non‐linear pharmacokinetics and distinct exposure‐response relationships, TMDD can introduce safety risks and complicate dose regimen selection. This perspective emphasizes the importance of early identification of TMDD and integration of quantitative pharmacology approaches to inform development strategy, mitigate risks, and support data‐driven decision‐making in early‐phase clinical studies.

BACKGROUND

Non‐linear pharmacokinetics of small molecules can result from target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD), a phenomenon that can be of clinical relevance. TMDD occurs when a drug binds with high affinity to a pharmacological target that has capacity‐limited binding, resulting in dose‐dependent changes in distribution and elimination. Historically, TMDD has been less common for small molecules than for large molecules. If preclinical pharmacokinetic studies use doses that fully saturate the target, then non‐linear pharmacokinetics due to TMDD might only be identified during clinical development with unexpected deviations from predicted clinical pharmacokinetics. This may necessitate FIH protocol amendments and can greatly complicate clinical development. The rising prevalence of highly potent small molecules has led to an increasing number of TMDD examples reported, including binders to tissue‐based targets such as MAO‐B (e.g., sembragiline) and circulation‐based targets such as dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (e.g., linagliptin). 1 Factors influencing TMDD and its appearance in small molecules include binding affinity, target‐binding capacity, the compartmental or anatomical location and nature of the target (i.e., membrane‐bound receptor versus circulating enzyme), competition from other body‐binding constituents, the bioanalytical assay sensitivity and the administered dose. 1 , 2

Although TMDD can be expected for protein therapeutics, its less frequent manifestation in small molecules introduces distinct challenges for interpretation and study design. Consequently, TMDD may present differently from what is typically observed with biologics (Table 1), requiring careful consideration in the identification of non‐linear pharmacokinetics and in the design of clinical studies to appropriately characterize these effects.

INDICATORS OF TARGET‐MEDIATED DRUG DISPOSITION (TMDD) AND POINTS TO CONSIDER

From a drug development perspective, TMDD should always be considered when specific drug‐ and system‐related characteristics are present. Although it may be challenging to predict TMDD quantitatively from preclinical data, a highly expressed target and a potently binding drug are indications of that possibility. Then, if observed pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic behavior deviates from conventional expectations, the possibility of TMDD should be considered. A combination of findings often points to the presence of TMDD: non‐linear plasma pharmacokinetics and prolonged terminal elimination half‐life. For tissue‐based targets, concentration‐time profiles typically converge at later time points, and PK only becomes linear once the target is saturated. 4 , 5 High and sustained tissue retention, apparent dose‐dependent changes in half‐life or volume of distribution, and a discrepancy between predicted and observed exposure—if not accounted for, particularly at low doses—further support the presence of TMDD.

Translation from preclinical data might present limitations regarding quantitative prediction of clinical TMDD, i.e., availability of target expression across species; in vitro potency assays with unknown target concentration and no correction for free drug might mislead compounds with different true potencies to appear equipotent. 2 Nonetheless, preclinical studies such as the inclusion of non‐binding control compounds or knockout models may help confirm that observed PK profiles and parameters are target mediated. 3 When linagliptin was administered to wild‐type rats, it exhibited non‐linear pharmacokinetics due to saturable binding with its target, DPP‐4. In contrast, when the compound was evaluated in DPP‐4 deficient (knockout) animals, the absence of the target resulted in completely linear PK and shortened apparent terminal half‐life. 6

Additionally, a delayed or prolonged pharmacological response indicated by a hysteresis‐shaped PK/PD relationship may reflect sustained target binding. Several experimental approaches can be critical for identifying TMDD. For instance, implementing a low quantification limit to enhance sensitivity and an extended sampling schedule to accurately capture long terminal half‐lives. Evaluating tissue pharmacokinetics can uncover a disconnect between plasma exposure and target site concentrations, particularly for tissue‐bound targets. PET tracer occupancy studies may further elucidate target engagement and tissue distribution. 7

TMDD IN EARLY CLINICAL TRIALS

Single dose studies

For FIH studies, systemic exposure is often predicted using physiologically based pharmacokinetics models. If TMDD is not accounted for in the model, typically the observed plasma concentrations may be substantially lower than predicted. When such discrepancies arise after a single dose, TMDD should be considered as a potential underlying cause. This phenomenon poses significant challenges and should be carefully evaluated in dose decision meetings between cohorts in order not to put participants at undue risk. Nonlinear PK due to TMDD could result in supra‐proportional increases in exposures. For example, for small molecules exhibiting TMDD such as sembragiline, dose increments of 3‐fold have been associated with increases in systemic exposure exceeding 100‐fold at low doses. Thus, adjustment of the planned dose levels may be warranted in order not to exceed predefined maximum expected exposure and associated pharmacodynamic effect. Smaller dose increments should be considered, evaluating aspects such as the steepness of the dose–response curve, possible risks—especially if it is a novel mechanism of action—and potential effects on life‐critical functions.

It is particularly recommended that pharmacodynamic (PD) characteristics are assessed in FIH studies, as substantial changes in PD could be observed at low doses in the non‐linear pharmacokinetic range at low systemic concentrations. The linear PK range might only start once the target is saturated and a plateauing of the PD effect is achieved (Figure 1). Moreover, the maximum exposure explored in FIH studies should be within the pharmacodynamic dose range, which could be important from a safety perspective in order not to expose participants to unnecessarily high drug concentrations. Finally, it should be noted that for small molecules with a long half‐life due to TMDD, the therapeutic dose (after reaching PK and PD steady‐state conditions) is often much lower compared to single doses needed to reach the same PD effect due to large tissue accumulation to saturate the target.

Together, these profiles highlight how TMDD can substantially influence both exposure–response relationships and dose selection in early‐phase clinical studies.

As an intermediate step between single and multiple ascending dose (MAD) studies, two single doses with a wash‐out period might be explored to assess the period effect. As the terminal half‐life might be very long due to a slow dissociation of the small molecule from the target, there might be a carry‐over effect after a typical wash‐out period for small molecules (i.e., 1–3 weeks) despite concentrations being close or below the limit of quantification. The increase in exposure will be dependent on the dose/bioavailability and on the wash‐out period. A cross‐over design can improve PK characterization and parameter estimation (i.e., B max = maximum binding capacity) by reducing variability as assessed within the same participant. This has been applied in the sembragiline project, where two single doses of 5 mg were administered with a wash‐out period of 3 weeks. 8 C max and AUC exposures increased 3‐ to 5‐fold following the second dose compared to the first.

The risk of potential bias resulting from TMDD should be considered when designing a clinical pharmacology trial to study extrinsic factors (e.g., food effect study). A cross‐over design is usually considered to minimize the sample size. A parallel design might be preferred as it is more straightforward to analyze and more practical (due to the long apparent elimination half‐life), especially in case of a narrow therapeutic window and risk of positive food effect. Thus, the treatment (i.e., fasted and fed) could be administered to a separate group of subjects with similar demographics.

Multiple dose studies

For multiple dose studies, the time to reach steady‐state and accumulation might change for different dose levels due to TMDD. Achievement of steady‐state conditions might be mainly dependent on the doses to saturate the B max and the metabolic clearance. This assumes that the dissociation constant of the compound from the target (k off) is much lower than the association and the metabolic elimination rate constant and is driving the terminal half‐life of the compound. The lower the dose, the longer it takes to fill the B max and get to steady‐state conditions, whereas reaching systemic steady‐state conditions for high doses (close to or greater than B max) is mainly dependent on the metabolic clearance.

In MAD studies, lower dose cohorts may require longer treatment duration (e.g., 4 weeks) to reach and maintain steady‐state conditions for one week compared to higher doses (e.g., 2 weeks). Sembragiline exhibits nonlinear PK at low doses due to target‐mediated binding to MAO‐B. Upon saturation of this interaction (at ~5 mg), PK becomes dose‐proportional and time‐invariant, reflecting a transition from target‐mediated to linear kinetics. Consistent with slow dissociation from the target, the terminal half‐life is extended, despite earlier attainment of steady‐state under appropriate dosing conditions. Notably, the terminal half‐life derived from PK is broadly in line with the turnover of target engagement, with mean MAO‐B occupancy half‐lives in the brain estimated at 20–30 days, suggesting that slow target dissociation likely drives the observed terminal phase.

A loading dose can saturate limited target binding capacity, shortening the time to steady state. For sembragiline, the loading dose achieved steady‐state conditions within 14 days at once‐daily doses < 1 mg in a PET study, allowing robust PK/PD characterization over a wide dose range within a practical timeframe. 8

An example of target‐driven steady‐state behavior is observed with the DPP‐4 inhibitor linagliptin. Following administration, plasma concentrations declined multi‐phasic with a prolonged terminal half‐life (> 100 hours) due to slow target dissociation. Once the high‐affinity target is saturated, linagliptin's accumulation is mainly driven by its metabolic clearance, resulting in an effective half‐life of approximately 12 hours. Consequently, steady‐state plasma concentrations are rapidly achieved by the third once‐daily dose during therapeutic 5 mg regimens. 9

For drug–drug interaction studies, multiple‐dose administration of the substrate should be evaluated alone and in combination with multiple doses of the precipitant if the substrate shows time‐dependent PK. A cross‐over study design might be preferred over a parallel design to minimize inter‐subject variability. This requires achieving steady‐state conditions with the therapeutic dose first, followed by steady‐state dosing in combination with the precipitant.

For mass balance studies, multiple dose administration should be considered when the intended therapeutic dose lies in the nonlinear range after a single dose. A radioactive labeled dose is recommended only once steady‐state is reached, enabling ≥ 90% recovery of radioactivity in urine and feces. 10 This may be challenging after a single dose due to deep compartment binding and slow release from it.

CONCLUSIONS

As there is a growing number of small‐molecule drugs exhibiting TMDD due to increased potency of many novel drugs, TMDD in small‐molecule drug development must be proactively considered given the clinical implications. Beyond descriptive characterization, a key requirement is the ability to anticipate when TMDD is likely to occur and to understand its implications for study design and interpretation. Integrating TMDD into quantitative pharmacology approaches and adapting clinical study designs accordingly enable safer, more efficient early development and support data‐driven decision‐making for dose selection.
