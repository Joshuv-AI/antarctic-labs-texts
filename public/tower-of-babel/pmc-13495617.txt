Contrasting structural evolution of starch and protein in aged wheat is associated with interfacial disintegration: implications for nutrient utilization and predictive modeling
Authors: Peng-xue Sun, Ze-qian Lv, Gang Yang, Xin-Yu Liu, Xin-yi Tang, Xue-ning Chang, Liang-zhu Xu, Lin-na Guo, Jun-quan Tian, Yao-ming Cui, Guo-Hao Yang, Ying Liang, Jun-jun Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWheat aging alters starch-protein organization within the endosperm, but its consequences for nutrient digestion and animal performance remain unexplored. This study showed that starch disordering and protein oxidative cross-linking (disulfide bonds: 5.47 μmol/g) were associated with deterioration of the starch–protein interface. Quantitative confocal microscopy supported this interpretation, showing a 28.8% reduction in spatial colocalization. This deterioration was accompanied by altered digestive kinetics, characterized by an 11.35% increase in starch digestibility and a 51.17% decrease in protein digestibility (P < 0.05). In vivo broiler trials supported the physiological relevance of these alterations, as aged-wheat diets were associated with a 28.9% reduction in 42-day body weight. Gluten-hydration-based equations were developed to track nutrient evolution, and predictive models using physicochemical traits were established to assess digestibility (R2 ≥ 0.81) and broiler metabolic performance (R2 ≥ 0.91). These findings provide mechanistic insights and a preliminary framework for targeted utilization of aged wheat.Keywords: Starch-protein interface, Interfacial disintegration, Structure-function relationship, Nutrient digestibility, Predictive model

Introduction

Starch and proteins, as the principal biological macromolecules in wheat (Triticum aestivum), are essential for its nutritional and functional properties. To meet the rising global demand for this staple crop, large-scale, long-term storage of wheat grain has become necessary. During such storage, the structural and functional integrity of starch and proteins progressively deteriorates—a natural process known as wheat aging. This deterioration results in compromised processing performance and edibility of the grain, leading to substantial quantities of over-stored wheat and significant economic losses (Matthews et al., 2012; Ziegler et al., 2021). Importantly, while aging renders wheat unsuitable for direct human consumption, the core nutritional value of its starch and protein macromolecules is not completely lost. According to the Chinese industry standard (NY/T 117–2021, Wheat as Feed Material), aged wheat that has deteriorated during storage but remains unspoiled can be officially utilized as a feed ingredient, provided the safety limits stipulated by the Hygienic Standard for Feeds (GB 13078–2017) are strictly met. This establishes a clear regulatory pathway for the resourceful utilization of aged wheat. Simply diverting it to low-value applications such as fuel production would not only waste the preserved macromolecular nutrients, primarily starch and protein, but also undermine the sustainable goals of reducing food loss and fostering a circular agricultural economy. Therefore, a mechanistic understanding of how storage conditions remodel the molecular architecture of starch and protein, and how this remodeling governs their functional fate, is critically needed to enable the high-value utilization of this underutilized resource.

As the principal biological macromolecules in wheat, starch and proteins have been extensively studied regarding their structural and functional alterations during aging. Regarding detection techniques, emerging physical technologies have been employed to more efficiently assess storage-induced structural changes in these macromolecules (Gong et al., 2021). Regarding biochemical mechanisms, it has been well established that the aging process is accompanied by structural modifications of starch, including an increase in the proportion of short-chain amylopectin, along with oxidative modifications of proteins and lipids (Nagel et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2019). Recent proteomic studies have further revealed how lipid rancidity during storage directly drives oxidative modifications of proteins, leading to the formation of disulfide bonds, increased carbonyl content, and loss of key amino acids. These findings clarify, at the molecular level, that the lipid-protein oxidation cascade is one of the core biochemical pathways responsible for the structural deterioration of protein macromolecules (Xiong et al., 2025). Meanwhile, research on wheat post-harvest maturation indicates that storage systematically alters the multi-scale structure of starch macromolecules: it not only affects the ratio of amylose to amylopectin but also modifies crystallinity, molecular weight distribution, and the proportion of long-chain amylopectin. These structural changes vary significantly depending on the gluten strength of wheat varieties and are directly linked to functional properties such as pasting and gelation (Zhang et al., 2025). Concerning quality improvement, exogenous additives (e.g., γ-aminobutyric acid and orange peel powder) have been shown to enhance the germination rate of aged wheat seeds and the rheological properties of the corresponding dough, suggesting potential strategies for modulating macromolecular functionality (Han et al., 2021). Although proteomic studies have reported that upregulation of defense-related proteins is associated with storage tolerance (Lv et al., 2018), these findings primarily describe adaptive responses rather than revealing how structural changes in starch and proteins directly govern their functional deterioration. These studies have collectively established a foundation for understanding the structural and functional changes of starch and protein macromolecules in aged wheat, but a unifying framework linking the opposite structural evolution of these two macromolecules to their digestive and physiological outcomes remains lacking.

The wheat kernel is a precisely organized starch-protein composite system with spatial heterogeneity, where the composition and distribution of proteins in the starchy endosperm exhibit significant gradients modulated by both genetic and environmental factors (He et al., 2013). This composite architecture, described as a “continuous protein matrix embedded with starch granules,” governs the interfacial interactions between starch and protein macromolecules, which are pivotal for regulating digestive behavior. The gluten network can inhibit starch digestion (Peng et al., 2023), while starch conversely influences the aggregation and digestibility of the protein network (Ma et al., 2025). Other components (e.g., lipids, β-glucans) and exogenous additives can also modulate digestive kinetics by altering this macromolecular structural matrix (Qi et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024).

During long-term storage, the starch-protein macromolecular matrix undergoes spontaneous multi-scale structural evolution. However, the synergistic evolution of this composite, specifically how these two biopolymers coordinately remodel during aging and how their disassembly and reassembly govern in vitro and in vivo digestive fate, remains poorly understood.

Through systematic multi-scale characterization, we observed that during aging, starch progressively shifts toward molecular disordering (e.g., decreased crystallinity and increased amylose content), while proteins concurrently assemble into a denser cross-linked network (e.g., increased disulfide bonds and secondary structure rearrangements). Although these individual phenomena have been reported separately in the literature on grain aging (Pourfarzad et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2025), their explicit definition as ‘opposite structural evolution pathways’ and their relationship with starch-protein interfacial disintegration and opposite digestive outcomes, characterized by increased starch digestibility and decreased protein digestibility, have not been systematically investigated. These findings, derived from our systematic experimental data, prompted us to investigate how such divergent macromolecular changes may jointly contribute to disruption of the starch-protein interface and be associated with subsequent digestive and physiological outcomes. Building on this structural interpretation, we further developed predictive models based on physicochemical traits to quantitatively assess nutrient digestibility and provide a theoretical framework for the targeted utilization of aged wheat.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

The study utilized wheat samples consisting of first-grade grains harvested in 2022 from Xinxiang, Henan Province, China. These included both fresh wheat and samples subjected to accelerated aging. For the animal trial, soybean meal and a 5% broiler compound premix were purchased from Henan Yiwan Zhongyuan Feed Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China). Enzymes for the in vitro digestion model and component analysis, including pepsin (30,000 U/g, S10027–25 g), trypsin (250 U/g, S10032–25 g), glucoamylase (100,000 U/g, S10017–250 g), and α-amylase (3700 U/g, S10002–250 g), were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Assay kits for α-amylase (AMS, C016–1-1), trypsin (A080–2-2), lipase (LPS, A054–2-1), total protein (TP, A045–2-2), superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001–1), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, A005–1-2), malondialdehyde (MDA, A003–1-1), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015–1-2), and catalase (CAT, A007–2-1) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Bovine serum albumin (≥98%, Cat. No. A8012, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used as the protein standard. Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 was obtained from Shanghai Lanji Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for protein staining, and bile salts were procured from Huhui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for in vitro digestion simulation. All other chemical reagents, such as hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, sodium hydroxide, petroleum ether, absolute ethanol, 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), glucose, Fehling's reagent, phenolphthalein, urea, Ellman's reagent, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), rhodamine B, and titanium dioxide (TiO₂), were of analytical grade.

Accelerated aging model and sample preparation

An accelerated aging model was adopted from previously reported accelerated-aging studies (Tian et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Briefly, wheat samples (100 g) were placed in breathable nylon mesh bags and incubated at 40 °C and 75 ± 5% relative humidity for 42 days. Samples were collected at 0, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 days, then ground into whole wheat flour, sealed, and stored at −20 °C until analysis. For comparison, an ambient-storage reference group was prepared by storing a portion of the same batch in sealed ziplock bags under laboratory ambient conditions (10–25 °C, 30 ± 5% RH) to represent naturally occurring storage conditions. The aerobic plate count (APC) was determined according to ISO 4833-1:2013, with incubation at 30 °C for 72 h before enumeration. All results complied with the specified hygiene standards.

The basic nutritional composition was analyzed as follows. Total starch content was determined following AOAC 996.11. Samples were enzymatically hydrolyzed with α-amylase and amyloglucosidase, and the released glucose was quantified using the GOPOD method. Starch content was calculated by multiplying the glucose content by a conversion factor of 0.9 (expressed on a dry-basis percentage). Reducing sugars and non-reducing sugars were determined according to the Lane-Eynon constant-volume titration method described in GB 5513–2019 (Chinese national standard for the determination of reducing sugars and non-reducing sugars in grains and oils). Briefly, reducing sugars were measured directly by titration, whereas total sugars were determined after acid hydrolysis of the sample extracts followed by the same titration procedure. The non-reducing sugar content was calculated as the difference between total sugar and reducing sugar contents. Crude protein was determined according to ISO 20483:2013 using the Kjeldahl method, with a nitrogen conversion factor of 5.70 (dry basis). Crude fat content was determined following ISO 659:2009 by Soxhlet extraction with anhydrous petroleum ether for 6 h. Fatty acid value was assayed according to AACC 02 − 01 A by titration following extraction with anhydrous ethanol, using a KOH ethanol solution as the titrant.

Starch and protein structure determination

The physicochemical properties of the samples were characterized using a suite of instrumental techniques. The short-range ordered structure of starch was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy following Wang et al. (2023), with minor modifications, using a Spectrum TWO spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with 32 scans per spectrum and three replicate measurements per sample. Data processing was performed using OMNIC software with automatic baseline correction; the region 1200–800 cm−1 was deconvoluted using a half-width of 40 cm−1 and an enhancement factor of 1.9. The crystalline structure and relative crystallinity of starch were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) following Ni et al. (2022), with minor modifications. Dried wheat flour samples were analyzed without additional moisture equilibration. XRD measurements were performed using a Miniflex 600 diffractometer (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm) under the following conditions: tube voltage of 40 kV, current of 100 mA, step-scan mode with a step size of 0.02°, scan speed of 6°/min, and a 2θ range of 4°–40°. Relative crystallinity was calculated as the ratio of the crystalline peak area to the total diffraction area using MDI Jade 6.0 software. The thermal properties of wheat flour were determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) following Wang et al. (2010), with minor modifications. Exactly 2.5 mg of wheat flour on a dry basis was placed in a calibrated standard aluminum pan, and deionized water was added at a sample-to-water ratio of 1:3 (w/w). The pan was sealed and equilibrated at room temperature for 12 h before analysis. Differential scanning calorimetry was then performed using a TA-Q20 instrument (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) from 20 °C to 80 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min. An empty aluminum pan was used as the reference, and each sample was measured in triplicate. The microstructure of the wheat endosperm was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) following Faltermaier et al. (2015), with minor modifications, using a Quanta 250 FEG microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). For fluorescence microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), the samples were stained with FITC and rhodamine B according to Liu et al. (2025), with minor modifications. A 10.0 ± 0.2 mg portion of wheat flour from each aging treatment was placed in a 2.0 mL sterile centrifuge tube, mixed with 20 μL of fluorescent-labeling working solution containing fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, 20 μg/mL) and rhodamine B (25 μg/mL), and incubated in the dark for 10 min. After incubation, the sample was washed three times with 10 mmol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2) to remove excess dye. A small portion of the stained sample was spread onto a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. For conventional fluorescence microscopy, images were acquired using a fluorescence inverted microscope (Revolve FL, Echo Laboratories, San Diego, CA, USA), with FITC (excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm) staining starch green and rhodamine B (excitation 546 nm, emission 570 nm) staining protein red. For CLSM, images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS LSM 900, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 40× air objective (NA = 0.95), with the same excitation and emission settings for FITC and rhodamine B. Intermolecular forces, including ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions, were evaluated according to Iwaki et al. (2023) using four extraction solutions: 0.05 mol/L NaCl (F1), 0.6 mol/L NaCl (F2), 0.6 mol/L NaCl containing 1.5 mol/L urea (F3), and 0.6 mol/L NaCl containing 8 mol/L urea (F4). The differences in soluble protein content between successive extraction solutions were used to estimate the specific interactions, with F2 − F1 representing ionic bonds, F3 − F2 representing hydrogen bonds, and F4 − F3 representing hydrophobic interactions. For analysis of wheat gluten secondary structure, gluten was extracted from wheat flour samples subjected to different aging times by hand-washing with distilled water, freeze-dried, and ground to pass through a 100-mesh sieve. The secondary structure of gluten proteins was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy according to Fevzioglu et al. (2020), with minor modifications. The scanning wavenumber range was set at 4000–400 cm−1 with an optical resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 cumulative scans. The dynamic calibration accuracy of the interferometer was 0.01 cm−1. The amide I region of the FTIR spectra was deconvoluted and curve-fitted using PeakFit software to quantify the relative contents of the different protein secondary structures. Free and total sulfhydryl group contents were determined according to Wang et al. (2021), with minor modifications, using Ellman's reagent. Total and free sulfhydryl group contents were determined in the presence and absence of urea, respectively, and the disulfide bond content was calculated from their difference. The starch, glutenin, and gliadin fractions were characterized by UV spectroscopy with reference to He et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2022), with minor modifications. Their UV absorbance spectra were recorded from 250 to 330 nm in four extraction media (S1–S4). The overall spectral profiles and relative changes in absorbance intensity within this wavelength range were used for comparison among the samples. The four extraction media were prepared as follows: S1, 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer; S2, 8 mol/L urea prepared in 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer; S3, 0.05 mol/L sodium chloride prepared in 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer; and S4, 1.5 g/100 mL sodium dodecyl sulfate prepared in 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer. Each fraction (0.1 g) was incubated in the corresponding extraction medium for 3 h. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 15 min, and the UV absorbance spectra of the supernatants were recorded from 250 to 330 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-7500, Lengguang Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Analysis of water status andmacroscopic hydration behavior

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was used to analyze the wheat samples following a previously reported method for characterizing water mobility and distribution in wheat kernels (Liu et al., 2023) using a MesoMR23-060H-I medium-sized NMR analysis and imaging system (Niumag, Suzhou, China). The determination parameters were set as follows: preamp regulate gain, 2; time wait, 2000 ms; sampling number, 64; echo time, 0.25 ms; and number of echoes, 1500. The CONTIN algorithm was used to perform the inverse Laplace transform to convert the transverse relaxation curve into a continuous distribution of T2 relaxation times.

Determination of rehydration kinetics

Thirty kernels each of fresh wheat and wheat subjected to accelerated aging for 42 days were taken. The initial total mass on a wet basis (m₀) was recorded. At the same time, a separate set of parallel samples was used to determine the initial moisture content on a dry basis (M₀). The samples were placed in a constant temperature water bath at 25 ± 0.5 °C and soaked in excess deionized water. At time points of 0, 10, 20, 30, 60, 120, 180, 240, 300, and 360 min, the kernels were taken out, and their surface water was quickly blotted dry with absorbent paper. In vitro digestion experiment

Static and dynamic in vitro digestion models were established. The static digestion procedure was adapted from the standardized INFOGEST protocol (Brodkorb et al., 2019), with enzyme dosages adjusted to the wheat flour sample load and analytical requirements of this study. The final enzyme activities were: α-amylase 2.50 U/mL (oral phase), pepsin 250 U/mL (gastric phase), trypsin 1.56 U/mL (intestinal phase), and glucoamylase 50.00 U/mL (intestinal phase). Dynamic digestion was conducted in a bioreactor with automated sampling to simulate the continuous secretion of gastrointestinal fluids.

Establishment of the in vitro static gastrointestinal digestion model

Oral Digestion: Two grams of wheat flour were dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water, mixed with 10 mL of oral digestion fluid, and incubated in a 37 °C constant-temperature water bath shaker at 60 rpm for 5 min.

Gastric Digestion: The pH of the oral digest was adjusted to 1.3 ± 0.2 using 6 M HCl. Then, 10 mL of gastric digestion fluid was added, and the mixture was digested at 37 °C in a water bath shaker at 100 rpm for 120 min. Aliquots were collected at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min for analysis.

Intestinal Digestion: The gastric digest was adjusted to pH 6.8 ± 0.2 by dropwise addition of 1 M Na₂CO₃, followed by addition of 10 mL of intestinal digestion fluid. Digestion continued at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm for 180 min, with aliquots taken at 0, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min.

For all digestion stages, enzyme-free controls were included. All collected samples were immediately centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used for subsequent analyses.

Establishment of the in vitro dynamic gastrointestinal digestion model

The dynamic digestion experiment was performed using a T&J-AType 10 L bioreactor (T&J Bio-engineering (Shanghai) Co., LTD, China), which enables continuous secretion of digestive fluids to simulate the dynamic process and temporal regulation of gastrointestinal digestion.

Oral Digestion: Two grams of wheat flour were dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water. The mixture was digested for 5 min at 37 °C in a constant-temperature water bath shaker (60 rpm) while being continuously infused with oral digestion fluid at a steady rate of 2 mL/min.

Gastric Digestion: The pH of the oral digest was adjusted to 1.3 ± 0.2 using 6 M HCl. Gastric digestion was then performed for 120 min at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. During this phase, gastric digestion fluid was continuously infused at a steady rate of 0.084 mL/min. Aliquots were collected at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min for analysis.

Intestinal Digestion: The pH of the gastric digest was adjusted to 6.8 ± 0.2 by the dropwise addition of 1 M Na₂CO₃. Subsequently, intestinal digestion fluid was continuously infused at a steady rate of 0.042 mL/min. Digestion continued at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm for 240 min, with aliquots taken at 0, 60, 120, 180, and 240 min.

Enzyme-free controls were included for all digestion stages. All collected samples were immediately centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to immediate analysis.

Determination of digestibility parameters

The contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) were determined according to a previously reported method (Bi et al., 2017). For starch digestibility analysis, 50 μL of the supernatant collected at each designated time point was immediately mixed with 950 μL of absolute ethanol to terminate enzymatic hydrolysis. The reducing sugar content was then determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm. For soluble-protein analysis, 100 μL of the supernatant collected at each designated time point was immediately mixed with 5 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue reagent, vortexed, allowed to stand for 2 min, and measured at 595 nm using bovine serum albumin to prepare the standard curve. Residues collected at the endpoint of static digestion were dried at 40 °C, and the residual protein content was determined by the Kjeldahl method for calculation of protein digestibility.

Animal feeding experiment

Animal ethics statement

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Henan University of Technology (Approval No. HAUTETHI-2024-1010; approval date: 10 October 2024). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Chinese national standard Laboratory animal—Guideline for ethical review of animal welfare (GB/T 35892–2018) and the institutional guidelines of Henan University of Technology. At each sampling point, the selected broilers were electrically stunned until loss of consciousness and subsequently slaughtered by exsanguination through severing the major cervical blood vessels. Death was confirmed before tissue collection. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Animal experimental protocol

A total of 360 one-day-old male white-feather broilers were randomly allocated to five dietary treatment groups, with six replicate cages per treatment and 12 birds per cage. The environmental temperature was maintained at 33 ± 2 °C during days 1–21 and was gradually reduced to 27 ± 2 °C during days 22–42, while the relative humidity was maintained at 55–65%. Natural daylight was supplemented with artificial lighting at night. The feeding trial lasted for 42 days and consisted of a starter phase from days 1 to 21 and a grower phase from days 22 to 42. The complete ingredient composition and calculated nutrient levels of the experimental diets are presented in Table S1.

Growth performance measurement

The body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (ADFI/ADG, F/G) of broilers in each group were measured at 21 and 42 days of age, covering both feeding phases. All performance parameters were recorded, and daily mortality was monitored.

Sample collection and processing

On days 21 and 42, one broiler per replicate with a body weight closest to the group average was selected. Approximately 5 mL of blood was collected from the jugular vein into a plain tube. After standing at 4 °C, the blood was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. The serum was aliquoted into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at −20 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis. Immediately after blood collection, the broilers were electrically stunned until loss of consciousness and subsequently slaughtered by severing the major cervical blood vessels for exsanguination. Death was confirmed before tissue collection, after which the liver, spleen, bursa of Fabricius, intact pectoral and leg muscles, small intestine, and cecal tissues were immediately collected. Segments of the small intestine were sampled according to the following anatomical landmarks: duodenum (2 cm posterior to the pylorus), jejunum (at the midpoint of the yolk-sac diverticulum), and ileum (5 cm anterior to the ileocecal junction). All collected tissues were temporarily stored in a 4 °C icebox and processed promptly according to subsequent assay requirements: pectoral and leg muscle samples for meat quality analysis were packaged separately; approximately 2 cm segments of each intestinal section for morphological analysis were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution; the remaining tissue samples and digesta collected from each intestinal segment were stored at −80 °C for further use.

Slaughter performance and immune organ index determination

For each sampled bird, live body weight (BW), carcass weight, eviscerated weight, breast muscle weight, leg muscle weight, and abdominal fat weight were recorded. These data were used to calculate dressing percentage, eviscerated yield, breast muscle yield, leg muscle yield, and abdominal fat percentage. The liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were collected, trimmed of adherent fat, blotted dry, and weighed precisely. The relative weight of each immune organ (expressed as a percentage of live BW) was determined to obtain the hepatic, splenic, and bursal indices.

Intestinal morphology and digestive enzyme activity analysis

Intestinal morphological analysis was performed according to a previously reported method (Chang et al., 2024). Briefly, the collected tissue samples from the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Prepared sections were examined under an optical microscope (40× objective). For each sample, five intact villi were randomly selected, and their villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using image J software to calculate the VH/CD ratio. Separately, digesta samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice, and the activities of trypsin, amylase, and lipase were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of nutrient utilization rate

Apparent nutrient digestibility was determined via the exogenous indicator method using titanium dioxide (TiO₂). Dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), crude fat (EE), crude fiber (CF), and crude ash contents in diets were analyzed according to AOAC (2005) methods. Fecal digesta were collected on days 40–42; plastic sheets were placed over the litter in each pen to avoid contamination. Excreta from each pen were cleared of feathers and foreign matter, weighed, homogenized, dried, and ground for analysis. Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was carried out using SAS software (v. 9.2), with Fisher's LSD test for post-hoc comparisons to determine significant differences among groups (P < 0.05). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted in SIMCA (v. 14.1). Variables were screened based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values; their correlations with target indicators (e.g., digestibility and growth performance) were then evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. A multiple linear regression model was subsequently built from the key variables, with the goodness-of-fit assessed by the coefficient of determination (R2). All figures were prepared with Origin 2024.

Results and discussion

Structural evolution of starch and protein macromolecules during wheat aging

Systematic changes in kernel physicochemical properties

Aging induced systematic alterations in wheat kernel composition and functionality. The test weight decreased significantly from 837 g/L to 712 g/L during aging (Fig. 1A). Gluten water absorption decreased continuously with aging time, falling below 180% by day 35 (Fig. 1A), which may be associated with storage-induced changes in protein composition (Bu et al., 2023). Endosperm hardness decreased from 102.01 N to 80.85 N (Fig. 1B), demonstrating a weakening of its internal cohesive forces. The mean geometric particle size of the flour after milling decreased, which may be associated with changes in the starch-protein matrix during aging (Fig. 1B). With respect to hydration properties (Fig. 1C), water absorption remained relatively stable during the first 21 days of accelerated aging and subsequently decreased. The values at days 35 and 42 were significantly lower than those at days 0–21, whereas day 28 represented an intermediate stage (P < 0.05). Water absorption reached 84.97% at day 42, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 13.61% relative to day 0. Meanwhile, the wetting time was markedly shortened to 15 s at day 42, indicating faster wetting, which may be related to aging-induced structural changes in the starch-protein matrix (Ren et al., 2023). These collective changes confirm that significant structural modifications occurred in starch and protein macromolecules during aging.

In terms of chemical composition, the total starch content decreased from 60.46% to 54.93%, while the reducing sugar content accumulated to 1.03% (Fig. 1D), confirming starch hydrolysis (Lekjing & Venkatachalam, 2020). The amylose ratio increased from 18.07% to 22.28% (Fig. 1E), indicating an alteration in starch molecular composition (Chi et al., 2023). The total crude protein content remained stable, but the soluble protein content was significantly reduced from 7.19% to 4.79% (Fig. 1F), suggesting protein aggregation. The total free amino acid content increased, accompanied by a relative decrease in polar amino acids (e.g., glutamic acid) and an increase in non-polar amino acids (e.g., alanine) (Fig. 1G). As shown in Fig. 1H, the crude fat content was significantly reduced to 1.5% after 42 days of aging, while the fatty acid value rose to 73.35 mg/100 g, indicating the combined effects of lipid oxidation and hydrolysis during aging. Furthermore, the conductivity of the grain soaking solution increased sharply from 34.91 μS·cm−1·g−1 to 78.08 μS·cm−1·g−1 (Fig. 1I), which may reflect impaired cell membrane integrity during aging (Bevilacqua et al., 2021).

Structural changes and binding state of starch and protein macromolecules

Significant alterations were observed in the molecular and supramolecular structure of starch, reflecting a divergent evolution across different structural scales. The peak intensity ratio of 1047/1022 cm−1 in the FTIR spectra increased (Fig. 2A, Table S2), indicating an enhancement in short-range structural rearrangement (Pourfarzad et al., 2021). This reflects the localized re-association of starch chains into double-helical clusters, facilitated by the increased mobility of water. Conversely, XRD analysis revealed a decrease in relative crystallinity from 34.27% to 30.34% (Fig. 2B), suggesting long-range disordering (Pourfarzad et al., 2021). This divergence may reflect ‘localized ordering within global disorder,’ whereby double-helical structures form locally without being fully organized into long-range crystalline domains (Wang & Hedley, 2001), potentially owing to spatial constraints accompanying contraction of the surrounding protein matrix. According to the DSC results shown in Fig. 2C and Table S3, the onset gelatinization temperature showed an overall decrease during aging and was significantly lower at days 21–42 than at day 0. This pattern was consistent with the decrease in relative crystallinity and may indicate reduced crystalline integrity and thermal stability (Liu et al., 2024). Gelatinization enthalpy varied numerically from 7.15 to 8.24 J/g, but no significant differences were observed among the aging times (P > 0.05).

At the level of molecular interactions, long-term aging led to a significant reduction in ionic bonds, a significant increase in hydrogen bonds, and a marked enhancement of hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2D). These changes suggest that weakened electrostatic interactions, increased hydrogen bonding, and enhanced hydrophobic association contributed to protein aggregation and densification of the protein matrix. FTIR analysis (Fig. 2E and Table S4) further revealed significant rearrangements in protein secondary structure. After 42 days of aging, the β-sheet and α-helix contents decreased to 26.07% and 22.76%, respectively, whereas the β-turn content increased to 20.83%. Meanwhile, the random-coil structure, which was not detected during the early aging stages, became evident after 35 days and reached 30.38% at day 42.

Together, these changes indicated destabilization of the native protein conformation and were consistent with protein unfolding and aggregation during aging. The reduction in ionic interactions, together with the increases in hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, may have promoted protein aggregation and contributed to densification of the protein matrix. Unlike β-sheet-enriched ordered aggregation, such as amyloid fibril formation, the protein aggregation observed in this system was primarily associated with disulfide cross-linking and hydrophobic interactions and was therefore more consistent with predominantly amorphous aggregation (Yang et al., 2015). In such aggregates, the newly formed secondary structures were dominated by β-turn and random-coil conformations rather than ordered β-sheets. This interpretation is consistent with previous observations of decreased β-sheet content accompanied by disulfide-mediated protein cross-linking during oxidative aggregation (H. Zhang et al., 2020).

Changes in the protein redox state provided further evidence of oxidative cross-linking. As shown in Fig. 2F, the free sulfhydryl content decreased significantly to 3.25 μmol/g, whereas the disulfide bond content increased significantly to 5.47 μmol/g. The concurrent decrease in free sulfhydryl groups and increase in disulfide bonds indicated enhanced oxidative cross-linking of proteins (H. Zhang et al., 2020).

These opposite structural changes, namely starch disordering and protein aggregation, were accompanied by disintegration of the starch protein interface. SEM images (Fig. 2G) visually demonstrated that after aging, starch granules were exposed from the continuous protein matrix, displaying surface indentations and increased interparticle distances. Fluorescence microscopy observations (Fig. 2H) further revealed that starch granules, initially encapsulated by proteins, became substantially exposed after aging, and the degree of starch-protein aggregation was markedly reduced, indicating weakened interfacial adhesion.

To quantify the effect of aging on the spatial distribution of starch and protein, CLSM images were acquired. Colocalization analysis was performed using the Color 2 plugin in FIJI. Background correction was applied with the Costes auto threshold method. The no threshold Pearson correlation coefficient (R) and the thresholded Manders overlap coefficients (tM1 for protein over starch, tM2 for starch over protein) were calculated. Three independent samples were analyzed per group. All images were acquired with identical settings, and the saturated pixel ratio was below 0.1% in all images.

Raw CLSM images (Fig. 3) showed that in fresh wheat, the green (starch) and red (protein) signals overlapped extensively, forming dense yellow areas. This indicated that starch granules were completely encapsulated by the protein network. After aging, the green and red signals became clearly separated, and the yellow areas were greatly reduced. Many independent green spots (exposed starch granules) and red patches (aggregated protein networks) appeared, and the interfacial structure tended to become fragmented.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images and fluorescence colocalization scatter plots of fresh and aged wheat kernels.

Representative CLSM scatterplots(Fig. 3) further quantified the spatial relationship. In fresh wheat, the scatter plot showed a dense distribution of points along the theoretical 1:1 colocalization diagonal. This indicated a moderate positive correlation between starch and protein fluorescence intensities, with a high degree of spatial overlap and a tight, continuous interface. After aging, the points deviated significantly from the diagonal, with a large number of points enriched toward the horizontal and vertical axes, indicating a marked loss of synchronization between the two channel intensities (Zinchuk & Zinchuk, 2008).

The quantitative results are summarized in Table 1. The Pearson correlation coefficient R was used to evaluate the overall spatial correlation of the two fluorescence signals. For fresh wheat, the R value was 0.59 ± 0.07, indicating a moderate positive correlation and high spatial overlap. For aged wheat, the R value decreased to 0.42 ± 0.02, a 28.8% drop (p = 0.016), indicating a significant reduction in overall colocalization. The Manders tM2 coefficient directly reflects the proportion of starch signal that overlaps with protein. This value decreased from 0.92 ± 0.05 in fresh wheat to 0.76 ± 0.04 in aged wheat (p = 0.009), representing a 17.2% reduction and indicating decreased spatial association between starch granules and the protein matrix. The Manders tM1 coefficient reflects the proportion of protein signal that overlaps with starch. This value decreased from 0.87 ± 0.04 to 0.80 ± 0.06 (p = 0.189). Although a decreasing trend was observed, it did not reach statistical significance, which may be related to the nonuniform contraction of the protein network after aging.

These results showed that the spatial colocalization between starch and protein was significantly reduced during wheat aging, suggesting deterioration of the starch-protein interface and weakened spatial association between the two components. This interfacial disintegration provides a mechanistic link between the observed macromolecular structural changes and the deterioration of endosperm functionality. The loss of continuity at the starch-protein interface may contribute to the observed decrease in gluten water absorption, while increased exposure of hydrophobic groups on aggregated proteins and exposed starch granules may partly explain the shortened wetting time. These changes reflect a breakdown of the structural integrity of the composite system at the interface level, consistent with the loosened endosperm structure and reduced gluten performance described earlier.

Differentiation in solubility behavior of components reveals complexity of network reorganization

By comparing the UV absorbance responses of different components under selected extraction conditions, component-specific effects of aging were identified. For glutenin and total gluten proteins, opposite changes were observed under the S2 condition, whereas the overall absorbance profiles of both fractions increased under the S4 condition (Fig. 4). These extraction-dependent spectral responses suggest that aging altered the solubility and association states of gluten protein fractions under different chemical environments. When considered together with the enhanced hydrophobic interactions, secondary-structure rearrangement, and increased disulfide bond content described above, these changes were consistent with altered protein association during aging.

Gliadin exhibited a distinct extraction-dependent response (Fig. 4B, E), with increased absorbance under the S1 condition but decreased absorbance under the S4 condition after aging. These contrasting changes suggest that aging altered the extractability or association state of gliadin under different chemical environments. The increased absorbance under the mild extraction condition may reflect greater release of UV-absorbing gliadin components, whereas the lower response under the strong denaturing condition may be associated with reduced extractability or changes in protein association during extraction. Overall, the distinct responses of gliadin indicate that its chemical-environment-dependent behavior differed from that of glutenin and total gluten proteins during aging.

Compared with the protein fractions, starch exhibited relatively weak UV absorbance, which may primarily reflect trace surface-associated or co-extracted components, including proteins and lipids. The absorbance of aged starch increased under both the buffer and sodium dodecyl sulfate extraction conditions (Fig. 4C, F), indicating that aging altered the extraction-dependent spectral response of these associated components. When considered together with the SEM observations and the changes in starch molecular organization and relative crystallinity, these results were consistent with aging-related alterations in the starch-granule environment. Such changes may also be associated with the reduced spatial association observed between starch granules and the surrounding protein matrix.

Aging-induced reconfiguration of water distribution in starch and protein macromolecules and its impact on macroscopic hydration behavior

LF-NMR relaxation spectroscopy was used to distinguish water populations with different mobility and binding characteristics in wheat kernels. Based on differences in relaxation time (T₂), water was divided into three fractions: strongly bound water (T₂ < 1 ms), weakly bound water (1 ms < T₂ < 100 ms), and free water (T₂ > 100 ms) (Chen et al., 2022). Compared with fresh wheat, the proportion of strongly bound water decreased significantly from 6.07% to 4.45% in aged wheat (Fig. 4G). In contrast, the proportion of free water increased from 48.57% to 50.50%, whereas the proportion of weakly bound water remained largely unchanged (45.36% vs. 45.05%).

These changes indicate a redistribution of water from strongly bound states toward more mobile states during wheat aging (Y. Liu et al., 2023), which may be associated with alterations in the starch-protein interfacial environment. The shift from strongly bound toward more mobile water states may indicate partial disruption of the protein hydration shell. Such a change may reduce steric hindrance and increase the accessibility of internal reactive sites (Nawrocka et al., 2017). This interpretation was also consistent with the continuous decline in gluten water absorption (Fig. 1A), as well as the increases in protein hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bond content (Fig. 2D, F).

Consistent trends across multiple lines of evidence suggest that dehydration may have contributed to hydrophobic association and oxidative cross-linking of protein macromolecules and, consequently, to the formation of dense aggregates. These structural changes may have reduced the accessibility of hydrophilic binding sites and promoted contraction of the protein network, resulting in the release of tightly bound water from the hydration shell (Xu et al., 2024). This process may also have weakened the association between proteins and starch granules.

Meanwhile, the increase in the proportion of free water occurred alongside reduced spatial association between starch granules and the protein matrix and enlarged interfacial voids observed in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. These structural changes may provide greater space for more mobile water. However, the LF-NMR results do not directly determine the spatial destination of the redistributed water. The concurrent decrease in relative crystallinity (Fig. 2B) further indicated increased structural disorder in starch.

Fig. 4H shows the rehydration kinetic curves of fresh and aged wheat during soaking at 25 °C. The moisture content of both samples increased with soaking time and gradually approached equilibrium. The experimental rehydration data for fresh and aged wheat were well fitted by the Peleg model (R2 > 0.99).

The fitted parameters of the Peleg model are presented in Table 2. After aging, the initial water absorption rate (R0 = 1/K1) decreased significantly from 3.94 × 10−3 g/g/min in fresh wheat to 3.28 × 10−3 g/g/min in aged wheat (P < 0.05). To interpret this kinetic change, the rehydration results were considered together with the LF-NMR and structural observations. The lower initial water absorption rate was consistent with the decrease in the proportion of strongly bound water and with structural changes in the protein matrix. Densification of the protein network and the associated increase in surface hydrophobicity may have increased resistance to rapid water entry, thereby contributing to the slower initial hydration of aged wheat.

Integration of the LF-NMR and rehydration kinetic results indicated coordinated changes in water-state distribution and macroscopic hydration behavior during wheat aging. The increases in electrical conductivity and fatty acid value (Fig. 1H, I) were consistent with deterioration of cellular integrity, which may have facilitated water redistribution. Contraction of the protein macromolecular network may have contributed to the decrease in strongly bound water and the corresponding increase in more mobile water states, while protein-network densification may have increased resistance to water entry. Meanwhile, the larger internal space available for water storage may account for the relatively stable equilibrium moisture content. Overall, these concurrent changes suggest an association between protein-network contraction, altered water distribution, and deterioration of the starch-protein interface.

In summary, wheat aging was accompanied by divergent but coordinated structural changes in starch and protein, together with altered water distribution. Starch exhibited reduced molecular ordering and relative crystallinity together with an increased amylose proportion, whereas proteins showed enhanced hydrophobic interactions and oxidative cross-linking, secondary-structure rearrangement, reduced solubility, and aggregation. LF-NMR and rehydration analyses further indicated a redistribution of water from strongly bound toward more mobile states and a reduction in the initial water absorption rate. Together, these concurrent changes were associated with reduced starch-protein spatial association, deterioration of the interfacial structure, and declining wheat processing functionality.

Differentiated regulatory mechanisms of multiscale structural evolution on in vitro digestibility

The multiscale structural changes induced by aging were associated with contrasting effects on the in vitro digestion of starch and protein macromolecules, with deterioration of starch-protein interfacial organization likely representing an important contributing factor to these contrasting digestive responses.

The aging process significantly enhanced the in vitro digestibility of starch. As shown in Fig. 5A, a notable redistribution of starch fractions was observed: RDS and RS decreased to 20.37% and 11.33%, respectively, whereas SDS increased significantly to 68.30% (P < 0.05). In the static digestion model, the starch digestion rate of aged wheat was 11.35% higher than that of the fresh sample (P < 0.05). This enhancement was even more pronounced in the dynamic model, with a marked increase in digestion rate during the gastric phase (Fig. 5B, C). During the intestinal phase, rapid hydrolysis occurred in the early stage (0–120 min), followed by gradual saturation kinetics in the later stage (120–180 min) (Fig. 5D, E).

The improved starch digestibility resulted from starch component rearrangement during aging: cleavage of amylopectin side chains increased the amylose proportion, and molecular rearrangement induced local structural changes in starch (Tu et al., 2021; Y. Zhang et al., 2019). Meanwhile, the starch-protein interface underwent disintegration, leading to disruption of the protein coating that originally encapsulated starch granules and loss of its barrier effect. Consequently, the structurally loosened starch granules became directly accessible to digestive enzymes, greatly enhancing enzyme accessibility (Tu et al., 2021).

In the static model, protein solubility in aged wheat decreased significantly to 11.56% (P < 0.05), and the digestion rate fell markedly to 30.39% (P < 0.05). A similar but slower digestion trend was observed in the dynamic model, where the release of soluble proteins was approximately 10% lower than in the static model. The gastric digestion profile showed an initial lag, followed by a more rapid increase and subsequent stabilization.

Deterioration of the starch-protein interface may reduce the spatial confinement of the protein component and increase the exposure of pre-existing protein aggregates. The increased disulfide bond content measured in aged wheat before digestion was consistent with enhanced oxidative cross-linking during aging. This pre-existing cross-linking may have contributed to the formation of compact protein aggregates and reduced the accessibility of proteolytic cleavage sites, thereby contributing to the lower protein digestibility of aged wheat (P. Wang et al., 2024). The observed gastric digestion profile was also consistent with greater resistance of these cross-linked protein structures to proteolytic disassembly (Luz et al., 2024).

In summary, the starch-protein interface acts as a critical structural hub during wheat aging. The disintegration of this interface exerts a dual regulatory effect: on one hand, it removes the proteinaceous physical barrier surrounding starch granules, thereby increasing their susceptibility to enzymatic hydrolysis; on the other hand, the disruption of this interface deprives proteins of their original spatial anchoring and micro-aqueous environment, facilitating the formation of dense, enzyme-resistant aggregates driven by oxidative cross-linking. Consequently, the divergent digestive behavior observed in aged wheat is a direct manifestation of the breakdown of the starch-protein interfacial equilibrium.

Structural-mediated reduction in digestibility suppresses physiological metabolism and production performance in broilers

The altered digestive characteristics associated with deterioration of the starch-protein interface likely influenced nutrient utilization and growth performance in broilers. Growth performance showed an overall decline with increasing replacement of fresh wheat by aged wheat (Table 3). Body weight at 42 days decreased from 1.87 kg in the control group to 1.69, 1.48, 1.48, and 1.33 kg in the 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% replacement groups, respectively. Similarly, average daily gain during days 0–21 decreased from 26.0 to 22.0, 19.6, 18.7, and 17.8 g, while breast muscle yield decreased from 23.8% to 21.8%, 21.6%, 19.4%, and 17.1%. Feed conversion ratio and some slaughter indices varied among treatments but did not exhibit a strictly monotonic pattern.

Changes in meat quality parameters were also observed across the dietary treatments. Breast-muscle shear force decreased significantly (P = 0.004), whereas the change in leg-muscle shear force was not significant (P = 0.060). Meat color was characterized by lower a* values (P = 0.005) and higher b* values (P < 0.001). Alterations in immune-organ indices revealed a typical “growth-retardation effect” (Table 3). At 21 and 42 d of age, broilers fed aged wheat exhibited an increasing trend in the relative weights of the liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius. For example, the relative bursa weight at 42 d rose from 0.10% in the control group to 0.22%. This trend was not interpreted as enhanced immunity; rather, it resulted from the relatively smaller decline in the absolute mass of immune organs under conditions of severe overall growth retardation (a 28.9% decrease in 42-day BW), leading to a passive increase in their proportion of BW.

Gut morphometric data are presented in Table S5. During both the starter (days 1–21) and grower (days 22–42) phases, the absolute length and weight of the intestinal segments decreased, whereas their relative lengths increased in association with the lower body weight. For example, relative duodenal length increased from 36.5 to 46.1 cm/kg, and relative ileal length increased from 60.5 to 89.4 cm/kg.

This asynchronous pattern indicated altered intestinal growth in broilers receiving aged-wheat diets. The higher relative values likely reflected the greater reduction in body weight rather than enhanced intestinal growth. These morphometric changes were accompanied by lower overall growth performance (Y. Liu et al., 2026; Goo et al., 2023).

Digestive function and nutrient-utilization data directly explained the observed growth inhibition (Table 4, Table 5). With increasing dietary inclusion of aged wheat, duodenal trypsin activity decreased significantly from 146 U/g prot to 105 U/g prot. Concurrently, a comprehensive decline in nutrient metabolic rates was observed: the apparent metabolic rate of crude protein dropped from 88.4% to 79.5%, total phosphorus utilization fell markedly from 52.1% to 27.0%, and apparent metabolizable energy declined from 12.9 MJ/kg to 12.3 MJ/kg. This in vivo reduction in nutrient utilization was consistent with the digestive imbalance associated with deterioration of the starch-protein interface.

Taken together, the present findings support an ‘interface-structure-function- performance’ conceptual framework for interpreting the effects of aged wheat under the experimental conditions investigated. Within this framework, deterioration of starch-protein interfacial organization was associated with contrasting in vitro digestive responses, characterized by enhanced starch hydrolysis and reduced protein digestibility. At the animal level, dietary inclusion of aged wheat was accompanied by altered intestinal growth indices, changes in selected digestive enzyme activities and nutrient utilization, and lower growth performance in broilers. By positioning the starch–protein interface as a key organizational level linking macromolecular restructuring, digestive function, and animal performance, this framework provides an interface-centered, cross-scale interpretation of the nutritional consequences of wheat aging.

Development and application value of a rapid prediction model based on key indicators

Gluten water absorption is a key indicator for evaluating the deterioration of wheat storage quality and can reflect the characteristic stages of wheat aging. During aging, total protein content does not change significantly, whereas gluten water absorption, soluble protein content, starch content, and fat content all undergo systematic changes. Therefore, a rapid prediction model for nutrient content was constructed based on gluten water absorption, using soluble protein content as the representative indicator for protein changes (since total protein remained constant). This model enables the simultaneous estimation of starch, soluble protein, and fat content from a single measurement.

These results indicated that the models achieved acceptable internal predictive performance and showed potential for quantitative assessment in practical production applications, although external validation is still required.

Conclusions

Under the accelerated-aging conditions examined, starch and protein in wheat underwent contrasting structural changes. Starch exhibited reduced relative crystallinity and an increased amylose proportion, whereas proteins showed enhanced hydrophobic interactions and disulfide-mediated cross-linking, secondary-structure rearrangement, reduced solubility, and aggregation. These changes were accompanied by reduced starch-protein spatial association and redistribution of water from strongly bound toward more mobile states, together with contrasting digestive responses characterized by increased starch digestibility and reduced protein digestibility. In the broiler feeding trial, dietary inclusion of aged wheat was associated with reduced digestive enzyme activity, nutrient utilization, and growth performance. Together, these findings support an ‘interface–structure–function–performance’ conceptual framework in which the starch–protein interface represents a key organizational level linking macromolecular restructuring, digestive responses, and animal performance under the experimental conditions evaluated. Predictive models based on selected physicochemical indicators provided preliminary estimates of digestibility and growth-related outcomes within the present dataset. The broader applicability of the proposed conceptual framework and predictive models requires validation in independent samples and other biological systems.
