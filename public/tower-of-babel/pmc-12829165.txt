Comparative β-cell development: insights from Rodents, humans and induced pluripotent stem cell models
Authors: Radwan Darwish, Yasmine Alcibahy, Manjula Nandakumar, Alexandra E. Butler, Abu Saleh Md Moin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stepwise pancreatic β-cell differentiation protocols, designed to recapitulate key developmental milestones of pancreatic organogenesis—from definitive endoderm to mature, glucose-responsive β-cells—draw on insights from both rodent and human developmental biology. These protocols consist of three critical stages: the formation of definitive endoderm, the induction of pancreatic progenitors, and the maturation of functional β-cells. Here, we discuss human and rodent embryonic development together with stem-cell differentiation protocols in concert to identify gaps and bottlenecks that limit the practicality and scalability of current protocols. Ultimately, the aim of refining these processes is to produce functional β-cells from pluripotent stem cells to treat, and potentially cure, diabetes.

Introduction

A deep understanding of pancreatic β-cell physiology and development, particularly in the context of diabetes, is necessary for the development of robust, scalable stem cell (SC) differentiation protocols that produce functional insulin-secreting β-cells. However, most studies are limited to rodent models, particularly utilizing Mus Musculus, primarily due to limited access to human pancreatic tissue [1–5]. In patients with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM), β-cells undergo an autoimmune destructive process led by CD8 + T-cells, resulting in near-complete destruction by the time of diagnosis [6]. The onset of T1DM is indeed difficult to predict, but, with the advent Genetic Risk Scores (GRS), prediction of early disease onset and subsequent development have become possible [7–9]. Additionally, there are limitations in obtaining well preserved human pancreatic tissue to enable detailed study of human pancreatic development, particularly during the first trimester.

Many researchers have highlighted the poor translatability of supposedly novel biological findings in rodents to humans [10], and this extends to the field of β-cell biology. One fundamental difference between human and rodent β-cells is that human β-cells primarily express glucose transporter protein type 1 (GLUT1), while rodent β-cells express GLUT2, explaining why the former have a lower maximal glucokinase (GK) velocity (VMAX) and Km (Michaelis constant) [11, 12]. In pancreatic β-cells glucokinase functions as the principal glucose sensor. A higher Km allows responsiveness across physiological glycemic ranges, whereas changes in Vmax alter the maximal rate of glucose phosphorylation and thus influence the maximal insulin-secretory capacity.

Dynamics of β-cell proliferation also differ in development, adulthood, and adaptive states between rodent and human. During late human pancreatic development, β-cell proliferation was relatively low, around 0.5%. A significant but temporary increase occurred during the neonatal period. By the age of 2 years, the proliferation rate declined to about 0.5–1%, a level maintained throughout childhood. In adolescence and adulthood, β-cell proliferation became rare, remaining consistently below 0.5% [13, 14]. Studies in aged adult mice demonstrate that β-cell proliferation is an exceptionally rare event. 5-bromo-2-deoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation assays revealed average daily proliferation rates of ~ 0.07% [15], markedly lower than the ~ 3% previously reported in rats [16]. Considerable inter-animal heterogeneity was observed, with proliferation ranging from virtually absent (as few as 1 in 11,764 β-cells dividing per day) to relatively higher levels (up to 1 in 321 β-cells per day). Even when tested in genetically homogeneous BALB/c mice, replication rates remained below 1% per day, averaging ~ 0.68% in males and ~ 0.24% in females, with substantial variation across individuals. Collectively, these findings underscore that adult β-cell replication in mice is a very infrequent process, with approximately 1 in 1,400 β-cells dividing daily by one year of age [15].

During pregnancy, the β-cell fractional area increases modestly by approximately 1.4-fold, yet studies of human pancreata have not detected a corresponding rise in proliferation markers, implying that expansion occurs largely without substantial replication [17]. Similarly, in the setting of diet induced obesity, rodent β-cells demonstrate a robust expansion of β-cell mass achieved through marked increases in islet size and replication [18], however, this expansion is not matched by improved insulin secretory function, suggesting inadequate functional adaptation of the enlarged β-cell mass [19]. In humans, autopsy studies show a ~ 50% increase in β-cell mass in obese compared to lean adults (∼0.8 g vs. ∼1.2 g), with very low proliferation indices (~ 0.02% in obese individuals), suggesting very infrequent replication and remained unaffected by obesity [20].

It is therefore evident that the functional and adaptive capacities of β-cells differ significantly between rodent and human. While genetic lineage tracing showed that maintenance and adaptive increases occur primarily via self-duplication of existing β-cells [16], in humans, direct lineage tracing is not feasible. Convergent evidence from thymidine-analog labeling and radiocarbon birth-dating indicates that meaningful β-cell turnover is largely confined to early life with very low replication in adulthood [21]. Moreover, adult human studies show no robust regenerative surge after ~ 50% partial pancreatectomy [22]. Thus, this lack of a durable in vivo regenerative mechanism in humans, further underscored by key differences in their transcriptional landscapes [23], necessitates alternative therapeutic approaches.

A solution to circumvent the poor replicative capacity of adult human β-cell lies in the advent of stem cell biology, specifically the development of robust differentiation protocols that ultimately aim to produce commercial “off-the-shelf” islets ready for transplantation. Current protocols are based upon knowledge gleaned from both human and rodent β-cell biology, as briefly summarized in Fig. 1 below. Despite significant progress, challenges remain in achieving full β-cell maturation and functionality in vitro comparable to that in vivo. Recent innovations, such as three-dimensional culture systems (e.g., human islet-like organoids (HILOs)) [24] and single-cell transcriptomics [25], have provided deeper insights into β-cell heterogeneity and differentiation pathways. These advances, coupled with improved differentiation protocols and encapsulation techniques, hold great potential for clinical translation. However, issues such as immune rejection, tumorigenic risks, and the need for prolonged maturation remain to be addressed [26, 27]. Here, we provide an overview of the current state of stem cell-derived β-cell production, examining different developmental stages in the murine pancreas and its human counterparts. We discuss stage-specific in vitro recapitulation, recent advances, existing limitations, and future research directions.

Gastrulation, definitive endoderm, and pancreatic bud formation

Embryonic development of the endocrine pancreas starts with the process of gastrulation, establishing three germ layers – ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm. These events occur around embryonic day (E) 7.5–8.5 in mice [28–30], and human gestational day (hd) 22–26 in humans [31]. The primitive gut tube, which later gives rise to the ventral and dorsal pancreatic buds, is predominantly formed by the definitive endoderm (DE) [32].

In mice, at around E8, Activin-β and fibroblast growth factor (FGF2), both notochord-derived signals, inhibit the expression of sonic hedgehog (Shh), thus initiating pancreatic organogenesis [33]. At this point, the pre-pancreatic endoderm is located immediately ventral to the notochord. This enables the expression of Pdx1 (pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1), essential for pancreatic outgrowth [34–36]. As a result, the notochord is displaced from the endoderm once the dorsal aortae fuse. The pancreatic bud then emerges at E9.5, and the ventral bud at E10.5 [29, 37]. Ptf1a regulates the endodermal transition to pancreatic progenitor cells, and, in its absence, endodermal cells alternatively differentiate into intestinal epithelial cells [38, 39]. Patterning of the gut tube also relies on the secretion of retinoic acid (RA), bone morphogenic proteins (BMPs), Activins, and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs, specifically FGF10) from the lateral plate mesoderm [40, 41]. The effect of FGF10 signaling on expansion is mediated by activation of the Notch signaling pathway [42–44]. Beyond Pdx1 and Ptf1a, the expression profiles in the progenitor pool are poorly understood, though generally characterized by Hlxb9 [35, 45], Sox9 [46], Nkx6.1, Nkx6.2, and Nkx2.2 [47].

This aspect of pancreatic patterning is very similar to that in humans, though it occurs at a later stage. Folding of the foregut endoderm and formation of the anterior intestinal portal (AIP), the site of the future pancreas, begins at hd 25–27 [31], suppressing SHH in the pancreatic domain and allowing PDX1 expression by hd 29–31 [31]. Both dorsal and ventral pancreatic buds have emerged by hd 30–33, marked by SOX9, GATA4 and PDX1 expression [48], [49, 50]. Unlike mice, the pool of pancreatic progenitors is preserved for a long duration of time, allowing for expansion [50].

A standard in vitro differentiation protocol recapitulates these steps [51]. It begins with embryoid bodies (EBs) – pluripotent stem cell (SC) spheroids that mimic early embryos. EBs naturally form the three germ layers, after which definitive endoderm (DE) is induced via high Nodal/Activin signaling [52]. To mimic Nodal activity, Activin A, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, activates BMP, FGF and Wingless-related integration site (WNT) signaling pathways to promote gastrulation, while also suppressing Shh [53–55]. Once DE is established, pancreatic precursors that can potentially differentiate along pancreatic lineages to form any type of endocrine or exocrine cell, are formed. The FGF signaling pathway is especially important for this step, where FGF10, a mitogenic factor, stimulates the proliferation of pancreatic cells [56, 57]. Further, studies show that culture conditions relying on FGF10 stimulation, extended and continuous retinoic acid (RA) exposure, and a particularly low endodermal cell density yield precursors co-expressing Pdx1 and NKx6.1, both transcription factors found in mature functional β-cells [58]. At this point, FGF2 maintains the expression levels of Pdx1 [55], while KGF (keratinocyte growth factor), alternatively known as FGF7, promotes the generation of Pdx1 + precursors [59]. KGF also promotes β-cell regeneration by acting on ductal cells [60]. Finally, protein kinase C (PKC) is often used in differentiation protocols to promote β-cell differentiation [61], as it enhances the mass and proliferation rates of β-cells in vitro [62, 63].

Branching morphogenesis and progenitor segregation

After E10.5, mouse pancreatic tissue begins to expand rapidly, and forms a clearly branched structure. This process occurs through a “tip and trunk” model driven by primary multipotent pancreatic progenitor cells (MPCs) [48, 64]. Pancreatic cell fates are driven by the temporal expression of transcription factors, with tip cells expressing high levels of Sox9, Ptf1a, and Cpa1, but low levels of Pdx1 and Oc1 [65]. These differential expression patterns enable cells to differentiate into endocrine, exocrine or ductal cells. Tip cells are eventually demarcated by Ptf1a expression, such that they develop into peripheral exocrine cells. Conversely, trunk cells retain expression of Oc1, Sox9, Hnf1β and Nkx6.1, allowing them to develop into either ductal or endocrine cells [65]. Around E13.5–14.5, pre-endocrine cells activate Neurog3 expression and commit to the endocrine cell lineage [66, 67], at which time Sox9 expression is lost [68].

In humans, differences in expression profiles are not evident until hd 45–47 when trunk (central) cells attain a SOX9+ / NKX6.1+ profile, and tip (peripheral) cells a SOX9+ /GATA4+ / NKX6.1+ profile [50]. Tip cells lose NKX6.1 expression by around WD (weeks of development) 10. These differential expression patterns allow the central component to develop into the future endocrine pancreas, and the peripheral component into the future exocrine pancreas. Prenatally, however, these regions are not as well-demarcated in humans as they are in mice [69, 70]. Islet architecture in humans is refined in the postnatal period. Unlike in mice [68], SOX9 expression in humans is not lost until WD 10–14 [68], though exact timings are not known. Cells committed to an exocrine cell lineage harbor a greater capacity to proliferate prenatally, compared to their centrally located endocrine counterparts [49, 71, 72].

The differential expression patterns and temporal dynamics observed during pancreatic branching morphogenesis have directly informed the development of in vitro differentiation protocols. Modern stem cell differentiation strategies attempt to recapitulate the tip versus trunk cell dichotomy [50] by precisely modulating key transcription factors such as SOX9, PTF1A, and NKX6.1 at specific developmental timepoints [73]. The observation that human SOX9 expression persists significantly longer than in mice, extending until WD 10–14 compared to the earlier loss at E13.5-14.5 in rodents [74], has necessitated species-specific modifications in protocol design and timing. This extended SOX9 expression window in humans requires longer culture periods and adjusted growth factor exposure schedules to properly recapitulate normal developmental progression.

The temporal activation of NEUROG3 [75] around E13.5-14.5 in mice has been translated to equivalent developmental stages in human differentiation protocols, typically corresponding to day 10–14 of in vitro culture [61]. Understanding that tip cells destined for exocrine lineages possess greater proliferative capacity compared to centrally located endocrine progenitors has influenced expansion strategies and cell enrichment methods in protocol optimization. Further, the complex branched architecture observed during natural pancreatic morphogenesis has inspired the development of three-dimensional culture systems and pancreatic organoid approaches that better recapitulate the spatial organization and cell-cell interactions of the developing pancreatic tissue [76]. These insights into progenitor segregation mechanisms have also led to the development of specific methodologies for enriching distinct cell populations during directed differentiation, allowing for more efficient generation of desired pancreatic cell types for therapeutic applications.

Endocrine specification and β-cell maturation

In mice, Neurog3 is transiently expressed to allow pancreatic progenitor cells located centrally (trunks) to commit to an endocrine cell lineage [75, 77, 78]. However, we now know that progenitors expressing Neurog3 lack Pdx1. Previous evidence suggests that Pdx1 activity in essential for endocrine specification and β-cell differentiation [79], with its deficiency leading to a loss of β-cell identity and resulting in maturity onset diabetes [80].

Arx and Pax4 direct endocrine fate: Arx, first seen at E9.5 and later in glucagon/pancreatic polypeptide co-expressing cells, drives α-cell specification [81–83] while Pax4, also detected at E9.5, promotes β-cell commitment when overexpressed and, if deleted, leads to β-to-ε cell transdifferentiation [81, 84, 85]. MafA and MafB, both only expressed in hormone-positive cells, are also important. MafB expression is first detected at E10.5 in both immature glucagon and insulin expressing cells. MafB is not silenced until after term and remains elevated in presumptive α-cells [86, 87]. MafA expression is first detected later at E13.5 in insulin-expressing cells only after terminal differentiation of β-cells occurs [88–91].

Once Pdx1+ / NKx6.1+ progenitors are established, Notch signaling must be inhibited to permit the expression of NEUROG3, a master regulator of endocrine cell specification [92, 93]. DAPT (Da, Purity >99%. γ-Secretase inhibitor), a Notch inhibitor, drives the formation of NEUROG3+ cells propelled to endocrine cell specification [92]. NEUROG3 transcription is further enhanced by thyroid hormones and Activin A, which acts by inhibiting Mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 4 (SMAD4), a mediator of the TGF-β signaling pathway [94]. Retinoic acid can also be used here to suppress the alternative formation of exocrine cells [95–97]. The fidelity of this endocrine lineage is further maintained by the addition of Noggin, a BMP antagonist, which prevents hepatic differentiation [93, 98].

NEUROG3 expression increases significantly in pancreatic progenitor, while high NEUROG3 coincides with loss of SOX9, though it persists in presumptive ductal cells [49, 50, 99, 100]. NEUROG3 expression peaks approximately by the end of the first trimester, eventually reaching sub-detectable levels at WD 35 [101]. Accordingly, fetal β-cells arise centrally by differentiation from cytokeratin (CK)19-positive precursors rather than by proliferation of pre-existing hormone-positive cells, both during the first trimester [71] and during later human fetal development [102], suggesting the involvement of ductal epithelial cells in development of β-cells [49]. Co-expression of prohormone convertase (PC)1/3 and islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP; amylin) along with insulin and peripheral expression of GLUT-2 and chromogranin A (at 12 and 14 WD, respectively) implies that fetal β-cells may be capable of processing and secreting insulin [49]. The ability of fetal β-cells to proliferate is enhanced during pregnancy in the presence of prolactin and placental lactogen [103–107]. It is thought that NEUROG3 expression is switched off at around WD 26–28, as was demonstrated by Capito and colleagues by transplanting human fetal pancreas into mice [108]. During that same time, β-cell clusters, which are well-developed and vascularized by around WD 10 [49, 50], lose their replicative capacity, indicating that NEUROG3 expression is essential for replication of endocrine progenitors. This also indicates that the fetal β-cell mass expands through proliferation from pre-existing β-cells, rather than neogenesis (where apoptosis rates would be expected to be high) [74]. β-cell proliferation continues until term, with fractional β-cell area in the pancreas increasing linearly [109]. Interestingly, MAFA [110] and MAFB [111] expression patterns are slightly different in humans versus mice, with MAFB expression never diminishing in presumptive β-cells, and MAFA expression only detected postnatally [112].

The final stage involves the maturation of β-cells into glucose-responsive, insulin-secreting cells [73]. This is achieved by supplementing specified endocrine cells with factors present in the adult, functioning pancreas including betacellulin [113, 114], which enhances the production of insulin and Pdx1, and nicotinamide which, aside from its antioxidant properties [115], favors the development of cells expressing insulin, glucagon and somatostatin [116]. Exendin-4, an analogue of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) [117], is also used, acting through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-dependent pathways to enhance the functionality of hPSC-derived β-cells without increasing β-cell number [118]. However, a recent 3D hPSC-islet study found that short exendin-4 exposure (50 nM for the final 3 days of differentiation) markedly enhanced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion without increasing the proportion of NKX6.1+/insulin+ β-cells; this effect was associated with upregulation of maturation (MAFA, NeuroD1, SIX2), glucose-sensing (GCK, GLUT2) and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation genes (PGC-1α, TFAM, NDUSF1/2) and increased mitochondrial complex proteins, suggesting functional maturation via transcriptional and mitochondrial remodeling rather than by expansion of β-cell number [119].

Further, thyroid hormones interact with the MafA promoter to enhance β-cell maturation [120]. All these cellular factors act synergistically to ensure the development of functional β-cells, while preserving the β-cell mass. β-cell differentiation also relies on optimal conditions in the extracellular matrix (ECM), achieved predominantly through the addition of type IV collagen and laminin [121]. Type IV collagen promotes the secretion of insulin from islets [122, 123], while laminin upregulates the expression of transcription factors, namely Pdx1, glucagon, insulin and GLUT2 [124]. Collectively, collagen and laminin provide specific biochemical and mechanical cues that facilitate β-cell maturation. These signals must be closely evaluated, as developing cells are sensitive to subtle changes in the timing, spatial distribution and availability of these cues [125]. There remain some challenges in achieving full maturation of β-cells using such a stem cell-derived β-cell protocol. Recent evidence points to the importance of the islet microenvironment, still poorly understood, in creating β-cells identical to those seen in vivo [127]. This can be better achieved using three-dimensional culture systems, such as organoids. However, a critical limitation in current differentiation protocols lies in the dimensionality disparities between traditional 2D monolayer cultures and 3D organoid systems, which profoundly affects β-cell functionality and therapeutic potential [125]. Conventional 2D cultures produce β-like cells with immature phenotypes characterized by constitutive rather than glucose-responsive insulin secretion, disrupted cell-cell communication networks, and reduced expression of mature β-cell markers such as MAFA and GLUT2 [128, 129]. In contrast, 3D spheroid cultures better recapitulate native islet architecture, promoting enhanced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, improved cell-cell adhesion through gap junctions, and establishment of proper paracrine signaling networks between β-, α-, and δ-cells [24, 130]. The superior functionality of 3D systems stems from physiologically relevant extracellular matrix interactions [121], mechanical signaling through compressive forces that activate YAP/TAZ pathways [131], and the creation of oxygen and nutrient gradients that promote metabolic heterogeneity within β-cell populations [132]. However, 3D cultures present scalability challenges for clinical translation, including increased production complexity, batch-to-batch variability, and limited vascularization that restricts nutrient diffusion to organoid cores [133, 134], necessitating the development of hybrid microfluidic platforms that combine the controlled environment of 2D systems with the architectural benefits of 3D cultures [51]. That said, Yoshihara et al. generated human islet-like organoids (HILOs), that, upon transplantation into diabetic Non-Obese Diabetic Severe Combined Immunodeficient (NOD-SCID) mice, achieve glucose homeostasis. These HILOs are derived from iPSCs and are driven to metabolic maturation via the non-canonical WNT4 signaling pathway [24]. These models are further complemented by recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics that offer insight into the heterogeneity of β-cells and, accordingly, the different trajectories of differentiation that they follow [25].

A persistent limitation of current differentiation protocols is the immature phenotype and relatively low efficiency of iPSC-derived β-cells. This immaturity is reflected by fetal-like transcriptomes, low insulin content and weak glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, and it can limit both in vitro modelling and therapeutic potential [24, 135]. Several complementary strategies have proven effective in improving maturation and yield. Precise temporal control of signaling pathways during endocrine induction and maturation, particularly modulation of TGF-β signaling, enhances dynamic insulin secretion [135]. Engineering the three-dimensional microenvironment by using hydrogels or co-culture with endothelial and stromal cells increases β-cell marker expression and functional responsiveness [51, 130]. Reaggregation and enrichment of immature β-like cells into islet-sized clusters promotes metabolic and mitochondrial maturation and markedly improves stimulus–secretion coupling [93, 128]. Targeted metabolic cues such as WNT4 signaling and selected growth factors accelerate metabolic maturation and insulin secretory competence [24, 135]. Transient modulation of lineage and maturation transcription factors, and the use of small molecules or growth factors that mimic in vivo cues, can further increase maturity and yield [114, 115]. Finally, in vivo transplantation or implantation provides a robust route to final maturation and functional stabilization for many protocols, supporting translation to preclinical models [135, 136]. With these advances come new markers that can be exploited in updated differentiation protocols and potentially create scalable protocols that can be translated to clinical practice. Concluding remarks and future perspectives

The differentiation of stem cells into functional β-cells is guided by developmental milestones identified in both mice and humans. Protocols have been rigorously refined since their inception, though the process has been long and expensive. Clinical trials have explored pancreatic progenitor cells and islet-like organoids. The first hESC-derived pancreatic progenitor trial was hindered by a foreign body response, but device modifications enabled angiogenesis, cell survival and C-peptide production [137, 138]. Preclinical studies of Vertex’s islet-like organoids showed a 60% reduction in insulin requirements in non-human primates [137], while ongoing trials (NCT04786262, NCT02239354) aim to assess the safety and efficacy of both fully differentiated islet cells and pancreatic endoderm cells [139].

Despite this progress, safety concerns cannot be ignored. Undifferentiated or partially differentiated cells may interfere with desired functions or present tumorigenic risks [27]. To address these risks, strategies such as antibody-toxin conjugates to eliminate non-differentiated cells [140] and encapsulation devices for controlled transplantation [141] are being explored.

Still, the clinical application of human pluripotent SC-derived β-like cells faces two major challenges: generating functional, mature β-like cells in large-scale quantity and protecting them from immune rejection [142]. Enhanced differentiation protocols attempt to cluster immature β-like cells into endocrine-enriched niches [128], assemble islet-like organoids on hydrogels [51, 130], and use organ-on-a-chip platforms [134] or decellularized pancreatic scaffolds [143]. This, coupled with good manufacturing practice (GMP) guidelines, including standardized preparation protocols and quality controls to prevent off-target cell effects, is essential for advancing therapies [136]. The results have been promising. Velazco-Cruz et al., for example, demonstrated that their updated protocol generated β-like cells with better first- and second-phase insulin secretion, though these cells still showed reduced insulin secretion compared to primary human islets [135].

To date, a major problem with SC-islet transplantation has been the need for lifelong immunosuppression to prevent rejection of SC-islets with regression to a pre-transplantation diabetic state [144]. It is debatable whether the difficulties and possible complications of living with immunosuppression are worse than those associated with diabetes. However, in the first of its kind in-human phase 1 clinical trial, a type 1 diabetic 25-year-old woman underwent autologous transplantation of chemically induced pluripotent stem-cell-derived islets under the abdominal anterior rectus sheath. Results showed that she was insulin-independent 75 days post-transplantation and remained that way for the entire follow-up period of 1 year documented in the report [145]. Whilst follow up beyond 1-year post-transplantation is ongoing, longer-term effects of transplantation are not yet known. Of note, this patient was rather unique as she was already immunosuppressed following a prior liver transplant. Though allogeneic stem cell transplants seemingly circumvent the need for lifelong immunosuppression in some patients, such as those with type 2 diabetes, the underlying autoimmune response in T1DM is likely ongoing, leading to the destruction of any newly transplanted unencapsulated β-cells [146]. In another pilot study, a T2DM patient with impaired insulin secretion received an intrahepatic implantation of islet tissue (E-islets) differentiated in vitro from autologous non-tumorigenic human endoderm stem cells (EnSCs). The grafts were well-tolerated and the islet function was rescued, as glycemic control improved [147].

There are also many uncertainties in terms of scalability and commercialization of these differentiation protocols. Current protocols must be adapted to scalable bioreactor systems to pragmatically be considered as cell-based therapies. Further, SC-islets generated in vitro must be preserved using cryopreservation techniques which, currently, are cost-prohibitive [133]. The end goal is to generate universal “off-the-shelf” islets that can be used to cure diabetes without requiring any type of immunosuppression [147]. Should such islets be generated, their pharmacodynamics must be studied like any other drug.

Looking forward, the integration of multi-omic strategies has great applicability in the field of SC-derived β-cells. Firstly, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has also been instrumental in enabling detailed transcriptomic profiling of β-like cells, allowing comparison between in vitro-generated cells and their in vivo counterparts [129, 148]. However, the true power of multi-omics lies in its ability to go beyond transcriptomics—integrating epigenomics, spatial proteomics, and metabolomics in concert to create a more holistic blueprint of functional β-cell identity. For instance, single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC)-seq can unravel regulatory elements that govern β-cell fate, identifying key epigenetic barriers that limit maturation and functionality or, alternatively, promoters that can be exploited to achieve or even accelerate maturation. Meanwhile, spatial proteomics can be used to map and accordingly understand microenvironmental cues that promote or suppress insulin secretion, or perhaps indirectly affect the dynamics of secretion. Moreover, single-cell metabolomics may provide information about energy states at each point in the differentiation protocol. If metabolic impasses exist and can be resolved, this could promote full maturation of SC-derived β-cells. The prospects are limitless, and perhaps the biggest question is not what can be done, but rather what should be done first.
