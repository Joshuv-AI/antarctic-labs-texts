Protocol for collection and generation of bat primary lung and kidney cells
Authors: Manuela MT Pereira, Rita M Quintela-Tizon, Amanda Vicente-Santos, Beckett L Olbrys, BR Ansil, Nancy B Simmons, Daniel J Becker, Kaushal Baid, Arinjay Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySome bat species host pathogenic viruses and represent recognized models for studying antiviral immunity. Here, we present a protocol for collecting and generating primary cells from wild bat tissues. We describe steps for tissue collection, enzymatic digestion, isolation, and cell expansion. We then detail procedures for immunocompetency testing of the established cultures. This protocol describes the generation of in vitro models for understudied non-model species, facilitating physiologically relevant assays to improve understanding of bat biology, immunology, and pathogen-host interactions.Subject areas: Cell culture, Cell isolation, Immunology, Microbiology, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Bats represent the second most diverse mammalian order and account for over 1,500 species distributed across all continents except Antarctica.1 Some bat species have been recognized as hosts for highly pathogenic zoonotic viruses, some of which are able to cause severe disease in humans and livestock.2,3 However, bat species that are infected with these viruses often do not develop severe disease.4,5,6 Work over the last three decades has explored various biological characteristics that enable bats to resist or tolerate viral infections. Yet the lack of bat-specific tools remains a major limitation in the field. Bat cell lines have been developed from only a very limited number of species, constraining the possibilities of obtaining physiologically relevant data. This is a significant limiting factor, because the immune response across bat species cannot be generalized and needs to be studied within specific host–virus settings. Therefore, increasing the taxonomic breadth and diversity of available bat in vitro models will significantly improve the quality of the data being produced, which will increase our overall understanding of bat biology and pathogen–host interactions.7

Here, we describe the process of tissue collection and enzymatic digestion using trypsin for bat kidneys and DNase I-pronase cocktail for bat lungs. Tissues were collected from two focal Neotropical bat species known to harbor potentially zoonotic viruses, including those in the Coronaviridae family: the little yellow-shouldered Mesoamerican bat (Sturnira parvidens) and the northern black mastiff bat (Molossus nigricans).8,9 Both species have a conservation status of least concern, enabling limited lethal sampling (IUCN Red List of Threatened Species). Tissues were digested for several time periods to allow for appropriate isolation of single cells to be subsequently cultured and maintained until confluency and storage. In addition, we validated the application of these newly developed kidney- and lung-derived primary cell cultures by assessing their immunocompetence and suitability as models for virological studies. Immunocompetence was assessed through transfection with polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)], followed by infection with vesicular stomatitis virus engineered to express a green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP). Poly(I:C) is a synthetic double-stranded RNA analog that mimics viral infection and stimulates an antiviral interferon response. VSV-GFP presents a safe reporter virus that enables rapid infection readout. Decreased VSV-GFP infection levels indicate that cells are able to mount a competent immune response to poly(I:C) stimulation.

Innovation

Bats are being increasingly recognized as hosts of multiple zoonotic viruses including filoviruses, coronaviruses, henipaviruses, and rabies virus, among others. Despite causing serious disease in other mammalian species, these viruses do not typically cause clinical signs of disease in experimentally or naturally infected bats. Thus, bats are an intriguing model to study host–virus interactions to discover antiviral processes that have evolved to specifically counteract these groups of viruses. Moreover, primary cells are critical tools for studying host–virus interactions. To date, few studies have reported the establishment of primary cell cultures from bat tissues. For example, Crameri et al. established primary cultures from multiple organs of Pteropus alecto using tissue dissociation and culture prior to immortalization.10 Likewise, Banerjee et al. attempted to establish primary cultures from lungs, kidneys, and other tissues from Eptesicus fuscus using trypsin-based dissociation; however, only kidney-derived cells were successfully isolated and expanded.11 These studies demonstrated feasibility of generating bat cell cultures, but detailed tissue-specific protocols for generating and establishing primary bat cells remain limited, particularly for lung tissues (Table 1). Here, we build upon previous work by providing a detailed, step-by-step workflow for generating primary bat cells from both kidney and lung tissues. In addition to tissue processing and culture establishment, the protocol includes procedures for cell expansion, cryopreservation, species confirmation by cytochrome b sequencing, and functional assessment of antiviral immunocompetency.

Institutional permissions

Prior to sampling wild bats, researchers must obtain permission from relevant institutions, including but not limited to animal care and use committees, wildlife agencies, and other governmental entities. For this study, field sampling was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Oklahoma (protocol R21-021), the Belize Forestry Department (scientific collection permits FD/WL/1/24 (06) and FD/WL/1/22(07)), and the Belize Institute of Archaeology (permit IA/H/1/24(02)). Tissue samples were imported into the United States through approval from the Belize Forestry Department (permits FD/WL/7/24(56) and FD/WL/7/24(57)) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (permit 20240306-0860A). Tissues were imported into Canada through approval from the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. We also note the importance of assessing species conservation status (e.g., via the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species or local conservation prioritization) when deciding on the need for lethal sampling as well as sample size. All laboratory studies were conducted under containment level 2 conditions. Researchers should consult with their institutional biosafety office prior to starting work.

Generation of kidney-derived primary cells

Timing: 3 h

This step details the procedure for generating primary cells from bat kidney tissue through sequential trypsin digestions.

Note: Trypsin is a pancreatic enzyme that hydrolyzes long peptide chains into smaller peptides, thereby allowing tissue dissociation into single primary cells for culture.

Testing antiviral competency of primary cells

Timing: 3 days

This step describes the procedure for testing whether the newly generated primary cells can mount an antiviral response. Our approach was based on transfecting cells with polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)], a synthetic analog of viral double-stranded RNA that triggers the innate immune response. When performed in combination with a reporter virus infection, like VSV-GFP, this assay serves as a functional readout of antiviral immunity in the newly developed primary cell cultures.

Note: This assessment can be performed by using any reporter virus. If this is not possible, other viruses might be used alongside appropriate assessment of viral readouts such as TCID50 or plaque assays. Additionally, quantitative polymerase chain reactions (qPCR) for antiviral genes (such as MX1, IFIT1, IFNB) can be another tool to assess the antiviral competency of the newly developed cells. A cell line known to respond to poly(I:C) can be included in this step to act as a technical positive control in the assay

Expected outcomes

Primary cells are a valuable resource for research as they provide relevant models for investigating molecular processes and pathogen-host interactions. For wildlife species like bats, the generation of primary cells can aid in the study of immune responses.11,13 Here, we present a tested, detailed step-by-step protocol for generating kidney- and lung-derived cells from bats. We also provide methods for an assay to test cell immunocompetency through immune stimulation and antiviral assays.

The number of cells obtained from the established cultures will depend on the original size of each animal species (larger animals have more tissue material), as well as on the tissue dicing technique. Primary cells from newly processed tissues should start attaching and growing in the plates during the first week (Figure 1). As is the case with primary cells, maintaining them post passage 5–10 is not recommended to avoid cell death, senescence, or other unwanted non-relevant physiological responses. If immortalized cell lines are preferred, several methods exist.11,14,15

Through the sequencing of the cytochrome b gene from the newly developed primary cells, genus- or species-level identity can be confirmed if reference genomes or partial target sequences are available in sequence repositories like NCBI. Cytochrome b is widely used to confirm wildlife species, due to the extensive number of sequences available in public databases. Upon optimization of poly(I:C) transfection and VSV-GFP infection levels (or other appropriate readout), cells are expected to show immunocompetency in response to viral stimulation in a cell- and virus-dependent manner (Figure 2). In our case, both newly generated S. parvidens kidney cells and M. nigricans lung primary cells demonstrated immunocompetence in our assays, albeit at different concentrations of poly(I:C). S. parvidens kidney cells show null infection levels upon stimulation with as little as 25 ng/mL poly(I:C), whereas M. nigricans lung cells require a minimum of 100 ng/mL poly(I:C) to show a decrease in viral replication. These results highlight tissue- and species-dependent differences that affect the overall susceptibility and immunocompetency of the generated cells.

Limitations

The generation of primary cells from bat tissues has been described in other publications, but a detailed protocol such as this does not exist. Our goal in creating this protocol is to support the efforts of new researchers in the field and to help boost mechanistic studies into bat biology. The biggest limitation of this protocol is access to healthy, viable tissues from bat species. The number of research colonies of bats has grown, but these remain rare and cover far less than 1% of bat species diversity. Proper tissue preservation can also be a limiting factor, due to poor accessibility of some field work sites with inability to carry the most efficient equipment.

Once tissues are in the laboratory, the necessary kits and materials used in the protocols are not expensive and widely accessible to most laboratories. However, even with processing times of 1–2 days, cells may take several weeks to grow and establish a cell population. Moreover, variable cell-dependent susceptibility to available immunocompetency screening tools may require further optimization. It is worth noting that researchers should be aware of species-specific variabilities, if any, in cell culture conditions, potential contamination by microbes and the limited lifespan of primary cells. The population doubling time (PDL) of the generated cell lines was not measured but may be desirable if the observed growth kinetics differ significantly between batches or individual samples. Some other limitations of the study include: (i) the protocol was validated in two bat species from a single geographic region; applicability to other bat families (e.g., Pteropodidae, Rhinolophidae) should be tested and optimized by researchers and, (ii) immunocompetency assay tests dsRNA-sensing arm of innate immunity (like TLR3/MDA5 pathways) and it is recommended that researchers identify and characterize other innate immune pathways as relevant for their studies.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low yield of primary cells (related to steps 4–6 and 13).

Potential solution

Cell viability is at its highest shortly after tissue harvesting. If tissues need to be stored prior to processing, freeze in liquid nitrogen in individual vials fully filled with storage media to make sure that tissues are embedded in it and not floating on top of the freezing media. Upon thawing, quickly warm the vial in a 37°C water bath and immediately start processing. Additionally, cutting the tissues into smaller pieces may increase the surface area and, therefore, the chances of primary cell isolation in the digestion media.

Problem 2

Contamination of primary cell culture (related to steps 2 and 3).

Potential solution

Take precautions during tissue collection in the field to ensure that clean techniques are used and tissues are not in contact with external materials like soil. Concentration of antibiotics in the media may be increased to address mild contamination, but this is not ideal. Since the goal of these cells is to use them in downstream molecular studies, any hint of contamination while establishing these cells should lead to decontamination of cells, followed by discarding them using institutional biohazard disposal processes. Fresh tissues will have to be used to start a new culture.

Problem 3

Primary cells are growing very slowly (related to steps 18 and 22).

Potential solution

Attached cells may be transferred from a 6-well plate to a 12-well plate, as they may prefer to be in closer proximity to each other. Concentration of FBS in the media may be increased to help. Ensure that media is changed regularly, preferably every 2–3 days.

Problem 4

Primary cells are not attaching to the plate (related to steps 9 and 18).

Potential solution

Ensure that the plate containing processed primary cells remains undisturbed for the first 2–3 days to allow for proper cell attachment. If the problem persists, spin the cells and resuspend in fresh media before reseeding into the same plate.

Problem 5

Digestion yields no primary cells (related to steps 7 and 14).

Potential solution

For generating primary cells from lung tissues, digestion times vary widely to accommodate the characteristics of each tissue. If the well seems empty after processing, try a shorter digestion time to optimize the isolation of primary cells from that species.
