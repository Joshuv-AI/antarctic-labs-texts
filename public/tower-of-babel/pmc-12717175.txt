A Chromosome-level genome assembly of the black ant Formica fusca
Authors: Hao Chen, Shuo Jin, Zicen Li, Haonan Duanmu, Chenghao Huang, Jing Liang, Runguo Shu, Fei Li, Shuping Wang, Kang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Formica fusca is an ideal model organism for investigating social behaviour and chemical communication in insects. Here, we present the first high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of F. fusca, generated using PacBio HiFi sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding technologies. The resulting genome assembly spans 535.26 Mb, is anchored to 27 chromosomes, and achieves a scaffold N50 of 20.27 Mb. This assembly comprises 368.74 Mb of repetitive elements and 13,269 protein-coding genes. The chromosome-level genome assembly of F. fusca provides a valuable genomic resource to facilitate studies on insect social organization and the molecular basis of ant social behaviour.

Background & Summary

Ants serve as exemplary models for studying social insects1. Individual ants exhibit clearly defined social roles, engage in intricate communication within their colonies, and interact reciprocally with other species, collectively forming highly integrated social systems. Such systems enable complex biological activities, including foraging, reproduction, defence, and social immunity2. The sophisticated social behaviours of ants have enabled their remarkable diversification, with over 14,000 species inhabiting most terrestrial corners of the Earth3, thereby making them ideal organisms for investigating biological complexity and social behaviours.

F. fusca is a widely distributed ant species known for its precise recognition capabilities4,5, strong learning abilities6, and diverse social structures7, establishing it as an excellent model for studying chemical communication and social behaviour. Previous research has investigated the population structure5,7, social immunity8, chemical communication9, and group performance10 of F. fusca, and a comprehensive transcriptomic dataset is also available. In 2025, Vizueta et al. reported the first scaffold-level reference genome assembly for F. fusca11. However, a high-quality chromosome-level genome remains unavailable, limiting our understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying this species’ complex social behaviours.

Here, we present the first chromosome-level genome assembly of F. fusca generated by integrating PacBio High Fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing with high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) technology (Fig. 1). The genome size of F. fusca was estimated to be 478.09 Mb by flow cytometry (Fig. 2). Compared to the assembly reported by Vizueta et al.11, our genome assembly features a larger genome size, improved scaffold N50 value, and anchoring to 27 chromosomes (Table 1). Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) analysis demonstrated 97.41% completeness of conserved orthologs, indicating enhanced reliability relative to the previous assembly (Table 2). We identified 368.74 Mb (~68.86%) of repetitive sequences, with 94.76% classified as known elements (Table 3). Compared with the genome assembly reported by Vizueta et al., our assembly exhibits a substantially larger genome size, a markedly higher proportion of repetitive elements, and a considerably lower number of protein-coding genes (Table 1). We attribute these discrepancies primarily to the sequencing platforms and assembly strategies. Vizueta et al. generated their genome assembly using stLFR sequencing with MaSuRCA and SLR-superscaffolder, resulting in a scaffold-level assembly. Due to the limitations of short-read data and sparse linkage information, repetitive regions were severely collapsed, leading to an underestimation of repeat content and a higher number of predicted protein-coding genes with fragmentation. In contrast, we employed PacBio HiFi long reads assembled with hifiasm and further scaffolded with Hi-C to achieve a chromosome-level assembly. This approach allowed for more accurate resolution of repetitive sequences and complex gene structures, yielding the true genomic features of this species.

To sequence the whole genome of F. fusca, we first estimated the genome size using both flow cytometry and k-mer analysis (Table 5). The results showed that the genome size estimated by flow cytometry was 57.37 Mb (∼10.7%) smaller than that estimated by k-mer analysis, while the latter was highly consistent with the final Hi-C assembly. As reported by Cong et al., such differences are not primarily associated with sequencing platforms or assembly algorithms but are largely attributable to the proportion of repetitive sequences12. Genomes with higher repeat content tend to produce assembly sizes larger than the flow cytometry estimates. In summary, this high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of F. fusca elucidates the genomic architecture of this species, providing a fundamental resource for investigating its biological characteristics and the genetic underpinnings of its complex social behaviours.

Methods

Sample collection

F. fusca was collected from a forest in Changchun City, Jilin Province, China (43°52′51.769″N, 126°40′0.815″E) and was provided with a diet of white sugar under controlled laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 12 L:12D, 50 ± 10% RH).

Estimation of genome size

The genome size of F. fusca was estimated using flow cytometry (FC) and k-mer analysis. FC was performed according to an established protocol15. Briefly, heads from 20 CO2-anesthetized workers were pooled and homogenized in ice-cold Galbraith’s buffer to release nuclei. The nuclei suspension was then filtered, centrifuged (2000 rpm, 4 °C, 5 min), and washed with PBS. Following staining with propidium iodide (50 μg/mL) and RNase A (20 μg/mL) for 5–20 min, samples were analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur™ flow cytometer. Instrument calibration and parameter settings (488 nm excitation; 2 C peak at 500 V) followed manufacturer guidelines (CellQuest Pro v5.1). Drosophila melanogaster was used as an internal reference. We further estimated the genome size and features of F. fusca using k-mer analysis. With GCE v1.0.0, a total of 40.51 Gb of clean short-reads data were analyzed with k = 17. The results indicated a genome size of 540.17 Mb, a heterozygosity rate of 0.53%, and a repeat content of 68.14% (Table 7).

PacBio HiFi sequencing and de novo assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted from a single worker using the FineOut Universal Plant and Animal Genomic Extraction Kit. The integrity and purity of DNA were monitored using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop™ One UV-Vis spectrophotometer, while the concentration of genomic DNA was accurately measured using Qubit fluorescence photometer. Five micrograms of genomic DNA were sheared using the Megaruptor 3, followed by the removal of single-stranded overhangs and the repair of damaged DNA fragments. Purification was performed using AMPure PB magnetic beads. The SMRTbell libraries were prepared using the Pacific Biosciences SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0. Fragment size selection was performed using the BluePippin™ system to ensure that the constructed library had an insert size of 15 kb. Primer annealing was then carried out, followed by the binding of SMRTbell templates to polymerase using the DNA/polymerase binding kit. Sequencing was performed on the Pacific Biosciences Revio platform at Annoroad Gene Technology Company (Beijing, China).

Using the Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) mode, a total of 728,437 HiFi reads amounting to 13.22 Gb were generated, with an average read length of 19,489.38 bp and a CCS N50 of 19,816 bp. De novo assembly of PacBio HiFi reads for F. fusca was performed using hifiasm v0.19.616 under default parameters. The resulting de novo genome assembly had a length of 564,248,499 bp, a contig N50 of 1,378,053 bp, the longest sequence length of 14,446,803 bp, and a GC content of 39.33%.

Chromosomal-level genome assembly of Formica fusca

The initial contig-level genome assembly was further anchored to chromosomes using Hi-C sequencing data17. One gram of whole body tissue from F. fusca was treated with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 20 min to cross-link chromatin-interacting proteins. DNA was digested with MboI, and the ends of the resulting restriction fragments were labelled and filled with biotinylated nucleotides to facilitate proximity ligation. After reversing the cross-links, the ligated DNA was purified and fragmented to a size range of 300–500 bp. Ligation junctions were extracted using streptavidin beads, generating 70.15 Gb of raw Hi-C reads. The raw data were processed with fastp v0.20.018 to remove adapters and filter out reads with average quality scores below 20, yielding 63.24 Gb of clean reads. A high-confidence contig interaction matrix was obtained using HiCUP v0.7.219 with default parameters. The original 1,287 contigs were anchored to chromosomes using the 3D-DNA pipeline. Finally, manual curation was performed using Juicebox v1.11.0820 to correct misassemblies such as chromosomal translocations and inversions. This resulted in 490.58 Mb (~91.62%) of contig-level sequences being anchored onto 27 chromosomes (Fig. 4; Table 1). Genome integrity was assessed using BUSCO v5.6.121 (Table 2).

Gene prediction and functional annotation

De novo identification of genomic repetitive sequences was performed using RepeatModeler v2.0.522. Predictions were merged with Insecta repetitive sequences from the Dfam23 repeat database to create a customized repeat library. RepeatMasker v4.1.524 was subsequently used with the parameters “-xsmall -gff -html -pa 24” to annotate and soft-mask repetitive elements (Table 3). Gene prediction was conducted following an established genome annotation pipeline developed in our laboratory and utilized in InsectBase 2.025, integrating de novo, transcriptome-based and homology-based approaches.

Fourteen transcriptomes of F. fusca were downloaded from the NCBI SRA database (DRR140512, DRR140514, DRR140515, DRR140518, DRR140519, DRR140520, DRR140521, DRR140522, DRR140523, DRR140525, DRR140526, DRR140527, DRR140529, DRR140531). These transcriptomes were processed using Hisat2 v2.2.126, StringTie v2.1.727, and TransDecoder v5.7.1 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder) to generate predictions based on transcriptomic evidence. De novo predictions were generated using BRAKER v3.0.828. Additionally, homologous evidence-based predictions were obtained using miniprot v0.1329 and a homology protein database for insects from OrthoDB30.

Finally, gene models for F. fusca were predicted by integrating the results from these three methods using EVidenceModeler31. Predicted protein-coding genes were functionally annotated through BLASTP searches against the Swiss-Prot (https://www.uniprot.org/) and the Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) searches using diamond v2.1.032. Predicted genes were classified into different functional categories based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg) and Gene Ontology (GO, https://geneontology.org/) annotations (Table 4).

Phylogenetic analysis

To elucidate the evolutionary status of F. fusca within the Formicidae family, we included 18 additional Hymenoptera species for a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis. Bombus bifarius (Family Apidae) was used as the outgroup, while the other 17 species were all classified within the Formicidae family. Protein sequences for all species, except F. fusca, were downloaded from InsectBase 2.0 (Table 5).

OrthoFinder233 was used to identify orthogroups for the 19 species, ultimately retrieving 1,457 single-copy orthogroups. For each species, protein sequences in all single-copy orthogroups were extracted and concatenated to form a single ultra-long gene. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using MAFFT v7.52534, followed by sequence trimming with trimAl v1.5.rev035 using default parameters. The phylogenetic tree was constructed with IQ-TREE v2.3.636 using the parameters “-m TEST -nt AUTO -B 1000” and the resulting tree was visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Chromosomal synteny analysis

Based on the phylogenetic analysis, which indicated a close evolutionary relationship between F. fusca and F. selysi, whole-genome synteny analysis was conducted between these two species using JCVI v1.4.2237. The syntenic relationships across chromosomes were visualized using Circos v0.69.938.

Data Records

The raw data for this genome have been deposited in the NGDC (National Genomics Data Center) database with accession number GSA: CRA04593339 and under BioProject accession number PRJCA043571. The chromosome-level assembly is stored in GenBank with accession number GCA_052642515.140. In addition to GenBank, the CDS sequences, protein sequences, and other annotation files for this genome are also available in InsectBase 3.0 under accession ID IBG_0340041.

Technical Validation

The chromosome-level genome assembly has a size of 535.46 Mb, with 91.62% of the sequences successfully anchored to the chromosomes and a scaffold N50 of 20.27 Mb. As a benchmark for genome completeness, the BUSCO assessment based on the hymenoptera_odb10 dataset showed a complete BUSCO (C) score of 97.41%, indicating that 97.41% of the BUSCO genes (5,836 in total) were fully identified. This strongly demonstrates the high completeness of the F. fusca genome assembly.

Furthermore, the Hi-C heatmap revealed well-organized interactions along the diagonal in the chromosomal inversion regions. A high degree of synteny was also observed between the F. fusca genome and the chromosome-level genome assembly of the closely related species Formica selysi, further confirming the high accuracy of the F. fusca chromosomal assembly.
