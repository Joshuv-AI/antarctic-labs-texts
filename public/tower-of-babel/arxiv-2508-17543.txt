Validity of relativistic hydrodynamics beyond local equilibrium
Reghukrishnan Gangadharan

Abstract
We examine the applicability of relativistic hydrodynamics far from equilibrium by constructing formal solutions of the Boltzmann moment equations in the relaxation time approximation. These solutions naturally decompose into a divergent gradient series and exponentially decaying non-perturbative modes that encode initial conditions. The non-perturbative contributions are essential for understanding causality, the divergence of the gradient series, and the unexpected effectiveness of relativistic hydrodynamics far from equilibrium. In the 0 + 1 0+1 D Bjorken scenario, we demonstrate that the exact evolution of non-equilibrium terms shares the same structural form as the gradient expansion, differing only through modified transport coefficients that reflect both initial data and free-streaming dynamics. Extending to 3 + 1 3+1 D, we find that hydrodynamics remains effective not because the system is close to equilibrium, but because it interpolates smoothly between free streaming and collective behavior. This perspective offers a natural explanation for the remarkable success of hydrodynamics in modeling quark–gluon plasma evolution.



I
Introduction

Relativistic hydrodynamics is an effective theory that describes the macroscopic evolution of energy–momentum and conserved charges in a fluid at relativistic energies. It finds key applications ranging from astrophysical plasmas and cosmology to high-energy nuclear collisions.

The applicability of relativistic hydrodynamics to the quark–gluon plasma (QGP) is somewhat puzzling. In principle, hydrodynamics is expected to be valid only near local thermal equilibrium, in systems that are sufficiently large and slowly varying compared to microscopic scales. In contrast, the QGP produced in heavy-ion collisions is created in a highly anisotropic, far-from-equilibrium state. Despite these conditions, viscous hydrodynamics provides a quantitatively accurate description
Clare and Strottman 1986
of collective flow observables like particle spectra and flow coefficients
Teaney et al. 2001
;
Kolb and Heinz 2003
;
Teaney 2010
;
Heinz and Schenke 2024
. Even more surprisingly, flow-like correlations observed in small systems such as
p–Pb
and high-multiplicity
p–p

Khachatryan et al. 2016
;
Aad et al. 2016
collisions can also be reproduced within a hydrodynamic framework. These successes indicate that hydrodynamic behavior can emerge much earlier and in much smaller systems than expected. These observations raises the hydrodynamisation question: why does hydrodynamics work so well beyond its domain of validity?

One possible solution proposed to address this puzzle has been the emergence of hydrodynamic attractors. In highly symmetric settings, solutions of the fluid dynamical equations have been found to converge toward a universal attractor well before the system reaches local equilibrium
Romatschke 2018
;
Heller et al. 2012
;
Heller and Spalinski 2015
;
Buchel et al. 2016
;
Heller et al. 2013
. These attractors have provided useful insights in cases such as Bjorken and Gubser flow, but their extension to non-conformal systems and fully three-dimensional QGP evolution failed to show early time universal behavior
Dash and Roy 2020
;
Chen and Yan 2022
;
Kamata et al. 2023
;
Jaiswal et al. 2022a
;
Gangadharan et al. 2024
. Consequently, attractors do not provide a full resolution of the applicability of hydrodynamics in heavy-ion collisions.

An alternate perspective highlights the role of rescaled transport coefficients and fixed points in the effective description
Blaizot and Yan 2018
;
Blaizot and Yan 2020
;
Blaizot and Yan 2021a
;
Blaizot and Yan 2021b
;
Jaiswal et al. 2022b
. It has been demonstrated that second-order hydrodynamic equations can be mapped onto the moment equations of the Boltzmann equation. When these moment equations are truncated at a suitable order, they can reproduce the exact evolution provided the transport coefficients are properly rescaled to align with the fixed points of the exact dynamics.

In this work, we build on these earlier insights to provide a more systematic theoretical account of how hydrodynamics emerges from kinetic theory. We demonstrate that the exact solution of the Boltzmann equation, as well as its common model approximations, naturally decomposes into a gradient expansion accompanied by exponentially decaying non-perturbative contributions. In relativistic hydrodynamics, these non-perturbative contributions manifest as the so-called “non-hydrodynamic” modes. We explore the origin and implications of these non-perturbative terms using singular perturbation theory within the context of a simple toy model.

We then carry this analysis over to the moment equations by deriving the exact solution of the distribution function moments for a
0+1
D system undergoing Bjorken flow. From this solution, we derive the gradient expansion along with the associated non-perturbative corrections. We obtain evolution equations for the non-equilibrium components of the moments, showing that the dynamical equations obtained using gradient expansion resemble that of the full non-equilibrium dynamics.

Examining the equations for bulk and shear anisotropies, we show that hydrodynamics can can be extended to the non-equilibrium free-streaming regime if the transport coefficients are appropriately rescaled. This suggests that conventional relativistic hydrodynamics, with suitably modified transport coefficients, can interpolate smoothly between free-streaming and equilibrium regimes, effectively capturing non-equilibrium effects. We conclude by discussing how these insights extend to systems with more general flow profiles and spacetime dimensions.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the foundations of relativistic hydrodynamics, with emphasis on its derivation from kinetic theory via the Chapman–Enskog expansion. In Section 3, we discuss the breakdown of this expansion and explain the origin of its divergence. Section 5 presents the exact solution of the
0+1
D RTA moment equations, from which we derive the corresponding hydrodynamic evolution equations and show how non-equilibrium effects can be incorporated through rescaled transport coefficients. Building on these results, Section 6 extends the analysis to
3+1
D relaxation-type equations.

Relativistic Hydrodynamics

A systematic way to derive these dynamical equations is to begin with the evolution equations of an underlying microscopic theory and use the connection between macroscopic moments and microscopic degrees of freedom to obtain their dynamics. The relativistic Boltzmann equation serves as a natural choice for such a microscopic description.

Divergence of the Gradient Expansion

The Chapman–Enskog expansion is an asymptotic, and generally divergent, solution of the Boltzmann equation

;
Heller et al. 2013
;

\delta\frac{df(t)}{dt}=-\left(f(t)-g(t)\right)\,,

(21)

In (

(23)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-t/\delta}f(0)+\left(g(t)-e^{-t/\delta}g(0)\right)

\displaystyle-\delta\left(~g^{\prime}(t)-e^{-t/\delta}g^{\prime}(0)\right)+\dots\,.

This expression matches the perturbative series in the late-time limit,

If

(25)

where

\frac{\gamma(n+1,w)}{n!}=\left(1-e^{-w}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{w^{k}}{k!}\right)\,.

(26)

This expression is advantageous because it represents the solution primarily through local derivatives, each weighted by time-dependent coefficients. As a result, when the pre-factor

The Fixed point perspective

The two limits of the exact solution to (

III.2
Validity of the Gradient Expansion

Before discussing the validity of the relativistic gradient expansion, it is important to distinguish between the gradient expansion itself and relativistic hydrodynamics. The gradient expansion refers specifically to the asymptotic series obtained via a perturbative expansion in the Knudsen number (
Kn
). Relativistic hydrodynamics, on the other hand, encompasses not only this gradient series but also non-perturbative corrections—an aspect that will be made more rigorous in later sections. The expansions shown in (
III.1
) and (
III.1
) naturally include non-perturbative contributions—often labeled as “non-hydrodynamic” modes. This label, however, can be misleading as such modes are essential for ensuring both causality
Gavassino et al. 2024
and convergence
Gangadharan and Roy 2025
of the hydrodynamic theory. It is more accurate to interpret them as non-perturbative corrections, not non-hydrodynamic.

The exponentially decaying non-perturbative terms in (
III.1
) can be rewritten
Gangadharan and Roy 2025
in the form,

f_{NP}=-e^{-\xi^{\prime}(t,t^{\prime})}\left[-\frac{\tau_{R}(t,t_{0})}{p\cdot U(t,t_{0})}p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\right]^{n}f_{eq}(t,t_{0})\,.

(33)

It is clear that these non-perturbative contributions encode information about the initial conditions and become relevant when the Knudsen number approaches unity (
Kn\sim 1
)—that is, during early times when
\xi_{0}<1
, or equivalently, when
t<\tau_{R}
for constant relaxation time. Unlike the toy model, these corrections are non-local due to the dependence of
\tau_{R}
on the evolving moments of the distribution function. Physically, this regime corresponds to the pre-collisional or free-streaming phase, where interactions have yet to dominate. In this limit, the traditional gradient expansion breaks down because it cannot fully capture the free streaming structure of the distribution function.

The Chapman-Enskog gradient expansion remains valid as long as free-streaming effects are suppressed—i.e., when
\chi_{0}
or
\xi_{0}\gg 1
—even when the gradients are large.
We therefore argue that the applicability of the gradient expansion should not be judged by small gradients alone, but rather by how far (
\chi_{0}~\text{or}~\xi_{0}\gg 1
) it is from the free-streaming regime. In the simple
1
D toy model this condition implies each other i.e
t/\delta>>1\implies\delta\frac{d}{dt}<<1
, but in general .

For a smooth, well-posed dynamical system, fixed points can only be approached asymptotically and never reached at finite time. The Chapman–Enskog gradient expansion, however, implicitly assumes that one can construct a solution valid at every finite time by expanding around the fixed point (the local equilibrium). This clashes with the actual dynamics: trying to self-consistently enforce the fixed point at finite time might be the origin of acausality.

Importantly, the validity of relativistic hydrodynamics is distinct from the validity of the Chapman-Enskog gradient expansion. This distinction will be clarified and further explored in the next sections.

is the bulk pressure and the shear tensor is reduced to the form
\pi^{\mu\nu}=\text{diag}(0,\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
. The transverse pressure
P_{T}=P+\Pi+\frac{\pi}{2}
and the longitudinal pressure is
P_{L}=P+\Pi-\pi
.

The RTA Boltzmann equation in for this system takes the simplified form,

\partialderivative{f}{\tau}-\frac{p^{z}}{\tau}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{z}}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}(f-f^{eq})\,.

(36)

The distribution function
f(p^{0},p^{z},\tau)
depend on the particle energy
p^{0}
, the
z
component of momentum
p^{z}
and the proper time
\tau
. The symmetries in the transverse plane imply transverse momentum space isotropy. The transverse momentum is given by the energy-momentum relation
(p^{0})^{2}-(p^{z})^{2}-p_{T}^{2}=m^{2}
, where
m
is the mass of the particle and
p_{T}=\sqrt{(p^{x})^{2}+(p^{y})^{2}}
.

IV.1
Moment Equations

We define
Denicol and Noronha 2016
the the moments
\rho_{n,l}
of the distribution function as

\rho_{n,l}(\tau)=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}p^{0}}(p^{0})^{n+1}\left(\frac{p^{z}}{p^{0}}\right)^{2l}f(p^{0},p^{z},\tau)\,.

(37)

Here, the index
n
encodes the energy scaling, while
l
characterizes the anisotropy in the momentum distribution. In particular, the number density (
n
) corresponds to the moment
\rho_{0,0}
, and the energy density (
\epsilon
) is given by
\rho_{1,0}
. The equilibrium moments
\rho_{n,l}^{eq}
when
f
has the Maxwell-Juttner form has the expression,

\rho_{n,l}^{eq}(T,z)=\frac{T^{n+3}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{G_{n+3,l}(z)}{2l+1}e^{\mu/T}\,,

(38)

where
G_{n,l}(z)
is a function (Appendix (
A
)) that depends on the reduced mass
z=m/T
. In the massless limit
\lim_{z\to 0}G_{n,l}(z)=\Gamma(n)
.

The dynamics of the moments in
0+1
D can be obtained
Denicol and Noronha 2016
by integrating the RTA equation (
36
) ,

\partial_{\tau}\rho_{n,l}+\frac{2l+1}{\tau}\rho_{n,l}-\frac{2l-n}{\tau}\rho_{n,l+1}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\left(\rho_{n,l}-\rho_{n,l}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,.

(39)

In the moment equations Eq.(
39
), the
l
th moment is coupled with the
l+1
th moment, forming an infinite set of coupled differential equations. These equations are typically solved numerically by using a truncation scheme where all moments for
l>l_{m}
is set to zero
de Brito et al. 2024a
;
Denicol and Noronha 2016
;
Jaiswal et al. 2022b
;
Gangadharan et al. 2025
for some arbitrary choice of
l_{m}
.

IV.2
Solution of the moment equations

A set of coupled linear equations can always be written in a matrix form. Though (
39
) is a nonlinear differential equation due to
\tau_{R}
in general depending on temperature, it has a linear algebraic structure. Exploiting this we can write (
39
) in the form,

\left[\partial_{\tau}+\frac{2l+1}{\tau}\mathbf{\hat{I}}-\frac{2l-n}{\tau}\mathbf{\hat{S}}\right]\rho_{n,l}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\mathbf{\hat{I}}\left(\rho_{n,l}-\rho_{n,l}^{\text{eq}}\right)\,,

(40)

where we defined the identity operator
\mathbf{\hat{I}}
and the
l
shift operator,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{S}}[h(l)]=h(l+1)\,.

(41)

For reasons that will become clear later we define the operator,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{F}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{2l+1}{\tau}\mathbf{\hat{I}}-\frac{2l-n}{\tau}\mathbf{\hat{S}}\,,

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}+\mathbf{\hat{F}}(\tau)\,

(43)

The operator
\mathbf{\hat{F}}
can be interpreted as a Liouville-like operator acting in moment space. It is now instructive to look at the solutions to the collision free equation,

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+\mathbf{\hat{F}}(\tau)\right]g_{n,l}(\tau)=0\,.

(44)

Using the integrating factor method we can write down the solution (Appendix (
B.1
)),

g_{n,l}(\tau)=e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}(\tau,\tau_{0})}}g_{n,l}(\tau_{0})\,

(45)

where
\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})\equiv\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathbf{\hat{F}}(\tau^{\prime})d\tau^{\prime}
. If the initial values
g_{n,l}(\tau_{0})
are the moments of a distribution function
f
, then the evolved quantities
g_{n,l}(\tau)
represent the moments of the free-streamed distribution function
f(\tau,\tau_{0})
.

We can interpret this more elegantly if we consider the vector space spanned by the moments
\rho_{n,l}(\tau)
. Then the operator
\mathbf{\hat{F}}(\tau^{\prime})
is the generator of free streaming group with the representation (propagator)
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}=e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}
.

An exact expression for the free streaming solution can be obtained (see Appendix (
B.1
))

g_{n,l}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\,K_{n,l,k}(\tau,\tau_{0})g_{n,l+k}(\tau_{0})\,,

(46)

where

K_{n,l,k}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{\left(l-\frac{n}{2}\right)^{(k)}}{k!}\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{\tau}\right)^{2l+1}\left[1-\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{\tau}\right)^{2}\right]^{k}\,.

(47)

The leading order late time behavior can be easily read off from the expression
g_{n,l}(\tau>>\tau_{0})\sim\left(\frac{\tau_{0}}{\tau}\right)^{2l+1}
, which is independent of
n
.

We can now write down a formal solution to the RTA moment equation,

\displaystyle\rho_{n,l}(\tau)

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{n,l}^{0}(\tau)

\displaystyle+\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\frac{e^{-\xi^{\prime}}}{\tau_{R}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}\rho_{n,l}^{\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})\,,

(48)

for the moment equation (
40
), where
\xi^{\prime}=\int_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime\prime}/\tau_{R}
. The similarity of (
IV.2
) to (
23
) and (
III.1
) is immediately obvious. The integral term in the above expression
\rho_{G}
, called the hydrodynamic generator in
McNelis and Heinz 2020
can be expanded as ( Appendix (
B
))

\displaystyle\rho^{G}_{n,l}(\tau)=\,\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\frac{e^{-\xi^{\prime}}}{\tau_{R}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}K_{n,l,k}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\rho_{n,l+k}^{\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})\,.

(49)

In the conformal case (
m=0
), this expression simplifies ( Appendix (
B
)) to the known closed-form result in terms of hypergeometric functions
Strickland and Tantary 2019
. Since the hypergeometric function is absolutely convergent in the relevant domain, the order of summation and integration can be interchanged. This further confirms that the exact gradient expansion derived from this expression is convergent.

IV.3
Gradient expansion of the moments

A gradient for the moments can be obtained( Appendix (
B.2
)) from the solution (
IV.2
),

\displaystyle\rho_{n,l}(\tau)

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{n,l}(\tau_{0})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau)\,.

(50)

The first term represents the damped free-streaming solution, while the second term encodes both the gradient expansion and non-perturbative corrections through the incomplete gamma function
\gamma(k,\xi_{0})
. We note the similarity of the expression to the singulant formalism introduced in
Heller et al. 2022
.

This structure can be made more transparent by explicitly separating the perturbative and non-perturbative parts,

\displaystyle\rho_{n,l}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau)

\displaystyle-e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-\rho_{n,l}(\tau_{0})\right)\,,

(51)

The system asymptotically approaches the gradient expansion, while the contribution from free-streaming modes—encoded in the second term—decays exponentially. The expression in brackets in (
IV.3
) quantifies the discrepancy between the exact initial condition and its gradient expansion. However, it is important to note that neither of the two terms in (
IV.3
) is convergent by themselves. This behavior mirrors the expansion obtained for the full distribution function, where the exact solution contains both a divergent asymptotic gradient series and a non-perturbative term carrying initial data.

IV.4
Dynamics of anisotropy

To derive the hydrodynamic equations for the
0+1
D system, we begin by decomposing the moments into equilibrium and non-equilibrium ( anisotropic ) parts,

\rho_{n,l}=\rho_{n,l}^{eq}+\pi_{n,l}\,

(52)

where
\rho_{n,l}^{eq}
given in (
38
) is a function of
T
and
\mu
. The evolution of
T
and
\mu
or alternatively
\epsilon
and
n
is obtained from the conservation equation with the Landau matching constraint,

\displaystyle\rho_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0,0}^{eq}(T,\mu)\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\rho_{1,0}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1,0}^{eq}(T,\mu)\,.

This leads to the two conservation equations,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}\rho_{0,0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle-~\text{(particle number conservation)}\,,

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}\rho_{1,0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle-~\text{(energy conservation)}\,,

which govern the dynamics of the equilibrium variables. The evolution of the non-equilibrium components
\pi_{n,l}
follows directly from (
40
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}\pi_{n,l}=-\frac{\pi_{n,l}}{\tau_{R}}-\mathbf{\hat{D}}\rho^{eq}_{n,l}(\tau)\,.

(55)

Together, equations (
54
) and (
55
) form a complete set describing the system’s evolution and are equivalent to the full moment hierarchy (
39
). We can obtain a Chapman-Enskog type gradient expansion for
\pi_{n,l}
iterating on (
55
), to get the gradient expansion,

\pi_{n,l}^{G}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{eq}_{n,l}(\tau)\,.

(56)

Using the formal solution from (
IV.2
) or (
IV.3
), we can express the solution to (
55
) as,

\displaystyle\pi_{n,l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau)

\displaystyle+e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\left(\rho_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau_{0})\right)\,.

(57)

This exact expression motivates us to subdivide the non-equilibrium components into gradient
\pi_{n,l}^{G}
and the exponentially decaying non-perturbative
\pi_{n,l}^{T}
terms ,

\pi_{n,l}=\pi_{n,l}^{G}+\pi_{n,l}^{T}\,.

(58)

The non-perturbative term being,

\pi_{n,l}^{T}(\tau)=e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\left(\rho_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau_{0})\right)\,.

(59)

Note that the term enclosed in the brackets of (
IV.4
) is
\pi_{n,l}^{T}(\tau_{0})=\pi_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-\pi_{n,l}^{G}(\tau_{0})
with
{\pi}_{n,l}(\tau_{0})={\rho}_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-{\rho}_{n,l}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau_{0})
, which can be thought of as a measure of how well the gradient expansion at
\tau_{0}
captures the initial data.

The corresponding evolution equations for the anisotropic components are,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}\pi_{n,l}^{G}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi_{n,l}^{G}}{\tau_{R}}-\mathbf{\hat{D}}\rho^{eq}_{n,l}

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}\pi^{T}_{n,l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi^{T}_{n,l}}{\tau_{R}}

(61)

A particularly striking observation is that the evolution equation (
60
) for the gradient component
\pi_{n,l}^{G}
is structurally identical to the full non-equilibrium evolution equation (
55
). Moreover, the non-perturbative term can be expressed through the gradient contributions:

\pi_{n,l}^{T}(\tau)\sim e^{-\xi_{0}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\xi_{0}^{k}}{k!}\right)\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau)

(62)

These observations are central to explaining the effectiveness of hydrodynamics even far from local equilibrium. They indicate that one can construct hydrodynamics starting from the gradient expansion, provided the transport coefficients are suitably adjusted. This approach yields evolution equations that reproduce the exact dynamics, and it closely mirrors the heuristic used in standard derivations of relativistic hydrodynamics. We demonstrate this explicitly by deriving the exact hydrodynamic equations in the following subsection.

IV.5
Hydrodynamic Equations

Building on the insights from the previous section, we now derive the hydrodynamic evolution equations from the anisotropic moment equations. In the
0+1
D setting, the relevant quantities are the energy density
\epsilon
, the bulk viscous pressure
\Pi
and the shear pressure
\pi
. Our goal is to obtain a closed set of dynamical equations for these quantities.

Energy-momentum conservation provides the evolution equation for the energy density,

\partial_{\tau}\epsilon=-\frac{\epsilon+P_{L}}{\tau}\,,

(63)

where the longitudinal pressure
P_{L}=P+\Pi-\pi
.
The pressure anisotropies are related to the anisotropic moments
\pi_{1,1}=\Pi-\pi
and
\pi_{-1,0}=-\frac{3}{m^{2}}\Pi
. From the equations for the anisotropic components we get,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Pi+\frac{\Pi}{\tau_{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pi}{\tau}-\mathbf{\hat{D}}P-\frac{m^{2}}{3}\frac{\pi_{-1,1}}{\tau}

(64)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\pi+\frac{\pi}{\tau_{R}}

\displaystyle=-(4-c_{s}^{2})\frac{\pi}{\tau}+2(2-c_{s}^{2})\frac{\Pi}{\tau}-\frac{\pi_{1,2}}{\tau}-\frac{m^{2}}{3}\frac{\pi_{-1,1}}{\tau}

(65)

where the operator
\mathbf{\hat{D}}P
captures the pressure gradients and is given by,

\mathbf{\hat{D}}P=-c_{s}^{2}\frac{(\epsilon+P+\Pi-\pi)}{\tau}-\frac{\kappa}{\tau}+\frac{3P}{\tau}-\frac{\rho^{eq}_{1,2}(\tau)}{\tau}\,,

(66)

with
c_{s}^{2}=\partialderivative{P}{\epsilon}
representing the square of the speed of sound and
\kappa=n\partialderivative{P}{\text{n}}
bulk modulus.

In deriving this expression, we used the trace of the energy-momentum tensor, which satisfies

T^{\mu}_{\mu}=\epsilon-3(P+\Pi)=m^{2}\rho_{-1,1}
. This relation serves as an effective equation of state. In the conformal limit (
m=0
), the bulk pressure vanishes (
\Pi=0
), and the equation of state reduces to
\epsilon=3P
.

The evolution equations (
64
) and (
65
) closely resemble those found in second-order hydrodynamics derived from traditional gradient expansion techniques
Denicol et al. 2010
;
Denicol et al. 2012
;
Jaiswal 2013a
;
Jaiswal 2013b
;
Panda et al. 2021a
;
Panda et al. 2021b
, though it’s important to stress that no approximation has yet been made in the derivation yet.

To close the system of equations, the higher order moment couplings,
\pi_{-1,1}
and
\pi_{1,2}
, must be expressed in terms of the hydrodynamic variables
\epsilon
,
n
,
\Pi
, and
\pi
. This can be achieved, in principle, by utilizing the expressions derived in equation (
IV.3
),

\displaystyle\pi_{-1,1}

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{-1,1}(\tau_{0})-e^{-\xi_{0}}\rho_{-1,1}^{\text{eq}}(\tau)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{-1,1}(\tau)\,,

(67)

\displaystyle\pi_{~1,2}

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{1,2}(\tau_{0})-e^{-\xi_{0}}\rho_{1,2}^{\text{eq}}(\tau)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{1,2}(\tau)\,.

(68)

However we see that a complete closure is not possible as the free streaming initial conditions
e^{-\xi_{0}}e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{-1,1}(\tau_{0})
cannot be captured solely by gradient terms. Nonetheless, if

\frac{e^{-\xi_{0}}\left(e^{-\mathbf{\hat{K}}(\tau,\tau_{0})}\rho_{n,l}(\tau_{0})-\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}(\tau)\right)}{\pi_{n,l}}\ll 1

(69)

for
(n,l)=(-1,1),~(1,2)
, we can approximately close the system and write,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\Pi

\displaystyle+\frac{\Pi}{\tau_{R}}=\frac{\Pi}{\tau}-\mathbf{\hat{D}}P

\displaystyle+\frac{m^{2}}{3}\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{-1,1}(\tau)\right]\,,

(70)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\pi

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{\tau_{R}}=-(4-c_{s}^{2})\frac{\pi}{\tau}+2(2-c_{s}^{2})\frac{\Pi}{\tau}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{1,2}(\tau)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{m^{2}}{3}\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{-1,1}(\tau)\right]\,.

(71)

These expressions coincide with those obtained from the gradient expansion, with the crucial difference that the transport coefficients are rescaled by non-perturbative corrections arising from the
\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})
terms. Furthermore, the contributions
\left[\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\rho^{\text{eq}}_{n,l}
can always be expressed in terms of the hydrodynamic variables
T
,
\mu
,
\Pi
, and
\pi
(see Appendix (
B.3
) for explicit formulas). This provides a rigorous understanding of the re-normalized transport coefficients proposed in previous studies
Blaizot and Yan 2018
;
Blaizot and Yan 2021b
.

The key takeaway from the analysis is that

Hydrodynamic evolution

\displaystyle\sim\textit{gradient expansion}

\displaystyle+\textit{ modified transport coefficients }

and the accuracy of this hydrodynamic approximation is quantified by the smallness of the ratio in (
69
).

V
Generalisation to
3+1
D

The extension of our argument to the full
3+1
D case rests on two key observations drawn from the
0+1
D system. First, the evolution equations governing the exact non-equilibrium components structurally resemble those governing the gradient expansion terms. Second, the non-perturbative, exponentially decaying corrections that carry initial data can be written in terms of scaled versions of the local gradients. In this section, we demonstrate how these properties persist in the
3+1
D framework and explore their implications for formulating hydrodynamic equations.

V.1
The linearized moment equations

Motivated by the moment evolution equations of the Boltzmann equation, we consider a generalized relaxation-type model,

\left[\partial_{\tau}+\hat{\textbf{F}}\right]\vec{\rho}=-\hat{\textbf{C}}\vec{\rho}\,,

(72)

which preserves its essential features. We note that the structure of the linearized moment equations parallels that of the Boltzmann equation itself. This equation can be thought of as a moment version of the RTA equation with
\hat{{\mathbf{C}}}[\vec{\rho}]=\hat{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}\vec{\rho}-\hat{{\mathbf{G}}}\vec{\rho}
. This has the added advantage of being able to incorporate multiple time scales and therefore more generalized collision kernels. This structure typically emerges in momentum dependent RTA models
Rocha et al. 2021
;
Biswas et al. 2022
;
Gangadharan et al. 2025
.

The spectrum of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
should be positive semi-definite much like that of the spectrum of the linearized Boltzmann collision kernel. Since
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
acts on an infinite-dimensional vector space, its spectrum typically contains a continuous component. The kernel of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
comprises the five conserved moments associated with energy, momentum, and number conservation. Additionally, within the moment space, there is one more null vector corresponding to the equilibrium moments, satisfying
\hat{\mathbf{C}}[\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}]=0
. When the system is truncated, the collision kernel
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{N}
becomes finite dimensional and its spectrum discrete.
The operator
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is positive definite and invertible, as it corresponds to the collision frequency term projected into the moment space—generally non-zero. In the Anderson–Witting form of the RTA,
\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
reduces to
\hat{\mathbf{I}}/\tau_{R}
with
\hat{{\mathbf{G}}}\vec{\rho}\to\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}
, with a single eigenmode and decay rate given by the relaxation time
\tau_{R}
.

Here
\mathbf{\hat{F}}
is the moment space Liouville operator. The operator
\mathbf{\hat{F}}
has a matrix structure
Rocha and Denicol 2021
;
de Brito and Denicol 2023
;
de Brito et al. 2024b
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{F}}=\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}+\mathbf{A}\,

(73)

where
\nabla^{\mu}
are the commoving derivatives and
\mathbf{F}_{\mu}
,
\mathbf{A}
are matrix(linear) operators that couples the moments.

V.2
Hydrodynamics in
3+1
D

For simplicity, we limit our analysis to the RTA collision kernel, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{R}=\hat{\mathbf{I}}/\tau_{R}
. Most of the discussion in this section does not rely on this specific form of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}
; it only requires that the operator be invertible with positive definite eigenvalues. However, certain explicit results—such as the modifications to the transport coefficients—are derived under the assumption of the AW-RTA form.

Under a general relaxation time model, the evolution equation for the moments is,

\mathbf{\hat{D}}\vec{\rho}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}\left(\vec{\rho}-\vec{\rho}^{~eq}\right)\,,

(74)

where
\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\left[\partial_{\tau}+\hat{\textbf{F}}\right]
.

Decomposing the moments into equilibrium and non-equilibrium parts,
\vec{\rho}=\vec{\rho}^{~eq}+\vec{\pi}
, the evolution of the non-equilibrium contribution becomes

\mathbf{\hat{D}}\vec{\pi}(\tau)=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}~\vec{\pi}-\mathbf{\hat{D}}\vec{\rho}^{~eq}\,.

(75)

We further split
\vec{\pi}
into a gradient expansion piece and a non-perturbative piece,
\vec{\pi}=\vec{\pi}^{G}+\vec{\pi}^{T}
. The iterative gradient expansion yields

\vec{\pi}^{G}(\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~eq}(\tau)\,.

(76)

As
\vec{\pi}^{G}
satisfies (
75
), the non-perturbative terms should satisfy,

\mathbf{\hat{D}}{\vec{\pi}^{T}}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}~\vec{\pi}^{T}\,.

(77)

This shows that even in 3+1D the structural equivalence between the gradient expansion and the exact anisotropy evolution continues to hold. The next step is to demonstrate that the exact evolution can be reproduced by suitably modifying the transport coefficients. To this end, we construct a formal solution of the moment evolution equation.

The moment equation (
74
) is a linear time-evolution equation structurally similar to the Schrödinger equation. A formal solution can therefore be constructed using standard perturbative methods familiar from quantum mechanics. The extension to this setting, however, introduces new subtleties. In particular, unlike in the
0+1
D case presented earlier, the operators
\hat{\textbf{F}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}
generally do not commute, as reflected in the non-vanishing commutator
[\mathbf{F}_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu},\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}]\neq 0
.

V.2.1
Solutions to the moment equation

Consider the free streaming equation,

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+\mathbf{\hat{F}}\right]\vec{\rho}=0,

(78)

for which we can write down a propagator which satisfies,

\partial_{\tau}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}=-\mathbf{\hat{F}}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\,.

(79)

The free streaming solution can be written as,

\vec{\rho}^{~F}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\vec{\rho}(\tau_{0})

(80)

Now consider the damped equation,

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\tau}+\mathbf{\hat{F}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}\right]\vec{\rho}=0\,.

(81)

To solve (
81
), we treat the damping term
\hat{\mathbf{\nu}}_{R}
as an “interaction,” in analogy with the interaction (or collision) picture. We introduce the collision–picture moments

\vec{\rho}_{c}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\vec{\rho}(\tau)\,,

(82)

together with the transformed operator

\hat{\mathbf{\nu}}_{c}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}~\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}(\tau)~\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\,.

(83)

In this representation, the evolution equation becomes,

\left[\partial_{\tau}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}\right]~\vec{\rho}_{c}(\tau)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}~\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}_{c}(\tau)

(84)

Its solution is given by

\vec{\rho}_{c}{(\tau)}=\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}~\vec{\rho}_{c}{(\tau_{0})}+\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}~\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}~\vec{\rho}_{c}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})\,,

(85)

where
\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}=\mathcal{T}e^{-\int_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}}
denotes the time–ordered exponential propagator satisfying,

\partial_{\tau}\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}~\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\,.

(86)

We now consider the case where
[\mathbf{F}{\mu}\nabla^{\mu},\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}]=0

holds at all times. In this situation the dynamics simplify, closely resembling the familiar
0+1
D case. In particular, we have
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{c}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{R}
, and the propagator reduces to

\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}=e^{-\xi^{\prime}}

(87)

, with
\xi^{\prime}=\int_{\tau^{\prime}}^{\tau}\frac{d\tau^{\prime\prime}}{\tau_{R}}
. The solution in the Schrödinger picture then takes the form

\displaystyle\vec{\rho}(\tau)=e^{-\xi_{0}}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\vec{\rho}(\tau_{0})+\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau’\,\frac{e^{-\xi’}}{\tau_{R}}\,\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau’}\,\vec{\rho}^{\text{eq}}(\tau’).

(88)

From this we can obtain the series expansion,

\displaystyle\vec{\rho}(\tau)

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi_{0}}\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\vec{\rho}(\tau_{0})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k},\gamma(k+1,\xi_{0})}{k!}\left[-\tau_{R}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau).

(89)

Thus, the analysis of the commuting case proceeds in direct analogy with the
0+1
D result. The solution (
V.2.1
) in the commuting case is obtained by performing the coordinate transformation
[\tau’,\tau]\to[\xi’,0]
and expanding the integrand functions in a Taylor series in
\xi’
. This procedure relies on
\xi’
being monotonic and commuting with
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
, allowing it to act as a scalar parameter in
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{0,\xi’}
. In the non-commuting case, such a simplification is no longer possible, and the modification of transport coefficients cannot be expressed in the same compact form. However we can still get expressions for the gradient and non-perturbative decompositions.

Using the hydrodynamic generator in (
85
) together with the propagator property (
86
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}~\vec{\rho}_{c}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}~\vec{\rho}_{c}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})\Bigg|_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}

\displaystyle-\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}d\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-1}_{c}\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}\vec{\rho}_{c}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau^{\prime})\,.

(90)

From this it follows that (Appendix (
C.1
)),

\displaystyle\vec{\rho}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau)

\displaystyle+\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}~\left(\vec{\rho}{(\tau_{0})}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}~\left[-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau_{0})\right)\,.

(91)

We can now write down an explicit expression for the non-perturbative component,

\vec{\pi}^{T}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau,\tau_{0}}\left(\vec{\pi}(\tau_{0})-\vec{\pi}^{G}(\tau_{0})\right)\,,

(92)

where
\vec{\pi}(\tau_{0})=\vec{\rho}(\tau_{0})-\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau_{0})
and the term in the bracket is the initial non-perturbative anisotropy
\vec{\pi}^{T}(\tau_{0})=\vec{\pi}(\tau_{0})-\vec{\pi}^{G}(\tau_{0})
mirroring the
0+1
D expression.

For a solution that is analytic in
\tau
, each gradient term,
\left[-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau_{0})
in
\vec{\pi}^{G}(\tau_{0})
can be written in terms of the corresponding terms
\left[-\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\right]^{k}\vec{\rho}^{~\text{eq}}(\tau)
via a Taylor expansion about
\tau
. The Taylor coefficients then gives the appropriate modifications to the transport coefficients to the gradient expansion. Deriving an explicit form analogous to the
0+1
D solution (
62
) or the commuting case (
V.2.1
) is, however, algebraically involved. In Appendix (
C.2
), we present these expressions explicitly for the Anderson–Witting kernel under suitable simplifying assumptions.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we showed that solutions of the Boltzmann equation and its simplified models share a universal structural form, decomposing into a Chapman–Enskog gradient contribution and an exponentially decaying non-perturbative modes. This separation reflects the presence of two fixed points of the collision dynamics, namely the collisionless regime and local thermal equilibrium, where the collision kernel vanishes. The gradient contribution describes the behavior near equilibrium, while the non-perturbative terms encodes the approach to the collisionless regime. A consistent description of collective dynamics in interacting systems requires interpolation between these two limits.

Israel–Stewart theory and its generalizations were originally developed to resolve the causality issues inherent in the gradient expansion. These prescriptions promote non-equilibrium contributions to independent fields that relax to the equilibrium in finite time. We demonstrated that the resulting equations, constructed from the gradient expansion, are structurally similar to the exact evolution equations of the anisotropies, and thus naturally accommodate non-perturbative dynamics. Furthermore, we showed that free-streaming behavior can be effectively reproduced within the gradient expansion, provided that transport coefficients are appropriately modified at each order. In this way, our work confirms—and extends through purely analytical methods—the results reported in
Blaizot and Yan 2018
;
Blaizot and Yan 2020
;
Blaizot and Yan 2021a
;
Blaizot and Yan 2021b
;
Jaiswal et al. 2022b
.

Relativistic hydrodynamics therefore emerges as an interpolation between free streaming and collective dynamics, rather than a strict expansion around equilibrium. In the case of quark–gluon plasma simulations, transport coefficients are typically tuned to reproduce experimental observations. The ability of relativistic hydrodynamics to connect the early-time, weakly interacting regime with the late-time equilibrium regime may therefore underlie its remarkable success in describing far-from-equilibrium systems such as the quark–gluon plasma.
