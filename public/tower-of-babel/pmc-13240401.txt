Real‐World Data of Second‐Line Amrubicin Monotherapy in Patients With Extensive‐Stage Small Cell Lung Cancer Who Received First‐Line Chemoimmunotherapy or Chemotherapy: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Kei Sonehara, Toshiharu Tsutsui, Shuhei Nozawa, Toshihiko Agatsuma, Manabu Yamamoto, Akemi Matsuo, Masanori Nakanishi, Tomoshige Chiaki, Akane Kato, Takashige Miyahara, Tsutomu Hachiya, Shintaro Kanda, Taro Hirabayashi, Katsuya Yanagisawa, Taisuke Araki, Kazunari Tateishi, Masayuki Hanaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChemoimmunotherapy is widely used as the first‐line treatment for extensive‐stage small cell lung cancer (ES‐SCLC), but treatment options for second‐line have not changed. Amrubicin monotherapy is used as the standard treatment for relapsed SCLC, but since chemoimmunotherapy became an additional indication for ES‐SCLC, the efficacy and safety of second‐line amrubicin have not been sufficiently investigated.MethodsWe enrolled a total of 131 relapsed SCLC patients who received second‐line amrubicin at eleven institutions in Japan between August 2019 and June 2023. We retrospectively examined the efficacy and safety of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy.ResultsTwenty‐five (19.1%) and 106 patients (80.9%) had sensitive and refractory relapse, respectively. 51 (38.9%) and 80 (61.1%) patients received first‐line chemoimmunotherapy and first‐line chemotherapy, respectively. The median progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 3.6 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 3.1–4.1 months) and 7.9 months (95% CI, 6.7–9.1 months), respectively. The median PFS and OS were significantly longer in the sensitive group compared with the refractory group (PFS: 6.4 vs. 3.5 months, p = 0.008, OS: 9.4 vs. 6.4 months, p = 0.021, respectively). Treatment‐related adverse events were as follows: Grade 4 neutropenia in 51 patients (38.9%), grade 3 or higher febrile neutropenia in 18 patients (13.7%), and all grade interstitial lung disease in 13 patients (9.9%). Treatment‐related death was 1 patient (0.8%).ConclusionSecond‐line amrubicin monotherapy for relapsed SCLC may be useful and well‐tolerated as a treatment option after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of lung cancers, and approximately 70% of cases at the time of diagnosis are extensive stage (ES) with metastatic lesions [1, 2] Platinum‐based chemotherapy has long been the main first‐line treatment for ES‐SCLC, but the prognosis was poor, with a median overall survival (OS) of 10–12 months [3, 4, 5] Recently, two phase III studies reported that the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy (platinum‐based chemotherapy plus anti‐programmed death ligand‐1 (PD‐L1) antibody, such as atezolizumab or durvalumab) for ES‐SCLC was superior to platinum‐based chemotherapy [6, 7] Furthermore, with the introduction of chemoimmunotherapy, long‐term responses can now be expected even in ES‐SCLC, similar to those in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [8, 9] In clinical practice, compared with conventional chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy was associated with increased OS in ES‐SCLC patients [10].

However, the concept of subsequent treatment after chemoimmunotherapy has not changed from conventional methods. It has been reported that the overall response rate (ORR) of second‐line treatment is high in cases of relapsed SCLC in which the period until recurrence is long after the first‐line chemotherapy, and treatment is selected by dividing patients into two groups: Those with a long period until recurrence (sensitive relapse) and those with a short period until recurrence (refractory relapse) [11].

Amrubicin monotherapy has been shown to be effective in both the sensitive and refractory groups as a second‐line treatment for relapsed SCLC. Amrubicin hydrochloride is a third‐generation anthracycline and a potent topoisomerase II inhibitor. First, in the sensitive relapsed SCLC, a randomized controlled phase II trial showed that overall survival (OS) was equivalent to that of the standard treatment, topotecan, and a systematic review of nine trials also showed that the efficacy for Japanese patients was good, with an ORR of 61% and a 1‐year OS rate of 51% [12, 13] Second, in the refractory relapsed SCLC, a phase II trial in Japan showed an ORR of 32.9% and a median OS of 8.9 months, and a systematic review of nine trials also showed that the efficacy for Japanese patients was good, with an ORR of 38% and a 1‐year OS rate of 34% [13, 14].

In clinical practice, a previous retrospective study reported that the median progression‐free survival (PFS) and OS in 320 relapsed SCLC patients who received second‐line amrubicin monotherapy were 2.6 and 13.6 months, respectively [15] In a database study examining treatment regimens for ES‐SCLC from a Japanese database (hospital‐based and payer‐based), the most selected regimen for second‐line treatment was amrubicin monotherapy (59.7%–68.7%). In addition, even after 2020, when chemoimmunotherapy became the standard first‐line treatment for ES‐SCLC, amrubicin monotherapy was selected in 60.3%–73.1% of cases in which second‐line treatment was administered [16] There are two retrospective studies in Japan that have examined the efficacy and safety of amrubicin monotherapy after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy for ES‐SCLC. A previous study examining the efficacy of 40 relapsed SCLC patients who received amrubicin monotherapy after carboplatin plus etoposide plus atezolizumab reported the ORR of 32.5%, median PFS of 3.4 months, and median OS of 9.9 months. Regarding toxicity, the incidence of febrile neutropenia (FN) was 10%, and that of interstitial lung disease (ILD) was 2.5% [17].

Another previous study examining the efficacy of 89 relapsed SCLC patients who received amrubicin monotherapy after chemoimmunotherapy reported the ORR of 29.2% and a median PFS of 2.99 months. The ORR and PFS in the sensitive relapse group were significantly better than in the refractory relapse group. Regarding toxicity, the incidence of FN was 13.5%, and that of ILD was 6.7% [18].

As mentioned above, there are reports examining the efficacy and safety of amrubicin monotherapy after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy, but even after chemoimmunotherapy became an additional indication for ES‐SCLC, there are many cases in which platinum‐based chemotherapy is selected as the first‐line treatment due to reasons such as older age, interstitial pneumonia (IP), and poor performance status (PS). The purpose of this study was to examine the efficacy and safety of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy in ES‐SCLC patients who received first‐line chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy, divided into two groups based on the relapse pattern (sensitive relapse or refractory relapse) following first‐line treatment used in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This multicenter retrospective study was approved by the Shinshu University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (approval no. 6076). One hundred thirty‐one ES‐SCLC patients who received amrubicin monotherapy as second‐line treatment after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy as first‐line treatment between August 1, 2019, and June 30, 2023, are enrolled in this study. All data relevant to this study were extracted from electronic medical records in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. An opt‐out procedure was adopted where patients could refuse to participate by filling out a form on the website of all institutions participating in this study. All patient data in this study were anonymized. This study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN 000053134).

Data Collection

Patient data were retrospectively collected and evaluated at the induction of first‐line treatment. The best objective response to amrubicin was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (version 1.1). The ORR was defined as complete response (CR) plus partial response (PR). The disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the ORR plus stable disease (SD). The PFS was calculated from the induction of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy to the date of progressive disease (PD). The OS was calculated from the induction date of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy to the date of death or last follow‐up visit. Regarding the relapse pattern, sensitive relapse (sensitive group) was defined as a response to first‐line treatment and relapse more than 90 days after completion of first‐line treatment. Refractory relapse (refractory group) was defined as no response to first‐line treatment and relapse within 90 days after completion of first‐line treatment. Treatment‐related adverse events (TRAEs) were evaluated using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Assessing the efficacy and toxicity was conducted at the discretion of the attending physician.

Statistical Analysis

The cutoff date for the outcome analysis was April 30, 2024. Comparisons of categorial variables associated with clinical findings between sensitive and refractory groups were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. The PFS and OS analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method. with log‐rank tests. The log‐rank test was used for intergroup survival comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The prognostic predictors associated with PFS and OS were identified using the univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard analysis. Significant variables (p < 0.05) from the univariate analysis and clinically appropriate and relevant, were included in the multivariate analysis. We used the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analyses.

Discussion

Herein, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy for ES‐SCLC, and suggested that the relapse pattern (sensitive relapse or refractory pattern) is a useful factor in predicting the efficacy of amrubicin, as previously reported.

ES‐SCLC would be expected to be effective with both first‐line chemoimmunotherapy and chemotherapy, but subsequent treatment options after recurrence are scarce. In sensitive‐relapsed SCLC, the efficacy of topotecan monotherapy, cisplatin plus etoposide plus irinotecan, carboplatin plus etoposide, and amrubicin monotherapy has been shown in previous clinical trials, but in refractory‐relapsed SCLC, the significance of drug therapy has not been established, and only reports have shown the efficacy of amrubicin monotherapy [14, 19, 20, 21, 22]. In particular, regarding the relapse pattern of ES‐SCLC, refractory‐relapsed SCLC accounts for 70%–86.5% of cases in previous studies, and 80.9% of cases in this study were refractory‐relapsed SCLC. Based on the above, amrubicin, which has been suggested to be effective in cases of refractory relapse, is a key drug for relapsed SCLC [17, 18] This study is the first to report on second‐line amrubicin monotherapy after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy and chemotherapy since chemoimmunotherapy became the standard first‐line treatment for ES‐SCLC; similar to previous studies, the relapse pattern might be a predictor associated with the efficacy of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy. The ORR, DCR, and PFS in the sensitive group were significantly higher than those in the refractory group. These results suggested that the trend was similar to that observed before the introduction of chemoimmunotherapy.

It was also suggested that the efficacy of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy may not be related to the type of first‐line treatment. In NSCLC patients who received docetaxel plus ramucirumab, it has been reported that docetaxel plus ramucirumab was more effective in patients performed post immunotherapy than in patients performed before immunotherapy [23, 24] Possible mechanisms of the immunomodulatory effects of chemotherapy include depletion of T regulatory cells (Tregs), enhancement of antigen‐specific immune responses, enhanced antigen presentation and processing [25, 26] However, in SCLC, it has not been suggested that immunotherapy may not be effective due to a lack of antigen presentation and the tumor microenvironment (low PD‐L1 expression and low CD8+ T cells), which is different from those of NSCLC [27] In this study, the PFS in the chemotherapy and chemoimmunotherapy groups were 3.8 and 3.6 months, respectively, and there was no significant difference (HR 0.96 [95% CI, 0.64–1.43], p = 0.835). This study is the first to report that the efficacy of amrubicin monotherapy for relapsed SCLC was not related to whether or not it was administered concomitantly with prior immune checkpoint inhibitor.

In the multivariate analysis of PFS and OS, sensitive relapse was an independent, favorable prognostic predictor associated with PFS but not with OS. Regarding the OS, only PS was an independent prognostic predictor. The reason why the relapse pattern was not an independent prognostic predictor associated with OS was likely due to the efficacy of subsequent therapy. However, the ORR and DCR were significantly higher in the sensitive group, and sensitive relapse was an independent, favorable prognostic predictor associated with PFS, suggesting that the relapse pattern may be a useful marker for predicting the efficacy of second‐line amrubicin monotherapy. A previous report has shown that amrubicin is a useful treatment option for third‐line treatment of relapsed SCLC [28]. In the future, especially in sensitive‐relapsed SCLC, it will be necessary to consider whether the treatment strategy is first‐line chemoimmunotherapy followed by platinum‐based chemotherapy or followed by amrubicin monotherapy.

Regarding TRAEs, the incidence of grade 4 neutropenia was 38.9%, and the incidence of grade 3 or higher FN was 13.7%, which were high as previously reported. A previous study reported second‐line amrubicin monotherapy with P‐PEG reduced the incidence of FN (P‐PEG group: 4.8%, non‐P‐PEG group: 30.8%, p = 0.02) [29]. In this study, P‐PEG was used in 42 patients (33.9%). The incidence of FN in the P‐PEG group was 3 patients (7.1%), and the incidence of FN in the non‐P‐PEG group was 12 patients (14.6%), with no significant difference (p = 0.226) (Table S1). Although it is possible that the fact that the starting dose of amrubicin varied from case to case may have had an influence, the incidence of FN in the P‐PEG group was lower than in a previous clinical trial, so it is necessary to accumulate more cases and examine the utility of P‐PEG [30].

The incidence of ILD in patients with IP was 4 (22.2%), and the incidence of patients without IP was 9 (8.0%), with no significant difference (p = 0.081) (Table S2). A previous study for 100 SCLC patients who received amrubicin monotherapy reported the incidence of ILD in patients with IP was 3 (3/12: 33.3%), and the incidence of patients without IP was 3 (3/88: 3%) [31] Although no significant difference was found, the incidence of ILD in patients with IP was high (22.2%), so amrubicin should be used with caution in patients with IP as before.

In this study, the survival time from the induction date of first‐line treatment the date of death or last follow‐up visit in all patients, the sensitive group, and the refractory group were 13.9 months (95% CI, 13.6–15.3 months), 18.2 months (95% CI, 16.5–19.9 months), and 12.2 months (95% CI, 10.2–14.2 months), respectively (Figure S1). The prognosis of ES‐SCLC remains poor even in cases where second‐line amrubicin monotherapy can be administered. In the future, tarlatamab, which is a bispecific T‐cell engager immunotherapy targeting delta‐like ligand 3 and CD3, will be available for relapsed SCLC patients, and it is expected to improve the prognosis, but as there is a lack of data on clinical practice, it is necessary to accumulate more cases and examine [32].

This study had some limitations. First, it was a multicenter retrospective study with a small sample size in a limited area. Second, the selection of amrubicin is at the discretion of the attending physician, so there is selection bias. Third, we could not evaluate the quality of life of patients.

In conclusion, second‐line amrubicin for relapsed SCLC may be useful and well‐tolerated as a treatment option after first‐line chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy. Also, it was suggested that the relapse pattern after first‐line treatment, regardless of whether or not it was used in combination with an anti‐PD‐L1 antibody, is a useful predictor of the efficacy of amrubicin.
