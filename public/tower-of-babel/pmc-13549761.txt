Bisphosphonate therapy for thalassemia-related bone disorders in children and young adults: a systematic review
Authors: Ghea Mangkuliguna, I Wayan Andrew Handisurya, Nicholas Adrianto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkeletal complications represent a major morbidity in transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT), particularly among children and young adults. Osteopenia and osteoporosis arise from marrow expansion, iron overload, endocrine dysfunction, and vitamin D deficiency, leading to impaired bone mass accrual. Although bisphosphonates are widely used in osteoporosis, their role in thalassemia has not been systematically appraised. This review aims to evaluate the role of bisphosphonates in pediatric and young adult patients with TDT-related bone disorders. A systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Cochrane Library, ProQuest, and gray literature was conducted from inception to August 2025. Eligible studies included randomized, nonrandomized, and prospective observational trials evaluating bisphosphonates in patients ≤25 years with TDT-related osteopenia or osteoporosis. Primary outcomes were changes in lumbar spine and hip bone mineral density (BMD). Secondary outcomes included biochemical markers, bone pain, fracture incidence, and safety. The review followed PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251138498). Eleven nonrandomized prospective studies involving over 300 participants were included. Six studies evaluated pamidronate, 3 zoledronate, and 2 alendronate. Pamidronate generally improved lumbar spine and hip BMD, with more consistent gains when combined with calcium/vitamin D. Zoledronate was associated with BMD improvement but required pediatric-adjusted dosing to avoid hypocalcemia. Alendronate improved BMD z-scores and reduced bone pain in adolescents. Across studies, bisphosphonates lowered bone turnover markers and were generally well tolerated. Bisphosphonates may improve bone density and alleviate pain in TDT-related bone disease. Larger randomized controlled trials are required to determine long-term efficacy, fracture outcomes, and optimal treatment regimens.Keywords: Bisphosphonate, Bone disorder, Systematic review, Transfusion-dependent thalassemia

Highlights

· Skeletal complications are a significant source of morbidity in patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT).

· Bisphosphonates are a mechanistically rational, well-tolerated, and potentially beneficial treatment for TDT-associated osteoporosis.

· Calcium and vitamin D coadministration may optimize skeletal benefits.

Introduction

Transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) affects an estimated 1.3 million people worldwide, with more than 120,000 new cases each year, most commonly in Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean. Advances in transfusion and chelation have extended survival, but children and young adults continue to face substantial morbidity [1,2], including a high prevalence of skeletal complications ranging from 15% to 82% [3,4]. These complications may manifest during early childhood, with signs such as low bone mineral density (BMD), growth retardation, and biochemical abnormalities. More definitive skeletal manifestations, including vertebral fractures and overt osteoporosis, typically emerge in the second decade of life [4,5]. The mechanisms arise from a complex interplay of ineffective erythropoiesis, iron overload (and chelation toxicity), endocrine dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies. These factors collectively disrupt bone remodeling, impair peak bone mass acquisition, and lead to early-onset osteoporosis and deformities [6,7]. Notably, patients in low- and middle-income countries face higher complication rates due to limited access to optimal transfusion and chelation [1,2].

Bisphosphonates, potent inhibitors of osteoclastic bone resorption through farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase inhibition, are established treatments for osteoporosis [8,9]. Emerging evidence suggests they may also benefit patients with TDT by improving bone density, reducing bone pain, and potentially lowering fracture risk [10-12]. The current evidence, however, is limited and heterogeneous. Most available studies are small, single-center, and nonrandomized, with considerable variation in the type of bisphosphonate used, treatment schedules, supplementation practices, and duration of follow-up [13]. Data specifically addressing children and young adults with TDT are particularly scarce. As a result, comparisons across studies are difficult, and the long-term impact on fracture prevention, functional outcomes, and safety remains uncertain. This systematic review aims to evaluate the role of bisphosphonate therapy in children and young adults with TDT-related bone disorders.

Materials and methods

1. Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Cochrane Library, ProQuest and gray literature sources, such as ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN (International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number), Google Scholar, and WorldCat, from inception to 31 August 2025. The search combined keywords and MeSH (medical subject headings) terms (Supplementary Table 1) related to bisphosphonates (e.g., pamidronate, zoledronate, alendronate) and thalassemia (e.g., β-thalassemia major, TDT). No language restrictions were applied. Two reviewers (GM, NA) independently screened the records. Duplicates were removed, and titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance. Full texts were retrieved for potentially eligible studies and assessed against predefined inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (IH) providing arbitration when required. Reasons for exclusion were recorded, and the study selection process is outlined in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) flow diagram. The diagram provides an overview of the search strategy and selection process used to identify articles eligible for inclusion.

Studies were eligible if they evaluated bisphosphonate therapy in subjects aged 25 years or younger with TDT who received regular transfusions requiring at least 8 transfusions per year over a minimum of 12 months or transfusions every 2–4 weeks or 100–150 mL/kg/yr of blood infusion and were on chelation therapy by standard protocol [14,15]. Bone disease was defined according to the World Health Organization criteria as osteopenia, with a BMD z-score between -1.0 and -2.5, or osteoporosis, with a BMD z-score of -2.5 or lower [16]. Randomized and nonrandomized controlled trials as well as prospective observational studies were included. Case reports, reviews, commentary articles, and studies without available full text were excluded. Eligible comparators included placebo, standard of care, or no treatment. The primary outcomes of interest were changes in BMD at the lumbar spine and hip, expressed either as absolute BMD values or as z-scores. Secondary outcomes included changes in biochemical markers of bone metabolism, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcium, phosphate, and vitamin D, together with clinical outcomes including fracture incidence, bone pain, and other reported adverse events.

This review adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines [17]. Ethical approval was not required because the analysis relied on previously published data. The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251138498).

2. Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by 3 reviewers (GM, IH, NA). Extracted information included author, year of publication, geographical setting, study design, sample size, baseline participant characteristics, transfusion and chelation regimens, intervention details including bisphosphonate type, dosage, frequency, duration, and any supplementation, comparator groups where applicable, and reported outcomes. Where relevant data were missing, study authors were contacted. Disagreements in extraction were resolved by consensus.

A narrative synthesis was utilized to systematically integrate and interpret the findings. This method enabled a comprehensive evaluation of the role of bisphosphonates in thalassemia-induced bone disorders while accounting for the diverse methodologies and patient populations studied. The results were organized thematically, focusing primarily on clinical efficacy and safety. Findings were presented using descriptive summaries and tabular formats to compare study characteristics and outcomes (Table 1, Supplementary Table 2). Tables outlined key details for each study, including sample sizes, interventions, and reported results, facilitating a clear understanding of trends across the literature. Accompanying narrative descriptions provided context, offering insights into the broader implications of the data.

3. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) tool [18]. This instrument evaluates 7 domains of potential bias, which include confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each domain was judged as carrying a low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias. Three reviewers (GM, IH, NA) performed these assessments independently, with consensus used to resolve discrepancies. The overall results were summarized graphically using the robvis tool [19].

Results

1. Search results

The database search identified 581 records. After removal of duplicates and irrelevant titles, 36 articles were retained for full-text review. Eleven studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis [10-12,20-27]. Reviews and case reports or case series were excluded. Studies that mostly employed adult subjects were also omitted. The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

2. Characteristics of included studies

This review included 11 nonrandomized prospective trials enrolling over 300 subjects that evaluated the use of bisphosphonates in patients with TDT with skeletal complications. The studies were conducted across diverse geographical regions, primarily in the Middle East and Asia, and encompassed pediatric, adolescent, and young adult populations. Both male and female patients were represented. All patients were transfusion-dependent and received iron chelation therapy, while several cohorts also reported associated endocrine dysfunctions (e.g., hypogonadism, delayed puberty) [10,11,23] or vitamin D deficiency [20,22,26].

Six studies investigated pamidronate [10,11,20,22,23, 26], 3 evaluated zoledronic acid [12,24,25], and 2 examined alendronate [21,27]. Pamidronate was administered intravenously at intervals of 1 to 3 months, with singleinfusion doses between 15 mg and 30 mg, often in combination with calcium and vitamin D. Zoledronic acid was given intravenously at doses ranging from 1 mg to 4 mg every 3 months. Alendronate was administered orally at a daily dose of 10 mg or weekly dose of 25 mg. Treatment duration ranged from 6 months to 3 years. Comparators included untreated controls or standard care with calcium and vitamin D. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Overall, the risk of bias across studies was judged as moderate to serious. The most common concern was confounding, as none of the trials used randomization and only a few employed methods to adjust for potential confounders [10,11,21,23-27]. Participant selection, intervention classification, and adherence were generally well described, though one trial raised concerns due to mid-study dose modification. Missing data were limited, though some studies incompletely addressed attrition [24,25]. Most outcomes were assessed using standardized densitometric and biochemical measures, but patient-reported outcomes such as pain were less objective [10-12,24,25,27]. Across studies, absence of prespecified protocols contributed to moderate risk of selective reporting. A detailed assessment of study quality is presented in Fig. 2.

3. Efficacy

Pamidronate therapy was associated with improvements in lumbar spine bone density across multiple studies. In a 2-year pediatric follow-up by Patıroğlu et al. [10], mean L1–L4 BMD increased from 0.55±0.10 to 0.62±0.12 g/cm2 at 24 months (P=0.001). An earlier 12-month assessment in that study showed a smaller, nonsignificant BMD rise (to 0.58±0.10 g/cm²). In contrast, lumbar spine z-scores did not significantly differ at any time. Another study by Demir et al. [11] observed a significant increase in lumbar BMD after 1 year of quarterly pamidronate (from 0.56±0.11 to 0.59±0.11 g/cm² and z-score from -2.34±0.77 to -2.17±0.88, P<0.05). Correspondingly, Naderi et al. [23] reported lumbar BMD rising from 0.72±0.10 to 0.783±0.10 g/cm² and lumbar spine z-score from -2.98±0.96 to -2.44±0.89 over 12 months (P<0.001). Noroozi et al. [26] found the mean lumbar spine z-score improved from -2.92±1.14 at baseline to -1.95±1.20 after 6 months of low-dose pamidronate (P<0.001). Moreover, El-Hawy and Saleh [20] observed a significant increase in mean spine BMD from 0.71±0.07 to 0.81±0.07 g/cm² and z-score from -3.53±0.55 to -2.10±0.32 following 1 year of pamidronate (P<0.001), whereas a calcium/vitamin D-only group showed minimal improvement (mean BMD from 0.74±0.07 to 0.78±0.07 g/cm² and z-score from -3.17±0.61 to -3.11±0.6, P>0.05). Similar findings were reported by Leung et al. [22], who found that monthly infusion of pamidronate combined with vitamin D and calcium supplementation for 3 years significantly increased (P=0.018) lumbar spine BMD z-score (median [interquartile range, IQR]: -3.01 [-2.63 to -3.89] at baseline and -2.12 [-2.06 to -3.72] at the end of the study).

Similar improvements were documented at the hip region. Patıroğlu et al. [10] reported that femoral neck BMD increased from 0.63±0.07 to 0.68±0.08 g/cm² after 2 years of pamidronate (P=0.002). A 1-year study likewise showed a modest but significant femoral neck BMD gain (0.63±0.12 to 0.68±0.14 g/cm², P<0.05) [11]. Naderi et al. [23] reported the total hip BMD rose from 0.68±0.09 to 0.71±0.10 g/cm² (P=0.015). Consistent with BMD changes, Noroozi et al. [26] documented an average hip z-score increase from -2.00±0.65 to -1.55±0.77 (P=0.01). Naderi et al. [23] noted the femoral neck z-score improving from -1.96±0.727 to -1.47±0.856 (P=0.003) over 12 months. In a 3-year cohort study, only the group receiving pamidronate (in addition to calcium/vitamin D) showed significant gains (P=0.028) in median BMD z-score of the hip from -3.05 (IQR, -2.70 to -3.52) to -2.09 (IQR, -1.85 to -3.25). In contrast, patients on calcium/vitamin D alone had no significant improvement, and untreated controls experienced a decline in hip Z (median worsened over the 3-year period, P=0.034) [22].

Two studies investigated intravenous zoledronic acid in thalassemia-related osteoporosis. Otrock et al. [12] reported that the zoledronate group’s lumbar spine z-score increased from -2.84±0.57 to -2.20±0.76 (P<0.01), and total hip z-score from -1.62±0.59 to -1.34±0.68 (P< 0.01). By contrast, the control group showed no significant change in BMD over 12 months. Another study by Naithani et al. [24] likewise showed significant increases in BMD with zoledronic acid therapy. After 1 year of treatment, there were statistically significant increases in BMD at the lumbar spine (P=0.002), forearm (P=0.04), and hip intertrochanteric region (P=0.041).

Two studies investigated oral alendronate in thalassemia-associated osteoporosis. After 12 months of onceweekly alendronate (25 mg) with calcium supplementation, patients demonstrated significant improvement in BMD. At baseline, 80% met the criteria for osteoporosis and 20% for osteopenia. Following treatment, 25% achieved normal BMD, 35% were osteopenic, and only 40% remained osteoporotic. This redistribution across diagnostic categories was statistically significant (P< 0.05) [27]. Similarly, Elshanshory et al. [21] showed that daily administration of alendronate 10 mg resulted in significant improvement in BMD z-scores. Initially, 60% of the subjects presented with very low BMD (z-score < -2.5) and 40% with low BMD (-1.0 up to -2.4). After 6 months of treatment, 20% showed normal BMD, 50% low BMD, and 30% very low BMD (P<0.05).

In addition to densitometric outcomes, bisphosphonate therapy was associated with reductions in bone pain. Patıroğlu et al. [10] noted an improvement in chronic bone pain during pamidronate therapy. Otrock et al. [12] observed that patients receiving zoledronate experienced a significant decrease in bone pain over the 12-month period. The number of painful sites and the need for analgesics were reduced in the treatment group, whereas controls had no pain relief. Naithani et al. [24] also reported that bone pain resolved in all patients who initially had pain, following zoledronate therapy. Alendronate therapy was also associated with marked reductions in bone pain. Before treatment, 50% of patients experienced bone pain (and 60% had back pain); after 1 year, only 10% reported residual bone or back pain [27].

Biochemical markers of bone metabolism also demonstrated favorable changes. Demir et al. [11] reported a modest decline in PTH levels (from 39.34±20.17 to 32.32±15.58 ng/mL, P<0.05) with pamidronate therapy, while calcium, phosphate, and ALP remained unchanged. In contrast, Naderi et al. [23] observed a large drop in serum ALP from 385.85±89.94 to 251.95±113.66 IU/L (P<0.001) after 12 months. El-Hawy and Saleh [20] similarly found that ALP declined by ~37% in the pamidronate group (351.50±68.07 to 220.40±59.26 IU/L, P<0.001), whereas it remained static in the calcium/vitamin D group. Noroozi et al. [26] reported significant increases in serum phosphate (4.45±1.00 to 5.90±0.85 mg/dL, P=0.001) and a decrease in ALP (333±118.75 to 275.5±167.25 IU/L, P=0.004) over 6 months, with no significant change in calcium and vitamin D levels.

High-dose zoledronic acid led to marked suppression of bone turnover markers. In a study by Otrock et al. [12], serum osteocalcin dropped from 26.54±16.66 to 9.58±7.84 ng/mL (P<0.001) and bone-specific ALP from 59.08± 39.05 to 17.89±12.13 U/L (P<0.001) after 1 year. Meanwhile, Sharma et al. [27] showed that mean serum calcium rose from 8.27±0.77 to 9.26±1.11 mg/dL (P=0.0001) after a year of alendronate therapy. Serum ALP and phosphorus showed a small, nonsignificant increase.

4. Safety

In the 3-year cohort by Leung et al. [22], no cases of osteonecrosis of the jaw or other long-term skeletal complications were observed following treatment with pamidronate. Another study by El-Hawy and Saleh [20] reported that no fractures occurred in either the pamidronate or calcium/vitamin D groups over 12 months, and treated children experienced improved well-being without acute bone complications. Sharma et al. [27] reported that no new fractures occurred following treatment with alendronate. Only 2 out of 40 patients (5%) experienced mild perioral twitching due to transient hypocalcemia symptoms. No patients reported abdominal discomfort or musculoskeletal pain.

Initial infusions of 4 mg of zoledronate (the standard adult dose) were associated with significant toxicity in Naithani et al. [24,25]: 4 patients (≈15%) developed grade 4 hypocalcemia, with 3 exhibiting tetany requiring urgent management. These serious events prompted a protocol amendment to reduce the dose to 1 mg IV every 3 months, which was subsequently well tolerated. At the 1 mg dose, no severe hypocalcemia occurred; only mild, transient postinfusion reactions (low-grade fever, nausea, dizziness) were reported. Otrock et al. [12] similarly reported that zoledronate was generally well tolerated over 12 months. About half of patients experienced mild, transient flu-like symptoms or joint pain after the first infusion, but these did not recur with subsequent doses. Renal impairment was not observed in the treated patients.

Discussion

Studies of bisphosphonates in thalassemia-related bone disease suggested benefits on skeletal outcomes among pediatric patients and young adults. Intravenous pamidronate regimens generally produced significant improvements in lumbar spine and hip BMD and z-scores over follow-up. Zoledronic acid trials likewise demonstrated meaningful increases in bone density at the lumbar spine, hip and forearm regions accompanied by reductions in bone pain. Oral alendronate treatment produced a shift from more severe to milder categories of low bone mass and substantial relief of bone and back pain. Biochemical markers of bone turnover generally changed in a direction consistent with reduced bone resorption and improved mineral metabolism. Overall tolerability was acceptable in pediatric and young adult cohorts, with no signal for osteonecrosis of the jaw, renal toxicity, or new fractures during follow-up; however, initial use of zoledronic acid at standard adult doses was associated with serious hypocalcemia in some patients, necessitating protocol-mandated dose reduction and underscoring the importance of cautious dosing and close biochemical monitoring in this highrisk group. The skeletal benefits observed in this review aligned with previous studies highlighting the efficacy of bisphosphonates as a first-line treatment for thalassemia-related bone disease in the adult population [13,28].

Improvements in lumbar spine density are particularly important, as vertebral trabecular bone is metabolically active and vulnerable to osteoclastic activity; strengthening this region reduces the risk of compression deformities, vertebral fractures, and impaired linear growth. Gains in hip BMD, meanwhile, reflect improvements in cortical bone, which is crucial in preventing fragility fractures in weight-bearing regions later in life [29,30]. Beyond densitometric outcomes, reductions in bone pain consistently reported in pamidronate, alendronate, and zoledronate studies suggest functional benefits that enhance quality of life, physical mobility, and adherence to other aspects of thalassemia care [13]. The successful binding of bisphosphonates to hydroxyapatite crystals and their inhibition of farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase within osteoclasts contributed to the reductions in bone turnover markers such as ALP, osteocalcin, and PTH [31].

Bisphosphonate therapy consistently showed greater efficacy when combined with vitamin D and calcium, reaffirming the fundamental pharmacological necessity of coadministration with potent antiresorptive agents. This standard-of-care practice prevents hungry bone syndrome by ensuring adequate mineral substrate availability for remineralization following suppressed osteoclast activity, while also addressing the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and calcium malabsorption common in TDT due to malnutrition, chelation therapy, and hepatic dysfunction [32,33]. Supplementation helps correct these deficits and appears to enhance the antiresorptive action of bisphosphonates, as reflected in more pronounced improvements in BMD and z-scores in cohorts receiving combined therapy with pamidronate or zoledronate compared with supplementation alone [34,35].

The safety profile of bisphosphonates in TDT patients was generally favorable. Pamidronate was consistently well tolerated, with no renal toxicity, osteonecrosis of the jaw, or severe hypocalcemia reported even after repeated infusions for up to 3 years. Its relative safety may be explained not only by its lower potency and slower infusion schedule, but also by its gradual accumulation in bone, which results in steadier suppression of bone resorption compared with more potent agents [36,37]. Zoledronate is the most potent bisphosphonate, and rapid osteoclast inhibition following standard adult dosing (4 mg) can precipitate acute hypocalcemia and flu-like symptoms, particularly in adolescents with high baseline bone turnover. Reducing the dose to approximately 0.0125–0.025 mg/kg (equivalent to ~1 mg every 3 months) and ensuring adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation substantially lowers these risks while maintaining therapeutic efficacy [38,39]. Alendronate is generally tolerable, though its primary concern relates to gastrointestinal discomfort and musculoskeletal pain. Our findings showed no increased risk of those adverse events. However, adherence among adolescents is frequently suboptimal, which may reduce therapeutic effectiveness [40,41].

Overall, intravenous pamidronate appears to be a reasonable first-line option for growing patients, as it provides consistent antiresorptive activity, has a favorable safety profile, and can be conveniently administered alongside transfusion schedules under clinical supervision. Zoledronate may be reserved for older patients and for severe or refractory cases, or when fewer infusions are preferred, but it should be given at pediatricadjusted doses with appropriate vitamin D and calcium supplementation to minimize the risk of hypocalcemia. Oral alendronate is suitable for patients who are clinically stable, able to follow administration instructions, and have reliable access to monitoring, or in settings where infusion services are not readily available. Regardless of the chosen agent, treatment should be integrated into comprehensive TDT care, including correction of endocrine deficits, maintenance of adequate chelation, and establishment of treat-to-target goals such as achieving a lumbar spine z-score above -2.0 and resolving bone pain. The greatest advantage is expected in adolescents and young adults at high risk of failing to achieve normal peak bone mass, particularly those with hypogonadism, a heavy transfusion burden, or markedly low baseline spinal BMD, as they stand to gain improved vertebral strength, reduced bone pain, and a lower risk of future fractures.

Despite encouraging findings, several limitations warrant consideration. All of the included studies were nonrandomized trials and most had a serious risk of bias, thereby reducing confidence in the effect estimates. The small sample sizes and predominance of uncontrolled before-and-after designs raised concerns about potential overestimation and hampered causal inference. Potential confounding might also arise from the lack of age-related or sex-related adjustments in bone density measurement or bone marker changes, as well as inadequate adjustment for puberty status or sexual maturity rating. Due to these limitations, we were unable to conduct a meta-analysis, preventing quantitative synthesis of results or firm conclusions. Publication bias was also a concern, since small studies with positive results tend to be published while those with null findings often go unreported.

The recommended sites for pediatric dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in evaluating osteoporosis are the lumbar spine and total body less head (TBLH), as these sites offer the most reliable and clinically relevant data in the pediatric population. Alternative sites such as the proximal femur, forearm, and lateral distal femur are used when standard sites are not feasible. The latter options may be relevant to older adolescents and young adults. However, their use in younger, growing children warrants careful consideration due to the limited reference data [42]. In addition, TBLH was not measured as a clinical outcome in our included studies, necessitating careful interpretation of the DXA measurements.

All of the included studies used World Health Organization (WHO) criteria to define osteopenia and osteoporosis, which were extrapolated from adult data. The studies did not employ pediatric-specific criteria as recommended by the International Society for Clinical Densitometry, which defines osteoporosis as: (1) a BMD z-score of negative 2 or lower combined with clinically significant fractures (either 2 or more long bone fractures by age 10 or 3 or more long bone fractures by age 19); or (2) at least 1 vertebral compression fracture without high-energy trauma or local pathology, regardless of BMD z-score [43]. Using WHO adult criteria in the pediatric population may lead to misclassification, as these thresholds do not reflect the normal range of bone density during growth and development. Conversely, children at genuine risk for fractures may be missed if their BMD does not fall below adult thresholds [44].

There were concerns regarding the subjective outcome (bone pain), because all but one study [12] did not report the method of pain assessment. Follow-up periods were generally short, leaving uncertainty about the durability of BMD improvements, the attainment of peak bone mass, and the long-term effect on fracture risk. Fracture outcomes themselves were rarely reported, making it unclear whether gains in bone density translate into meaningful reductions in fracture burden. We also could not exclude the possibility that an increase in absolute BMD, with or without a corresponding z-score improvement, more likely reflects normal bone mass accrual during childhood growth instead of a true therapeutic effect. In addition, safety reporting was very limited, especially with regard to the risk of nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis due to potential hypercalciuria. Functional outcomes such as growth, mobility, and quality of life were infrequently assessed, further limiting the applicability of current evidence to clinical practice.

In summary, bisphosphonate therapy represents a mechanistically sound and clinically beneficial intervention for TDT-associated osteoporosis. Pamidronate appears particularly safe and effective for children and adolescents, while carefully dosed zoledronate and alendronate provide additional options in selected contexts. The low-quality evidence from the current review, however, warrants cautious interpretation. Larger randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up are warranted to define optimal regimens, assess fracture outcomes, and confirm the long-term safety of these therapies in thalassemia.
