Tent transformed order 2 2 nets and quasi-Monte Carlo rules with quadratic error decay
Bernd Käßemodel, Nicolas Nagel, Tino Ullrich

Abstract
We investigate the use of order 2 2 digital nets for quasi-Monte Carlo quadrature of nonperiodic functions with bounded mixed second derivative over the cube. By using the so-called tent transform and its mapping properties we inherit error bounds from the periodic setting. Our analysis is based on decay properties of the multivariate Faber-Schauder coefficients of functions with bounded mixed second weak derivatives. As already observed by Hinrichs, Markhasin, Oettershagen, T. Ullrich (Numerische Mathematik 2016), order 2 2 nets work particularly well on tensorized (periodic) Faber splines. From this we obtain a quadratic decay rate for tent transformed order 2 2 nets also in the nonperiodic setting. This improves the formerly best known bound for this class of point sets by a factor of log ⁡ N \log N . We back up our findings with numerical experiments, even suggesting that the bounds for order 2 2 nets can be improved even further. This particularly indicates that point sets of lower complexity (compared to previously considered constructions) may already give (near) optimal error decay rates for quadrature of functions with second order mixed smoothness.

Introduction

1.1
The quasi-Monte Carlo rule

High-dimensional quadrature is an important research field in numerical analysis and approximation theory. There are various methods and algorithms for this task but the simplest one is arguably the
quasi-Monte Carlo integration rule
. Given a domain
\Omega
together with a normalized measure
\mu
(that is
\mu(\Omega)=1
) we aim to approximate

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in X}f(x)

where
X\subseteq\Omega,\#X=N
is a finite set of evaluation nodes and
f
is a real-valued function over
\Omega
, usually fulfilling some smoothness assumptions. The relative worst case error for this algorithm measures the quality of the point set
X
in
\Omega
.

In this paper we focus on the case where
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional unit cube and
f
is a (not necessarily periodic) function with dominating mixed second order smoothness. Let
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
be the Sobolev space of bounded mixed smoothness
2
as will be defined in Section
2
. The
worst case error
of the quasi-Monte Carlo quadrature rule of a finite set
X\subseteq[0,1]^{d},\#X=N
over this space is given by

e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d}))\coloneqq\sup\limits_{\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|

and the
optimal worst case error
for a given number of points
N
is defined by

e^{\text{QMC}}(N,H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d}))\coloneqq\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq[0,1]^{d}\\
\#X=N\end{subarray}}e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})).

The asymptotic behavior of this quantity over certain function spaces as
N
goes to infinity is a popular and well-studied problem. The classical result in this direction is the Koksma-Hlawka inequality
[
26
,
30
,
32
]
where the norm is given by the Hardy-Krause variation. In this case the worst case error turns out to be the star discrepancy of the point set
X
. This is a general theme and in many cases the worst case error possesses such a geometric interpretation via discrepancy. For more details and asymptotic results on discrepancy see
[
5
,
16
,
36
]
.

1.2
Quasi-Monte Carlo via order
2
nets

Our setting is strongly inspired by
[
11
,
28
]
where the analogous setting for periodic functions (that is where the domain is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
) was investigated. Upper bounds via constructive point sets were considered in
[
12
]
(building on
[
11
]
), where the bound

\displaystyle e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{s}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{ds}}{N^{s}}

(1.1)

via order
s
digital nets
X
was determined. Digital nets use bit-manipulation techniques to generate point sets that distribute themselves well in the cube (in this paper we will only consider nets in base
b=2
). The order parameter indicates a further modification, where we compress high-dimensional nets via digital interlacing to construct point sets with a fine structure that adapts itself to higher smoothness parameters
s
. Details will be discussed in Section
5
. Later on, it was shown in
[
1
]
that one can achieve

\displaystyle e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{s}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{ds/2}}{N^{s}}

via randomly shifted order
s
nets. However, this makes the point set probabilistic and nonconstructive.

The optimal behavior of the worst case error was determined in
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Indeed, for integer smoothness
s\geq 2
it was shown that

\displaystyle e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{s}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{(d-1)/2}}{N^{s}}

(1.2)

for order
2s+1
nets in base
2
(order
2s
if one also considers sufficiently large bases
b>2
), giving an explicit construction. For smoothness
s=2
one thus needs order
5
binary nets according to this method.

For another, randomized setting see
[
13
]
and for recent results concerning lattice constructions see
[
21
]
.

In this paper we will consider a simple modification to order
2
digital nets coming from a periodization procedure called the
tent transform
. For these constructive point sets we will prove, for smoothness
s=2
, a rate of

e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{2d-1}}{N^{2}},

(1.3)

see Theorem
5.2
. This gives an improvement over (
1.1
) for the same order parameter
2
and shows that (
1.1
) is not best possible for constructions based on order
2
nets. Numerical experiments suggest that this bound might even be further improved, see Section
7
. The utility of the tent transform for quasi-Monte Carlo integration with digital nets was already observed in
[
8
]
, although the author there could not determine a logarithmic factor.

Reducing the order of the constructed point sets is of interest for two reasons. First, from a practical point of view, it is easier to construct lower order digital nets than higher order ones. This is due to the construction described in Section
5
, where one needs to start with an
\alpha d
-dimensional digital net to get a point set in
d
dimensions. Since computer implementations of these point sets are highly nontrivial and are usually only available up to dimensions
33

[
33
]
, lowering the order parameter is highly required. For example, using this data base one can generate order
2
nets up to
d=16
dimensions, but order
5
nets only up to
d=6
dimensions.

A second, more theoretical aspect is the question of how complex a point set must be to achieve (asymptotically) the optimal rate. This can be made precise as follows. The point sets considered in
[
24
]
for smoothness
s=2
consist of nodes whose coordinates use
5\log_{2}N
bits in their binary representation. The tent transformed order
2
nets only use
2\log_{2}N-1
bits in the representation of their components. It is thus natural to ask how many bits are necessary to obtain point sets of asymptotically optimal behavior. In general, order
\alpha
digital nets are, in the sense as just described, of complexity
\alpha\log_{2}N
. The question of the required complexity is thus related to the smallest order parameter necessary (depending on the smoothness parameter) for optimal bounds of the worst case error. We are not aware if this aspect has been studied in the existing literature, although it is similar to
[
29
]
, where point sets which can be well approximated by permutations have been determined to be globally optimal for the periodic case in dimension
d=2
and smoothness
s=1
. This can of course be considered, also using other notions of complexity, for other problems in discrepancy and quasi-Monte Carlo theory.

1.3
Faber-Schauder coefficients and tent transform

For functions
f\in H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
possessing second order mixed weak derivatives bounded in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we rigorously prove the following decay property of the Faber-Schauder coefficients
d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)
,
\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d},\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see Section
3.2
. It holds

\sup\limits_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(1.4)

This is our main tool for proving the error bound (
1.3
). The above relation directly implies the corresponding relation for spaces
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
. The left-hand side does not seem to fit properly to right-hand side if one has in mind the corresponding relation for
1/2<s<2
, see
[
28
]
. However, the results in
(
20
, Theorem 1.8)
indicate that the left-hand side might even be an equivalent norm on the space
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}
. We leave this as an open problem.

We further use the boundedness of the tent transform operator

\mathcal{R}:f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\mapsto f(|2x_{1}-1|,\dots,|2x_{d}-1|)\quad(\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d})\,,

in various situations, see Section
4
. Namely, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}&:H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})\rightarrow H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\quad\mbox{(Theorem \ref{thm:FS_charact.} and Theorem \ref{thm:tent})}\\
\mathcal{R}&:H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})\rightarrow\mathbf{E}^{2}_{d}\quad\mbox{(Corollary \ref{cor:Korobov})}\\
\mathcal{R}&:\mathbf{B}_{1,\infty}^{2,\text{dyad}}([0,1]^{d})\to\mathbf{B}_{1,\infty}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).\end{split}

(1.5)

The last two embeddings are relevant due to the fact that several applications, for example in mathematical finance, require the treatment and quadrature of sums of tensor products of piecewise linear functions. Clearly, these functions neither belong to
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}
nor to
H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}
. Numerical experiments show (see Figure
7
below) that the errors decay with main rate
N^{-2}
or even better. There are several ways to explain this effect. One is that the tent transform maps such functions into the space
\mathbf{E}^{2}_{d}
, the classical Korobov space, see
(
10
, Section 3.3)
, where one knows the corresponding decay rates
[
11
]
. This direction will be elaborated on in Section
6
. Here we want to point out a different reason, namely the third embedding in (
1.5
), where for
1\leq p<\infty

\|f\|_{\mathbf{B}_{p,\infty}^{2,\text{dyad}}}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{|\mathbf{j}|_{1}(2-1/p)}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

Note, that in this notation we have the identification
\mathbf{B}_{2,\infty}^{2,\text{dyad}}=H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}
. The range of
\mathbf{k}
in the sum above depends on which domain (
[0,1]^{d}
or
\mathbb{T}^{d}
) is used, see Definition
3.1
, its higher dimensional analogue and Definition
3.2
below. The mentioned test functions belong to such spaces, where we trade
L_{2}
-integrability against
L_{1}
-integrability and gain smoothness. Here we use the dyadic versions of corresponding Besov spaces with dominating mixed smoothness, similar as in
(
20
, Section 1.3)
. As a direct consequence we establish an analogous bound to (
1.3
), however, now for the significantly larger space
\mathbf{B}_{1,\infty}^{2,\text{dyad}}([0,1]^{d})
, see (
7.1
).

2
Sobolev spaces and their tensor products

The univariate Sobolev space
H^{2}([0,1])
of smoothness
2
can be defined by

H^{2}([0,1])=\{f\in L_{2}([0,1]):f^{\prime\prime}\in L_{2}([0,1])\},

where
f^{\prime\prime}=f^{(2)}
denotes the second weak derivative. We will only consider real valued functions
f
. There are multiple, equivalent norms we can define on this space as to turn it into a Hilbert space. It is common to use

\|f\|_{H^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\coloneqq\|f\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}^{2}+\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}^{2},

also giving the space an obvious inner product turning it into a Hilbert space. From
(
4
, Examples 21-23)
we will also consider

\displaystyle\|f\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}+\left(\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(0)^{2}+f(1)^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(0)^{2}+f^{\prime}(0)^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

These norms even turn out to give
H^{2}([0,1])
the structure of a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS), see
[
4
]
for details. For
\|\cdot\|_{j},j=1,2,3
, the kernels can be given in closed form by

\displaystyle K^{1}_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1+B_{1}(x)B_{1}(y)+\frac{1}{4}B_{2}(x)B_{2}(y)-\frac{1}{24}B_{4}(|x-y|),

\displaystyle K_{2}^{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1-x-y+2xy+\frac{1}{6}(x-y)_{+}^{3}-\frac{1}{6}x(1-y)\left(x^{2}-2y+y^{2}\right),

\displaystyle K_{3}^{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1+xy+\frac{1}{3}\min\{x,y\}^{3}-\frac{1}{2}(x+y)\min\{x,y\}^{2}+xy\min\{x,y\},

respectively, where

B_{1}(x)=x-\frac{1}{2},\quad B_{2}(x)=x^{2}-x+\frac{1}{6},\quad B_{4}(x)=x^{4}-2x^{3}+x^{2}-\frac{1}{30}

are Bernoulli polynomials. The
d
-variate Sobolev space with bounded mixed second derivatives
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
is given by the
d
-fold tensor product of
H^{2}([0,1])
with itself. We again obtain RKHSs with kernels given by

K_{j}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}K_{j}^{1}(x_{i},y_{i})

for
j=1,2,3
respectively. The theory of numerical integration over RKHSs is especially nice since we can express the worst case error explicitly by
[
38
]

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\|f\|_{j}\leq 1}\left|\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{[0,1]^{d}}K_{j}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}K_{j}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}K_{j}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

which can be given for
j=1
by

\displaystyle-1+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}K_{1}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

for
j=2
by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{61}{120}\right)^{d}-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x_{i}}{24}-\frac{x_{i}^{3}}{12}+\frac{x_{i}^{4}}{24}\right)+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}K_{2}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

and for
j=3

\displaystyle\left(\frac{13}{10}\right)^{d}-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{x_{i}}{2}+\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{4}-\frac{x_{i}^{3}}{6}+\frac{x_{i}^{4}}{24}\right)+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in X}K_{3}^{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Given a point set
X
it is thus easy to calculate the worst case error with respect to a given norm as the square root of these expressions.

Definition 3.2
.

Define the Banach spaces

\displaystyle H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}([0,1]^{d})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{f\in C_{b}([0,1]^{d}):\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}([0,1]^{d})}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}([0,1]^{d})\hookrightarrow H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3.5)

Also note the continuous embeddings

4
The tent transform

The
tent transform
of a function
f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}f(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})=f(|2y_{1}-1|,\dots,|2y_{d}-1|).

We can think of
\mathcal{R}f
as being defined over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Assume that
f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous. Notice that sampling a transformed function
\mathcal{R}f(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
is equivalent to sampling
f(|2y_{1}-1|,\dots,|2y_{d}-1|)
. In particular we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in Y}\mathcal{R}f(\mathbf{y})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathcal{R}f(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(4.1)

where, for a given set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we construct the pullback under the tent transform (which might be a multiset)

X=\mathcal{R}Y\coloneqq\{(|2y_{1}-1|,\dots,|2y_{d}-1|):(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in Y\}.

Throughout the paper we will denote points set in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by
Y
and point sets in
[0,1]^{d}
by
X
.

We now study the tent transform
\mathcal{R}
as an operator between function spaces. To this end we first present the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

The tent transform embeds continuously

\mathcal{R}:H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})\rightarrow H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Combining this embedding with (
4.1
), we see that the worst case error of the quasi-Monte Carlo rule over
H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
translates to upper bounds for the worst case error over
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
under the tent transformation.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Note that a tent transformed element of the nonperiodic Faber basis
v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}\in\mathsf{Fab}({[0,1]^{d}})
can be expressed as a finite, linear combination of elements of the periodic Faber basis
v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}\in\mathsf{Fab}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
(one level higher). To be precise, in the one dimensional case we have for
j\geq 0,k\in\mathbb{D}_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}v_{-1,0}

\displaystyle=v_{0,0}^{\text{per}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}v_{-1,1}

\displaystyle=v_{-1,0}^{\text{per}}-v_{0,0}^{\text{per}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}v_{j,k}

\displaystyle=v_{j+1,2^{j}+k}^{\text{per}}+v_{j+1,2^{j}-k-1}^{\text{per}}.

A comparison of coefficients in the Faber representation shows that the coefficients transform under
\mathcal{R}
by (with
j\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{-1,0}(\mathcal{R}f)&=d_{-1,1}(f),\\
d_{0,0}(\mathcal{R}f)&=d_{-1,0}(f)-d_{-1,1}(f),\\
d_{j,k}(\mathcal{R}f)&=\begin{cases}d_{j-1,2^{j-1}-k-1}(f)&,0\leq k<2^{j-1}\\
d_{j-1,k-2^{j-1}}(f)&,2^{j-1}\leq k<2^{j}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.2)

We see that for each
j\geq 1
and
k\in\{2^{j-1},2^{j-1}+1,\dots,2^{j}-1\}
there is exactly one
\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{j-1}
such that

\displaystyle d_{j,k}(\mathcal{R}f)=d_{j,\tilde{k}}(\mathcal{R}f).

(4.3)

In the multivariate case, we use the product structure of the mixed differences (
3.4
) and use the results for univariate functions. Fix
\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}
. Define
\phi(\mathbf{j})\coloneqq((j_{i})_{+}-1)_{i=1}^{d}
and decompose
[d]=M_{-}\cup M_{0}\cup M_{+}
into disjoint (possibly empty) sets

\displaystyle M_{-}\coloneqq\{i:j_{i}=-1\},\quad M_{0}\coloneqq\{i:j_{i}=0\},\quad M_{+}\coloneqq\{i:j_{i}\geq 1\}.

By sorting the product according to the above decomposition and applying (
4.3
) respectively (
4.2
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}

\displaystyle|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(\mathcal{R}f)|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}\left|\prod_{i\in M_{-}}d^{(i)}_{-1,0}\prod_{i\in M_{0}}d^{(i)}_{0,0}\prod_{i\in M_{+}}d^{(i)}_{j_{i},k_{i}}(\mathcal{R}f)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\phi(\mathbf{j})}^{\text{per}}}\left|\prod_{i\in M_{-}}d^{(i)}_{-1,1}\prod_{i\in M_{0}}(d^{(i)}_{-1,0}-d^{(i)}_{-1,1})\prod_{i\in M_{+}}d^{(i)}_{j_{i}-1,k_{i}}(f)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\phi(\mathbf{j})}^{\text{per}}}\left|\prod_{i\in M_{-}}d^{(i)}_{-1,1}\sum_{A\subseteq M_{0}}(-1)^{\#A}\prod_{i\in M_{0}}d^{(i)}_{-1,\chi_{A}(i)}\prod_{i\in M_{+}}d^{(i)}_{j_{i}-1,k_{i}}(f)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\phi(\mathbf{j})}}\left|d_{\phi(\mathbf{j}),\mathbf{k}}(f)\right|^{2},

and obtain the desired

\sup_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(\mathcal{R}f)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\sup_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus
\mathcal{R}
defines a continuous operator between
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
and
H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
∎

It is natural to ask for similar mapping properties of
\mathcal{R}
between further spaces. Such questions have been studied in the recent preprint
[
40
]
. Corollary 4.4 there is in a similar spirit to what we looked at here but does not quite reach smoothness
s=2
.

5
Tent transformed order 2 nets

5.1
General definitions of higher order digital nets

Digital nets go back to the construction of Halton
[
25
]
via digit reversals. It was only realized later how this construction should be generalized in a usable way, for historical remarks see
[
16
]
. On the one hand, this class of point sets should be large enough to capture the important properties a point set needs to posses for good approximation properties. On the other hand, the class should still be sufficiently structured to get a grasp of the involved analysis for the error of the corresponding quasi-Monte Carlo rule. We aim to give an accessible introduction to these point sets, further information and generalizations can be found in
[
1
,
12
,
16
]
.

Generally speaking, a digital
(t,n,d)
-net in base
b
is a subset
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
of size
\#Y=b^{n}
such that for each elementary,
d
-dimensional,
b
-adic interval

J=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[\frac{a_{i}}{b^{\ell_{i}}},\frac{a_{i}+1}{b^{\ell_{i}}}\right)\quad(a_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,b^{\ell_{i}}-1\})

of volume
|J|=b^{-\ell_{1}-\dots-\ell_{d}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}b^{t-n}
it holds
\#(Y\cap J)=b^{t}
. That is every elementary interval up to a given volume contains as many points as would be expected from a “uniformly distributed” set. The parameter
t
can be seen as measuring the quality of the point set, where we would like to have
t
small. It is common to only consider prime powers
b=p^{m}
as bases. In what follows we will only be interested in the case
b=2
(which in general also yields lower implicit constants in the asymptotic bounds).

Analogous to how lattices can be generated from a generating vector
\mathbf{v}\in[0,1)^{d}
by pushing it through the torus, digital nets can be constructed from generator matrices

C_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}-&c_{i,1}^{\top}&-\\
&\vdots&\\
-&c_{i,n}^{\top}&-\end{matrix}\right)\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}

for
i=1,\dots,d
, where
\{0,1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
will be interpreted as the field with
2
elements. The points
\mathbf{y}^{k}
in the set
Y=Y(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})=\{\mathbf{y}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N-1}\}\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
with
N=2^{n}
are then constructed as follows: collect the binary digits
k=\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1}+\dots+2^{n-1}\kappa_{n-1}
with
\kappa_{j}\in\{0,1\}
in a vector

\kappa=\left(\begin{matrix}\kappa_{0}\\
\kappa_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\kappa_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right)

and determine, using arithmetic in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, the product

C_{i}\kappa=\left(\begin{matrix}c_{i,1}^{\top}\kappa\\
\vdots\\
c_{i,n}^{\top}\kappa\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\kappa^{(i)}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\kappa^{(i)}_{n}\end{matrix}\right).

The
i
-th component of
\mathbf{y}^{k}
is then given by

y_{i}^{k}=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{(i)}}{2^{1}}+\frac{\kappa_{2}^{(i)}}{2^{2}}+\dots+\frac{\kappa_{n}^{(i)}}{2^{n}}.

Given a set of generator matrices
C_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n},i=1,\dots,d
the parameters
n
and
d
can be seen immediately. The quality parameter
t
of
Y
translates into the following property for the generator matrices
[
16
]
: for any
0\leq\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}\leq n
with
\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{d}=n-t
the initial rows

\displaystyle c_{1,1},\dots,c_{1,\ell_{1}},\dots,c_{d,1},\dots,c_{d,\ell_{d}}

(5.1)

of the generator matrices are linearly independent. Clearly, the smaller the value of
t
is, the stronger this property gets. Indeed, the smallest possible
t
for which (
5.1
) holds is also the smallest
t
for which
Y
is a
(t,n,d)
-net.

To improve the quality of such nets even further one introduces the order parameter
\alpha

[
1
,
12
,
28
]
. We will formulate it in terms as we will need it later on (also see
[
22
,
23
]
).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
t,n,d,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and let
C_{1},\dots,C_{d}\in\{0,1\}^{\alpha n\times n}
be matrices with rows
c_{i,j}^{\top},i=1,\dots,d,j=1,\dots,\alpha n
respectively. Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d},\#Y=2^{n}
be the digital net generated by the matrices
C_{1},\dots,C_{d}
as above (with the obvious modification for non-square matrices).

The point set
Y
is called an
order
\alpha
digital
(t,n,d)
-net
if for all indices
1\leq j_{i,\nu_{i}}<\dots<j_{i,1}\leq\alpha n
, where
0\leq\nu_{i}\leq n
, with

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\min\{\alpha,\nu_{i}\}}j_{i,\ell}\leq\alpha n-t

the row vectors

c_{1,j_{1,\nu_{1}}}^{\top},\dots,c_{1,j_{1,1}}^{\top},\dots,c_{d,j_{d,\nu_{d}}}^{\top},\dots,c_{d,j_{d,1}}^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

are linearly independent over
\{0,1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Note how the indices
j_{i,1}>\dots>j_{i,\nu_{i}}
are ordered descendingly, so that we only constrain how far down the (at most)
\alpha
last rows can be that we pick out of every matrix
C_{i}
. If
\nu_{i}\geq\alpha
we thus may assume that also
j_{i,\alpha+1}=j_{i,\alpha}-1
,
j_{i,\alpha+2}=j_{i,\alpha}-2
,
\dots
,
j_{i,\nu_{i}}=1
. If
\alpha=1
we recover the definition of a digital
(t,n,d)
-net.

Again, the parameter
t
controls the quality of the point set where we would like to have
t
small. The parameter
\alpha
is connected to the smoothness of the underlying functions that we want to integrate via the quasi-Monte Carlo rule
[
12
,
23
]
. Also,
\alpha
can be interpreted as a
complexity parameter
since every component of a point of an order
\alpha
digital
(t,n,d)
-net requires
\alpha\log_{2}N
bits to write down in binary.

5.2
Constructions

Higher order nets can be constructed from standard digital nets via the following
[
1
,
12
,
28
]
. Let
\tilde{Y}=\{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{0},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{N-1}\}\in\mathbb{T}^{\alpha d}
,
N=2^{n}
be a
(\tilde{t},n,\alpha d)
-net (generated from square matrices
C_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n},i=1,\dots,\alpha d
). Every component of
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{k}
can be written in base
2
using only
n
digits after the comma

\tilde{y}^{k}_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\eta^{k}_{i,\ell}}{2^{\ell}}\quad(\eta^{k}_{i,\ell}\in\{0,1\}).

Define now
Y=\{\mathbf{y}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N-1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
N=2^{n}
as above via

y_{i}^{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{\alpha}\frac{\eta^{k}_{(i-1)\alpha+s,\ell}}{2^{(\ell-1)\alpha+s}}

for
i=1,\dots,d
and
k=0,1,\dots,N-1
. This operation interlaces the binary digits of
\tilde{y}^{k}_{(i-1)\alpha+1},\dots,\tilde{y}^{k}_{i\alpha}
giving a number with
\alpha n
digits after the comma. The resulting set
Y
is then an order
\alpha
digital
(t,n,d)
-net where
t
can be chosen as
[
1
,
12
,
28
]

t=\alpha\tilde{t}+\frac{d\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2}.

Starting with a digital net we can thus construct digital nets of arbitrarily high order. Although note that this also raises the quality parameter
t
.

It is a nontrivial task to construct digital nets with a good quality parameter, especially as the dimension
d
increases. There are multiple constructions available by now. Digital sequences
(\mathbf{y}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots)
are, roughly speaking, sequences of points such that any initial block of
2^{n}
elements constitutes a digital net, where the corresponding quality parameter
t
could depend on
n
, ideally in a controllable manor. For precise definitions consult
[
16
]
. Examples of such sequences include the Sobol’-construction
[
42
]
and the Niederreiter-construction
[
35
]
. The ones that we will use and that generally yield the lowest
t
values are the Niederreiter–Xing sequences
[
37
,
44
]
. These sequences use sophisticated, algebraic tools to construct the generator matrices. Fortunately, computer implementations are available
[
39
]
. We will use the database
[
33
]
available under

people.cs.kuleuven.be/
\sim
dirk.nuyens/qmc-generators/

which contains implementations for these sequences in Matlab/Octave, C++ and Python2/3. Given a digital sequence
\{\mathbf{y}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots\}
in dimension
\alpha d-1
from the Niederreiter-Xing construction we can construct a
(t,n,\alpha d)
-net via

\{(\lfloor y^{k}_{1}\rfloor_{n},\dots,\lfloor y^{k}_{\alpha d-1}\rfloor_{n},k/2^{n}):k=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\},

where
\lfloor y\rfloor_{n}
truncates all but the first
n
binary digits of
y
after the comma. Using the digital interlacing from above we can turn this into an order
\alpha
digital
(t,n,d)
-net (with a possibly different
t
value), in particular for
\alpha=2
.

5.3
The worst case error for tent transformed order 2 nets

We now come to our central result on tent transformed order
2
nets, giving a bound for the decay for the quasi-Monte Carlo rule.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
be an order
2
digital
(t,n,d)
-net of cardinality
\#Y=N=2^{n}
. Let
X=\mathcal{R}Y\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
be the tent transformed point set with
\#X=N
. Then

e^{\text{QMC}}(X,H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim 2^{t}N^{-2}(\log N)^{2d-1},

where the implicit constant depends only on
d
.

Proof.

We will follow a similar strategy as in
[
28
]
with some adaptations for the case of smoothness
2
, using Proposition
3.4
.

Let
f\in H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
, we need to upper bound the quantity

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in Y}\mathcal{R}f(\mathbf{y})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathcal{R}f(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right|.

Let
g=\mathcal{R}f\in H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Writing

g=\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(g)v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}

we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in Y}g(\mathbf{y})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(g)\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in Y}v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}(\mathbf{y})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right).

Setting

c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in Y}v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}(\mathbf{y})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}^{\text{per}}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in X}f(\mathbf{x})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(g)c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(g)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|g\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

By Theorem
4.1
we have
\|g\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2,\text{dyad}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})}
, so that it remains to show

\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{2d-1}}{N^{2}}.

Decompose the sum

\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\mathbf{j}|_{1}<M\end{subarray}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\mathbf{j}|_{1}\geq M\end{subarray}}+\sum_{e\subsetneq[d]}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}(e)},

where
M\coloneqq n-\lceil t/2\rceil\leq n
and
\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}(e)\coloneqq\{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}:\{i\in[d]:j_{i}\neq-1\}=e\}
. We will apply
(
28
, (5.9-12))
(in their notation
r=2,p=p^{\prime}=2,q^{\prime}=1
), which uses a bound for
c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)
from
[
34
]
, see
(
28
, Proposition 4.3, Lemma 5.1, Lemma 5.2)
, only valid because we work with order
2
nets. To be specific, from (5.9) and the subsequent estimate we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\mathbf{j}|_{1}<M\end{subarray}}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}&\lesssim 2^{-2n+t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\ell^{d-1}(M-\ell)^{d-1}\\
&\lesssim 2^{-2n+t}M^{2d-1}\lesssim 2^{t}N^{-2}(\log N)^{2d-1}.\end{split}

(5.2)

From (5.10) and the subsequent estimate we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\mathbf{j}|_{1}\geq M\end{subarray}}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}&\lesssim 2^{-2n+t}n^{d-1}+2^{-2n}n^{d-1}\\
&\lesssim 2^{t}N^{-2}(\log N)^{d-1}.\end{split}

(5.3)

Finally, from (5.12) we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}(e)}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim 2^{-2n}n^{\#e-1}\lesssim N^{-2}(\log N)^{\#e-1},\end{split}

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{e\subsetneq[d]}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}(e)}2^{-3|\mathbf{j}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}^{\text{per}}}|c_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(Y)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim N^{-2}(\log N)^{d-2}.

(5.4)

Putting (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) together yields the claim.
∎

6
The tent transform and classical Korobov spaces

In this section we want to give another mapping property of the tent transform for spaces relevant to quasi-Monte Carlo quadrature and only requiring Fourier information. We define the
classical Korobov space

\mathbf{E}^{s}_{d}\coloneqq\mathbf{E}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with smoothness
s>0
via the norm

\|f\|_{\mathbf{E}^{s}_{d}}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})|\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+|k_{i}|)^{s},

where

\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

denotes the Fourier coefficient over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(contrary to
\mathcal{F}[\cdot]
which denotes the Fourier transform over
\mathbb{R}
). One sometimes finds a different notion of “Korobov space” in the literature, see for example
[
12
]
or
[
17
]
(which in our notation corresponds to
H_{\text{mix}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
or
H_{\text{mix}}^{s}([0,1]^{d})
). Here we use the space as defined in
[
31
]
and
(
10
, Section 3.3)
and call it “classical Korobov space” to emphasize this distinction. In the sequel we will prove that the tent transform embeds from
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
into
\mathbf{E}^{2}_{d}
. As preparation for the general setting let us start with the case for
d=1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

In the univariate case the tent transform
\mathcal{R}
maps
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1])
continuously to
\mathbf{E}^{2}_{1}
, that is

\mathcal{R}:H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1])\to\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{T}).

Proof.

It is clear that

(\mathcal{R}f)^{\wedge}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}f(|2x-1|)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

so that
|(\mathcal{R}f)^{\wedge}(0)|\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1])}
. Similarly, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{(\mathcal{R}f)}^{\wedge}(k)&=\int_{0}^{1}f(|2x-1|)\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}f(|y|)\exp(-\pi\mathrm{i}k(y+1))\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\frac{\exp(-\pi\mathrm{i}k)}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}f(|y|)\exp(-\pi\mathrm{i}ky)\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\cos(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2}\mathrm{i}\int_{-1}^{1}f(|y|)\sin(\pi ky)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

The second integral disappears by symmetry, so that we continue with the first integral and observe via integration by parts

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\cos(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t&=\left[\frac{f(y)\sin(k\pi y)}{k\pi}\right]_{0}^{1}-\frac{1}{\pi k}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\sin(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\frac{1}{\pi k}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\sin(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

We apply partial integration again and get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\sin(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{1}{k\pi}\left[f^{\prime}(t)\cos(\pi kt)\right]_{t=0}^{1}+\frac{1}{\pi k}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(t)\cos(\pi kt)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}|{(\mathcal{R}f)}^{\wedge}(k)|&\leq\frac{1}{|\pi k|^{2}}\left(|f^{\prime}(1)|+|f^{\prime}(0)|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}\right)\,\\
&\lesssim|k|^{-2}\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1])}\end{split}

and we observe the desired decay rate of
|k|^{-2}
.
∎

As for the multivariate situation we will prove the following stronger result which looks similar to the one in Theorem
4.1
(and Proposition
3.4
). For this we will introduce the function space
\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose defining norm will be given in the statement.

Theorem 6.2
.

For any function
f\in H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+|k_{i}|)^{2}\left(\sum\limits_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|{[\mathcal{R}f(\cdot-\mathbf{m})]}^{\wedge}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

The proof is, at parts, similar to the one of Proposition
3.4
.

Step 1.
Analogous to the proof of Lemma
6.1
for
f\in S(\mathbb{R})
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
it holds

\displaystyle[\mathcal{R}f(\cdot-m)]^{\wedge}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(\pi k)^{2}}\left(f^{\prime}(-m+1)+(-1)^{k+1}f^{\prime}(-m)+\int_{-m}^{-m+1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\cos(\pi kx)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(\pi k)^{2}}\left(2\chi_{2\mathbb{Z}+1}(k)f^{\prime}(-m)+\int_{-m}^{-m+1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)(1+\cos(\pi kx))\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

for
k\neq 0
and
[\mathcal{R}f(\cdot-m)]^{\wedge}(0)=\int_{-m}^{-m+1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.

Step 2.
For multivariate
f\in S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by tensorization and, if necessary, by swapping differentiation and integration, reordering and using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we obtain terms of the form

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1+|k_{i}|^{2}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-n}}|f^{(\underline{\beta})}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}

with
n\in\{0,\dots,d\}
and
\underline{\beta}\in\{1\}^{n}\times\{0,2\}^{d-n}
(In the cases were
n=0
or
n=d
the sum respectively the integral disappears). To get rid of the point evaluations we fix
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-n}
and decompose
g_{\mathbf{x}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\mathbf{s}\mapsto f^{(\underline{\beta})}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x})
as in (
3.9
)

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{x}}(s)=\sum_{\underline{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}g_{\mathbf{x},\underline{\ell}}(s).

and estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}|g_{\mathbf{x},\underline{\ell}}(\mathbf{m})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{|\underline{\ell}|_{1}}\|g_{\mathbf{x},\underline{\ell}}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 2^{-|\underline{\ell}|_{1}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}2^{2|\mathbf{k}|_{1}}\|g_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-|\underline{\ell}|_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{n}[g_{\mathbf{x}}](\mathbf{s})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+|s_{j}|)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-|\underline{\ell}|_{1}}\sum_{\underline{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\|g_{\mathbf{x}}^{(\underline{\gamma})}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

by using Shannon’s sampling theorem and Plancherel’s identity.
With that in mind we get

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-n}}|f^{(\underline{\beta})}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{x})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\underline{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}2^{-|\underline{\ell}|_{1}/2}\left(\sum_{\underline{\gamma}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-n}}\|g_{\mathbf{x}}^{(\underline{\gamma})}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\underline{\gamma}\in\{0,1,2\}^{d}}\|f^{(\underline{\gamma})}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

So for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
f\in S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+|k_{i}|)^{2}\left(\sum\limits_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|{[\mathcal{R}f(\cdot-\mathbf{m})]}^{\wedge}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Step 3.
In the last step we use a density argument to generalize the result to
f\in H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a sequence converging to
f
in
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In step 2 we have seen that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also a Cauchy sequence in
\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. So there is a
\tilde{f}\in\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also converging to
\tilde{f}
in
\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Functions in
\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
do not have to be continuous but
\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
continuously embeds into
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This follows by an application of Parseval’s identity. Indeed, for
g\in\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds

\displaystyle\|g\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|g(\cdot-m)\|^{2}_{L_{2}([0,1]^{d})}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|\mathcal{R}g(\cdot-m)\|^{2}_{L_{2}([0,1]^{d})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(1+|k_{i}|)^{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}{(1+|k_{i}|)^{4}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|[\mathcal{R}g(\cdot-m)]^{\wedge}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g\|^{2}_{\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Now we get

\displaystyle\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|f-f_{n}\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|f_{n}-\tilde{f}\|_{\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

that is
f=\tilde{f}
almost everywhere and finally

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|f\|_{H_{\text{mix}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

the intended result.
∎

From the previous theorem we directly obtain the following result as a corollary.

Corollary 6.3
.

The tent transform
\mathcal{R}
is a continuous mapping

\mathcal{R}:H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})\to\mathbf{E}^{2}_{d}.

This should be particularly helpful for the analysis of tent transformed lattice rules in
H_{\text{mix}}^{2}([0,1]^{d})
.

7.3
Piecewise linear and smoother test functions

It is interesting to look at how tent transformed order
2
nets perform for functions lying outside of the Sobolev space of dominating mixed smoothness
2
. For this we will take the tensor product of piecewise linear functions. We generate these functions by first choosing random nodes
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}=1
and corresponding values
y_{i}\in[-1,1],i=0,1,\dots,k
uniformly at random. The univariate function
f
is now given by the conditions
f(x_{i})=y_{i}
with linear interpolation between the nodes. We generate such a function for each coordinate and tensorize to obtain a
d
-variate function. Figure
7
shows the absolute error of the true integral and the quasi-Monte Carlo approximation using tent transformed order
2
nets in
d=3
dimensions (where we choose
3
,
4
and
5
intermediate nodes for each dimension respectively). The colored plots show
3
random instances each for tent transformed and untransformed order
2
nets. These tensorized, piecewise linear functions fulfill a similar condition to Theorem
3.3
, namely

\|f\|_{\mathbf{B}^{2,\text{dyad}}_{1,\infty}([0,1]^{d})}=\sup_{\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{-1}^{d}}2^{|\mathbf{j}|_{1}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{D}_{\mathbf{j}}}|d_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}(f)|<\infty.

(7.1)

With an analogous analysis as done in the proof of Theorem
5.2
(again using bounds from
[
28
]
) we obtain that for such functions the worst case error of the quasi-Monte Carlo rule for tent transformed order
2
nets can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle e^{\text{QMC}}(X,\mathbf{B}^{2,\text{dyad}}_{1,\infty}([0,1]^{d}))\lesssim\frac{(\log N)^{2d-1}}{N^{2}}.

We conclude with a look at functions with smoothness higher than
2
. We consider a cutout of a quadratic B-spline

f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}-x^{2}&,0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2},\\

which was already used in

,

]
as a numerical example and has regularity

on the scale of Sobolev-Hilbert spaces. We tensorize this function and look at the error of the quasi-Monte Carlo rule with tent transformed and untransformed order
2
nets, see Figure

. We also compare this to order
