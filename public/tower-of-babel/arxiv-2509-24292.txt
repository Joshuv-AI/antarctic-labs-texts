Strongly Hopfian and Co-Hopfian Acts over Monoids: Structure and Characterizations
Ali Madanshekaf, Farideh Farsad

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce and explore new classes of S S -acts over a monoid S S , namely, strongly Hopfian and strongly co-Hopfian acts, as well as their weaker counterparts, Hopfian and co-Hopfian acts. We investigate the relationships between these newly defined structures and well-studied classes of S S -acts, including Noetherian, Artinian, injective, projective, quasi-injective, and quasi-projective acts. A key result shows that, under certain conditions, a quasi-projective (respectively quasi-injective) S S -act that is strongly co-Hopfian (respectively strongly Hopfian) is also strongly Hopfian (respectively strongly co-Hopfian). Moreover, we provide a variety of examples and structural results concerning the behavior of subacts and quotient acts of strongly Hopfian and strongly co-Hopfian S S -acts, further elucidating the internal structure and interrelationships within this extended framework.

The study of modules (rings) by properties of their endomorphisms is a classical research subject. In 1986, Hiremath

surfaces. Hopfian and co-Hopfian modules (rings) have been investigated by several authors.

Likewise, the study of

Hopfian and co-Hopfian

We recall the following definitions and facts which we deal with in this paper.

Definition 2.1

We call an

We refer the reader to

Definition 2.2

(See

An

Example 2.3
.

(a)

Every finite
S
-act is Hopfian and co-Hopfian.

(b)

Let

S
-act. Then infinite product

because the surjective
S
-map
\varphi:P\to P

(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots)\mapsto(a_{2},a_{3},\cdots)

that is not injective so that it is not an automorphism.

(c)

Recall that an algebra is

[
4
]
). Then every simple

-act

is Hopfian. In fact, let

be a surjective endomorphism of

, then we have done. Otherwise,

.

Proposition 2.4
.

Every Noetherian
S
-act is Hopfian.

Proof.

Let
A
be a Noetherian
S
-act. For every surjective endomorphism

f
of
A
, the ascending chain
\mathcal{K}_{f}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{3}}\subseteq\cdots
stabilizes, so there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}=\cdots
. We show that
\mathcal{K}_{f}=\Delta_{A}
. For
(a,b)\in\mathcal{K}_{f}\subseteq A\times A
, since
f^{n}
is surjective, there exists
(c,d)\in A\times A
such that
f^{n}(c)=a
and
f^{n}(d)=b
. In view of
f(a)=f(b)
, we get
(c,d)\in\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
so that
a=f^{n}(c)=f^{n}(d)=b.
Hence
\mathcal{K}_{f}=\Delta_{A}
which implies that
f
is injective, as required.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Every Artinian
S
-act is co-Hopfian.

Proof.

Let
A
be an Artinian
S
-act. For every injective endomorphism

f
of
A
, the descending chain
\mathcal{I}_{f}\supseteq\mathcal{I}_{f^{2}}\supseteq\cdots
stabilizes.
So there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}=\cdots
. Now for every
a\in A
, consider
any
b\in A
with
b\neq a
, we have
(f^{n}(a),f^{n}(b))\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}
, but
f^{n}
is injective, this gives that

f^{n}(a)\neq f^{n}(b)
. Hence
(f^{n}(a),f^{n}(b))\in{\rm im}f^{n+1}\times{\rm im}f^{n+1}
, this means that there exists

(x,y)\in A\times A
such that
f^{n+1}(x)=f^{n}(a)
and

f^{n+1}(y)=f^{n}(b)
. Again by injectivity of

we conclude
that
f(x)=a
and
f(y)=b
. Consequently,

which implies

is surjective, as we needed.
∎

Consider the set of rational numbers

3
Strongly Hopfian and Strongly co-Hopfian
S
-acts

In realizing that every Noetherian (respectively Artinian)
S
-act
A
is Hopfian (respectively co-Hopfian), the ACC (respectively DCC) is only applied to chain of congruences of the form

(a)\quad\mathcal{K}_{f}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{3}}\subseteq\cdots\quad(\textrm{respectively,}\ (b)\quad\mathcal{I}_{f}\supseteq\mathcal{I}_{f^{2}}\supseteq\cdots)

where
f
is any endomorphism of
A
. As we have already seen, we give the following example:

Since the
\mathbb{Z}
-endomorphisms of
\mathbb{Q}
are either zero or automorphisms, it follow that
\mathbb{Q}
is a
non-Noetherian and non-Artinian
\mathbb{Z}
-act which satisfy both conditions
(a)
and
(b)
.

The above remark motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

An
S
-act
A
is called
strongly Hopfian
if for every endomorphism
f
of
A
the ascending chain
\mathcal{K}_{f}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{3}}\subseteq\cdots
stabilizes. That is, there exist a positive integer
n
such that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{m}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
for all
m\geq n.
An
S
-act
A
is called
strongly co-Hopfian
if for every
endomorphism
f
of
A
the descending chain
\mathcal{I}_{f}\supseteq\mathcal{I}_{f^{2}}\supseteq\mathcal{I}_{f^{3}}\cdots
stabilizes. That is, there exist a positive integer
n
such that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{m}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}
for all
m\geq n.

It is immediately clear that strongly Hopfian implies Hopfian and strongly co-Hopfian implies co-Hopfian. In Example
3.5
below we will present some Hopfian
S
-acts that are not strongly Hopfian.

Now we obtain:

Proposition 3.2
.

For an
S
-act
A
the following statements are equivalent:

(1)~A
is strongly Hopfian.

(2)
For every
f
in
{\rm End}_{S}(A)
there exists an integer
n\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}
.

(3)
For every
f
in
{\rm End}_{S}(A)
there exist an integer
n\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\bigcap\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\Delta_{A}
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) This is always true by definition of strongly Hopfian
S
-acts.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1) Suppose that
f\in{\rm End}_{S}(A)
. If, for some
n
,

{\rm im}(f^{n})
consists of one point, we are done. In the complementary case, we have
{\rm im}(f^{n})={\rm im}(f^{n+1})
for some positive integer
n
. Apply
f
to get the equality
{\rm im}(f^{n+1})={\rm im}(f^{n+2})
and so on.
Therefore, statements
(1) and (2) are equivalent.

To complete the proof it is enough to show that the remaining implications.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Suppose that
f\in{\rm End}_{S}(A)
and choosing an integer
n\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}
.
Let
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\bigcap\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}.
Then by
(a,b)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}
we have either
(a,b)\in\Delta_{A}
or
(a,b)\in{\rm im}f^{n}\times{\rm im}f^{n}
, it follow that there exists
(x,y)\in A\times A
such that
a=f^{n}(x)
and

b=f^{n}(y)
. Now by
(a,b)\in\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
we get
f^{n}(a)=f^{2n}(x)=f^{2n}(y)=f^{n}(b)
. By assumption
\mathcal{K}_{f^{2n}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
and so
a=f^{n}(x)=f^{n}(y)=b
, that is,
(a,b)\in\Delta_{A}
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2): Suppose that
f\in{\rm End}_{S}(A)
and choosing an integer
n\geq 1
that satisfies (3).
For every
(a,b)\in\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}
, we have
(f^{n}(a),f^{n}(b))\in\mathcal{K}_{f}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
. Therefore
((f^{n}(a),f^{n}(b))\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\bigcap\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\Delta_{A}
and
so
(a,b)\in\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
. This shows that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
. Hence,
(2) follows.
∎

In the following result, we present a slight extension of Proposition 3.3 in

Proposition 3.3
.

For an
S

(1)~A
is strongly co-Hopfian.

(2)
For every
f
in
{\rm End}_{S}(A)
there exists an integer

\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}
.

(3)
For every

there exists an integer

\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\vee\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=A\times A
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) The assertion is obvious.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1) Given
f\in{\rm End}_{S}(A)
such that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has
{\rm im}f^{n}={\rm im}f^{n+1}
. We claim that

\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{m}}
for every

It is enough to show that

{\rm im}f^{n}={\rm im}f^{n+2}
. Firstly, we have
{\rm im}f^{n+2}\subseteq{\rm im}f^{n+1}={\rm im}f^{n}
. Secondly, consider

. Now,
f^{n}(a)\in{\rm im}f^{n}={\rm im}f^{n+1}
and so there exists

such that

. This means

, as we required. We

, so that

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2) Let

be a positive integer and
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\vee\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=A\times A.
We will show that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}

(x,y)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}
. If
(x,y)\in\Delta_{A}
then

(x,y)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}
. Otherwise,
(x,y)\in{\rm im}f^{n}\times{\rm im}f^{n}
then one has
x=f^{n}(u)
and
y=f^{n}(v)

for some
u
and
v
in

. By the assumption
(u,v)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\vee\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}
, therefore by
[
11

there exist elements
b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n-1}\in A
such that
(u,b_{1})\in\tau_{1},(b_{1},b_{2})\in\tau_{2},\cdots,(b_{n-1},v)\in\tau_{n}
where
\tau_{i}\in\{\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}},\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}\}
for
i=1,\cdots,n.

Without loss of generality we may assume that

and there exist

for which one has
(u,b_{1})\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}},(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}},(b_{2},v)\in\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}
. Then we
distinguish four cases:

(I)

(II)

u=b_{1},f^{n}(b_{1})=f^{n}(b_{2}),(b_{2},v)\in{\rm im}f^{n}\times{\rm im}f^{n};

(III)

(u,b_{1})\in{\rm im}f^{n}\times{\rm im}f^{n},f^{n}(b_{1})=f^{n}(b_{2}),b_{2}=v;

.

Case (II). We have

(x,y)=(f^{2n}(r),f^{2n}(s))\in{\rm im}f^{n+1}\times{\rm im}f^{n+1}

Case (IV). We have

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3)

\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}.

\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}\vee\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=A\times A
.

Then we have

For a monoid

(1)

(2)

Proof.

(1)
First note that any

is completely determined by

. Therefore,

{\rm End}_{S}(S_{S})=\{\lambda_{a}\mid a\in S\}.

We now have:

S_{S}
is a strongly Hopfian act if and only if for all

there exists

such that
\mathcal{K}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{K}_{f^{n+1}}
if and only if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda^{n}_{a}}=\mathcal{K}_{\lambda^{n+1}_{a}}
if and only if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda_{a^{n}}}=\mathcal{K}_{\lambda_{a^{n+1}}}
if and only if for every

there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}

(2)
Similar to (1),
S_{S}
is a strongly co-Hopfian act if and only if for all

there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{f^{n}}=\mathcal{I}_{f^{n+1}}
if and only if for all
f\in{\rm End}_{S}(S_{S})=\{\lambda_{a}\mid a\in S\},
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\rm im}{f^{n}}={\rm im}{f^{n+1}}
if and only if for all
a\in S

there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\rm im}\lambda_{a^{n}}={\rm im}\lambda_{a^{n+1}}
if and only if for all
a\in S
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

if and only if
for all
a\in S
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

∎

So far, it has not been established whether the class of strongly Hopfian

-acts. In the following example, we demonstrate that it indeed is a proper subclass.

Example 3.5
.

To give an example of an
S
-act which is Hopfian but not strongly Hopfian, first, notice that it is easy to see that any commutative monoid as an
S
-act over itself is Hopfian. In fact, if

is such a monoid and let

, then there exist an element

If

, then

. By multiplying

to this equation on the right we get

, because
S
is commutative. Taking into account this fact, for any prime number

with the usual multipication. Let

with

, the

is not strongly Hopfian act.

Remark 3.6

Subacts of strongly Hopfian (respectively strongly co-Hopfian)

If

Hopfian objects and co-Hopfian objects have an elementary interaction with projective objects and injective objects.
In fact the two results are:

Proposition 4.1
.

An injective Hopfian
S
-act is co-Hopfian. Also, a projective co-Hopfian

-act is Hopfian.

Proof.

Let

\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{\rm id}_{A}}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{A}

f

-act. The monoid

Finitely generated acts is of as great importance in the theory of acts as it is in the theory of modules. In analogy to modules category, in this short section we will give two results regarding this class of acts, but let us have a definition first.
