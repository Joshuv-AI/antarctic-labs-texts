Risk Factors for Postpartum Weight Retention in Postpartum Women: An Integrative Review
Authors: Shanxia Chen, Meiyu Wang, Yue Wei, Zhuangwei Li, Xiaodan Wang, Hui Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo summarise and synthesise extant research studies that identify risk factors for the development of postpartum weight retention in postpartum women.DesignIntegrative review.Review MethodsThis review was guided by Whittemore and Knafl's methodology. Eleven databases were searched for relevant original studies from database creation to November 25, 2025. Two researchers independently screened articles against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, extracted data, and assessed the quality of the included studies using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale. Descriptive summaries and graphical representations were produced for the findings. PROSPERO registration number: CRD42024530948.ResultsOf 2750 retrieved sources, 26 studies were included in this review. The existing measurement approach failed to assess postpartum weight retention precisely. Overall study quality was moderate (n = 16), with seven rated as high quality and three as low quality. Risk factors for postpartum weight retention were categorised into three themes, nine subthemes, and 21 risk factors according to the Andersen model, most of which are modifiable.ConclusionThe accuracy of postpartum weight retention measurement approaches needs to be examined to provide reliable data. The incidence of postpartum weight retention is unsatisfactory, especially for women in upper‐middle‐income countries. Designing a comprehensive and evidence‐based health management strategy will support minimising risk factors of postpartum weight retention, and high‐quality empirical studies could be conducted to refine the theoretical framework for postpartum weight retention.Implications for Nursing PracticeThis study suggests that nursing managers should strengthen education and training for nurses, midwives, and postpartum care providers regarding postpartum weight management and regularly assess their ability to evaluate postpartum weight and identify risk factors for postpartum weight retention. These findings may also serve as a reference for nursing researchers in developing risk assessment instruments for postpartum weight retention and optimising predictive models.Reporting MethodThis review was conducted by following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for reporting systematic reviews.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Keywords: integrative review, postpartum weight retention, postpartum women, risk factors

Introduction

Over the past 40 years, the prevalence of overweight and obesity has increased at the global and regional levels. Assuming the continuation of historical trends, the number of adults living with overweight and obesity will reach 3.80 billion by 2050, over half of the likely global adult population at that time. Among them, the prevalence of overweight and obesity is increasing significantly faster among women of childbearing age than among men in the same age groups (GBD 2021 Adult BMI Collaborators. 2025). A series of adaptive metabolic changes occur in women to meet the needs of fetal growth and development during pregnancy, including gestational weight gain (GWG), adipocytes and inflammatory cytokines (Christian and Porter 2014; Xuming and Ruirui 2025; Cheng and Weirong 2025). Importantly, maternal weight gain over the 9 months of gestation is an anticipated feature of normal pregnancy (Kramer et al. 2024). However, if there is a difference between pre‐pregnancy weight and postpartum weight at a certain time (such as 6 months, 12 months), women will experience postpartum weight retention (PPWR), and the value of PPWR ≥ 5 kg is considered high postpartum weight retention (HPPWR) (Adamson et al. 2007; Quansah et al. 2020). PPWR is a potential risk factor for overweight and obesity over the short term (0–3 years) and long term (7–21 years) among postpartum women, and also contributes to the risk of suffering from chronic diseases such as diabetes, fatty liver disease, and cardiovascular disease (Ng et al. 2014; Mamun et al. 2014; Avorgbedor et al. 2022). Thus, how to identify PPWR in reducing future obesity‐related disease risk and promoting high‐quality maternal health has developed into a research priority for scholars.

Although some scholars have recognised the importance of identifying PPWR, the status of women's postpartum weight management is still unsatisfactory. A study by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) found that 48% of pregnant women gained more than the recommended range, and most women retained more than 5 kg by 1 year after delivery (FIGO Committee 2023; QuickStats 2015). Nearly 75% of low‐income women experienced PPWR at 1 year postpartum, including 47.4% of women who retained more than 4.53 kg (10 lbs) and 24.2% of women who retained more than 9.07 kg (20 lbs) (Endres et al. 2015). In Mexico, 36.6% of women were overweight, and 18.1% were obese at 1 month postpartum, increasing to 40.1% and 19.4% at 3 months postpartum (Mufti et al. 2019). A cohort study in China pointed out that 65.4% of women did not recover their pre‐pregnancy weight at 8 months postpartum, and 28.9% of them had high PPWR (Li et al. 2019). In India, 13% of women suffered from postpartum obesity, with a prevalence of over 40% in 37 districts (Kaur et al. 2021). Evidence from these studies shows that PPWR is a widespread and serious issue globally, constituting an ongoing health threat to women. Therefore, it is necessary to implement effective interventions to prevent and address PPWR.

The important step in preventing and addressing PPWR is identifying its risk factors effectively. Existing studies indicated that PPWR is associated with multiple factors, such as low pre‐pregnancy BMI, excessive GWG, non‐full breastfeeding, delivery by caesarean section, and unhealthy postpartum lifestyles (including incorrect dietary practices, low/moderate‐intensity physical activity, and inadequate sleep duration) (Hollis et al. 2017; Rong et al. 2015; Tahir et al. 2019; Quaderer et al. 2025; Kumari et al. 2023). Besides, maternal age, ethnicity, income, psychological status, education, and marital status may be considered predictors of PPWR (Yunan et al. 2016; Maturi et al. 2011; Bazzazian et al. 2023). However, the directionality and mechanism of these factors remain controversial and fragmented due to temporality and small sample size. Since PPWR results from a complex interaction of multiple risk factors, a systematic review of the literature is essential to identify those risk factors from different study designs and approaches.

Some scholars have attempted to identify risk factors for PPWR through systematic reviews. Meyer, Wang, and He discussed the impact of individual factors (GWG, mode of delivery, and postpartum lifestyles) on PPWR (Meyer et al. 2024; Wang, Chen, et al. 2024; Wang, Ji, et al. 2024; He et al. 2024). Kossou provided a limited summary of the risk factors of PPWR among African women (Kossou et al. 2023). Whilst their findings have contributed to understanding PPWR, these reviews have either focused solely on the population in a specific region or have limited themselves to a few risk factors, restricting the research content. Moreover, these studies lacked in‐depth analysis grounded in theoretical models or frameworks, failing to comprehensively recognise the risk factors and the influencing mechanisms for PPWR.

The Andersen model, as a widely applied theoretical framework for health service utilisation behaviour, indicated that individual health outcomes were jointly influenced by three dimensions: contextual characteristics, individual characteristics, and health behaviours (Andersen 1995; Shan and Yuee 2018). Contextual and individual characteristics are influencing factors at the external environment and internal situation levels, respectively. Both dimensions include predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and needs. Health behaviours refer to how individuals seek medical care for their health needs. Health outcomes are the assessments that reflect the effectiveness of individuals' utilisation of health care services. There are dynamic interactions (direct or indirect feedback) among the various dimensions of the model, constituting a non‐recursive structural equation model (Andersen and Davidson 2001). This model facilitates a more comprehensive dialogue with existing research and offers robust theoretical support for an in‐depth exploration of the factors influencing PPWR.

For these reasons, this integrative review proposes to systematically summarise and synthesise the risk factors and the association between risk factors and PPWR according to the Andersen model, which will facilitate the risk identification of PPWR as well as the construction of relevant risk assessment instruments to provide new insights for designing effective, long‐term strategies for relevant stakeholders, such as public health authorities, researchers, and community members.

Methods

Review Design

An integrative review followed Whittemore and Knafl's methodology and reported results by using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension (PRISMA) checklist (Whittemore and Knafl 2005; Page et al. 2021). The Whittemore and Knafl's methodology involves five stages: (1) problem identification, (2) literature search, (3) data evaluation, (4) data analysis, and (5) presentation of findings. This review has registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42024530948). The details of PRISMA checklist have been provided in the Supporting Information.

The eligibility criteria were defined following Population, Concept, Context (PCC) principles (Tricco et al. 2018).

Population

Women aged 18–45. It was because most women begin the period of peak ovarian reproductive and endocrine function at the age of 18, which lasts about 30 years (Le 2006). Women with infectious diseases, severe pregnancy complications, and mental illnesses were excluded.

Concept

PPWR was defined as the difference between postpartum and pre‐pregnancy weights at a certain time (e.g., 6 months, 12 months). To ensure completeness of the review, this review imposed no restrictions on the timing of measurements for PPWR, and the value of weight retention ≥ 5 kg was considered HPPWR (Quansah et al. 2020). Original quantitative studies, randomised controlled trials, and nonrandomized studies that explicitly reported the incidence of PPWR, risk factors, and the association between risk factors and PPWR were included. Original qualitative studies or original mixed‐methods studies that explicitly reported attitudes, perspectives, and experiences regarding PPWR, risk factors, and the association between risk factors and PPWR were included.

Context

Any setting, such as medical or other health care institutions. The literature screening consisted of two parts. Firstly, two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts based on the eligibility criteria. Secondly, two reviewers independently screened full‐text studies for inclusion or exclusion. Any disagreements regarding study selection and data extraction were resolved through consensus with a third reviewer. Considering that some editorial references were potential studies that were missed by the search strategy, this study conducted an additional hand search.

Study quality was evaluated by using a modified version of the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case–control and cohort studies (Wells et al. 2014). The NOS is a quality scale that judges studies based on three broad categories: (1) the selection of study groups, (2) the comparability of the groups, and (3) the ascertainment of exposure and outcome. Each item receives a star if the study is classified as high quality, except for the comparability item, which can receive two stars, totaling 9 points. Because the NOS is designed only to evaluate the quality of case–control or cohort studies, we used an adapted version for the cross‐sectional studies included in this review (Herzog et al. 2013). Notably, the adapted scale has added the ‘sample size’ rating in the ‘selection’ dimension and has specifically assigned one star to self‐reported outcomes. We categorised study quality based on their total scores, following previous publications: low (0–3), moderate (4–6), and high (7–9) (O'Sullivan et al. 2021). Two researchers independently rated the quality of the studies, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data Analysis and Presentation

Considering that the final included studies differed in study design, evaluation process, and measurement outcomes, high heterogeneity may limit the development of meta‐analyses, so a systematic content analysis was conducted. This analysis enabled iterative comparisons across primary data sources, which met the aim of an integrative review. The process involved data reduction, display, comparison, conclusion drawing and verification, and presentation (Whittemore and Knafl 2005).

The synthesis process was as follows:

Data reduction: The Andersen model was used as the prior theoretical framework, and its three dimensions (contextual characteristics, individual characteristics, and health behaviours) were established as coding categories ahead of data extraction. Two researchers independently extracted all risk factors associated with PPWR from the included studies. They classified them directly into the corresponding categories (predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, needs, use of personal health services, personal health practices, process of medical care) based on the operational definitions of each dimension. Where factors exhibited cross‐dimensional attributes, their classification was determined by tracing the primary explanatory logic in the original studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus‐building.Within this framework, contextual characteristics and individual characteristics were antecedents of health behaviours and were in a parallel structural relationship. Both dimensions included predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and needs. Predisposing characteristics were those that predate illness but may affect behaviours related to the use of long‐term care. Enabling resources were those that promote or inhibit the use of services in the event of disease. Need factors were those perceived and actual nursing needs that directly impacted long‐term care service utilisation. However, there were differences in measurement indicators among contextual and individual characteristics. Firstly, contextual characteristics. Its predisposing characteristics included demographic, social, and beliefs. Enabling resources included health policy, financing, and organisation. Needs included environmental and population health indices. Secondly, individual characteristics. Its predisposing characteristics included demographic, genetic, social, and beliefs. Enabling resources included financing and organisation. Needs included perceived and evaluated. Health behaviours refer to how individuals seek medical care for their health needs, including the use of personal health services, personal health practices, and the medical care process.

Results

Study Characteristics

Of 2750 studies screened, 96 full‐text studies were assessed, with 26 included. Twenty‐two cohort studies and four cross‐sectional studies were included. The exact process of literature screening is shown in Figure 2. Eleven studies were from China (nine from the Chinese mainland and two from Taiwan), four studies were from the United States of America, and two studies were from Brazil. There was one each from Denmark, Iran, Lebanon, Malaysia, the Netherlands, Norway, the United Kingdom, the United Arab Emirates, and Germany. In terms of national development patterns, 10 studies were from high‐income countries, 15 studies were from upper‐middle‐income countries, and one study was from lower‐middle‐income countries. Studies from low‐income countries were not identified in this review. The final sample included 41,449 postpartum women aged 18–45. Study Quality

The majority of the included studies were of moderate quality (n = 16), with seven rated as high quality and three as low quality. Most studies used an unrepresentative sample (n = 19) and failed to adequately control for confounding (n = 21). Nearly half of the cohort studies (n = 9) failed to complete follow‐up or had a follow‐up rate < 80% and no description of those lost. The Incidence of Postpartum Weight Retention

Twenty‐six studies derived PPWR values using the difference between postpartum and pre‐pregnancy weight (PPWR = postpartum weight—pre‐pregnancy weight). Most monitoring time points were 6 months (n = 18, 69.23%) and 12 months (n = 12, 46.15%) postpartum. The incidence of PPWR at 6 months postpartum was 10.6%–85.5%, and high PPWR was 35%–50%. The incidence of PPWR at 12 months postpartum was 5.2%–71.8%, and high PPWR was 20%–28.2%. The value of PPWR at 6 months postpartum was 1.4–6.61 kg, and at 12 months postpartum was 0.9–4.8 kg.

Three themes, nine sub‐themes, and 21 risk factors were determined according to the Andersen model. The themes and their sub‐themes are summarised in Figure 1.

Ten studies (38.46%) used the Institute of Medicine (IOM) GWG classification in 2009: insufficient (< 12.5 kg if underweight, < 11.5 kg if normal weight, < 7 kg if overweight and < 5 kg if obese), adequate (12.5–18 kg if underweight, 11.5–16 kg if normal weight, 7–11.5 kg if overweight and 5–9 kg if obese) and excessive (> 18 kg if underweight, > 16 kg if normal weight, > 11.5 kg if overweight and > 9 kg if obese) (Li et al. 2019; Hollis et al. 2017; Bazzazian et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2015; Xuehong et al. 2016; Shao et al. 2018; Shufang and Zhixu 2019; Archuleta and Chao 2021; Nasreddine et al. 2020; Brito et al. 2024). One study used the IOM GWG classification in 1990 (Olson et al. 2003). One study classified GWG into < 12 and ≥ 12 kg groups (Fadzil et al. 2018). One study classified GWG into < 15 and ≥ 15 kg groups (Aruna et al. 2016). One study classified GWG by using the quartile method: GWG ≤ P25 as the underweight GWG group, P25 to P75 as the moderate GWG group, and GWG ≥ P75 as the overweight GWG group (Meiyun and Yao 2018). Four studies were not classified (Lyu et al. 2009; Althuizen et al. 2011; Ai et al. 2015; Carvalho et al. 2023).

Needs‐Evaluated‐Lower pre‐pregnancy BMI: Six studies indicated that lower pre‐pregnancy BMI was associated with a higher incidence of PPWR with rank‐sum test, linear regression analyses, logistic regression analyses, and path analyses (Li et al. 2019; Hollis et al. 2017; Bazzazian et al. 2023; Xuehong et al. 2016; Meiyun and Yao 2018; Shufang and Zhixu 2019). Xuehong et al. (2016) demonstrated that if a woman's pre‐pregnancy BMI was lower, the PPWR would be higher at 6 and 12 months postpartum. Bazzazian et al. (2023) indicated that pre‐pregnancy BMI showed a direct and positive association with PPWR through path analyses.

Health Behaviours

(1) Use of personal health services‐Less access to postpartum care: Bazzazian et al. (2023) pointed out that access to postpartum care had both a direct positive and an indirect negative effect on PPWR. More access to the cyberspace of health systems and private centres showed an increase in PPWR.

(2) Personal health practices‐Feeding pattern: Six studies discussed the association between feeding pattern and PPWR with linear regression analyses, logistic regression analyses, and generalised estimation equation model (Olson et al. 2003; Zhenyan and Hongqiong 2016; Hollis et al. 2017; Shao et al. 2018; Fadzil et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019). Olson et al. (2003), Zhenyan and Hongqiong (2016), Hollis et al. (2017), Shao et al. (2018), and Fadzil et al. (2018) indicated that continuing breastfeeding to 1 year postpartum may benefit women in terms of PPWR, while women who chose artificial feeding were at a higher risk of PPWR. In contrast, Li et al. (2019) analysed the feeding patterns of postpartum women at multiple time points and controlled for other factors, concluding that women who continued breastfeeding for 8 months postpartum were more likely to develop PPWR. The differing conclusions may be related to the representativeness of samples. Only Hollis et al. (2017) met the NOS requirement for representativeness of the exposed cohort, while the other five studies exhibited selection bias.

(3) Personal health practices‐Inappropriate dietary intake: Seven studies reported that inappropriate dietary intake was associated with PPWR through linear regression analyses, logistic regression analyses, and linear mixed model (Olson et al. 2003; Lyu et al. 2009; Althuizen et al. 2011; Boghossian et al. 2013; Hollis et al. 2017; Fadzil et al. 2018; Nasreddine et al. 2020). Althuizen et al. (2011) further revealed the association between dietary intake and weight retention. He highlighted the fact that high intakes of saturated fatty acids in the first year postpartum were associated with a higher risk of substantial weight retention. Hollis et al. (2017) reported that lower serum vitamin D concentration in early pregnancy was associated with greater weight retention. Nasreddine et al. (2020) pointed out that participants with a high PPWR reported a greater intake of proteins, trans fat, cholesterol, sodium, and lower intakes of mono and polyunsaturated fat as compared to those with a low PPWR (below median).

(4) Personal health practices‐Less physical activity: Six studies explored that lower exercise frequency and lower exercise time were the essential predictors of PPWR with logistic regression analyses, linear regression analyses, linear mixed model, and chi‐square test (Olson et al. 2003; Lyu et al. 2009; Althuizen et al. 2011; Fadzil et al. 2018; Shufang and Zhixu 2019; Archuleta and Chao 2021).

(5) Personal health practices: Sleep duration: Bazzazian et al. (2023) pointed out that less sleep duration has a direct and negative association with PPWR through path analyses.

(6) Personal health practices—More sedentary behaviour: Zhenyan and Hongqiong (2016) revealed that too long postpartum sedentary time could increase the risk of weight retention with mixed‐effect model and multivariate logistic regression analyses.

(7) Personal health practices—Never used hot stone compression: Fadzil (2018) reported that theyhave never used hot stone compression and had significantly higher mean PPWR by linear regression analyses.

(8) Personal health practices—Unhealthy dietary intake pattern: Two studies explored the association between dietary patterns and PPWR through logistic regression analyses and linear regression analyses (Radwan et al. 2022; Brito et al. 2024). Radwan et al. (2022) revealed that adherence to the Mediterranean diet during pregnancy was associated with lower odds of PPWR with logistic regression analyses. Brito et al. (2024) pointed out that greater adherence to unhealthy dietary intake patterns based on red meat, eggs, industrialised products, processed foods, sausages, coffee, and primiparity was associated with maternal weight retention.

Overall, risk factors for PPWR could be categorised into contextual characteristics (social), individual characteristics (demographic, social, beliefs, financing, perceived, and evaluated), and health behaviours (use of personal health services, personal health practices). Among them, ethnic minority groups, Qatari nationality, lower maternal age, lost a job during pregnancy, lower postpartum nutritional awareness/beliefs, adverse psychosocial characteristics (anxiety/depression/distress), caesarean delivery, excessive GWG, lower pre‐pregnancy BMI, lower pre‐pregnancy weight, primiparity, less access to postpartum care, inappropriate dietary intake, less physical activity, less sleep duration, more sedentary behaviour, never used hot stone compression, unhealthy dietary intake pattern were the risk factors for PPWR. However, there were controversial findings regarding the directionality among three factors: education, feeding patterns, and income, which may be related to differing research quality (selection bias, information bias, and confounding bias) across these studies.

Discussion

The Accuracy of Postpartum Weight Retention Measurement Approaches Needs to be Examined

Accurately assessing PPWR is an important step in effectively implementing postpartum weight health management interventions. Included studies in this review used the difference between postpartum and pre‐pregnancy weight to derive the PPWR value, which was the most convenient and direct measurement. However, this measurement approach failed to distinguish PPWR from postpartum weight accrual, making it unsuitable for precisely assessing PPWR (Schneider et al. 2018). Besides, 12 studies identified PPWR and GWG based on BMI (Li et al. 2019; Hollis et al. 2017; Quaderer et al. 2025; Bazzazian et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2015; Xuehong et al. 2016; Meiyun and Yao 2018; Shao et al. 2018; Shufang and Zhixu 2019; Archuleta and Chao 2021; Legro et al. 2020; Brito et al. 2024). Wang indicated that BMI could not effectively differentiate between fat and muscle mass, nor could it provide an accurate reflection of a person's fat distribution (Wang, Chen, et al. 2024; Wang, Ji, et al. 2024). Similarly, waist circumference (WC), an indicator highly associated with BMI, failed to differentiate between fat mass and muscle mass. Some scholars suggested that ultrasound (Marín Baselga et al. 2025), conicity index (C‐index), and relative fat mass (RFM) could provide new perspectives for accurately assessing PPWR. Ultrasound could evaluate various fat compartments, including visceral, subcutaneous, and ectopic fat (Wang, Chen, et al. 2024; Wang, Ji, et al. 2024). C‐index was a superior anthropometric indicator for identifying the distribution of abdominal adiposity. RFM could be used to estimate overall body fat percentage. However, the strengths and weaknesses of these measurement approaches need to be robustly examined to provide a data reference for conducting empirical research on PPWR.

The Incidence of Postpartum Weight Retention Is Unsatisfactory, Especially in Upper‐Middle‐Income Countries With a Higher Incidence

Most countries had a high incidence of PPWR, especially some upper‐middle‐income countries (e.g., China, Iran) showed a higher incidence of high PPWR and PPWR values than high‐income countries (The incidence of PPWR at 6 months postpartum was 10.6%–76.5%, and the value of PPWR was 2.36–6.76 kg; the incidence of PPWR at 12 months postpartum was 68.9%–85.5%, the incidence of high PPWR up to 28.2%, and the value of PPWR was 1.48–5.42 kg). It may be related to the development of the country's socio‐economic system and changes in the food grain structure (Mendenhall and Singer 2019; NCD‐Ris 2024). The sustained development of the country's socio‐economic system and the in‐depth investment in innovative technologies stimulated the development of the food industry. There was a shift from subsistence and local foods to transported commercial foods in the food grain structure, characterised by diversification and convenience. Compared to traditional foods such as whole grains and fruits, commercial foods have increased total calories and a significantly higher proportion of sugars, saturated fats, trans fats, and salt influenced by food commercialisation and industrialisation (Pan et al. 2021). Excessive intake of commercial foods would increase the incidence of PPWR and obesity. Notably, some upper‐middle‐income countries, such as Brazil, had instituted the National Food and Nutrition Security System and endowed food and nutrition security with public policy status in 2006. Improving food quality and nutrition could decrease the possibility of obesity among women. Therefore, it is necessary to focus on food safety and nutritional quality (Wells et al. 2020). It should link with participants in food production, such as the Department of Agriculture, the Food and Drug Administration, investors, farmers, and nutrition organisations, strictly control the quality of commercial food production, and emphasise the development of a green, high nutritional, and healthy diet industry.

Insufficient High‐Quality Evidence on Risk Factors for Postpartum Weight Retention

This review identified three themes, nine sub‐themes, and 21 risk factors associated with PPWR according to the Andersen model. In terms of contextual characteristics‐predisposing characteristics, postpartum women who are members of ethnic minority groups or who hold Qatari nationality are more likely to experience PPWR. In terms of individual characteristics‐predisposing characteristics, postpartum women who are at a lower maternal age, lose their jobs during pregnancy, or have lower levels of postpartum nutritional awareness or beliefs are more likely to experience PPWR. In terms of individual characteristics‐needs, postpartum women who have adverse psychosocial characteristics (anxiety/depression/distress), caesarean delivery, excessive GWG, lower pre‐pregnancy BMI, lower pre‐pregnancy body weight, and are primiparous are more likely to experience PPWR. In terms of health behaviours‐use of personal health services, postpartum women who less access to postpartum care are more likely to experience PPWR. In terms of health behaviours‐personal health practices, postpartum women who have inappropriate dietary intake, less physical activity, less sleep duration, more sedentary behaviour, never use hot stone compression, and anunhealthy dietary intake pattern are more likely to experience PPWR. Among these, the majority of risk factors are modifiable, indicating that PPWR can be minimised by developing a comprehensive, evidence‐based health management strategy. However, most existing studies are quantitative, and there is less exploration of the individual experiences with difficulties in recovering pre‐pregnancy weight from a qualitative perspective. Hence, qualitative research and mixed‐methods research could be conducted to capture the non‐quantifiable influences on PPWR in the future to better refine the adjusted Andersen theoretical model for postpartum weight management and promote high‐quality maternal health.

Interestingly, there are controversial findings regarding the directionality among three factors: education, feeding patterns, and income, which may be related to differing research quality across these studies. Therefore, in order to properly examine the associations between education, feeding patterns, income and PPWR, it is essential to develop scientific, accurate, and effective empirical research designs to avoid weakening the strength of the evidence due to selection bias, information bias, and confounding bias.

Moreover, from the perspective of statistical analysis, most studies in this review employed regression analyses to verify the magnitude of influence of different independent variables on PPWR, but failed to explore the pathways through which these independent variables affect PPWR (Chen et al. 2022). Bazzazian et al. (2023) used path analyses to reveal the association between social determinants of health and PPWR, but its cross‐sectional study design limited the extent and depth of the survey and made it difficult to determine causal relationships between variables. Besides, the majority of included studies in this review had non‐representative study samples (due to insufficient sample size or absence of random sampling, resulting in low representativeness) (n = 19) and failed to control for confounding factors adequately (n = 22). Thus, it is possible to investigate the influence mechanisms of different variables on PPWR at various time points among postpartum women by conducting a multi‐center, large‐sample, high‐quality empirical study.

Limitations

This review has some limitations. First, this review focuses on factors contributing to PPWR, and not on interventions. This may be a limitation for readers who are interested in finding solutions to prevent PPWR. Another potential limitation is that the research population is limited. This review included studies from middle‐ and high‐income countries and failed to identify low‐income countries in South Asia and parts of Africa, which may limit generalizability. However, it does not mean these areas are not at risk of PPWR. They often have a ‘double burden’ of malnutrition, which is the coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition, due to their specific socio‐economic background. There is a need to focus on the status and risk factors of PPWR in low‐income countries to improve women's health and promote sustainable development. Furthermore, in terms of study design, this review identified only quantitative studies, and none of the qualitative or mixed‐methods studies were identified. Although these studies failed to quantify the association between PPWR and risk factors explicitly, they could provide valuable insights into the contextual, behavioural, or social determinants of PPWR. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a systematic review of qualitative and mixed‐methods studies on PPWR to provide a more comprehensive understanding of postpartum women's attitudes, perspectives, and experiences regarding PPWR.

Conclusions

PPWR is an important public health issue that cannot be ignored. A comprehensive identification of its risk factors may contribute to developing accurate health management strategies. This review reveals that the accuracy of PPWR measurement approaches needs to be examined to precisely assess PPWR and provide a data reference. Besides, the incidence of PPWR is unsatisfactory through a systematic review of existing studies, especially in upper‐middle‐income countries with a higher incidence. This review indicates that risk factors for PPWR can be categorised into three themes, nine sub‐themes and 21 risk factors according to the Andersen model: contextual characteristics (ethnic minority groups, Qatari nationality), individual characteristics (lower maternal age, lost a job during pregnancy, lower postpartum nutritional awareness/beliefs, adverse psychosocial characteristics (anxiety/depression/distress), caesarean delivery, excessive GWG, lower pre‐pregnancy BMI, lower pre‐pregnancy weight, primiparity) and health behaviours (less access to postpartum care, inappropriate dietary intake, less physical activity, less sleep duration, more sedentary behaviour, never used hot stone compression, unhealthy dietary intake pattern), most of which are modifiable. This suggests that comprehensively identifying risk factors as early as possible and developing scientific and effective strategies for postpartum weight management possess practical significance. In addition, some studies in this review had limitations in measurement and response bias due to a failure to control for confounders. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct multi‐centre, large‐sample, high‐quality empirical research to provide a strong evidence reference for refining the theoretical framework of PPWR.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The findings of this study provide both theoretical and practical insights into clinical nursing. Firstly, nursing managers should enhance education and training for nurses, midwives, and postpartum care providers on postnatal weight management. Increasing their understanding and awareness of how to identify risk factors for PPWR may provide a theoretical foundation for postpartum weight assessment, health education, risk screening, follow‐up, and referral. Also, nursing managers should regularly assess the ability of nurses, midwives, and postpartum care providers to evaluate postpartum weight and identify risk factors for PPWR, and the statistics can be used to further invest in professional development and redefine nursing curriculum. Thirdly, this integrated review contributes to the development of a risk assessment instrument for PPWR, laying a theoretical foundation for the construction and optimisation of predictive models of PPWR for nursing researchers.
