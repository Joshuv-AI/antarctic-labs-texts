Human In Vivo Disc Mechanics in Flexion, Extension, and Diurnal Loading With Aging and Degeneration
Authors: Harrah R. Newman, John M. Peloquin, Edward J. Vresilovic, Dawn M. Elliott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe intervertebral disc serves a critical role in spine function, but with aging and degeneration the disc undergoes changes that alter disc mechanics, and degeneration is often implicated in low back pain. The objective of this study was to quantify and evaluate the impact of aging and degeneration on in vivo lumbar disc mechanics across the lifespan.MethodsRepeated MRI was performed at a reference state and three loading states: flexion, extension, and diurnal loading for 84 subjects (evenly distributed by sex and age group from 18 to 83 years old). Intervertebral disc deformations were evaluated between the reference and loading states by change in wedge angle, anterior–posterior (A‐P) Shear displacement, and axial strain.ResultsFlexion induced the greatest changes in the lower discs of young subjects, but this shifted toward the central discs in older subjects. Flexion induced a gradient of anterior shear displacement in the upper levels and posterior shear displacement in the lower levels that was maintained across all ages. Extension had opposite trends in A‐P shear displacement with respect to level, with young subjects having greater posterior shear displacement at L1‐L2 and greater anterior shear displacement at L5‐S1 than older subjects. Extension also had large changes in wedge angle between L1 and L3. Diurnal loading consistently induced compression across all disc regions. Diurnal change in disc height was age‐dependent, with greater disc height loss in younger subjects.ConclusionsUsing MRI repeated in different loading states, we established a reference for expected disc behavior with controlled loading with aging, degeneration, and by disc level. Deviations in lumbar disc mechanical behavior from the reference baseline provided by this work may provide a useful marker of disc pathology.

Introduction

The intervertebral disc serves a critical role in spine function—maintaining and distributing large, multiaxial loads as a part of regular activity. The discs' multi‐tissue structure ensures it can withstand and distribute large axial compression, bending, and torsion loads while supporting spinal motion and stability. These tissue structures include the central nucleus pulposus (NP), which is proteoglycan rich, enabling high water content and pressurization, and the outer annulus fibrosus (AF), which is comprised of both proteoglycans, for sustaining compression and pressurization, and highly organized cross‐ply collagen, for supporting circumferential tension generated by the pressurized NP. Decades of tissue and spine segment cadaveric testing have elucidated the mechanical properties and behaviors of the disc and its tissues; however, there are few non‐invasive measurements of in vivo disc mechanics.

Spine kinematics in various loadings (postural changes) have been extensively measured by radiography, with some association between abnormal kinematics and low back pain [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Inclinometers have also been used to measure flexion curvature [1, 10], and stadiometry to measure diurnal and activity‐related height changes [1, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. More recently, MRI has begun to be used for this type of measurement [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. MRI is an ideal imaging modality for assessing the disc in vivo because it does not pose radiation concerns and provides significant soft tissue distinction. Accordingly, while the above work largely measured spine kinematics in terms of vertebral body positions, MRI is now being used to examine in detail the intervertebral discs themselves. We and others have successfully conducted repeated MRI to evaluate in vivo disc mechanics across several loading states; however, these assessments have been conducted on relatively small, young, and healthy populations [25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. The incidences of disc degeneration and low back pain increase with age, and the mechanics of the aging disc may be distinct from the young, healthy disc.

With disc degeneration and aging, progressive deterioration of the disc tissues alters disc mechanics and is often implicated in low back pain. The disc sustains compositional changes, particularly in the NP, which undergoes proteoglycan loss, water loss, and collagen accumulation [30, 31]. These compositional changes decrease NP pressure and alter the distribution of compressive load across the disc [32, 33]. Thus, with increasing age, the AF is subjected to greater compressive loads and the characteristic stress/load profile across the disc is increasingly variable with abnormal stress peaks [30, 33, 34, 35]. The structural and compositional changes that progress with regular aging are reflected in the high prevalence of degenerative features observable on imaging, the occurrence of which increases with age, independent of back pain and other symptoms [24, 36, 37, 38]. Furthermore, the lower lumbar levels tend to degenerate faster and more severely than the upper levels [39, 40, 41, 42, 43], such that aging may induce greater changes in the lower lumbar discs than in the upper levels. Given the variations in degeneration severity with age and disc level, it is imperative that studies of in vivo disc mechanics consider age, degeneration, and disc level.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to quantify in vivo lumbar disc mechanics across the lifespan, independent of back pain. To do so, repeated MRI was performed at a reference state and three loading states: flexion, extension, and diurnal loading. Flexion and extension (sagittal bending) were evaluated due to their clinical relevance; in many cases of mechanical low back pain, the pain can be mitigated or exacerbated by different postural states. Bending induces a gradient of disc wedge angle changes and anterior–posterior (A‐P) shear displacements through the lumbar spine, which enables evaluation of particular disc level's mechanics [25]. Diurnal compression loading was evaluated as it is a natural process that cycles the disc's interstitial fluid and height every day. The discs' capacity to handle daily compressive loads and fluid flow likely changes with aging and degeneration. Establishing these reference quantifications of disc mechanics may, in the future, help to distinguish disc pathology from expected aging changes.

Methods

To understand the impact of aging on disc mechanics, we conducted MRI across the lifespan to evaluate in vivo lumbar intervertebral disc mechanical responses to Flexion, Extension, and Diurnal loading. The subject population and MRI protocol are shared with a prior study that used the reference Supine scans to establish a statistical model of disc degeneration [44].

Subjects

Eighty‐four subjects were assessed with IRB approval and informed consent. To assess mechanical changes with age, subjects were evenly distributed across the lifespan such that 7 subjects of each sex per age decade (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–83 years old) were evaluated. These subjects were then distributed into 3 groups (18–39, 40–59, and 60–83 years old) for statistical analyses. Study inclusion criteria [44] were that the subjects never had back surgery, chronic low back pain (> 3 months duration), acute low back pain in the last 2 years, scored < 20% on the Oswestry Disability Index for low back pain [45], and had no conditions that would inhibit their safety in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Study Protocol

Subjects were asked to minimize their activity prior to the MRI session. Subjects arrived at the MRI facility early in the morning (~7 AM) and laid supine for at least 45 min to unload the spine from any unavoidable morning activity. Following spine unloading, subjects were scanned in supine, extension, and flexion positions (Figure 1).

Supine (AM and PM), extension, and flexion positions schematic and study timeline overview. The AM Supine scan was used as the reference to evaluate changes with flexion and extension and to evaluate diurnal changes to PM Supine. Unless otherwise noted, results are presented as the difference between the reference and loaded states.

In the supine position, the subject had a standard pillow under the head and a semi‐deformable knee wedge foam bolster (~8 in. maximum height) under the knees to enable the lumbar spine to be relaxed and make maximum contact with the MRI table. For extension, the pillow and knee wedge positions were maintained and a ~3 in., semi‐deformable foam bolster was positioned such that the bolster peak was beneath the most lordotic point of the subject's lumbar spine, just superior to the hip bone. For flexion, to enable the lumbar spine to relax into flexion bending, two pillows were positioned under the head, a semi‐deformable wedge was positioned under the shoulders, and the knee wedge bolster was rotated 180 degrees such that the shortest end (~3 in. tall) was positioned directly under the hip bone to raise the lower body.

Following the AM scanning session, subjects left the MRI facility and went about their regular daily activities (~10 h). Subjects returned to the MRI facility at least 10 h later (~7 PM) and the supine MRI was repeated (PM Supine) to assess diurnal loading effects.

MRI Protocol

The lumbar spine (L1‐S1) was assessed with MRI with three sagittal scans [25, 44, 46]: (1) a 3D T1‐weighted Fast Low Angle Shot (T1‐w FLASH) scan to capture the entire disc volume, (2) a single, mid‐sagittal slice T2‐weighted Carr‐Purcell Meiboom‐Gill (T2‐w CPMG) scan with 25 echoes of sequential multiples of 13.6 ms, up to 340 ms, and (3) a 3D T2‐weighted Turbo Spin Echo (T2‐w TSE) scan to assess disc degeneration by Pfirrmann grade [47] (Figure 2). All scans were conducted for the AM Supine Reference position. Only the FLASH, for assessing the disc geometry and mechanics, was repeated in the Flexion and Extension positions. Little fluid flow, and thus no significant change in T2 relaxation time, was expected between the AM Supine Reference and the Flexion and Extension MRIs. For the PM Supine scans, both the FLASH and CPMG scans were repeated as disc geometry and fluid content were both expected to change after 10 h of regular daily activities.

Spine Geometry

The sagittal Cobb angle was determined for all scan positions (AM Supine Reference, Flexion, Extension, PM Supine) to quantify spine curvature and quantify changes with posture. From the FLASH scan, the sagittal lumbar Cobb angle was evaluated as the angle between the inferior L1 and superior S1 [24, 25]. No change in Cobb angle was expected between the supine scans; however, Cobb angle changes were expected for the postural bending.

Disc Geometry

Discs were manually segmented in 3D, using ITK‐SNAP [48], from the sagittal T1w FLASH image slices in all scan positions [25, 44]. Disc volume was calculated as the segmented voxel quantity multiplied by voxel size (0.52 × 0.52 × 3.0 mm). The mid‐sagittal disc area was calculated as the number of pixels in the mid‐sagittal segmentation multiplied by in‐plane pixel size (0.52 × 0.52 mm) (Figure 3A). The mid‐sagittal anterior–posterior disc width was evaluated as the length of a line drawn from the anterior to the posterior of the disc in the mid‐sagittal slice (Figure 3A). Fiducial Markers (Figure 3B, labels 1–4) were placed at the end of the bone/disc interface to discriminate between the disc interspace and disc bulge. The disc height was calculated as the disc interspace area divided by the interspace width (Figure 3B).

T1‐w FLASH images were used to assess disc geometry and movement with loading. (A) Area was measured from the mid‐sagittal segmentation, and width was defined as the anterior–posterior length in the midsagittal slice. (B) Fiducial markers (1–4) were used to calculate disc height as the interspace area divided by interspace width. The wedge angle was calculated from the fiducial markers (C) in the AM Supine Reference and (D) in the Extension, Flexion, and PM Supine states. (E) A‐P shear displacement was calculated between the Reference and loaded states by the changes in fiducial marker locations. Anterior translation of the superior vertebra relative to the inferior vertebra is defined as anterior (positive) A–P shear displacement.

Disc wedge angle was measured using the same fiducial markers, with the wedge angle calculated as the acute angle (Figure 3C) between the resulting superior (fiducial line 1–2) and inferior (fiducial line 3–4) lines. The change in wedge angle (∆ wedge angle) was calculated as the difference between the loaded state's wedge angle (Figure 3D) and the reference state's wedge angle (Figure 3C). Lastly, the anterior–posterior shear displacement between the reference and loading states was determined by calculating the displacement vectors of the fiducial markers (Figure 3E) between reference (1–4) and loaded (1′, 2′, 3′, 4′) positions, then taking the average of the anterior–posterior component of the anterior and posterior displacement differences (A−PShear Disp=12∆1y+∆2y−∆3y−∆4y). The anterior–posterior and superior–inferior axes in these calculations were disc‐specific, derived from on its 3D shape using principal component analysis [25, 49, 50].

The reference state's fiducial markers were placed manually. The loaded states' wedge angle was determined by translating the reference state fiducial markers to the loaded states, using vertebral body motions obtained from rigid image registration of each vertebral body. The registration procedure was as defined in Peloquin+ 2026 Table 3 [51], with the exception that the registration was not initialized with its left–right axis parallel to the subject's left–right axis. The vertebral body registration method produced negligible errors in the ∆ wedge angle (error = −0.046° ± 0.33°), A‐P shear displacement (error = 0.036 ± 0.11 mm), and ∆ disc height (error = −0.016 ± 0.09 mm) [51]. The optimized vertebral body registration method employed had only Δ wedge angle variance of 0.3°–0.4°, whereas prior, manual methods produced Δ wedge angle variance of 2°–3° [51]. Out of 1512 registrations (6 VB × 84 subjects × 3 load states), 3 (0.2%) were excluded due to misalignment of the registration as identified by visual inspection.

Disc Strain

Axial disc strain was quantified in accordance with our prior work, with 3D image registration of the intervertebral disc between the AM Supine reference and Extension, Flexion, and PM Supine loading states [25, 49, 50]. For each of the Extension, Flexion, and PM Supine images, the MRI region containing each disc was registered using a deformable (diffeomorphic) displacement field to the AM Supine images. For validation, a manual disc segmentation of each loaded MRI (Extension, Flexion, or PM Supine) was warped to the reference AM Supine MRI using the registration's displacement field, and the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) was calculated between this warped segmentation and the original manual AM Supine segmentation (where 1 indicated perfect overlap and 0 was no overlap).

Registrations were run using ANTs [52], with both a mean square intensity (MSI) difference similarity metric and, separately, a cross‐correlation (CC) metric with a 3 pixel radius. The registration with the highest DSC was used for analysis [25]. From the 1260 registrations (5 discs × 84 subjects × 3 loaded states), 18 (1.4%) were excluded due to DSC < 0.75 and/or due to misalignment of the disc registration as determined by visual assessment. The DSC for this study after exclusions was 0.91 ± 0.03 (range 0.96–0.76), which is comparable to similar prior work [25, 53, 54].

Strain Regions

Each disc was divided into five regions: Anterior, Center, Posterior, Lateral (Left & Right), to evaluate average disc strain by region (Figure 4A) [25]. Axial disc strain was determined from the image registration displacement fields as the local change in disc height divided by the local height from the Reference MRI (Figure 4B) [25]. The average strain per region was then calculated and used to summarize the disc strain field (Figure 4C).

T2 Relaxation Times

The T2 relaxation times were assessed from the AM Supine reference and PM Supine scans and diurnal change in T2 time was calculated. As previously described [44], the T2 relaxation time was assessed from the T2‐w CPMG scan by two methods: by a circular region of interest (ROI) in the NP (Figure 5A) and by five disc regions along an anterior–posterior mid‐axial line (Figure 5B–D).

T2‐w CPMG images were used to determine T2 relaxation time. (A) Nucleus (NP) T2 time was calculated from the average intensity decay of a circular ROI and (B) across a mid‐sagittal anterior–posterior line. (C) The line was divided into five anatomic regions based on an average winged trapezoidal fit from discs with Pfirrmann Grade 1 or 2 (N = 184 discs). (D) The regions were anterior annulus (AAF), anterior transition (ATrans), NP, posterior transition (PTrans) and posterior annulus (PAF).

First, the T2 time was evaluated from the average intensity decay of a circular ROI in the disc NP in accordance with prior work (Figure 5A) [25, 44, 46]. The intensity decay was fit with a noise‐corrected, single‐term exponential fit as previously described [25, 46]. The NP ROI T2 relaxation time was used as a biomarker for disc health.

Second, regional variations in T2 time were evaluated across a 3‐voxel tall anterior–posterior line (Figure 5B). The MRI was sampled at 100 positions along the line, each of which was fit with a noise‐corrected exponential fit. The positions along the line were assigned to disc regions by fitting the resulting T2 vs. position profile to a winged trapezoidal function for discs with Pfirrmann grade 1 and 2 (N = 184), and averaging these trapezoidal fits to produce standardized region assignments by normalized anterior–posterior position (Figure 5C,D). Details are available in prior work [44]. The trapezoidal fit defined five regions of interest: anterior annulus fibrosus (AAF), anterior transition (Atrans), NP pulposus (NP), posterior transition (Ptrans), and posterior annulus fibrosus (PAF). The average T2 relaxation time per region was reported.

Pfirrmann Grade

Pfirrmann grade, which qualitatively evaluates disc health on a scale from 1 (healthy) to 5 (degenerate), was determined from the T2w CPMG scan [47]. Three trained graders analyzed the discs and the common grade across them was used. In cases of disagreement, the graders discussed until consensus was reached.

Age‐Corrected Degeneration (ACD) Score

We previously developed a statistical model to quantify disc level‐specific aging degeneration [44]. The model requires five inputs: disc level (L1‐S1), subject height, NP ROI T2 relaxation time, disc mid‐sagittal area, and disc volume. The model output is the expected effective age of the disc. The calculation of effective disc age enabled evaluation of discs by expected health/age and identification of discs with abnormal degeneration for their age and disc level through an Age‐Corrected Degeneration (ACD) Score.

The ACD Score is the difference between the calculated effective disc age and the subject's true age. An ACD score of zero indicates a disc aging as expected, a large, negative ACD score indicates a disc is less degenerated than expected for its age, and a large, positive ACD score indicates a disc is more degenerated than expected for its age. Given the asymptomatic population in the present study, the ACD scores were expected to be largely zero, within ±10 years, which was the root mean square error of the statistical model (Figure 6) [44].

The majority of discs (70%) had an age‐corrected degeneration (ACD) score less than 10 years, indicating degeneration consistent with expectations for the subject's age. Some discs (14.5%) had an ACD score less than −10 year, indicating the discs were less degenerated than expected for the subject age, while some (15.5%) had an ACD score greater than 10, indicating the discs were more degenerated than expected. All Pfirrmann grades are present in all 3 sections, shown by color scheme, indicating that Pfirrmann grade does not fully capture the age‐dependency of degeneration.

Statistical Methods

MRI measurements were summarized within 3 age groups: young (18–39 years old, N = 28 subjects), middle (40–59 years old, N = 28 subjects), and old (60–83 years old, N = 28 subjects); and by disc levels: L1‐L2, L2‐L3, L3‐L4, L4‐L5, and L5‐S1. To confirm whether the loading state caused a change, differences between the reference and loading states were checked for significant difference from zero with repeated matched pair t‐tests.

The effect of age and disc level were evaluated for significance between age groups (3 groups), disc levels (5 groups), and age groups crossed with disc levels (15 groups) with a linear mixed model with the following effects: age group (fixed), disc level (fixed), and subject ID (random). Subject sex was initially considered as a fixed effect, but had no significant effect, in agreement with others' findings [27, 41, 55], and was therefore excluded. The Tukey HSD test, which accounts for multiple comparisons, was used to assess differences between groups. Figures with box and whisker plots use standard quartiles, such that the center line is the median, the boundaries are 25% and 75% quartiles, and the whiskers are 1.5× the interquartile region; outlier points were excluded from plots but included in statistical assessments. These comparisons were made within each loading state and not across loading states.

Pearson correlations were evaluated between subject traits (height, weight, BMI, age), disc health measures (age‐corrected degeneration (ACD) score, NP ROI T2 relaxation time, Pfirrmann grade), and MRI mechanical measures (∆ wedge angle, A‐P shear displacement, regional strains) to systematically evaluate continuous relationships between aging, degeneration, and disc mechanical response. The Pearson's r values are reported in the text for significant (p < 0.05) correlations. There were no significant correlations between subject height/weight/BMI and any MRI measures. All statistical analysis was completed in JMP 17 Statistical software, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Confirmation of Induced Loading States

Measurement of the sagittal Cobb angle confirmed successful induction of lumbar Flexion (−9.5° ± 5.8°) and Extension (9.8° ± 4.3°), and no changes with Diurnal loading (0.1° ± 3.0°) (Figure 7). The reference (AM Supine) Cobb Angle was not age‐dependent; however, the Flexion Cobb angle was age‐dependent, but with low R 2, such that the induced loading states were largely independent of age.

Disc geometry was also assessed to confirm postural loading states across the lumbar region. The relative anterior–posterior shear displacement between the superior and inferior vertebrae for each loaded state agreed with prior work (Table 1) [25]. Wedge angle measurements also confirmed that the Flexion and Extension loading states induced the expected decrease and increase, respectively, in wedge angles across all lumbar levels (Table 2). The disc level trends will be further discussed in subsequent results sections by loaded state. Flexion

Disc Deformation

Disc deformations from flexion were evaluated between the reference AM Supine and Flexion states. Flexion‐induced changes in disc height, width, area, and volume did not depend on age or disc level so these changes are reported with all discs grouped (Figure 8A). Flexion bending subjected the disc to significant anterior compression, which tended to reduce the mid‐sagittal disc height (median = −1%) and push the annulus outwards inducing the increase in anterior–posterior width (median = 1%). The decreased mid‐sagittal height and displacement of the disc tissue laterally contributed to the decrease in mid‐sagittal area (median = −1%). The total disc volume was unchanged, indicating there was insufficient time under load for a significant fluid quantity to be pushed out of the disc.

(A) With Flexion bending, mid‐sagittal disc height decreased, anterior–posterior width increased, mid‐sagittal area decreased, and disc volume was unchanged. (B) Upper levels generally had anterior shear while lower levels had posterior shear (see also Table 1). (C) Average wedge angles changes were all negative. The youngest group had level‐dependent wedge angle changes and the L5‐S1 level had age group differences (see also Table 2). An asterisk (*) indicates the measurement was significantly different from zero, p < 0.05. Across disc levels, levels that do not share a letter are significantly different from each other; an absence of letters indicates no level‐dependent differences. A hashtag (#) indicates significant difference from the same level of the youngest age group (18–39 years old). All subfigures feature a reference line at y = 0.

Flexion bending induced a level‐dependent gradient of anterior–posterior shear throughout the lumbar region (Figure 8B and Table 1): the L1‐L2 had the greatest anterior shear (median = 0.3 mm), L2‐L3 had some anterior shear (median = 0.1 mm), L3‐L4 did not move consistently, L4‐L5 had posterior shear (median = −0.5 mm), and L5‐S1 had the greatest posterior shear (median = −0.7 mm). There were no age‐dependent changes in A‐P shear.

The ∆ wedge angles with Flexion were negative across all age groups and disc levels, with both age‐dependent and level‐dependent differences. Interestingly, the youngest group (18–39 years old) had level‐dependent wedge angles, where the inferior levels had progressively greater flexion ∆ wedge angle (Figure 8C and Table 2). In the middle and old age groups, there were no significant differences between disc level and ∆ wedge angle. The L5‐S1 ∆ wedge angle was the only age‐dependent level with significantly greater ∆ wedge angle in the young group compared to the middle and old age groups.

Disc Strain

Axial strain was evaluated in anterior, center, posterior, and lateral disc regions across disc levels and age groups. In agreement with the ∆ wedge angles, the anterior was generally compressed and the posterior extended. The anterior and posterior strains were significantly correlated with the ∆ wedge angle at all levels. The strongest correlations with ∆ wedge angle were in the lower levels for both anterior strain (L3‐S1, r ≥ 0.65) and posterior strain (L4‐S1, r ≥ 0.7). The compression‐extension gradient across the disc was age‐dependent, whereby the young subjects tended to have greater anterior and posterior strain magnitudes in the inferior levels, while the old subjects tended to have greater strain in the upper levels. This trend in strain distributions was present in individual subjects (Figure 9) and across the average regional strains (Figure 10).

Discs were resized to maximize strain distribution visualization (inconsistent size scale).

In general, there was little compressive strain in the center and lateral regions. The youngest age group (18–39 years old) had the most level‐dependent differences; the L1‐L2 had significantly less compressive strain compared to the lower levels in the anterior, center, and lateral regions. There was significantly greater posterior extension in the young group (18–39 years old) compared to the middle‐aged group (40–59 years old) at L5‐S1. Overall, the oldest group had greater central and lateral compression in the lower levels (L4‐S1), indicating a shift in the strain distribution in the oldest group.

Age and ACD Score Correlations

Correlations of MRI parameters with subject age (Figure 11A) were evaluated independently for each disc level. A‐P shear displacement was not correlated with subject age at any level. At L1‐L2, age was correlated with ∆ wedge angle (r = −0.26) and anterior strain (r = −0.23), such that young subjects had minimal changes while older subjects had anterior compression. Conversely, at L5‐S1, age was correlated with ∆ wedge angle (r = 0.36) and anterior strain (r = 0.28), such that young subjects had anterior compression while older subjects had minimal changes. At L3‐L4, posterior strain was correlated with age (r = 0.23) such that older subjects had greater posterior extension. At L5‐S1, posterior strain was correlated with age (r = −0.37) such that young subjects had posterior extension and older subjects had minimal changes.

Extension Results

Disc Deformation

Disc deformations from extension were evaluated between the AM Supine and Extension states. Extension‐induced changes in disc height, width, area, and volume did not depend on age or disc level so these changes are reported with all discs grouped (Figure 12A). Extension bending subjected the disc to significant anterior extension, which increased the mid‐sagittal disc height (2%) and distended the annulus superiorly/inferiorly, reducing the anterior–posterior width (−1%). The mid‐sagittal area was not significantly changed, likely due to an overall equilibration achieved by the opposing changes in height and width within the mid‐sagittal plane. The total disc volume was unchanged, indicating there was insufficient time under load for a significant fluid quantity to be pushed out of the disc. These changes are approximately opposite those seen in Flexion.

There were no age‐dependent or level‐dependent changes in disc height, width, area, or volume. (B) Upper levels generally had posterior shear, while the central and lower levels had anterior shear (see also Table 1). (C) The wedge angle changes were all positive (see also Table 2). An asterisk (*) indicates the measurement was significantly different from zero, p < 0.05. Across disc levels, levels that do not share a letter are significantly different from each other. Extension bending induced a level‐dependent gradient of A‐P shear displacement throughout the lumbar region. There were no age‐dependent changes and the change with level was inverted from the motions measured with Flexion bending (Figure 12B and Table 1). The upper levels, L1‐L3, had the greatest wedge angle increases with a trend of less ∆ wedge angle lower down the spine.

Discussion

The in vivo disc mechanics in asymptomatic lumbar spines across the lifespan were successfully evaluated with repeated MRI in Flexion, Extension, and Diurnal loading. The collected dataset and results provide a valuable baseline for expected disc mechanical behavior with particular considerations for subject age and disc level across these three loading cases.

Effect of Aging and Degeneration

The three loading conditions measured in this study are influenced in different ways by established aspects of spine anatomy, aging, and disc degeneration. For example, flexion and extension movements are limited by the facet joints and capsule, the intervertebral disc, and surrounding muscles and ligaments [30, 34, 56, 57]. Older discs are stiffer due to progressive reduction of hydrophilic capacity with proteoglycan breakdown with aging [27, 34]. We and others have previously found that disc height generally decreases with increasing age and degeneration, such that older subjects have shorter discs [34, 44, 58, 59].

Across the three loading states evaluated, only Flexion had significant Δ wedge angle differences between the 3 age groups evaluated (18–39 years old, 40–59 years old, 60–83 years old). The Δ wedge angle with Flexion in the young group was significantly greater in the L5‐S1 compared to older age groups. With reduced disc height and increased tissue stiffness, the anterior capacity to compress in flexion is reduced (Figure S4), resulting in the decreased ∆ wedge angle seen in the inferior levels of older subjects compared to the young group. Further, while the young subjects have relatively tall and healthy inferior discs to enable their flexion bending, the older subjects' reduced capacity for flexion bending in the inferior levels resulted in similar magnitudes of Δ wedge angle and strain across the entire lumbar region.

In extension there were no significant differences in Δ wedge angle and strain between age groups; however, there were some significant correlations between disc strain and age. While the strain magnitudes were greatest in the anterior and posterior regions, the correlations with age were in the center and lateral regions of the central L2‐L3 and L3‐L4 levels, indicating that the distribution of extension load and bending changes with age. In the central levels, young subjects had minimal (~0) strain in the center and lateral regions, but with increasing age and disc degeneration strain increased. This indicates a shift of the zero‐strain axis from the mid‐lateral in young subjects toward the posterior in older subjects. The shift is likely due to a combination of factors potentially including the reduced disc height in the older/degenerate discs, degeneration altering NP migration with bending [56, 60], as well as reduced or altered support of the facet joint and supporting muscles/ligaments [61], such that the load distribution is altered but still achieves a relatively consistent disc Δ wedge angle.

Prior measurements of diurnal disc strain has been largely conducted in younger and smaller populations, without examination of age‐related variation [12, 25, 29, 62, 63, 64, 65]. With the larger sample size and broader distribution of subject ages, diurnal changes in disc height were found to be significantly correlated with age at all levels, but normalized changes in disc height (i.e., strain) were at best, weakly correlated, depending on disc level. Thus, except at L5–S1, the amplitude of the diurnal height change cycle diminishes with age roughly in proportion to the reduction of disc height with age. Additional prior work has been published on age‐related variation in the diurnal T2 cycle. Some prior work has suggested that the diurnal T2 change only occurs in young subjects [65], while other work has found diurnal T2 changes in subjects of all ages [1, 12, 63]. In the present work we found a small (< 10 ms on average) but significant change in NP T2 time for all age groups.

Effect of Disc Level

Both flexion and extension bending had level‐dependent variations in wedge, shear displacement, and regional strains which aligned with prior work [10, 24, 25, 66, 67, 68]. In Young subjects, Flexion bending induced the greatest anterior/posterior strains and Δ wedge in the lower levels. With increasing age, the amount of flexion in the lower levels diminished, resulting in a more even distribution of Δ wedge angle across levels and a smaller total applied Δ Cobb angle. Previous work identified that lower disc levels potentially age at an accelerated rate compared to upper levels [39, 40, 41, 42, 43], so this altered distribution of flexion across levels could be due to accelerated deterioration of the lower levels with aging. Differences between levels in the degree of degeneration expected from normal aging are addressed by the effective disc age statistical model [44] which is level‐specific.

The greatest extension was induced at upper levels, L1‐L2 and L2‐L3, which consistently had posterior compression and anterior/center/lateral extension. With increasing distance between the center of the induced extension (the position of the bolster) (i.e., lower down the spine) there was a notable decrease in posterior compression, possibly due to facet joint contact or limited hip extension that could be achieved within the confines of the MRI bore. The most strain was in the posterior region, which is consistent with prior studies [25, 29].

Disc Strain States: Incremental Strain, Residual Strain, and True Strain

The strain measurements made herein and in similar works [25, 26, 28, 56, 69, 70], despite being called “strain”, are Incremental strains calculated with respect to an in vivo reference state, in which the disc is still subject to physiologic loading, and not the True strain which is mechanically defined relative to a stress‐free reference state. In addition, the intervertebral disc's tissues have an inherent Residual strain, such that the tissues are constantly in a loaded state even if all externally applied loads were absent [71, 72, 73]. Therefore, a true stress‐free reference configuration may not exist, and reported disc strains must be interpreted as Incremental strain overlaid on an ever‐present and poorly characterized resting in vivo strain state [25, 29, 49, 50, 70, 71, 73, 74]. It is further likely that in vivo strains are impacted to varying degrees by aging, degeneration, disc level, and tissue regions [71, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79] Some efforts have been made to represent resting in vivo strain in disc and spine models by imposing an NP pressurization consistent with discography measurements, and using the resulting state as a reference state [72, 80, 81]. Further work is needed to evaluate the magnitude and contribution of residual strain to disc behavior. The accuracy of strain measures was not calculated, though similar prior work has shown accuracy of ~10% of voxel size [26].

Comparisons With Prior Work

The flexion and extension states achieved in this study (~10°) reflect the bending achievable within the confines of the MRI bore, not the full lumbar range of motion. In prior radiography/inclinometer studies, which allow for larger movements, subjects achieved a maximum of ~40°–50° flexion and ~10°–20° extension [7, 68, 82]. For disc wedge angles evaluated with radiography in prior work [68], at 10° flexion, the change in wedge angles was ~1°–4° and at 10° extension, the change in wedge angles was ~2°–3°, which are similar to the wedge angles measured in the present study (Table 2) for both states. The individual discs' range of motion is known to be impacted by age, where decreasing NP pressure with aging reduces the disc's range of motion [33, 83]; notably, however, within the present work we did not observe any significant impact of age with extension, and only a few instances of age impacting flexion outcomes. This is not surprising, since the present study did not evaluate the extent to which range of motion is impacted with aging and degeneration, but rather used bending states that could be reasonably achieved for subjects of all ages.

Limitations

While this study provides a new and important reference of in vivo disc mechanics, it was not without limitations. The postural loading states were static, passive, and limited by the confines of the MRI bore; they did not account for creep or relaxation of the spinal tissues that could arise over a longer time period. The order of bending states was not randomized, as it was most time‐efficient to proceed from supine to extension and then flexion last. For diurnal loading, we did not control the 10 h of daily activity subjects conducted between scans which could vary depending on subjects' occupations; however, prior work in young populations showed no correlations with subject activity [25, 29]. The flexion and extension were induced by bolsters, such that subjects with greater natural lordotic curvature were subjected to less induced extension, though there was no such relationship for Flexion (Figure S5). Bolster‐induced postures also did not allow us to quantify the applied external loads or full lumbar range of motion (which could be impacted by aging and additional subject factors such as BMI). Both bending states are also potentially impacted by the function and health of supporting spinal structures and muscles, which were not evaluated directly in the present study.

While significant correlations and group differences were present, the correlation coefficients were relatively low and some group medians were also near zero, such that the generalizability of the study outcomes may be limited by inherent biological variability. However, given the broad range of these relationships and group ranges, future identification of discs which behave outside of these expectations as abnormal and potentially pathological may be possible. The intervertebral disc is not the only tissue of interest for spine function and in future work evaluation of surrounding structures and tissues will be included. The disc strain was evaluated as the average axial strain spanning the full disc height, such that voxel‐level variations across the disc height were not evaluated because assessment of voxel‐level variations would require validation of internal strain fields which remains an open challenge in image analysis. Finally, this work provides a reference of expected asymptomatic disc mechanics by disc level with age. It is not yet known which loading state or combination of loading states will be most informative for identifying disc pathology or abnormal behaviors in symptomatic subjects.

Future Work in Pathological Applications

While many degenerative changes progress with age, they complicate isolation of pathological mechanisms. Mechanical low back pain has been associated with disc degeneration [30, 36, 59], but specific mechanisms to distinguish between normal aging changes and pathological disc changes are limited. In vivo assessment of disc stress profiles found that asymptomatic and painful discs had similar peak stresses, but painful discs tended to have lower mean stress, multiple stress concentrations, and greater anisotropic stress distributions [35]. Assessment of the aging disc's mechanical behavior is necessary to enable isolation of pathological changes from aging degeneration. The present work provides context for expectations of the mechanical changes that the asymptomatic, aging disc undergoes with time. The present dataset is also valuable as a basis to support development and validation of physiologically accurate spine models that account for variation with aging.

Conclusion

We successfully quantified the mechanics of the asymptomatic lumbar spine in bending and diurnal loading with repeated MRI, using a study cohort that spanned all adult ages. Asymptomatic disc aging and degeneration often muddle identification of pain sources in people with LBP. Evaluation of disc mechanical behavior could be used to evaluate disc function and aid in elucidating pain sources/causes and enable development of improved, targeted therapies and treatments. Using MRI repeated in different loading states we were able to establish a reference for expected disc behavior with controlled loading with aging by disc level. Flexion induced the greatest changes in the lower lumbar discs of younger subjects, which reduced with age. Extension responses exhibited less age‐dependency. Deviations in lumbar disc mechanical behavior from the reference baseline provided by this work, that is, abnormal mechanics—may provide a useful marker of pathological disc dysfunction. If so, in future work, this may allow pathological disc dysfunction to be distinguished from expected changes with age and identification of specific disc levels with pathological significance in symptomatic individuals.
