Interpretation of stochastic primitive equations with relaxed hydrostatic assumption as a higher order approximation of 3D stochastic Navier-Stokes
Arnaud Debussche, Étienne Mémin, Antoine Moneyron

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the convergence of solutions of a stochastic representation of the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations to those of their primitive equations counterpart. Our analysis covers both weak and strong convergence regimes, corresponding respectively to rigid-lid and "fully periodic" boundary conditions. Furthermore, we explore the impact of relaxing the hydrostatic assumption in the stochastic primitive equations by retaining martingale terms as deviations from hydrostatic equilibrium. This modified model, obtained through a specific asymptotic scaling accessible only within the stochastic framework, captures non-hydrostatic effects while remaining within the primitive equations formalism. The resulting generalized hydrostatic model has been shown to be well-posed when the additional terms are regularized using a suitable filter for divergence-free noises under suitable assumptions. Within this setting, we demonstrate that the model provides a higher-order approximation of the 3D Navier-Stokes equations for appropriately scaled noises.

Introduction

Representing ocean fluid flows through stochastic modeling is a dynamic and evolving area of research. Due to the vast range of interacting scales, from large-scale solar heating and atmospheric forcing to small-scale turbulent dissipation, it is computationally infeasible to resolve turbulent fluid motion explicitly with high accuracy. Consequently, only approximate models are tractable for practical applications. Furthermore, three-dimensional turbulent flows exhibit strong chaotic behavior and can give rise to spontaneous stochasticity due to the non-uniqueness of solutions. Stochastic modeling has emerged as a powerful and flexible framework for capturing unresolved variability and uncertainty in such systems

. Societal needs related to climate change, economic and strategic concerns, as well as the monitoring of catastrophic events, also call for a transition toward probabilistic forecasting. Such approaches aim to provide physically relevant ensembles of realistic realizations, enabling, in particular, the quantification of uncertainties associated with specific events.

Stochastic modelling and calculus provide a rigorous framework for addressing these challenges. Early models in this domain were largely phenomenological, grounded in turbulence studies that often involved energy backscattering across scales
[
33
,
37
]
, and introduced stochastic parameterizations through multiplicative random forcing
[
9
,
47
]
. However, the noise variance in such models is typically uncontrolled

, necessitating the addition of an eddy viscosity term to absorb excess energy. While the precise form of this viscosity remains uncertain, it is commonly derived using the debated Boussinesq turbulence assumption to obtain plausible approximations
[
46
]
. Moreover, when random forcing is introduced without any connection to underlying conservation laws, the resulting systems often suffer from limited accuracy and reduced interpretability
[

]
.

Over the past decade, the location uncertainty (LU) approach has been developed and studied to propose physically consistent stochastic models
[

based on the work of
[

. Additionally, the study revealed that the stochastic model remains continuous as the noise vanishes, establishing a strong consistency with the deterministic Navier-Stokes equations. In the deterministic framework, the primitive equations are commonly used to model geophysical flows
[
50
]
. These equations are derived from the 3D Navier-Stokes equations, assuming that vertical acceleration is negligible. This leads to the classical hydrostatic equilibrium, where the vertical derivative of pressure is related to density fluctuations. Although, this balance is physically valid in large-scale ocean dynamics, it breaks down outside the shallow water regime or in the presence of thermodynamic effects, such as deep convection. This assumption is further referred to as the
strong hydrostatic hypothesis
. Notably, this deterministic model is known to be well-posed
[
10
]
under appropriate boundary conditions (rigid-lid), and its stochastic versions have been largely explored by the mathematical community. As a matter of fact, the well-posedness of the stochastic primitive equations with multiplicative noise was demonstrated in
[
18
,
17
]
, and more recently, with a specific class of regular transport noise in
[
8
]
. In a more recent study, it was shown that the stochastic primitive equations with transport noise, similar to the LU framework, are well-posed under the strong hydrostatic hypothesis, using water world boundary conditions
[
1
]
. However, this work assumes that the horizontal noise is independent of the vertical axis, which simplifies the treatment of the barotropic and baroclinic noises. This implies that the noise is bidimensional if divergence-free using rigid-lid boundary condition.

As pointed out previously, the strong hydrostatic balance does not allow to represent deep convection phenomena with strong up or down-welling of water. The LU setting allows to relax easily the strong hydrostatic balance, by considering the martingale terms of the vertical acceleration as deviation terms. This yields a generalisation of the primitive equations, which has been studied in
[
20
]
. There, the authors proposed various models to interpret the relaxed (or weak) hydrostatic assumption. This modification, which captures non-hydrostatic effects while remaining within the framework of the primitive equations, arises from a specific scaling that is only accessible in the stochastic setting. In particular, they proved the well-posedness of a low-pass filtered version of the model with relaxed hydrostatic assumption, using the fact that the horizontal noise is independent of the vertical axis as in
[
1
]
.

In addition, it has been shown that the solutions to the weak solutions to the 3D Navier-Stokes equations converge to the ones of the primitive equations for a vanishing aspect ratio
[
34
]
,
for a domain that is periodic in the three directions. A similar result about the convergence of the strong solutions is also established, proving that the (deterministic) 3D Navier-Stokes are well-posed for thin enough domains.

Following a similar method, we analyse the asymptotic error between the LU 3D Navier-Stokes equations to the low-pass filtered LU primitive equations, in the stochastic setting. One noticeable difference is that we use rigid-lid boundary conditions to study the
L^{2}-H^{1}
convergence. However, we commute back to the "fully periodic" domain in the study of the
H^{1}-H^{2}
convergence. In particular, we show that there exist regimes where the regularised tridimensional LU Navier-Stokes equations can be efficiently approximated by the LU primitive equations with (regularised) weak hydrostatic assumption, but not by the strongly hydrostatic ones. The parameters of interest are the aspect ratio
\epsilon
and a vertical noise coefficient
\alpha_{\sigma}
. In turn, we show that the regime mentioned above corresponds to
\alpha_{\sigma}=o(\epsilon^{-1})
but
\alpha_{\sigma}\neq o(\epsilon^{-1/2})
. When
\alpha_{\sigma}=o(\epsilon^{-1/2})
, we also show the convergence towards the strongly hydrostatic primitive equations.

Our study goes beyond extending deterministic results of
[
34
]
to a stochastic setting, the main challenge being to find a suitable framework in which we can use the tools of stochastic calculus. Specifically, we introduce a scaled gradient, to express the pressure terms arising in the scaled Navier-Stokes equations, as well as a scaled inner product. Then, we define a “modified” Leray projector to cancel these scaled gradient terms, in order to use the tools of the classical Leray theory. This is close in spirit to what was done in
[
8
]
for the primitive equations. In particular, this “modified” projector is crucial in our stochastic setting, since Itō’s lemma yields covariation contributions. Thus, our projector allows to cancel the terms stemming from the scaled gradient of the stochastic pressure. In this framework, we infer that the blow-up time of the scaled Navier-Stokes equations tends to infinity in probability for a vanishing aspect ratio. This stands as a generalisation of the well-posedness result of the Navier-Stokes equations for thin enough domains in the stochastic context. Notice that, to obtain a result similar as that of the deterministic setting, we would typically need almost sure energy estimates to use the arguments of
[
34
]
, while we only have second order moment estimate in our study. This difficulty and type of result are common for SPDEs.

In the following we introduce the LU framework and precise our assumptions. Then we describe the abstract setting of this study and state our main results. After this, we prove the convergence of the weak solution of LU 3D Navier-Stokes equations to the ones of the LU primitive equations, with rigid-lid boundary conditions. Thus, the solutions of the latter equations with strong hydrostatic hypothesis stand as zeroth order approximations of the solutions of the former, when the primitive equations noise is bidimensional. Moreover, our estimates allow to interpret the weakly hydrostatic LU primitive equations as a higher order approximation of the LU Navier-Stokes equations, using a specific scaling on the vertical velocity noise contribution. We finally establish a similar result for strong solutions, in "fully periodic" boundary conditions.

2
The LU formalism

In this section,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
denotes a 3D bounded spatial domain. The LU formulation is based on the following time-scale separation of the Lagrangian displacement
X
of flow:

dX_{t}=u(X_{t},t)dt+\sigma(X_{t},t)dW_{t}.

(2.1)

Importantly, this decomposition must be interpreted in the Itō sense. Moreover, the velocity component
u(X_{t},t)
denotes the large-scale Eulerian velocity (correlated in both space and time), and
\sigma(X_{t},t)dW
is a highly oscillating unresolved velocity (uncorrelated in time but correlated in space). The latter term is referred to as the (displacement) noise term.

Let us define this noise term more precisely. Consider a cylindrical Wiener process
W
in the space of square integrable functions
\mathcal{W}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
. Then there exists a Hilbert orthonormal basis
(e_{i})_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}
of
\mathcal{W}
, and a sequence of independent standard Brownian motions
(\hat{\beta}^{i})_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}
on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t},\mathbbm{P})
such that,

W=\sum_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}\hat{\beta}^{i}e_{i}.

Notice, however, that the sum
\sum_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}\hat{\beta}^{i}e_{i}
does
not
converge in
\mathcal{W}
. Thus, the above identity only makes sense in a larger space
\mathcal{U}\supset\mathcal{W}
, such that the embedding
\mathcal{W}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{U}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. For instance,
\mathcal{U}
may be taken as the dual of a reproducing kernel Hilbert subspace
of
\mathcal{W}
for the inner product
(\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})_{\mathcal{W}}
, e.g.
H^{-s}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
with
s>\frac{3}{2}
. We then define the noise through a deterministic, time dependent correlation operator
\sigma_{t}
. Let
\hat{\sigma}:[0,T]\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{2},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
be a bounded symmetric kernel and define

(\sigma_{t}f)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}\hat{\sigma}(x,y,t)f(y)dy,\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{W}.

With this definition,
\sigma_{t}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator mapping
\mathcal{W}
into itself, so that the noise can be written as

\sigma_{t}W_{t}=\sum_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}\hat{\beta}_{t}^{i}\sigma_{t}e_{i},

where the series converges in
\mathcal{W}
almost surely, and in
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{W})
for all
p\in\mathbbm{N}
. We interpret
\mathcal{W}
as the space carrying the Wiener process
W_{t}
, while we retain the notation
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
to denote the space of tridimensional velocities.

Moreover, there exists a Hilbert basis
(\phi_{n})_{n}
of
\mathcal{W}
consisting of eigenfunctions of the correlation operator
\sigma_{t}
, scaled by their corresponding eigenvalues. By a change of basis, there exists a sequence of independent standard Brownian motions
(\beta_{t}^{k})_{k}
, defined on the same filtered probability space, such that

\sigma_{t}W_{t}=\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}\beta_{t}^{k}\phi_{k}.

As such,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t},\mathbbm{P},W)
forms a stochastic basis. We may associate a covariance tensor to the random field
\sigma W_{t}
. Given two points
x,y\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, and two times
t,s\in\mathbbm{R}^{+}
, define the covariance tensor
Q
formally by

Q(x,y,t,s)\delta_{t,s}=\mathbbm{E}[(\sigma_{t}dW_{t})(x)(\sigma_{s}dW_{s})(y)]=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}\hat{\sigma}(x,z,t)\hat{\sigma}(z,y,s)dz\delta(t-s).

The diagonal part of this covariance tensor (in the sense of a 4x4 tensor) is referred to as the variance tensor, and is denoted

a(x,t)=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}\hat{\sigma}(x,y,t)\hat{\sigma}(y,x,t)dy=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\phi_{k}(x,t)\phi_{k}(x,t)^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3}.

(2.2)

We assume in addition that the variance tensor satisfies the integrability condition
a\in L^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3}))
. In the more general setting – when
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
is itself a random function – the random matrix-valued process
a
must satisfy the following integrability condition,

\mathbbm{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|a(\boldsymbol{\cdot},t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}<\infty,

where the norm
\|\boldsymbol{\cdot}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}
is the Hilbert norm on the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})
, equipped with the Frobenius matrix norm. Under these conditions, the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dW_{s}
defines a
\mathcal{W}
-valued Gaussian process with zero mean and finite variance:
\mathbbm{E}\big[\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dW_{s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]<\infty
. Finally, the quadratic variation of
\int_{0}^{t}(\sigma_{t}dW_{s})(x)
is given by the bounded variation process
\int_{0}^{t}a(x,s)ds.

Similarly to the classical derivation of the Navier-Stokes equations, the LU Navier-Stokes equations are derived using a stochastic version of the Reynolds transport theorem (SRTT)
[
35
]
. Let
q
be a random scalar quantity defined within a volume
\mathcal{V}(t)
transported by the flow. For incompressible unresolved flows – i.e., when
\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma_{t}=0
– the SRTT reads

\displaystyle d\Big(\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}q(x,t)dx\Big)=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\big(\mathbbm{D}_{t}q+q\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}(u-u_{s})dt\big)dx,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{D}_{t}q=d_{t}q+(u-u_{s})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla q\>dt+\sigma dW_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla q-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla q)dt,

(2.4)

where an additional drift
u_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}a
, termed the Itō-Stokes drift in
[
5
]
, appears. Here,
d_{t}q(x,t)=q(x,t+dt)-q(x,t)
is the forward time increment at a fixed spatial point
x
, and
\mathbbm{D}_{t}q
is a stochastic transport operator introduced in
[
35
,
41
]
, playing the role of the material derivative. Moreover, the Itō-Stokes drift is directly related to the divergence of the variance tensor
a
, which accounts for the effects of noise inhomogeneity on large scale dynamics. Such advection terms are commonly added as corrective terms in large-scale simulations, to represent the impact of surface waves and Langmuir turbulence
[
15
,
38
]
. As shown in
[
5
]
, the LU framework exhibits similar features, generalizing the effects of the small-scale inhomogeneity on the large-scale flow.

Furthermore, the stochastic transport operator features physically interpretable terms that contribute to a large-scale representation of fluid flows. The last term on the right-hand side of (
2.4
) is an inhomogeneous diffusion, representing the mixing effects induced by unresolved small-scale variability. This stochastic diffusion is entirely determined by the variance tensor of the noise and may be viewed as a matrix-valued generalization of the classical Boussinesq eddy viscosity assumption. The third term on the right-hand side corresponds to the transport of the large-scale quantity,

q
, by the unresolved (random) velocity fluctuations. Remarkably, the energy injected by this backscattering term is exactly balanced by the dissipation due to the stochastic diffusion term, as shown in
[
41
]
. This energetic balance can be interpreted as a direct manifestation of a fluctuation-dissipation relation at the level of the coarse-grained equations.

3
Scaled Navier-Stokes equations and primitive equations in the LU formalism

To investigate the convergence of the Navier-Stokes equations to the primitive equations within the LU framework, we begin by deriving a scaled version of the former, which formally converges to the latter in the limit of vanishing aspect ratio. Consider the LU Navier-Stokes equations posed on a thin domain
\mathcal{S_{\epsilon}}=\mathcal{S}_{H}\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\subset\mathbbm{R}^{3}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{H}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{2}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}_{H}
. The parameter
\epsilon>0
represents the aspect ratio, defined as
\epsilon=\frac{H}{L}
, with
H
and
L
denoting the characteristic vertical and horizontal length scales, respectively.

Throughout, we adopt the shorthand notations
\nabla_{H}=(\partial_{x}\>\partial_{y})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
and
\nabla_{3}=(\partial_{x}\>\partial_{y}\>\partial_{z})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
, to denote the horizontal and full spatial gradients. Accordingly, for a scalar or vector-valued field
f
, we define the horizontal and three-dimensional Laplace operators as

\Delta_{H}f:=\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\nabla_{H}f)=(\partial_{xx}+\partial_{yy})f,\quad\Delta_{3}f:=\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\nabla_{3}f)=(\partial_{xx}+\partial_{yy}+\partial_{zz})f.

(3.1)

We now state the three-dimensional LU Navier-Stokes equations, which are derived by applying the SRTT to the mass and momentum equations. The velocity field is decomposed as
u:=(v\>w)^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
, where
v
denotes the horizontal component and
w
the vertical component of the velocity. Additionally,
\theta
denotes a passive tracer, typically representing temperature or salinity.

Within the LU formalism, we refer to
\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma dW_{t}
as the noise term of the LU Navier-Stokes equation, and distinguish its horizontal and vertical components so that
\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma dW_{t}=\Upsilon^{1/2}(\sigma^{H}dW_{t}\>\sigma^{z}dW_{t})
. The prefactor
\Upsilon^{1/2}
encapsulates the scaling of the noise. The corresponding variance tensor is then
\Upsilon a
, implying that
\Upsilon
carries the physical unit of a viscosity, e.g.
m^{2}s^{-1}
. Moreover, we decompose the variance tensor as follows,

a:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{H}&a_{Hz}\\
a_{zH}&a_{zz}\end{pmatrix}

The Itō-Stokes drift is given by
u_{s}:=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}a
and we introduce the notations

\displaystyle(v_{s}\>w_{s})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}:=u_{s},\quad(v^{*}\>w^{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}:=u^{*}:=u-u_{s}=(v-v_{s}\>\>\>w-w_{s})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}.

Now we present the LU Navier-Stokes equations. In this formulation, the pressure is assumed to be a semimartingale process, denoted by
dp_{t}^{SM}:=pdt+dp_{t}^{\sigma}
, where
pdt
is the bounded variation part and
dp_{t}^{\sigma}
is the martingale component. In addition, we introduce a scaling parameter
\alpha_{\sigma}
, to emphasize the difference between the vertical and horizontal components of the noise. It allows for a direct comparison with the setting presented in
[
20
]
. In that work, the stochastic contribution in the vertical momentum equation are retained to derive a relaxed, or weak, form of the hydrostatic balance. As we shall see, in the regime
\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}
large and
\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon
small, the weak hydrostatic hypothesis provides a better approximation than the strong one.

Navier-Stokes equations with linear law of state (NS)

For
(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}\times\mathbbm{R}_{+}
, the (LU) Navier-Stokes equations with linear law of state (NS) read,

\displaystyle d_{t}v+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sigma dW_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)v-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}v)dt

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\mu,\nu}(vdt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma^{H}dW_{t})+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla_{H}(p\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma})=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle d_{t}w+\Big((u-\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}u_{s}

\displaystyle)dt+\alpha_{\sigma}\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma dW_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)w-\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}w)dt

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\mu,\nu}(wdt+\alpha_{\sigma}\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma^{z}dW_{t})+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}(p\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma})+\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}g=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta+\Big((u^{*}dt

\displaystyle+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma dW_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)\theta-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}\theta)dt-\Delta_{\mu,\nu}\theta dt=0,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\cdot}v+\partial_{z}w=0,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\theta).

(3.5)

Here, the anisotropic viscosity operator is given by
\Delta_{\mu,\nu}:=\mu\Delta_{H}+\nu\partial_{zz}
, where
\mu
and
\nu
are the horizontal and vertical viscosities, respectively. As is standard in the thin-domain literature
[
4
,
34
]
, we assume different scalings for horizontal and vertical viscosities, namely
\mu=O(1)
and
\nu=O(\epsilon^{2})
. For simplicity, we follow
[
34
]
and choose
\mu=1
and
\nu=\epsilon^{2}
.

The noise term
\sigma dW_{t}
is also assumed to be divergence-free:

\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma^{H}dW_{t}+\partial_{z}\sigma^{z}dW_{t}=0.

(3.6)

Note that equations (
3.2
) and (
3.4
) can be interpreted as instances of the SRTT with a noise term
\sigma dW_{t}
, applied to the horizontal momentum and the tracer. Similar yet different, equation (
3.3
) is obtained by applying the SRTT to the vertical momentum, with a rescaled noise term
\alpha_{\sigma}\sigma dW_{t}
. This distinction is crucial for capturing the weak hydrostatic regime discussed earlier.

In the deterministic setting, the approach developed in
[
34
]
is to consider scaled variables which take values in
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{H}\times[-1,1]
. Regarding the bounded variation terms involved in (NS), we essentially use the same scalings. However, the presence of martingale terms calls for a noise structure given a priori, so that we recover the dynamics of the LU primitive equations at the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

3.1
Discussion about the noise structure

3.1.1
The bidimensional case

Before presenting the rescaled version of the LU Navier-Stokes equations, we discuss the structure of the noise
\sigma dW_{t}
. Since the existence of global solutions to the LU primitive equations has been showed in
[
20
]
for bidimensional noises, these are the type of noises we consider in our proofs. We also assume that they are divergence-free. Let
W^{2D}
bidimensional cylindrical Wiener process over
\mathcal{S}_{H}
, and
\sigma_{0}^{2D}dW_{t}^{2D}
the noise to appear in the primitive equations. Then, the (non-scaled) Navier-Stokes noise is chosen as
\sigma^{2D}dW_{t}^{2D}=\sigma_{0}^{2D}dW_{t}^{2D}
.

Equivalently, it is possible to define this type of noises through tridimensional Wiener processes. Consider now
W
a cylindrical Wiener process over
\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}
, and Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\sigma
acting on
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
or
L^{2}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R}^{3})
. We also denote by
\hat{\sigma}
their associated kernels. Moreover, we denote by
\tilde{W}(x,y,z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}W(x,y,\epsilon z)
, which is a cylindrical Wiener process on
\mathcal{S}
by the scaling property. Thus, assuming that the primitive equation noise
\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}
is bidimensional is equivalent to

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{H}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{H}(x,y,0,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=0.

(3.8)

Then, we define the Navier-Stokes noise
\sigma_{NS}dW_{t}=(\sigma_{NS}^{H}dW_{t},\sigma_{NS}^{z}dW_{t})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
through its kernel: for all
(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{NS}^{H}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\epsilon\sqrt{\epsilon}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{H}(x,y,0,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},0),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{NS}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=0.

(3.10)

The velocity
u_{\epsilon}=(v_{\epsilon},w_{\epsilon})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
and noise
\sigma_{\epsilon}dW_{t}=(\sigma_{\epsilon}^{H}dW_{t},\sigma_{\epsilon}^{z}dW_{t})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
appearing in the scaled Navier-Stokes equations are defined by scaling the Navier-Stokes quantities (see
[
34
]
for an analogous derivation in the deterministic case),

\displaystyle v_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=v(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{H}dW_{t}(x,y,z,t)=\sigma^{H}dW_{t}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle w_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}w(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{z}dW_{t}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sigma^{z}dW_{t}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon z,t).

Additionally, reminding that
a:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{H}&a_{Hz}\\
a_{zH}&a_{zz}\end{pmatrix}
denotes the
non-rescaled
variance tensor, we define the
rescaled
variance tensor as
a^{\epsilon}:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{H}^{\epsilon}&a_{Hz}^{\epsilon}\\
a_{zH}^{\epsilon}&a_{zz}^{\epsilon}\end{pmatrix}
, where

\displaystyle a_{H}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=a_{H}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),\quad a_{zH}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}a_{zH}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle a_{Hz}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}a_{Hz}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),\quad a_{zz}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}a_{zz}(x,y,\epsilon z,t).

Hence, we can also define the rescaled Itō-Stokes drift
u_{s}^{\epsilon}=(v_{s}^{\epsilon}\>w_{s}^{\epsilon})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
, by setting

v_{s}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=v_{s}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),\quad w_{s}^{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}w_{s}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

where
u_{s}=(v_{s}\>w_{s})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
denote the non-rescaled Itō-Stokes drift. Then, we define
u_{\epsilon}^{*}=(v_{\epsilon}^{*}\>w_{\epsilon}^{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
with
v_{\epsilon}^{*}=v_{\epsilon}-v_{s}^{\epsilon}
and
w_{\epsilon}^{*}=w_{\epsilon}-w_{s}^{\epsilon}
.

The noise defined above can be rewritten in terms of
\tilde{W}
, which is a cylindrical Wiener process on
\mathcal{S}
. It yields ultimately
\sigma_{\epsilon}dW_{t}=\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}
. The derivation is performed below in the more general context of “true” tridimensional noises.

3.1.2
The tridimensional case

Even if our main results only cover bidimensional noises, we present the tridimensional noise setting below, as it gives some insights about the rescaling arguments. Considering a general primitive equations noise
\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}
, we define the (non-scaled) Navier-Stokes noise
\sigma dW_{t}=(\sigma^{H}dW_{t},\sigma^{z}dW_{t})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
, for all
(x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}
, through

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{H}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\epsilon\sqrt{\epsilon}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon^{-1}z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon^{-1}z^{\prime}),

(3.11)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon^{-1}z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon^{-1}z^{\prime}).

(3.12)

Notice that in this case,
\sigma dW_{t}
is divergence-free whenever
\sigma d\tilde{W}_{t}
is. Moreover, this choice leads to a scaled Navier-Stokes noise that is equal to
\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}
. To see this, we perform first a change of variable on
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{H}dW_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{H}(x,y,z)dW_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S_{\epsilon}}}\hat{\sigma}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})dW_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\sigma}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})dW_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sqrt{\epsilon}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\sigma}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})d\tilde{W}_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime\prime}=\sigma_{0}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon z)d\tilde{W}_{t}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{z}(x,y,z)dW_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathcal{S_{\epsilon}}}\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})dW_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})dW_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\epsilon}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon z^{\prime\prime})d\tilde{W}_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime})dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime\prime}=\sigma_{0}^{z}(x,y,z)d\tilde{W}_{t}.

Using the decomposition
\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}^{0}d\beta_{k}
, we define
a^{0}=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}^{0}(\phi_{k}^{0})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}
and
u_{s}^{0}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}a_{0}
. Thus, it holds that
a^{\epsilon}=a^{0}
and
u_{s}^{\epsilon}=u_{s}^{0}
.

Eventually, we can remark that, upon assuming
\sigma_{0}^{H}dW_{t}
is independent of
z
, we recover the type of noise proposed in
[
1
]
. Another possibility to recover this type of noises is to consider a general reference kernel
\hat{\sigma}_{ref}
, and a real number
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then, we define the following (non-rescaled) Navier-Stokes noise,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{H}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\epsilon\sqrt{\epsilon}}\hat{\sigma}_{ref}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon^{-\gamma}z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon^{-\gamma}z^{\prime}),

(3.13)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\hat{\sigma}_{ref}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon^{-1}z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon^{-1}z^{\prime}).

(3.14)

Thus, the kernel of the scaled noise would be,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\epsilon}^{H}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\hat{\sigma}_{ref}^{H}(x,y,\epsilon^{1-\gamma}z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\epsilon^{1-\gamma}z^{\prime})

(3.15)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\hat{\sigma}_{ref}^{z}(x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}).

(3.16)

As such, the scaled noise defined above converges in law to a noise of the desired form. However, if we impose a divergence-free condition on
\sigma_{0}dW_{t}
, together with the rigid-lid boundary conditions, then
\sigma_{0}dW_{t}
must be bidimensional. Consequently, the noises above are more general only for divergent noises. This setting is typically relevant in the context of weakly compressible dynamics, which emerges traditionally from the Boussinesq assumption.

3.2
Scaled Navier-Stokes equations and primitive equations

We derive below the scaled Navier-Stokes equations, on the scaled domain
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{H}\times(-1,1)
. For this purpose, we define the following scaled variables,

\displaystyle v_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=v(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{H}dW_{t}(x,y,z,t)=\sigma^{H}dW_{t}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle w_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}w(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{z}dW_{t}(x,y,z,t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sigma^{z}dW_{t}^{z}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle p_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=p(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle dp_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=dp_{t}^{\sigma}(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}-\rho_{0}=\epsilon(\rho-\rho_{0})(x,y,\epsilon z,t),

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}(x,y,z,t)=\epsilon\theta(x,y,\epsilon z,t).

Remark 3.1
.

Thus far,
\epsilon
has been interpreted as the aspect ratio
\epsilon:=\frac{H}{L}
, where
H
and
L
are the typical vertical and horizontal lengths, respectively. However, more general scalings may be considered. For instance, one could introduce the scaled parameter
\check{\epsilon}
, defined by

\check{\epsilon}^{2}=\frac{Fr^{2}Ro}{Bu}\epsilon^{2}=Ro^{3}Bu^{-2}\epsilon^{2}

where
Ro=\frac{U}{f_{0}L}
,
Bu=\frac{R_{d}^{2}}{L^{2}}
and
Fr^{2}=\frac{U^{2}}{gH}=Ro^{2}Bu^{-1}
,
R_{d}=\frac{(gH)^{1/2}}{f_{0}}
being the Rossby deformation radius,
f_{0}
the Coriolis parameter and
U
the typical velocity
[
40
]
.

Following the derivation of
[
34
]
, we find the scaled Navier-Stokes equations under location uncertainty (SNS). For consistency, we formulate our equations with the cylindrical Wiener process
\tilde{W}
on
\mathcal{S}
.

Scaled Navier-Stokes equation (SNS
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
)

For
(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbbm{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle d_{t}v_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt

\displaystyle+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)v_{\epsilon}-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}v_{\epsilon})dt

\displaystyle-\Delta_{3}(v_{\epsilon}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{H}d\tilde{W}_{t})+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla_{H}(p_{\epsilon}\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon})=0,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\Bigg(d_{t}w_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt

\displaystyle+\alpha_{\sigma}\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)w_{\epsilon}-\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}w_{\epsilon})dt

\displaystyle-\Delta_{3}(w_{\epsilon}dt+\alpha_{\sigma}\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{z}d\tilde{W}_{t})\Bigg)+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}(p_{\epsilon}\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon})+\frac{\rho_{\epsilon}}{\rho_{0}}g=0,

(3.18)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt+

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)\theta_{\epsilon}-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}\theta_{\epsilon})dt-\Delta_{3}\theta_{\epsilon}dt=0,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\cdot}v_{\epsilon}+\partial_{z}w_{\epsilon}=0,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\rho_{\epsilon}=\rho_{0}(1+\theta_{\epsilon}),

(3.20)

with again, a divergence-free assumption on the noise,

\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}=\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma_{0}^{H}d\tilde{W}_{t}+\partial_{z}\sigma_{0}^{z}d\tilde{W}_{t}=0.

(3.21)

As mentioned earlier,
\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}
is chosen to be the noise involved in the primitive equations below. Importantly, the Wiener processes
\tilde{W}
and
W
are not supported by the same spaces:
\tilde{W}
is defined on the “scaled” domain
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{H}\times(-1,1)
, while
W
is defined on the “physical” domain
\mathcal{S}_{H}\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. Nevertheless, the divergence-free assumption on
\sigma_{\epsilon}dW_{t}
implies that its limit
\sigma d\tilde{W}_{t}
is also divergence-free.

To cancel the pressure terms, we introduce hereafter the scaled gradient operator
\nabla_{\epsilon}
, defined, for any
f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R})
, as

\nabla_{\epsilon}f=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{H}f\\
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\partial_{z}f\end{pmatrix}.

(3.22)

Hence, the system (SNS
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) can be written more compactly as

\displaystyle d_{t}u_{\epsilon}+(u_{\epsilon}^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})u_{\epsilon}dt

\displaystyle+\Upsilon^{1/2}(\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}u_{\epsilon}-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}}u_{\epsilon})dt

\displaystyle-\Delta_{3}(u_{\epsilon}dt

\displaystyle+\Upsilon^{1/2}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla_{\epsilon}(p_{\epsilon}\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon})+\frac{\rho_{\epsilon}}{\epsilon^{2}\rho_{0}}g\mathbb{e}_{z}=0,

(3.23)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt+

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)\theta_{\epsilon}-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}\theta_{\epsilon})dt-\Delta_{3}\theta_{\epsilon}dt=0,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\cdot}v_{\epsilon}+\partial_{z}w_{\epsilon}=0,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\rho_{\epsilon}=\rho_{0}(1+\theta_{\epsilon}),

(3.25)

where we define
I_{\alpha}=Diag(1,1,\alpha)
, for all
\alpha>0
. Again, we assume that the incompressibility condition (
3.21
) holds.

Notice that the first term of equation (
3.18
) involves terms of order
\epsilon^{2}
,
\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}
and
\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}
. By analogy with the deterministic case, where the primitive equations are formally recovered by disregarding the terms scaling with
\epsilon^{2}
, we infer the expression of the primitive equations under location uncertainty with strong hydrostatic hypothesis (PE). They are found by disregarding all the terms mentioned above.

Primitive equations with strong hydrostatic hypothesis (PE)

For
(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbbm{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle d_{t}v+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)v

\displaystyle-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}v)dt-\Delta_{3}(vdt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}^{H})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla_{H}(p\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma})=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{H}\Big)\theta

\displaystyle-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}\theta)dt-\Delta_{3}\theta dt=0,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}dp_{t}^{\sigma}=0,\quad

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\partial_{z}p=-\rho g,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}v+\partial_{z}w=0,\quad

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\theta).

(3.29)

However, when
\alpha_{\sigma}\gg 1
, the terms scaling like
\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}
and
\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}
become much larger than those of scaling
\epsilon^{2}
. Reasoning similarly as in
[
20
]
, we retain only the former, so that we obtain the non-regularised primitive equations with weak hydrostatic assumptions, which are defined below. Thus, this model is intermediate between the LU primitive equations (PE) and the LU scaled Navier-Stokes equations (SNS
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
).

Non-regularised primitive equations with weak hydrostatic assumption

For
(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbbm{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle d_{t}v+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}

\displaystyle\nabla_{3}\Big)v-\Delta_{3}(vdt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{H}d\tilde{W}_{t})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla_{H}(p\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma})-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}v)dt=0,

(3.30)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)\theta-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}\theta)dt-\Delta\theta dt=0,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}p=-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}

\displaystyle g+\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}w),

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\partial_{z}dp_{t}^{\sigma}=-\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}[(\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle d\tilde{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w]+\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}\Delta(\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{z}d\tilde{W}_{t}),

(3.33)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}v+\partial_{z}

\displaystyle w=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\theta).

(3.34)

Regarding the martingale pressure term, we observe that the horizontal momentum equation includes the additional contribution

\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}\nabla_{H}\int_{z}^{1}[(\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w]dz^{\prime}.

(3.35)

This expression involves third-order derivatives in
v
, since
w
is determined through the divergence-free condition as
w=\int_{z}^{1}(\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}v)dz^{\prime}
. To regularise this term, we introduce a convolution kernel
K
, and define a smoothed approximation of the above system, (PE
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
). By construction the systems (PE
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) and (PE) coincide whenever
\epsilon=0
or
\alpha_{\sigma}=0
.

(Regularised) primitive equations with weak hydrostatic assumption (PE
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
)

For
(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbbm{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle d_{t}v+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}

\displaystyle\nabla_{3}\Big)v-\Delta_{3}(vdt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{H}d\tilde{W}_{t})

\displaystyle+\nabla_{H}(p\>dt+dp_{t}^{\sigma})-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}v)dt=0,

(3.36)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}p=-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}

\displaystyle g+\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{K}\nabla_{3}w),

(3.37)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}dp_{t}^{\sigma}=-\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}K*[(\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle d\tilde{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w]+\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}\Delta(\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}^{z}d\tilde{W}_{t}),

(3.38)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}v+\partial_{z}w=0,\text{ and }\>\nabla_{H}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma_{0}^{H}d\tilde{W}_{t}+\partial_{z}\sigma_{0}^{z}d\tilde{W}_{t}=0,\text{ and }\>\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\theta),

(3.39)

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta+\Big((u^{*}dt+\Upsilon^{1/2}\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle d\tilde{W}_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)\theta-\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a^{0}\nabla_{3}\theta)dt-\Delta\theta dt=0,

(3.40)

where we denote by

, so that stands the term

stands as a regularisation of
\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}w)

is regular enough – e.g.

[
20
]
. Moreover, we recover the primitive equations system (PE) by setting

(\sigma d\tilde{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w
in the vertical momentum equation. Do so typically increases the spatial length of this term, yet tempers the irregular dynamics arising from the higher third-order derivatives. Nevertheless, the deterministic-like non-linear terms remain unchanged by this regularisation.

where use the following notations: we denote the following operator by

3.3
Boundary conditions

In this subsection, we introduce the initial and boundary conditions we use with rigid-lid boundary conditions. Notice that “fully periodic” boundary conditions will be considered in Subsection
4.3.2
. First, we decompose the boundary as
\partial S=\Gamma_{u}\cup\Gamma_{b}\cup\Gamma_{l}
– respectively the upper, bottom and lateral boundaries – and equip this problem with the following free-slip rigid-lid type boundary conditions
[
8
,
18
,
20
]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}v=0,

\displaystyle w=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\theta+\alpha_{\theta}\theta=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{u},

(3.49)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}v=0,

\displaystyle w=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\theta=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{b},

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbb{n}_{H}}v\times\mathbb{n}_{H}=0,

\displaystyle v\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbb{n}_{H}=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbb{n}_{H}}\theta=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{l}.

4.2
Modified Leray projectors

In order to disregard the pressure terms in the scaled momentum Navier-Stokes and primitive equations (SNS), we introduce modified Leray projectors. We define first the barotropic and baroclinic projectors

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}:L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\mathbbm{R}^{2})\to L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{H},\mathbbm{R}^{2}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}:L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\mathbbm{R}^{2})\to L^{2}({\mathcal{S}},\mathbbm{R}^{2}),

(4.4)

of the velocity component as follows – see
[
8
]
. For
v\in H
, let

\mathcal{A}_{2}[v](x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}v(x,y,z^{\prime})dz^{\prime},\quad\mathcal{A}[v](x,y,z)=\mathcal{A}_{2}[v](x,y),\quad\mathcal{R}[v]=v-\mathcal{A}[v].

(4.5)

Remark that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{R}
are orthogonal projectors with respect to the inner product
(\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})_{H}
. To simplify notations, we will use
\bar{v}
in place of
\mathcal{A}_{2}[v]
or
\mathcal{A}[v]
, and
\tilde{v}
in place of
\mathcal{R}[v]
. We are now in position to define the projector
\mathbf{P}
, which acts on bi-dimensional vector fields on
{\mathcal{S}}
by combining the classical 2D Leray projection of the barotropic component with the (divergent) baroclinic component of the horizontal velocity,

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}^{2D}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{R},

(4.6)

where
\mathbf{P}^{2D}
denotes the standard 2D Leray projector associated to the boundary condition
\bar{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbb{n}=0
on
\partial\mathcal{S}_{H}

Furthermore, we introduce the following inner product, for all

(u,u^{\sharp})_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}=(v,v^{\sharp})_{H_{1}}+\epsilon^{2}(w,w^{\sharp})_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R})},

(4.7)

and we denote by
\|\boldsymbol{\cdot}\|_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}
its associated norm. Similarly, for
v,v^{\sharp}\in V_{1}
, we define

\displaystyle(u,u^{\sharp})_{V_{\epsilon}}=(v,v^{\sharp})_{V_{1}}+\epsilon^{2}(w,w^{\sharp})_{H^{1}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R})},

(4.8)

and
\|\boldsymbol{\cdot}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}
the associated norm. Moreover, we define the space

\mathcal{F}=\Big\{(v,\int_{z}^{1}\nabla_{H}\boldsymbol{\cdot}vdz^{\prime})^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm T}\>\Big|\>v\in H_{1}\Big\}.

(4.9)

Thus, we can introduce
\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}:L^{2}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R}^{3})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
, the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{F}
with respect to the inner product
(\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot})_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}
. In particular, all the terms of the form
\nabla_{\epsilon}f
are cancelled by
\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}

In this context,
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}
can be interpreted as “modified” Leray projectors for (PE
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) and (SNS
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) respectively, since they cancel pressure gradient terms (i.e. the horizontal pressure gradient and the full rescaled pressure gradient, respectively). The main difference however is that
\mathbf{P}
keeps the baroclinic pressure terms unchanged in (PE
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) – see
[
8
]
. The use of such projector is justified by a crucial remark mentioned in
[
10
]
: in the (deterministic) primitive equations, the pressure naturally decomposes into barotropic and baroclinic components. The baroclinic mode is explicitly determined via the hydrostatic balance, while the barotropic mode influences only the barotropic dynamics through a vertically averaged pressure. Consequently, the barotropic dynamics resemble those of a two-dimensional Navier-Stokes system, whereas the baroclinic component follows a structure akin to a forced Burgers equation, with the two modes coupled through nonlinear interactions. In this context, the projector
\mathbf{P}
acts as a horizontal Leray projector on the barotropic component, ensuring incompressibility in the vertically averaged, large-scale flow, while it reduces to the identity on the baroclinic dynamics.

Proposition 2

The problem

Assume that the assumptions of Theorem

5
Comparison to the (non-regularised) LU Navier-Stokes equations

In this section, we consider the (non-regularised) LU Navier-Stokes equations, to study their convergence towards the LU primitive equations. Although seeking the convergence of the “direct” system seems natural, ultimately this setting does not allow to distinguish orders of convergence of the weak and strong hydrostatic assumptions with regard to the coefficient
\alpha_{\sigma}

\epsilon

and
7
are given in the setting presented here (see Theorems
3
and
4

1
and
2
can be proven similarly. Notice that, since

{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}
) and (SNS
{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}

5.1

Let us derive first the abstract formulation of the scaled LU Navier-Stokes equations without regularistion, in the fashion of what was presented in Section

From now, for simplicity and without loss of generality, we assume that

\displaystyle d_{t}u_{\epsilon}+\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}(u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})u_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}(\sigma dW_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}u_{\epsilon}-\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}}u_{\epsilon})dt

\displaystyle d_{t}\theta_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt+

5.2
Main results

We state hereafter our main results. Essentially, the two first theorems adapt classical results from the (deterministic) Leray theory to the our stochastic setting. Notice that Definition

Proposition 3
.

The problem
(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}^{SNS})

The proof of Proposition

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\epsilon}^{0}
in the following. It is the counterpart of

that is adapted to the problem

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\epsilon}^{0}(\eta,\zeta):=\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{U}_{\epsilon}\|_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|\nabla_{3}\hat{U}_{\epsilon}\|_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}dt+\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\Theta}_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|\nabla_{3}\hat{\Theta}_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}dt,

(5.5)

Theorem 3

Assume that
K\in H^{3}

Remark 5.1
.

The estimate (

if

is not known precisely.

In turn, the estimate above shows that (PE

{}_{\alpha_{\sigma}}^{\epsilon}

The problem

K\in H^{3}(\mathcal{S},\mathbbm{R})
, we infer that

\sum_{k}\|\nabla_{H}\tilde{\pi}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}\leq C(1+\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}).

(7.13)

Hence,

\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}+\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A^{3/2})}^{2}dt\leq C\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\Big(1+\|v\|_{V}^{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}\Big)\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}+\|\theta\|_{H^{2}}^{2}dt.

(7.14)

This last result allows to conclude by the stochastic Grönwall lemma.

\square

7.2
Estimate in
H^{1}-H^{2}
for the error

In this subsection, we establish the results stated in Theorem
4
, with an emphasis on the estimate (
5.9
).

In the following, we write
\tau_{\epsilon}
in place of
\tau_{\epsilon}^{SNS}
to economize notation. Let
\tau
a stopping time such that
0<\tau<\tau_{\epsilon}
. We further make an abuse of notation by writing
U_{\epsilon}
,
V_{\epsilon}
and
W_{\epsilon}
in place of
(U_{\epsilon})_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}\wedge\tau}
,
(V_{\epsilon})_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}\wedge\tau}
and
(W_{\epsilon})_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}\wedge\tau}
, respectively. First, we state the evolution equations of
V_{\epsilon}
and
W_{\epsilon}
, which are meaningful since
u_{\epsilon}
is a local-in-time strong solution to (
\Pi_{\epsilon}^{SNS}
),

d_{t}V_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt+\sigma dW_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{H}\Big)V_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla\Big)vdt-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}V_{\epsilon})dt\\
-\Delta V_{\epsilon}dt+\nabla_{H}(P_{\epsilon}dt+dP_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon})=0,

and

d_{t}W_{\epsilon}+\Big((u_{\epsilon}^{*}dt+\alpha_{\sigma}\sigma dW_{t})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla\Big)W_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla\Big)wdt-\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}W_{\epsilon})dt-\Delta W_{\epsilon}\>dt\\
+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\partial_{z}(P_{\epsilon}dt+dP_{t}^{\sigma,\epsilon})+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{\rho_{\epsilon}-\rho}{\rho_{0}}gdt=-(I-K*)(\alpha_{\sigma}\sigma dW_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w+\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}((a-a^{K})\nabla w)dt.

Applying Itō’s lemma, and using an approximation argument that is similar to the one of Section
6
, with
\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}
, we deduce that

\frac{1}{2}d_{t}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}),AI_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}\Big)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}dt\leq-\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}dt\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)U_{\epsilon},AU_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}\\
-\Bigg(\Big((u^{*}dt+U_{\epsilon}dt)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)U_{\epsilon},AU_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}-(g\Theta_{\epsilon},AW_{\epsilon})_{L^{2}}dt-\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{2}\Big(\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}((a-a^{K})\nabla w)dt,AW_{\epsilon}\Big)_{L^{2}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\Big\|\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}\Big[\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{\sigma}(I-K*)(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w\end{pmatrix}\Big]\Big\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}dt\\
-\alpha_{\sigma}\epsilon^{2}\Big((I-K*)(\sigma dW_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)w\Big)_{L^{2}}-\Bigg(\Big(\sigma dW_{t}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}u,AU_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}\\
=:(J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}+J_{5})dt+\sum_{k}(J_{6}^{k}+J_{7}^{k})d\beta^{k}.

(7.15)

First, we remark the following about the last term on the LHS of (
7.15
),

\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(a\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}),AI_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}\Big)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\Big(a\nabla_{3}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon},\nabla_{3}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}\Big)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}\\
+C_{\xi}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{2})^{2}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\xi\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}.

(7.16)

Next, we develop the expression of
J_{1}+J_{2}
,

J_{1}+J_{2}=-\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)U_{\epsilon}+\Big(u^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)U_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)u,AU_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}\\
=-\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)V_{\epsilon}+\Big(u^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)V_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)v,AV_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L^{2}}\\
-\epsilon^{2}\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)W_{\epsilon}+\Big(u^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)W_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)w,AW_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L^{2}}.

(7.17)

Remark that, using again the Ladyzhenskaya-type relation of
[
34
]
,

\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)V_{\epsilon}+\Big(u^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)V_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)v,AV_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L^{2}}\leq\Big[\|\nabla_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\Delta_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\\
+\|\nabla_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\Big(\|\nabla_{3}v^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}v^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}+\|\nabla_{3}v\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}v\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\Big)\Big]\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)},

(7.18)

and

\Bigg(\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)W_{\epsilon}+\Big(u^{*}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)W_{\epsilon}+\Big(U_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\Big)w,AW_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L^{2}}\leq\Big[\|\nabla_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\nabla_{3}W_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}W_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\\
+\|\nabla_{3}v^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}v^{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\nabla_{3}W_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}W_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}+\|\nabla_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\nabla_{3}w\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\Delta_{3}w\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\Big]\|W_{\epsilon}\|_{H^{2}}.

(7.19)

Hence,

J_{1}+J_{2}\leq C_{\xi}\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2}\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}+C_{\xi}(1+\|v\|_{V}^{2})(1+\|v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2})\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2}\\
+C_{\xi}\epsilon^{2}(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2})(1+\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2}+\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2})+\xi\|\nabla U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}.

(7.20)

Additionally, for
J_{3}
,

J_{3}=-(g\Theta_{\epsilon},AW_{\epsilon})_{L^{2}}\leq\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}\|W_{\epsilon}\|_{V}\leq\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}\leq C\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2}+\xi\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}.

(7.21)

Moreover, for
J_{4}
,

\displaystyle J_{4}=-\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{2}\Big(\nabla_{3}\boldsymbol{\cdot}((a-a^{K})\nabla

\displaystyle w),AW_{\epsilon}\Big)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{2}\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}\|W_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\xi}\frac{\alpha_{\sigma}^{4}\epsilon^{2}}{2}\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\xi\epsilon^{2}\|W_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}.

(7.22)

Furthermore, for
J_{5}
,

\displaystyle J_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\Big\|\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}\Big[\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{\sigma}(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\end{pmatrix}\Big]\Big\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}

(7.23)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\xi}\alpha_{\sigma}^{4}\epsilon^{2}\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2}(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2})+\xi\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2}(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon},\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon})_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+C_{\xi}\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\sum_{k}\|(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Hence, taking the integral, the supremum then the expectation of (
7.15
) and gathering the previous estimates yields,

\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt\leq C\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt+\xi\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt\\
+C\epsilon^{2}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{4})\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}(1+\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2})(1+\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\|_{H^{1}}^{2})(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})dt\\
+C\sum_{k}\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\Bigg|\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}(J_{6}^{k}+J_{7}^{k})d\beta_{k}\Bigg|.

(7.24)

In addition, by the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}

\displaystyle\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\Bigg|\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\Bigg(\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}\Big[\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{\sigma}(I-K*)(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w\end{pmatrix}\Big],AI_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}d\beta_{k}\Bigg|

(7.25)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{E}\Bigg|\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\Bigg(\mathbf{P}_{\epsilon}\Big[\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3}I_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{\sigma}(I-K*)(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w\end{pmatrix}\Big],AI_{\alpha_{\sigma}}U_{\epsilon}\Bigg)_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}\Bigg|^{1/2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{2}}\mathbbm{E}\Bigg|\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|\phi_{k}\|_{H^{4}}^{2}(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|(I-K*)(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\|AU_{\epsilon}\|_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}\Bigg|^{1/2}dt

\displaystyle\leq C_{\xi}\epsilon^{2}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{4})\mathbbm{E}\|\phi_{k}\|_{H^{4}}^{2}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|(I-K*)(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w\|_{L^{2}}^{2})+\xi\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|AU_{\epsilon}\|_{L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}dt.

Therefore,

\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt\leq C\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt+\xi\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt\\
+C\epsilon^{2}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{4})\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\sum_{k}(1+\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2})(1+\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\|_{H^{1}}^{2})(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})dt.

(7.26)

Then we deduce that, for some constant
C_{1}>0
,

\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})+\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2})dt\leq C_{1}\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}dt\\
+C_{1}\epsilon^{2}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{4})\mathbbm{E}\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}(1+\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2})(1+\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\|_{H^{1}}^{2})(1+\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})dt.

(7.27)

Denote by

\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}=\inf\Big\{t\in\mathbbm{R}_{+}\Big|\sup_{[0,t]}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})+\int_{0}^{t}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2})dt>\frac{1}{4C_{1}}\Big\}\leq\tau_{\epsilon}.

(7.28)

Therefore, by the stochastic Grönwall lemma and Lemma
7.1
, if
\epsilon<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2C_{1}}}
,

\mathbbm{E}\sup_{[0,\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\wedge T]}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})+\mathbbm{E}\int_{0}^{\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2})dt\\
\leq C_{1}\epsilon^{2}(1+\alpha_{\sigma}^{4})\mathbbm{E}\int_{0}^{\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}(1+\|v\|_{H^{3}}^{2})(1+\|(a-a^{K})\nabla w\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\|(I-K*)[(\phi_{k}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{3})w]\|_{H^{1}}^{2})dt.

(7.29)

Concerning the asymptotic behaviour of
\tau_{\epsilon}
, the last equation implies that the following convergence happens in probability, whenever
\alpha_{\sigma}=o(\epsilon^{-1/2})
,

\sup_{[0,\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\wedge T]}(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2})+\int_{0}^{\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\Big(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}(A)}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}\Big)dt\rightarrow 0.

(7.30)

Then,
\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\rightarrow\tau_{\delta}
in probability. Since
\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\leq\tau_{\epsilon}
, we infer that
\tau_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\rightarrow\tau_{\delta}
in probability as well. In addition, remind that, for all stopping times
\eta<\zeta
,
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{1}(\eta,\zeta)
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{1}(\eta,\zeta):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sup_{[\eta,\zeta]}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{V_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{V}^{2}\Big)+\int_{\eta}^{\zeta}\Big(\|U_{\epsilon}\|_{D_{\epsilon}(A)^{2}}^{2}+\|\Theta_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}^{2}\Big)dt\text{ if $\zeta\geq\tau_{\epsilon}$},\\
\infty\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(7.31)

With this notation, equation (
7.30
) and the fact that
\check{\tau}_{\epsilon}\wedge\tau_{\delta}\rightarrow\tau_{\delta}
yield,

\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{1}(0,\tau_{\delta}\wedge T)\rightarrow 0,\text{ in probability.}

(7.32)

Hence, by taking the limit
\delta\rightarrow\infty
, we deduce that
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}^{1}(0,T)\rightarrow 0
in probability.

\square
