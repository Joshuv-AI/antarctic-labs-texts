Development of eco-friendly activated carbon from olive pits for the adsorption of tartrazine with isotherm kinetic and thermodynamic studies
Authors: Abderrahmane Remil, Abdelkrim Bendoukha Reguig, Mokhtar Saidi, Abdelghani Bouchama, Mohammed El Amine Monir, M. Fatmi, S. Alomairy, Talal M. Althagafi, Aseel Smerat, Mustafa Jaipallah Abdelmageed Abualreishh, Murat Yaylacı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthetic dyes discharged into water bodies pose a persistent environmental threat owing to their resistance to natural degradation, toxicity, and long-term ecological impact. In this study, discarded olive pits were valorized as a lignocellulosic precursor for the production of activated carbon through phosphoric acid activation. Multiple analytical tools including pHpzc, FTIR, SEM, XPS, and XRD—were used to examine the structural, physicochemical, and morphological characteristics of the prepared material. The influence of key process variables on Tartrazine uptake was assessed through a series of batch-mode experiments, including pH, initial dye concentration, adsorbent dose, contact time, and temperature. Maximum adsorption was achieved in acidic medium (pH = 2) using an adsorbent dose of 40 mg, an initial Tartrazine concentration of 40 mg L−1, a contact time of 24 h, and a temperature of 298 K. The maximum adsorption capacity reached 47.18 mg g−1 according to the BET model, while the pseudo-second-order kinetic model provided the best fit (R2 = 0.9996) with an experimental Qe value of 31.39 mg g−1. Thermodynamic parameters (ΔH° = − 10328 J mol−1; ΔG° = − 1409 to − 1794 J mol−1) confirmed that the process is exothermic and spontaneous. The results of the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and Thermodynamic analysis showed that the process is a mixed adsorption mechanism dominated by physisorption. These findings collectively establish H3PO4-activated olive pit carbon as a high-performance, cost-effective, and environmentally viable material for Tartrazine decontamination from water matrices.

Introduction

The widespread discharge of synthetic colorants into aquatic systems raises serious public health concerns, given their inherent toxicity, limited natural degradation, and cumulative effects on water quality. Among these dyes, Tartrazine (E102), an anionic azo dye widely utilized in food, pharmaceutical, and textile industries, has attracted significant attention. This growing concerns stems from its documented potential to cause harmful effects on aquatic ecosystems, environmental quality, and possible risks to human health1. Documented health effects linked to Tartrazine intake encompass allergic manifestations, respiratory issues, migraine episodes, behavioral disturbances in children, and possible neurotoxic consequences. Environmentally, its high low biodegradation and chemical stability lead to persistence in aquatic systems, where it can significantly inhibit natural photosynthesis, reduce dissolved oxygen levels, and exert toxic effects on various aquatic organisms, thereby disturbing ecological balance. Conventional remediation strategies coagulation-flocculation, chemical oxidation, biological degradation have been widely applied, yet their practical utility is often hampered by elevated operational costs and technical constraints. Adsorption has therefore emerged as an efficient and economical alternative for removing organic pollutants from wastewater2. Among adsorbents, activated carbon stands out for its remarkably high specific surface area, highly developed pore network, and diverse surface functionalization. Recently, increasing interest has focused on biomass-derived adsorbents because of their low cost, renewability, and environmental sustainability. Agricultural wastes have been extensively investigated for pollutant removal, showing promising adsorption performance while contributing to waste valorization and environmental protection3. Numerous studies have confirmed that agricultural waste–based adsorbents (e.g., Luffa acutangula peels, Punica granatum seeds, Neolamarckia cadamba leaves, rubber seed pericarp) are efficient, reusable, and cost-effective for removing various dyes (methylene blue, crystal violet, methylene green) and organic pollutants such as diclofenac sodium, with adsorption behavior often following Langmuir isotherm models and pseudo-second-order kinetics4–7. Advanced oxidation processes (e.g., persulfate activation with nZVI) and modified carbon-based composites (AC-nZVI, magnetic Fe₃O₄/zeolite) have also demonstrated high efficiency for dye removal (Acid Blue 113, methylene blue), humic acid, and pharmaceutical compounds such as metronidazole, with removal efficiencies reaching 95–100% and adsorption following pseudo-first-order or pseudo-second-order kinetics8–14. These findings collectively confirm that carbon-based and modified adsorbents represent highly effective and sustainable materials for water and wastewater treatment applications. Other biomass-derived adsorbents such as spent coffee grounds (crystal violet, Cr(VI)), sugarcane bagasse (malachite green, up to 97% removal), and argan nut shells (malachite green oxalate) have also shown excellent adsorption performance with Langmuir/Freundlich isotherm behavior, pseudo-second-order kinetics, and spontaneous, often exothermic, thermodynamic profiles, confirming the potential of agricultural wastes for sustainable wastewater treatment15–18. Carpobrotus edulis has also been successfully used as a low-cost biosorbent for phosphate ions (up to 888.9 mg/g after acid modification), methylene blue (153.9 mg/g after alkali treatment), and crystal violet (166.6 mg/g), with adsorption following Langmuir isotherm behavior and pseudo-second-order kinetics, and good regeneration ability19–21. In contrast to previous studies, the present work focuses on the valorization of olive pits collected from western Algeria as a sustainable precursor and low-cost for activated carbon preparation using H₃PO₄ chemical activation. The originality of this study lies in combining detailed surface characterization (pHpzc, FTIR, SEM, XRD, and XPS) with comprehensive adsorption investigations of Tartrazine, an anionic azo dye that has received limited attention compared to other dyes. Furthermore, the simultaneous evaluation of isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic behaviors provide more comprehensive insight into the underlying adsorption mechanism, highlighting the strong potential of the prepared activated carbon for practical wastewater treatment applications. Olive pits, an abundant by-product of the olive oil industry, constitute a low-cost and renewable lignocellulosic precursor material for the sustainable production of activated carbon, In particular. Chemical activation using phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) effectively enhances surface functionality and pore development by generating micro- and mesoporous structures and introducing oxygenated and phosphate groups22. Several studies have demonstrated the high efficiency of olive pit–derived Activated Carbon for removing inorganic and organic contaminants, highlighting the sustainability of this approach23. The scientific novelty and originality of this work lies in the valorization of locally available olive pits from western Algeria as a sustainable material for production of activated carbon combined with comprehensive surface characterization (FTIR, SEM, XRD, XPS, and pHpzc) and a complete adsorption investigation of Tartrazine, a relatively less-studied anionic azo dye. Unlike previous studies, this work integrates BET multilayer isotherm modeling with kinetic, thermodynamic, and mechanistic analyses in a single framework, providing deeper insight into the adsorption process and demonstrating the strong potential of olive pit–derived activated carbon for large scale and real-world application wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tartrazine (E-102, 89% purity) served as the model pollutant. Chemical activation was performed using orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4, 40%), while pH regulation was carried out with NaOH (≥ 98%) and HCl (37%) solutions, and sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99%) for pHpzc determination. The main physicochemical properties of Tartrazine are summarized in Table 1. All chemicals employed in this study were of analytical grade purity and were used directly as received from the supplier without any additional purification steps. Distilled water was systematically used in all experimental procedures to ensure consistency. Olive pits, obtained from the food industry were used as precursors for Activated Carbon preparation.

Preparation of Activated Carbon

Following collection, the raw pits underwent sequential hot-water and distilled-water washing to eliminate surface impurities, after which the samples were subsequently oven-dried at 110 °C over a 24-hour period. A preliminary grinding and coarse sieving step were performed to eliminate unwanted particles. Thermal carbonization was performed in a muffle furnace using a covered ceramic crucible with restricted headspace, thereby establishing an auto-generated reducing atmosphere that suppressed oxidation. A controlled heating ramp of 10 °C/min brought the system to a final pyrolysis temperature of 700 °C, which was sustained for 120 min. Surface area development and pore formation were promoted by impregnating 10 g of carbonized material with solution (100 mL of 40% H3PO4) under continuous mechanical agitation for 24 h. Excess acid was neutralized by washing the impregnated solid with 0.1 M NaOH, followed by repeated distilled water rinsing until the effluent reached a near-neutral pH (~ 7). Finally, the Activated Carbon was dried at 110 °C for 24 h and stored in a desiccator for later use24. Characterization methods

The physicochemical and structural properties of the prepared Activated Carbon were characterized using several complementary analytical techniques. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted using a Perkin-Elmer spectrophotometer within the 400–4000 cm⁻¹ range to identify surface functional groups25. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) measurements were conducted on an FEI Nova Nano SEM 230 microscope operating at 8 kV to observe morphology, pore size and distribution, and surface roughness26. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was used to evaluate the crystalline or amorphous structure, employing a Bruker D4 ENDEAVOR diffractometer equipped with a CuKα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) and a graphite monochromator27. (XPS) ont fourni des informations complètes et approfondies qui ont permis une compréhension précise de la composition de surface et des caractéristiques chimiques du matériau using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer (Al Kα, 1486.6 eV) equipped with a hemispherical analyzer28. Additionally, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) was determined by the pH equilibrium method. A series of solutions (50 mL) were prepared with initial pH values ranging from 2 to 12 adjusting with HCl or NaOH solutions. Each solution was mixed with 0.05 g of Activated Carbon and stirred for 24 h at 25 °C. The pHpzc was defined as the initial pH for which ΔpH (pHf – pHi) equals zero. The ΔpH values were graphed versus the initial pH to identify the (pHpzc)29.

Adsorption experiments

To evaluate the uptake efficiency and adsorption performance of the Activated Carbon derived from olive pits, Tartrazine (E-102) was chosen as a model contaminant adsorbate due to its widespread use in food, pharmaceutical, and textile industries. This synthetic anionic dye appears as an orange powder with a purity of 89%. A stock solution of 1000 ppm was obtained by dissolving 1 g of Tartrazine in 1 L of distilled water and stirred for 24 h to ensure homogeneity. From this solution, serial dilutions were made to obtain different concentrations from 1 to 40 mg/L. The maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) was determined using UV-visible spectrophotometry within the 400–500 nm range and found to be 427 nm30. The absorbance of each diluted solution was measured at this wavelength, allowing the establishment of a calibration curve (A = f(C)), later used to determine residual Tartrazine concentrations after adsorption. The adsorption experiments were conducted using both raw and activated olive-pit carbon. Solutions of 40 ppm were prepared from the 1000 ppm stock solution. In 50 mL test tubes, 2 mL of Tartrazine solution was diluted with distilled water and mixed with 40 mg of the adsorbent. All suspensions were agitated on a reciprocating shaker for 24 h a duration established in preliminary tests as sufficient to guarantee equilibrium attainment across all tested conditions, followed by centrifugation three times at 10,000 rpm for 15 min31. Residual dye concentrations were measured at λmax = 427 nm using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer. Each experiment was replicated three times under consistent operating conditions; Mean values are reported with an associated relative standard deviation not exceeding ± 5%.

Results and discussion

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis of the raw olive pit material (NO) discloses spectral features typical of lignocellulosic structures, including a broad hydroxyl stretching band (3650–3750 cm−1) associated with cellulose/hemicellulose and a carbonyl absorption near 1700 cm−1 attributable to carboxylic functionalities, a signal at 1540 cm−1 attributed to the aromatic rings of lignin, as well as a distinct peak at 670 cm−1 corresponding to the aromatic C–H bonds (Fig. 2). These signals reflect a high content of oxygenated groups. After carbonization, the intensity of these bands decreases sharply, indicating the thermal decomposition of volatile compounds. In contrast, the persistence of the band at 1600 cm−1 testifies to the stability of the carbonized aromatic structures. Upon H3PO4 activation, two new absorption bands emerge at 1292 and 1120 cm−1, characteristic of P=O stretching and P–O–C linkages, which are diagnostic of phosphorus incorporation onto the carbon framework. The functional groups identified on the activated carbon surface play a crucial role in the adsorption of Tartrazine. Hydrogen bond formation between Tartrazine molecules and the surface hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl functionalities, combined with π-π conjugation between the graphitic carbon backbone and the dye’s aromatic rings, collectively drive the adsorption process. In acidic conditions, the protonation of surface functional groups lead to the generates positively charged sites, which enhances electrostatic attraction toward the anionic Tartrazine molecules. In addition, phosphate-related groups introduced by H2PO4 activation increase surface polarity and provide additional active sites for adsorption. These synergistic interactions, namely electrostatic forces, hydrogen-bond formation, and π–π stacking between aromatic structures, play a major role in enhancing the adsorption efficiency of the activated carbon. These structural transformations give Activated Carbon developed porosity and active groups capable of interacting with organic molecules such as Tartrazine via hydrogen bonds, π–π and electrostatic interactions. Comparable results have been reported in earlier studies, where phosphoric acid activation was shown to promote the formation of phosphate and oxygen-containing functional groups, enhancing surface polarity and improving the adsorption performance toward organic pollutants through electrostatic attraction and π–π interactions36,37.

SEM analysis

Pre-adsorption SEM imaging of the activated carbon reveals a markedly irregular surface topology featuring an interconnected network of pores with varied geometry (Fig. 3). The micrographs highlight the presence of interconnected macropores and mesopores, as well as cavities of various sizes, providing a large specific surface area conducive to adsorption. The rough and fissured texture suggests the existence of multiple active sites promoting interaction with tartrazine molecules. These morphological characteristics confirm the ability of the material to effectively trap organic dyes, particularly due to the availability of accessible pores and the irregular nature of its surface38–40. However, a closer examination of the SEM image indicates that the surface is predominantly irregular and fragmented, with rough edges, fissures, and void-like structures rather than clearly defined cylindrical pores. The observed morphology suggests that chemical activation mainly induced structural disruption and surface roughening, which contribute to the creation of adsorption sites and facilitate dye diffusion toward the internal structure of the carbon.

XRD analysis

The XRD pattern of the H3PO4-activated carbon is dominated by two diffuse scattering halos centered at approximately 2θ ≈ 22–25° and 2θ ≈ 42–45°, which can be assigned to the (002) and (100) reflections of turbostratic graphitic domains (Fig. 4). The breadth of these reflections is indicative of a predominantly disordered carbon structure, consistent with the disruptive effect of H3PO4 on long-range graphitic order during activation. The absence of well-defined crystalline peaks confirms that the material does not contain significant amounts of crystallized mineral phases, which is expected after effective post-activation washing. The first halo (≈ 24°) nevertheless attests to the existence of ordered but very small carbon micro-domains. Phosphoric acid activation is known to introduce oxygenated functional groups and promote the formation of micropores and mesopores, resulting in a strong structural disorder visible in the diffractogram. This amorphous microstructure, combined with an increased specific surface area, is particularly favorable for the adsorption of Tartrazine41–43.

Quantitative XPS analysis of H₃PO₄ activated carbon reveals a surface rich in carbon (64.99%), oxygen (29.07%), and phosphorus (5.94%), confirming the effective integration and uniform incorporation of phosphorus atoms within the carbon matrix of the material. (Fig. 5). The qualitative XPS spectrum reveals the presence of characteristic peaks of C 1s, O 1s, and P 2p, reflecting a dominant aromatic carbon structure enriched in oxygenated and phosphate functional groups. The decomposition of the C 1s peak shows, in addition to the C–C and C=C bonds (~ 284.6 eV), the presence of C–O (~ 286 eV), C = O (~ 287 eV) and O–C=O (~ 288.5 eV) functional groups, associated with hydroxyls, carbonyls and carboxyls, as well as a higher energy signal (~ 290–292 eV) attributed to C–O–P bonds generated by acid activation. The O 1s spectrum confirms the coexistence of carbonyl (~ 531 eV), hydroxyls and phosphates (~ 532.5 eV), and carboxyls (~ 533.5 eV) groups, while the P 2p spectrum indicates P–O/P=O (~ 133–134 eV) and C–O–P (~ 135 eV) bonds. These results demonstrate that H2PO4 activation is not limited to opening the porosity, but significantly enriches the surface chemistry with polar and phosphate groups, thus improving the reactivity and adsorption capacity of Activated Carbon towards organic molecules, in particular anionic dyes such as Tartrazine44–47. This indicates that the surface changes from a generally positive state to a negative state around pH 7.9, which corresponds to (pHpzc). This value suggests that the material has a neutral surface around pH 7.9. Thus, in an acidic environment (pH < pHpzc), the Activated Carbon surface will be positively charged, promoting the adsorption of (Tartrazine). On the other hand, at basic pH (pH > pHpzc), electrostatic repulsion can reduce the adsorption efficiency of these same anions. However, adsorption may still occur beyond the pHpzc due to alternative interaction mechanisms independent of electrostatic attraction. Even under conditions where both the adsorbent surface and the pollutant are negatively charged, non-electrostatic interactions such as π–π stacking between the aromatic rings and the graphitic structure, hydrogen bonding between surface functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –PO₄) and dye molecules, as well as van der Waals forces, can significantly contribute to dye uptake. In addition, the developed high specific surface area facilitates physical adsorption within micro- and mesopores, partially compensating for electrostatic repulsion. These results are consistent with the amphoteric nature of Activated Carbon surfaces and confirm the importance of pH control to optimize adsorption performance48,49. The significant enhancement in adsorption capacity observed in the vicinity of pH 5 can be explained by the establishment of an optimal equilibrium between the protonation state of the adsorbent surface functional groups and the degree of ionization of the dye molecules, which together favor stronger electrostatic interactions and improved adsorption efficiency. At this pH, the activated carbon surface remains positively charged, enhancing electrostatic attraction, while competition from excess H+ ions is minimized compared to strongly acidic conditions (pH ≈ 2). Furthermore, the increase observed after pH 8 may be related to the contribution of non-electrostatic interactions, such as π–π interactions between the aromatic structure and the graphitic domains, as well as possible hydrogen bonding. Even though electrostatic repulsion becomes significant above the pHpzc, these additional interaction mechanisms can partially compensate, resulting in a secondary rise in adsorption capacity. Recent studies have reported that dye adsorption on Carpobrotus edulis–based adsorbents is governed by a combination of electrostatic attraction. In particular, acid-treated C. edulis composites exhibited deep porous structures and active surface sites, leading to high adsorption capacities for rhodamine B and crystal violet, with equilibrium fitting the Langmuir isotherm and adsorption kinetics following the pseudo-second-order model, confirming a spontaneous adsorption process50. Similarly, native C. edulis biomass demonstrated effective crystal violet removal (Qm ≈ 86.95 mg/g), where the adsorption mechanism accompanied by exothermic and spontaneous thermodynamic behavior and reasonable regeneration stability over multiple cycles51.

In addition, the adsorption behavior at intermediate pH values reflects the combined influence of electrostatic interactions and surface functional group availability. The progressive decrease in adsorption from strongly acidic to near-neutral conditions can be attributed to a reduction in surface protonation, which weakens electrostatic attraction forces. Regarding the mass of Activated Carbon, an gradual increase in the quantity of adsorbent results in a decrease in the Qe expressed in mg/g, probably due to an overabundance of unused sites and the agglomeration of particles reducing internal accessibility53. Increasing the initial Tartrazine concentration improves the Qe, reflecting a favorable adsorption process where the concentration gradient acts as a driver of mass transfer54. Although removal efficiency (%) was not plotted separately, it can be directly calculated from the adsorption capacity data. As expected, removal efficiency decreases with increasing initial dye concentration due to saturation of available active sites, while Qe increases owing to the higher driving force for mass transfer. The effect of contact time reveals a two-step kinetic process: a rapid initial adsorption phase, followed by a slowdown step beginning at 6 h, which gradually leads to a Qe plateau after 24 h55. Although increasing the adsorbent mass and contact time enhances dye removal, a complete saturation plateau was not strictly achieved within the investigated experimental ranges. This behavior can be attributed to gradual occupation of active sites, intraparticle diffusion resistance within the porous structure of the activated carbon, and possible particle agglomeration at higher dosages, which can significantly reduce the accessibility of the effective surface area. Moreover, longer contact times may be required to allow full diffusion of Tartrazine molecules into the internal micropores, particularly in heterogeneous multilayer adsorption systems. Finally, increasing temperature induces a decrease in Qe, indicating an exothermic process where increased thermal agitation promotes desorption, making adsorption thermodynamically less favorable at high temperatures56. Based on the comprehensive parametric study, the optimal operating conditions were determined as pH = 2, adsorbent dose = 40 mg, initial Tartrazine concentration = 40 mg/L, contact time = 24 h, and temperature = 298 K. These conditions were therefore selected for isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic investigations.

The adsorption mechanism of Tartrazine onto H2PO4-activated carbon is governed by multiple interactions. At acidic pH (pH < pHpzc), the surface of carbon becomes positively charged, thereby enhancing strong electrostatic attraction and π–π interactions. Hydrogen bonding between surface oxygen-containing functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –PO4) and dye molecules also contributes to adsorption. Furthermore, van der Waals forces facilitate multilayer adsorption, as confirmed by the BET isotherm model. Therefore, the overall adsorption process follows a mixed mechanism dominated by physisorption with a minor contribution of chemisorption.

Conclusion

The present work establishes olive pits a abundantly available agro-industrial residue as an effective and sustainable precursor for producing activated carbon with high Tartrazine removal efficiency from water. H3PO4 mediated activation yielded a microporous amorphous carbon material with an enriched surface chemistry featuring oxygenated and phosphate groups that markedly amplified the adsorbent-dye interaction strength. Characterization by FTIR, SEM, XRD, and XPS confirmed successful carbonization and activation, revealing a heterogeneous morphology and a chemically reactive surface. Batch adsorption experiments highlighted that the process strongly depends on pH, contact time, initial dye concentration, adsorbent dose, and temperature. Maximum adsorption occurred in acidic conditions and decreased with temperature, confirming the exothermic process. Kinetic modeling indicated that the pseudo-second-order model describes the prosses with a best fits the data (R2 = 0.999), indicating a mixed mechanism dominated by physisorption with a minor contribution of chemisorption. The Freundlich, Temkin, and especially BET isotherms provided the best fits (R2 = 0.946, 0.969, and 0.997, respectively), demonstrating multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface, with a BET maximum adsorption capacity of 47.18 mg g−1, while the Langmuir model yielded a higher theoretical Qm of 80.00 mg g−1. Thermodynamic analysis revealed negative ΔG° values, confirming the spontaneous process, while the negative ΔH° value indicated an exothermic interaction. These findings validate that the H2PO4- Activated Carbon derived from olive pits is an efficient, and a low-cost, and sustainable adsorbent for dye removal. In conclusion, olive pit–based Activated Carbon represents a economical, and high-performance adsorbent for the efficient removal of Tartrazine. Its use promotes agricultural waste valorization and environmental protection. After adsorption, the spent activated carbon can be regenerated by thermal treatment or solvent washing for potential reuse. If regeneration is not feasible, the exhausted adsorbent may be safely disposed of through controlled incineration or stabilization followed by landfilling in accordance with environmental regulations, thereby minimizing secondary pollution risks. Despite the promising results obtained in this study, some limitations should be acknowledged. The adsorption experiments were conducted using synthetic dye solutions under batch conditions, and real industrial wastewater was not investigated. In addition, regeneration and reuse were discussed but not experimentally evaluated. Future work should therefore focus on column studies, regeneration cycles, economic assessment, and application to real effluents to further validate the practical feasibility of the developed adsorbent.
