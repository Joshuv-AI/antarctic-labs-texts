Acute Toxicity From Short-Course Radiation Therapy for Rectal Cancer: A Single-Institution Experience
Authors: Shane S Neibart, Katie N Lee, Yu-Hui Chen, Alexander Droznin, Mai Anh Huynh, Christian Guthier, Neil Martin, Joseph D Mancias, Miranda B Lam, Ron Shiloh, Luke Peng, Kimmie Ng, Rishi Surana, Peter C Enzinger, Jeffrey A Meyerhardt, Harvey J Mamon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeShort-course radiation therapy (SCRT) is an important neoadjuvant treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer. Although SCRT offers logistical advantages and possibly reduced toxicity compared to long-course chemoradiation, emerging data have raised concerns about long-term outcomes and underrecognized adverse effects. This study examines acute toxicities after SCRT, including a poorly understood syndrome, acute lumbosacral plexopathy (ALSP).Methods and MaterialsPatients with locally advanced rectal cancer treated between 2016 and 2022 with neoadjuvant SCRT (25 Gy/5 fractions) were retrospectively analyzed. Acute toxicity was assessed up to 30 days after SCRT completion. ALSP was defined as pelvic or buttock pain with or without radiation down the lower extremities. Patients without reported ALSP were matched to cases based on N stage, tumor location, treatment position, and treatment modality, and the lumbosacral plexus and psoas muscles were contoured for matched patients. Dosimetric and clinicodemographic variables were compared for patients with and without toxicity using the Mann-Whitney U test and χ2 test.ResultsAmong 71 patients identified, the median age was 56 years, and approximately half were female. The 30-day incidence of any-grade toxicity was 75%; one-third of first toxicities occurred more than 10 days after the start of SCRT. Notably, 10 (14%) patients developed ALSP, presenting at a median of 2.5 days after treatment initiation and resolving within 1 week. Most were treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or acetaminophen, but 2 required additional medications. ALSP was not associated with planning target volume dose, small bowel dose, lumbosacral plexus dose, or muscle composition parameters.ConclusionsThree-quarters of patients treated with SCRT develop any acute toxicity, and ALSP was identified in 14% of patients. All cases of ALSP were transient, without late sequelae. These findings highlight the importance of toxicity surveillance beyond the treatment window. Future work should identify predictors of ALSP and potential mitigation strategies.

Introduction

Radiation therapy plays a critical role in the neoadjuvant treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer. Although many neoadjuvant treatment approaches exist, including chemotherapy alone, radiation therapy is widely accepted as an efficacious and safe treatment to reduce the risk of local recurrence.1, 2, 3, 4 The optimal delivery of radiation therapy is debated with different practice patterns in the United States and Europe.2,3 The safety and efficacy of short-course radiation therapy (SCRT) versus long-course chemoradiation (LCCRT) is at the center of these conversations.

The Polish I and Trans-Tasman Radiation Oncology Group (TROG) 01.04 trials suggested no difference in local control or overall survival whether radiation was delivered with SCRT or LCCRT.5, 6, 7, 8 In the decades following publication of these studies, newer treatment approaches have included radiation omission, total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), and nonoperative management.4,9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Among these complex paradigms, SCRT as part of TNT presented in the RAPIDO trial became an area of interest, given an improvement in disease-related treatment failure with TNT in a high-risk population.16,17 The benefit of SCRT as part of TNT was then questioned in the setting of excellent outcomes after TNT with LCCRT and an unexpectedly increased rate of locoregional recurrence after SCRT in long-term follow-up of the RAPIDO trial (10% vs 6% in the LCCRT arm).9,12,13,18

Despite these concerns, SCRT with TNT remains an important paradigm, especially for patients who must travel a great distance to receive treatment or for patients with distant metastases to limit time off systemic therapy. One gap in our understanding of SCRT is the true measure of acute toxicity. Although initial estimates from the Polish I trial suggested a remarkable reduction of acute toxicity with SCRT versus LCCRT (24% vs 85%), some have questioned the evaluation of toxicity given the possibility of not capturing low-grade toxicity occurring in the 1 to 2 weeks following SCRT.5,6 Understanding the true burden of toxicity from SCRT is even more critical in the setting of new hypofractionated treatment paradigms for other pelvic malignancies, especially prostate cancer (NCT05946213),19 where dose constraints are still being optimized.20, 21, 22

In this report, we describe our institutional experience with SCRT as part of definitive treatment for rectal cancer, with a focus on acute treatment-related toxicities. We emphasize the timing of acute adverse effects, particularly those occurring between SCRT and chemotherapy or surgery. We also describe the incidence of an underreported syndrome of acute lumbosacral plexopathy (ALSP). We explore clinical, anatomic, and dosimetric factors that may predict ALSP, with the goal of better understanding the etiology of this adverse effect.

Methods and Materials

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients treated with preoperative SCRT for rectal cancer at a single academic medical center and its affiliated network sites (4 total sites). Patients with locally advanced disease and planned curative intent treatment were included. Patients with metastatic disease were excluded from the analysis. The primary objective of the study was to identify the acute (<30 days) toxicities of patients receiving SCRT. The secondary objectives were to describe predictors of acute toxicity and describe oncologic outcomes.

Treatment

All patients were treated with a prescription dose of 25 Gy in 5 fractions to the rectum and pelvis. Patients were simulated in a supine or prone position per the treating physician’s discretion. Patients were treated with 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (4-field box or 3-field) or intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) based on the treating physician’s discretion.

Measures

Acute toxicity was measured through a systematic review of all patient encounters in the electronic health record from the start of radiation therapy until 30 days posttreatment. This review included all patient portal messages and telephone encounters during this period. Toxicity was measured according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0.23 For the purposes of this investigation, ALSP was defined as new-onset pelvic or buttock pain, with or without radiation to the posterior thighs or calves, occurring during SCRT or within 30 days of its completion. Although the precise etiology of this pain syndrome is not fully characterized, the symptom distribution most closely follows the course of the lumbosacral plexus (LSP). The qualifier “acute” differentiates this self-limited presentation from chronic or progressive lumbosacral plexopathy, which can occur months to years after pelvic radiation therapy.24, 25, 26 Clinical and demographic characteristics were extracted for all patients at the time of diagnosis. Magnetic resonance imaging features were extracted from the staging magnetic resonance imaging.

Dosimetric predictors of acute toxicity were extracted from the treatment planning system. In addition to the small bowel and planning target volume (PTV) structures, the psoas muscles and LSP were contoured retrospectively to assess if muscle or plexus dose-volume metrics correlated with toxicity. The psoas muscle was contoured from the third lumbar vertebral body through the fifth sacral vertebral level. Psoas muscle area was computed at the middle of the third lumbar, and psoas muscle index was computed as the quotient of the average area (left and right) and height squared, as previously described.27 The LSP was contoured in accordance with a standardized atlas.28

A retrospective review of the electronic health record was conducted by 2 physician investigators (S.S.N. and K.N.L.). Both investigators independently abstracted records for the first 10 patients and for all patients identified as having experienced ALSP, in order to review discrepancies and ensure data consistency.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for continuous (median and interquartile range) and categorical (number and percent) variables. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test and χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, respectively. The cumulative incidence of any-grade acute toxicity and ALSP was calculated using Fine-Gray regression, with day of radiation initiation as time zero. For patients experiencing multiple toxicities, only the first event was considered for time-to-event analyses.

To explore dosimetric relationships of the psoas and LSP with ALSP, a subsample of the cohort was analyzed. To reduce confounding from anatomic and treatment-related factors, we performed a stratified case-control matching procedure. Controls were grouped by tumor location, nodal involvement, treatment position, and treatment modality; patients with ALSP were matched in a 1:1 ratio to ensure clinical comparability. Muscle composition metrics and dose-volume metrics were compared between patients with and without ALSP using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Seventy-one patients treated with neoadjuvant SCRT with definitive intent were identified. The median age was 56 years, and 35 (49%) patients were female. The majority of tumors were located in the middle and upper rectum and were muscle-invasive (cT3) and node negative (cN0). The most common presenting symptoms were hematochezia, followed by diarrhea, and weight loss. All clinicodemographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

No patients reported any weakness or sensory changes. Pain was reported as mild in 7 patients and moderate in 3, which informed the toxicity grading. One patient had moderate symptoms requiring treatment with gabapentin and hydrocortisone acetate suppository (pain was presumed related to rectal pain), and one patient was prescribed low-dose (2-4 mg daily) dexamethasone. After identification of this toxicity, potential predictors of ALSP were then explored. There were no significant associations between ALSP and any clinicodemographic factors, including timing of chemotherapy (Table E2). In this limited sample, there was some suggestion that patients reporting ALSP were younger than those who did not report this toxicity (median ages 49.5 vs 68 years, P = .17). No patients who developed ALSP were treated with IMRT (0% vs 12%, P = .58), with a 3-field technique (0% vs 3%, P = 1.00), or in a supine position (0% vs 20%, P = .28). There were no differences in PTV metrics, but patients with ALSP had lower small bowel volumes receiving at least 20 Gy (V20Gy) and 25 Gy (V25Gy, Table 3). Overall heterogeneity was low with a median PTV maximum dose (Dmax) of 26.5 Gy (106%) and a maximum PTV Dmax of 27.6 Gy (110%). Additionally, there were no significant correlations between other reported symptoms and ALSP (Fig. E1).

Discussion

This retrospective study presented real-world estimates of acute toxicity after SCRT for locally advanced rectal cancer. In an era of increased patient access to their providers through “patient portals,” we identified toxicities from SCRT that occur outside of the window of usual care. Using this new source of data, we have identified a previously underreported toxicity, ALSP, which was reported in 14% of patients. This study adds to our understanding of possible acute effects of 25 Gy in 5 fractions to the pelvis, which can inform rectal cancer management as well as hypofractionated elective nodal coverage offered in treating other pelvic malignancies.

Previous estimates of acute toxicity from SCRT are mainly derived from clinical trials. The Polish I study estimated that the risk of acute toxicity was 24% after SCRT and 85% after LCCRT.5,6 One explanation for the lower rate of toxicity appreciated after SCRT is a delay of symptom onset until after completion of treatment. This is partially explained by our data, where 25% of adverse effects occurred 10 days after the start of RT. Although other studies, such as the Stockholm III trial, did report acute toxicity measures between SCRT and surgery, data were limited to grade 3 to 4 toxicity necessitating hospital admission (6% after SCRT).29 Similarly, the RAPIDO trial did not specify the timing of toxicity after SCRT as part of TNT, and they only assessed toxicity weekly while patients were on treatment.16,17 Our data suggest that an accurate accounting of grade 1 to 2 toxicity after SCRT or other similar hypofractionated regimens necessitates deliberate patient outreach in the 2 weeks after completing treatment or inclusion of remotely collected patient-reported outcomes.

Prior retrospective series have not identified the same rates of acute toxicity, with estimates ranging from 25% to 33%.30, 31, 32, 33 Our study identified a 75% rate of any-grade acute toxicity, with acute diarrhea as the most common adverse effect, occurring in nearly 50% of the patients, which is strikingly similar to estimates from the TROG 01.04 trial.8 These differences could be explained by the incorporation of patient portal messages and clinical communications in toxicity assessments. Reassuringly, high rates of acute grade 3 to 4 toxicity were not observed in our cohort, matching a contemporary prospective series.34

Prior to the advent of patient portal messaging, the syndrome of ALSP was underrecognized at our institution. Although this constellation of symptoms has been previously reported after SCRT, there is little mention of these effects in modern series. Frykholm et al35 produced the most comprehensive description of these symptoms. In a careful review of 443 patients treated between 1979 and 1992, they reported a crude incidence of 2.5% (11 cases); in 1993, they noted an increase in rates of toxicity to 25.4% (15 cases). They exonerated technical issues in delivery as the cause for the increase in toxicity in 1993. Patients were treated with a 3-field technique before 1994, and after implementing a 4-field-box technique in 1994, transient pain was reported in 2 (4.2%) patients. These incidence rates, ranging from 4% to 25%, are consistent with our estimates of 14%. Interestingly, their series did note that some patients had developed late toxicity, including pain and neurologic symptoms (weakness or numbness), whereas no patients in our cohort or in the TROG 01.04 trial developed similar findings.7

Frykholm et al35 attempted to explore possible causes for these symptoms, including analyzing hot spots near the LSP and assessing for anatomic variations in the pelvis. Although they did not compute statistics, they found that the Dmax to the LSP did not exceed 109% in the patients they tested. In simulation studies, they found that treatment with a 3-field technique without posterior shields could increase hot spots in the LSP to 112%. To mitigate exposure to the LSP, they proposed the use of a 4-field technique and lowering the superior field border to below the sacral promontory. Patients in our cohort had a median Dmax to the PTV of 26.5 Gy (106%) and to the LSP of 26.3 Gy (105%); the maximum dose to the PTV in any patient was 27.6 Gy (110%) and to the LSP was 27.1 Gy (108%). We did not find any differences in LSP dosimetry between matched patients, and we even demonstrated a trend for lower LSP V26Gy among patients with ALSP. These data suggest that if the etiology of this syndrome is secondary to LSP dose, individual susceptibility likely varies among patients. In other words, without LSP hot spots >109%, development of ALSP may ultimately be idiosyncratic. Another unexplained finding is the lower small bowel V20Gy and V25Gy in patients with ALSP, which may reflect small differences in field position or type I errors. Notably, no patients treated with IMRT in our cohort developed ALSP, although the use of IMRT was rare overall (12% of patients without ALSP and only 10% of the overall cohort). Although this finding was not statistically significant, it suggests a possible link between treatment approach and ALSP. We also investigated potential psoas muscle dose-volume associations with ALSP and did not find any significant trends that would elucidate the etiology of this pain syndrome. Given the radiating character of the pain, including extension down the posterior lower extremities to the calves in some patients, LSP irritation is more likely the cause of the symptoms than psoas muscle spasm.

Our study had several notable limitations. The retrospective nature of this investigation leads to potential misclassification of acute toxicity. Another risk is underdiagnosis, given the lack of scheduled regular follow-up during the posttreatment period. Grading errors, especially for grade 1 versus grade 2 toxicity, may also have occurred. Incorporating patient portal messages in toxicity grading is a novel technique that may also introduce bias and error in data abstraction. For example, younger and more fit patients may be more likely to use the patient portal, and grading toxicity from patient messages (without clinical evaluation) has not been validated. Another important limitation is our classification of a previously unclassified symptom cluster as a distinct toxicity. In a retrospective setting, it can be challenging to discern if a patient's report of symptoms represents a novel toxicity, whereas prospective collection may have yielded a different diagnosis. Finally, our limited sample size introduces type II error in our evaluation of predictors of ALSP, especially given our limited analysis of a 20-patient case-control subcohort. We cannot fully exclude the possibility of important associations between dose-volume metrics and ALSP.

Conclusions

SCRT is a valuable tool in the management of patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, and this study clarifies the temporality and burden of acute adverse effects. Diarrhea was the most common adverse effect, and ALSP, an underreported and poorly understood toxicity, occurred in 14% of patients. All cases of ALSP occurred within the first week of treatment, resolved within 1 week, and were not associated with late pain or neurologic sequelae. As SCRT and other hypofractionated pelvic radiation regimens gain wider adoption, toxicity assessments should extend into the 2 weeks following treatment, and patients should be counseled on the potential for ALSP and other delayed acute effects. Future work should interrogate the etiology of ALSP and explore mitigation strategies.
