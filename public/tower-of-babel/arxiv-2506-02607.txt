On the shape of the typical Poisson-Voronoi cell in high dimensions
Matthias Irlbeck, Zakhar Kabluchko, Tobias Müller

Abstract
We study the typical cell of the Poisson-Voronoi tessellation. We show that when divided by the d d -th root of the intensity parameter λ \lambda of the Poisson process times the volume of the unit ball, the inradius, outradius, diameter and mean width of the typical cell converge in probability to the constants 1 / 2 , 1 , 2 , 2 1/2,1,2,2 respectively, as the dimension d → ∞ d\to\infty . We also show that the width of the typical cell, when rescaled in the same way, is bounded between 2 ​ 5 / ( 2 + 5 ) − o d ​ ( 1 ) 2\sqrt{5}/(2+\sqrt{5})-o_{d}(1) and 3 / 2 + o d ​ ( 1 ) 3/2+o_{d}(1) , with probability 1 − o d ​ ( 1 ) 1-o_{d}(1) . These results in particular imply that, with probability 1 − o d ​ ( 1 ) 1-o_{d}(1) , the Hausdorff distance between the typical cell and any ball is at least of the order of the diameter of the typical cell. In addition, we show that for all k k with d − k → ∞ d-k\to\infty , with probability 1 − o d ​ ( 1 ) 1-o_{d}(1) , all faces of dimension k k have a diameter that is of a much smaller order than the diameter, inradius, etc., of the full typical cell. The same is true for “almost all” faces of dimension d − k d-k with k k fixed. And, we show that the number of such faces is ( ( k + 1 ) ( k + 1 ) / 2 / k k / 2 ± o d ​ ( 1 ) ) d \left((k+1)^{(k+1)/2}/k^{k/2}\pm o_{d}(1)\right)^{d} with probability 1 − o d ​ ( 1 ) 1-o_{d}(1) .

Introduction and statement of results

Throughout this paper,
{\mathcal{Z}}
will denote a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of constant intensity
\lambda>0
.
The
Voronoi cell
of a point
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
is defined as

{\mathcal{V}}(z):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-z\|\leq\|x-z^{\prime}\|\text{ for all $z^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}$ }\}.

The Voronoi cells
{\mathcal{V}}(z):z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
constitute a dissection of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, called the
Poisson-Voronoi

tessellation. A standard fact states that, almost surely, every Voronoi cell
{\mathcal{V}}(z)
is a convex polytope
with
z
in its interior. (For a proof, see e.g.
[
19
]
, Lemma 10.1.1 and the discussion
immediately preceding Theorem 10.2.1.)
The Poisson-Voronoi tessellation is one of the central models
in stochastic geometry. It is studied in connection with many different applications and has
a long history going back at least to the work of Meijering
[
16
]
in the early fifties.
For an overview, see the monographs
[
19
,
21
]
and the references therein.

We will be considering the
typical cell
, which is the Voronoi
cell of the origin
\underline{0}
in the Voronoi tessellation for
{\mathcal{Z}}\cup\{\underline{0}\}
, the Poisson point process with the origin
added in. Throughout the paper we will denote the typical cell by
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
Again,
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is almost surely a polytope with the origin in its interior (see again
[
19
]
for the proof).

The significance of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is that, as the name “typical cell” suggests, it describes the
average behaviour of the cells of the Poisson-Voronoi tessellation.
For instance, if we take the fraction of all Poisson points
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
inside the ball
B(\underline{0},r)
around the
origin of radius
r

for which
{\mathcal{V}}(z)
has precisely
k
vertices then, as
r\to\infty
, this fraction converges
almost surely to
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\text{ has $k$ vertices})
.
(Here and in the rest of the paper
B(x,r)
denotes the
d
-dimensional open ball with center
x
and radius
r
.)
More generally, for
h
a translation invariant and appropriately measurable function from the space of
polytopes
into
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(\underline{0},r)}h\left({\mathcal{V}}(z)\right)}{\left|{\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(\underline{0},r)\right|}={\mathbb{E}}h({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\quad\text{ a.s. }

(1)

(See e.g.
[
6
]
and the references therein.)

The Poisson-Voronoi tessellation is a classical subject in stochastic geometry and its typical cell has been studied
quite extensively. The behaviour of the typical cell as the dimension grows is however still relatively unexplored.
Here we will study the behaviour of some parameters of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
related to its shape and size, as the dimension grows.
Namely, we consider the inradius, outradius, diameter, width
and mean width, denoted by
\inr(.)
,
\outr(.)
,
\diam(.)
,
\width(.)
,
\meanw(.)
, respectively.
(Detailed definitions can be found in Section
2
below.)
We denote by
\vol(.)
the
d
-dimensional volume (Lebesgue measure) and by
B:=B(\underline{0},1)
the

d
-dimensional unit ball.

Theorem 1

For any
\lambda=\lambda(d)>0
we have

\frac{\outr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}1,\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{\inr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1}{2},

\frac{\diam({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}2,\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{\meanw({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}2.

To clarify, we emphasize that in the above theorem the intensity
\lambda
is allowed to vary with the dimension.
Applying Stirling’s approximation to the Gamma function in the expression (
2
) for
\vol(B)
below,
it is easily seen that in the case when
\lambda>0
is a fixed constant, the denominators
in Theorem
1
can be replaced by
\sqrt{2\pi e/d}
.

We would like to mention that while finalizing this paper, we learned that K. Alishahi has shown in his PhD
thesis
[
1
]

that the ratio
\outr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})/\inr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
tends to 2 in probability as
d\to\infty
, using different methods
to the ones we employ. Alishahi’s thesis is written in Persian and does not appear to
be available online at the time of writing.

We also offer the following less precise result on the width of the typical cell:

Proposition 2

We have, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda=\lambda(d)>0
:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{2+\sqrt{5}}-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\leq\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon\right)=1-o_{d}(1).

To clarify, we emphasize that in the above result we choose
\varepsilon>0
fixed as the dimension
increases towards infinity.
Note that
2\sqrt{5}/(2+\sqrt{5})\approx 1.0557>1
. So Proposition
2

shows that the width is both bounded away by a multiplicative constant from twice the inradius (a trivial lower bound)
and bounded away by a multiplicative constant from the diameter (a trivial upper bound).
We were not able to prove, but conjecture that
\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})/\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}
converges to a constant
in probability.

A well-known folklore result states that
{\mathbb{E}}\vol({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})=1/\lambda
, in any dimension. (This can for instance be
seen from (
1
) together with some relatively straightforward considerations.)
Alishahi and Sharifitabar
[
2
]
have shown that
\Var\left(\vol({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\right)=o_{d}(1)
as
the dimension
d\to\infty
and the intensity
\lambda
is kept constant, which
in particular implies that the volume of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
tends to
1/\lambda
in probability as

d\to\infty
. In the light of (
1
), this can be informally paraphrased as: “in high dimension, almost all
Voronoi cells have volume arbitrarily close to
1/\lambda
”.
The result of Alishahi and Sharifitabar was later extended by Yao
[
23
]
, who showed that the variance of the
volume of the intersection of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
with any measurable set is bounded by the variance of the volume of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
Additional results in
[
2
]
imply that if
r=\sqrt[d]{1/\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}
is such that

\vol(B(\underline{0},r))=1/\lambda
then almost all mass of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is contained in
B(\underline{0},(1+\varepsilon)r)
and
almost all mass of
B(\underline{0},(1-\varepsilon)r)
is contained in
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
By this we mean that both the ratios
\vol({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\cap B(\underline{0},(1-\varepsilon)r))/\vol(B(\underline{0},(1-\varepsilon)r))
and

\vol({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\cap B(\underline{0},(1+\varepsilon)r))/\vol({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
tend to one in probability as
the dimension
d\to\infty
.
This can be interpreted as credence for the idea that the typical cell is somehow “ball like”, at least as far as the
volume is concerned.
On a similar note, Hörmann et al.
[
9
]
investigated the asymptotics of the expected number of

k
-faces of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
as the dimension goes to infinity.
Based on their findings, they mention (page 13, paragraph following Theorem 3.20) “… we roughly speaking see that
the typical Poisson-Voronoi cells are approximately spherical in the mean …”.

In contrast, our results on diameter, mean width and outradius all seem to support the idea the typical cell is somehow close to
a ball of the same volume, while the (proof of the) results on the inradius and width
suggest the typical cell behaves rather differently from a ball of the same volume.
Our results for instance imply that the Hausdorff distance between
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
and any ball is large (i.e. at least
of the same order as the diameter of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
).
For completeness we spell out the short argument demonstrating this in Appendix
A
.

During the course of the proofs of the above results, we will derive and heavily rely on
the following observation that is of independent interest:
with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
all
vertices of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
have norm
close to
\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}
.
This generalizes as follows. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(P)
denote the set of
k
-faces of the polytope
P
.
The union
\bigcup{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(P)
of all
k
-faces is sometimes also called the

-skeleton of

Proposition 3

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed and
k=k(d)
be such that
d-k\to\infty
.
For any
\lambda=\lambda(d)>0
we have:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\bigcup{\mathcal{F}}_{k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\subseteq B(\underline{0},(1+\varepsilon)r)\setminus B(\underline{0},(1-\varepsilon)r)\right)\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{}1,

where

This last proposition tells us that, with probability

, all faces of non-constant co-dimension
are contained in an annulus around
\underline{0}
of width

and

(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot\sqrt[d]{\lambda\vol(B)}
.
From this it can be seen that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, every face of non-constant co-dimension
is “microscopic” (i.e. of diameter
o\left(\sqrt[d]{\lambda\vol(B)}\right)
).

Corollary 4

Let
k=k(d)
be such that
d-k\to\infty
.
For any
\lambda=\lambda(d)>0
we have:

\frac{\max_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}\diam(F)}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}0.

(For completeness, we spell out the short derivation of Corollary
4
from
Proposition
3

Theorem

tells us that Proposition

What is more, Corollary

As part of the proof of Proposition

We briefly recall the definitions of the main parameters we’ll be studying in the present paper.
The

The

\begin{array}[]{l}\inr(A):=\sup\{r>0:\text{$\exists p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ such that $B(p,r)\subseteq A$}\},\\

We remark that (in the case when
A
is the typical cell of
a Poisson-Voronoi tessellation) when defining the inradius, respectively outradius,
some authors (e.g.
[
7
]
) take the
\sup
, respectively
\inf
,
of the radius of all balls centered on the origin
\underline{0}
that are contained in
A
, respectively contain
A
.

We denote by
S^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere (in ambient
d
-dimensional space).
The
width in the direction
u\in S^{d-1}
is defined by

w(u,A):=\sup_{a\in A}u^{t}a-\inf_{a\in A}u^{t}a.

Here and in the rest of the paper

denotes the inner product of the vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The (ordinary)

is

\width(A):=\inf_{u\in S^{d-1}}w(u,A).

The

, as its name suggests, is the average of

.
One of several equivalent ways to define this more formally is by setting

\meanw(A):={\mathbb{E}}w(U,A),

where
U
is point chosen uniformly at random on
S^{d-1}
.

For

Proofs

We recall that a dilation of an intensity
\lambda
Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a factor of

\rho>0
yields a Poisson process of intensity
\rho^{-d}\lambda
whose typical cell is
just the typical cell of the original process rescaled by
\rho
.
In particular, for the proofs of our results we can take any value of
\lambda

we like (and the result will follow for all choices
\lambda
).

We find it convenient to take

\lambda:=1/\kappa_{d}.

(This way the numerator in our main results equals one; and also
the expected number of Poisson points in a ball of radius
s
is simply
s^{d}
).
We will be using this choice of
\lambda
throughout the remainder of the paper, without
stating it explicitly every time.
We will denote by
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
the Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda=1/\kappa_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.1
All vertices have norm approximately one

Lemma 10

For every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, all vertices of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
have norm
>1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We use the elementary observation that if
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
then
there are
d
points
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
of the Poisson process such that

z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in\partial B(p,\|p\|)
and in addition
{\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(p,\|p\|)=\emptyset
.
(This because a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
must lie on the common boundary of
d+1
Voronoi cells, one of which must be
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}

itself. This translates to
p
being equidistant to
\underline{0}
and
d
points of the Poisson point process
{\mathcal{Z}}
,
and in addition no point of
{\mathcal{Z}}
can be closer to
p
than the common distance – which must be
\|p\|
.)

We let
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)<\varepsilon/1000
be a small constant.
Any vertex
v
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
with norm
\leq 1-\varepsilon
is contained in one of the cubes of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\delta}
as defined above.
That cube itself is completely contained in

B(\underline{0},1-\varepsilon+\delta)\subseteq B
. Let
c
be one of the corners of the cube containing
v
.
By the elementary observation above,
B(c,1-\varepsilon+2\delta)
contains at least
d
points of the Poisson process.
Hence, if
E
denotes the event that there exists a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
with norm
\leq 1-\varepsilon
then by the union bound:

{\mathbb{P}}(E)\leq N_{\delta,1}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Po((1-\varepsilon+2\delta)^{d})\geq d\right),

(The first term is an upper bound on the number of cubes considered and the second term is the probability
the ball around a corner of a given cube has
\geq d
Poisson points in it.)

By (
6
) we have
N_{\delta,1}=e^{O(d)}
.
On the other hand, using the version of the Chernoff bound we presented as Lemma
9
above,
we see that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Po((1-\varepsilon+2\delta)^{d})\geq d\right)\leq\exp\left[-(1-\varepsilon+2\delta)^{d}H\left(\frac{d}{(1-\varepsilon+2\delta)^{d}}\right)\right]=e^{-\Omega(d^{2})}.

Combining these bounds, we see that
{\mathbb{P}}(E)=o_{d}(1)
, as claimed in the lemma statement.

\blacksquare

Lemma 11

For every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, every vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
has norm
<1+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

As in the proof of the previous lemma, we use that if
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
then
there are
d
points
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
of the Poisson process such that

z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in\partial B(p,\|p\|)
and in addition
{\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(p,\|p\|)=\emptyset
.

We fix some
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)\in(0,\min(\varepsilon/2,1))
.
If
p\in B(\underline{0},2)\setminus B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)
is a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
then
there is some cube
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\delta}
satisfying that
q\subseteq B(\underline{0},3)
and

B(c,1+\varepsilon-\delta)\cap{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset
for
c
any corner of
q
.
Similarly, if for some
n\geq 2
a point
p\in B(\underline{0},n+1)\setminus B(\underline{0},n)
is
a vertex of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
then
there is some cube
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\delta}
satisfying that
q\subseteq B(\underline{0},n+2)
and

B(c,n-\delta)\cap{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset
for
c
any corner of
q
.

It follows that if
E
is the event that
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
has at least one vertex outside
B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)
then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(E)&\leq&N_{\delta,3}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\Po((1+\varepsilon-\delta)^{d})=0)+\sum_{n\geq 2}N_{\delta,n+2}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\Po((n-\delta)^{d})=0)\\
&=&e^{O(d)-(1+\varepsilon-\delta)^{d}}+\exp[O(d)]\cdot\sum_{n\geq 2}(n+2)^{d}\cdot e^{-(n-\delta)^{d}}\\
&=&o_{d}(1),\end{array}

using (
6
) above.

\blacksquare

3.2
The outradius, diameter and mean width

Since
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is the convex hull of its vertices, Lemma
11
implies that

{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)
(for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
).
In particular:

Corollary 12

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
\meanw({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq\diam({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2+\varepsilon
and
\outr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<1+\varepsilon
.

We need to prove matching lower bounds.
The following observation does the trick for the diameter and outradius.

Lemma 13

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have

(1-\varepsilon,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
and
(\varepsilon-1,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.

Proof.

By symmetry and the union bound, we just need to show that

{\mathbb{P}}((1-\varepsilon,0,\dots,0)\not\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})=o_{d}(1)
.
We simply note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}\left((1-\varepsilon,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\right)&=&{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap B((1-\varepsilon,0,\dots,0),1-\varepsilon)=\emptyset)\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(\Po((1-\varepsilon)^{d})=0)\\
&=&e^{-(1-\varepsilon)^{d}}\\
&=&1-o_{d}(1).\end{array}

\blacksquare

Corollary 14

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
\diam({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>2-\varepsilon
and
\outr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>1-\varepsilon
.

For the mean width the lower bound also follows quite easily.

Lemma 15

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have

\meanw({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>2-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We pick
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
a small constant to be determined.
We define

{\mathcal{X}}:=\{u\in S^{d-1}:w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta\},\quad X:=\sigma({\mathcal{X}})/\sigma(S^{d-1}),

where
\sigma
denotes the Haar measure on
S^{d-1}
(the “
(d-1)
-dimensional area”).
Put differently,
X
is the fraction of
S^{d-1}
that is covered by directions in
which the width is
<2-\delta
.
We have

\meanw({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\geq(1-X)\cdot(2-\delta).

Next, we observe that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}X&=&\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma(S^{d-1})}\int_{S^{d-1}}1_{\{w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta\}}\sigma({\dd}u)\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma(S^{d-1})}\int_{S^{d-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(1_{\{w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta\}}\right)\sigma({\dd}u)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma(S^{d-1})}\int_{S^{d-1}}{\mathbb{P}}(w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta)\sigma({\dd}u)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma(S^{d-1})}\int_{S^{d-1}}{\mathbb{P}}(w(e_{1},{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta)\sigma({\dd}u)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(w(e_{1},{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta).\end{array}

where
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
denotes the first standard basis vector,
we used Fubini for non-negative integrands in the second equality and symmetry in the fourth.
Applying Lemma
13
:

{\mathbb{E}}X={\mathbb{P}}(w(e_{1},{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<2-\delta)\leq 1-{\mathbb{P}}((1-\delta/2,0,\dots,0),(\delta/2-1,0,\dots,0)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})=o_{d}(1).

Markov’s inequality thus gives

{\mathbb{P}}(X>\delta)\leq{\mathbb{E}}X/\delta=o_{d}(1).

It follows that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
\meanw({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>(2-\delta)\cdot(1-\delta)>2-\varepsilon
, where
the last inequality holds having chosen
\delta
appropriately.

\blacksquare

3.3
The inradius

A lower bound on the inradius of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 16

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
B(\underline{0},\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.

Proof.

We simply note that if
{\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(\underline{0},1-2\varepsilon)=\emptyset
then
B(\underline{0},1/2-\varepsilon)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
, and
hence

{\mathbb{P}}\left(B(\underline{0},1/2-\varepsilon\right)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\Po((1-2\varepsilon)^{d})=0)=e^{-(1-2\varepsilon)^{d}}=1-o_{d}(1).

\blacksquare

To derive a matching upper bound we need to show that
no
ball of radius
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
is contained in
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}

(with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
).
Even if
B(\underline{0},\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon)\not\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
there are in principle still infinitely
many balls of the same radius but with a different center that need to be excluded.
In order to deal with this issue, we will make use of the following observation.

Lemma 17

For every
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
we have

{\mathbb{P}}(B(p,r)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(B(\underline{0},r)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}.

Proof.

By symmetry, we can take
p
on the positive
x_{1}
-axis without loss of generality.
We set

A:=B(\underline{0},2r),\quad A^{+}:=A\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}>0\}.

We note that
A
can be written alternatively as
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:B(\underline{0},r)\not\subseteq H_{x}\}
,
where
H_{x}
is as defined by (
3
).
Appealing to the observation (
4
), we have

{\mathbb{P}}(B(\underline{0},r)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})={\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap A=\emptyset).

We claim that if
{\mathcal{Z}}\cap A^{+}\neq\emptyset
then
B(p,r)\not\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
To see this, let
x\in A^{+}
be arbitrary.
Then there exists a
y\in B(\underline{0},r)\setminus H_{x}
.
That is,
y\in B(\underline{0},r)
and
x^{t}y>x^{t}x/2
.
But then
w:=y+p\in B(p,r)
and

x^{t}w=x^{t}y+x^{t}p>x^{t}y>x^{t}x/2,

where the first inequality holds by choice of
p
and
A^{+}
(
p
is on the positive
x_{1}
-axis and
x
’s first coordinate is
positive). This establishes that
B(p,r)\setminus H_{x}\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in A^{+}
.
In other words, we’ve proven the claim that
\{{\mathcal{Z}}\cap A^{+}\neq\emptyset\}\subseteq\{B(p,r)\not\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\}
.

This gives

{\mathbb{P}}(B(p,r)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap A^{+}=\emptyset)=e^{-\lambda\cdot\vol(A^{+})}=e^{-\lambda\cdot\vol(A)/2}=\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap A=\emptyset)}.

The lemma follows.

\blacksquare

Lemma 18

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
\inr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Again we let
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
be an appropriately chosen small constant.
As we’ve already established that
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
it suffices to show that there is no
p\in B(\underline{0},1/2)
such that
B(p,1/2+\varepsilon)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
Each such
p
lies inside some cube
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\delta,1}
, and any corner
c
of
q

must satisfy that
B(c,1/2+\varepsilon-\delta)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.

Applying the previous lemma we find

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(\inr({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>1/2+\varepsilon)&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\not\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\right)+N_{\delta,1}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(B(\underline{0},1/2+\varepsilon-\delta)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}\\
&=&o_{d}(1)+e^{O(d)}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(\underline{0},1+2\varepsilon-2\delta)=\emptyset)}\\
&=&o_{d}(1)+e^{O(d)}\cdot e^{-(1+2\varepsilon-2\delta)^{d}/2}\\
&=&o_{d}(1),\end{array}

having chosen
0<\delta<\varepsilon
appropriately.

\blacksquare

3.4
Upper bound for the width

Lemma 19

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have

\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<3/2+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

As noted before, it follows from Lemma
11
that
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)
with
probability
1-o_{d}(1)
.
Therefore we also have, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, that

{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\cap H_{z},

for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
– where
H_{z}
is as defined by (
3
) above and we use (
4
).
This gives that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})&\leq&\displaystyle\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\width(B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\cap H_{z})\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\inf_{u\in S^{d-1}}w(u,B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\cap H_{z})\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}w\left(\frac{z}{\|z\|},B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\cap H_{z}\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle 1+\varepsilon+\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|z\|/2.\end{array}

Since

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Z}}\cap B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset)=1-e^{-(1+\varepsilon)^{d}}=1-o_{d}(1),

it follows that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\|z\|<1+\varepsilon
.
In other words, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq(3/2)\cdot(1+\varepsilon)
.
Adjusting the value of
\varepsilon
, the result follows.

\blacksquare

3.5
Lower bound on the width

Recall that the
polar
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

A^{\circ}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:a^{t}y\leq 1\text{ for all }a\in A\right\}.

(For background and an overview of properties of the polar set, see e.g.
[
5
]
.)
We will find it convenient to switch attention to the polar
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ}
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the map defined by
x\mapsto(2/\|x\|^{2})x
for
x\neq\underline{0}
and

\underline{0}\mapsto\underline{0}
.
We let the point set
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined by

{\mathcal{Y}}:=f[{\mathcal{Z}}],

where
{\mathcal{Z}}
as usual is the Poisson point process of constant intensity
\lambda=1/\kappa_{d}
that we
used to define the typical cell
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
By the mapping theorem (see e.g.
[
14
]
, page 18)
{\mathcal{Y}}
is also a Poisson point
process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.
Its intensity
function can easily be worked out to be
\text{const}\cdot\|x\|^{-2d}
, but we shall not be needing that.
Convex hulls of Poisson processes with power-law intensity and their duals were studied
in
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

We will rely on the following well-known observation.
For a proof, see for instance the footnote on page 17 of
[
10
]
.

Proposition 20

Almost surely,
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ}=\conv({\mathcal{Y}})
.

Although we shall not need this fact below, let us remark that
\underline{0}
belongs to the interior
of
\conv(\mathcal{Y})
almost surely (see Corollary 4.2 on page 1040 of
[
12
]
).

We define for
u\in S^{d-1}
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\varphi(u,A):=\sup\{u^{t}a:a\in A\},\quad\psi(u,A):=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0:\lambda u\in A\}.

We remark that we can write

w(u,A)=\varphi(u,A)+\varphi(-u,A).

Another well-known, elementary observation is the following.
It for instance occurs as equation (14.42) in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 21

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact and convex with
\underline{0}\in\inter A
and
all
u\in S^{d-1}
we have

\varphi(u,A)=\frac{1}{\psi(u,A^{\circ})}.

Lemma 22

Let
0<r<2
be fixed.
With probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, any two distinct
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
with
\|Y_{1}\|>r,\|Y_{2}\|>r

satisfy

\alpha<\angle Y_{1}{\underline{0}}Y_{2}<\pi-\alpha,

where
\alpha:=\arcsin\left(r^{2}/4\right)<\pi/2
.

Proof.
Let us write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}X&:=&\displaystyle\left|\left\{(Y_{1},Y_{2})\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}:\begin{array}[]{l}\|Y_{1}\|,\|Y_{2}\|>r\text{ and }Y_{1}\neq Y_{2}\text{ and }\\
\angle Y_{1}{\underline{0}}Y_{2}\leq\alpha\text{ or }\angle Y_{1}{\underline{0}}Y_{2}\geq\pi-\alpha\end{array}\right\}\right|\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left|\left\{(Z_{1},Z_{2})\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}:\begin{array}[]{l}\|Z_{1}\|,\|Z_{2}\|<2/r\text{ and }Z_{1}\neq Z_{2}\text{ and }\\
\angle Z_{1}{\underline{0}}Z_{2}\leq\alpha\text{ or }\angle Z_{1}{\underline{0}}Z_{2}\geq\pi-\alpha\end{array}\right\}\right|.\end{array}

Using the Mecke formula (see e.g.
[
19
]
, Corollary 3.2.3), we find

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}X&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\|z_{1}\|,\|z_{2}\|<2/r\text{ and }\\
\angle z_{1}{\underline{0}}z_{2}\leq\alpha\text{ or }\angle z_{1}{\underline{0}}z_{2}\geq\pi-\alpha\end{array}\right\}}\dd z_{1}\dd z_{2}\\[21.52771pt]
&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\cdot\vol(B(\underline{0},2/r))^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\angle U_{1}\underline{0}U_{2}\leq\alpha\text{ or }\angle U_{1}\underline{0}U_{2}\geq\pi-\alpha)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&2\cdot(4/r^{2})^{d}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\angle U_{1}\underline{0}v\leq\alpha)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&o\left((4/r^{2})^{d}\cdot\sin^{d}\alpha\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&o_{d}(1),\end{array}

where
U_{1},U_{2}
denote points chosen uniformly at random on
S^{d-1}
and
v\neq\underline{0}
is an arbitrary
but fixed point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
(The second line follows by symmetry considerations; the third line uses the choice of
\lambda
and
more symmetry considerations, and;
we applied Lemma
8
in the fourth line.)

\blacksquare

Lemma 23

For every
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq B(\underline{0},2+\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

|{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus B(\underline{0},2+\varepsilon)|=|{\mathcal{Z}}\cap\clo(B(\underline{0},2/(2+\varepsilon)))|\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Po\left(\left(2/(2+\varepsilon)\right)^{d}\right),

where
\clo(.)
denotes topological closure. We see that

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{Y}}\not\subseteq B(\underline{0},2+\varepsilon))\leq\left(\frac{2}{2+\varepsilon}\right)^{d}=o_{d}(1).

\blacksquare

For the remainder of this section, we set

r:=\frac{4}{\sqrt{5}},\quad\alpha:=\arcsin(r^{2}/4).

(7)

(Note that
r<2
and
\alpha<\pi/2
.)
We separate out the following observation as a lemma.

Lemma 24

With
r,\alpha
as given by (
7
), we have
\cos(\alpha/2)=r/2
.

Proof.

By the double angle formula

4/5=r^{2}/4=\sin(\alpha)=2\sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)=2\cos(\alpha/2)\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}(\alpha/2)}.

We see that we must have either
\cos(\alpha/2)=1/\sqrt{5}
or
\cos(\alpha/2)=2/\sqrt{5}
.
We can exclude the first possibility, as
\cos(\alpha/2)=1/\sqrt{5}<1/\sqrt{2}
would imply that

\alpha/2>\pi/4
, which in turn would imply that
\alpha>\pi/2
,
but clearly
\alpha=\arcsin(4/5)\in(0,\pi/2)
.
It follows that
\cos(\alpha/2)=2/\sqrt{5}=r/2
, as stated by the lemma.

\blacksquare

Lemma 25

For all
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that the following holds.
If
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are such that

(i)

V\subseteq B(\underline{0},2+\delta)
, and;

(ii)

\|v\|>r+\varepsilon
, and;

(iii)

\angle v{\underline{0}}w\geq\alpha/2

w\in V

\|w\|>r
,

v\not\in\conv(V)
.

Proof.

Applying a suitable isometry, we can assume without loss of generality that
v=(x,0,\dots,0)
with
x>r+\varepsilon
.
We consider the projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
onto the first coordinate.

For every
w\in V
with
\|w\|\leq r
we of course have
\pi(w)\leq r
as well.
For
w\in V
with
\|w\|>r

\pi(w)=\cos\left(\angle v{\underline{0}}w\right)\cdot\|w\|\leq\cos(\alpha/2)\cdot(2+\delta)=r\cdot(1+\delta/2),

where we’ve used Lemma

and the specific choice of

.
Having chosen

appropriately, see that

\pi[\conv(V)]\subseteq(-\infty,r+\varepsilon].

So
x=\pi(v)\not\in\pi[\conv(V)]
and in particular

as well.

\blacksquare

Lemma 26

For all
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have

\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\left(\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})+\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})\right)\leq 2+r+\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary and let
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)
be as provided by Lemma
25
.
We assume that
{\mathcal{Y}}
is such that the conclusions of Lemmas
22
and

hold

1-o_{d}(1)
), with

\min(\delta,\varepsilon)
taking the role of
\varepsilon
in Lemma

and

as specified by (
7
).

Aiming for a contradiction, suppose that there is some
u\in S^{d-1}
such that both

\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})
and
\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})
are
>r+\varepsilon
.
In other words, we can find
\lambda,\mu>r+\varepsilon
such that the points

.

Adjusting the value of

\blacksquare

Corollary 27

For all
\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have

\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>\frac{4}{2+r}-\varepsilon.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
21
(and recalling that
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is almost surely a polytope with
\underline{0}\in\inter({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
),
almost surely, for every
u\in S^{d-1}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})}+\frac{1}{\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})}+\frac{2}{\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})}\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&\geq&\displaystyle\frac{2}{\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})+\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})\right)}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{4}{\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})+\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})},\end{array}

using the convexity of
x\mapsto 2/x
(or the arithmetic-harmonic inequality).
Hence

\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})=\inf_{u\in S^{d-1}}w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\geq\frac{4}{\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}\left(\psi(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})+\psi(-u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}^{\circ})\right)}.

The result thus follows from Lemma
26
, adjusting the value of
\varepsilon
.

\blacksquare

3.6
Faces of large co-dimension.

Having already obtained Lemma
11
, in order to prove Proposition
3

it suffices to show:

Lemma 28

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed, and let
k=k(d)
satisfy
d-k\to\infty
.
With probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, we have
\min\left\{\|x\|:x\in\bigcup{\mathcal{F}}_{k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\right\}>1-\varepsilon
.

The proof is essentially the same as that of Lemma

. For completeness we spell it out.

Proof.

We use the observation that if
x\in F
for some
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
then there must be

z_{1},\dots,z_{d-k}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
such that

z_{1},\dots,z_{d-k}\in\partial B(x,\|x\|)
.
If there exists any such
x\in B(\underline{0},1-\varepsilon)
then there exists a cube
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\delta}
with

x\in q\subseteq B(\underline{0},1-\varepsilon+\delta)
, where
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
is a constant, to be chosen sufficiently small.
If
c
is any corner of
q
then
B(c,1-\varepsilon+2\delta)
contains at least
d-k
points of
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
We arrive at:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\min\left\{\|x\|:x\in\bigcup{\mathcal{F}}_{k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\right\}>1-\varepsilon\right)&\leq&N_{1,\delta}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Po((1-\varepsilon+2\delta)^{d})\geq d-k\right)\\

again using (
6
) and Lemma
9
.

\blacksquare

Corollary
4
follows immediately from the following observation and Proposition
3
.

Lemma 29

If
F\subseteq B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(\underline{0},1-\varepsilon)
is convex then
\diam(F)\leq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
p,q\in F
be two arbitrary points. The midpoint
m:=(p+q)/2
also lies on
F
.
We write
\alpha:=\angle p\underline{0}m,\beta:=\angle q\underline{0}m
. Since
m
is the middle of the
line segment between
p
and
q
, we have
\alpha+\beta=\pi
. One of
\alpha,\beta
is
\geq\pi/2
.
Without loss of generality it is
\alpha
.
The cosine rule gives:

\|p\|^{2}=\|m\|^{2}+\|p-m\|^{2}-2\|m\|\|p-m\|\cos\alpha\geq\|m\|^{2}+\|p-m\|^{2}.

The assumptions on
\varepsilon,F
now give:

\|p-m\|^{2}\leq\|p\|^{2}-\|m\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{2}-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}=4\varepsilon.

Hence

\|p-q\|=2\|p-m\|\leq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Since
p,q\in F
were arbitrary, this shows that
\diam(F)\leq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

\blacksquare

3.7
Faces of constant co-dimension

In this section, we will prove Theorems
5
and
6
and Proposition
7
.
Before we can start in earnest, we need some more definitions and
preliminary observations.

For the remainder of the section, we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
For notational convenience, we’ll write:

v_{k}:=\left(\frac{(k+1)^{k+1}}{k^{k}}\right)^{1/2},\hskip 34.44434pt\ell_{k}:=\left(\frac{2(k+1)}{k}\right)^{1/2}.

As mentioned earlier,
v_{k}
equals
k!
times the volume and
\ell_{k}
equals side-length of
a regular simplex inscribed in
S^{k-1}
.
We leave the elementary considerations verifying this to the reader.
(Computing
v_{k}
for instance occurs as an exercise with a difficulty rating of 2
in Section 13 of
[
15
]
and
the value of
v_{k}
is simply stated in Section 49 of
[
8
]
, without further elaboration.
Determining
\ell_{k}
is even easier, at least in the opinion of the
authors.)

Also for notational convenience, we write for
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}):=\left|\det\left(u_{1}|\ldots|u_{k}\right)\right|,

(8)

where
(u_{1}|\ldots|u_{k})
denotes the
k\times k
matrix whose columns are
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
.
(Note we consider
k
vectors in
k
-dimensional Euclidean space.)
We denote by
P(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
the
parallelopiped spanned by
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. That is:

P(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}):=\{\lambda_{1}u_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{k}u_{k}:0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq 1,\dots,0\leq\lambda_{k}\leq 1\}.

A standard elementary fact states that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})&=&\vol(P(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}))\\
&=&k!\cdot\vol\left(\conv\left(\left\{\underline{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right\}\right)\right).\end{array}

(9)

(This last identity is for instance stated as equation (7.6) in
[
19
]
.)

For notational convenience, for
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):=\inf\left\{r>0:\text{$\exists x$ such that $\underline{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\partial B(x,r)$}\right\}.

(Note that now the ambient dimension
d
is not necessarily the same as the number of points
k
.)
We collect some observations on
\rho
needed in the sequel.

Lemma 30

(i)

If
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are linearly independent, then
0<\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})<\infty
and
there is a unique open ball
B
of radius
\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
such that
\underline{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\partial B
.

(ii)

The map
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is continuous on the
set
I_{k,d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{kd}
given by

I_{k,d}:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\text{ are linearly independent}\right\}.

Proof.

A ball that has
\underline{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
on its boundary has to be of the form
B(x,\|x\|)
with
x
satisfying

\|x\|=\|x-x_{1}\|=\dots=\|x-x_{k}\|.

Squaring and rewriting the squared norm in terms of the inner product gives

\langle x,x\rangle=\langle x-x_{i},x-x_{i}\rangle=\langle x,x\rangle-2\langle x_{i},x\rangle+\langle x_{i},x_{i}\rangle\quad(i=1,\dots,k).

Reorganising gives:

\langle x_{i},x\rangle=\|x_{i}\|^{2}/2\quad(i=1,\dots,k).

Writing
A
for the
k\times d
matrix whose
i
-th row is
x_{i}^{t}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for the
vector whose
i
-th entry equals
\|x_{i}\|^{2}/2
, we see that
x
must satisfy

Ax=b,

(10)

and any
x
satisfying this equation defines a ball of the sought form.
If
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are linearly independent then the
k\times k
matrix
AA^{t}
is non-singular.
In particular
(AA^{t})^{-1}
is well defined. So if
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are linearly independent then

x:=A^{t}(AA^{t})^{-1}b

(11)

solves (
10
).
Note that
x
is a linear combination of the columns
A^{t}
.
In other words, it lies in the linear hull
{\mathcal{L}}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\})
of
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
.

Now let
y
be an arbitrary solution of (
10
).
We have
A(y-x)=\underline{0}
, so that
\langle y-x,x_{i}\rangle=0
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
But then we also have
\langle y-x,x\rangle=0
.
Pythagoras’s theorem tells us that

\|y\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}+\|y-x\|^{2}.

So
x
is the (unique) solution with the smallest possible norm. This proves
(i)
.

To see
(ii)
we note that, using the Leibniz formula for the determinant
and Cramer’s rule for the matrix inverse, each individual coordinate of
x=x(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

can be written as
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})/Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
where
P,Q
are polynomials in the
coordinates of
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
and specifically
Q=\det(AA^{t})
.
The polynomial
Q
is non-zero on
I_{kd}
, so that
x
is a continuous function of
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
on
I_{kd}
.
So
\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\|x\|
is also continuous on
I_{k,d}
.

\blacksquare

The unique ball that has
\underline{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
on its boundary, guaranteed to exist
when
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are linearly independent,
will be denoted by
B(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.
We shall only consider
B(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
for
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
distinct elements of the Poisson point process
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
As long as
k\leq d
, almost surely any set of
k
distinct points of
{\mathcal{Z}}
is linearly independent.

Another observation we’ll use is that for
\mu>0
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we have

\rho(\mu u_{1},\dots,\mu u_{k})=\mu\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}),\quad D(\mu u_{1},\dots,\mu u_{k})=\mu^{k}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}).

(12)

We set:

{\mathcal{X}}(r):=\left\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{k}:\begin{array}[]{l}z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\text{ are distinct, and }\\
\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\leq r\end{array}\right\},\quad X(r):=\left|{\mathcal{X}}(r)\right|,

(13)

Applying the Mecke formula we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}X(r)=\lambda^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq r\}}\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{k}.

(14)

The following approximations will be reused a few times.

Lemma 31

(i)

For
r>0

\displaystyle\sqrt[d]{{\mathbb{E}}X(r)}=r^{k}v_{k}+o_{d}(1)
.

(ii)

For

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}X(r)^{2}=(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot\left({\mathbb{E}}X(r)\right)^{2}

The proof of Lemma

makes use of the following observations.

Lemma 32

(i)

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\max_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\atop\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq 1}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=v_{k}.

Moreover, the maximum is attained by
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

if and only if
\underline{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
form the vertices of a regular simplex of side-length

.

(ii)

For every

, we have

\max_{{\tiny\begin{array}[]{l}u_{1},\dots,u_{k+\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell},\\

Before we get to the proof of Lemma

, we first deal with Lemma

.
We make use of a “folklore” fact about simplices inscribed in the unit ball.

Proposition 33

Among all simplices inscribed into the unit sphere, only the regular simplices maximize the volume. More precisely, for

w_{0},\dots,w_{k}\in S^{k-1}
we have

\vol\left(\conv\left(\left\{w_{0},\dots,w_{k}\right\}\right)\right)\leq v_{k}/k!,

with equality if and only if
\|w_{i}-w_{j}\|=\ell_{k}
for all
0\leq i<j\leq k
.

This result can be found in the literature.
It is for instance derived in
[
20
]
and (independently)
[
22
]
. We believe it is significantly
older, but have not managed to find an earlier, explicit reference.
In both
[
20
]
and
[
22
]
the proposition is a consequence of a more general result.
There is a much shorter and simpler proof of Proposition

however.
See for instance the start of the proof of Lemma 13.2.2 in
[
15
]
.

Proof of Lemma
32
.

The relations (
12
) show that when computing the sought maximum we can restrict attention
to
k
-tuples
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
such that

\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=1
.

Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be such that
\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=1
.
Let
B
be the (unique) ball of radius one such that
\underline{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\partial B
and let
c
denote its center.
If we set
w_{i}=u_{i}-c
for
i=0,\dots,k
where
u_{0}:=\underline{0}
, then
w_{0},\dots,w_{k}\in S^{k-1}
.
In fact, this procedure provides a one-to-one correspondence between

(k+1)
-tuples
w_{0},\dots,w_{k}\in S^{k-1}
and
k
-tuples
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying
\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=1
.
We also have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})&=&\displaystyle k!\cdot\vol\left(\conv\left(\left\{\underline{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right\}\right)\right)\\
&=&\displaystyle k!\cdot\vol\left(\conv\left(\left\{w_{0},\dots,w_{k}\right\}\right)\right).\end{array}

Part
(i)
now follows immediately from Proposition
33
.

For the proof of part
(ii)
we use the first interpretation of the absolute value of the determinant
given in (
9

The volume of the parallelopiped

can be written as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k+\ell})&=&\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k+\ell}\dist(u_{i},{\mathcal{L}}(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}\}))\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\dist(u_{i},{\mathcal{L}}(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}\}))\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\dist(u_{k+j},{\mathcal{L}}(\{u_{k+1},\dots,u_{k+j-1}\})\right)\end{array}

where

\dist(x,A):=\inf_{y\in A}\|x-y\|
.
Writing
v_{k,\ell}
for the maximum in the LHS in Part
(ii)
of the lemma statement, we find:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}v_{k,\ell}&\leq&\displaystyle\left(\max_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell},\atop\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq 1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\dist(u_{i},{\mathcal{L}}(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}\}))\right)\cdot\left(\max_{w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell},\atop\rho(w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell})\leq 1}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\dist(w_{i},{\mathcal{L}}(\{w_{1},\dots,w_{i-1}\}))\right)\\

&=&v_{k}\cdot v_{\ell},\end{array}

where in the penultimate line we use that if we apply an orthogonal transformation
T

to

.

\blacksquare

Proof of Lemma

Applying the linear Blaschke-Petkantschin

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}X(r)&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{k}\cdot\frac{(d+1)\cdot\dots\cdot(d-k+2)}{(k+1)\cdot\dots\cdot 2}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{d+1}\cdot\dots\cdot\kappa_{d-k+2}}{\kappa_{k+1}\cdot\dots\cdot\kappa_{2}}\cdot\\

.
Now the Mecke formula gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}X_{k}&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{2k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\left\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\rho(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{2k})\leq r\right\}}\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{2k}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\lambda^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq r\}}\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{k}\right)^{2}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&{\mathbb{E}}X(r)^{2}.\end{array}

(21)

For the remaining values
0<\ell<k
a little more work is needed.
Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
31
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}X_{\ell}&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{k+\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\left\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq r,\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-\ell},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+\ell})\leq r\right\}}\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{k+\ell}\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\lambda^{k+\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\left\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq r,\rho(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+\ell})\leq r\right\}}\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{k+\ell}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle d^{O(1)}\cdot\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}}(\underline{0},2)}\dots\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}}(\underline{0},2)}1_{\{\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq r,\rho(u_{k+1},\dots,u_{k+\ell})\leq r\}}\\[8.61108pt]
&&\displaystyle\hskip 150.69397pt\cdot D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k+\ell})^{d-(k+\ell)}\dd u_{1}\dots\dd u_{k+\ell}\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle d^{O(1)}\cdot\left(\kappa_{k+\ell}2^{k+\ell}\right)^{k+\ell}\cdot\left(r^{k+\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}\right)^{d-(k+\ell)}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\left(r^{k+\ell}v_{k}v_{\ell}+o_{d}(1)\right)^{d},\end{array}

(22)

where we used Part
(ii)
of Lemma
32
in the penultimate line.

We have
v_{1}=2
and for
\ell\geq 2
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{\ell}}{v_{\ell-1}}\right)^{2}&=&\displaystyle\frac{(\ell+1)^{\ell+1}(\ell-1)^{\ell-1}}{\ell^{\ell}}=\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}}{\ell}\cdot\left(\frac{(\ell+1)(\ell-1)}{\ell^{2}}\right)^{(\ell-1)/2}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{2}+2\ell+1}{\ell}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\right)^{\ell-1}\geq(\ell+2)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\\[8.61108pt]
&\geq&\displaystyle(\ell+2)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell}\right)\geq 4\cdot(1-1/2)=2.\end{array}

So if
r>1/\sqrt{2}
then
1<rv_{1}<r^{2}v_{2}<\dots<r^{k}v_{k}
.
Thus, by Part
(i)
and (
22
),
provided
r>1/\sqrt{2}
:

{\mathbb{E}}X_{\ell}=o\left({\mathbb{E}}X(r)^{2}\right)\quad(\ell=0,\dots,k-1).

Part
(ii)
now follows by combining with (
20
) and (
21
).

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary and we let
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,k)>0
be a small constant to be chosen more precisely
in the remainder of the proof.
We note that if
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
define a
(d-k)
-face
F
then
every
x\in F
must satisfy
\|x\|\geq\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
as

B(x,\|x\|)
contains
\underline{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
on its boundary.
Since every face contains a vertex, Lemma
11
implies that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
the only
k
-tuples
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
that define a
(d-k)
-face
must satisfy
\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})<1+\delta
.
We have:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\leq X(1+\delta)\right)=1-o_{d}(1).

(23)

Provided
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,k)
was chosen sufficiently small, Markov’s inequality tells us that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(X(1+\delta)>(1+\varepsilon)^{d}v_{k}^{d}\right)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}X(1+\delta)}{(1+\varepsilon)^{d}v_{k}^{d}}=\left(\frac{(1+\delta)^{k}v_{k}+o_{d}(1)}{(1+\varepsilon)v_{k}}\right)^{d}=o_{d}(1).

Combining with (
23
), this gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt[d]{f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}>(1+\varepsilon)v_{k}\right)&\leq&\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})>X(1+\delta)\right)\\
&&\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}\left(X(1+\delta)>(1+\varepsilon)^{d}v_{k}^{d}\right)\\
&=&o_{d}(1).\end{array}

(24)

It remains to derive an upper bound on
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt[d]{f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}<(1-\varepsilon)v_{k}\right)
that tends to zero with
d
.
For this purpose, we define

Y:=\left|\left\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{k}:\begin{array}[]{l}z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\text{ are distinct, and}\\
\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\leq 1-\delta,\text{ and}\\
B(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\cap{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset\end{array}\right\}\right|.

(25)

Clearly each
k
-tuple counted by
Y
defines a
(d-k)
-face of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
As each
k
-tuple has
k!
re-orderings, we have

f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\geq\frac{1}{k!}\cdot Y.

(We also use that, almost surely,
no two distinct
k
-sets of points in
{\mathcal{Z}}
define the same face.)
Using the Mecke formula once again we have

{\mathbb{E}}Y=\lambda^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\{\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq 1-\delta\}}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(B(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\cap{\mathcal{Z}}=\emptyset)\dd x_{1}\dots\dd x_{k}.

Comparing to (
14
) we see that

e^{-(1-\delta)^{d}}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}X(1-\delta)\leq{\mathbb{E}}Y\leq{\mathbb{E}}X(1-\delta)
.
In other words

{\mathbb{E}}Y=(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot{\mathbb{E}}X(1-\delta).

(26)

Since
Y\leq X(1-\delta)
, provided
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,k)
was chosen sufficiently small,
Part
(ii)
of Lemma
31
now gives

{\mathbb{E}}Y^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}X(1-\delta)^{2}=(1+o_{d}(1))\cdot\left({\mathbb{E}}Y\right)^{2}.

By (

) and Part
(i)
of
Lemma
31
, provided
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,k)
is chosen sufficiently small, we have

k!\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{d}v_{k}^{d}<\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}Y,

for
d
sufficiently large.
We can now apply Chebyshev’s inequality to obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt[d]{f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}<(1-\varepsilon)v_{k}\right)&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(Y<k!\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{d}v_{k}^{d})\\
&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(|Y-{\mathbb{E}}Y|>\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}Y)\\
&\leq&4\Var Y/\left({\mathbb{E}}Y\right)^{2}\\
&=&o_{d}(1).\end{array}

Together with (
24
) this establishes Theorem
6
.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Recall that if
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
is defined by
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
then

\|x\|\geq\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
for all
x\in F
.
So by Lemma
11
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
any face defined by some
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
with
\rho(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\geq 1-\varepsilon
is contained in the annulus

B(\underline{0},1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(\underline{0},1-\varepsilon)
.
By Theorem
1
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
has diameter at most
2+\varepsilon
.
Applying Lemma
29
we obtain that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
:

\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}\diam(F)\leq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})+(2+\varepsilon)\cdot X(1-\varepsilon).

We note that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(X(1-\varepsilon)>\varepsilon f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(X(1-\varepsilon)>\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon/2)^{d}v_{k}^{d}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})<(1-\varepsilon/2)^{d}v_{k}^{d}\right).

The first term in the RHS is
o_{d}(1)
by Lemma
31
and Markov’s inequality.
The second term is
o_{d}(1)
as well, by Theorem
6
.
We arrive at:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}\diam(F)>4\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon(2+\varepsilon)\right)=o_{d}(1).

The theorem follows.

\blacksquare

It remains to prove Proposition

.
We need one more preparatory lemma.

Lemma 34

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
\delta>0
and
c<v_{k}

such that the following holds.
For all
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
that are not
\varepsilon
-near regular, we have

\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})>1+\delta
or
D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq c
, or both.

Proof.

Let us set

\displaystyle g(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\|u_{i}\|-\ell_{k}\right)^{2}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\left(\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|-\ell_{k}\right)^{2},

W:=\left\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:\begin{array}[]{l}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\geq v_{k}/2,\text{ and,}\\
\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq 1,\text{ and,}\\
g(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4\end{array}\right\},

and

c^{\prime}:=\max\left(\sup_{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in W}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}),\frac{v_{k}}{2}\right).

We claim that
c^{\prime}<v_{k}
. If
W=\emptyset
then this is clearly true, so suppose
W
is non-empty.
Since
D,g
are continuous, the set
W_{0}:=\{D\geq v_{k}/2,g\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4\}
is closed.
By Lemma
30
,
\rho
is continuous on
W_{0}
. So
W
is closed too.
It is also bounded, since
\rho\leq 1
implies
\|u_{1}\|,\dots,\|u_{k}\|\leq 2
.
We see that
W
is compact, and hence the supremum
c^{\prime\prime}:=\sup_{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in W}D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
is
attained by some
(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\in W
. Lemma
32
tells us that

c^{\prime\prime}<v_{k}
as
g(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\neq 0
implies that
\underline{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
is not a regular simplex
of side length
\ell_{k}
.
So
c^{\prime}=\max(v_{k}/2,c^{\prime\prime})<v_{k}
as claimed.

We now choose a small
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,k)>0
, small enough so that
(1+\delta)^{k}c^{\prime}<v_{k}
and

(\ell_{k}+\varepsilon)/(1+\delta)>\ell_{k}+\varepsilon/2
.
Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
be such that
\rho(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\leq 1+\delta
and

u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
is not
\varepsilon
-near regular, but otherwise arbitrary.
Setting
w_{i}:=u_{i}/(1+\delta)
we see that
\rho(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\leq 1
by (
12
).
Moreover, if
i,j
are such that
\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|\geq\ell_{k}+\varepsilon
then
\|w_{i}-w_{j}\|\geq\ell_{k}+\varepsilon/2
.
Similarly, if
\|u_{i}\|\geq\ell_{k}+\varepsilon
then
\|w_{i}\|\geq\ell_{k}+\varepsilon/2
.
We also have
\|w_{i}\|\leq\|u_{i}\|,\|w_{i}-w_{j}\|\leq\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|
.
It follows that
g(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\geq\varepsilon^{2}/4
.
Appealing to (
12
), we find that

D(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=(1+\delta)^{k}D(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\leq(1+\delta)^{k}c^{\prime}<v_{k}.

This shows that the lemma holds with the choice
c:=(1+\delta)^{k}c^{\prime}
.

.

, let

, we find:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}N&=&\displaystyle\lambda^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\tiny\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq 1+\delta,\text{ and }\\

4
Discussion and suggestions for further work

We have shown that the inradius, outradius, diameter and mean with of the typical Poisson-Voronoi cell,
after normalization and when the dimension tends to infinity,
all tend in probability to explicit constants. For the width we’ve shown non-matching upper and lower bounds.
As already stated in the introduction, we could not prove the following natural conjecture.

Conjecture 35

There is a constant
c
such that

\frac{\width({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})}{\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}c.

The lower bound on the width can probably be improved via a more technical variation on our argument,
but it seems unlikely to us that it will give a sharp result without significant new ideas.
The argument giving the upper bound exhibits a direction
u\in S^{d-1}
such that

w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
is small. The direction
u
is chosen perpendicular to one of the facets of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
A priori we see no reason to believe a direction of this type should minimize
w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
over all
possible directions.
Let us however point out that if
u
is the direction that minimizes
w(u,{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
then each
of the two supporting hyperplanes
perpendicular to
u
must contain some face and the sum of the dimensions of these two faces
must be at least
d-1
. (Otherwise, a small perturbation of
u
will yield a direction with even smaller
width.) Put differently, the union of the two supporting hyperplanes perpendicular to
u

contains at least
d+1
vertices of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
We have not been able to turn this observation into an argument that gives better bounds than the
ones provided by Proposition
2
, but perhaps other teams will be able to succeed in doing that.

An important ingredient in our proofs was the observation that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, all
vertices of the typical cell have approximately the same norm (namely
1\pm o_{d}(1)
under the scaling
\lambda=1/\kappa_{d}

used throughout the paper).
As can for instance be seen from Theorem 1.2 in
[
9
]
, the expected number of
vertices of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
is
\exp[(d/2)\cdot\ln d\cdot(1+o_{d}(1))]
.
It seems natural the compare the behaviour of the typical cell to that of the convex hull of the same number
of points taken i.i.d. uniformly at random on
S^{d-1}
.
The latter set-up has been studied by Bonnet and O’Reilly
[
3
]
. A relevant result in
[
3
]

is Theorem 3.4, which tells us the convex hull of
n=\exp[(d/2)\cdot\ln d\cdot(1+o_{d}(1))]

i.i.d. uniform points on
S^{d-1}
has the property that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, each of its facets has
distance
1\pm o_{d}(1)
to the origin.
This is rather different from the behaviour of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
. In the proof of Lemma 14 we have exhibited
the existence (with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
) of some facet with distance
1/2\pm o_{d}(1)
to the origin.
A straightforward adaptation of the argument shows that for all
1/2\leq r\leq 1
there exist
(with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
) some facet of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
whose distance to the origin is
r\pm o_{d}(1)
.
So, in some sense the typical Poisson-Voronoi cell is less “symmetric” or “regular” than the
convex hull of
n
i.i.d. points on the unit sphere, if
n
is taken comparable to the (expected) number of
vertices of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.

Another natural direction for further research is to try and obtain more precise results on the behaviour of the
faces of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
. E.g. give more detailed quantitative bounds on the diameter of (almost all)
k
-faces.
In the same line of questioning, one may want to determine more precise estimates on the
f_{d-k}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}})
than
Theorem
5
provides. As mentioned earlier, in a forthcoming article we plan to
provide more detailed asymptotics.

As the reader may have noticed – and if not can readily check – all the error probabilities

o_{d}(1)
obtained in our proofs were in fact exponentially small in
d
or much smaller in many cases, with
the exception of the lower bound in Proposition
2
.
We’ve always considered the probability that the considered quantity (inradius, outradius, etc.) differs
by a constant
\varepsilon>0
from the target value. A natural set of follow-up questions is to see
how fast we can let
\varepsilon
tend to zero as
d\to\infty
.
In the same vein, it would be natural to find or bound the variances or perhaps even find a normalization
that yields non-trivial limiting distributions.

Appendix A
The Hausdorff distance between
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
and any ball is large

Here we substantiate the claim from the introduction that, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, the Hausdorff distance
between the typical cell
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
and any ball is at least
a constant times the diameter of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
.
For completeness let us first recall that the
Hausdorff distance
between sets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
given by:

d_{H}(X,Y):=\max\left(\sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|,\sup_{y\in Y}\inf_{x\in X}\|x-y\|\right).

We note that the Hausdorff distance satisfies

2\cdot d_{H}(X,Y)\geq|\diam(X)-\diam(Y)|,|\width(X)-\width(Y)|.

If
Y
is a ball then
\diam(Y)=\width(Y)
.
So for
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
arbitrary
and
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
a ball we must have

\diam(X)-2\cdot d_{H}(X,Y)\leq\diam(Y)=\width(Y)\leq w(X)+2\cdot d_{H}(X,Y),

giving

d_{H}(X,Y)\geq(\diam(X)-w(X))/4.

Hence, by Theorem
1
and Proposition
2
, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
, the typical
cell
X={\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}
satisfies:

d_{H}({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}},Y)\geq(1/8-o_{d}(1))\cdot\sqrt[d]{\lambda\cdot\vol(B)}=(1/16-o_{d}(1))\cdot\diam({\mathcal{V}}_{\text{typ}}),

for every ball
Y
.
