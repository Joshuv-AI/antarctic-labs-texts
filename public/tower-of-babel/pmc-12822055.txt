The predictive value of right ventricle speckle tracking echocardiography in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ali Saad Al-shammari, Ahmed Sermed Al Sakini, Mohammed Tarek Hasan, M Rafiqul Islam, Maryam Shahzad, Razan Adib Alsawadi, Shanmukh Sai Pavan Lingamsetty, Ahmed Ibrahim, Mohammed Dheyaa Marsool, Mohammed Elgebaly, Rohit Masih

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRight ventricular (RV) dysfunction is a critical predictor of adverse outcomes in cardiac surgery patients. The assessment of right ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) echo has emerged as a promising tool for detecting early RV dysfunction. This meta-analysis evaluates the predictive value of RV GLS in these patients.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted. Introduction

Speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) for the evaluation of global and regional right and left ventricular (LV) function has developed rapidly in recent years due to technological advances, and 2D speckle‐tracking analysis is becoming an integral part of echocardiographic assessment in most clinical centers [1, 2]. STE is a novel measurement technique that enables detailed strain imaging by quantifying the shortening (negative strain) and lengthening (positive strain) of specific myocardial segments. This two-dimensional (2D) echocardiographic method relies on tracking the displacement of acoustic markers, or “speckles,” frame by frame through the myocardium [3, 4]. By assessing myocardial motion in longitudinal, radial, and circumferential dimensions, STE provides a comprehensive evaluation of ventricular function [5, 6]. STE stands out from traditional echocardiography by its resistance to artifacts caused by probe movement on the chest wall or the natural motion of the heart within the chest cavity [6, 7]. Additionally, it allows for accurate image processing in non-parallel image planes, which addresses common issues associated with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), particularly in complex post-cardiothoracic surgery (CTS) evaluations, therefore making STE a reproducible, and practical method for evaluating ventricular systolic function [6]. Furthermore, it has gained widespread acceptance as an important prognostic tool for assessing long-term cardiovascular mortality and morbidity [2]. By providing a detailed analysis of myocardial deformation, STE is capable of detecting subclinical impairments in longitudinal myocardial function and is found to play a predictive role in patients with heart failure (HF) as well as those scheduled for cardiovascular surgery [8–10]. Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is common, affecting nearly one-fourth of patients who undergo cardiac surgery, and is associated with high post-operative mortality [11]. However, RV assessment is challenging due to its anterior location and complex crescent-shaped geometry, which complicates imaging, particularly in the intraoperative setting [11]. The angle-independent capability of speckle tracking can address the limitations of traditional 2D linear measurements, such as Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion (TAPSE) and Systolic Velocity of the Tricuspid Annulus (S’), making it particularly well-suited for assessing right ventricular function in this setting [12, 13]. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the value of preoperative and postoperative STE parameters in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the role of right ventricular global longitudinal strain (RV-GLS) as a predictive factor for postoperative RV dysfunction.

Methods

We conducted the study based on the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews on Interventions. During the drafting of our manuscript, we strictly followed the recommended reporting items for the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) statement guidelines and the Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [14, 15]. (Fig. 1) Additionally, the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool was used to assess the methodological quality of the studies included [16].

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across four databases- PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science (WOS) databases to identify studies that evaluated the predictive value of RV-GLS in detecting RV dysfunction among patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The search was done from database inception until 15th November 2024. The MeSH keywords and free search terms included: (“right ventricular global longitudinal strain” OR “RV-GLS” OR “right ventricular dysfunction” OR “RV dysfunction” OR “cardiac surgery” OR “cardiac operation OR “speckle-tracking echocardiography “). No restrictions were placed on language or publication date during the initial search. Additionally, the reference lists of all eligible studies were manually reviewed to identify any further relevant articles not retrieved in the database searches. A detailed search strategy employed for each database is in Table S1.

Studies were excluded if they did not meet the PICOS criteria. Specifically, the exclusion criteria were as follows: non-human studies, conference abstracts, unpublished data, letters to the editor, case reports, narrative reviews, posters, and studies published in languages other than English. Studies focusing on non-surgical patient populations were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers (A.S and M.R.I) screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles. Full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (A.S.A). Data extraction was performed using a standardized data extraction form, which included study characteristics (author, year of publication, sample size, patient demographics), details of the intervention (RV-GLS), and reported outcomes (sensitivity, specificity, True positive rates, False positive rates, True negative rates, and False negative rates).

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool [16]. This tool evaluates the risk of bias and applicability concerns across four key domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain was rated as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” risk of bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among the reviewers.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0), specifically employing the meta, mada, and metafor packages [18].

Meta-Analysis

The diagnostic accuracy of RV GLS was evaluated by calculating the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic (SROC) curve, along with its 95% Confidence Interval (CI). The AUC was used to quantify RV-GLS’s overall discriminative ability in predicting RV dysfunction. The pooled sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were also calculated. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the I² statistic and τ². An I² value of less than 50% was considered indicative of low to moderate heterogeneity, while a value above 50% suggested substantial heterogeneity [19]. A τ² value was also reported to assess between-study variance. Publication bias was evaluated using a Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test and the LFK Index (Luis Furuya-Kanamori Index), with values close to zero indicating low bias [20, 21].

Sensitivity and specificity

The pooled sensitivity and specificity of RV-GLS were computed using a bivariate random-effects model, which accounts for the correlation between sensitivity and specificity within each study. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the heterogeneity of the findings.

Optimal cutoff analysis

A multiple-cutoff model was applied using the logistic regression framework to identify the optimal cutoff value for RV-GLS. The optimal cutoff was determined based on the balance between sensitivity and specificity, with both measures maximized at the identified threshold. The corresponding SROC curve was generated to visualize the performance of RV-GLS across different cutoff values.

Statistical significance

Statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05 for all tests. Confidence intervals were reported at the 95% level. All data and results were visualized using forest plots and SROC curves generated by the meta and mada packages.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

This meta-analysis included ten observational studies comprising a total of 583 patients who underwent various cardiac surgical procedures [22–31]. The mean age across cohorts was 37 years, and 56% were male. The included surgeries were Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) repair (n = 5), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG; n = 1), atrial septal defect (ASD) closure (n = 1), continuous-flow left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation (n = 1), and atrial switch operations (n = 2). Each study assessed the value of right ventricular global longitudinal strain (RV-GLS) for detecting postoperative right ventricular (RV) dysfunction. Cutoff thresholds varied across studies and were synthesized to determine an optimal value (Table 1).

Diagnostic performance of RV-GLS

The pooled diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) for RV-GLS was 6.34 (95% CI: 3.20–12.57), indicating over sixfold higher odds of RV dysfunction in patients with abnormal strain values. For RV free-wall longitudinal strain (FWLS), the DOR was 13.83 (95% CI: 3.16–60.51), suggesting a potentially stronger diagnostic utility (Fig. 2). Both measures showed no significant heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The Doi plot revealed no significant asymmetry, with an LFK index of 0.6, indicating a low risk of publication bias (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity and specificity analysis

The pooled sensitivity of RV-GLS was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.69–0.86; I² = 57.2%, p = 0.0219), while FWLS demonstrated a higher sensitivity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.59–0.95; I² = 0%) (Fig. 4). The pooled specificity of RV-GLS was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.71–0.84; I² = 20.8%), compared to 0.71 (95% CI: 0.56–0.82; I² = 0%) for FWLS (Fig. 5).

A multiple-threshold model identified − 15.25% as the optimal RV-GLS cutoff value. At this threshold, both sensitivity and specificity were estimated at 0.7423, reflecting a balanced diagnostic trade-off (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This meta-analysis evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of right ventricular global longitudinal strain (RV-GLS) for detecting postoperative RV dysfunction in cardiac surgery patients. Data from ten observational studies (583 patients) showed strong diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.90. Pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.79 and 0.78, respectively. The diagnostic odds ratio was 6.34, indicating that abnormal RV-GLS values were associated with a sixfold higher likelihood of RV dysfunction. These findings support RV-GLS as a reliable, non-invasive marker in this setting. Several studies in this meta-analysis linked abnormal RV-GLS to higher rates of low cardiac output, longer ICU/hospital stays, and postoperative arrhythmias. Ting et al. found RV dysfunction significantly associated with POAF, with perioperative RV-GLS via transesophageal echocardiography serving as a useful predictor [32]. Ting et al. (2019) identified preoperative RV dysfunction as an independent predictor of high postoperative VIS, with pre-incisional RV-GLS effectively forecasting adverse events [23]. Ternalce et al. also reported RV-GLS as a sensitive marker linked to postoperative mortality [33]. These findings highlight RV-GLS as a valuable tool for assessing perioperative and postoperative risk beyond RV dysfunction alone. Our findings support the predictive value of RV-GLS for detecting RV dysfunction in cardiac surgery patients, aligning with prior studies showing its superiority over conventional measures like FAC and TAPSE [34, 35]. Wang et al. (2024), Ro et al. (2023), Morris et al. (2017), and Ternacle et al. (2013) further emphasized RV-GLS’s ability to detect early RV changes often missed by TAPSE and FAC [8, 36–38]. The strong predictive value of RV-GLS is reflected in its pooled sensitivity (0.79) and specificity (0.78), consistent with studies showing higher sensitivity than TAPSE and FAC [35, 39]. This advantage stems from its assessment of longitudinal strain across the entire RV, not just the basal segment [40–44]. Ternacle et al. and Rong et al. also identified RV-GLS as a strong predictor of adverse surgical outcomes, supporting our results [8, 9]. The high diagnostic accuracy of RV-GLS holds important clinical value. In the perioperative setting, it offers a non-invasive, reliable tool for early detection of RV dysfunction, aiding timely management [45]. Given the complex hemodynamics of cardiac surgery, incorporating RV-GLS into routine echocardiography may help identify high-risk patients and improve outcomes [8, 46]. RV-GLS enables early identification of at-risk patients, allowing for targeted interventions such as preload/afterload optimization, inotropes, or mechanical support [8, 44]. Its ability to detect subtle RV changes before symptoms emerge may support proactive management and better outcomes. Recent studies support early RV assessment as a proactive strategy that improves postoperative outcomes and reduces costs, including ICU stays and resource use [47–49]. This meta-analysis has several strengths, including a comprehensive search, rigorous methodology, and adequate sample size. Robustness is supported by multiple statistical analyses and QUADAS-2 quality assessment [16]. Minimal publication bias was shown through Deeks’ funnel plot and LFK index, consistent with Deeks et al. (2019) [19, 20]. However, most included studies were observational and single-centered, raising concerns about selection bias and confounding. Despite sensitivity analyses and quality assessments, residual bias may persist [14]. Additionally, heterogeneity in populations, study designs, and RV-GLS protocols may limit generalizability [19, 40, 50, 51]. Another limitation is the variability of RV-GLS cutoff values in the literature [40]. While our meta-analysis suggests an optimal cutoff, further research is needed to standardize measurement and interpretation. speckle tracking echocardiography suffers from a number of technical limitations, such as intervendor variability, dependency on optimal image quality, sufficient frame rates (typically ≥ 40 fps), operator expertise, hemodynamic conditions, and finally the potential influence exerted by a concave-shaped chest wall conformation on myocardial strain parameters due to compressive phenomena [52–54].

Conclusion

These findings highlight the predictive value of RV GLS in detecting RV dysfunction through STE among cardiac surgery patients. The reported AUC and the favorable sensitivity and specificity suggest that RV GLS is a helpful non-invasive tool for identifying patients at risk of adverse outcomes related to RV dysfunction. We recommend conducting further large-scale randomized controlled trials and retrospective studies to test and enhance the prognostic impact of this imaging modality.
