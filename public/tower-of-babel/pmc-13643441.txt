From Migration to Control
Authors: Antonio Marzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: endovascular retrieval, failure-mode planning, foreign body retrieval, right heart, venous stent migration, venous stenting

Endovascular treatment has become an established component of contemporary venous practice. Stenting is now routinely used for venous stenosis and outflow obstruction across iliocaval, iliofemoral, renal, hepatic, and central venous territories. With broader adoption, however, rare complications inevitably become more visible. Among them, venous stent migration remains 1 of the most feared, not because it is frequent, but because when it occurs, it can rapidly transform a planned or urgent procedure procedure into a life-threatening emergency.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Zamora et al1 describe the percutaneous retrieval of a 14 × 60-mm self-expanding nitinol stent that migrated from the hepatic vein–inferior vena cava junction into the right ventricle after treatment of posthepatectomy Budd–Chiari syndrome. Their report is relevant beyond the unusual hepatic venous origin of the embolized device. It highlights a broader principle: Retrieval of migrated venous stents is not merely a matter of snaring a foreign body, but of regaining mechanical control over a large, self-expanding, radially forceful structure.

A Rare Complication With Uncertain Denominators

The true incidence of venous stent migration remains difficult to define. In the systematic review by Sayed et al,2 31 studies described 54 migration events, with the evidence base composed almost entirely of case reports and small case series. Such literature is intrinsically vulnerable to underreporting, selective publication, and heterogeneous definitions. Nevertheless, the clinical consequences are sufficiently severe to warrant dedicated attention. Once a stent reaches the right atrium, right ventricle, or pulmonary circulation, potential complications include arrhythmia, tricuspid or pulmonary valve injury, right ventricular outflow obstruction, perforation, tamponade, pulmonary embolization, and sudden death.2,3

The risk factors reported in the venous literature are diverse but converge around 1 dominant concept: inadequate anchoring. Migration has been associated with undersizing, insufficient stent length, short or unstable landing zones, dynamic nonfixed lesions, and venous segments whose diameter and geometry may change with respiration, hydration, posture, or decompression after treatment.2,3 Tortuosity and excessive angulation may plausibly worsen deliverability, coaxiality, and apposition, although their independent role in venous stent migration remains insufficiently quantified. In the arterial field, by contrast, device loss has long been linked to complex anatomy, calcification, tortuosity, angulation, and inadequate support during percutaneous coronary intervention.4 These arterial lessons should not be transferred uncritically to venous interventions, but they remind us that device stability depends on the interaction between anatomy, delivery system, landing zone, and operator strategy.

A similar caution applies to sex-related risk. Female sex is consistently associated with more challenging anatomy and less-favorable outcomes after endovascular aortic aneurysm repair, including smaller access vessels and aortic tortuosity.5 Whether comparable sex-related anatomical vulnerability influences venous stent migration is unknown. This remains an intriguing but hypothesis-generating question, particularly because several venous disorders treated with stents affect women at younger ages and may expose them to decades of device-related risk.

From Snaring to Mechanical Control

When migration occurs, management may be surgical or endovascular. In hemodynamically stable patients without perforation, severe valvular injury, or chronic device incorporation, percutaneous retrieval is increasingly favored. Gabelmann et al6 reported high success with percutaneous retrieval of lost or misplaced intravascular objects, including stents. Yet venous stents are not passive embolized catheters. Large self-expanding nitinol devices have radial force, shape memory, sharp struts, foreshortening behavior, and the ability to appose or embed against the caval wall.

This is where the case by Zamora et al1 becomes particularly instructive. The authors first retrieved the stent from the right ventricle into the inferior vena cava using smooth, coaxial traction across the tricuspid valve. The critical failure occurred afterward: Once in the cava, the stent recovered its diameter, became wall-apposed, and resisted conventional re-snaring. The Triple-Rail Snare Technique solved this problem by converting the stent from an uncontrolled wall-apposed cylinder into a linearized endovascular construct. One intraluminal stiff wire provided axial control and prevented buckling; two extraluminal soft wires acted as guide rails, allowing the snare to track around the distal struts and achieve secure capture.

This technique belongs to an evolving family of retrieval strategies. Simple snaring has been followed by dual-snare retrieval,7 balloon-assisted large-bore methods,8 and through-and-through or balloon-mounted snare techniques for large migrated venous stents.9 The common denominator is not the specific device combination, but the principle: As stent caliber and radial force increase, success depends less on capture alone and more on restoring coaxiality, limiting rotation, controlling foreshortening, and protecting cardiac structures.

Failure-Mode Planning

The most important lesson remains prevention. Careful preoperative planning, appropriate indication, intravascular ultrasound when needed, correct stent diameter and length, adequate lesion coverage, and avoidance of forced endovascular solutions in anatomically unsuitable settings are the only reliable ways to reduce the likelihood of migration.3,10 Undersizing should be considered a preventable technical error whenever stable anchoring is required. Likewise, short stents in dynamic venous segments should be viewed with caution.

However, prevention alone is not enough. Complex endovascular procedures require failure-mode planning. Before deployment, the team should already know what will be done if the device migrates; where it is likely to embolize; what access route would be used; what sheath size is required; whether snares, balloons, stiff wires, or through-and-through access may be necessary; and when surgical conversion becomes safer than continued endovascular manipulation. In right-heart retrieval, cardiac surgery should not be an abstract backup but an immediately available pathway.

Current venous guidelines appropriately emphasize diagnosis, imaging, patient selection, and treatment planning, but they provide limited procedural guidance for migrated-device retrieval.10 Given the increasing complexity and volume of venous and arterial endovascular interventions, international societies should consider dedicated registries and consensus algorithms for endovascular device migration. Standardized reporting should include device type, indication, landing zone, sizing, timing of migration, retrieval strategy, complications, and long-term sequelae.

Zamora et al1 offer more than a successful rescue. They provide an example of biomechanical reasoning under pressure. The future of migrated-device management should move from improvisation to preparedness: better planning before implantation, structured rescue during complications, and shared registries afterward. In endovascular therapy, progress is measured not only by what we can implant but also by how safely we can recover control when implants fail.
