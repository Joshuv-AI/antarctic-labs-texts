A novel case of Tropicoporus texanus and Aspergillus niger post-traumatic endophthalmitis
Authors: William C. Carter, Arjun G. Merchant, Rithwick Rajagopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo present a case of post-traumatic fungal endophthalmitis due to novel infection with Tropicoporus texanus and co-infection with Aspergillus niger.ObservationsA 16-year-old girl developed a recalcitrant fungal endophthalmitis five weeks after sustaining a corneal laceration and traumatic cataract after accidental injury by a metal welding wire. Despite a timely initial vitrectomy with injection of intravitreal vancomycin, ceftazidime, and voriconazole as well as initiation of oral fluconazole, her endophthalmitis worsened and she developed a traction retinal detachment within one week of the procedure. She required urgent repeat vitrectomy with injection of vancomycin, ceftazidime, voriconazole, and amphotericin B as well as repair of the detachment with membranectomy and silicone oil tamponade. Intraoperative examination and post-operative tissue microscopy were concerning for invasive Aspergillus infection, prompting a transition to oral voriconazole. Symptoms and visual acuity (VA) then began to improve, with VA improving from hand motions at initial presentation to 20/200 seven months after the repeat vitrectomy despite aphakic status. Fungal cultures ultimately grew Aspergillus niger and Tropicoporus texanus.Conclusions and importanceThe Tropicoporus genus has rarely been implicated in a variety of human infections including keratitis. Ophthalmologists should be aware of Tropicoporus species as a potential cause of aggressive, treatment-resistant fungal endophthalmitis.

Introduction

Endophthalmitis is a well-established complication of ocular trauma, occurring in approximately 3.4% of cases of open globe injury.1 The majority of these cases are due to bacterial infection, with fungi making up only 12.6% of post-traumatic endophthalmitis isolates.2 Candida albicans and Aspergillus species are the most common fungi involved.1,2 Tropicoporus is a genus of tropical and subtropical fungi which has never previously been reported as a cause of endophthalmitis.3 Although Aspergillus is a common genus isolated in fungal endophthalmitis, most cases are due to A. fumigatus or A. flavus, with A. niger endophthalmitis representing less than 15% of Aspergillus endophthalmitis cases.4 Here we report a case of traumatic fungal endophthalmitis secondary to Aspergillus niger and Tropicoporus texanus refractory to initial vitrectomy with intravitreal voriconazole injection.

Case report

A 16-year-old girl with no significant past medical or ocular history was referred to the emergency department at our academic center for evaluation of ruptured globe following penetrating trauma to the right eye. The day prior, she had accidentally struck herself in the eye with a piece of metallic welding wire and noticed immediate vision loss and pain. On presentation, visual acuity was hand motions in the right eye, and she was noted to have a 1.4 mm Seidel-positive superior corneal laceration as well as a cataract and anterior lens capsule violation. Computed tomography of the orbits with contrast showed no retained intraocular foreign body. She then underwent urgent repair of her corneal laceration with intravitreal injection of vancomycin (1 mg; 0.1 mL) and ceftazidime (2.25 mg; 0.1 mL). Two weeks later, the patient underwent cataract extraction with anterior vitrectomy for worsening traumatic cataract with prolapsing lens material (Fig. 1). The patient recovered well following surgery, with vision improving to count fingers without correction and 20/200 with pinoles by postoperative week one. However, when she was seen for follow-up in clinic approximately three weeks postoperatively, she complained of worsening redness, photosensitivity, and tearing and had a visual acuity of light perception only. Fibrin reaction prevented examination of the posterior segment, but ultrasonographic B-scan revealed echodense material in the vitreous cavity concerning for inflammation from a retained lens fragment versus infectious vitritis (Fig. 2A). She started a short trial of topical corticosteroids but experienced worsening pain and vision loss. Due to these concerns, an urgent vitrectomy was performed. During this procedure, the vitreous cavity was noted to be full of fluffy white material, suggestive of fungal infection. A thorough and complete vitrectomy with induction of a posterior vitreous separation was achieved, with near complete removal of all the white intravitreous material. Undiluted vitreous fluid was sent for bacterial and fungal culture and the procedure was finalized with intravitreal injection of vancomycin (1 mg; 0.05 mL), ceftazidime (2.25 mg; 0.1 mL), and voriconazole (100 mcg; 0.1 mL). At postoperative day one, B scan showed complete resolution of vitritis compared to pre-operatively (Fig. 2B). Microscopic examination of the vitreous specimen was completed, with no fungal elements seen. Still, oral fluconazole (400 mg daily) was initiated given strong clinical suspicion for fungal infection. By postoperative day six, the patient noted progressively increasing pain with examination showing hypopyon and worsening vitritis on ultrasonography (Fig. 2C). Due to the patient's interval worsening, repeat vitrectomy was performed urgently. Intra-operatively, anterior findings were notable for massive iris ischemia with spontaneous iris dehiscence (Fig. 3). The vitreous cavity was once again filled with white fluffy material. Once this material was debrided and the view to the retina was restored, the superior retina was noted to be detached with large atrophic breaks and a substantial tractional membrane originating from the iris and ciliary body. The macula itself was spared from the detachment. Scattered throughout the retina were focal areas of whitening with intraretinal hemorrhages indicative of an occlusive vasculitis (Fig. 4). The detachment was repaired by membranectomy, superior retinectomy, endolaser, and silicone oil injection tamponade. Vitreous aspirates were again sent for culture, and injections of amphotericin B (5 mcg; 0.1 mL), voriconazole (100 mcg; 0.1 mL), vancomycin (1 mg; 0.1 mL), and ceftazidime (2.25 mg; 0.1 mL) were given intravitreally. On postoperative day one, microscopic examination of the surgical specimen revealed septate hyphae consistent with Aspergillus, and oral antifungal therapy was transitioned to voriconazole 200 mg twice a day.Fig. 3A.) Discussion

Tropicoporus texanus is a mold with septate hyphae from the Hymenochaetaceae family first discovered in 2019 after causing grapevine disease at several vineyards in central Texas.5 Prior reports of T. texanus infection in humans are absent (per PubMed search for “Tropicoporus texanus” on December 21, 2025), although its relative T. tropicalis has rarely been implicated in osteomyelitis, soft tissue abscesses, brain and pulmonary nodules, and keratitis.3 Of the eight known cases of T. tropicalis infection, all were in men, and five occurred in patients with chronic granulomatous disease (CGD).3 Five patients were in the United States, with the remaining three in Spain, Guatemala, and India.3 Treatment strategies have generally involved the use of the azole class of antifungals with or without the addition of amphotericin B.3 As in this case, the aforementioned case of T. tropicalis keratitis did not respond to initial treatment—despite administration of topical voriconazole, the corneal ulcer perforated, and infection resolution was achieved only after escalation to an intensive regimen of topical amphotericin B sixteen times a day and oral ketoconazole 200 mg twice a day.6

Aspergillus species are among the most common causes of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis, particularly post-surgical endophthalmitis, and the second-most common cause of endogenous fungal endophthalmitis second only to Candida species.7,8 Treatment of Aspergillus endophthalmitis consists of oral azoles, vitrectomy, and intravitreal injection of antifungal agents.4 Infection generally follows a subacute course, with an average time from inoculation to diagnosis of approximately one month.9 A. fumigatus and A. flavus are most commonly implicated, with A. niger infection relatively rare.4

Here we report a case of post-traumatic fungal endophthalmitis exhibiting novel infection with T. texanus and co-infection with A. niger. Interestingly, cultures from the first vitrectomy only grew T. texanus while cultures from the second vitrectomy were positive for both species. However, given the high false-negative rate of ocular fungal culture, previously reported at 47%,10 this holds little clinical meaning as the first culture may have simply not picked up an active A. niger infection. Lenticular Aspergillus infection has been reported to occur following ocular trauma alongside corneal laceration and traumatic cataract.11 Given the location of the wire puncture in the anterior chamber with capsular violation, this case may represent initial lenticular fungal infection with subsequent inoculation of the vitreous, perhaps by lenticular manipulation during cataract surgery, although primary vitreous seeding is also possible. Alternatively, given the progression of clinical findings after the initial vitrectomy, elevation of the posterior hyaloid may have potentially played a role in the development of invasive vasculitis. The severe iris ischemia and complete dehiscence observed in this case is also noteworthy. Iris dehiscence is a rare complication of endophthalmitis,12 with this case being particularly remarkable for its level of severity.

This case raises the question of how a patient's history and presentation should affect antifungal selection. Guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America recommend treatment of Candida endophthalmitis with either systemic fluconazole or voriconazole, provided the organism is susceptible to azoles.13 Intravitreal amphotericin B or voriconazole is recommended when the infection involves the macula.13 Regarding Aspergillus endophthalmitis, guidelines from the same society recommend treatment with systemic voriconazole (with no option for fluconazole) and intravitreal amphotericin B or voriconazole regardless of macular involvement.14 This is because first-generation triazoles like fluconazole have poor activity against filamentous fungi, whereas voriconazole, a second-generation triazole, has strong activity against these organisms.15 Given the high proportion of post-traumatic fungal endophthalmitis cases that are due to Aspergillus or other filamentous species, initial treatment with systemic voriconazole rather than fluconazole might have been considered in this case.8,16 The choice to add amphotericin B to the intravitreal injection regimen at the second vitrectomy was driven by the resurgence of infection despite the previous intravitreal administration of voriconazole. There is only limited data regarding whether intravitreal amphotericin B or voriconazole is more effective for Aspergillus endophthalmitis—amphotericin B was the standard choice before the advent of second-generation triazoles, but a recent study in guinea pig models showed superior outcomes with intravitreal voriconazole.17,18 Use of either drug seems to be a reasonable and guideline-supported first choice for treatment.

Ultimately, microscopic examination of the specimens from the first vitrectomy was unrevealing, but similar examination following the second vitrectomy prompted an appropriate transition to systemic voriconazole. This highlights important features of microscopic specimen examination for fungi: the absence of fungi visible on microscopy does not exclude fungal infection, yet early microscopic examination is important as positive results can affect treatment decisions on a much faster timeline than slow-growing cultures. Close collaboration with microbiological laboratory specialists is important in ordering the proper studies and interpreting them accurately to optimize care of our patients.

Prophylaxis for bacterial infection with intravitreal vancomycin and a cephalosporin following open globe injuries is a common, evidence-based strategy to prevent post-traumatic endophthalmitis.19 This case highlights the deficiency of standard prophylactic regimens in preventing fungal infection of dirty wounds. Although the patient was immunocompetent, it should be noted that both classes of fungi isolated in this case have been associated with CGD, a disease involving a deficiency of NADPH oxidase leading to impaired phagocytic destruction of catalase-positive organisms. In this case the patient was not evaluated for CGD, as this X-linked disorder is highly unlikely in a female patient. Although it is impossible to separate out the relative contributions of A. niger and T. texanus to this patient's presentation, ophthalmologists should be aware of Tropicoporus species as a source of ocular infection. This case, in conjunction with the previously reported case of T. tropicalis keratitis, suggests that the genus requires aggressive treatment with local amphotericin B and systemic broad-spectrum azole antifungals.
