The use of a validated competency framework to guide the implementation of integrated care into the curriculum and accreditation of health and social care professionals in Australia
Authors: Frances Barraclough, Sabrina Pit, Jennifer Smith-Merry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImplementation of integrated care into the training of our health and social care workforce is lacking or inconsistent. This study engaged discipline leads across Australian higher education providers to ascertain whether a validated International Competency Framework for High-Quality Workforce Development in Integrated Care (the Framework) could be used to guide the implementation of integrated care into curriculum.MethodsTwelve curriculum leads participated in individual interviews across different disciplines. Interviews focused on participants’ understanding of integrated care and the potential utility of the Framework in their work. We used reflexive thematic analysis to identify the key themes across the data set.ResultsWe identified barriers to implementing the Framework including a lack of understanding of what integrated care is and how the Framework can be used. Three main areas were identified in which the Framework could be implemented. These were: national workforce reform, curriculum transformation and leadership and culture.ConclusionOur findings show training in integrated care needs to be mandated and incorporated into the training of health and social care professionals. Our Framework serves as a national standard for guiding this training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09342-0.

Background

Internationally there are serious ongoing concerns about entrenched health inequity, and the increasing needs of both an ageing population [1] and the population of people experiencing chronic and preventable conditions. These concerns, coupled with health workforce shortages worldwide [2] has led to a need to reconsider how the health and care workforce are trained to meet these changing population needs [3].

A system-wide transformation such as integrated care is needed to create a sustainable and resilient health and care workforce that collaborates across a range of health and social care sectors to address these health inequities [4].

The World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe (2013) [5] define integrated care as:The management and delivery of health services such that people receive a continuum of health promotion, health protection and disease prevention services, as well as diagnosis, treatment, long-term care, rehabilitation, and palliative care services through the different levels and sites of care within the health system and according to their needs.

The management and delivery of health services such that people receive a continuum of health promotion, health protection and disease prevention services, as well as diagnosis, treatment, long-term care, rehabilitation, and palliative care services through the different levels and sites of care within the health system and according to their needs.

However, the current training of the health and social care workforce does not enable an understanding of integrated care [6, 7]. Training for example, is undertaken in silos with a focus on the diagnosis and treatment of acute diseases and delivered through fragmented, outdated and static curricula focussing on specific elements of an issue or disease rather than overall health and wellbeing [7].

There is also a lack of emphasis within curricula on community and integrated care experiences and developing skills in primary care settings [6, 8, 9]. While many training programs teach primary care, students often find themselves in placement settings that focus on acute care [10].

Limited availability of curriculum developers and leads with knowledge of how to implement integrated care further hampering the expansion of existing competencies and curricula [11].

Other recognised barriers to implementing integrated care into health and social care worker training include a lack of faculty expertise; financial constraints; and organisational and logistical issues [12–14].

Integrated care also extends beyond health to social care, because needs are not necessarily only met by health services. It is therefore important to consider the broader ‘health and social care workforce’ which includes different kinds of clinical and non-clinical workers responsible for public and primary health and care interventions [5]. For the purposes of this study, the term social care workforce will refer to health related disciplines in Australia who receive vocational training rather than university based professional qualifications. This includes workers who provide frontline, community-based services within health and social care settings such as disability support workers and aged care workers.

For action on integrated care to be implemented throughout the health system the approach needs to be system wide. Proficiency standards and competencies need to be developed to guide this system-wide transformation to enable a shared understanding of best practice in integrated care across curriculum and policy developers; accreditation bodies; and within health and care settings.

Gonzci (1994) [15] describes competency as an integrated set of knowledge, attitudes, values and skills required for specific situations. Competency frameworks provide a clear structure and benchmarks for best practice and a basis for sharing core content across and between program providers [16].

A competency framework could be used to support a multi-level and multi-stakeholder approach to curriculum change that is required to implement integrated care across our health and social care systems.

An international competency framework for integrated care

This study explores how and why integrated care can best be incorporated into the training of our health and care workforce in Australia. It aims to provide clear guidance on ways to implement current national reforms such as the recommendations from Unleashing the Potential of our Health Workforce: Scope of Practice Review: Final Report (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care (2024) [3] and the National Medical Workforce Strategy 2021–2023 [17]. It is important that consideration is given to how integrated care is included in the implementation of these reforms.

We previously developed and validated an International Competency Framework for High-Quality Workforce Development in Integrated Care (the Framework) to guide the training and accreditation of the health and social care workforce [18]. The Framework aims to guide health and social care teaching by informing the development and revision of healthcare curricula and accreditation processes for health and social care professionals. Within this paper we focus on the Framework as a way of both testing the conditions in which the Framework can be implemented and the broader contextual factors that serve to enable or hinder the implementation of training for integrated care.

The initial development of the Framework involved two stages of work. In the first stage, a scoping review of the literature [9] was conducted to identify applicable competencies, curriculum interventions and pedagogical approaches for integrated care. This two-round modified Delphi process included 21 international participants with expertise in delivering education in integrated care in university settings. The study resulted in a final Framework consisting of eight domains and forty competency statements. This current study draws from results from the third stage of the overall research. In this stage, we conducted interviews with twelve curriculum developers to obtain feedback on the Framework to determine whether and how the Framework could be used to guide curricula for health and social care workers in Australia. This paper reports on the major themes from these interviews, which focused on the implementation context as both a facilitator and barrier to Framework implementation. We chose to focus the study on Australia to allow for a deeper understanding of how the Framework can influence curriculum design and implementation in a setting where integrated care training is not universal or comprehensive.

Methods

We chose qualitative research methods for our study to obtain deep knowledge from curriculum leads and developers about their understanding and experiences of how integrated care is and could be implemented in the training of Australia’s health and social care workforce. We wanted participants to reflect on how the Framework could be implemented within their own context and interviews therefore provided us with the ability to explore the value of the Framework in these different settings [19]. Participants were asked for their feedback and to reflect on ways to implement the Framework. We also wanted to identify any potential barriers to using the Framework. Thus, considering these goals, we aimed to answer the following research questions:

Study design

We conducted interviews with 12 curriculum leads from nine universities across five Australian states, as well as one vocational education provider. We wanted to gather rich descriptions from senior educators who lead curricula in healthcare disciplines about their experience and knowledge of integrated care and whether and how our proposed Framework could be used to guide their practice.

The data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s [19, 20] six-phase process for conducting reflexive thematic analysis. Phase 1 involved reading and re reading the transcripts and noting any early ideas and observation. Phase 2 involved identifying meaningful features across the entire data set. Phases 3 and 4 narrowed down the themes under each of the research questions and phases 5 and 6 involved weaving the data together to tell the overall story of the data. This approach allowed us to identify individual curriculum developers’ understanding of integrated care as well as patterns within and across the data. We used narrative descriptions and quotations to report on the data. Consistent with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, reflexive discussions occurred multiple times between all authors to determine the themes. These discussions focused on exploring differences in meaning and interpretation rather than trying to align coding. For example, we chose data and meanings that were most relevant to our research questions. Differences between authors interpretations were explored through reflexive discussions to make sense of the meanings within the data. The authors met frequently to decide what data was relevant to include, to identify the broader story and what to include in the final manuscript. Reflexive journalling helped to capture ideas and observations after each interview and facilitated coding and theme development.

We took guidance from the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research guidelines [21] (see Appendix A Table A1). According to this framework we described the research team and their experience, the theoretical framework and setting and how the participants were selected. However, the reporting guidelines were adapted to fit the process for reflexive thematic analysis, where reoccurring data with no meaning was not coded. Coding consensus among the researchers was also not conducted as it is not aligned with the principles of reflexive thematic analysis, which emphasizes the researcher’s active role in theme development rather than inter-coder agreement [20].

Participants

The participants were curriculum leads of university health degrees in Australia. We also chose to interview a leader in vocational training for health and social care workers because vocational training is highly connected to the frontline of healthcare work in Australia, where staff trained in the vocational system work alongside university trained staff. This vocational education lead had a statewide role and extensive vocational health workforce training and experience at a state and national level so could provide broad critique. Purposive homogeneous sampling was used to maximise efficiency in identifying individuals from different universities with a high level of knowledge about and leadership responsibilities for curriculum development in ten different health disciplines. As part of the participant recruitment procedure, we applied the following inclusion criteria:

Participants were interviewed individually to elicit information and opinions about the best ways to implement the Framework for health and social care professionals. The study used semi-structured interviews with open ended questions. An interview guide was developed for this study with input from the authors to capture key topics and issues to be discussed. See Appendix B Semi-structured interview guide (supplementary materials) for the text of the interview guide. Field notes were taken immediately after each interview to capture the immediate sense of the interview and to make a summary of any important themes or ideas that would require further investigation. The interviews lasted between 30 and 45 min.

The study received ethics approval from The University of Sydney (Project 2021/701) and data collection commenced in November 2023. Sample diversity was important for this study which we addressed, for example, by including program leads across Australian universities and varied disciplines. Potential participants for the study were identified through searching university websites. Potential interviews were then sent an email invitation. Personal bias was avoided during the selection process, for example we did not recruit friends or colleagues that we work closely with on an individual basis. All participants provided written consent. One week before their interview, participants were provided with an explanation of the goals of the research and a copy the Framework and an explanation of how it was developed.

All interviews were conducted via Zoom and recorded and transcribed with permission from the participants. The digital audio recordings were transcribed into Microsoft Word documents by the lead author. Key Themes

Research participants’ understanding of integrated care and its incorporation in curricula

The findings of this study reveal a widespread lack of clarity about integrated care. That is, the meaning of integrated care is frequently misunderstood, often being narrowly interpreted as referring to interprofessional learning where different healthcare disciplines are trained together. Participants provided examples of gaps in interprofessional learning where healthcare disciplines are mostly trained in silos. Interprofessional teamwork and collaborative practice is one of the domains in the global Framework.

Participants acknowledged that they were still training their students in silos with limited opportunities for interprofessional learning confined to the early years of training. (Nursing Lead)IPL [interprofessional learning] is not yet sorted in nursing, most of it is done in first year. It should be done across the curriculum providing the opportunity for people to come together and interact and learn in mutual environments. (Nursing Lead)

Using the framework to guide integrated care training of current and future health and care professionals

Operationalising the framework

Key themes under this section reinforced a lack of understanding of integrated care, training the health workforce disciplines in silos and not training the traditional health workforce together with the social care sector. Participants provided suggestions on how to improve the wording of the Framework. Under Domain 1, person-centred care, it was suggested to add the need to ask clients/patients/carers about whether they currently receive any funding or support packages (e.g. My Aged Care or the National Disability Insurance Scheme) and what this funding entails. Further, for Domain 4, digital skills and technology, participants suggested including providing clients with the skills to be able to engage with technology. In addition, for Domain 5, which is health promotion, it was highlighted that there is a need to upskill community service providers (e.g. management and administrative staff) in providing inclusive care and practice. For Domain 7, which is leadership, participants suggested it would be beneficial to find opportunities for patients to take leadership roles when reviewing curricula and implementing the Framework to increase participation and engagement.

Discussion

The aim of this research was to explore how curriculum leads across Australia understand integrated care, and to determine whether the integrated care framework could be used to guide the implementation of integrated care within health and social care professional curricula.

The results of this study are consistent with the literature in that they demonstrate that health and social care professionals are still being trained in silos [12, 22, 23]. Participant responses also showed that there is a lack of understanding of what integrated care is and how it is applied in practice. The health and social care workforce are not trained to understand complex referral processes to the social care sector because health professionals are still focusing on diagnosis and treatment of acute diseases [6, 12]. In addition, current training of health and social care professionals does not include how to coordinate care across sectors [24].

Another barrier to integrated care is that health educators are still focusing on interprofessional learning [25] and associated frameworks that focus on acute care environments and do not include key concepts of integrated care such as care coordination across sectors, health promotion and disease prevention [26].

There is also a lack of understanding among health educators of how training in integrated care is different from interprofessional learning (IPL) [24]. Interprofessional learning is a foundational element of integrated care that aims to build mutual respect, understanding of roles and shared decision making. It often occurs in educational settings. Whereas integrated care focuses on coordinated service delivery across systems and sectors. Following from these findings there are broad areas of reform represented below as the final themes of this study.

Curriculum transformation

This study has demonstrated the need for curriculum reform through changing the entrenched teaching practices in healthcare disciplines in university and vocational care settings. The research revealed that higher education providers, including the vocational education sector, often recruit staff with in-depth experience working in acute settings but without an understanding of the need for integrated care, what it is and how to embed it within the curriculum.

Given the number of universities and vocational training settings involved in training our health and care workforce, there needs to be a focus on curriculum transformation and reform on a broad national scale. This would be enabled by policy changes that address curricula (discussed below) but also changes to how we train existing professionals and educators.

Results showed that major barriers to implementing the Framework included old traditional thinking and teaching practices, for example in the vocational education and acute sectors, as well as ‘lack of exposure’ to best practice due to existing curricula. It is therefore important to develop consistent approaches to upskilling university and vocational curriculum leads to understand integrated care and how to incorporate the Framework into curricula [27].

There also needs to be a focus on incorporating integrated care into continuing professional development programs to upskill professionals that are responsible for providing supervision to health students on placement in different health and care settings.

Targeting leadership and culture to promote enabling environments at various levels was identified as a critical component to implementing system-level reform. For example, respondents spoke about building the confidence of higher education staff to make changes to curriculum and the importance of implementing key integrated care champions.

The Framework can be used as a resource to help key champions understand and articulate to others what integrated care looks like in practice, and to help them identify gaps in training and how integrated care can be implemented into their specific curriculum. Taking a broader approach where these champions and educators have the flexibility to adapt the content of their curricula is important for ensuring its relevance to local contexts such as rural settings [16].

Developing community leads such as community stakeholders and people with lived experience who can advocate for and champion the Framework or be involved in its establishment within curricula is another important aspect of the process of implementing the Framework.

The role of the framework in curriculum reform

Participants identified key challenges to national implementation of the Framework, including discipline diversity, varied credentialing processes, ambiguity around integrated care, differing competency levels across education tiers, and engaging the social care sector. Curriculum reform can address these challenges by co-designing the curriculum delivery with social care stakeholders, scaffolding learning across education levels, and embedding clear, practical models of integrated care [28, 29].

The Framework can be used to assist education professionals in higher education and vocational settings to understand the components of integrated care so they can be incorporated into curricula. Integrating the Framework’s competencies throughout training programs rather than teaching them in discrete content modules would better reflect the nature and philosophy of integrated care [16].

Curriculum development across health care disciplines requires intentional consideration of integrated care using multiple methods of teaching and assessment. Continuous course review and a high level of collaboration between teaching staff and faculties and commitment to the consideration of a larger picture framework needs to be considered to implement the Framework [16, 30]. Health and care education curricula must be able to incorporate emerging problems but be sufficiently scripted to ensure the foundational competencies in the global Framework are taught adequately and consistently across settings [28].

There also needs to be involvement of people with lived experience when implementing the Framework. Consumers can offer personal insights into how their care is provided, where there are gaps, and what their priorities are, including supports for carers. The Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA), [31] which regulates Australian health practitioners, and the National Boards, which regulate individual health professions, are supportive of this process and have recently published principles to strengthen the involvement of consumers in accreditation (2024) [32].

National health workforce strategy reform

Professional regulatory bodies in Australia have begun to recognise the importance of disease prevention, person centredness, care integration and providing interdisciplinary care across sectors to manage health and care. Several factors influence the health education and training landscape in Australia. AHPRA (2024) [31] creates the regulatory environment and works with various national organisations, such as the Australian Medical Council (2024) and the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia (2024) [31], to develop and approve accreditation standards. However, some professions, such as social workers and speech pathologists, are self-regulated, which introduces inconsistencies in training and practice which can limit consistency in approaches to integrated care curriculum.

These inconsistencies in regulatory approaches may also inhibit health professionals from working to their full scope of practice. For example, health professionals in Australia face different rules depending on the state or territory that can limit their ability to perform tasks and can create confusion and restrict service delivery. Individual employer policies and role design is another example of restricting practice. This issue was identified in the recent national primary care scope of practice review Unleashing the Potential of our Health Workforce [3]. This review focused on developing consistent interprofessional competencies and national standards. AHPRA (2025) also released a guideline for embedding good practice in health practitioner education [33]. This includes recommendations for broader clinical placement sites.

Our study recommends that as a foundation for this, further work needs to be done in upskilling accreditation bodies and policy makers about the importance of integrated care and understanding what integrated care means and how to implement a national approach to practice and training. There is a need for better alignment between education/training for health professionals and accreditation/ registration bodies.

The findings show that the Framework has strong relevance for the sector and can assist government and accreditation bodies to better understand the components of integrated care and to ensure they are embedded into both policy and curricula [22, 29]. Implementing a regulatory framework such as the Framework presented here will enable accreditation bodies and higher education bodies and to support the workforce changes that are needed to deliver integrated care [29].

Implications and future research directions

Our research highlights the importance of developing a national approach to creating curricula in health and social care disciplines and ensuring that all health and care professionals in Australia share a common understanding of and can practice and implement the competencies required for integrated care according to the Framework. There is a need for Australian-based guidelines on integrated care to enable specific advice to be provided to accreditation bodies such as the AHPRA Accreditation Committee; higher education curriculum review committees; and state and national government bodies such as the national boards to guide their current work. The Framework has potential to function as the basis of these guidelines and should continue to be refined and discussed to fulfil this role.

For the domains included in the Framework, further research is needed to explore a range of examples of what integrated care looks like and the agreed standards for students at novice, intermediate and entry level and at undergraduate and postgraduate levels. Another use of the Framework that participants described was using it to upskill healthcare teachers, placement supervisors, academics and industry partners. It was suggested by participants that the competencies and domains be put into a diagram for ease of translation. A draft of this diagram was developed in response and has been included in the supplementary materials (see Figure C1). The Framework itself was also changed to incorporate suggestions from the participants. For example, include as part of domain one asking if our patients or clients are currently receiving any support such as aged care funding. As part of domain four consider providing our clients with the skills to engage with technology. As part of domain five, we need to upskill community service providers in providing inclusive care and practice. Under domain seven we also need to include opportunities for consumers to take leadership roles when reviewing curricula and implementing the Framework.

The need to work more closely with the vocational training sector was emphasised in the results. There are opportunities for the Framework to be incorporated into the design and provision of education and training within the vocationally trained workforce or as part of continuing professional development for the existing health workforce. This study has demonstrated ways that the Framework can be used to build the capacity of integrated care into the training of the health and care workforce.

Strengths and limitations

As the first analysis of curriculum developer experiences of understanding and incorporating integrated care competencies in health education in Australia, this study makes a much-needed contribution to the literature on implementing workforce development in integrated care. In relation to our Framework the study provides valuable insight into the difficulties of incorporating these competencies into curricula, the complexity of implementing the Framework across multiple disciplines and suggestions for how implementation can be improved.

A strength of this study is the diversity of the participants. The sample was heterogeneous in relation to gender and in the universities and health disciplines where the participants worked, which increases the transferability of the findings. A limitation of the study was the use of the preconceived lens, that is, the Framework, which can limit participants’ responses and produce a less rich description of the participants experiences and understanding of integrated care. Other limitations of the study are that just one vocational education provider was interviewed, limiting the input from the vocational training sector. We also did not interview other sectors who are involved in integrated care such as non-health service providers such as schools and community organisations.

Conclusion

This study aimed to elicit the opinions of higher education providers on their understanding of integrated care and how to best use the global Framework to guide curriculum development. Higher education jurisdictions; curriculum developers; accreditation and registering bodies; policy developers; and national governments need explicit preparation and direction to understand the training gaps in and requirements for implementing integrated care. They need to recognise that integrated care involves a different set of knowledge, skills, and attitudes from those in which Australia’s current health and social care workforce are traditionally trained. Training in integrated care must be mandated and incorporated into the education curricula of all undergraduate, postgraduate, vocational and continuing education health and social care professionals.
