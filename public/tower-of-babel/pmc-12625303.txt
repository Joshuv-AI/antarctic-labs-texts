Preanalytical stability of plasma calcitonin gene–related peptide: clinical and research implications
Authors: Florian Frank, Michael Thomas Eller, Sarah Maier, Laura Janisch, Samuel Labrecque, Katharina Kaltseis, Karl Messlinger, Markus Reindl, Gregor Broessner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a pivotal molecule in migraine pathophysiology and a major target of current therapeutic strategies. Accurate plasma quantification is essential for biomarker research, yet concerns persist regarding its preanalytical stability.ObjectiveTo assess the stability of plasma CGRP in relation to clinically relevant delays in sample processing and different centrifugation protocols.MethodsWe conducted an analytical study including 40 healthy participants (22 females, median age 26 years), recruited at the Medical University of Innsbruck, Austria, between January and June 2023. Blood samples were obtained under standardized conditions and processed at predefined intervals (6–180 min), either immediately or following delayed centrifugation. CGRP concentrations were determined by a validated sandwich ELISA. Data analysis included repeated-measures ANOVA with corrections for age and sex, as well as paired t tests.ResultsMedian plasma CGRP concentrations demonstrated a nonsignificant numerical increase over time, with slightly higher values in women. Repeated-measures ANOVA showed no significant effect of delayed processing on CGRP levels (P = 0.326). The interaction between time and sex was statistically significant (partial η² = 0.069; P = 0.040), but separate analysis for females and males did not yield significant within-subject effects. Immediate versus delayed centrifugation (30 and 60 min) did not significantly influence CGRP concentrations (P = 0.548 and P = 0.305, respectively).ConclusionPlasma CGRP is stable over a range of preanalytical delays and under varying centrifugation conditions. These findings support robust biomarker approaches in clinical and multicenter studies by minimizing concerns about technical variability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02205-x

Introduction

The development of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeted therapies represents a paradigm shift in migraine management, with monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and gepants demonstrating a significant reduction of monthly migraine/headache days in both, clinical trials and real-world cohorts [1–5]. However, persistent variability in treatment efficacy (30–40% of patients exhibit a suboptimal reduction) underscores the unmet need for reliable biomarkers to predict therapeutic outcomes [6, 7]. Central to this challenge lies the historical instability of CGRP quantification methods, which created logistical barriers for multicenter trials and epidemiological studies to correlate plasma levels with clinical response. While early pharmacokinetic studies reported a short plasma half-life of 6.9–26.4 min through radioimmunoassays (RIA), recent methodological advances reveal substantial interindividual variability in baseline measurements, raising questions about the biological and technical factors influencing these disparities [8–10]. Our systematic evaluation of preanalytical variables addresses these technical factors analyzing the stability of CGRP in human plasma across delayed processing intervals (6–180 min), challenging prior assumptions about rapid peptide degradation derived from radioimmunoassay studies [11].

Previous studies have investigated different aspects of CGRP measurement, while Messlinger et al. performed basic methodological studies on CGRP measurement in plasma with peptidase inhibitors and Gárate et al. analyzed stability in serum over longer time periods, our study focuses specifically on the systematic investigation of CGRP stability in plasma under clinically realistic processing delays and different centrifugation protocols [12, 13].

Numerous editorials and papers have speculated on the origin of the large inter-individual differences in CGRP levels in different headache disorders [14]. The discussion as to whether these differences are mostly due to different phases of the disease, i.e. during or outside of an attack, episodic versus chronic migraine, or only due to analytical shortcomings, has enormously limited the generalizability of the results and confused the headache scientific community for decades. Furthermore, there are several commercial CGRP ELISA kits with different detection ranges leading to heterogeny results, limiting inter-study comparisons [15].

This methodological advancement enables the establishment of standardized biomarker protocols in multicenter studies and routine clinical diagnostics critical for identifying non/super-responders to CGRP therapies while addressing safety concerns linked to the peptide’s vascular activity.

Pathophysiological context of CGRP in migraine

CGRP exerts its effects in migraine through both peripheral and central mechanisms. In the trigeminovascular system, CGRP binding to G-protein coupled receptors on meningeal vessels induces potent vasodilation via cAMP-mediated smooth muscle relaxation (100-1,000 times more potent than other vasodilators) [16]. Concurrently, it may promote neurogenic inflammation through its potent vasodilatory effect and its ability to degranulate mast cells in the dura mater with subsequent substance P release, sensitizing trigeminal nociceptors [17, 18]. Central sensitization occurs via phosphorylation of postsynaptic receptors in the spinal trigeminal nucleus, amplifying transmission of nociceptive information, a process acutely reversed by triptans through suppression of neuropeptide release [19]. Paradoxically, while CGRP infusion triggers migraine-like attacks in susceptible individuals, interictal plasma levels show extreme interindividual variability across many studies (15.48-1,889.31 pg/ml), complicating biomarker interpretation [19, 20].

Implications for therapeutic monitoring and safety

The stability profile of CGRP has urgent clinical relevance given possible cardiovascular effects [21]. Though generally well-tolerated, mAbs directed against CGRP or its receptors carry risks of hypertension (e.g., erenumab: 43% incidence) and vasodilation-related flushing, likely tied to individual differences in baseline vascular CGRP (receptor) expression [16]. Reliable plasma measurements would enable longitudinal studies correlating CGRP trajectories with:

Treatment efficacy: Early identification of non-responders through static vs. declining peptide levels.

Adverse event prediction: Monitoring CGRP surges in patients with pre-existing endothelial dysfunction.

Drug selection: Stratifying patients to receptor antibodies or antagonists (erenumab or gepants) vs. ligand-binding mAbs based on vascular reactivity profiles.

By eliminating stringent preprocessing requirements, researchers can feasibly conduct multicenter trials and large-scale biomarker studies using routine clinical sampling protocols, a critical step toward personalized migraine therapeutics.

Methods

Study design

This experimental study enrolled 40 healthy volunteers recruited via social media, in-house announcements, and the outpatient clinic of the Department of Neurology, Medical University of Innsbruck. Inclusion criteria were age 18–85 years, signed informed consent, and ability to comply with study requirements. Exclusion criteria included neurological/psychiatric disorders encompassing migraine and cluster-headache (except infrequent tension-type headache), substance abuse (DSM-IV), hematological or cardiovascular disease including arterial hypertension (> 140 mmHg systolic and/or > 95 mmHg diastolic blood pressure), recent surgery/malignancy, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

To ensure scientific rigor, we aimed for a cohort composition with at least 50% female participants. This approach was motivated by two key considerations. Firstly, balanced inclusion of women is essential in studies evaluating biological markers. Secondly, since females constitute approximately two thirds of the migraine patient population, a demographic in which calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is of central pathophysiological relevance, a sufficient representation was necessary to ensure the translational validity of our findings.

The use of EDTA-K tubes without peptidase inhibitors was deliberately chosen to develop a simplified, clinically feasible protocol that meets the logistical requirements of multicenter studies. In contrast to Gárate et al. who used serum and Messlinger et al. who investigated plasma with peptidase inhibitors, our approach closes a methodological gap and directly addresses the practical challenges in clinical scenarios where complex sample processing protocols are often not feasible [12, 13].

Ethics

The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Medical University of Innsbruck (reference number [1214/2023]), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and archived for 30 years (§ 51 Abs 3 ÄrzteG; § 7 Abs 6 GBVO).

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were obtained via structured interview (age, gender, race, BMI, headache history per ICHD-3, psychiatric comorbidities, medication history).

We only included data from complete datasets and performed a listwise deletion of cases showing any missing data, resulting in exclusion of two cases.

Blood sampling

To provide an immediate further processing of drawn blood samples, blood collection was performed at our in-house laboratory. All samples were collected at the same time of day (9am to 10am) and subjects were asked to abstain from caffeine intake 12 h prior to sample collection. In total, one EDTA-K tube with 9 ml volume and two EDTA-K tubes with 2.7 ml volume were drawn per healthy volunteer out of the cubital vein. The EDTA-K tube containing 9 ml of full blood was centrifuged immediately at 4 ° Celsius and 4400 rpm/3000 g for 5 min. Separated blood-plasma was transferred to 8 aliquots (500 µl of blood-plasma per aliquot) by an Eppendorf Pipette. Each aliquot was kept unprocessed at ambient temperature until one of the eight predefined time-points (6 min, 10 min, 15 min, 20 min, 30 min, 60 min, 90 min and 3 h) were reached. Aliquots were transferred to a − 80 ° Celsius freezer until ELISA measurement starts.

The two 2.7 ml EDTA-K tubes were kept on site and not centrifuged immediately after blood collection. Full blood was unprocessed at ambient temperature until one of the two predefined time-points (30 min and 60 min) was reached. These two EDTA-K tubes were centrifuged as described above and transferred to one aliquot (500 µl of blood-plasma) per EDTA-K tube. The two 500 µl containing aliquots were frozen at − 80 ° Celsius until ELISA measurement starts.

Blood draws were performed using a 21G butterfly needle with minimal tourniquet time and immediate gentle inversion of EDTA tubes to reduce mechanical stress and red cell rupture. All plasma samples were visually inspected for hemolysis before aliquoting. Samples with visible pink or red discoloration were excluded from analysis.

CGRP quantification

CGRP concentrations were measured using a validated double-sandwich antibody ELISA for human CGRP (CGRP Enzyme Immunoassay #A05481, Bertin Bioreagent, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) [12]. The applied kit has a 100% cross-reactivity for human α- and β-CGRP enabling a comprehensive analysis of both biologically active compounds and reducing the possibility of underestimation of one isoform.

Statistical analysis

CGRP was not normally distributed and therefore underwent log-transformation to meet the assumptions of normality (transformed CGRP = log10(CGRP + 1)). Descriptive statistics for CGRP were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while the transformed values were presented as means and standard deviations (SD). To examine the potential effect of time points (between six minutes to 180 min) on CGRP, a repeated measures ANOVA (adjusted for age and sex) was performed. In case of a significant effect of the interaction of time and sex, the ANOVA was conducted separately for females and males. To evaluate the effect of immediate vs. non-immediate centrifugation, paired t-tests and repeated measures ANOVA (adjusted for age, sex and time points) were done. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS version 28.0.

Sample size calculation

To provide a representative cohort a sample size calculation was performed assuming a statistical difference (< 0.5 SD) in CGRP concentrations between samples centrifuged immediately after blood collection and samples not centrifuged immediately after blood collection. The calculation was performed using data from Messlinger et al. [12]. The mean CGRP concentration in human blood plasma is reported to be 96.94 pg/ml with a standard deviation of 89.96 pg/ml. The final number of our study cohort with significant findings defined by differences in CGRP levels immediately and not immediately centrifuged > 0.5 of SD and alpha < 0.05 resulted in 35 subjects adding 10% as possible drop-outs for a total of 38 subjects.

Role of funding source

There was no funding source for this study.

Results

Demographic data

Our study cohort comprised of 22 females (57.9%). Median age was 26 years (24–28) and mean body mass index was calculated with 22.8 (3.2). Four subjects were active smokers, three consumed nicotine via alternative methods. Active ailments were reported in five subjects comprising hypothyroidism, factor XII deficiency, psoriasis and familiar hypercholesterolemia. All subjects were regularly practicing some kind of sportive activity.

Group comparisons and analysis of outliers revealed no tendency or significant difference in CGRP baseline values (6 min) with regard to age, active smoking or subjects with active ailments.

Descriptive analyses of CGRP showed a numerical increasing trend of medians over time with higher values in females (Table 1). The same tendency can be seen when looking at the means of the transformed values (Supplement Table 1). Repeated-measures ANOVA did not indicate a significant effect of time on the transformed CGRP values (P = 0.326). Interaction of time and sex was significant with a partial eta squared of 0.069 (medium effect, P = 0.040; Table 2). Analyses of between-subject effects did not show any significant results (age: P = 0.527, sex: P = 0.089; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) concentrations in human plasma are remarkably stable across a range of preanalytical processing delays and centrifugation protocols. These findings directly address a long-standing methodological dispute in migraine biomarker research and have immediate implications for both clinical and translational studies.

Our results demonstrate that neither delayed centrifugation (up to 60 min) nor variations in sample handling (i.e. freezing delay up to 180 min) significantly affect measured plasma CGRP levels. This stability was observed consistently across sexes and age groups, with only minor interaction effects detected, which did not translate into significant within-group differences. Importantly, immediate versus delayed centrifugation (30 and 60 min) yielded comparable CGRP concentrations, further supporting the robustness of the peptide under routine clinical sampling conditions.

A relevant decay of CGRP can be largely ruled out by our data. The slight increase in CGRP values over time could be due to a continuous release of the peptide after removal from blood cells or release from protein bindings. Methodologically, it must be taken into account that despite high stability, minimal changes due to non-specific cell release or contact with sample tubes cannot be ruled out. However, the overall small and non-significant increase in the values speaks against a dominant influence of technical artifacts and underlines the stability of the peptide under practical conditions. These results suggest that biological factors, such as hormonal influences or individual differences in release dynamics, contribute by far more to the variability of CGRP values than pre-analytical aspects [22].

The stability of CGRP in plasma observed here is consistent with recent methodological studies, which have shown that deep-freezing and prompt processing with or without peptidase inhibitors preserve CGRP integrity for at least several days [12]. Previous literature has reported conflicting results regarding CGRP as a migraine biomarker, with considerable inter-study variability attributed to differences in assay methods, sample handling, and patient populations [13, 23]. Our data help resolve these discrepancies by demonstrating that technical artifacts related to preanalytical processing are unlikely to account for the wide range of baseline CGRP levels reported in the literature. Instead, biological factors, such as individual differences in CGRP receptor density, isoform expression, and blood-brain barrier permeability, are likely to underlie the observed interindividual variability [19, 23]. In comparison to the study of Gárate et al. we found a numerical increase in CGRP values over time, that cannot be easily explained [13]. Possible interpretations include a different assay sensitivity as RIAs show higher susceptibility to matrix effects and peptide fragmentation and preferentially detect shorter CGRP fragments that degrade faster. Second, our centrifugation protocol (i.e. 4 °C) minimizes platelet activation by preservation of platelet membranes. Therefore, we potentially delay the initial rapid CGRP surge during early processing and find less enzymatic degradation due to inhibited platelet proteases.

Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of assay selection, noting that different ELISA kits and the detection of specific CGRP isoforms (alpha vs. beta) can yield divergent results [13]. Our use of a sandwich ELISA validated for research laboratory use and application of standardized protocols aligns with these recommendations and strengthens the reliability of our findings. Furthermore, our results confirm that plasma CGRP remains stable for at least several hours at room temperature prior to freezing, provided that samples are processed according to established protocols [12, 24].

A significant time × sex interaction was observed, suggesting that sex may modulate CGRP dynamics over time. However, subsequent sex-stratified analyses did not reveal significant within-subject effects, and pairwise comparisons between time points remained non-significant after correction for multiple testing. Given the small-to-medium effect size and the limited power of our study to detect sex-specific effects, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to clarify whether this interaction reflects biologically relevant differences.

Our results therefore extend the current understanding of CGRP stability in blood samples in an important way. While Gárate et al. demonstrated stability of CGRP in serum for up to 24 h at 4 °C, our study demonstrates for the first time the robustness of CGRP measurements in plasma at room temperature over a clinically relevant time windows (6–180 min) and under different centrifugation protocols [13]. These findings are particularly significant as they show that CGRP measurements in plasma can be performed without specific cooling or inhibitor requirements, a key advantage for standardizing biomarker protocols in multicenter studies.

The methodological robustness demonstrated here enables the design of multicenter studies and large-scale biomarker trials without the need for highly restrictive sample handling protocols. This is particularly relevant for the identification of CGRP as a predictive biomarker for response to CGRP-targeted therapies, an area of urgent clinical need given the substantial proportion of patients who do not respond optimally to these treatments [19, 25]. Reliable CGRP measurement may also facilitate the early detection of adverse events, such as hypertension or vasodilatory impairment, in patients receiving CGRP monoclonal antibodies or gepants.

By eliminating stringent preanalytical requirements, our findings lower the logistical barriers for the implementation of CGRP monitoring in routine clinical practice and research settings. This methodological advance supports the feasibility of integrating CGRP assays into pragmatic clinical trials and real-world studies, accelerating progress toward personalized migraine therapy.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the rigorous control of preanalytical variables and the use of a well-validated assay, which together minimize technical confounders. The inclusion of both immediate and delayed processing conditions reflects real-world clinical scenarios. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study population consisted exclusively of healthy volunteers; extrapolation to migraine patients-particularly during ictal and interictal periods-should be undertaken with caution. Previous studies showed elevated CGRP levels in ictal and interictal phase of migraine patients suggesting a complex pathophysiological role of CGRP in these patients. Second, we did not directly compare different assay platforms or CGRP isoforms, which may influence absolute concentration measurements. Third, we used a validated ELISA kit from a single supplier, which may limit the comparability of our results with other studies, given the known variability across assay platforms. Fourth, long-term storage effects beyond the tested intervals were not assessed, although prior studies suggest a gradual decline in CGRP with prolonged freezing [13].

Future research should extend these findings to patient populations, including those with episodic and chronic migraines or other primary headaches, and examine the relationship between plasma CGRP dynamics and clinical outcomes. Comparative studies of assay platforms and isoform-specific measurements are warranted to further standardize CGRP quantification. Additionally, longitudinal studies assessing the predictive value of CGRP trajectories for treatment response and adverse events will be essential for establishing CGRP as a clinically actionable biomarker.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that CGRP in human plasma is highly stable under a variety of clinically relevant preanalytical conditions. This methodological advance removes a key barrier to the widespread adoption of CGRP as a biomarker in migraine and other primary headache research and clinical care, paving the way for large-scale, multicenter studies and personalized therapeutic strategies.
