Association of platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio with hyperuricemia among Qatari adults: a national primary care study
Authors: Melis Dorter, Ahmad Eid, Naela Darwish Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperuricemia is increasingly recognized as a metabolic condition associated with obesity and cardiometabolic risk, yet data from Middle Eastern populations remain limited. We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using electronic health records from Primary Health Care Corporation centers in Qatar between 1 January 2020 and 1 January 2025. Adult Qatari patients (≥ 18 years) with available same-day platelet count, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (mmol/L), and serum uric acid measurements were included. Among 60,841 participants, 7771 (12.8%) had hyperuricemia. In multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusting for major cardiometabolic factors, higher platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) was independently associated with hyperuricemia. When categorized into tertiles, participants in the highest PHR tertile had significantly greater odds of hyperuricemia compared with the lowest tertile (adjusted odds ratio 1.55, 95% confidence interval 1.44–1.66, p < 0.001), demonstrating a clear dose–response relationship. Associations remained consistent across body mass index strata and after additional adjustment for estimated glomerular filtration rate. Although the per-unit effect size was modest, the graded association across tertiles supports the relevance of PHR as a marker of inflammatory–metabolic risk. Further prospective studies are warranted to clarify its role in risk stratification and cardiometabolic assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-64730-2.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia is a common metabolic disorder characterized by elevated serum urate and underlies a spectrum of clinical conditions, including gout and cardiometabolic disease. Over the last decades, the global burden of hyperuricemia and gout has risen substantially, with prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 2.6 to 36% across different populations, and projections indicating continued increases through 20501,4. Epidemiological patterns also demonstrate marked demographic and ethnic differences in hyperuricemia prevalence, highlighting the need for population-specific analyses2.

Beyond classical complications such as gout, hyperuricemia has been associated with multiple cardiometabolic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease3. These associations are supported by biological mechanisms linking uric acid to inflammation and oxidative stress. Experimental studies have shown that soluble uric acid can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering downstream pro-inflammatory signaling pathways5. Together, these findings suggest that hyperuricemia reflects not only purine metabolism dysregulation but also systemic inflammatory activation.

Inflammation and dyslipidemia are central features of cardiometabolic disease. Platelets serve as mediators and markers of inflammatory and thrombotic processes, whereas high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (mmol/L) exerts anti-inflammatory and vasculoprotective effects6. Integrating these opposing biological pathways, the platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) has emerged as a composite biomarker reflecting inflammatory and lipid-related risk. Recent observational studies have demonstrated that elevated PHR is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including coronary artery disease severity and long-term mortality7,8, and has been proposed as a novel biomarker in heart failure populations9.

However, evidence directly examining the relationship between PHR and hyperuricemia remains limited. A recent large cross-sectional analysis based on the United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2007–2016) demonstrated a significant positive association between higher PHR and hyperuricemia, including nonlinear relationships and consistent findings across subgroups defined by age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)10. Despite these insights, evidence from Middle Eastern populations—particularly from real-world primary care cohorts—is lacking.

In Qatar, previous work from the Qatar Biobank has reported a substantial prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia among young and middle-aged adults, with associations with prediabetes, dyslipidemia, and subclinical inflammatory markers11. Nevertheless, the relationship between PHR and hyperuricemia has not been evaluated in a nationally representative primary care population in Qatar. Given the high prevalence of obesity and metabolic disorders in the region, examining this association within the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC) electronic health record system provides a unique opportunity to assess clinically accessible biomarkers in routine practice.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate the association between platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) and hyperuricemia among Qatari adults attending PHCC centers between 2020 and 2025, adjusting for cardiometabolic confounders including BMI. Additionally, we sought to evaluate potential effect modification by BMI and to assess the discriminatory performance of PHR within multivariable models. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale primary care study in the Middle East evaluating the association between PHR and hyperuricemia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using electronic health records extracted from the Primary Health Care Corporation (PHCC) system in Qatar. PHCC is the principal governmental provider of primary healthcare services in Qatar and maintains a unified electronic medical record system covering a nationally representative adult population. The study period extended from January 1, 2020 to January 1, 2025.

Study population

Adult Qatari patients aged ≥ 18 years with available laboratory measurements of platelet count, HDL cholesterol, and serum uric acid obtained on the same date were eligible for inclusion.

If multiple laboratory records were available during the study period, the earliest complete record per individual was retained to avoid duplication.

Patients with missing key variables required for PHR calculation or hyperuricemia classification were excluded from multivariable analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between PHR and hyperuricemia. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all VIF values were < 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity among included variables.

PHR was modeled both continuously and by tertiles to assess potential dose–response relationships.

Effect modification by BMI was evaluated by including an interaction term (PHR × BMI) and by conducting BMI-stratified analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was not included in the primary model due to its strong physiological relationship with serum urate levels and potential mediator role. However, a sensitivity analysis was conducted with additional adjustment for eGFR to assess the robustness of the findings.

Model performance

Discriminatory performance of the final model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

Missing data handling

Approximately 8.1% of participants had missing BMI data (4950/60,841). Complete-case analysis was used for regression models. Given the relatively low proportion of missing BMI data and the large sample size, complete-case analysis was considered appropriate and unlikely to materially influence the primary estimates. As an additional sensitivity analysis, the primary multivariable model was repeated excluding BMI, and the association between PHR and hyperuricemia remained materially unchanged. Nevertheless, potential selection bias due to missing data cannot be completely excluded.

Statistical software

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31; IBM Corp., USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Primary Health Care Corporation Institutional Review Board (PHCC, IRB), Qatar (Approval Number: BUHOOTH-D-25-00,070). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of de-identified electronic health records. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline characteristics

Participants with hyperuricemia were more likely to be male and had significantly higher body mass index (BMI) and PHR values compared with those without hyperuricemia (all p < 0.001). The prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and uric acid–lowering therapy use was significantly higher among individuals with hyperuricemia.

Although statistically significant differences in age were observed between groups, the absolute mean difference was small.

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), the association between PHR and hyperuricemia remained statistically significant and materially unchanged (adjusted OR 1.002, 95% CI 1.001–1.003, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1), indicating robustness of the findings. In an additional sensitivity analysis excluding BMI from the multivariable model, the association between PHR and hyperuricemia remained essentially unchanged (adjusted OR 1.003, p < 0.001), supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this large real-world primary care cohort of Qatari adults, hyperuricemia affected 12.8% of participants, confirming a substantial burden in a setting where obesity and cardiometabolic disease are highly prevalent. Our primary finding was that higher platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) was independently associated with higher odds of hyperuricemia after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and urate-lowering therapy. This association showed a clear dose–response pattern across PHR tertiles and remained robust in sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for eGFR. Overall model discrimination was acceptable (AUC 0.73), supporting the potential role of PHR as an adjunct marker.

Prevalence and context compared with prior literature

The observed hyperuricemia prevalence (12.8%) is broadly consistent with international heterogeneity in hyperuricemia epidemiology and rising global burden reported across regions and over time. Recent comprehensive reviews highlight that hyperuricemia prevalence varies widely (approximately 2.6–36% depending on population characteristics and definitions), reflecting differences in diet, obesity, comorbidities, and healthcare systems12. In Qatar specifically, Qatar Biobank analyses reported substantial prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia among young healthy adults and demonstrated links with metabolic risk and subclinical inflammation, supporting the local relevance of urate-related cardiometabolic risk in the region. Compared with the Qatar Biobank work (younger, healthier participants), our PHCC cohort is a broader “real-world” primary care population with a wider comorbidity spectrum, which may partly explain differences in prevalence and reinforces the value of PHCC EHR data to capture routine clinical patterns and treatment exposure11.

PHR as an inflammatory–metabolic marker and association with hyperuricemia

Our results align closely with the most directly comparable study by Yan et al. (NHANES 2007–2016), which reported a significant positive association between PHR and hyperuricemia and suggested nonlinear patterns across subgroups. We extend this evidence by demonstrating a similar independent association in a Middle Eastern primary care setting and by quantifying a graded relationship across tertiles in a very large Qatari cohort—addressing an important geographic evidence gap highlighted by the predominantly East Asian and US-based PHR literature10. Given the scale of PHR, the per-unit odds ratio appears numerically small; however, a one-standard-deviation increase in PHR (SD = 74.25) corresponds to approximately a 16% increase in odds of hyperuricemia, providing a more clinically interpretable estimate. Compared with isolated platelet count or HDL-C levels alone, PHR integrates pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways into a single readily available metric, which may enhance practical metabolic risk assessment in routine primary care settings.

Beyond hyperuricemia, PHR has been repeatedly linked to cardiometabolic and vascular outcomes, supporting its plausibility as a “summary” marker of pro-inflammatory/pro-thrombotic milieu (platelets) and reduced anti-inflammatory/vasculoprotective capacity (HDL). For example, PHR has been associated with long-term mortality in coronary artery disease cohorts, and has also been evaluated in population-based analyses of heart failure8. Similarly, PHR has been associated with NAFLD in NHANES data, reinforcing that PHR may reflect hepatic–metabolic inflammation and insulin resistance pathways that are also biologically linked with urate metabolism13.

Biological plausibility and mechanistic interpretation

Mechanistically, hyperuricemia is not only a marker of altered purine metabolism and renal urate handling, but also appears to participate in inflammatory activation. Experimental evidence shows soluble uric acid can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering downstream inflammatory signaling14. In parallel, HDL exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, while platelets contribute to vascular inflammation and thrombosis. The integration of platelet burden and HDL protection into a single ratio (PHR) therefore provides a biologically coherent marker of systemic inflammatory–metabolic risk that could plausibly correlate with hyperuricemia13. However, direct inflammatory biomarkers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were not available in this dataset, and therefore the inflammatory interpretation of PHR remains inferential.

Dose–response relationship across tertiles

A key strength of our findings is the graded association across PHR tertiles (T2 and T3 showing progressively higher odds compared with T1), supporting a dose–response pattern. This mirrors the “graded risk” concept described in prior PHR studies in other outcomes (e.g., cardiovascular mortality and stroke associations demonstrating threshold or nonlinear effects)15. A tertile-based result is also clinically interpretable, supporting potential use of PHR for stratifying risk categories in routine primary care.

BMI stratification, effect modification, and interpretation

We observed that the PHR–hyperuricemia association remained significant across BMI strata and appeared stronger in individuals with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). This is consistent with broader hyperuricemia epidemiology showing close links between obesity measures and hyperuricemia, as obesity contributes to insulin resistance, increased urate production, and reduced renal urate excretion12. The amplification of association in obesity may reflect higher inflammatory burden and more adverse lipid–platelet profiles in this subgroup, which PHR may capture more sensitively than either platelet count or HDL alone16.

From a public health perspective, this interaction supports a pragmatic message: in obesity-heavy primary care populations, PHR could help flag individuals at elevated urate-related risk who may benefit from closer metabolic assessment and preventive counseling.

ROC performance and clinical utility

The AUC of 0.73 indicates acceptable discrimination for hyperuricemia in a multivariable model that includes routinely available clinical variables. This is clinically meaningful because uric acid testing and cardiometabolic screening often occur opportunistically in primary care; a simple composite marker derived from standard tests could facilitate risk stratification. Importantly, AUC values in this range are common for multifactorial metabolic conditions where multiple pathways contribute to risk and where single biomarkers rarely produce high discrimination alone10. However, given the cross-sectional design, the ROC analysis should be interpreted as descriptive rather than diagnostic or predictive.

Renal function and sensitivity analysis: why eGFR matters

Because urate handling is strongly influenced by kidney function, adjustment for eGFR can be contentious: eGFR may function both as a confounder and as a mediator in the relationship between metabolic risk and serum urate. Therefore, we excluded it from the primary model but included it in sensitivity analyses, where results remained consistent17. This finding strengthens inference that PHR captures inflammatory–metabolic pathways relevant to hyperuricemia beyond kidney filtration alone.

Interpretation of covariates: sex, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and urate-lowering therapy

Consistent with established epidemiology, male sex and higher BMI were strongly associated with hyperuricemia12. Hypertension and dyslipidemia also showed positive associations, aligning with evidence that hyperuricemia clusters with adverse metabolic risk profiles and may predict cardiovascular events in longitudinal studies18.

Urate-lowering therapy demonstrated a large positive association with hyperuricemia status, which is expected in cross-sectional EHR-based research and is best interpreted as confounding by indication: individuals prescribed urate-lowering agents are typically those with documented hyperuricemia/gout or a history of elevated urate. Therefore, this variable captures treatment exposure and severity rather than a causal risk factor. Similar issues are well recognized in observational analyses of urate-lowering therapy and outcomes19.

Diabetes mellitus showing an inverse association: plausible explanations

In our multivariable models, diabetes mellitus showed an inverse association with hyperuricemia. This pattern has been reported in other population-based studies, where hyperuricemia prevalence can be lower among individuals with diabetes/prediabetes compared with normoglycemic groups, potentially related to glycosuria-driven uricosuria at higher glucose levels or treatment effects20. In modern clinical practice, SGLT2 inhibitors reduce serum uric acid and are associated with gout risk reduction, providing an additional plausible explanation—particularly in healthcare systems where SGLT2 use is common21. This inverse association should be interpreted with caution. Potential explanations include glycosuria-induced uricosuria and the effects of medications such as SGLT2 inhibitors, which are known to reduce serum uric acid levels. However, detailed medication data were not available, limiting further interpretation. Additional mechanisms may include altered renal tubular urate handling and insulin-related changes in urate metabolism.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study has several strengths: a large, nationally representative primary care cohort; use of routinely collected biomarkers; comprehensive adjustment for key cardiometabolic confounders; evaluation of dose–response (tertiles), effect modification (BMI interaction), discrimination (ROC), and sensitivity analysis including renal function.

Limitations

First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and temporality, and reverse causation cannot be excluded.

Second, residual confounding remains possible due to unavailable information on dietary purine intake, alcohol consumption, physical activity, socioeconomic factors, and detailed medication exposure such as diuretics, statins, and SGLT2 inhibitors22.

Third, direct inflammatory biomarkers such as hs-CRP, platelet activation markers, HDL function, and established cardiovascular disease status were not available.

Fourth, reliance on EHR diagnostic coding may introduce misclassification.

Fifth, the use of primary care EHR data may introduce selection bias because individuals undergoing laboratory testing may differ from the general population.

Finally, complete-case analysis may introduce selection bias if missingness is not random, although the proportion of missing BMI data was relatively low23.

Implications and future directions

Our findings suggest that PHR, a simple ratio derived from routine platelet count and HDL-C (mmol/L), is independently and progressively associated with hyperuricemia in a real-world Qatari primary care population, with stronger associations in obesity. If validated prospectively, PHR could serve as a practical adjunct marker to identify individuals with heightened inflammatory–metabolic risk linked to elevated urate, guiding targeted screening and early preventive interventions in primary care settings.

Conclusion

In this large, nationally representative primary care cohort of Qatari adults, higher platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) was independently and progressively associated with hyperuricemia after adjustment for major cardiometabolic risk factors. The observed dose–response relationship across tertiles and the robustness of findings after sensitivity adjustment for renal function support the relevance of PHR as a marker of inflammatory–metabolic risk linked to elevated serum urate.

Given that platelet count and HDL cholesterol (mmol/L), are routinely measured in primary care, PHR may offer a simple and accessible tool for identifying individuals at increased risk of hyperuricemia, particularly among those with obesity. Prospective studies are warranted to clarify the temporal relationship and evaluate whether PHR may serve as a potential adjunct marker for cardiometabolic risk assessment and early prevention strategies.
