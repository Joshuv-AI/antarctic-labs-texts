An evidence-fused neutrosophic framework for uncertainty-aware treatment selection
Authors: Mukesh Mann, Rakesh P. Badoni, Preeti Narooka, Pooja Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents an integrated decision-support framework for selection of healthcare treatment based on the use of Neutrosophic Logic, Dempster-Shafer Theory (DST), and Interval-Valued Fuzzy Sets (IVFS) in an extended Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) structure. Healthcare decision-making often involves incomplete clinical data, conflicting expert opinions, and context-dependent variability, which are not adequately addressed by conventional MCDM approaches. Existing methods exhibit key limitations: classical models assume precise inputs, fuzzy models capture vagueness but not indeterminacy, and existing neutrosophic approaches lack a mechanism for resolving inter-expert conflict prior to ranking. The proposed framework addresses these gaps through a sequential uncertainty-handling process in which neutrosophic logic models truth, indeterminacy, and falsity, IVFS captures variability via interval-valued representations, and DST performs evidence-theoretic fusion to reconcile conflicting expert inputs before ranking. To overcome these problems, the proposed framework is used to transform neutrosophic evaluations into fuzzy representations by using interval-valued representations, which liberalizes the treatment of uncertainty. The DST systematically integrates expert judgment to support structured evidence fusion while avoiding premature consensus. The extended TOPSIS method is subsequently applied to generate treatment rankings using belief-weighted neutrosophic scores. The framework is evaluated using sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulation, where variations in criterion weights and stochastic perturbations representing expert variability are introduced to assess ranking stability under uncertainty. The framework is applied to an illustrative numerical evaluation in a healthcare setting, where treatment options are assessed in terms of efficacy, adverse effects, cost, recovery period, and patient satisfaction. Sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulations are employed to validate the approach, demonstrating its robustness and stability under varying weighting schemes and expert opinion perturbations. Results from a synthetic illustrative scenario indicate stable and interpretable rankings under weight perturbations and stochastic noise, suggesting robustness while requiring further validation with real clinical data. The proposed approach provides an uncertainty-aware decision-support tool for clinicians, administrators, and policymakers, offering interpretable treatment prioritization while remaining scalable for complex healthcare environments.

Introduction

In the evolving global healthcare landscape, emerging economies face distinct challenges in maximizing access to medical treatment options. These countries have rapidly growing populations and face dual threats from infectious diseases, such as coronavirus and tuberculosis, and non-communicable diseases. Therefore, the healthcare system must meet increasing demand and adapt to population characteristics, reflecting demographic changes such as an aging population and the growth of middle-class incomes. Although there has been significant improvement in healthcare infrastructure, inequality in access to quality care remains a major issue. Healthcare services and specialists are available in urban areas, but rural areas generally lack the clinical expertise and healthcare infrastructure necessary for essential diagnostic services. These disparities have resulted in variations in treatment decisions, which are not always based on clinical recommendations but are often guided by costs and logistics that dictate the final choice. Moreover, due to the high proportion of out-of-pocket healthcare costs, patients are financially marginalized, making it difficult for them to base decisions on evidence, which leads to higher costs. These realities highlight the importance of robust and scalable, situation-specific decision support systems that can effectively address such complexity and provide equitable care.

To illustrate the scale of these challenges quantitatively, the World Health Organization (WHO) reports that approximately 400 million people worldwide lack access to essential health services, with the burden disproportionately falling on low- and middle-income countries. Urban tertiary hospitals may offer 15–20 treatment modalities for a single condition, whereas rural primary health centres are often limited to 2–3 options. This disparity underscores the need for decision-support frameworks capable of operating under data heterogeneity and resource asymmetry. More importantly, this structural imbalance introduces multi-source uncertainty in clinical decision-making, where treatment choices must be made under incomplete patient records, heterogeneous infrastructure constraints, and often conflicting expert recommendations, thereby necessitating models that can explicitly represent and reconcile such uncertainty.

Even in resource-limited, heterogeneous and diverse environmental conditions, medical decision-making turns out to be highly nontrivial. In comparison to developed countries, where trends in clinical practice, digitized health records and data-driven medical response systems are becoming increasingly accessible, most medical institutions in emerging economies are still operating within a fragmented, paper-based system of practice. The lack of coherent electronic health records, inconsistent documentation methods and diagnostic capabilities results in life-or-death decisions being based on incomplete or conflicting facts. There is also medical diversity within the views of healthcare providers, which further complicates the matter. A single diagnosis can receive different suggestions for the treatment from allopathic, Ayurvedic or homeopathic doctors because they operate under different concepts and possess unique evidence bases. These inconsistencies are the result of regional variations in clinical training and exposure to peer-reviewed research, which add variability to the treatment decision-making process. From a decision-theoretic perspective, such environments are characterized not only by vagueness in expert judgment but also by genuine indeterminacy arising from missing evidence and contradiction among sources, which cannot be adequately captured using single-valued or probabilistic representations alone. In such circumstances, decision-support frameworks that depend exclusively on deterministic or probabilistic modeling are inefficient. Specifically, deterministic models fail to accommodate incomplete information, while purely probabilistic approaches require prior distributions that are often unavailable or unreliable in clinical settings with sparse data. Expert-based decision models that are structurally designed to accommodate uncertainties, contradictions and incompleteness cannot simply abandon the fact that the treatments selected should still be evidence-informed and contextually appropriate.

However, Data-driven decision-support systems are becoming popular in the world’s healthcare systems to improve the selection of treatment options and patient care. Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) techniques have found a wide application where structured medical databases exist. The methods enable ordering treatment options based on various factors such as efficacy, safety, cost, and recovery time. The classical methods like the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)1 and Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS)2 have been helpful in a well-organized environment, where the input data is consistent, complete, and quantitatively defined. Nevertheless, the methods usually assume expert assessments to be precise and in mutual agreement. This assumption implies an implicit consensus structure, which rarely holds in clinical practice where multiple experts may provide divergent, and sometimes contradictory, recommendations based on incomplete or context-dependent evidence. This assumption makes them inapplicable in areas where patient history is incomplete, there exists a contradiction among different experts in the field, and there are differences in the treatment results in various clinical settings. Consequently, classical MCDM approaches tend to produce overly confident rankings that do not reflect the underlying uncertainty or disagreement, limiting their reliability in high-stakes medical decisions. Thus, traditional MCDM techniques are not always adequate in providing evidence-based medical decision-making in a real-life scenario of ambiguity and uncertainty.

In order to check these limitations, the researchers looked at fuzzy extensions of the classic MCDM models. The fuzzy AHP and fuzzy TOPSIS techniques3 incorporate the concept of a membership function to incorporate vague judgments into expertise. These approaches extend classical models by allowing partial membership, thereby capturing imprecision in linguistic assessments; however, they fundamentally assume that uncertainty can be represented along a single continuum of vagueness. Although these models provide some flexibility by allowing leeway for the need for unique input values, they still do not effectively capture the innermost indeterminacy when medical opinion varies, when patient behavior is unpredictable, or when input data are unavailable. In particular, situations involving missing clinical evidence or conflicting expert opinions introduce epistemic uncertainty that cannot be reduced to fuzzy membership functions alone, as such representations do not distinguish between ambiguity and genuine lack of knowledge. Dilemmas consisting of multiple specialists with conflicting treatment recommendations or unclear side effects, combined with some forms of risk factors in individual patients, are examples of cases where both classical and fuzzy models are inadequate. As a result, fuzzy-based MCDM methods may yield averaged or smoothed evaluations that mask critical disagreements, thereby reducing their discriminative power in high-stakes medical decisions.

In response to this shortcoming, more recent studies have resorted to the use of neutrosophic logic, which is more holistic. Compared to fuzzy logic, neutrosophic logic treats truth, falsity, and indeterminacy as different dimensions of evaluation, which is more suitable for representing the uncertainty of such complex medical contexts. This explicit separation enables the modeling of indeterminacy as an independent component, allowing the framework to retain unresolved or conflicting evidence rather than forcing premature consensus.

Neutrosophic MCDM4 has now emerged as a promising extension of conventional decision support approaches, providing a structured mechanism for introducing and managing uncertainty, inconsistency, and incomplete information in healthcare decision-making. By representing expert assessments as triplets (T, I, F), neutrosophic models allow simultaneous quantification of support, contradiction, and indeterminacy, thereby offering a richer descriptive structure than single-valued or interval-only approaches. Unlike classical and fuzzy methods which make use of single-valued or probabilistic measurements, neutrosophic logic uses three values, truth, indeterminacy and falsity, to provide a more detailed analysis on expert judgment. The latter is highly applicable to clinical situations where conflicting opinions are given due to insufficient diagnostic data or in the case of inconsistent interpretation. For example, one physician may advocate one treatment option, while the other objects due to a lack of patient-specific data. In such cases, the indeterminacy component captures the unresolved uncertainty arising from incomplete diagnostics, while the falsity component reflects explicit disagreement, enabling a more faithful representation of the underlying decision context. And to reconcile such antagonistic perspectives, neutrosophic models provide a mathematically sound method to consider such aspects in the decision-making process.

However, existing neutrosophic MCDM frameworks primarily focus on representation and aggregation, and often lack a principled mechanism for resolving conflicts among multiple expert sources prior to ranking, which can lead to propagation of inconsistency into the final decision scores. Moreover, due to the multiplicity of stakeholders in healthcare, i.e., physicians representing different medical traditions, policy analysts, and administrators, there is an increasing need for models that are able to synthesize different expert opinions into coherent treatment protocols. This highlights a critical methodological gap: while neutrosophic logic captures indeterminacy, it does not inherently provide an evidence-theoretic framework to combine heterogeneous expert beliefs, thereby motivating the need for integrating complementary mechanisms for conflict resolution and uncertainty propagation.

Against this conceptual background, recent advances in decision theory have combined the neutrosophic MCDM with other uncertainty management tools, including Dempster-Shafer Theory (DST)5,6 and Interval-Valued Fuzzy Sets (IVFS)7. While neutrosophic logic provides a structured representation of truth, indeterminacy, and falsity, it does not inherently resolve conflicts arising from multiple expert opinions, particularly when evidence is contradictory or incomplete. In DST, it is possible to add the different belief functions of different experts to conflicting assessments, so that probability masses can be attached to sets of hypotheses, as opposed to individual outcomes. This enables a principled mechanism for aggregating heterogeneous expert beliefs, where the conflict coefficient explicitly quantifies disagreement and redistributes belief mass in a mathematically consistent manner, thereby preventing arbitrary averaging of contradictory inputs. This proves particularly useful in cases where no single expert has complete knowledge of the clinical condition. IVFS also increases flexibility in modeling by providing a way to express uncertainty in terms of intervals (rather than point estimates), which allows for a wider range of expert opinion.

In particular, the interval representation allows the model to capture variability and hesitation in expert judgments without forcing precise numerical commitments, thereby preserving the range of plausible clinical outcomes. Combining these methods is a strong basis for evaluating medical decisions made in situations where the data was fragmented and there was disagreement between specialists. Importantly, this combination is not merely additive but sequentially complementary: neutrosophic logic captures multi-dimensional uncertainty, IVFS expands this representation into bounded intervals to reflect variability, and DST subsequently resolves inter-expert conflicts before the final ranking stage, ensuring that uncertainty is both represented and reconciled in a coherent decision pipeline.

This study proposes an evidence-fused neutrosophic framework that incorporates Neutrosophic Sets, IVFS, DST, and an extended TOPSIS formulation to support treatment decision-making under uncertainty. The integration of Neutrosophic Logic, Interval-Valued Fuzzy Sets, and Dempster-Shafer Theory within an extended TOPSIS framework yields more stable and discriminative treatment rankings under uncertainty by explicitly modeling indeterminacy, capturing variability through interval bounds, and resolving conflicting expert evidence prior to ranking.

To provide both practical feasibility and theoretical strength, the model incorporated Monte Carlo simulations and sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity analysis is performed to see the sensitivity of the decision criteria weight to treatment rankings and Monte Carlo simulations are used to create some stochastic perturbations to account for the uncertainty of the real world. Specifically, sensitivity analysis examines how variations in criterion weights influence ranking consistency, while Monte Carlo simulations model randomness associated with expert variability and incomplete data, thereby providing statistical evidence of robustness rather than relying solely on deterministic outcomes. Research motivation and requirement

Medical decision-making is in itself a complex phenomenon that typically involves considering several potentially competing factors, such as treatment effectiveness, possible side effects, costs (both personal and economic) that patients may incur, patient preferences and the prognosis of recovery. These complexities are even more pronounced in macro-heterogeneous healthcare systems due to systemic inequalities regarding resources, infrastructure and expertise. Quite often, patient records are disassociated or incomplete; there is a lack of consensus among experts and institutions have widely varying capabilities, which makes it difficult to determine the best treatment options. Such environments are inherently characterized by multi-source uncertainty, where incomplete clinical data, heterogeneous infrastructure, and conflicting expert opinions coexist, necessitating decision models that can simultaneously represent variability, disagreement, and indeterminacy in a unified framework.

MCDM-based decision support systems have been implemented in various institutions to assist healthcare professionals. However, the decision inputs in traditional MCDM models are accurate and comprehensive, which is unlikely to be true in real clinical practice. Clinicians use qualitative terms to make judgments such as “moderately effective” or “low risk” without providing specific numerical assessments. The traditional methods, such as AHP and TOPSIS, may require perfect pairwise comparisons; however, real-world clinical cases may have incomplete information and conflicting expert views. This mismatch between model assumptions and real-world data characteristics leads to structurally biased decision outcomes, where uncertainty and disagreement are either ignored or implicitly averaged, reducing the reliability of the resulting rankings. This contradiction highlights the fact that more sophisticated models were necessary to make decisions that need to allow for ambiguity, information lack and even contradictory judgments.

Fuzzy MCDM models have also been proposed to reflect the imprecision of individual expert judgments, but these models tend to be limited when there is an extreme lack of data or disagreement among experts. While fuzzy representations effectively model vagueness through graded membership, they do not explicitly distinguish between ambiguity arising from linguistic imprecision and indeterminacy caused by missing or conflicting evidence, thereby limiting their applicability in complex clinical environments. Using oncology as an example of a representative case, a choice between surgery, chemotherapy, or hormone therapy is based on more than just an assessment of survival rates. It involves a rational evaluation of adverse effects, financial implications and all case-specific conditions. In addition, treatment recommendations often vary between urban tertiary hospitals and rural clinics, due to differences in available resources. Such contextual variability further amplifies the decision complexity, as the same treatment option may yield different outcomes depending on infrastructure availability and patient-specific conditions, which cannot be adequately captured through static or single-valued evaluation models. Traditional MCDM approaches cannot capture this level of contextual variation.

A similarly important challenge occurs when incomplete or non-standardized patient data are available, for example, in emergency-related scenarios such as acute stroke management, which require timely action. In such circumstances, clinicians often do not have much diagnostic information to base a quick decision. This introduces a critical form of epistemic uncertainty, where decisions must be made under severe information scarcity, making it essential to preserve indeterminacy rather than forcing premature numerical precision. Most of the current modeling tools are unsuitable for these high-stakes, data-poor scenarios because they operate on the assumption that the available information is complete and consistent.

Given these difficulties, the present manuscript addresses a novel approach termed as evidence-fused neutrosophic framework, integrating neutrosophic logic, DST and IVFS. The necessity of this integration arises from complementary functional roles: neutrosophic logic enables explicit modeling of truth, falsity, and indeterminacy; IVFS extends this representation by capturing variability through interval bounds; and DST provides a principled mechanism for aggregating and reconciling conflicting expert evidence prior to decision ranking. This integrated clinical decision model framework was proposed to better address the real-world uncertainties, ambiguities and information void about using such a medical decision-making environment. Unlike standalone approaches, the proposed framework follows a sequential uncertainty-handling pipeline, ensuring that uncertainty is first represented, then expanded to capture variability, and finally reconciled before ranking, thereby reducing the propagation of inconsistency into final decisions.

To leverage the advancements of such progress in these emerging theories, we aim to develop a model that provides mathematically sound yet tractable evaluations for most healthcare scenarios with limited resources. The primary objective of this work is to bridge the gap between theoretical decision models and the complex realities of clinical practice. The framework further assumes independent expert contributions, normalized criterion weights, and a controlled uncertainty parameter governing interval expansion, enabling systematic analysis of model behavior under varying decision conditions. The framework proposed in this study is designed to facilitate more robust, contextually grounded, and equitable decisions despite incomplete data and the often divergent opinions of experts. By providing interpretable and stability-aware treatment rankings, the proposed approach supports clinicians, administrators, and policymakers in making informed decisions under uncertainty, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare environments.

The model suggested uses Neutrosophic Logic to explicitly model uncertainty in terms of three different elements, namely truth, indeterminacy, and falsity. This triadic representation ensures that uncertainty due to incomplete knowledge is not collapsed into single-valued approximations, thereby preserving critical information for subsequent decision stages. Such division allows a more subtle and lifelike representation of a medical situation, especially one that is ambiguous and contradictory. To avoid contradictory and even diverse views, DST is introduced to integrate clinical beliefs based on the evidence theory and keep its integrity and richness of domain knowledge for the model. By explicitly modeling the degree of conflict among expert opinions, DST enables consistent aggregation without suppressing disagreement, which is essential in multi-expert healthcare environments. IVFS also contributes to such a structure by way of utilizing interval-valued terms over precise scalar approximation to model variation in treatment effectiveness, side-effects and risk. This allows the framework to capture variability across patient populations and clinical conditions, ensuring that uncertainty bounds are retained rather than simplified prematurely.

To apply such an evaluation for further medical decisions, the conventional TOPSIS is improved by adding neutrosophic distance measures and by using belief-based dynamic weighing of decision criteria. This enhancement ensures that ranking is performed on uncertainty-aware representations, where both the magnitude and reliability of expert evaluations influence the final ordering. It is this enhancement that ensures final treatment rankings that are resistant to epistemic uncertainty and are sensitive to shifts in clinical priorities as well as new patient data. In order to ensure the stability of the model under expert’s and incomplete data type judgment, extensive sensitivity analysis and a Monte Carlo simulation are conducted to verify that the model is stable. Sensitivity analysis systematically examines the impact of variations in criterion weights, while Monte Carlo simulation models stochastic fluctuations in expert inputs, thereby validating the consistency and robustness of the proposed framework under realistic uncertainty conditions.

The evidence-fused neutrosophic framework is able to overcome salient drawbacks found in existing healthcare decision-support models and methods via introducing fine-grained uncertainty handling, a formal conflict-resolution mechanism, adaptive ranking mechanisms and flexible data representation. By integrating these components within a unified decision pipeline, the framework ensures that uncertainty is represented, expanded, and reconciled prior to ranking, thereby improving both interpretability and reliability of treatment decisions. It proposes a rigorously designed, operationally feasible, and uncertainty-tolerant approach that can be applied to facilitate high-stakes decision-making in difficult, heterogeneous and resource-limited clinical settings.

Background and related work

Healthcare decision making inherently involves many, often opposing, criteria including efficacy of a treatment, side effects, patient satisfaction, cost, and recovery period. In order to deal systematically with this complexity, Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) frameworks have been widely used in clinical as well as health policy settings11. Classical MCDM methods, e.g. Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)1, TOPSIS11, VIKOR2 and PROMETHEE12 have been extensively used for various purposes such as resource allocation, treatment prioritization and selection of healthcare technology. While effective in deterministic environments, these models encounter difficulties to treat uncertainty, vagueness and conflicting opinions of experts which are prevalent in real-world healthcare environments11,12.

AHP, for example, is known for its structured pairwise method and it has been broadly applied in areas as varied as diagnostic aid systems13 and choice of therapy for chronic diseases14. Nevertheless, the classical AHP framework is based on the presumption of precise and internally consistent input judgments, which is often unrealistic in medical environments characterized by subjective and imprecise assessments15. To mitigate these limitations, Fuzzy AHP (FAHP) was introduced16, allowing the use of linguistic variables and fuzzy numbers to more accurately reflect expert preferences under uncertainty17. FAHP has shown considerable promise in applications such as hospital performance evaluation18 and oncology-related decision-making19. Despite its improved handling of vagueness, FAHP does not provide a systematic mechanism to reconcile divergent or conflicting expert opinions, which constrains its suitability for consensus-driven healthcare scenarios20.

A fundamental limitation of both classical and fuzzy AHP-based approaches lies in their reliance on consistency-preserving aggregation mechanisms, which implicitly assume that expert judgments can be reconciled into a coherent preference structure. In heterogeneous clinical environments, however, expert opinions may be inherently conflicting rather than merely imprecise, and such models lack a clear distinction between uncertainty due to vagueness and disagreement arising from contradictory evidence. As a result, structural inconsistencies are often absorbed into aggregated representations, potentially masking clinically relevant divergences.

TOPSIS is another prominent ranking-based technique that evaluates alternatives based on their proximity to ideal and anti-ideal solutions21. Fuzzy TOPSIS is its extension that incorporates fuzzy set theory to account for imprecise and linguistic data. It has been successfully used in applications such as COVID-19 treatment evaluation22 and medical risk assessment5. However, Fuzzy TOPSIS is limited by its inability to effectively combine conflicting expert opinions or adequately account for missing data23. From a structural perspective, TOPSIS-based methods, including their fuzzy extensions, rely on distance-based ranking principles that assume well-defined and comparable evaluation spaces. In the presence of indeterminacy and incomplete evidence, however, the notion of a fixed ideal solution becomes ambiguous, as the underlying data may not support a single coherent reference point. Consequently, such methods may yield stable rankings under deterministic assumptions but fail to adequately capture uncertainty propagation in real clinical decision contexts.

To overcome these limitations, the concept of interval-valued fuzzy sets (IVFS) was introduced in MCDM models, where uncertain evaluations are represented as intervals rather than precise values24. IVFS has demonstrated effectiveness in applications such as personalized care planning25 and surgical strategy selection26, owing to its flexibility in modeling variability. However, IVFS lacks an explicit belief fusion mechanism, which limits its effectiveness in scenarios involving strongly divergent expert opinions27. Although IVFS enhances uncertainty representation through interval bounds, it remains primarily descriptive rather than inferential, as it does not provide a mechanism to reconcile conflicting interval estimates from multiple experts. When interval overlaps arise from disagreement rather than genuine uncertainty, the absence of conflict resolution may lead to inflated uncertainty ranges, thereby reducing the discriminative capability of subsequent ranking procedures.

A key advantage of IVFS over traditional fuzzy sets lies in its ability to represent expert hesitancy through bounded intervals rather than single-point estimates. In contrast to Pythagorean fuzzy sets, which relax membership constraints at the cost of interpretability, IVFS maintains a clearer semantic relationship between interval bounds and expert confidence levels.

As an alternative to conventional probabilistic reasoning, Dempster–Shafer Theory (DST) provides a mathematical framework for representing uncertainty through belief functions and allows for the combination of evidence from multiple sources5. DST has been effectively applied to diagnostic modeling28, clinical risk assessment9, and evidence synthesis under conflict29. Despite these strengths, DST does not inherently support preference ranking among alternatives1. While hybrid models combining DST with AHP, TOPSIS, and fuzzy techniques have emerged2, they typically lack explicit mechanisms for handling indeterminacy, constituting a critical dimension of real-world medical uncertainty30.

Neutrosophic logic, initiated at first by Smarandache31, has become a powerful decision making paradigm that has the power of both capturing uncertainty and indeterminacy. Different from traditional fuzzy and intuitionistic fuzzy systems, Neutrosophic logic allows one extra dimension, called indeterminacy, which makes it possible to model more refined incomplete, inconsistent and contradictory information31. The use of Neutrosophic AHP and TOPSIS in decision making in healthcare has demonstrated great potential, especially in expert driven areas where there is a high degree of ambiguity and disagreement32,33. Despite these improvements of Neutrosophic MCDM frameworks, the overall implementation of these for clinical practice is still at the beginning with most of the implementations being experimental and context-specific6.

Fuzzy logic, on the other hand, has matured into a foundational tool for modeling vagueness and linguistic ambiguity. Among recent advancements, picture fuzzy sets (PFSs)34 have emerged as an effective means to capture complex human sentiments by incorporating degrees of affirmation, rejection, neutrality, and refusal. Unlike classical fuzzy sets (FS) and intuitionistic fuzzy sets (IFSs), PFSs introduce a refusal degree derived from the interplay of positive, neutral, and negative evaluations. This added representation layer has proven useful in sentiment analysis and medical decision support domains, where responses are often non-binary and emotionally complex35.

The usefulness of PFSs in representing hesitant or ambiguous opinion of experts has led to their increases in use for use in high uncertainty environments. Bani-Doumi et al.35 proposed a PFS-based decision framework designed to capture multi-dimensional expert feedback from complex areas, such as healthcare diagnosis. Their model provided a better degree of interpretability in scenarios where interpretations include a combination of acceptance, neutrality, rejection, and refusal judgments. In a related research, He and Wang36 also applied PFS theory to the problem of sentiment classification, and their results showed that PFS theory can effectively distinguish overlapping emotional states, which cannot be done in traditional sentiment modeling methods. These contributions highlight the relevance of PFSs in situations where patient preference modeling and assessments by stakeholders are of interest.

Building on these developments, Yager37 introduced Pythagorean fuzzy sets (PyFS), which extend the expressive capability of fuzzy logic, where the sum of the squares of the membership and non-membership degrees lies between 0 and 1. This increase in flexibility makes PyFS a well-suited application in areas such as psychological assessments and wellness monitoring where subjective accounts tend to take center stage in the decision landscape.

Recent developments have expanded the MCDM framework through the application of neutrosophic approaches. Bolturk and Kahraman38 proposed an interval-valued neutrosophic AHP (IVN-AHP) using cosine similarity measures which enhance the reliability of similarity assessments between elements with vague or overlapping inputs. Ye et al.39 introduced a trigonometrically weighted single-valued neutrosophic (SVN)-based MCDM model where decision makers can quantify the different degrees of varying confidence and imprecision in their judgments. Giri et al.40 extended the classical TOPSIS by incorporating single-valued neutrosophic hesitant fuzzy sets (SVNHFS) to integrate multiple ratings for each criterion. This approach is particularly useful in medical decision-making, where stakeholders’ opinions often differ and achieving consensus remains a challenge.

Real-world implementations of such evolving models of decision-making have started to show their applicability in the domains of healthcare and allied fields. Zhang et al.41 evaluated the performance of insurance companies with a hybrid TODIM-BSC technique combining Pythagorean fuzzy AHP and multi-attributive ideal-real comparative analysis with the final consensus obtained using the Borda count method. The approach they offer is a transferable framework for evaluating hospitals and treatment alternatives. Vafadarnikjoo et al.42 improved the classical best-worst method (BWM) by introducing neutrosophic logic, in addition to the different levels of decision-maker confidence, to increase the reliability of the expert-based prioritizations.

In a related attempt, Mahapatra et al.43 proposed a neutrosophic correlation-based TOPSIS model for selecting health insurance providers, which resulted in more robust rankings in a complex and imprecise data environment than conventional distance-based methods. Recent work has further explored the application of advanced MCDM frameworks in healthcare supply chain contexts. For instance, Momena et al.44 proposed a probabilistic linguistic term set (PLTS)-based MCDM model to address adaptability and operational challenges in sustainable medicinal supply chains. Their approach incorporates uncertainty modeling and sensitivity analysis to enhance decision robustness in complex healthcare logistics environments.

Several other frameworks have increased the applicability of neutrosophic and fuzzy MCDM models. These are based on the Delphi-AHP model developed based on a neutrosophic environment45, the Interval-Valued Neutrosophic AHP based on cosine similarity38, and the single-valued neutrosophic hesitant fuzzy TOPSIS (SVNHFS-TOPSIS) method proposed by Giri et al.40. To enhance the TOPSIS method, the Hamming distance has been incorporated46, divergence measures have been introduced47, and sophisticated aggregation measures have been added48. Applications in health insurance decision-making and clinical prioritization have benefited from these methods, as shown in the works of Sekar et al.49 and Masoomi et al.50, which used fuzzy and neutrosophic enhanced BWM to optimize healthcare choices. In addition, recent research has found applications of integrated Neutrosophic-Dempster-Shafer frameworks in areas such as remote medical care51 and smart device selection in healthcare systems52, demonstrating the versatility of these models in dealing with conflicting and uncertain evidence.

Altogether, these contributions signify a paradigm shift for the state of the art of MCDM models from traditional approaches to more succinct and adaptive techniques. The basic models, such as AHP and TOPSIS, are still highly effective but are supplemented or ousted by advanced models that they address, especially, vagueness, inconsistency and indeterminacy problems. Picture fuzzy sets, neutral sets, fuzzy logic theory and the SVNHFS hybrid MCDM technique are providing empirical findings for the case of a requirement of quality expert modeling.

Integrated possibilistic approaches for sustainable production53,54 and intuitionistic fuzzy DEA cross-efficiency methodologies for group decision-making55 have further advanced multi-criteria evaluation under uncertainty. Interval-valued hesitant fuzzy group outranking approaches56 have provided useful benchmarks for comparative evaluation of MCDM frameworks. Recent applications of fuzzy MCDM frameworks in healthcare settings57–59 provide direct methodological precedents for the present integration of neutrosophic and evidence-theoretic methods. Applications of hybrid uncertainty frameworks in logistics and operational research60–62 further demonstrate the cross-disciplinary applicability of the techniques employed in this study. Probabilistic approaches for alternative selection using correlation-based neutrosophic TOPSIS63 and weighted correlation methods under neutrosophic fuzziness64 have further expanded the theoretical foundation of neutrosophic ranking methods.

Despite these advances, existing MCDM frameworks address uncertainty, variability, and expert conflict in a disjoint manner, where individual methods capture only partial aspects of the decision problem. Collectively, this leads to a fragmented treatment of uncertainty, in which representation, aggregation, and ranking are handled in isolation rather than as a coherent process. Neutrosophic approaches effectively represent three-dimensional uncertainty (truth, indeterminacy, falsity), yet lack a structured mechanism for resolving inter-expert disagreement prior to ranking. Interval-valued fuzzy models capture uncertainty bounds but do not incorporate belief-based conflict reconciliation, while Dempster–Shafer theory provides a principled framework for evidence aggregation but remains oriented toward belief combination rather than decision prioritization. In isolation, DST does not impose an ordering structure over alternatives, and without a complementary representation of indeterminacy, it cannot fully capture the multi-dimensional nature of clinical uncertainty. Consequently, these approaches result in partial or sequential information loss, where uncertainty is either simplified, averaged, or insufficiently resolved before the final ranking stage, thereby affecting both interpretability and robustness of treatment decisions.

This motivates the need for a unified decision-support framework in which uncertainty representation, conflict resolution, and ranking are integrated in a sequentially coherent and non-redundant manner. The proposed framework addresses this gap by combining neutrosophic modeling, interval-valued uncertainty expansion, and DST-based evidence fusion within a single pipeline, ensuring that conflict resolution precedes uncertainty propagation and final decision ranking.

The synthesis from this literature review demonstrates a methodological progression, consistent with the increasing complexity of healthcare decision making. Certainly, with the tendency that both multidimensional aspects and conflicting expert views have been regarded as standard, researchers are adapting fuzzy logic, DST and neutrosophic reasoning in the MCDM structures to improve interpretability, accuracy and robustness. Methodological framework

The proposed evidence-fused neutrosophic framework combines single-valued and interval-valued neutrosophic sets, interval-valued fuzzy sets (IVFS), Dempster–Shafer theory (DST), and an extended version of the TOPSIS method to support robust and transparent decision-making in healthcare systems under uncertainty. This section describes the mathematical formulation and logical structure of the methodology with special emphasis on modeling indeterminacy and inconsistency in treatment selection.

The proposed framework is grounded in the following foundational model assumptions: (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {A}_1$$\end{document}) evaluation criteria are considered mutually independent to avoid redundancy; (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {A}_2$$\end{document}) expert weights are initially treated as uniform to ensure unbiased aggregation; (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {A}_3$$\end{document}) the decision environment remains static during the evaluation interval; (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {A}_4$$\end{document}) the uncertainty parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} is applied uniformly across the criteria space; and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {A}_5$$\end{document}) assessments are represented as neutrosophic triplets (T, I, F) within the standard bounds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0 \le T+I+F \le 3$$\end{document}. The robustness of the model regarding these assumptions is rigorously tested through sensitivity analysis in “Results and sensitivity evaluation”.

Problem definition

In the field of uncertainty-aware clinical decision-making, treatment selection represents a complex multi-criteria evaluation task that is often influenced by incomplete data, conflicting expert opinions, and heterogeneous medical evidence. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A = \{A_1, A_2, \dots , A_m\}$$\end{document} denote a finite set of candidate treatment alternatives, where each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} corresponds to a therapeutic option such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted drug therapy. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C = \{C_1, C_2, \dots , C_n\}$$\end{document} denote the set of evaluation criteria pertinent to the clinical decision context. These factors may encompass overall treatment effectiveness, adverse side effects, costs to the patient and society, patient-reported outcomes, and time to recovery after therapy. The five evaluation criteria, namely effectiveness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document}), side effects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document}), cost (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_3$$\end{document}), recovery time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_4$$\end{document}), and patient satisfaction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_5$$\end{document}), were selected based on the WHO essential medicines model list67 and systematic reviews of treatment comparison studies9.

Due to the vagueness and subjectivity within clinical assessment, traditional MCDM models based on deterministic inputs cannot quantify the ambiguity or uncertainty involved in expert opinions and patient differences. In order to overcome these problems, a new structure is proposed, which is called the neutrosophic decision matrix (NDM). Here, each treatment option \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} is assessed against the criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} with a neutrosophic triplet making up the decision matrix:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D = \left[ (T_{ij}, I_{ij}, F_{ij}) \right] _{m \times n}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{ij}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{ij}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ij}$$\end{document}, respectively, represent the degrees of truth, indeterminacy, and falsity in the evaluation of alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} under criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}, as assigned by medical domain experts. This tripartite representation enables the explicit modeling of the confounding nature of medical judgments, which incorporate elements of confidence, hesitation, and contradiction. The neutrosophic set theoretic framework follows the foundational definitions of Smarandache31, with BPA formulations based on standard DST as established by Shafer14. The neutrosophic constraint is enforced as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} 0 \le T_{ij} + I_{ij} + F_{ij} \le 3, \quad \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots , m\}; \quad j \in \{1, 2, \dots , n\}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This requirement is essential to constrain the sum of truth, indeterminacy and falsity degrees within the boundaries of the neutrosophic logic to preserve the mathematical interpretable validity of the decision matrix. Such a structure assumes that combined knowledge may be represented in a subtler fashion, which is instrumental to model other complexities underlying medical decisions and serves as the basis for extending DST by integrating other advanced aggregation operators.

In real clinical practice, the treatment options that are favored by experts are more or less subjective decisions, largely biased by personal feeling and differences in medical expertise for different cases. However, these examinations could be imperfect or even inconsistent or conflicting and may vary depending on the range of clinical experience, institutional protocols and scientific evidence. To systematically address this heterogeneity, the proposed analytical framework is built around an aggregation engine that organizes multiple expert opinions to represent diverse perspectives in a compatible and balanced manner.

Let p denote the number of medical experts involved in the evaluation process. Each expert provides a neutrosophic evaluation of treatment alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} under criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}, represented by the triplet \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(T_{ij}^{(k)},~I_{ij}^{(k)},~F_{ij}^{(k)})$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = 1, 2, \ldots , p$$\end{document}. The aggregated neutrosophic value for each alternative–criterion pair is computed as the arithmetic mean of the expert inputs:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} (T_{ij}, I_{ij}, F_{ij}) = \frac{1}{p} \sum _{k=1}^{p} (T_{ij}^{(k)}, I_{ij}^{(k)}, F_{ij}^{(k)}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This averaging method helps to ensure equal participation of all experts to avoid potentially biased opinions and allows a consensus-based modeling of the clinical uncertainty. The resultant aggregated NDM provides a structured input for the weighted evaluation process.

Given that the various evaluation criteria can have different levels of importance within the domain of medical decision-making, a weight vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W = \{w_1, w_2, \dots , w_n\}$$\end{document} is introduced to reflect the relative importance of each evaluation criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}. The weights satisfy the following normalization condition:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^{n} w_j = 1, \quad w_j \ge 0, \quad \forall j \in \{1, 2, \dots , n\}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The criterion weight vector W can be determined through: (i) subjective methods such as Delphi-based expert consensus; (ii) objective methods such as entropy weighting; or (iii) hybrid methodologies. In “ Illustrative numerical evaluation of the evidence-fused neutrosophic framework for healthcare treatment selection”, an equal-weight scheme (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_j = 0.2$$\end{document}) is adopted to isolate the effect of the proposed aggregation methodology. The sensitivity analysis in “Results and sensitivity evaluation” subsequently varies the weight vector across five distinct clinical priority configurations. Incorporating these weights results in the weighted neutrosophic decision matrix (WNDM):5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D^W = \left[ (T'_{ij}, I'_{ij}, F'_{ij}) \right] _{m \times n}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Each element of the WNDM is calculated by scaling the aggregated neutrosophic values by the corresponding criterion weight:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T'_{ij} = w_j \cdot T_{ij}, \quad I'_{ij} = w_j \cdot I_{ij}, \quad F'_{ij} = w_j \cdot F_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This weighting procedure allows the decision model to account for clinical judgment under uncertainty while maintaining the hierarchical importance of evaluation criteria. For instance, while treatment effectiveness may be the primary factor, other considerations, including economic affordability and estimated recovery time, are incorporated with a specific degree of influence, thereby facilitating patient-centered and evidence-informed clinical decision-making.

As an example to show how the design of this modified method can be performed in the healthcare field, consider a case where a decision maker has three treatment alternatives, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_3$$\end{document}. Medical alternatives are then evaluated according to five crucial and clinical nutritional criteria: efficiency of the treatment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document}, intensity of the side effects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document}, economic cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_3$$\end{document}, estimated recovery time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_4$$\end{document} and patient satisfaction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_5$$\end{document}. After defuzzifying neutrosophic values into IVFS, the subsequent stage of the model is closer and more flexible everywhere for analyzing treatment alternatives. This extension allows the model to cope with heterogeneity in patient covariates and clinical vagueness by fuzzy intervals which characterize the interval of spans for the experts’ judgments. By using the interval-valued membership functions, the proposed framework can better cope with imprecision, subjectivity and partial knowledge encountered in clinical decision making.

The proposed methodological structure is designed in three interrelated stages. In the first stage, neutrosophic triplet-based evaluations provided by experts are transformed into interval-valued fuzzy representations to capture uncertainty in bounded form. In the second stage, these refined evaluations are aggregated using Dempster–Shafer theory to systematically combine expert opinions while explicitly resolving uncertainty, incompleteness, and potential conflicts. In the final stage, the competing alternatives are ranked using an extended neutrosophic TOPSIS method, where each alternative is evaluated relative to ideal and anti-ideal solutions. Collectively, this methodological pipeline constitutes the proposed evidence-fused neutrosophic framework for decision-support in complex clinical environments characterized by ambiguity, incomplete information, and conflicting expert judgments. The complete methodological procedure is described step-by-step for transparency and reproducibility.

Each stage of the proposed framework addresses a distinct limitation of existing decision models and operates in a functionally non-redundant manner. The neutrosophic representation captures three-dimensional uncertainty inherent in expert assessments, explicitly modeling truth, indeterminacy, and falsity. The interval-valued fuzzy transformation extends this representation by introducing bounded uncertainty intervals, thereby quantifying the confidence dispersion around expert judgments. The Dempster–Shafer-based aggregation then resolves inter-expert conflict through belief fusion, ensuring that contradictory evidence is systematically reconciled prior to decision evaluation. Finally, the extended TOPSIS mechanism translates the processed uncertainty into a structured and interpretable ranking of alternatives. Together, these stages form a sequentially complementary pipeline in which uncertainty representation, uncertainty expansion, conflict resolution, and decision ranking are handled in distinct yet interdependent phases, ensuring that no stage redundantly replicates the function of another.

Step 1: Conversion to interval-valued fuzzy sets (IVFS). The transformation from single-valued neutrosophic numbers to IVFS serves a specific methodological purpose: while neutrosophic triplets capture three dimensions of uncertainty, they remain point estimates. By introducing interval bounds, the IVFS representation explicitly models the confidence width of each assessment. In this framework, the uncertainty parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} is set proportional to the standard deviation of inter-expert agreement. For the present clinical scenario, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 0.05$$\end{document} is adopted. A sensitivity analysis examining the stability of results across \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.08, 0.10\}$$\end{document} is presented in “Results and sensitivity evaluation”. Furthermore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max (0, \cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min (1, \cdot )$$\end{document} clipping constraints are applied to ensure all membership bounds remain within the standard [0, 1] interval. The IVFS is defined as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \tilde{T}_{ij} = (T_{ij} - \delta , \, T_{ij} + \delta ) \\ \tilde{I}_{ij} = (I_{ij} - \delta , \, I_{ij} + \delta ) \\ \tilde{F}_{ij} = (F_{ij} - \delta , \, F_{ij} + \delta ) \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} represents the uncertainty parameter determined in compliance with the reliability of the input data and the confidence levels expressed by medical experts. This transformation is designed to make ambiguity, inconsistency, and partial knowledge explicit in the evaluation process, leading to a more nuanced and patient-centered decision framework.

Step 2: Expert opinion aggregation via Dempster–Shafer theory (DST). The DST provides a mathematically principled mechanism for aggregating expert opinions that extends beyond simple averaging. This theory explicitly models and resolves informational conflicts through the conflict coefficient K. Formally, the frame of discernment is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega = \{T, I, F\}$$\end{document}. Each expert’s neutrosophic triplet is interpreted as a basic probability assignment (BPA) over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}, normalized such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_k(T) + m_k(I) + m_k(F) = 1$$\end{document}.

For a given alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} evaluated under criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document}, each expert \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k \in \{1, 2, \dots , p\}$$\end{document} provides a BPA, denoted by:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} m_k(T_{ij}) + m_k(I_{ij}) + m_k(F_{ij}) = 1, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_k(T_{ij})$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_k(I_{ij})$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_k(F_{ij})$$\end{document} represent the degrees of belief in truth, indeterminacy, and falsity assigned by the k-th expert, respectively. To resolve discrepancies arising from divergent expert opinions, Dempster’s rule of combination is applied. The aggregated BPAs are calculated as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} m(T_{ij}) = \frac{\sum _{k=1}^{p} m_k(T_{ij}) \prod _{l=1, l \ne k}^{p} (1 - m_l(F_{ij}))}{1 - K}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} m(F_{ij}) = \frac{\sum _{k=1}^{p} m_k(F_{ij}) \prod _{l=1, l \ne k}^{p} (1 - m_l(T_{ij}))}{1 - K}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The aggregated indeterminacy is obtained as the residual mass, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m(I_{ij}) = 1 - m(T_{ij}) - m(F_{ij})$$\end{document}, ensuring the total belief sums to unity after conflict normalization. The conflict coefficient K is determined as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} K = \sum _{k=1}^{p} m_k(T_{ij}) \cdot m_k(F_{ij}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}These formulations facilitate the DST-based fusion of the NDM, yielding the consolidated evidence matrix:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D^{DST} = \left[ (m(T_{ij}), \, m(I_{ij}), \, m(F_{ij})) \right] _{m \times n}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This fusion process ensures that uncertainty, partial knowledge, and expert disagreement are formally integrated, thereby enhancing the reliability of the resulting decision model.

Step 3: Min-max normalization of the aggregated decision matrix. The normalization procedure is critical for ensuring comparability across heterogeneous criteria. Crucially, the normalization direction depends on the criterion type: criteria are classified as either benefit-type (where a higher truth value T is desirable, e.g., effectiveness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1$$\end{document} and patient satisfaction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_5$$\end{document}) or cost-type (where a lower T is desirable, e.g., side effects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_2$$\end{document}, cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_3$$\end{document}, and recovery time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_4$$\end{document}). For benefit-type criteria, standard increasing normalization is applied, whereas for cost-type criteria, an inverted formula ensures that lower raw values receive higher normalized scores.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{ij}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{ij}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ij}$$\end{document} denote the corresponding scores for truth, indeterminacy, and falsity of alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document} under criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} following DST-based aggregation. The normalized values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T'_{ij}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I'_{ij}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F'_{ij}$$\end{document} are determined via min-max scaling as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} T'_{ij} = \dfrac{T_{ij} - \min T_j}{\max T_j - \min T_j} \\ I'_{ij} = \dfrac{\max I_j - I_{ij}}{\max I_j - \min I_j} \\ F'_{ij} = \dfrac{\max F_j - F_{ij}}{\max F_j - \min F_j} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max T_j$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min T_j$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max I_j$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min I_j$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max F_j$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min F_j$$\end{document} represent the maximum and minimum values of the corresponding dimensions for criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} across all alternatives. This procedure scales the requirements to a standard range, where higher values of truth indicate more desirable treatments, while higher values of indeterminacy and falsity indicate greater ambiguity and conflict. This normalization guarantees that all evaluation features are consistently presented on a uniform scale, enabling unbiased integration and facilitating fair treatment prioritization in subsequent decision-making processes.

To transition from the interval-valued normalized scores to the scalar inputs required for final aggregation, a weighted midpoint-boundary defuzzification scheme is employed. The scalar value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{ij}$$\end{document} for each neutrosophic component is determined as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_{ij} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \text {mid}(\tilde{T}'_{ij}) + \frac{1}{6} \cdot T'^{-}_{ij} + \frac{1}{6} \cdot T'^{+}_{ij}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {mid}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the midpoint of the interval, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T'^{-}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T'^{+}$$\end{document} represent the lower and upper bounds, respectively. This scheme prioritizes the central tendency of the expert assessments while accounting for the inherent boundary uncertainty. Subsequently, these defuzzified values are utilized in the final score computation.

Step 4: Computation of interval-weighted neutrosophic scores (IWNS). We propose a novel scoring function termed the IWNS to aggregate the normalized truth, indeterminacy, and falsity values across all criteria while incorporating their relative importance. The IWNS is a newly proposed scoring function in this work, designed specifically for interval-valued normalized neutrosophic data. Unlike existing neutrosophic score functions, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = T - I - F$$\end{document} proposed by Ye68, the IWNS integrates criterion-specific weights directly into the aggregation process. The IWNS satisfies the properties of boundedness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {IWNS}_i \in [-1, 1]$$\end{document}) and monotonicity, where an increase in the truth component T leads to a strictly increasing IWNS. By quantifying how effectively each treatment alternative satisfies the evaluation criteria, we generate a single composite score for each alternative, facilitating an interpretable and transparent ranking. This formulation enables the comprehensive evaluation and prioritization of alternatives under uncertainty, consistent with the foundational principles of neutrosophic MCDM.

The proposed procedure for healthcare treatment selection based on the evidence-fused neutrosophic framework is outlined in Algorithm 1. The framework is designed as a sequential decision pipeline in which each stage addresses a specific aspect of uncertainty-aware evaluation. Initially, a neutrosophic decision matrix (NDM) is constructed to represent expert assessments in terms of truth, indeterminacy, and falsity, thereby preserving multi-dimensional uncertainty. These evaluations are subsequently transformed into interval-valued fuzzy representations using a controlled uncertainty parameter, enabling bounded modeling of variability in clinical judgments. Dempster–Shafer theory is then employed to fuse multi-expert evidence, where conflicting inputs are quantified through the conflict coefficient and systematically redistributed to avoid distortion from linear aggregation. The aggregated values are normalized to ensure comparability across criteria, and weighted normalized scores are used to compute the IWNS. The extended TOPSIS method is then applied to evaluate the proximity of each alternative to the ideal and anti-ideal solutions using Euclidean distance measures, resulting in a RC coefficient that forms the basis for ranking. This sequential design ensures that uncertainty is first represented, then reconciled, and finally propagated into the ranking stage, thereby enhancing interpretability while maintaining robustness under both subjective disagreement and stochastic variability. The alternative with the largest coefficient is considered the most preferred treatment. The general mechanism yields uncertainty-aware, context-sensitive decision support that is formalized and tailored to complicated healthcare environments. Conversion to interval-valued fuzzy sets (IVFS)

In the second phase of the framework, the NDM is translated into an IVFS representation. This conversion is essential because expert assessments in neutrosophic sets, while informative, inherently involve uncertainty and variability. By utilizing interval limits, the IVFS formulation captures the ranges of possible values for the truth, indeterminacy, and falsity components, rather than relying on static point estimates, thereby enabling explicit quantification of confidence dispersion in expert judgments. This provides a more flexible and realistic representation of the dynamic nature of expert evaluations. The interval-valued transformation introduces controlled flexibility in representing expert assessments, allowing minor variations in judgment to be captured without altering the underlying preference structure. This ensures that uncertainty is propagated in a bounded and interpretable manner across subsequent computational stages.

Aggregating expert opinions using Dempster–Shafer theory (DST)

In the third stage of the framework, DST is employed to integrate expert evaluations while explicitly accounting for uncertainty, inconsistency, and potential conflicts between judgments. DST provides a formal mathematical structure for representing belief functions and handling disagreements, thereby combining assessments from multiple experts into a coherent structure for informed decision-making. Each expert provides a set of belief values for the truth (T), indeterminacy (I), and falsity (F) components of the neutrosophic triplet for each alternative–criterion pair. At this stage, DST plays a critical role in distinguishing between variability due to uncertainty and disagreement arising from conflicting expert opinions. Unlike arithmetic aggregation, which averages inputs and may obscure disagreement, DST explicitly quantifies conflict through the conflict coefficient and redistributes belief mass accordingly, resulting in a more informative and structurally consistent aggregation.

Normalization of the aggregated decision matrix

Normalization of the DST-aggregated NDM is a critical step to facilitate a fair comparison of evaluation criteria reported on heterogeneous scales and units. Comparing raw values without normalization would result in biased or mathematically inconsistent interpretations. For this purpose, we utilize the min-max normalization technique to transform the neutrosophic truth T, indeterminacy I, and falsity F into a standardized range. This scaling ensures uniformity among all attributes and serves as the foundation for subsequent aggregation and ranking. In addition to ensuring comparability, this normalization step preserves the relative ordering within each criterion while preventing scale-induced distortions in the ranking stage, thereby maintaining the structural integrity of the decision process.

The normalization is performed individually for each criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} and treatment alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_i$$\end{document}. The transformation, defined in Eq. (13), is applied to the neutrosophic components based on the minimum and maximum observed values within each criterion. This formulation yields higher truth-membership values while imposing a penalty for higher indeterminacy and falsity, aligning with the neutrosophic semantics employed in multi-criteria decision-making. This criterion-wise normalization also ensures that each attribute is evaluated relative to its own distribution, enabling interpretable comparison across criteria with differing variability and measurement ranges.

Results and sensitivity evaluation

In this section, the results obtained from the proposed evidence-fused neutrosophic framework approach are presented. The study indicates the benefits of incorporating neutrosophic representation, the Dempster-Shafer aggregation rule, normalization and interval-weighted scoring to obtain consistent and interpretable solutions in the healthcare case. The outcomes validate the capability of the framework to manage conflicting objectives, including efficacy, side effects, cost, recovery time and patient satisfaction in addition to explicitly handling uncertainty and expert opinion variability.

To further substantiate the reliability of the proposed framework, this section provides a structured evaluation of parameter sensitivity and ranking stability under controlled perturbations. The analysis focuses on identifying whether variations in uncertainty modeling and criterion weighting influence the relative ordering of treatment alternatives, thereby assessing the consistency of the decision pipeline beyond a single deterministic configuration. The validation strategy is organized into three complementary layers: deterministic sensitivity analysis evaluates invariance under parameter variations, Monte Carlo simulation examines probabilistic consistency under stochastic perturbations representing expert variability, and comparative analysis provides mechanism-level justification by contrasting the proposed framework with established methods.

The section also examines the robustness of the proposed framework along with the ranking outcomes. In the complex environment of decision-making, it is necessary to maintain stability under varying model parameters, criterion weights and expert judgments. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis is employed to evaluate the effects of changes in decision parameters on the rankings of alternatives. This validation ensures transparency, trustworthiness and practical utility of the decision-making approach in real-life medical situations.

Robustness evaluation using Monte Carlo simulation

The robustness of the proposed framework was assessed by simulating stochastic perturbations in expert judgments to complement the sensitivity analysis. Using the DST-aggregated and the min–max normalized neutrosophic matrix as the baseline, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N=1000$$\end{document} Monte Carlo simulations were performed. The stochastic perturbations are introduced to emulate variability in expert assessments arising from measurement noise, subjective interpretation, and incomplete information, thereby enabling evaluation of the decision pipeline under realistic uncertainty conditions. In all runs, criteria-wise valuations were perturbed with Gaussian noise centered at their normalized means and with standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma =0.05$$\end{document}. The choice of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma =0.05$$\end{document} represents moderate variability in expert assessments, corresponding to approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 5\%$$\end{document} fluctuation around normalized values, which is consistent with typical disagreement levels observed in multi-expert clinical evaluation settings and ensures coherence with the uncertainty range introduced during IVFS transformation. This level of perturbation reflects moderate disagreement without distorting the underlying decision structure, thereby allowing assessment of robustness within a realistic variability regime.

Importantly, these perturbations propagate through the DST fusion stage, where conflicting evidence is redistributed rather than averaged, ensuring that the stochastic noise does not accumulate linearly but is moderated through belief combination. Comparison with existing methods

The performance is compared with four baseline approaches: Crisp TOPSIS70, Fuzzy TOPSIS71, IFS–TOPSIS72, and a Neutrosophic TOPSIS variant without DST fusion73. This fourth baseline is included to isolate the contribution of DST fusion by retaining the IVFS–TOPSIS pipeline while omitting only the evidence aggregation step. Two complementary assessment scenarios are considered to examine both structural and uncertainty-driven differences.

The comparative design aligns with the sensitivity (“Sensitivity analysis”) and Monte Carlo (“Robustness evaluation using Monte Carlo simulation”) analyses by evaluating whether the ranking invariance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \succ B \succ A$$\end{document} persists when (i) uncertainty modeling is simplified (crisp and fuzzy cases) and (ii) evidence fusion is removed (neutrosophic without DST). This enables attribution of robustness to specific modeling components rather than to the underlying data configuration. A deterministic-versus-neutrosophic comparison is also included: the same alternatives are evaluated using purely deterministic inputs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I=F=0$$\end{document}) and the resulting rankings are contrasted with the full neutrosophic formulation to quantify the effect of explicitly modeling indeterminacy. This comparison further clarifies whether improvements arise from richer uncertainty representation or from aggregation mechanics.

In the subsequent segments, we briefly describe the four approaches considered for comparison along with the proposed DST-fused IVNS-TOPSIS model. To ensure a fair comparison, each of these approaches is presented in a unified structure emphasizing the computational steps most relevant to the comparative analysis that follows.

Crisp TOPSIS

The classical TOPSIS approach is implemented on a crisp decision matrix. Each single-valued neutrosophic element (T, I, F) is converted into a scalar score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = T-I-F$$\end{document}, representing net support after accounting for indeterminacy and opposition. This conversion follows the neutrosophic score function proposed by Ye68. The mapping collapses the three-dimensional representation into a single scalar, thereby eliminating the explicit treatment of indeterminacy and redistributing its effect linearly into the score. Consequently, conflicting or uncertain assessments are averaged into a single value, which may mask disagreement among experts and reduce the model’s ability to distinguish closely competing alternatives. All columns are normalized using the min-max method and weighted by the corresponding importance of a criterion. The positive and negative ideal solutions are then calculated, followed by the Euclidean distance from each alternative to the ideal solutions. The closeness coefficient is obtained as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CC = \frac{D^{-}}{(D^{+} + D^{-})}$$\end{document}, where higher CC values indicate preferable alternatives.

Fuzzy TOPSIS

To retain uncertainty, each (T, I, F) triplet is transformed into a fuzzy membership interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\mu ^\ell , \mu ^u]$$\end{document} using parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}, fixed to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =\beta =1$$\end{document}. The midpoint of each interval provides a fuzzy-adjusted score that captures partial belief. This interval representation introduces bounded uncertainty; however, the transformation aggregates indeterminacy and falsity into a single membership structure without preserving their distinct semantic roles. As a result, while variability is captured, explicit conflict between expert opinions is not represented, in contrast to DST-based fusion where conflicting evidence is quantified and influences the aggregation outcome. After column-wise normalization and weighting, TOPSIS is performed identically to the crisp variant to obtain the closeness coefficients and final ranking.

IFS–TOPSIS

In this model, evaluations are represented as intuitionistic fuzzy triples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu , \nu , \pi )$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu =T,~\nu =F,$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi =I$$\end{document}. The components are normalized and weighted across criteria. Ideal points are defined to maximize membership while minimizing non-membership and hesitation. Euclidean distances to both positive and negative ideals are computed in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mu , \nu , \pi )$$\end{document} space, and the conventional TOPSIS closeness coefficient is then used for ranking. Although this formulation explicitly retains a hesitation degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}, it enforces the constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu +\nu +\pi =1$$\end{document}, which couples uncertainty components and restricts their independent variation. Consequently, scenarios involving simultaneous uncertainty and conflict across experts are compressed into a constrained representation, limiting sensitivity to nuanced differences compared to neutrosophic models where (T, I, F) are independently represented.

Neutrosophic TOPSIS (without DST)

This variant applies the IVFS–TOPSIS pipeline directly to the mean-aggregated neutrosophic decision matrix without the DST evidence fusion step. Each element (T, I, F) is expanded into interval-valued bounds using the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 0.05$$\end{document} parameter, normalized, and then ranked using the extended TOPSIS procedure. By retaining interval modeling but replacing evidence fusion with arithmetic aggregation, this baseline isolates the role of conflict resolution. In particular, when expert opinions diverge, averaging smooths discrepancies without quantifying disagreement, leading to potential information loss regarding conflict intensity. As a result, closeness coefficients tend to be less sharply separated and more sensitive to perturbations, which explains the comparatively lower robustness observed in “Sensitivity analysis” and “Robustness evaluation using Monte Carlo simulation”. This formulation follows the neutrosophic TOPSIS paradigm of Zulqarnain et al.73, extended here to interval-valued representations.

Proposed DST-fused IVNS–TOPSIS

For the multi-expert scenario, expert evaluations are first fused using DST to form a consolidated single-valued neutrosophic matrix. Each fused element (T, I, F) is expanded into an interval-valued neutrosophic representation by introducing a small uncertainty radius \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} around each component and restricting values to the range [0, 1]. The resulting IVNS matrix is normalized and weighted according to the criterion vector. TOPSIS is then applied within the IVNS space for benefit-type criteria to compute the final closeness coefficients. In the single-NDM scenario, expert fusion is omitted. While the baseline methods rely on arithmetic averaging for multi-expert aggregation, the proposed framework employs DST fusion, ensuring more robust handling of uncertainty and conflicting evidence. The key distinction lies in the explicit treatment of inter-expert conflict through the DST combination rule, where conflicting evidence contributes to the conflict coefficient and is redistributed during belief fusion rather than being linearly averaged. This results in a non-linear aggregation process in which consistent evidence is reinforced while contradictory inputs are attenuated, leading to improved discrimination and reduced susceptibility to noise.

All the approaches considered for comparison were independently re-implemented based on the parameter settings described above, rather than directly using implementations from the referenced manuscripts. Although there are similar variants of these algorithms that are used extensively in the literature on MCDM70–73, we focus on the core formulations in the current study. Each algorithm was carefully designed and re-implemented within the present SVNS/IVNS framework to provide a fair and consistent like-for-like comparison. All methods are evaluated under identical preprocessing steps, including normalization scheme, parameter selection (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta =0.05$$\end{document}), and weighting configuration, to ensure that observed differences in ranking outcomes arise from the modeling structure rather than implementation variability. This controlled setup supports reproducibility and enables direct attribution of performance differences to the treatment of uncertainty and aggregation mechanisms.

Conclusion and future directions

This article presents a neutrosophic healthcare treatment selection approach using an MCDM framework that integrates Neutrosophic Logic, IVFS, DST, and an extended TOPSIS method. The proposed approach provides a structured mechanism to address uncertainty, indeterminacy, and conflict in expert evaluation, thereby enabling a flexible and robust framework for evidence-based decision-making in complex clinical environments. The primary contribution of this work lies in the structured and sequential integration of interval-valued neutrosophic representation with DST-based evidence fusion within a TOPSIS framework, enabling simultaneous modeling of uncertainty, indeterminacy, and inter-expert conflict. Rather than acting as a standalone replacement for existing MCDM methods, the framework extends them by preserving multi-dimensional uncertainty and introducing a principled conflict-resolution mechanism, thereby improving interpretability and reliability of the decision process.

The results show that Treatment C is consistently identified as the most preferred option, followed by Treatments B and A, demonstrating the framework’s ability to effectively discriminate among competing alternatives. The single NDM design yields enhanced separation between alternatives, particularly between A and B, without affecting the stability of the top-ranked Treatment C. In multi-expert settings, less favorable alternatives are naturally attenuated, while consistently supported options are reinforced through the aggregation mechanism. This improved discrimination arises from the framework’s ability to explicitly separate indeterminacy from support and opposition, and to resolve inter-expert conflict prior to ranking, thereby avoiding the loss of information typically observed in classical and fuzzy aggregation schemes. This behavior can be attributed to the complementary roles of IVFS and DST within the decision pipeline: the interval-valued representation captures bounded uncertainty around expert assessments, while DST fusion redistributes conflicting evidence through the conflict coefficient, preventing linear averaging of disagreement. As a result, consistent evidence is reinforced, and contradictory inputs are moderated, leading to stable rankings and improved separation among competing alternatives, as observed across both sensitivity and stochastic analyses.

The ranking order remains invariant across a wide range of weighting configurations, as demonstrated through sensitivity analysis, while robustness is further confirmed through Monte Carlo simulation under stochastic perturbations. The consistent ordering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \succ B \succ A$$\end{document} highlights the reliability of the proposed framework under both subjective preference variation and uncertainty in expert inputs. In this approach, indeterminacy and expert disagreement are explicitly incorporated through DST-based fusion and IVNS-based normalization, extending beyond traditional MCDM models. The computational structure of the framework remains tractable, with each stage operating in polynomial time with respect to the number of criteria, alternatives, and experts, ensuring feasibility for moderate-scale clinical decision problems.

From a practical and managerial perspective, the proposed framework supports decision-making in multi-expert clinical environments where uncertainty and disagreement are inherent. The ability to maintain consistent rankings under varying assumptions enables clinicians to make more confident treatment selections, while the interpretable RC scores facilitate transparent communication of decisions to stakeholders. Additionally, the framework assists administrators and policymakers in structured resource allocation, prioritization of treatment strategies, and policy formulation under constrained and uncertain conditions.

Generalizability and broader applicability

Although the proposed framework is demonstrated within a healthcare treatment selection context, its methodological structure is inherently domain-independent. The integration of neutrosophic representation, interval-valued uncertainty modeling, and DST-based evidence fusion enables its applicability to a wide class of multi-criteria decision-making problems characterized by incomplete information, conflicting expert judgments, and heterogeneous evaluation criteria. In particular, the framework can be extended to domains such as healthcare logistics planning, telemedicine service selection, pharmaceutical prioritization, and environmental risk assessment, where uncertainty and multi-expert inputs are central to the decision process. The modular design allows domain-specific criteria, expert structures, and evaluation parameters to be incorporated without altering the underlying computational pipeline, thereby ensuring scalability and transferability across diverse real-world decision environments.

Despite these contributions, the current study is based on a limited-scale illustrative setting involving a small number of criteria and treatment alternatives. Future studies should extend the framework to more comprehensive and data-rich environments, including heterogeneous clinical datasets and patient-reported outcomes, to improve generalizability and enable more data-driven decision support in complex healthcare systems. It is important to note that the current study relies on synthetically generated expert evaluations, which allow controlled validation of the methodological pipeline but may not fully capture the complexity of real-world clinical scenarios. Future directions

Future research should focus on validating the proposed framework using real-world clinical datasets and expert panels to establish external validity. Extensions may include adaptive or criteria-specific uncertainty parameters, integration with real-time clinical decision-support systems, and scalability to larger decision spaces with numerous alternatives and criteria. Furthermore, hybridization with data-driven and learning-based models may enhance dynamic decision-making capabilities, while comparative analysis with alternative uncertainty frameworks, such as Pythagorean, spherical, and picture fuzzy sets, can provide deeper insights into modeling trade-offs.
