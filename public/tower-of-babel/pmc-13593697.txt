Performance evaluation of the BioFire respiratory panel 2.1 plus and exploratory analysis of Ct values for respiratory pathogen quantification
Authors: Alessandra Vitale, Ziggy Mahler, Valentin Loup, Zahera Dance, Gilbert Greub, Onya Opota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsThe BioFire FilmArray RP2.1 plus is a syndromic multiplex PCR panel for qualitative detection of 20 respiratory pathogens. BioMérieux recently introduced FIREWORKS, a software platform providing access to amplification data, including cycle threshold (Ct) values.ObjectivesThis study evaluated the diagnostic performance of the BioFire RP2.1 plus and explored the potential utility of Ct values for semi-quantitative assessment of pathogen load.MethodsThis retrospectively study included 72 specimens. Some were serially diluted for quantitative analyses, yielding 130 PCR tests. Samples were tested on the BioFire RP2.1 plus, and compared with Xpert, Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus (Cepheid) or in-house real-time PCR assays QuantStudio, resulting in 708 PCR results. Agreement was assessed qualitatively using the kappa coefficient, and exploratory analyses were performed using Passing-Bablok regression and Spearman correlation.ResultsThe BioFire RP2.1 plus demonstrated excellent qualitative agreement with comparator methods (kappa of 0.963). Among 130 tests, 117 were concordant. Nine discordant negatives involved Bordetella pertussis (n = 4), Coronavirus NL63 (n = 1), Influenza A (n = 2), and Influenza B (n = 2) predominantly in specimens with high Ct values (low pathogen load). Four samples were negative by QuantStudio but detected by BioFire RP2.1 plus. Exploratory quantitative analyses demonstrated strong correlations between BioFire Ct values and reference assays, with Spearman coefficients of 0.71 (QuantStudio) and 0.93 (Xpert).ConclusionsOverall the BioFire RP2.1 plus showed high qualitative agreement with routine assays for respiratory pathogens detection. Quantitative analyses suggested a reproducible relationship between BioFire-derived Ct values and comparator methods, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory design and methodological limitations of cross-platform comparisons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05545-x.

Introduction

Respiratory infectious diseases are a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in children, elderly and immunocompromised patients and represent a significant cause of hospital admission [1]. Viruses account for more than 40 million reported respiratory infections annually [2]. These infections are often characterized by nonspecific symptoms such as cough, fever, headache and muscle pains, underscoring the importance of rapid sensitive and specific diagnostic tools [3, 4]. As illustrated during the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, identification of respiratory pathogens is crucial for both patient management and optimizing public health responses.

To address these clinical needs, automated multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have been developed to simultaneously detect multiple respiratory pathogens from a single specimen. The Xpert Xpress CoV-2/Flu/RSV plus test was established to differentiate between SARS-CoV-2, influenza A and B types, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in nasopharyngeal swabs or anterior nasal swab specimens [5]. Other syndromic platforms identify additional microorganisms such as the BioFire Respiratory Panel 2.1 plus, which detects 16 viruses (adenovirus, coronavirus 229E/HKU1/NL63/OC43, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus – MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, human metapneumovirus, human rhinovirus/enterovirus, Influenza A and B, parainfluenza virus 1–4 and RSV) and 4 bacteria (Bordetella parapertussis, B. pertussis, Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae) in nasopharyngeal swabs [6]. The molecular diagnostic laboratory of the Service of Microbiology at the University Hospital of Lausanne (CHUV, Switzerland) developed an in-house high-throughput molecular diagnostic real-time PCR platform (hereafter referred as “QuantStudio”) for detecting the above listed viruses and bacteria [7].

Although PCR assays were commonly used for qualitative detection of respiratory pathogens, the ability to estimate viral load using Ct values was shown to be important for assessing contagiousness, disease severity, and treatment response during the COVID-19 pandemic. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were used to follow kinetics of SARS-CoV-2 infection and improve patient management [8, 9]. In contrast, other studies demonstrate that Ct values could be used as a possible marker of disease severity but with variable consistency depending on the virus (SARS-CoV-2, Influenza, rhinovirus/enterovirus and RSV) and patients population [10–12]. The BioFire RP2.1 plus now provides access to Ct values via the FIREWORKS software, enabling exploration of the relationship between Ct values and viral loads. Ct values are subject to variability between platforms, and no standardized conversion method exists for translating Ct values into pathogen loads in the BioFire RP2.1 plus kit. The nested reaction in the BioFire system reflects quantification based on a two-stage amplification process. Some studies have shown a linear correlation between Ct values from nested PCRs and conventional RT-PCR assays [13–15], making it interesting to investigate the BioFire Ct values for their potential clinical and research applications. While several studies have compared the qualitative performance of the BioFire RP2.1 plus, quantitative evaluations have not been conducted yet [16–19].

In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the qualitative performance of the BioFire RP2.1 plus and to explore the relationship between BioFire-derived Ct values and comparator molecular assays using routine clinical and quality control specimens.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

This retrospective study was conducted on clinical specimens collected and stored at −80 °C at the University Hospital of Lausanne between 2022 and 2024. A total of 72 specimens were selected including 15 quality controls (3x B. pertussis, 5x B. parapertussis, 1x adenovirus, 2x coronavirus NL63, 1x coronavirus OC43, 2x parainfluenza 1, 1x coronavirus 229E), 1 expectoration (M. pneumonia), 3 bronchoalveolar lavages (adenovirus, SARS-CoV-2 and rhinovirus/enterovirus), 1 throat swab (M. pneumoniae) and 52 nasopharyngeal swabs or secretions (all the other samples). Quality control specimens were obtained from external quality assessment schemes, specifically QCMD (clinical materials in transport medium or culture isolates in physiological buffer or saline solution) and INSTAND (lyophilized cell lysates). While all specimens were validated for use on the reference methods described below, the BioFire Respiratory Panel is developed for nasopharyngeal swabs. For quantitative analysis, some samples were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline giving a total of 130 tests. Residual specimens from subject of all ages were used with Ct values between 10 and 38 (referring to a variety of different pathogens concentrations) for comparative analysis. The selected specimens have been retested with the reference method in parallel to the BioFire FilmArray in 2024. All samples were anonymized, and no clinical data were acquired.

BioFire respiratory panel 2.1 plus

Specimens were tested with the BioFire Respiratory Panel 2.1 plus (BioMérieux, Reference: 423740) according to manufacturer’s instructions [6]. The quality controls, expectoration, bronchoalveolar lavages and diluted samples were processed with the same procedure as for the nasopharyngeal swabs or secretions by transferring a total volume of approximately 300 µl in the pouch. The nucleic acid extraction, reverse transcription, amplifications (nested PCR) and results analysis are automated and last for about 45 min per run. A result was reported as positive for either assay if at least two of the three technical replicates gave a corresponding melting curve and semi-quantitative Ct ≤ 30. Ct values were directly obtained from Biomérieux for this study.

Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus

The Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus test (Cepheid, Reference: XP3COV2/FLU/RSV-10) was used for detection of SARS-CoV-2, influenza A or B, or RSV in the selected clinical specimens. Approximately 300 µl of each sample were transferred to the cartridge, in which all the steps of the RT-PCR are combined and automated in about 30 min. A result is interpreted as positive if one of the RNA targets is detected with Ct ≤ 45. The targeted sequences are: nucleocapsid (N), envelope (E) and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) for SARS-CoV-2; matrix (M), basic polymerase (PB2) and acidic protein (PA) for influenza A; matrix (M) and non-structural protein (NS) for influenza B; A and B nucleocapsid genes for RSV.

In house molecular diagnostic platform

In house high-throughput molecular RT-PCR were performed as previously described in the molecular diagnostic laboratory of the Institute of Microbiology (University Hospital of Lausanne) [7]. Molecular materials were amplified in the QuantStudio 7 Pro RT-PCR System (ThermoFisher, Publication number: 4489826). A total of 200 µl of clinical specimens were used for DNA extraction, of which 10 (RNA) or 5 (DNA) µl were amplified. The workflow (DNA extraction, reverse transcription and amplification) takes about 3 h and quantification data are given in Ct values and copies/mL [9]. This method was used as a reference technique for this study. A result is interpreted as positive if detected with Ct ≤ 45 for any pathogen tested.

Data analysis

To evaluate the qualitative agreement between the Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus test or the in-house molecular RT-PCR and the BioFire Respiratory Panel 2.1 plus, samples were classified as positive or negative. The positive percentage agreement (PPA) and the negative percentage agreement (NPA) were calculated. The Clopper-pearson interval was used to infer the confidence interval (CI) for the PPA and the NPA. The overall study revealed an almost perfect agreement with a PABAK coefficient of 0.963. While the negative percentage agreement for all pathogens varies between 95 and 100%, the positive percentage agreement fluctuates between 55 and 100%. Nine specimens were not detected on the BioFire RP 2.1 plus and were categorized as “false negative” (Coronavirus NL63, n = 1, Ct 37.4; Influenza A, n = 2, Ct 37.5 and 37.1; Influenza B, n = 2, Ct 37.1 and 37.3; B. pertussis, n = 4, Ct 29.7, 30.8, 37.9 and 41.2). Seven of these nine false negative specimens (Coronavirus NL63, n = 1; Influenza A, n = 2; Influenza B, n = 1, Ct 37.1; B. pertussis, n = 3, Ct 30.8, 37.9 and 41.2) are diluted samples, which were initially found positive on both methods. The two other samples are one nasopharyngeal swab with Influenza B (Ct 37.3) and one quality control containing a culture isolate of B. pertussis (Ct 29.7). Excluding the latter in the analysis increases the PPA (95% CI) for B. pertussis to 62.5 (24.5–91.5%). On the other hand, 4 samples were not detected on the QuantStudio 7 but were detected on the BioFire. These samples were grouped as “false positive” (Coronavirus HKU1, n = 1, Ct 22.9; Rhinovirus/Enterovirus, n = 1, Ct 20.8; C. pneumonia, n = 1, Ct 22.2; RSV, n = 1, Ct 19.2). Three of these four false positive specimens (Coronavirus HKU1, C. pneumonia and RSV) are diluted samples, which were initially found positive on both methods.

Quantitative evaluation of the BioFire RP 2.1 plus

The performance of the BioFire RP 2.1 plus was evaluated in the 72 samples (130 tests) by comparing the Ct values of the BioFire RP 2.1 plus to the reference method (Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus test for SARS-CoV-2, Influenza and RSV, and QuantStudio 7 for the other pathogens). The Ct values between the different techniques were highly concordant with Spearman’s correlation coefficient of 0.71 for QuantStudio and 0.93 for Xpert. In addition, the 95% confidence intervals for the slope range from 0.86 to 1.19 (QuantStudio) and 0.85 to 1.33 (Xpert), suggesting a strong correlation between the methods. The Passing-Bablok analysis showed constant bias of −13.53 and − 14.19 for QuantStudio and Xpert, respectively, indicating that Ct values from the BioFire RP 2.1 plus are lower than the Ct values from the reference methods (Fig. 1a and b).

Discussion

Automated syndromic molecular tests have the potential to improve diagnostics and, consequently, patient management by offering faster turnaround times (TAT), and streamlined workflows. Their rapid TAT can facilitate earlier detection of pathogens, enabling prompt initiation of appropriate treatment and improved infection control measures. However, the extensive multiplexing capabilities of these assays may sometimes impact performance in terms of sensitivity and specificity. Moreover, their clinical impact may be limited if these tests are not properly integrated into the diagnostic workflow or if they are not supported by a well-defined diagnostic and antibiotic stewardship strategies to ensure appropriate test ordering and result interpretation [20, 21].

In the present study, we first evaluated the qualitative performance of the BioFire RP2.1 plus before exploring the potential utility of BioFire-derived Ct values for semi-quantitative assessment. We compared the agreement between the BioFire RP 2.1 plus and our reference methods (Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV plus and the QuantStudio Pro RT-PCR). Given the inherent platform-dependence of Ct values, direct comparison between Xpert Xpress and QuantStudio 7 carries methodological limitations. Among the 20 microorganisms tested in the BioFire RP 2.1 plus, only the MERS-CoV has not been assessed due to the lack of positive samples in our laboratory. The BioFire RP 2.1 plus identified 117/130 targets in comparison to the reference methods with an overall PPA of 94.7% and with an almost perfect agreement of 0.963. Most of the studies on the performance of the BioFire RP 2.1 plus focused on the SARS-CoV-2 with comparable PPA of 98% (n = 49) [22] and 100% (n = 25) [23] to our study (100%). In contrast, Lade et al. evaluated the BioFire RP 2.1 plus for the other pathogens of the panel (except for coronavirus 229E/NL63/OC43/HKU1, Influenza B, hMPV), and reported a detection of 98/154 positive specimens with major discrepancy for the enterovirus/rhinovirus [24]. In our study, the BioFire RP 2.1 plus had an excellent PPA of 100% and no false negative for these two viruses. The false negative samples concerned other pathogens (Coronavirus NL63, Influenza A, Influenza B, B. pertussis). The latter had high Ct values (29.7–41.2), which could explain the lack of amplification. The BioFire RP 2.1 plus is validated for nasopharyngeal swabs and 1/9 of the false negatives was a quality control (B. pertussis), other specimens were a bronchoalveolar lavage (Influenza A) and nasopharyngeal or nasal secretions (Influenza A and B, B. pertussis and Coronavirus NL63). Non-validated samples may compromise the performance of the BioFire assay and impact the reliability of the results. Another evaluation of the BioFireRP 2.1 plus showed a PPA of 89.9% for the detection of B. pertussis in nasopharyngeal swabs [25]. Two other studies showed a detection rate of 89% and 66.7% for B. pertussis in nasopharyngeal swabs with false negative results for specimens with low bacterial loads (Ct > 35) [26, 27]. This highlights the importance of integrating molecular test results with clinical evaluation to ensure appropriate patient management.

Beyond qualitative detection, access to amplification data through the FIREWORKS software now enables exploratory evaluation of Ct values generated by the BioFire RP2.1 plus. Quantitative molecular data may provide clinically relevant information regarding pathogen burden, disease kinetics, transmission potential, or interpretation of complex co-detection profiles. For example, in cases of multiple pathogens detected, quantification may assist in distinguishing the primary causative agent from colonizers or co-infections, thereby improving the interpretation of complex diagnostic profiles. However, interpretation of Ct values remains challenging because Ct measurements are inherently assay-dependent and influenced by multiple analytical and pre-analytical variables, including extraction efficiency, amplification chemistry, specimen quality, and target selection. In our study, quantitative data were compared between the BioFire RP2.1 plus and our reference methods (Xpert Xpress Cov-2/Flu/RSV and the QuantStudio 7 Pro RT-PCR), for which the Ct values were higher with a constant bias of approximately − 14. This is explained by the double amplification of the targets in the nested PCR technique of the BioFire with the final Ct value representing the amplification of the first amplified product [6]. We acknowledge that directly comparing Ct values derived from nested PCR and single-step qPCR introduces a methodological limitation, as differences in amplification dynamics and sensitivity may affect the validity and interpretability of the resulting linear regression. This can influence limits of detection of the different methodologies, which were not compared and would be interesting to investigate. Overall, we had a high positive correlation between the different techniques (Spearman’s correlation coefficients of 0.71 for QuantStudio and 0.93 for Xpert). These results suggest that the pathogen load (in copies/mL) could be explored from the Ct values if standardization is established on the BioFire system. We compared the number of copies/mL obtained by QuantStudio to Ct values of the BioFire or QuantStudio on 41 PCR tests and observed a strong correlation with Spearman coefficients of −0.73 and − 0.93, respectively (Figure S1). Standardization for the pneumonia panel on the BioFire system had already been implemented for semi-quantification (104 to ≥107 copies/mL) of 15 bacteria out of 27 pathogens tested (Chlamydia pneumonia, Legionella pneumophila, Mycoplasma pneumonia and viruses are detected qualitatively) in sputum-like specimens (induced or expectorated sputum, or endotracheal aspirates) and bronchoalveolar lavage specimens. The intrinsic challenges associated with the standardization of respiratory samples are further underscored, together with the pivotal role of the pre-analytical phase, encompassing appropriate specimen collection and handling. In addition, the quantification depends on the targeted sequences and the number of gene copies. For example, the BioFire RP2.1 plus detects multiple genes for some organisms with: (i) the ones encoding the spike and membrane for SARS-CoV-2; (ii) the hemagglutinin and neuraminidase in addition to two other tests for Influenza A; and, (iii) five different amplifications (no details on the specific targets) are performed for the adenovirus.

While Ct-based quantification offers valuable insights into pathogen load, its interpretation requires caution. Some limitations of our study include the following points: Ct values can vary depending on sample type, extraction methods, and amplification efficiency, potentially influencing pathogen load estimation. Additionally, the BioFire RP2.1 plus targets multiple genes for some pathogens, which may introduce variability in quantification outcomes. Despite these limitations, our findings support the high qualitative agreement of the BioFire RP2.1 plus with routine molecular assays for respiratory pathogen detection. The exploratory quantitative analyses further suggest that BioFire-derived Ct values may provide additional information regarding pathogen burden and deserve further investigation in larger prospective studies using standardized quantitative approaches. Future studies assessing the reproducibility and standardization of Ct-based quantification across different platforms would be valuable. The integration of the BioFire RP2.1 plus into clinical workflows must be supported by a well-defined diagnostic stewardship strategy. Ensuring appropriate test ordering, timely result interpretation accompanied by interpretative comments, and linkage to patient management decisions will be crucial to maximizing its clinical impact.

In conclusion, our comparative analysis demonstrates a strong concordance between the BioFire RP2.1 plus and standard platforms for the detection of respiratory pathogens. In addition, the system shows promise for semi-quantitative analysis through Ct values, though further validation is needed and rapid access to these Ct through laboratory information system is mandatory. Integrating such platforms into clinical workflows will require robust diagnostic stewardship to ensure appropriate use, cautious interpretation, and improved impact on patient care.
