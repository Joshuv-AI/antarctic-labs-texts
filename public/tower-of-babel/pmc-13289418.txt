The value of preoperative CD4+ T-cell count in predicting infectious complications after endoscopic lithotripsy for upper urinary tract calculi among human immunodeficiency virus-infected patients
Authors: Penghui Lai, Enming Shao, Zhe Zhu, Jun Ma, Kewen Chen, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore whether preoperative CD4+ T-cell count is associated with postoperative infectious outcomes after endoscopic lithotripsy for upper urinary tract calculi in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected patients.MethodsThe HIV-infected patients who underwent endoscopic lithotripsy for upper urinary tract stones at Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center from May 2019 to May 2025 were enrolled for this study. The exposure of interest was the preoperative peripheral blood CD4+ T-cell count. The primary endpoint was urosepsis. Secondary outcomes included postoperative fever, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and other postoperative complications.ResultsA total of 120 patients were enrolled in this study, including 20 patients with a CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL and 100 patients with a CD4+ T-cell count ≥ 200 cells/µL. None of the patients developed urosepsis or SIRS postoperatively. The overall rate of postoperative fever was 17.5% (21/120). Fever was observed in 15.0% of the patients with a CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL, compared with 18.0% of those with a CD4+ T-cell count ≥ 200 cells/µL, and there was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Logistic regression analysis further showed that CD4+ T-cell count was not significantly associated with fever (OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 0.998–1.003, P = 0.586), whereas urine white blood cell count, stone density, and surgical approach were independently associated with fever. Sensitivity analyses using propensity score matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting showed similar results.ConclusionIn this retrospective single-center cohort, preoperative CD4+ T-cell count was not significantly associated with postoperative fever after endoscopic lithotripsy in HIV-infected patients. However, because no urosepsis events occurred and the sample size, particularly in the CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL subgroup, was limited, the study could not adequately evaluate the primary endpoint. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies incorporating HIV viral load and procedure-specific analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13413-4.

Urolithiasis is one of the most common urological diseases globally [1]. It affects not only the general population but also those infected with HIV. Notably, some antiretroviral drugs for HIV infection can even induce urinary calculi [2]. Minimally invasive surgical treatments, such as percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and ureteroscopy (URS), have been demonstrated to be effective for upper urinary tract calculi. However, postoperative infectious complications, spanning from fever to urosepsis, still pose a significant clinical challenge [3]. Particularly, it is crucial to identify risk factors of infectious complications and improve strategies of perioperative management accordingly in an immunocompromised population.

Because HIV infection is characterized by variable degrees of immune dysfunction, perioperative risk assessment in this population remains complex. CD4+ T lymphocytes are routinely used in HIV care as one marker of immune status. Consequently, HIV patients with a decreased CD4+ T-cell level face an increased risk of postoperative infections [3, 4]. It has already been proved that patients with CD4+ T-cell count less than 200 cells/µL have a 3–4 times greater risk of postoperative complications [5]. It is also showed that a lower preoperative CD4+ T-cell count is significantly correlated with a higher incidence of postoperative urinary tract infections among patients with urinary calculi [6]. However, CD4+ T-cell count alone does not fully capture immunological competence in HIV-infected patients, particularly in the era of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), in which viral suppression, treatment adherence, comorbidities, and perioperative management also influence surgical outcomes.

For elective surgeries, it is generally advised to elevate the CD4+ T-cell level to exceed 200 cells/µL prior to surgery in HIV-infected patients [7]. Nevertheless, some studies demonstrate that CD4+ T-cell count exhibits minimal association with surgical outcomes [8–11]. This gives rise to a debate concerning the optimal “CD4+ T-cell count threshold” for elective surgeries in HIV-infected patients [12, 13]. Specifically, there still remains a lack of evidence investigating the relationship between preoperative CD4+ T-cell count and infectious complications after the urinary calculus surgeries which are mainly minimally invasive.

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective exploratory cohort study to assess whether preoperative CD4+ T-cell count was associated with postoperative infectious outcomes after endoscopic lithotripsy in HIV-infected patients with upper urinary tract calculi, aiming to offer more evidence to facilitate clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study employed a retrospective cohort design, enrolling patients with HIV infection and upper urinary tract stones who underwent endoscopic lithotripsy at Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center from May 2019 to May 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18 years or older; (2) patients with HIV infection confirmed by Shanghai Center for Disease Control; (3) patients diagnosed with upper urinary tract stones (including kidney stones and ureteral stones) by computed tomography (CT); (4) patients having surgical indications and receiving surgical treatment, such as PCNL and URS. PCNL entails establishing a percutaneous access into the renal calyx and utilizing a nephroscope for lithotripsy and stone removal; URS involves the retrograde insertion of a ureteroscope through the urethra to reach the stone and perform laser lithotripsy. All surgeries were performed by an experienced surgeon, and antiretroviral therapy was maintained perioperatively. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with severe dysfunction of vital organs such as the heart, brain, lungs, and liver; (2) patients with urinary tract malformations or tumors; (3) patients with pregnancy. HIV viral load was not used as an eligibility criterion because viral load data were unavailable for a substantial proportion of patients in this retrospective cohort. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (Approval No. 2026-S012-02; approved on March 17, 2026). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and anonymized data handling.

Predictors

The CD4+ T lymphocyte count was determined via flow cytometry with a peripheral venous blood sample. Urinalysis was conducted with a sample of morning urine prior to the surgery. Clean mid-stream urine was utilized for urine culture. A positive culture was defined as the presence of bacteria along with a colony count of > 105 CFU/mL in females, >104 CFU/mL in males, or > 104 CFU/mL in all patients with catheterized urine samples [14]. The stone size was defined as the longest diameter of a stone in the CT axial images. In cases where multiple stones were present, the sum of the long diameters of all stones was utilized. The stone density was independently measured by a radiologist who was blinded to the group assignment. Circular regions of interest were placed on three non-continuous axial levels of the CT images of a stone, covering 50% to 70% of the stone’s cross-sectional area. The average CT value of the three measurements was taken as the stone density. Detailed perioperative antibiotic regimen data were unavailable in this retrospective cohort and therefore could not be analyzed further.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was urosepsis. As per the Sepsis 3.0 criteria, urosepsis was diagnosed when the sepsis-related sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score increased by at least 2 points from the baseline and the urine culture yielded a positive result [15]. The secondary endpoints included SIRS, fever, and other complications. SIRS was defined as the presence of two or more of the following conditions: body temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C; heart rate > 90 beats per minute; respiratory rate > 20 breaths per minute or PaCO2 < 32 mmHg; peripheral blood WBC count > 12 × 109/L or < 4 × 109/L [16]. Fever was defined as a body temperature reaching or exceeding 37.3 °C. Because postoperative fever is a relatively sensitive and non-specific clinical finding, it was interpreted as an exploratory secondary outcome rather than a direct surrogate for clinically confirmed severe infection. All infectious complications were evaluated within 7 days subsequent to surgery. Other complications were evaluated in accordance with the Clavien-Dindo complication grading system [17].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], and the Student t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were employed for comparisons as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage, and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was utilized. To explore factors associated with postoperative fever, logistic regression analysis was performed. Given the limited number of postoperative fever events, covariates for the multivariable model were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and univariate association while considering the risk of overfitting. The final multivariable model included CD4+ T-cell count, gender, stone size, stone density, C-reactive protein (CRP), urine WBC count, urine culture, operation time and surgical treatment. Statistics were presented as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Sensitivity analysis was further conducted with PSM and IPTW to mitigate confounding bias. PSM was executed with a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching approach, and the caliper value was set at 0.02. The matching variables were identical to those employed in the logistic regression analysis. IPTW was computed based on the propensity score to re-weight the original sample, ensuring that the baseline characteristics of the two groups were balanced after weighting. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was deemed as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 120 patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in this study. The baseline characteristics of all the patients are presented in Table 1. Most of the patients were male (95.0%), aged 40–60 years (51.7%), and suffered from ureteral stones (72.5%). Preoperative urinary tract infections presented in 22 patients (18.3%). Among them, 20 cases had a preoperative CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL, compared with 100 cases with a CD4+ T-cell count ≥ 200 cells/µL. There were no statistically significant differences when comparing the baseline characteristics between the two groups of different CD4+ T-cell levels, except a higher CRP level in the < 200 cells/µL group (P = 0.023).

Postoperative outcomes

The overall postoperative rate of fever was 17.5% (21/120). The fever rates in the CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL group and ≥ 200 cells/µL group were 15.0% and 18.0%, respectively, without statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Neither urosepsis nor SIRS was observed in all patients. According to the Clavien-Dindo complication grading system, 90.0% of the patients had no complications or only grade I complications, and there was no significant difference in the complication status between the two groups (Table 2).

Univariate and multivariable analysis

Univariate analysis indicated that gender, urine WBC count, urine culture, stone size, stone density, operation time, and surgical approach were associated with postoperative fever (P < 0.05). Further, logistic regression analysis demonstrated that urine WBC count (OR = 1.011, 95% CI: 1.002–1.021, P = 0.020), stone density (OR = 6.314, 95% CI: 1.608–24.794, P = 0.008), and surgical approach (OR = 11.448, 95% CI: 2.221–59.009, P = 0.004) were independent risk factors for fever. In contrast, the preoperative CD4+ T-cell count did not predict postoperative fever (OR = 1.001, 95% CI: 0.998–1.003, P = 0.586) (see Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were conducted using the methods of PSM and IPTW to control for potential confounders and test the robustness of the conclusions. As presented in Table S1, a total of 38 patients were incorporated after matching with a 1:1 ratio in each group and exhibited balance in baseline characteristics. There were no statistically significant difference between the CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL group and the ≥ 200 cells/µL group regarding postoperative fever, urosepsis, and other complications (P > 0.05). The effective sample size after IPTW weighting amounted to 119 cases, and there were no notable differences in baseline characteristics between the two post-weighting groups (Table S2). Likewise, there were no statistically significant differences between the CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL and ≥ 200 cells/µL groups in terms of fever and other complications (P > 0.05). The findings of the sensitivity analysis were in accordance to those of logistic regression analysis.

Discussion

In this retrospective single-center cohort of HIV-infected patients undergoing endoscopic lithotripsy for upper urinary tract calculi, we did not observe a statistically significant association between preoperative CD4+ T-cell count and postoperative fever. However, no cases of postoperative urosepsis or SIRS occurred in the cohort; therefore, the primary endpoint could not be formally evaluated. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of no association.

A recent large-scale retrospective cohort study demonstrated that the postoperative complication rate of patients with CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL was not significantly different from that of patients with higher CD4+ T-cell count [18]. Previous studies have suggested that the CD4+ T-cell count serves as a predictor of postoperative complications in HIV-infected patients [3–5]. Nevertheless, these studies were mostly conducted when HAART was not widespread or optimized. In recent years, a growing body of evidence indicates that the surgical safety of HIV-infected patients has been significantly improved in the modern era of HAART therapy [13]. The safety is approaching that of non-HIV-infected patients. The predictive value of immune indicators, such as the CD4+ T-cell count, may be diminishing [12]. These findings directly challenge the old risk-assessment model based on a single threshold. It is recommended that optimized perioperative management, which includes ensuring viral suppression, preventing opportunistic infections, and meticulous medication management, is crucial for surgical safety [19].

However, the absence of an observed association between CD4+ T-cell count and postoperative fever should be interpreted cautiously. First, the effect estimate for CD4+ T-cell count in the multivariable model was very close to 1.0 (OR = 1.001 per cell/µL), suggesting that within the observed range of values, any per-cell change in fever risk was numerically very small and of limited immediate clinical interpretability. At the same time, this result does not establish that CD4+ T-cell count is clinically unimportant; rather, it indicates that in this dataset, CD4+ T-cell count alone was not a strong discriminator of postoperative fever. Future studies may benefit from evaluating CD4+ T-cell count using clinically interpretable increments or non-linear approaches.

The findings of this study can be ascribed to several reasons. Firstly, the optimized HAART therapy serves as a cornerstone for improving the surgical prognosis of HIV-infected patients. The majority of patients in this study underwent standardized HAART, which effectively suppressed the viral load. The effective suppression not only stabilizes or elevates the CD4+ T-cell count but also notably improves the overall immune function of the patients, thus reducing the risk of opportunistic infections and postoperative infectious complications. Secondly, the standardized perioperative management was of critical importance. For all stone patients, particularly those with immune deficiencies, we rigorously adhered to a comprehensive set of standard procedures. These included the prophylactic administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics prior to surgery, meticulous operation during surgery to minimize intrarenal pressure, and intensive monitoring after surgery. These measures might have effectively compensated for the patients’ inherent immune deficiencies and mitigated the potential risk of infection. Thirdly, the low CD4+ T-cell count in HIV patients primarily increases the risk of opportunistic infections, such as viral and fungal infections. However, the low T-cell count appears to have minimal impact on the common pathogenic bacteria of the urinary tract, such as Gram-negative bacilli. Lastly, minimally invasive surgeries utilized for urinary calculi caused less trauma to patients and less impact on patients’ immune status. Therefore, postoperative infectious complications were not significantly increased.

The present study has several important limitations. First, the primary endpoint of urosepsis could not be evaluated because no such events occurred, and this substantially limits the ability of the study to address its main clinical question. Second, the sample size was limited, particularly in the subgroup with CD4+ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL (n = 20), which reduced statistical power and increased the possibility of type II error. Third, postoperative fever was the main observed infectious outcome, but fever is a non-specific clinical finding and may not adequately represent clinically significant postoperative infection. Fourth, HIV viral load serves as another indicator for evaluating the status of HIV-infected individuals [20]. However, due to severely missing data of viral load in this cohort, it was not possible to incorporate viral load into the analysis. Fifth, the cohort combined patients undergoing PCNL and URS, which differ in baseline risk of postoperative infection, thereby introducing procedural heterogeneity. Because of the limited sample size, especially in the low-CD4 subgroup, procedure-specific subgroup analyses were not feasible. Sixth, detailed perioperative antibiotic regimen data were unavailable in this retrospective dataset. Finally, as a retrospective single-center study, the findings may not be generalizable to other settings or patient populations.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center cohort, preoperative CD4+ T-cell count was not significantly associated with postoperative fever after endoscopic lithotripsy in HIV-infected patients with upper urinary tract calculi. However, because no urosepsis events occurred, the primary endpoint could not be adequately assessed. In addition, the limited sample size, missing viral load data, procedural heterogeneity, and incomplete perioperative antibiotic data restrict interpretation. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory, and larger multicenter studies are required before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding perioperative decision-making based on CD4+ T-cell count in this clinical setting.
