Computing Expansions in Infinitely Many Cantor Real Bases via a Single Transducer
Émilie Charlier, Pierre Popoli, Michel Rigo

Abstract
. Representing real numbers using convenient numeration systems (integer bases, β \beta -numeration, Cantor bases, etc.) has been a longstanding mathematical challenge. This paper focuses on Cantor real bases and, specifically, on automatic Cantor real bases and the properties of expansions of real numbers in this setting. We develop a new approach where a single transducer associated with a fixed real number r r , computes the 𝐁 \mathbf{B} -expansion of r r but for an infinite family of Cantor real bases 𝐁 \mathbf{B} given as input. This point of view contrasts with traditional computational models for which the numeration system is fixed. Under some assumptions on the finitely many Pisot numbers occurring in the Cantor real base, we show that only a finite part of the transducer is visited. We obtain fundamental results on the structure of this transducer and on decidability problems about these expansions, proving that for certain classes of Cantor real bases, key combinatorial properties such as greediness of the expansion or periodicity can be decided algorithmically.

1.
Introduction

There are multiple ways to represent real numbers by bounded sequences of non-negative integers. We first recall the two classical definitions of
\beta
-numeration and Cantor base. Then, we show how Cantor real bases unify these two concepts. Finally, we detail our contributions to Cantor real bases.

1.1.
\beta
-expansions and Cantor bases

Let
\beta>1
be a real number. On the one hand, a
\beta
-representation
of a real number
r\in[0,1]
is a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-negative integers such that

(1)

r=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta^{n+1}}.

These representations were first studied by Rényi
[
Rén57
]
and Parry
[
Par60
]
with sequences taking values in
\{0,\ldots,\lceil\beta\rceil-1\}
. Whereas a real number usually has an uncountable number of
\beta
-representations
[
Sid03
]
, one of them is distinguished. It is computed by the greedy algorithm thanks to the so-called
\beta
-transformation

(2)

\mathsf{T}_{\beta}\colon[0,1]\to[0,1),\ r\mapsto\beta r-\lfloor\beta r\rfloor.

Note that we also defined this map on
1
, which is unusual; this choice was made intentionally because it will facilitate the presentation of the transducer
\mathcal{T}
in
Section

3
.
By letting
a_{n}=\lfloor\beta\mathsf{T}_{\beta}^{n}(r)\rfloor
for all
n\geq 0
, the obtained representation is called the
(greedy)
\beta
-expansion
of
r
and is denoted
d_{\beta}(r)
. It is maximal in lexicographic order among all
\beta
-representations of
r
and is characterized by the inequalities

(3)

\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta^{n+1}}<\frac{1}{\beta^{\ell}}\qquad\forall\ell\geq 1.

For a general reference on
\beta
-numeration systems, see
[
Lot02
, Chap. 6]
. For some related computational questions, see, for instance,
[
Hic14
,
HP16
,
AFH07
]
.

On the other hand, Cantor
[
Can69
]
introduced another type of expansion of real numbers based on a sequence
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of integers greater than or equal to
2
. Every
r\in[0,1)
has a unique expansion of the form

r=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{q_{0}\cdots q_{n}}

where
0\leq a_{n}<q_{n}
and
a_{n}\neq q_{n}-1
infinitely often.

1.2.
Cantor real base

The framework of Cantor real bases unifies
\beta
-expansions with a single real base
\beta
and Cantor bases built on a sequence of integers.

{definition}

A
Cantor real base
is a sequence
{\mathbf{B}}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers greater than
1
such that
\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\beta_{n}=+\infty
. If
{\mathbf{B}}
is a constant sequence, we recover the numeration systems in a real base
\beta
. If
{\mathbf{B}}
is a sequence of integers, we get back to classical Cantor bases.

We let
\val_{{\mathbf{B}}}\colon\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the (partial)
{\mathbf{B}}
-valuation
map, i.e., if
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of natural numbers, we set

\val_{{\mathbf{B}}}(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots):=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{n}}

provided that the series converges. A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
over
\mathbb{N}
is said to be a
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation
of
r\in\mathbb{R}
whenever
\val_{{\mathbf{B}}}(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots)=r
.

{definition}

[Greedy algorithm]
Let
r\in[0,1]
. Let
a_{0},\ldots,a_{N-1}
be the first
N\geq 0
digits of the greedy
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation of
r
. The next digit
a_{N}
of the greedy
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation of
r
is the largest integer such that

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{n}}\leq r.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the digit
a_{n}
belongs to the alphabet
\{0,\ldots,\lceil{\beta_{n}}\rceil-1\}
. The corresponding
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation is called the (greedy)
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion
of
r
, and is denoted by
d_{{\mathbf{B}}}(r)
.

The greedy algorithm can be used in order to obtain a distinguished
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation of
r\in[0,1]
: for all
n\geq 0
, we let
a_{n}=\lfloor\beta_{n}\mathsf{T}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{0}(r)\rfloor
. The corresponding
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation is called the (greedy)
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion
of
r
, and is denoted by
d_{{\mathbf{B}}}(r)
. It is maximal in the lexicographic order among all the
{\mathbf{B}}
-representations of
r
and it is characterized by the inequalities

(4)

\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{n}}<\frac{1}{\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{\ell-1}}\qquad\forall\ell\geq 1.

{definition}

[Alternate bases]
If the Cantor real base
{\mathbf{B}}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})^{\omega}
is a periodic sequence, we say that
{\mathbf{B}}
is an
alternate base
. These systems have been extensively studied in
[
CC21
,
CCK24
,
CCD23
,
CCMP23
]
. In this paper, we will also encounter a slightly more general case. If
{\mathbf{B}}
is ultimately periodic, we say that
{\mathbf{B}}
is an
ultimately alternate base
.

1.3.
Our contribution

Our initial motivation is to study Cantor real bases that are more general than the alternate ones. From the perspective of combinatorics on words, it seems natural to first consider sequences with low factor complexity that have a rich combinatorial structure. Thus, the initial approach of this work was to consider a Cantor real base defined by an automatic sequence. For details on automatic sequences, we refer to
[
AS03
]
.

Let
E
be a finite set, called
alphabet
, of real numbers greater than
1
. We consider Cantor real bases
{\mathbf{B}}
belonging to
E^{\mathbb{N}}
. In the initial spirit of Cantor, who considered only products of integers, we first examine automatic Cantor bases
{\mathbf{B}}
over an alphabet included in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. In that setting, with
Section

2.2
, our main result is the following. Consider a family of Cantor bases
{\mathbf{B}}
obtained as images under a monoid morphism
\psi
over an alphabet included in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, not necessarily uniform but with the property that there exists a constant
\delta
such that, for all letters
a
, the product of all letters occurring in
\psi(a)
is equal to
\delta
. In particular, this setting covers the Cantor bases obtained as images of Parikh-constant morphisms. If
{\mathbf{B}}=\psi(\mathbf{a})
where
\mathbf{a}
is any infinite word, then
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion of any real number
r
in
[0,1)
is obtained from the
\delta
-expansion of
r
by applying some well-defined morphisms in the order prescribed by the preimage infinite word
\mathbf{a}
. To simplify the reading of this paper and carry on intuition, in
Section

2
, we first address the case of a Thue–Morse Cantor base over
\{2,3\}
.
Then we prove that, for any fixed rational number
r\in[0,1)
, one can associate a single finite transducer that outputs the
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation of
r
for infinitely many Cantor bases given as input.

Section

3
contains, with
Theorem

3.1
, what we consider to be the main contribution of this work. Let
\delta
be an algebraic integer and let
E
be a finite alphabet of Pisot numbers sharing the same algebraic degree and belonging to
\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
. For all
r\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\cap[0,1]
, there exists a finite letter-to-letter transducer
†
†

A
Mathematica
implementation is available on
https://hdl.handle.net/2268/333750
.
which produces
d_{\mathbf{B}}(r)
when fed with any Cantor real base
{\mathbf{B}}\in E^{\mathbb{N}}
. This result changes the commonly used perspective. Typically, computational machines are designed for a fixed numeration system and operate on representations of infinitely many real numbers. In contrast, our original approach fixes a single number and considers its representations across infinitely many bases.

In the next section, under the same assumption as above, we show with
Section

4.2
that the Cantor real bases
{\mathbf{B}}\in E^{\mathbb{N}}
such that the quasi-greedy expansion
d_{{\mathbf{B}}}^{*}(r)
is ultimately periodic are exactly the ultimately alternate bases if and only if the transducer
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{E,r}
has a special form. Roughly speaking, it does not contain two closed walks with different inputs and the same output. This
2
-walk property is decidable and we discuss some examples. For instance, in
Section

4.2
we show that
d_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}(1)
is purely periodic for uncountably many aperiodic Cantor bases built from the smallest Pisot number.

Since the structure of the transducer determines the kinds of
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansions that we may have, it is natural to look at its connectedness. In the short
Section

5
, we give examples showing that all situations may appear: the Cantor bases being built with simple and/or non-simple Parry numbers.

Finally,
Section

6
explores questions related to decidability. Returning to our initial question when the Cantor base is automatic, we derive from our main theorem on the finiteness of the transducers that if
{\mathbf{B}}
is an automatic sequence, then many combinatorial properties of
d_{{\mathbf{B}}}^{*}(1)
, such as periodicity or power avoidance, become decidable. Actually, we may ask many questions considered by Shallit and his co-authors
[
Sha23
]
. This leads us to the following question: given an infinite word — typically an automatic sequence, since the key issue is how the word is specified using a finite amount of information — can we determine whether it is
{\mathbf{B}}
-admissible, i.e., a valid
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion? We give several cases where this question admits a positive resolution (for instance, including an example with a transcendental base). This section relies on Büchi’s theorem on the decidable first-order theory of
\langle\mathbb{N},+,V_{b}\rangle

[
Büc60
,
Sha23
]
.

2.
Cantor base over an alphabet of integers

The arguments developed in this section are rather straightforward. They rely on elementary arithmetic. However, these results highlight the flavor of this paper: obtaining the
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion of a real number by transforming an infinite word into another. In this paper, we use both the notation
\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\cdots
and
(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots)
to designate a Cantor real base
{\mathbf{B}}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The second will be mostly used whenever we need avoiding ambiguity between product of numbers and concatenation of letters.

2.1.
Working out a classical example

Consider the finite alphabet of numbers
E=\{2,3\}
and let
{\mathbf{T}}
be the celebrated Thue–Morse sequence

{\mathbf{T}}=23323223322323322\cdots,

over
E
starting with
2
, which is an infinite fixed point of the morphism

(5)

\tau\colon E\to E^{*},\ 2\mapsto 23,3\mapsto 32.

The general question is the following one:
What can be said about the structure of
{\mathbf{T}}
-expansions?
We give a first answer here and a second one with
Section

3.1
.

Every real number
r\in[0,1)
has a (usual)
6
-expansion
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the sense of (
1
) and (
3
).

{definition}

Every
c\in\{0,\ldots,5\}
can be uniquely decomposed as

c=a\cdot 3+b\quad\text{ with }\quad a\in\{0,1\},\ b\in\{0,1,2\}.

We define the morphism
h_{2}:c\mapsto ab
, i.e.,

h_{2}\colon 0\mapsto 00,\ 1\mapsto 01,\ 2\mapsto 02,\ 3\mapsto 10,\ 4\mapsto 11,\ 5\mapsto 12.

Similarly, every
c\in\{0,\ldots,5\}
can be uniquely decomposed as

c=a\cdot 2+b\quad\text{ with }\quad a\in\{0,1,2\},\ b\in\{0,1\}.

We define the morphism
h_{3}:c\mapsto ab
, i.e.,

h_{3}\colon 0\mapsto 00,\ 1\mapsto 01,\ 2\mapsto 10,\ 3\mapsto 11,\ 4\mapsto 20,\ 5\mapsto 21.

2.1
.

For all
r\in[0,1)
, the
{\mathbf{T}}
-expansion of
r
is the sequence

h_{2}(c_{0})h_{3}(c_{1})h_{3}(c_{2})h_{2}(c_{3})\cdots

obtained from
d_{6}(r)=c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}\cdots
by application of the morphisms
h_{2}
and
h_{3}
in the ordering given by the Thue–Morse word
{\mathbf{T}}
.

Proof.

Write
{\mathbf{T}}=(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and let

\mathbf{y}=y_{0}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots=h_{2}(c_{0})h_{3}(c_{1})h_{3}(c_{2})h_{2}(c_{3})\cdots h_{\beta_{n}}(c_{n})\cdots.

Otherwise stated, we set
h_{\beta_{n}}(c_{n})=y_{2n}y_{2n+1}
for all
n\geq 0
. We show that
\val_{\mathbf{T}}(\mathbf{y})=r
and

\forall\ell\geq 1,\quad\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}<\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\beta_{i}}

proving that
\mathbf{y}
is indeed get the
{\mathbf{T}}
-expansion of
r
. Let us first prove that
\mathbf{y}
is a
\mathbf{T}
-representation of
r
. We have

\displaystyle\val_{\mathbf{T}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{y_{2n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n}\beta_{i}}+\frac{y_{2n+1}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n+1}\beta_{i}}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{6^{n+1}}(y_{2n}\beta_{2n+1}+y_{2n+1}).

It suffices to show that
c_{n}=y_{2n}\beta_{2n+1}+y_{2n+1}
.
We have two cases to consider regarding the value of
\beta_{n}
. If
\beta_{n}=2
then, on the one hand,
c_{n}=y_{2n}\cdot 3+y_{2n+1}
and on the other hand,
\tau(\beta_{n})=\beta_{2n}\beta_{2n+1}=23
, hence
\beta_{2n+1}=3
. Similarly, if
\beta_{n}=3
then
c_{n}=y_{2n}\cdot 2+y_{2n+1}
and
\beta_{2n+1}=2
since
\tau(\beta_{n})=\beta_{2n}\beta_{2n+1}=32
.

We now turn to the greediness of the
\mathbf{T}
-representation. Let us first assume that the tail is starting with an even index
2\ell
. Then

\sum_{n=2\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}=\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{y_{2n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n}\beta_{i}}+\frac{y_{2n+1}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n+1}\beta_{i}}\right)=\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{6^{n+1}}<\frac{1}{6^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{2\ell-1}\beta_{i}},

where we used the fact that
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the greedy
\delta
-expansion of
r
. Assume now that the tail is starting with an odd index
2\ell+1
. We get

\sum_{n=2\ell+1}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}=\frac{y_{2\ell+1}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2\ell+1}\beta_{i}}+\sum_{n=\ell+1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{y_{2n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n}\beta_{i}}+\frac{y_{2n+1}}{\prod_{i=0}^{2n+1}\beta_{i}}\right)=\frac{y_{2\ell+1}}{6^{\ell}}+\sum_{n\geq\ell+1}\frac{c_{n}}{6^{n+1}}.

Again, we have two cases. If
\beta_{\ell}=2
then

y_{2\ell+1}\leq 2
by definition of the morphism
h_{2}
. Using that
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\delta
-expansion and that
\beta_{2\ell}=2
, we see that the above quantity is less than

\frac{2}{6^{\ell+1}}+\frac{1}{6^{\ell+1}}=\frac{3}{2\cdot 3\cdot 6^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2\ell}\cdot 6^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{2\ell}\beta_{i}}.

If
\beta_{\ell}=3
then
y_{2\ell+1}\leq 1
by definition of the morphism
h_{3}
. Similarly, the above quantity is less than

\frac{1}{6^{\ell+1}}+\frac{1}{6^{\ell+1}}=\frac{2}{2\cdot 3\cdot 6^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2\ell}\cdot 6^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{2\ell}\beta_{i}}.

∎

2.2.
Generalization to Cantor bases built from blocks of constant product

The Thue–Morse case can be readily generalized to the following family of Cantor bases.

{definition}

Let
\delta\geq 2
be an integer. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\ell_{k}\geq 1
be an integer and
\gamma_{k,0},\ldots,\gamma_{k,\ell_{k}-1}\geq 2
be integers whose product equal to
\delta
:

(6)

\prod_{j=0}^{\ell_{k}-1}\gamma_{k,j}=\delta.

Let
r
be a real number in
[0,1]
with
d_{\delta}(r)=c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, every
c_{k}
belongs to
\{0,\ldots,\delta-1\}
and can be uniquely written as

(7)

c_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell_{k}-1}a_{k,j}\cdot\gamma_{k,j+1}\cdots\gamma_{k,\ell_{k}-1}

where for each
j
, the coefficient
a_{k,j}
is a non-negative integer less than
\gamma_{k,j}
.

2.2
.

With the notation of
Section

2.2
, consider the Cantor base

{\mathbf{B}}=\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\cdots=\prod_{k=0}^{+\infty}(\gamma_{k,0}\cdots\gamma_{k,\ell_{k}-1})

(where the product of the right-hand side is the concatenation product). Then the
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion of
r
is the sequence

d_{{\mathbf{B}}}(r)=\prod_{k=0}^{+\infty}(a_{k,0}\cdots a_{k,\ell_{k}-1}).

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as the proof of
Theorem

2.1
. We have
d_{\delta}(r)=c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots
, and we let

\mathbf{y}=y_{0}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots=\prod_{k=0}^{+\infty}(a_{k,0}\cdots a_{k,\ell_{k}-1}).

First, we show that
\mathbf{y}
is a
{\mathbf{B}}
-representation of
r
. By decomposing into blocks of length
\ell_{k}
and by using (
6
) and (
7
), we compute

\displaystyle\val_{{\mathbf{B}}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell_{k}-1}\frac{y_{\ell_{0}+\cdots+\ell_{k-1}+j}}{\prod_{i=0}^{\ell_{0}+\cdots+\ell_{k-1}+j}\beta_{i}}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\delta^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell_{k}-1}\frac{a_{k,j}}{\prod_{i=0}^{j}\gamma_{k,i}},

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\delta^{k+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell_{k}-1}(a_{k,j}\cdot\gamma_{k,j+1}\cdots\gamma_{k,\ell_{k}-1})=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{k}}{\delta^{k+1}}=r.

Now let us check the greediness of the expansion, i.e., the inequalities given in (
4
). Let
\ell\geq 1
. We have to prove that

\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}<\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\beta_{i}}.

Again, we need to decompose our computation into blocks of length
\ell_{k}
. There exists a unique
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ell_{k}\leq\ell<\sum_{k=0}^{K}\ell_{k}.

Let

\ell^{\prime}=\ell-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ell_{k}.

Thus,
\ell^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,\ell_{K}-1\}
. By the same reasoning as above, we get

\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}=\frac{1}{\delta^{K+1}}\sum_{j=\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell_{K}-1}a_{K,j}\cdot\gamma_{K,j+1}\cdots\gamma_{K,\ell_{K}-1}+\sum_{k=K+1}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{k}}{\delta^{k+1}}.

Since
c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots
is the
\delta
-expansion of
r
, we get from (
3
) that

\sum_{k=K+1}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{k}}{\delta^{k+1}}<\frac{1}{\delta^{K+1}}.

The greediness of (
7
) implies that

\sum_{j=\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell_{K}-1}a_{K,j}\cdot\gamma_{K,j+1}\cdots\gamma_{K,\ell_{K}-1}\leq\gamma_{K,\ell^{\prime}}\cdots\gamma_{K,\ell_{K}-1}-1.

Putting together the latter two inequalities, we get

\sum_{n=\ell}^{+\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}\beta_{i}}<\frac{\gamma_{K,\ell^{\prime}}\cdots\gamma_{K,\ell_{K}-1}}{\delta^{K+1}}=\frac{1}{\delta^{K}\gamma_{K,0}\cdots\gamma_{K,\ell^{\prime}-1}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\beta_{i}}.

∎

As a particular case, this result applies to the following situation.

{definition}

\delta\geq 2
be an integer, and let

a\in A

\displaystyle 61=2\cdot 24+1\cdot 12+0\cdot 3+1,

\displaystyle 61=3\cdot 18+1\cdot 6+0\cdot 2+1,

,

{corollary}

With the notation of

{\mathbf{B}}=\psi(a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots)

is the sequence

h_{a_{0}}(c_{0})h_{a_{1}}(c_{1})h_{a_{2}}(c_{2})\cdots

a\in A

Observe that in

h_{2}

[Greedy transducer]

•

•

[Quasi-greedy transducer]

{definition}

The following norm is also called (discrete) supremum norm, Chebyshev norm or max norm when the supremum is in fact a maximum.

{definition}

[Max norm]

Let
d\geq 1
and let
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
be a
d
-tuple of pairwise distinct complex numbers.
On the finite dimensional vector space
\mathbb{R}_{<d}[X]
of polynomials of degree less than
d
, we define a norm by

\lvert\lvert P\rvert\rvert_{z}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|P(z_{i})|.

Verifying that it is a norm is straightforward. Note that positive definiteness follows from the fundamental theorem of algebra.

For an algebraic number
\delta
of degree
d
, we let
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
denote its minimal polynomial. The field extension
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}[X]/\langle\mathcal{M}_{\delta}\rangle
which can be identified with the set
\mathbb{Q}_{<d}[\delta]
of polynomials in
\delta
of degree less than
d
where the operations are performed modulo
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
. If moreover
\delta
is an algebraic integer, then there is also a ring isomorphism between
\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
and
\mathbb{Z}[X]/\langle\mathcal{M}_{\delta}\rangle
which can be identified with
\mathbb{Z}_{<d}[\delta]
. Indeed, in this case, the minimal polynomial
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
is a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. By Euclidean division, any polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
can be uniquely written as
P=Q\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\delta}+R
for some
R\in\mathbb{Z}_{<d}[\delta]
. If
\delta=\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{d}
are the Galois conjugates of
\delta
, i.e., the roots of the minimal polynomial
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
, then, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, evaluation at
\delta_{i}
gives

P(\delta_{i})=R(\delta_{i})
. This explains one of the assumption of the forthcoming
Theorem

3.1
.

Moreover
\mathbb{Z}_{<d}[X]
is a subset of the vector space
\mathbb{R}_{<d}[X]
. So it can be equipped with a norm as in
Section

3.1
for any choice of
d
complex numbers. Nevertheless, because of the equality
P(\delta_{i})=R(\delta_{i})
, we will consider the norm associated with the particular

-tuple
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{d})
.

{lemma}

Let
\delta
be an algebraic number of degree

. The number

and its conjugates belong to

. In particular, if

Let

be the splitting field of the minimal polynomial of

For all conjugates

We recall some well-known facts on Pisot numbers. For the sake of completeness, we sketch a proof.

{lemma}

Every real algebraic number field
K
of degree
d
contains a Pisot number of degree
d
. Moreover, the set of all Pisot numbers of degree
d
in
K
is closed under multiplication.

Proof.

The first part of this result can be found in
[
Sal63
, Thm. 2]
(it relies on Minkowski’s theorem on linear forms).

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two Pisot numbers of degree
d
belonging to
K
and let us prove that
\gamma=\alpha\beta
is also a Pisot number. We have
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
, hence
\beta=P(\alpha)
where
P\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
. Thus, if the conjugates of
\alpha
are
\alpha_{1}=\alpha,\alpha_{2}\ldots,\alpha_{d}
, then by

3.1
, the conjugates of
\beta
are exactly given by

(since

is of degree

) and the conjugates of
\gamma
are among
\alpha_{1}P(\alpha_{1})=\gamma,\alpha_{2}P(\alpha_{2}),\ldots,\alpha_{d}P(\alpha_{d})
. Clearly,

. Since

for

and since
\gamma
is an algebraic integer (the algebraic integers form a ring), we get that
\gamma
is a Pisot number. Let us argue that

still has degree

G:=(X-\alpha_{1}P(\alpha_{1}))\cdots(X-\alpha_{d}P(\alpha_{d}))

has rational coefficients (since each of its coefficients can be expressed as a symmetric polynomial in
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
with rational coefficients, and since the polynomial
(X-\alpha_{1})\cdots(X-\alpha_{n})
has rational — and even integral — coefficients). Since all its roots

4.1.
Relationship with an extension of a theorem of Schmidt to alternate bases

For an alternate base

have periodic

-expansions, then

]

is a Pisot number and the bases

having an ultimately periodic

-expansion is precisely

.

A family of length-

alternate bases with the property that all rational numbers in

have purely periodic
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansions is given in
[
MP24
]
. In our setting, by using our graph-theoretical approach, we can easily show that for any ultimately alternate base

over a set of bases

satisfying the assumptions of

3.1
, all numbers in

have periodic
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansions. The novelty here lies in the used technique, since this result is in fact a direct consequence of the above-mentioned extension of Schmidt’s theorem obtained in
[

]
. Indeed, given an alternate base
{\mathbf{B}}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})^{\omega}
, Lemma
3.1
implies that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
are stronger than asking that the product
\beta_{0}\cdots\beta_{p-1}
is a Pisot number and

.

\delta
be an algebraic integer of degree

and let

in (

3.1

6.
Decidability

The main question discussed in this section is the following one. Given an infinite word (typically, an automatic sequence) can we decide whether it is a valid
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion?

6.1.
Some background

To be self-contained, we briefly recap some classical results about first-order logic. We refer the reader to
[
BHMV94
,
Rig14
]
for details.

A
structure

\mathcal{S}=\langle D,(R_{i})_{i}\rangle
consists of a domain
D
and relations
R_{i}
on
D
.
A first-order formula with no free variables is a
sentence
.
The
first-order theory

\text{Th}(\mathcal{S})
(also denoted by
\text{FO}(\mathcal{S})
in other contexts) of a structure
\mathcal{S}
is the set of true sentences of
\mathcal{S}
. It is
decidable
, if there exists an algorithm that may decide if any sentence in
\mathcal{S}
is true or false. Otherwise, it is
undecidable
.

Let
b\geq 2
be an integer. We let
V_{b}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be the map defined by
V_{b}(0)=1
and for
n\geq 1
,
V_{b}(n)=i
if
b^{i}
is the greatest power of
b
that divides
n
. We are interested in a particular structure of domain
\mathbb{N}
and with relations given by the graphs of
+
and
V_{b}
.
A fundamental result of Büchi is that
\text{Th}(\langle\mathbb{N},+,V_{b}\rangle)
is decidable
[
Büc60
]
. This implies that many properties of
b
-automatic sequences are decidable. For a comprehensive presentation, we refer the reader to
[
Sha23
]
. We state the main result that we will use and, which is in essence Büchi’s theorem
[
Sha23
, Thm. 6.4.1]

6.1
.

There is an algorithm that, given a formula
\phi
with no free
variables, phrased in first-order logic, using only the universal and existential
quantifiers, addition and subtraction of variables and constants, logical operations, comparisons, and indexing into a given automatic sequence
\mathbf{x}
, will decide the truth of that formula.

This result is generalized to a larger family of numeration systems: those such that addition can be computed by a finite automaton and for which the set of greedy expansions of integers is regular. Hence the results that we will present are straightforward to adapt to this class. For instance, one can consider a sequence such as the Fibonacci word and, more generally, sequences that are automatic with respect to a Pisot numeration system
[
BH97
,
Rig14
]
.

Putting together
Theorem

6.1
and
Section

3.1
, if
{\mathbf{B}}
is an automatic sequence, then we can decide many combinatorial properties of
d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)
such as periodicity, avoiding powers, etc.

6.2.
A general (and technical) result

An infinite sequence
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-negative integers is said to be
{\mathbf{B}}
-admissible if
\mathbf{a}=d_{{\mathbf{B}}}(x)
for some
x\in[0,1)
. The quasi-greedy expansions of
1
can be used to determine if an infinite sequence
\mathbf{a}
is
{\mathbf{B}}
-admissible as expressed by the following result generalizing Parry’s theorem to Cantor real bases.

6.2
[
CC21
]
.

Let
{\mathbf{B}}
be a Cantor real base. An infinite sequence
\mathbf{a}
over
\mathbb{N}
is
{\mathbf{B}}
-admissible if and only if

\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{a})<_{\mathrm{{\mathrm{lex}}}}d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Parry’s conditions from
Theorem

6.2
are far from being trivial to test, even in a simple situation where the Cantor base is the Thue–Morse word
\mathbf{T}=2332\cdots
over
\{2,3\}
. While the set
\{\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{B}):n\geq 0\}
is finite whenever
{\mathbf{B}}
is an alternate base, the set
\{\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{T}):n\geq 0\}
is infinite since
\mathbf{T}
is aperiodic. This explains why the case of alternate bases is simpler to handle.

Furthermore, the set
\{d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1):n\geq 0\}
does not seem to have any simple expression. The following technical proposition asserts a decidable question for an automatic development in a Cantor real base. Later on, we will provide three applications of this result.

6.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}_{>1}
be a finite alphabet of real bases, let
{\mathbf{B}}\in E^{\mathbb{N}}
be a Cantor real base, and let
b\geq 2
be an integer base. Let
A
be the finite alphabet of digits that can occur in a
{\mathbf{B}}
-expansion, and assume that, for all
a\in A
, we are given a first-order formula
\varphi_{a}(j,n)
of the structure
\langle\mathbb{N}+,V_{b}\rangle
that is true if and only if
[d^{*}_{\sigma^{n}{({\mathbf{B}})}}(1)]_{j}=a
, i.e., the digit at position
j
in
d^{*}_{\sigma^{n}{({\mathbf{B}})}}(1)
is
a
.
Then it is decidable whether any given
b
-automatic sequence over
\mathbb{N}
is
{\mathbf{B}}
-admissible.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{a}=a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
be a
b
-automatic sequence over
\mathbb{N}
.
Due to
Theorem

6.2
, we have to check that

(10)

\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{a})<_{{\mathrm{lex}}}d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

For two sequences
\mathbf{u}=u_{0}u_{1}\cdots
and
\mathbf{v}=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots
, the lexicographic inequality
\mathbf{u}<_{{\mathrm{lex}}}\mathbf{v}
can be expressed as

(\exists j)\left[u_{j}<v_{j}\wedge(\forall i<j)(u_{i}=v_{i})\right].

So, the Parry conditions (
10
) become

(\forall n)(\exists j)\left[a_{n+j}<[d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)]_{j}\wedge(\forall i<j)(a_{n+i}=[d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)]_{i})\right].

The inequality
a_{n+j}<[d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)]_{j}

can be expressed as the first-order formula

\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,a^{\prime}\in A\\
a<a^{\prime}\end{subarray}}[a_{n+j}=a\wedge\varphi_{a^{\prime}}(j,n)]

while the equality
a_{n+i}=[d_{\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{B}})}^{*}(1)]_{i}
can be expressed as the first-order formula

\bigvee_{a\in A}[a_{n+i}=a\wedge\varphi_{a}(i,n)]

Therefore, we may apply
Theorem

6.1
and conclude with the proof.
∎

Deciding with an alternate base

{corollary}

Let
{\mathbf{B}}

{\mathbf{B}}

Proof.

Using

of the structure

that is true if and only of

by

\bigvee_{i=0}^{p-1}\left(\big[(\exists q)(n=q\cdot p+i)\big]\wedge\psi_{i,a}(j)\right).

∎

Parry alternate bases
are alternate base

with the property that all quasi-greedy
\sigma^{i}({\mathbf{B}})
-expansions of

]

6.3

-automatic for all

.
Also, note that one can easily extend this corollary to the case of an ultimately alternate base. The formula is straightforward to adapt.

6.4.
Deciding with a single transcendental base

Section

6.3
permits us to consider some decision problems that were not considered before in the framework of
\beta
-numeration systems. Interestingly, it applies to some transcendental base
\beta

-automatic sequence is
