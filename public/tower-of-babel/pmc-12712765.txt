Osteocytes under Mechanical Loading Regulate PC‐3 Cancer Cell–Mineral Interactions during Early‐Stage Metastasis to Bone
Authors: Kimberly Seaman, Bryan Guo, William W Du, Burton Yang, Yu Sun, Lidan You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone metastasis causes severe complications for patients suffering from advanced‐stage prostate cancer. Exercise is recommended to help maintain musculoskeletal health during treatment. As primary mechanosensors and regulators of bone homeostasis, osteocytes recently investigate for their roles in prostate cancer bone metastasis. Recently, in vivo studies show that exercise mitigates prostate tumor progression and preserves bone structure. In contrast, in vitro studies indicate direct prostate cancer–osteocyte interactions under mechanical loading conditions promote prostate cancer growth and migration but exclude other host cells present in the metastatic bone microenvironment. Thus, these in vitro findings are not consistent with recent in vivo results. In this study, the role of mechanically stimulated osteocytes during the initial stages of metastatis in osteoblast‐rich areas is examined. When treated with conditioned media from flow‐stimulated osteocytes, osteoblasts reduce PC‐3 wound healing migration and invasion compared to static controls. Of interest, osteoblasts treated with flow‐stimulated MLO‐Y4 and primary osteocyte conditioned media suppress PC‐3 cancer cell growth, alter cancer cell morphology, and preserve mineralized matrix in a microfluidic co‐culture assay. Overall, the inhibitory role of mechanical loading of osteocytes on the early‐stage metastasis of the endosteal surface during prostate cancer bone metastasis is demonstrated.Keywords: mechanobiology, microfluidics, osteoblasts, osteocytes, prostate cancers

Introduction

Advanced‐stage prostate cancers have a high tendency to metastasize to bone due to the creation of a favorable microenvironment for cancer cells during the normal bone remodeling process.[ 1 , 2 ] Specifically, growth factors and cytokines released during bone remodeling enable the establishment of metastatic prostate tumors in bone tissue. Circulating prostate cancer cells that have detached from the primary tumor extravasate through the endothelial barrier and home to bone tissue using the tastromal cell‐derived factor‐1/C‐X‐C chemokine receptor type 4 signaling pathway.[ 3 ] Metastatic prostate cancer cells then disrupt normal bone homeostasis by secreting factors that simultaneously stimulate both osteoblastic and osteoclastic activities by releasing transforming growth factor beta and endothelin‐1 to promote osteogenic differentiation and activity, as well as receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa‐B ligand and macrophage colony‐stimulating factor to induce osteoclastogenesis.[ 2 ] In return, bone effector cells secrete interleukin 6 (IL‐6), vascular endothelial growth factor, and insulin‐like growth factor‐1 to promote the growth and survival of prostate cancer cells at the secondary site.[ 2 , 4 , 5 ]

In contrast to other osteotropic tumors such as breast cancers that result in the formation of cancer‐induced osteolysis, prostate cancers form mixed metastatic lesions that are mostly osteoblastic but contain a concurrent osteolytic component.[ 5 ] A generally accepted theory on prostate cancer bone metastasis stipulates that osteolytic lesions are first produced during the initial stages of metastasis, and then switch to a more osteoblastic phenotype once activated osteoclasts degrade a sufficient quantity of healthy bone tissue.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] However, the specific mechanism(s) behind the formation of osteoblastic and/or osteolytic lesions have not yet been elucidated. Nonetheless, osteoblastic bone lesions characteristic of prostate cancer bone metastases result in uncontrolled bone formation, where newly formed bone tissue has reduced mechanical properties, such as a lower Young's modulus and toughness due to the abnormal alignment of collagen fibers and hydroxyapatite crystals.[ 9 ] This ultimately results in an elevated risk of skeletal‐related events and a severely compromised quality of life for patients suffering from bone metastases.[ 10 ]

Current standard‐of‐care therapies for patients suffering from prostate cancer bone metastases, such as chemotherapies and radiation can slow tumor progression, but do not provide a cure.[ 10 ] Moreover, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) that is typically administered in combination with standard treatments induces bone loss.[ 11 , 12 ] A recent study by Gibson et al. implemented a finite element analysis to show that ADT reduces the mechanical and densiometric properties of vertebrae.[ 11 ] Another study by Handforth et al. demonstrated that ADT reduced estimated bone strength and mineral density and caused a decline in the microstructural properties of bone.[ 12 ] Therefore, exercise has often been recommended for patients to mitigate adverse side effects from therapies, maintain musculoskeletal health, and improve quality of life.[ 13 ] Several pioneering studies have examined the role of exercise in breast cancer bone metastasis progression.[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] In the context of prostate cancers, a few recent in vivo studies investigating the role of physical activity have suggested that exercise could provide preventative or anti‐tumoral effects on the development and progression of this disease.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] Initial studies investigating the role of dynamic tibial loading on prostate cancer bone metastasis demonstrated that loading reduces the incidence of metastases and tumor burden while preserving various bone structure parameters in murine models.[ 19 , 20 ] Several studies, including Arredondo, who studied the role of mechanical loading on prostate cancer bone metastasis using male immunocompromised mice, surmised that dynamic tibial loading produces an osteogenic response in bone which helps protect bone from the establishment of the vicious cycle of metastasis.[ 14 , 16 , 19 , 20 ] Arredondo also showed that mechanical loading downregulates Dickkopf 1 (DKK1), a Wnt inhibitor expressed by osteocytes and osteoblasts.[ 19 ] A recent study of interest by Feng et al. demonstrated that tibial mechanical loading results in breast and prostate tumor suppression using mechanisms both dependent and independent of osteocytic lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 5, a Wnt‐β‐catenin protein receptor.[ 20 ]

Osteocytes comprise of 95% of cells found in bone tissue and function as the primary mechanosensors of the bone microenvironment.[ 21 , 22 ] These cells transduce mechanical cues into biological signals using several cellular mechanisms to control other cells in their surroundings, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and endothelial cells.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] Osteocytes subjected to mechanical loading have only been recently investigated for their role in prostate cancer bone metastasis, with in vitro studies in the literature presenting conflicting results.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] A study from Verbruggen et al. demonstrated that mechanical loading of osteocytes stimulated the migration of PC‐3 prostate cancer cells using a microfluidic platform.[ 25 ] On the other hand, a study by van Santen et al. demonstrated that human osteocyte‐like cells subjected to pulsatile fluid flow inhibited DU‐145 invasion and modulated the expression of epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition markers. Regarding interactions between prostate cancer cells and osteocytes only, conflicting results presented in the literature are most likely due to variations in experimental setup, loading regimes (magnitude, frequency, and duration), and the prostate cancer cell lines used, in addition to the stage of bone metastasis these in vitro studies aim to model. A number of studies have also investigated prostate cancer‐osteoblast interactions using novel microfluidic platforms, or 3D scaffolds (with cells or decellularized) to mimic the mineralized bone environment due to the osteoblastic nature of prostate cancers.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] Of note, Bischel et al. used a microfluidic device to examine C4‐2B prostate cancer cells and MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblast signaling and observed that co‐culture conditions with pre‐osteoblasts induced the formation of cancer cell protrusions, indicative of a more invasive phenotype.[ 29 ] Bock et al. developed a physiologically relevant model of bone metastasis using tissue engineering strategies to study the effects of anti‐androgen therapies in metastatic prostate cancers.[ 28 , 32 ] Reichert et al. used primary human osteoblast matrices to observe the interactions of prostate cancer cells with the mineralized bone microenvironment.[ 31 ] However, none of these in vitro studies have considered the effects of osteocytes, which are crucial for regulating osteoblast activity.

Over the past few years, our research group has strategically implemented the use of in vitro double‐conditioned media assays to examine cancer–osteocyte interactions under mechanical loading conditions during the initial phases of breast cancer bone metastasis, while incorporating the effects of other host cells present in the metastatic bone niche.[ 33 , 34 ] Previous studies from our group have investigated the role of osteocytes subjected to oscillatory fluid flow (OFF) at physiological levels of shear stress on both endothelial cells and osteoclasts in the context of breast cancer bone metastasis.[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] Studies by Ma et al. and Cheung et al. demonstrated that flow‐stimulated osteocytes modulate endothelial adhesion molecule expression and reduce endothelial permeability.[ 33 , 36 ] Moreover, Ma et al. demonstrated that flow‐stimulated osteocytes suppress osteoclastogenesis, which in turn suppresses MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cell migration and enhances cancer cell apoptosis.[ 34 ] To interrogate real‐time cell signaling between osteocytes, endothelial cells, and breast cancer cells, Mei et al. developed a microfluidic co‐culture platform that can recapitulate cancer cell extravasation to bone.[ 35 ]

In this study, we present evidence of the regulation of prostate cancer cell metastatic behaviors by OFF‐stimulated osteocytes via signaling to mineralized osteoblasts. Specifically, we investigated the initial phase of the metastasis to bone where prostate cancer cells have already extravasated through the endothelial barrier but have yet to establish the secondary tumor in osteoblast‐rich areas. To study bi‐directional signaling between prostate cancer cells and osteoblast populations based on the results of our standard in vitro conditioned media assays, we created a novel co‐culture assay using commercially available ibidi µ‐slide channels to mimic and probe interactions between mature mineralized osteoblasts and PC‐3 prostate cancer cells on the endosteal surface of bone. We then leveraged this microfluidic co‐culture assay to observe a number of outcome variables over the course of 72 h, such as PC‐3 prostate cancer cell growth, morphology, directional orientation, as well as endpoint analyses of bone mineral produced by differentiated osteoblasts.

Results

Osteocytes Regulate PC‐3 Cell Behaviors through Osteoblasts in Conditioned Media Assays

Prostate cancers tend to metastasize in osteoblast‐rich areas on the endosteal surface due to the stromal‐derived factor 1 (also known as CXCL12)/CXCR4 signaling axis which drives PCa cell homing to bone.[ 3 ] Prostate cancer cells extravasate through the endothelial barrier then establish the vicious cycle of bone metastasis by signaling to bone effector cells. We first investigated whether soluble factors released by osteocytes in static or loading (OFF) conditions could modulate prostate cancer cell migration and invasion toward the endosteal surface of bone with osteoblasts as an intermediary. Here, differentiated MC3T3‐E1 osteoblast cultures were treated with MLO‐Y4 osteocyte conditioned media (CM) for 72 h. MLO‐Y4 CM used to treat osteoblasts were collected from samples that had undergone OFF or remained static. Following treatment with MLO‐Y4 CM in either static or OFF (mechanical loading) conditions, MC3T3‐E1 cultures were treated with fresh growth media (without addition of osteogenic factors, and the osteoblast CM were collected after 24 h. To assess cancer cell migration, we performed a would healing migration assay where PC‐3 monolayers were scratched with a p10 pipette tip and treated with osteoblast CM for 12 hours (Figure 1A). Of interest, at the 12‐hour timepoint, the gap closure in loading groups was significantly reduced (p < 0.05) compared to that of the static controls. Specifically, the average wound healing gap closure in control groups was measured at 34.1%, static groups had a gap closure of 25.8% whereas OFF groups had a gap closure of 16.4% (Figure 1B). Control groups for the wound healing migration assay involved osteoblast CM collected from samples that were not treated with any MLO‐Y4 osteocyte CM. To determine the indirect role of mechanical loading of osteocytes on the invasive properties of PC‐3 cancer cells, invasion assays were performed by polymerizing Matrigel in Transwells, then allowing PC‐3 cancer cells in the top compartment to invade toward osteoblast CM over 24 h. We observed a 41% reduction in PC‐3 invasion when osteocytes were subjected to OFF compared to static control groups (Figure 1C,D).

Mechanical Loading of Osteocytes Regulates PC‐3/Osteoblast Interactions in a Microfluidic Co‐Culture Assay

Given that conditioned media assays only represent one‐way interactions between cell populations, we wanted to examine the effects of osteocyte mechanical loading when both PC‐3 cells and osteoblasts were present. Several studies have developed prostate cancer–bone mineral interactions with or without osteoblasts using 3D tissue‐engineered scaffolds.[ 8 , 31 , 32 ] Bischel et al. developed a microfluidic platform to examine signaling between prostate cancer cells and MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblasts, however, this platform did not incorporate mature or mineralized osteoblasts. Therefore, we developed a microfluidic co‐culture platform that permits the mineralization of osteoblasts to best mimic the endosteal surface of bone (Figure 2A). We utilized ibidi µ‐slide channels to reduce the use of osteogenic differentiation reagents and to precisely control cancer cell seeding on the mineralized osteoblast matrix (Figure 2B).

Schematic and experimental workflow of prostate cancer‐osteoblast direct contact co‐cultures. A) Cross‐section of co‐culture setup, where PC‐3 prostate cancer cells are seeded on top of mineralized osteoblasts. B) Experimental workflow beginning from osteoblast differentiation and mineralization to endpoint alizarin staining after co‐culture with cancer cells to confirm mineralization.

Once osteoblast mineralization was achieved via the formation of small calcium nodules along the length of the channel, mineralized osteoblast cultures were conditioned in MLO‐Y4 osteocyte CM from static or loading (OFF) groups for 3 days. Osteoblasts were treated with the osteocyte CM daily. The osteocyte CM was collected from MLO‐Y4 cells that were subjected to OFF in parallel plate flow chambers or were placed in flow chambers without mechanical loading (static groups). After 3 days of conditioning, the osteoblast samples were conditioned in osteocyte CM, PC‐3 cancer cells were then seeded into the channels and imaged every 24 h over the course of 3 days. To confirm mineralization after co‐culture, alizarin red staining was performed to visualize calcium deposits by osteoblasts.

In microfluidic co‐culture, PC‐3 cancer cells took on an elongated morphology compared to typical monoculture conditions 24 h after seeding cancer cells onto the mineralized osteoblast matrices (Figure 3A,C). The elongated cell morphology is characteristic of a mesenchymal cancer cell phenotype associated with enhanced invasion and motility.[ 37 ] This observation aligns with a previous study investigating prostate cancer–mineral interactions using decellularized bone scaffolds.[ 31 ] PC‐3 cancer cells also appeared to be oriented in a similar general direction (Figure 3A). Of particular interest, PC‐3 cell growth was attenuated when osteoblasts were conditioned in MLO‐Y4 osteocyte CM from loading groups (Figure 3B) when compared with their static counterparts. The average number of cells per image in static conditions at the 72‐hour timepoint was 111.5 versus 92.9 in OFF conditions, resulting in a 16.7% reduction in cell growth in OFF‐stimulated groups. To quantify cell morphologies, the aspect ratio of PC‐3 cells was measured using ImageJ. Static controls had significantly more elongated cell morphologies compared to groups that were conditioned with osteocyte CM from OFF during the first 48 h of co‐culture (Day 1, average cell aspect ratio of 6.2 in static groups, 4.9 in OFF groups; Day 2, cell aspect ratio of 5.6 vs 4.9 in OFF groups). In co‐cultures, PC‐3 cells in both static and loading groups had significantly elevated aspect ratios compared to the monoculture control group (p < 0.0001). After 72 h of co‐culture, samples were fixed and stained with alizarin red. Although staining was initially performed to confirm and observe the formation of osteoblastic calcium deposits, we also observed a notable decrease in the mineralized area in static groups compared to loading groups (Figure 3E,F). While the PC‐3 cell line in vivo recruits and signals to osteoclasts to induce osteolytic lesions,[ 38 ] in vitro studies on PC‐3 bone metastasis have shown that this particular cell line can directly degrade both unmineralized and mineralized bone matrices, as well as bone fragments via the secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)‐1,2, and 9.[ 39 ]

Control groups were added to microfluidic co‐culture experiments with osteoblasts, both of which were not treated with MLO‐Y4 CM. Controls were treated with a 1:1 mixture of osteogenic media and fresh MLO‐Y4 culture media. This was done to understand whether the increased mineralized area in OFF groups was due to regulatory effects from the mechanical loading of osteocytes. Here, the positive control group included the addition of PC‐3 prostate cancer cells, whereas the negative control group did not include the addition of any PC‐3 cancer cells. Thus, the only cell type being cultured in these negative control samples was the mineralized osteoblasts. In control groups with or without the addition of PC‐3 cancer cells, PC‐3‐positive controls tended to have lower mineralized areas, compared to their PC‐3 negative counterparts (PC‐3‐positive control 54% mineralized area versus PC‐3 negative control 63.4%) (Figure 3F). Moreover, between experimental groups treated with osteocyteCM, we noted a decrease in the percent mineralized area between static and OFF groups (55.2% mineralized area in the static group, versus 61.1% in the OFF group) demonstrating the role of flow‐stimulated osteocytes in regulating cancer‐induced bone matrix degradation. We therefore surmised that the elevated cell aspect ratio and elevated secretion of MMPs as evidenced by matrix degradation could be indicative of a more invasive/metastatic PC‐3 cancer cell phenotype in static cultures. However, it is important to note that the direct degradation of bone tissue by cancer cells has not been widely studied in vivo, likely due to the more dominant formation of osteoclastic bone lesions induced by aberrant PC‐3 signaling during the establishment of the vicious cycle of bone metastasis.[ 40 ] Nonetheless, these initial prostate cancer‐osteoblast co‐culture experiments provided an interesting insight on the effects of osteocyte mechanical loading in a controlled microenvironment to observe cancer cell‐specific behaviors, including growth, morphology, and metastatic potential.

Despite being the most common cell line used to study osteocyte mechanobiology, one major limitation of the initial experiments performed using MLO‐Y4 CM is that this cell line does not express sclerostin, an osteocyte‐specific marker that inhibits osteoblast differentiation by blocking canonical Wnt signaling.[ 41 , 42 ] Co‐culture experiments were therefore repeated using primary osteocyte CM from cells extracted from 2‐month‐old male mice that indeed express sclerostin (Figure S2, Supporting Information) to enhance the biological relevance of this study (Figure 4 ). Of interest, we observed that PC‐3 behaviors were the same in these co‐cultures, suggesting that the observed PC‐3 cancer cell behaviors could be independent of sclerostin (Figure 4).[ 43 ] Specifically, the average number of cells per view at the 72‐hour timepoint in static conditions was 90.9, versus 70.9 in OFF conditions, resulting in a 22% reduction in PC‐3 cell growth between static and OFF groups. Regarding PC‐3 cell morphology during the first 48 h of co‐culture, we measured the average cell aspect ratio to be 7.1 in static groups versus 5.0 in OFF groups on Day 1, and 6.1 in static groups versus 4.2 in OFF groups on Day 2. Trends between groups in terms of the percent mineralized area were also similar, a mineralized area of 48.9% in static groups versus a mineralized area of 52.5% in OFF‐stimulated groups was measured (PC‐3‐negative control 54.8% mineralized area; PC‐3‐positive control 48.4% mineralized area).

Mechanical Loading of Osteocytes Regulates MMP Expression of PC‐3 Prostate Cancer Cells

To determine whether elevated PC‐3 MMP secretion could indeed contribute to the observed reduction in mineralized area, we administered 5 µm of an MMP‐2,9 inhibitor (MMPi) during the 72‐hour co‐culture period with PC‐3 cells. We used MLO‐Y4 osteocyte CM to condition osteoblasts (as in Figure 3) to allow for comparison with groups that were not treated with the inhibitor. We observed that addition of the MMP inhibitor abolished the difference in mineralized area as well as the cell aspect ratio (Figure 5C,E) between static and OFF groups, but did not change the observed cell growth patterns between static and loading groups (Figure 5A, average of 88.7 cells per view in static groups, versus 70.3 cells per view in OFF groups). On Day 1, we measured an average cell aspect ratio of 4.3 in static groups and 4.2 in OFF groups, and on day 2, we measured an average cell aspect ratio of ≈4 in both static and OFF groups. Of interest, the differences in the percent mineralized area between positive and negative PC‐3 control groups (no osteocyte CM) as well as experimental groups treated with osteocyte CM were abolished, with a mineralized area of 59.2% in PC‐3‐negative groups, and 61.7% in PC‐3‐positive control groups. In experimental groups treated with osteocyte CM, we observed a mineralized area of 59.4% in static groups and 58.5% in OFF‐stimulated groups. Das et al. showed that MMP‐2,9 activity is essential for eliciting matrix stiffness‐dependent responses in invasive cancer cell lines.[ 44 ] Specifically, treatment with the MMP inhibitor GM6001 caused cell rounding and reduced the number of focal adhesions, thus impairing cell spreading and motility on stiff matrices.[ 44 ] This suggests that osteocytes may regulate PC‐3 MMP activities that are critical for prostate cancer cell‐mediated stiffness mechanosensing by signaling to mature osteoblasts, and matrix remodeling during the early stages of metastasis to bone.

The directional orientation of cancer cells during metastasis can be largely attributed to its surrounding matrix. Collagen I is the main ECM molecule found in bone and is predominantly secreted by MC3T3‐E1 cells, and the co‐culture here contains partially mineralized areas.[ 45 ] Collagen fiber alignment often directs cancer cell orientation, motility, contact guidance, collective migration, and other behaviors such as cell spreading.[ 44 , 46 ] Aligned collagen fibers enhance cell motility and the metastatic potential of cancer cells.[ 46 , 47 , 48 ] Both MMPs 2 and 9 remodel collagen fibers and facilitate integrin‐mediated adhesion and directional cell spreading; inhibition of MMPs leads to reduced cell spreading, and motility, and enhances cell rounding by disrupting cancer cell–ECM interactions.[ 44 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] Considering that cancer cell–ECM interactions and resulting cancer cell phenotypes are strongly driven by ligand‐integrin binding,[ 50 ] we surmise that the observed differences in directional orientation are due to altered levels of MMPs, as observed through the abrogation of PC‐3 cell alignment under inhibition. Our findings on the directional orientation of PC‐3 cancer cells suggest that osteocytes may indirectly regulate how PC‐3 cells interact with collagen fibers, remodel their surrounding matrix and exploit the organization of collagen for directed migration.

Mechanical Loading of Osteocytes Indirectly Regulates PC‐3 Metastatic Potential by Signaling to Osteoblasts

We further investigated the expression of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers from treating PC‐3 cells with osteoblast CM and examined the immunofluorescent protein expression of both E‐ and N‐cadherin, which are markers representing an epithelial or mesenchymal phenotype respectively (Figure 7 ). The process for generating the osteoblast CM remained the same as that for the migration and invasion assays. Specifically, osteoblast CM was collected from MC3T3‐E1 cultures that were either treated with MLO‐Y4 CM collected from osteocyte samples that were in static conditions or were subjected to OFF. In EMT, E‐cadherin functions as a tumor suppressor protein by maintaining apical‐basal polarity and mediating cell‐to‐cell adhesions.[ 51 ] N‐cadherin promotes a more metastatic cancer phenotype by enhancing collective cell migration and cancer cell‐integrin interactions as an adhesion molecule.[ 52 ] We observed “cadherin switching” toward a more epithelial phenotype where E‐cadherin was upregulated (Figure 7B) and N‐cadherin was downregulated (Figure 7C) in groups where osteoblasts were treated with CM from OFF‐stimulated osteocytes.[ 53 ] On the other hand, a more mesenchymal phenotype, where N‐cadherin was upregulated and E‐cadherin was downregulated in groups where osteoblasts were treated with CM from osteocytes in static conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we created a co‐culture assay to probe prostate cancer‐osteoblast/mineral interactions and combined this with our research group's use of an in vitro conditioned media approach to observe how flow‐stimulated osteocytes may regulate several outcome variables on prostate cancer cells including cell growth, morphology, directional orientation, and an endpoint analysis of mineralized area (Figures 3, 4, 5). We prioritized the development of a reliable prostate cancer‐osteoblast co‐culture system considering that prostate cancers tend to metastasize to osteoblast‐rich areas in bone and that interactions between prostate cancers and osteoblasts have not been fully elucidated.[ 28 , 54 , 55 , 56 ]

After seeding PC‐3 cells onto mineralized osteoblasts, we observed an elongated, spindle‐shaped PC‐3 cell morphology on Day 1 of co‐culture (Figure 3A,C). The aspect ratio of these PC‐3 cells in both static and OFF conditions was significantly elevated compared to monocultures in ibidi channels (Figure 3C,D). This may be attributed to the acquisition of a more mesenchymal cancer cell phenotype in the presence of osteoblast mineral, as well as microenvironmental cues such as stiffness and ECM components from bone mineral.[ 31 ] This was further confirmed with our immunofluorescent staining of PC‐3 cancer for EMT‐associated markers E‐cadherin and N‐cadherin (Figure 7). Moreover, we noted that PC‐3 cells tended to be aligned in the same directional orientation in co‐culture; however, these differences in orientation were ablated when treated with an MMP‐2,9 inhibitor (Figure 6). During cancer‐induced ECM remodeling, cell elongation and locomotion occur partially due to the generation of traction forces through actin cytoskeleton reorganization.[ 57 ] This cell‐generated tension is thought to drive spatial orientation and morphology of cells.[ 57 , 58 ] Invasive breast cancer cell lines cultured on stiff matrices indeed become polarized, whereas normal mammary epithelial cells do not undergo this polarization.[ 59 ] Moreover, protrusions formed in aggressive cancer cells have a higher directional orientation on stiff matrices.[ 59 ] Taken together, the cell morphology observations, MMP‐2,9 inhibitor experiments, and directionality analyses suggest that OFF‐stimulated osteocytes may reduce PC‐3 MMP activities (Figures 5 and 6), observed through the neutralization of mineralized area between static and loading groups, changes to cell morphology as measured by the aspect ratio, as well as a change in the proportion of cells oriented in a preferred direction.

We observed PC‐3 cell growth was reduced when osteoblasts were treated with CM from flow‐stimulated osteocytes, compared to static controls. We also noted that PC‐3 cell growth tended to plateau over the course of 72 h in both static and loading conditions (Figures 3, 4, 5B) in the presence of mineralized osteoblasts. The inhibition of prostate cancer cell growth in the presence of osteoblasts is consistent with previous studies examining prostate cancer osteoblast co‐culture in vitro.[ 60 , 61 ] While we directed our investigation toward the metastatic potential of PC‐3 cancer cells for this study, we hope to implement this co‐culture for further examination of prostate cancer cell dormancy. Several studies have established that osteoblasts induce dormancy signatures in prostate cancer cells via paracrine signaling and/or direct contact between cells, whereas osteoclasts reactivate dormant cancer cells, resulting in metastatic outgrowth.[ 60 , 61 ] However, no studies have demonstrated how osteocytes regulate dormant cancer cells that have disseminated to bone. More investigation into these mechanisms is required, thus future studies will be aimed toward determining whether flow‐stimulated osteocytes indeed control the expression of pro‐dormancy factors secreted by osteoblasts. This will allow us to determine whether the observed outcomes on PC‐3 cell growth could be attributed to the induction of dormancy by examining the expression of dormancy‐specific markers in osteoblasts.

An obvious limitation of this study is the use of MLO‐Y4 osteocytes for several experiments, especially when examining osteocyte‐osteoblast interactions. Despite the MLO‐Y4 cell line being often used for in vitro osteocyte mechanobiology studies, it is well‐established that these cells lack sclerostin.[ 62 ] We attempted to address this shortcoming by using primary osteocytes extracted from 2‐month‐old male mice, which expressed sclerostin, as well as the osteocyte‐specific marker E11/podoplanin (Figures S2,S4, Supporting Information). We observed in our co‐culture experiments that the behaviors of PC‐3 cells indirectly involving primary osteocyte CM were similar to that of experiments involving MLO‐Y4 CM (Figure 3 with MLO‐Y4s versus Figure 4 with primary osteocytes). Treatment of several prostate cancer cell lines with recombinant sclerostin unexpectedly inhibited invasion in PC‐3, DU145, and C4‐2Bm cells, whereas treatment with recombinant DKK1 significantly enhanced cancer cell invasion.[ 43 ] Dynamic tibial loading also reduced serum levels of DKK1 in mice, and fluid flow on MG‐63 pre‐osteoblasts and SaOS2 osteoblasts downregulated DKK1 expression in vitro.[ 19 ] These findings suggest that mechanical loading protects bone from the vicious cycle of metastasis by producing an osteogenic response regulated by host cells instead of aberrant signaling induced by metastatic prostate cancer cells.[ 19 ] The role of DKK1 in the progression of metastasis is very much context‐dependent, as DKK1 has been shown to be upregulated in early‐stage prostate cancer bone metastasis, promoting the formation of osteolytic lesions in vitro and in vivo.[ 63 , 64 , 65 ] Subsequently, as prostate cancers take on a more osteoblastic phenotype in the later stages of bone metastases, downregulation of DKK1 enables uncontrolled bone formation.[ 65 ] Future studies will be aimed toward determining the role of osteocyte‐specific DKK1 under both static and loading conditions and how this may affect prostate cancer–osteoblast interactions, especially given that osteocytes comprise 95% of all cells found in bone tissue.

We exclusively used the PC‐3 bone metastatic prostate cancer cell line for this study.[ 66 ] Contrary to the hallmark formation of osteoblastic bone lesions, PC‐3 tumors solely form osteolytic lesions in xenograft models.[ 67 , 68 ] Thus, use of this particular cell line could provide a representative model of early‐stage bone metastasis, where metastatic prostate cancer cells are initially establishing the secondary tumor on the endosteal surface of bone and likely take on a more osteolytic phenotype. We hope to eventually incorporate the C4‐2B cell line in our bone metastasis studies, as C4‐2Bs have been reported to produce mixed lesions in xenograft models that may be more clinically relevant in the context of prostate cancer bone metastasis.[ 68 ] An additional limitation regarding the cell lines involved in this study is the potential for cross‐species variation through the use of both human (PC‐3) and murine (MLO‐Y4/primary osteocytes, MC3T3‐E1) cells. Nonetheless, use of these particular cell lines allows for comparison with other studies in the literature, as PC‐3 cells are typically used as an experimental model for bone metastasis, and MLO‐Y4 cells are most commonly used to study osteocyte mechanobiology and have a well‐characterized response to mechanical loading via OFF.[ 65 , 69 ] The use of primary murine osteocytes further enhances the biological relevance of this study and helps address the drawbacks of MLO‐Y4 cells previously mentioned. Furthermore, MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblasts have been used in microfluidic cultures with human prostate cancer cell lines, and more importantly have been reported to produce bone mineral similar to native mouse calvarial bone in terms of both ultrastructure and composition in contrast to synthetic bone mineral.[ 29 , 70 ]

To address the limitations of in vitro CM studies that represent one‐way signaling, we developed a co‐culture assay involving prostate cancer cells and mineralized osteoblasts. However, we did not incorporate osteocytes into this platform. Rather, osteoblasts and prostate cancer cells were co‐cultured together, with osteocyte CM being used to treat osteoblasts for 72 h before seeding cancer cells (Figure 2). While this limits the conclusions made on the observations of the role of osteocytes and their effects on prostate cancer and osteoblast interactions, we are still able to make comparisons to other studies in the literature involving the co‐culture of osteoblasts and prostate cancer cells. Specifically, we have observed similar phenomena produced in other studies that allows our work to be compared to the literature, such as an elongated cancer cell morphology and development of protrusions on a mineralized matrix (Figure 3D), an organized spatial arrangement of prostate cancer cells on osteoblast mineral (Figure 6), as well as the inhibition of cancer cell growth during direct contact co‐culture with osteoblasts (Figures 3, 4).[ 29 , 31 , 61 ] Future studies will work toward developing a microfluidic platform to observe multi‐directional signaling between osteocytes, osteoblasts, and prostate cancer cells.

Conclusion

Here, we show indirect regulation of prostate cancer cell metastatic behaviors by osteocytes subjected to mechanical loading via signaling to mature osteoblasts as an intermediary during early‐stage metastasis to bone. We observed that flow‐stimulated osteocytes can regulate migration (wound healing) and invasion of PC‐3 cells. We then created a co‐culture assay involving osteoblasts and prostate cancer cells to study the dynamic interactions between these cell populations and mimic the osteoblast‐rich endosteal surface of bone, which prostate cancer cells tend to metastasize to. Moreover, we implemented the double CM strategy developed by our research group to demonstrate how osteocytes regulate various prostate cancer cell behaviors in co‐culture. The co‐culture assay presented here allows for the observation of several outcome variables, including PC‐3 prostate cancer cell growth, morphology, directional/spatial orientation, as well as endpoint analyses on bone mineral. We then implemented the use of primary murine osteocytes extracted from 2‐month‐old male mice to enhance the biological relevance of our studies involving MLO‐Y4 osteocytes. We observed that PC‐3 cancer cell growth is decreased when co‐cultured mineralized osteoblasts are treated with CM from OFF‐stimulated osteocytes, and also resulted in altered cell morphology and reduced MMP activity in PC‐3 cancer cells. We observed that mechanical loading of osteocytes indirectly promotes a more epithelial phenotype in PC‐3 cancer cells by signaling to mineralized osteoblasts when compared to static controls.

Experimental Section

Cell Culture—PC‐3

The culture of PC‐3 cancer cells was described in a previous study.[ 69 ] Briefly, PC‐3 prostate cancer cells (ATCC; RRID: CVCL_0035; acquired in January 2014), a human cell line derived from bone metastases, were cultured in complete PC‐3 growth media consisting of 89% F‐12K basal media (Kaighn's modification) (Gibco, 21 127 022), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 1 248 302), and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco, 15 140 122). Cells were grown in tissue culture‐treated dishes (Corning, 430 167), passaged every four days by trypsinization (Gibco, 25 200 072), and reseeded at a 1:10 ratio.

MC3T3‐E1

MC3T3‐E1 osteoblast precursors (ATCC subclone 11; RRID: CVCL_VT93; acquired March 2009) were grown in α‐MEM (Gibco, 12 571 063) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The MC3T3‐E1 osteoblast precursor line was derived from the calvaria of C57BL/6 mice. The deposited mineralized matrix from this line was similar to that found in native murine calvarial bone.[ 70 ] Cells were grown to 80% confluence before passaging.

MLO‐Y4

The culture of PC‐3 cancer cells was described in a previous study.[ 69 ] MLO‐Y4 osteocyte‐like cells (RRID: CVCL_M098; acquired March 2009) were a gift from Dr. Lynda Bonewald at Indiana University.[ 62 ] The MLO‐Y4 cell line was established by Dr. Bonewald's research group in 1997 and were commonly used to study osteocyte mechanobiology.[ 62 ] Cells were cultured dishes (Corning) coated with a type I rat tail collagen solution (0.15 mg mL−1 collagen (Corning, 354 236) diluted in 0.02 N acetic acid (Sigma‐Aldrich, A6283‐500ML)). Cells were grown to 80% confluence in complete growth media consisting of 94% α‐MEM, 2.5% FBS, 2.5% newborn calf serum (Gibco, 16 010 159), and 1% P/S before passaging.

Primary Osteocytes

All animals were handled in accordance with the relevant guidelines established by the Sunnybrook Research Institute (AUP #2022‐224). Primary osteocytes were extracted from the long bones of freshly sacrificed 2‐month‐old male C57BL/6 mice using LiberaseTM (high purity collagenase) (Roche, 5 401 119 001), in a similar manner by Stern et al., and following the same procedure as in Seaman et al.[ 69 , 71 ] Briefly, excess tissues (muscle, periosteum, bone marrow) were carefully removed from samples, and bones were cut into small 2–3 mm pieces before undergoing nine rounds of digestion in Liberase TM. Cells were cultured in primary osteocyte media (89% α‐MEM, 5% FBS, 5% newborn calf serum, 1% P/S) and allowed to recover for up to one week after extraction and tested for the presence of mycoplasma (MycoStrip, InvivoGen) before use in experiments.

Oscillatory Fluid Flow on Osteocytes

To apply OFF on osteocytes, MLO‐Y4s were cultured on sterile collagen‐coated glass slides (Corning, 2947‐75X38) until 80% confluency was achieved (48 h). Then, the glass slides were loaded into parallel plate flow chambers and connected to a glass syringe on a linear actuator. OFF was applied to osteocytes at a frequency of 1 Hz at a peak shear stress of 1 Pa for 2 h. After OFF, glass slides were immediately removed from the chambers, placed into culture dishes containing 10 mL of MLO‐Y4 growth media and were subsequently incubated for 24 h before collecting CM.

The peak shear stress of 1 Pa was selected based on previous findings, which have estimated that osteocytes experience physiological shear stresses ranging from 0.8–3 Pa, and 1 Pa approximately represented mechanical loading or shear stresses in the lacunocanalicular network that occur during mild exercise.[ 72 ] The length of time that OFF was applied as well as the post‐flow incubation time of 24 h were selected based on evidence showing that CM collected from MLO‐Y4 osteocytes with the selected parameters could regulate osteoblast activity in addition to a number of other cells in bone.[ 34 , 69 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 ]

Osteoblast Differentiation and Validation

MC3T3‐E1 cells were seeded into culture dishes at 5000 cells cm−2 in osteogenic differentiation media, which consisted of growth media containing 50 µg mL−1 fresh L‐ascorbic acid (Sigma‐Aldrich, A4544‐25G), 10 mm β‐glycerophosphate (Sigma‐Aldrich, G9422‐10G), and 10 nm dexamethasone (Sigma‐Aldrich, D4902‐25MG). Osteoblasts were differentiated over the course of 2 weeks, with osteogenic media replaced every 3 days. MC3T3‐E1 osteoblasts produced calcified nodules over the course of 2 weeks which were verified via alizarin red staining (Sigma‐Aldrich, 2 003 999) (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Alkaline phosphatase activity in MC3T3‐E1 cultures was also verified at the 1‐week time point.

To determine the presence of calcified nodules produced by osteoblasts, alizarin red staining was performed on osteoblast cultures. Cells were washed 3 times in PBS (Sigma‐Aldrich, D8537‐500ML) and fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for 20 min. Following fixation, cells were washed five times in deionized water and subsequently stained in an alizarin red staining solution. The alizarin red staining solution was pre‐prepared by the manufacturer and was applied at its original concentration of 40 mm. Cells were incubated in the staining solution for 30 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. After staining, cells were washed at least 4 times in deionized water to completely remove any residual stain.

Generation of Double Conditioned Media with Differentiated Osteoblasts

Double‐conditioned media were generated to determine the indirect effects of mechanical loading of osteocytes, employing a similar strategy used in previous studies.[ 34 , 73 ] To generate osteoblast CM (double conditioned media), MC3T3‐E1s were seeded into 10 cm Petri dishes and differentiated in osteogenic medium for 10 days. Then, MC3T3‐E1s were incubated with a 1:1 ratio of osteogenic medium and fresh MLO‐Y4 CM for 72 h. After treatment, the media were replaced with MC3T3‐E1 growth medium (no differentiation factors), and osteoblast CM was collected after 24 h and was used for the wound healing migration assay, invasion assay, and immunofluorescent staining of EMT markers described below.

Wound Healing Migration Assay

PC‐3 prostate cancer cells were seeded into 24‐well plates at a density of 100 000 cells well−1 and grown until 95–100% confluency (monolayer) was achieved. The monolayers were then serum starved in F‐12K basal media supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BioShop, ALB001.50) 12 h before the experiment was conducted. The following day, cell monolayers were scratched with a 10 µL pipette tip to create a wound gap. After gently washing in PBS, osteoblast CM were added into the well plates and cells were left to migrate over 12 h. To pinpoint a reference location for consistent image acquisition, a small dot was placed at a random location by the scratch. Images were collected every four hours (0, 4, 8, and 12 h) after the scratch was created using a phase‐contrast microscope. The gap (or cell‐free) area was computed using an ImageJ Wound Healing Area plugin.[ 77 ] Data at each timepoint were presented as % gap coverage, which was calculated by subtracting the final gap area from the initial gap area, then dividing the difference by the initial gap area. Control groups consisted of PC‐3 monolayers treated with fresh MC3T3‐E1 growth medium.

Transwell Invasion Assay

Invasion assays followed a similar procedure described by Ma et al.[ 33 ] Transwell inserts (12‐well, 8 µm pore size) (Corning, CA62406‐176) were coated with 200 µL of 2 mg mL−1 Matrigel (Corning, 356 231) and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min to polymerize. Following incubation, 80 000 PC‐3 prostate cancer cells labeled with CellTracker Green (Invitrogen, C2925) were added to each upper compartment of the Transwells by depositing 200 µL of a 400 000 cells mL−1 suspension (80 000 cells Transwell−1 insert). OB CM (1.2 mL) was added to the lower compartment of the Transwells, and the PC‐3 cells were left to invade toward CM for 24 h. Cells were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and cells that did not invade through Matrigel were removed with a cotton swab. Transwell membranes were then imaged to quantify the number of invaded PC‐3 cells with ImageJ.

Microfluidic Direct Contact Co‐Culture

Ibidi µ‐slide VI 0.4 channels (ibidi, 80 606) were coated in 0.15 mg mL−1 type I collagen at room temperature for 1 h. Of note, individual channels were 17 mm in length, 3.8 mm in width, and 0.4 mm in height. Following collagen coating, channels were then rinsed several times in PBS and in osteogenic differentiation media. MC3T3‐E1 cells were then seeded into in ibidi channels at 800 000 cells mL−1 and differentiated in osteogenic medium for 17–20 days. To maintain an aseptic environment over this long differentiation period, ibidi devices were placed in empty autoclaved pipette boxes in the cell culture incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). Media in the channels were replaced daily during the first week of differentiation, then twice daily afterward to achieve mineralization. Osteoblasts were then conditioned with a 1:1 ratio of MLO‐Y4 CM and osteogenic media for 72 h, with media replenished daily. Control groups in microfluidic co‐culture experiments were not treated with any MLO‐Y4 CM, rather, these samples were treated with a 1:1 mixture of fresh MLO‐Y4 culture media and osteogenic media. The positive groups in these experiments included the addition of PC‐3 cancer cells, whereas the negative control groups did not include PC‐3 cells, where only mineralized osteoblasts were cultured in the channels. After the 72‐hour conditioning period, each channel was rinsed twice with 100 µL MC3T3‐E1 culture media and once with 100 µL PC‐3 culture media to remove the MLO‐Y4 CM and osteogenic factors prior to seeding cancer cells. PC‐3 cells labeled with CellTracker green were directly seeded on top of mineralized osteoblasts in the ibidi channels at a density of 650 000 cells mL−1 and left to attach for 20 min. Loosely attached cancer cells were then removed by gently rinsing the channels three times with a 1:1 mixture of PC‐3 and MC3T3‐E1 growth media. Images of PC‐3 cells were acquired daily over the course of 72 h with a fluorescent microscope. Cell growth and aspect ratio were quantified with ImageJ. After three days, ibidi channels were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and underwent alizarin red staining as described above. Mineralized nodules were then imaged with a phase contrast microscope at random locations along the length of the channel. The % mineralized area was quantified with ImageJ using a preset threshold consistent throughout experiments to compute the total area stained with alizarin red. The preset threshold was determined by staining an extra mineralized osteoblast sample that was not involved in the experiments for this study, but was differentiated in the same conditions. The threshold was then determined and optimized using the images acquired from these samples (10 in total). Mineralization data for all experiments were analyzed by converting images to an 8‐bit format and applying a threshold between pixel values of 0–110 using ImageJ's Default method to identify areas stained with alizarin red. After the threshold was applied, the % Area (i.e., the alizarin red/mineralized area) was computed with ImageJ.

Primary Osteocyte Conditioned Media Experiments

To determine whether the observed effects in co‐cultures were dependent on sclerostin, MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblasts were seeded and differentiated as previously described but were instead conditioned in primary osteocyte CM instead of MLO‐Y4 CM. Primary osteocytes were extracted from two 2‐month‐old male mice, pooled together for enzymatic digestion. Each experimental repeat used a different source of primary cells. Following primary osteocyte extraction, 500 000 primary cells were seeded onto a collagen‐coated glass slide, cultured for 24 h in 10 mL, then placed in parallel plate flow chambers. In each experiment, primary osteocytes were seeded onto two glass slides (500 000 cells slide−1), where one slide was subjected to OFF, and the other slide served as a static control. OFF was applied at 1 Hz and 1 Pa for 2 h, whereas static controls were placed in flow chambers without OFF in the same incubator. Following the application of OFF, the glass slides were placed in 10 mL of fresh primary osteocyte media in a 100 mm dish, and primary osteocyte CM were collected 24 h post‐flow for co‐culture experiments. It should be noted the number of cells seeded on glass slides is similar to the cell numbers for MLO‐Y4 CM (≈500 000 cells per glass slide).[ 69 ]

MMP‐2,9 Inhibition Experiments

To determine the effects of matrix metalloproteinases secreted by PC‐3 cancer cells, an MMP‐2,9 inhibitor was added into the growth media during co‐culture. Potent MMP‐2,9 inhibitor (5 µm; Sigma‐Aldrich, 444 241) was introduced to the co‐cultures 2.5 h after seeding cancer cells into ibidi channels. The MMP inhibitor ((2R)‐2‐[(4‐Biphenylylsulfonyl)amino]‐3‐phenylpropionic acid, C21H19NO4S) was reconstituted at 10 mm in DMSO (Sigma‐Aldrich, D2650‐100ML), aliquoted, and stored in −20 °C. Aliquots were thawed only once prior to use. Co‐cultures were treated with the MMP inhibitor daily during media replenishment. Media containing the MMP inhibitor were prepared fresh daily to prevent degradation of the inhibitor. The MMP‐2,9 inhibitor was reconstituted in DMSO and stored according to the manufacturer instructions. To allow for comparison between experiments, MLO‐Y4 CM was used to condition osteoblast cultures before introducing PC‐3 cancer cells into the system.

To verify whether the MMP inhibitor had effects on cell growth and viability of both PC‐3 and MC3T3‐E1 cells, 20 000 cells well−1 were seeded in a 48‐well plate and were treated with 0–10 µm of the MMP inhibitor and a DMSO vehicle control over 72 h. Similar to the co‐culture assay, media containing the MMP inhibitor were replaced daily. Cell viability was assayed by performing live‐dead staining on cultures by adding 20 µL NucBlue Live solution and 20 µL NucGreen Dead solution per well (Invitrogen, R37609) and incubating cells for 30 min in the dark. Images were captured at three random locations per well with a fluorescent microscope. Images were analyzed with ImageJ to determine the number of cells per image as well as % viability. The % viability was calculated by subtracting the number of dead cells from the total number of cells counted, then dividing the difference by the total number of cells. A positive control for dead cells was prepared by treating cells with 70% ethanol for 15 min before adding the staining solution.

Directionality Analysis

Analysis of the directional orientation of cells with or without the addition of the MMP‐2,9 inhibitor was performed using the directionality function on ImageJ/Fiji. The dispersion and amount resulting from the analysis were recorded for each image in the dataset. Briefly, dispersion was defined as one standard deviation from the peak angular orientation (center of the Gaussian) obtained from the Gaussian fit function. The amount was defined as the sum of values (from raw data) from −1 standard deviation to +1 standard deviation from the center of the Gaussian peak, divided by the total sum of the histogram.[ 78 ]

To be more specific, the Directionality function computed a histogram from a provided image indicating the proportion of structures oriented in the same direction.[ 78 ] The plugin would also try to generate statistical parameters on the highest peak identified in the histogram, which was fit by a Gaussian function.[ 78 ] Relevant parameters for this study included the Dispersion, which reported the standard deviation (STD) of the Gaussian, as well as the Amount, which was the sum of the histogram (from raw image data) from the center (of the Gaussian)‐STD, to the center+STD divided by the total sum of the histogram.[ 78 ] Essentially, the amount reported the proportion of structures (cells) generally aligned in the same directional orientation as defined by the Gaussian fit.[ 78 ]

Immunofluorescent Staining—E‐ and N‐Cadherins

PC‐3 prostate cancer cells were cultured in ibidi channels at 500 000 cells mL−1 and cultured for 24 h. OB CM was then added to the channels and cultured for another 24 h. Immunofluorescent staining followed previously described procedures.[ 69 ] Following incubation with CM, cells were washed and fixed in 4% (v/v) methanol‐free formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 28 908) that was diluted in PBS for 10 min. Cells were then permeabilized in 0.3% (v/v) Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich, T8787‐50ML) for 5 min and then blocked in 5% (v/v) normal donkey serum (D9663‐10ML) and 1% (v/v) BSA for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with a primary goat anti‐human E‐cadherin antibody (R&D Systems, AF748‐SP) at a 1:100 dilution overnight (4 °C), followed by another overnight incubation at 4 °C with a primary mouse anti‐human N‐cadherin antibody (Novus Biologicals, NBP1‐48309SS) at a 1:200 dilution. Samples were then incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti‐goat secondary antibody (E‐cadherin) (Invitrogen, A11055) as well as an Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti‐mouse secondary antibody (N‐cadherin) (Invitrogen, A31570) with 1% BSA (both 1:100 dilution) in the dark for 2 h.[ 69 ] Samples were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich, MBD0015‐1ML) at a 1:1000 dilution at room temperature in the dark for 10 min and imaged within 48 h of completing the secondary antibody staining. The imaging and analysis of samples also followed previously published methods.[ 69 ] Five random images were captured per channel using a confocal microscope (Nikon) with a 20× objective lens. Image processing and analysis were performed on ImageJ. Using the captured DAPI (blue) channel images, 20 random nuclei were selected and overlayed with the corresponding E‐cadherin (green) and N‐cadherin (red) channels. The 20 randomly selected cells were then manually contoured by creating several points with the polygon selection tool to measure the mean fluorescence of each marker. The average background intensities of each image were measured by randomly selecting five different areas on the image to correct for background noise.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were described previously.[ 69 ] All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. All experiments were repeated independently at least twice, with at least three biological replicates for each experimental group. Data presented were from a representative experiment or all experiments. Data were all presented as the mean ± standard deviation. For immunofluorescent data, sample‐averaged data (n = 6/group) shown as a scatter plot were overlayed with violin plots of individual cell measurements (n = 300 or 600/group) to create “superplots”, as demonstrated in the study by Seaman et al.[ 69 , 79 ] For experiments with two groups, two‐tailed Student's t‐tests were performed to determine statistical significance. For experiments with three or more groups, either a one‐ or two‐way or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by a post‐hoc Tukey test to define statistical significance between groups. Statistical significance for all experiments was taken at α = 0.05.
