A convergence framework for Airy β line ensemble via pole evolution
Jiaoyang Huang, Lingfu Zhang

Abstract
. The Airy β line ensemble is an infinite sequence of random curves. It is a natural extension of the Tracy-Widom β distributions, and is expected to be the universal edge scaling limit of a range of models in random matrix theory and statistical mechanics. In this work, we provide a framework of proving convergence to the Airy β line ensemble, via a characterization through the pole evolution of meromorphic functions satisfying certain stochastic differential equations. Our framework is then applied to prove the universality of the Airy β line ensemble as the edge limit of various continuous time processes, including Dyson Brownian motions with general β \beta and potentials, Laguerre processes and Jacobi processes.

Introduction

During the 18th century, De Moivre established the Gaussian distribution for sums of independent binomial variables, a concept later generalized by Laplace. Gauss popularized the central limit theorem, used for error evaluation in systems characterized by independence.
In recent decades, there has been increasing interest in understanding fluctuations in highly correlated systems, leading to the emergence of a different family of distributions known as Tracy-Widom

Many of these models are related to each other through their underlying integrable structures, and are in the so called Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class, a topic in probability theory that has been intensively studied in recent years.

A motivation of the current paper is to better understand the mathematical structures behind Tracy-Widom

The main object studied here is the Airy

In this paper, we take a new perspective, and our main result is a characterization of ALE

Our result provides a new framework to prove convergence. As some examples, we prove convergence to ALE

1.1.1.
Edge limit of general
\beta

Tracy-Widom
β

\beta>0

[

[

More generally,

(1.1)

\frac{1}{Z_{n,\beta,W}}\prod_{i<j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{\beta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}W(x_{i}),

There are three special cases referred to as the classical ensembles, which are defined by

\displaystyle W(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-\beta x^{2}/4},&\text{Hermite/Gaussian ensemble}\\
x^{\beta(m-n+1)/2-1}e^{-\beta x/2},&\text{Laguerre ensemble}\\
x^{\beta(p-n+1)/2-1}(1-x)^{\beta(q-n+1)/2-1},&\text{Jacobi ensemble}\\
\end{array}\right.

These classical ensembles for
\beta=1,2,4
originated from the study of eigenvalue distributions of random matrices. They represent the joint distributions of the eigenvalues of size
n
Gaussian, Wishart, and Jacobi matrices. These matrices and their extreme eigenvalues, with
\beta=1

[

[

As mentioned earlier, as

β

\beta=1,2,4

For

Based on a tri-diagonal random matrix model discovered by Dumitriu and Edelman

Later, such Airy

1.2.2.
Uniqueness and universality

As indicated by the convergence of both DBM and Gaussian corners process to the same limit, i.e., ALE
β
, in
[
64
]
, it is natural to expect that ALE
β
is also the scaling limit of many other well-known processes.
Some examples include DBM with general potentials, non-intersecting random walks
[
61
,
96
,
74
]
, various other
\beta
–corners processes
[

,

,

]
and measures on Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns
[

,

]
, Macdonald processes
[

]
, and

-distributions on lozenge tilings
[

,

]
.

A robust and general approach to establishing convergence involves a suitable characterization of ALE
β
. Specifically, this means identifying easily verifiable properties of ALE
β
and demonstrating that these properties uniquely determine ALE
β
. To prove convergence, it would then suffice to establish tightness and confirm that any subsequential limit satisfies these properties.

For ALE
β
with
\beta=2
, an elegant characterization is the Brownian Gibbs property
[
6
]
as mentioned above.
However, this does not hold for any
\beta\neq 2
.
The next natural candidate of characterization would be an ‘infinite dimensional DBM’, by taking
n\to\infty
in (
1.5
).
For example,
[
100
]
shows that ALE
β
(for
\beta\geq 1
), i.e., the edge limit of finite dimension DBM, is a solution to such an infinite dimensional DBM in a weak sense.

However, there are several technical challenges in developing a characterization and convergence framework along this direction.
First, particles (i.e., those
\lambda_{i}
in (
1.5
)) may collide or adjacent particles may get too close, leading to singularities in the drift
1/(\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t))
term.
In
[
100
]
, for
\beta\geq 1
such singularities are ruled out using existing level replusion estimates (see
[
100
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
25
, Theorem 3.2]
), which are known only for stationary DBM whose laws are given by
\beta
-ensembles. Deriving such estimates for other models could be difficult. Moreover, for
\beta<1
collisions are inevitable.
Second, the long-range interactions introduce additional complications when analyzing infinitely many particles. In fact, even the well-posedness of the infinite-dimensional DBM starting from a fixed intial condition appears to be absent from the literature, except for the cases
\beta=1,2,4

[
108
,
111
,
88
,
91
]
where the underlying algebraic structure is used. Note that for an analogous infinite-dimensional SDE corresponding to the bulk limit of DBM, such well-posedness has been achieved for
\beta\geq 1
(see
[
90
,
107
,
127
]
). A key property used in the bulk setting is the cancellation of particle interactions from left and right, and that is absent at the edge.
For example, in the recent paper
[
12
]
, which studies a random matrix eigenvalue dynamics similar to DBM, an infinite-dimensional SDE obtained from the edge limit is shown to admit multiple solutions.
As a result, for the infinite-dimensional DBM, both proving the uniqueness of solution and verifying it for any subsequential limit face various barriers.

To overcome these difficulties, in this paper we take an alternative approach, and characterize ALE
β
as the pole dynamics of meromorphic functions, satisfying a function-valued SDE.
In other words, we characterize ALE
β
via the dynamics of its Stieltjes transform.
This method completely circumvents the issue of long-range interactions and collisions, and is applicable for any
\beta>0
.

(iv) Stieltjes transform and poles dynamics.

Stieltjes transforms and Nevanlinna functions have been widely used to investigate and characterize eigenvalue distributions of random matrix ensembles. See

The concept of characterizing the evolution of interacting particle systems through the pole dynamics of meromorphic functions has been explored previously. In integrable systems, it has been demonstrated that the movement of poles in certain solutions of various nonlinear PDEs can be formally linked to the dynamics of particle systems interacting through simple two-body potentials. This discovery was initially made in
[

for equations such as the Korteweg-de Vries and Burgers-Hopf equations, and in

[

[

[

[

We also comment that other than pole dynamics, interacting particle systems such as the Gaussian

1.4.
Convergence framework

Given the characterization presented in
Theorem

1.15
, to prove convergence to ALE
β

(1)

establish the tightness of the Stieltjes transforms of the empirical particle density at the microscopic scale, and

(2)

verify that the scaling limit is Airy-like, and satisfies the SDE (

As a demonstration of this approach, we prove the convergence to ALE

We next give the formal statement of our result.

We use a strong topology of uniform in compact convergence for line ensembles. More precisely, for a sequence of ordered families of functions
\{f_{i}^{(1)}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}},\{f_{i}^{(2)}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}},\ldots
, they converge to
\{f_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
under the uniform in compact topology, if for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i}^{(n)}=f_{i}
uniformly in any compact interval.

Theorem 1.16
.

ALE
β
is the edge scaling limit of stationary DBM with certain general potentials (satisfying
Assumption

7.1
below), stationary Laguerre process, and stationary Jacobi process, all with parameter
\beta
, under the uniform in compact topology. We refer to
Theorem

7.2
for a more detailed statement.

The definitions and background of these processes, as well as the precise statement and proof, will be given in
Section

We emphasize that these convergence results are new even for the classical cases of
\beta=1,4
(except for the DBM with

), which can be viewed as the joint convergence of eigenvalues of time-evolved classical ensembles with real or quaternion entries.

We remark that the developed framework can also be applied to prove convergence to ALE

for the other mentioned models. The main remaining task is to establish the desired tightness given in (1).
While such tightness are not available from
[
26

, a plausible way is to utilize the dynamical loop equation, as in

where local laws down to any mesoscopic scale have been proven for random tilings.
We leave this for future works.

1.5.
Other properties

In addition to proving convergence to ALE
β
, our new characterization can be leveraged to further investigate its properties.
First, we can study the regularity of ALE
β
. The Brownian regularity for the ALE has been intensively studied in
[
36
,
69
,
68
,
37
]
. For ALE
β
with
\beta\geq 1
, it has been established in
[
100
]
that the lines of ALE
β
are locally Brownian.
In
Section

4
we show that the lines of ALE
β
are Hölder continuous with an exponent
1/2
for any
\beta>0
.
The second property we study is the collision of lines. For
\beta\geq 1

[

that the lines of ALE

1.6.
Proof ideas

We give an outline of our proofs, highlighting the main difficulties and ideas.

To prove the uniqueness in law as stated in
Theorem

1.15
,
the overall strategy is to establish a certain sense of ‘mixing in time’ of the dynamics (given by
Assumption

1.10
).
More precisely, we take two families of random particle-generated Nevanlinna functions, both satisfying the two assumptions.
Using the SDE (
1.14
) we reconstruct the dynamics of the poles, which are ‘infinite dimensional DBM’ in a certain sense.
We couple the two ‘infinite dimensional DBM’ obtained from both functions, by coupling the driving Brownian motions. Then we show that the poles get closer in time under this coupling.
Thus since both dynamics start from time
-\infty
, necessarily they are the same.

For both the reconstruction of DBM and the coupling, an essential input is that the poles have Airy-zero approximation, uniformly in time.
This is implied by the uniform in time Airy-like property, as explained in
Remark

1.8
.
Then under
Assumption

1.9
, it remains to show that such an approximation propagates in time, for which we again resort to the SDE (
1.14
).

In summary, three tasks are inline: propagation of Airy-zero approximation, reconstruction of DBM, and coupling.
We next explain each of them in more details.

1.6.1.
Propagation of Airy-zero approximation

Our
Assumption

1.9
concerns specific times, implying that the
i
-th pole remains constant away from the
i
-th zero of the Airy function. Utilizing the SDE (
1.14
), we manage to get refined estimates: the
i
-th pole approximates the
i
-th zero with a polynomially small error over arbitrarily long time intervals with high probability, as demanded for later steps. To achieve this, in
Section

3
, we analyze (
1.14
) along certain characteristics which offset the singularity of the nonlinear term. This idea has previously been used (see e.g.,
[
76
,
1
,
24
]
) to study DBM down to any mesoscopic scale, where the distance from the spectral parameter
w
to the particles is much bigger than particle fluctuations.
However, in our analysis of (
1.14
), we operate at a microscopic scale, where the distance from
w
to the poles is of the same order as their fluctuation size. While a straightforward union bound over characteristic flows from polynomially many points suffices at the mesoscopic scale, our case demands careful selection of characteristic flows and precise estimation of error terms in the SDE, tailored to their initial positions.

1.6.2.
DBM reconstruction

As already mentioned, there are significant challenges in analyzing DBM due to the singular repulsive interaction and possible particle collisions, in particular when
\beta\in(0,1)
. Even for finite dimensional DBM, establishing the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution requires the theory of multivalued SDE, see
[
34
,
33
]
. Our approach through pole evolutions circumvents these issues entirely. Notably, there are no singularities even when poles collide.

On the other hand, a key challenge of our method lies in reconstructing the dynamics of each pole, which requires ruling out the possibility of poles adhering to each other for prolonged periods. To address these, in
Section

4
we first establish that the trajectory of each pole is Hölder continuous solely utilizing (
1.14
). Together with the Airy-zero approximation, for any short time interval, we can identify a large index
k
such that the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-th poles remain bounded away from each other. This enables us to localize the system and study the evolution of the first
k
poles, treating the remaining poles’ influence as an additional potential.
For this
k
poles system, in
Section

5
,
by employing classical Itô calculus on certain elementary symmetric polynomials, we show that the time of collisions almost surely has measure zero. We note that similar ideas have been employed to show the instant
diffraction of the particles for DBM
[
66
]
.
Subsequently, the evolution of each pole can be reconstructed by performing a contour integral of (
1.14
).
As a result, the
k
poles system can be interpreted as a
k
-dimensional DBM with a time-dependent random drift, which exhibits a monotonicity property.

1.6.3.
Uniqueness via coupling

In
Section

6
, we take two solutions to (
1.14
), and design a coupling where the poles get closer in time.
Consider the
k
-dimensional DBMs with random drifts reconstructed in the previous step, for these two solutions respectively.
Our coupling is by using the same set of driving Brownian motions for both.
Note that such
k
-dimensional DBMs with random drifts are constructed with random
k
, and only for a short time interval; but we need a coupling for a long time (to let the poles get closer).
A trick here is to concatenate these short intervals, and allow for different
k
in each of them, as long as
k
is always large enough.

There is a monotonicity property: if the
i
-th pole of the initial data for the first solution dominates that of the second solution for each
i
, then at any time after the
i
-th pole of the first solution dominates that of the second solution for each
i
.
Then we can sandwich one solution between affine shifts of the other, while keeping the error arising from the affine shifts arbitrarily small. Such sandwiching forces the poles of the two solutions to get closer in time. By taking long enough time intervals, they must coincide, establishing the uniqueness as desired. Such coupling and sandwiching strategies have been used to establish local statistics universality in random lozenge tilings
[
3
,
5
,
77
]
.

, we have

\left|Y(w)-\sqrt{w}\right|\leq\frac{{\rm Im}[\sqrt{w}]^{1-\delta}}{{\rm Im}[w]}.

Then
Y
has infinitely many poles
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots
, and
|x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|<CK^{4}i^{-\delta/6}
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, where
C>0
is a universal constant.

In particular, these conditions are satisfied by Airy-like Nevanlinna functions (with
K
large and
\delta={\mathfrak{d}}
).

We also note that the domain of
w
in
Lemma

2.3
is different from that in
Definition

1.7
.
The domain in
Definition

1.7
is taken to be easily verifiable for the sub-sequential limit of various models, as will be evident in
Section

7
.
In fact, bounds on
|Y(w)-\sqrt{w}|
for
w
closer to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
can be readily deduced for Airy-like Nevanlinna functions (see
Lemma

2.5
).

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
({\mathfrak{d}},C_{*})
-Airy-like Nevanlinna function
Y
(with poles
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots
), there exists
B>0
depending only on
C_{*}
, such that
|x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\leq B
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

The proof of
Lemma

2.3
relies on the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula, which has become standard in random matrix theory. Therefore, we defer it to
Section

A.3
.

2.2.
Proof of
Proposition

2.1

Thanks to (
1.11
) and (
1.10
), we have

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}-\sqrt{w}\rightarrow 0,

when
w\to\infty
along any direction in
(-3\pi/4,3\pi/4)
.
By
|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\sim(3\pi i/2)^{2/3}
from (
1.9
), we have

(2.8)

\displaystyle|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}+(3\pi i/2)^{2/3}|,|x_{i}+(3\pi i/2)^{2/3}|\leq B

for a large
B>0
; in particular
x_{1}\leq B
. If we take
w
with
\arg(w)\in(-3\pi/4,3\pi/4)
and
|w|>2B
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{{}={}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-x_{i}|}{|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{B}{|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|}\\
&=\sum_{i>|w|^{3/2}}\frac{B}{|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|}+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor|w|^{3/2}\rfloor}\frac{B}{|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|}\\

where in the last line we used that, when

,
|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|\gtrsim i^{4/3}
for the first term,
and
|x_{i}-w||{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|\gtrsim|w|^{2}
for the second term (since

).

Therefore, (
2.7
) and (

) together give that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}-\sqrt{w}\rightarrow 0,

for
w\to\infty
along any direction in
(-3\pi/4,3\pi/4)
.
Also note that, thanks to (
2.8
), we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}-\frac{x_{i}}{1+x_{i}^{2}}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1+x_{i}(x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i})}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}(1+x_{i}^{2})}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|B+B^{2}}{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|(1+(({\mathfrak{a}}_{i}+B)\wedge 0)^{2})}<\infty.

By plugging (
2.11
) into the representation (
2.2
) for
Y
, we can rewrite

as (for some

)

(2.12)

\displaystyle Y(w)=b+cw+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}-w}-\frac{x_{i}}{1+x_{i}^{2}}\right)=b^{\prime}+cw+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}.

By our assumption,
Y(w_{n})-\sqrt{w}_{n}\rightarrow 0
as

in (

when

) holds.

Finally, if

is Airy-like, then

implies that
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|<\infty
, and

as

) holds.
∎

2.3.
Domain extension

As mentioned below
Lemma

2.3
, for an Airy-like Nevanlinna function
Y
, we also provide a bound of
|Y(w)-\sqrt{w}|
for
w
close to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, which will be useful later.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
Y:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\cup{\mathbb{R}}
being any
({\mathfrak{d}},C_{*})
-Airy-like Nevanlinna function, we have

|Y(w)-\sqrt{w}|\leq B|w|^{-1/2},\quad\forall\arg(w)\in(0,3\pi/4),|w|>B.

for
B>0
depending only on
{\mathfrak{d}}
and
C_{*}
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

2.4
, there is
B^{\prime}>0
with each
|x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\leq B^{\prime}
.
Then, for any
w\in\mathbb{H}
with
\arg(w)\in(0,3\pi/4),|w|>2B^{\prime}
, we have
|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|\leq 2|x_{i}-w|
.

By
Proposition

2.1
and (
1.10
), we have

\left|Y(w)+\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(w)}{\Ai(w)}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w}-\frac{1}{x_{i}-w}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{x_{i}-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}{(x_{i}-w)({\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w)}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2B^{\prime}}{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|^{2}}.

Using (
1.9
), we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor|w|^{3/2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|^{2}}+\sum_{i=\lfloor|w|^{3/2}\rfloor+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}-w|^{2}}\lesssim\frac{|w|^{3/2}}{|w|^{2}}+\sum_{i=\lfloor|w|^{3/2}\rfloor+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{4/3}}\lesssim|w|^{-1/2}.

Thus with (
1.11
), the conclusion follows.
∎

2.4.
Topological statements

As we shall derive convergence to ALE
β
from Stieltjes transforms, we will need several statements on the functional spaces, which we provide here.

Definition 2.6
.

For any locally finite measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, we say that they
converge in the vague topology
to another locally finite
\mu
, if
\mu_{n}(f)\to\mu(f)
, for any
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is compactly supported and smooth.

Such vague topology arises naturally from Nevanlinna function convergence.

Lemma 2.7
.

Take Nevanlinna functions
Y_{1},Y_{2},\cdots
and
Y
such that
Y_{n}\to Y
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly in any compact subset of
\mathbb{H}
.
Suppose the corresponding measures (in their Nevanlinna representation) are
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots
and
\mu
, respectively,
then
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in the vague topology.

Proof.

Take any
f
that is compactly supported and smooth, and let
K
be a large enough number such that
f=0
outside
[-K,K]
.
Take a smooth function
\chi:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, such that
\chi=1
on
(0,1)
, and
\chi=0
on
(2,\infty)
.
By
Lemma

A.1
, we have

\mu_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{H}}-{\rm Re}[Y_{n}(x+\mathrm{i}y)]yf^{\prime}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)-{\rm Im}[Y_{n}(x+\mathrm{i}y)](yf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)+f(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

We note that
yf^{\prime}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)=f(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)=0
whenever
(x,y)\not\in[-K,K]\times[1,2]
.
Moreover,
yf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)=0
whenever
(x,y)\notin[-K,K]\times(0,2]
.
For
y\leq 2
, by the monotonicity in
Lemma

2.2
, we have
{\rm Im}[Y_{n}(x+\mathrm{i}y)]\leq\frac{2}{y}{\rm Im}[Y_{n}(x+2\mathrm{i})]
.
Therefore, we have that the integrand in the above integral is non-zero only in
[-K,K]\times(0,2]
; and it is bounded by a constant, which is independent of
n
by the uniform convergence of
Y_{n}
in
[-K,K]\times[1,2]
.
Thus we can apply dominated convergence theorem to deduce that the above integral converges to

\mu(f)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{H}}-{\rm Re}[Y(x+\mathrm{i}y)]yf^{\prime}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)-{\rm Im}[Y(x+\mathrm{i}y)](yf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)+f(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

So the conclusion follows.
∎

On the other hand, in the setting of particle-generated measures, vague topology convergence can imply pole convergence.

Lemma 2.8
.

For a sequence of particle-generated measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots
, such that
\mu_{k}\to\mu
as
k\to\infty
in the vague topology, the limit
\mu
must also be particle-generated.
Moreover, if there is some
K>0
such that
\mu_{k}([K,\infty))=0
for each
k
, then the followings are true.
We denote by
x^{k}_{i}
the
i
-th largest pole of
\mu_{k}
(with the convention that
x^{k}_{i}=-\infty
if there are less than
i
poles).
For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, either
x^{k}_{i}\to-\infty
as
k\to\infty
, or
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}_{i}
exists and is a pole of
\mu
.
Also, all the poles of
\mu
are given by such limits.

Proof.

By vague topology convergence, for any
a<b
, we have that
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}([a,b])\leq\mu([a,b])
, and
\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}((a,b))\geq\mu((a,b))
.
Then for any
a<b
with
\mu([a,b])<1
, we must have
\mu_{k}([a,b])=0
for
k
large enough, since each
\mu_{k}([a,b])
must be an integer.
Therefore,
\mu_{k}((a,b))=0
for
k
large enough, and
\mu((a,b))=0
.
These imply that
\mu
in any open interval is either
\geq 1
or zero.
Therefore, in any compact interval,
\mu
is supported on finitely many points.

Now take any
x
where
\mu(\{x\})>0
. Take any
a<x<b
such that
\mu((a,b))=\mu([a,b])=\mu(\{x\})
.
Then for
k
large enough,
\mu_{k}((a,b))=\mu_{k}([a,b])=\mu(\{x\})
; so
\mu(\{x\})\in\mathbb{N}
.
These imply that
\mu
is particle-generated.

Under the additional assumption, there is
\mu((K,\infty))=0
. So we can write the poles of
\mu
as
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots
(with the convention that
x_{i}=-\infty
if there are less than
i
poles).
Then we can show
x^{k}_{i}\to x_{i}
via induction in
i\in\mathbb{N}
, using that for each
a<b
with
a,b\not\in\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
\mu_{k}((a,b))=\mu((a,b))
for any
k
large enough.
∎

3.
Pole evolution: uniform rigidity in time

In this section, we prove a uniform in time estimate for the poles. More precisely, the following proposition states that for the SDE (
1.14
),
with high probability, its pole evolution gives a line ensemble (i.e., all the poles are bounded from above, and the trajectories are continuous), and the poles are close to the zeros of the Airy function, uniformly in time.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
{\mathfrak{d}},C_{*}>0
, there exist small
\delta,c>0
and large
C>0
, such that the following holds.
Take any particle-generated
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.10
, and large
T>0
. Conditional on the event that
Y_{0}
is
({\mathfrak{d}},C_{*})
-Airy-like,
with probability at least
1-e^{-c(\log T)^{2}}
, we have

(1)

The poles of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in[T,2T]}
give a line ensemble
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in[T,2T]}
, and for each
t\in[T,2T]
and
w\in\mathbb{H}
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle Y_{t}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}.

(2)

For each
t\in[T,2T]
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
y\leq C
, we have that

(3.2)

|x_{i}(t)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\leq\frac{C(\log T)^{40}}{i^{\delta}},

and

(3.3)

|\{i\in\mathbb{N}:x_{i}(t)\in[y-1,y+1]\}|\leq C\sqrt{|y|+1}.

This immediately implies the first part of
Theorem

1.15
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.9
and
Assumption

1.10
, its poles give a line ensemble
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, and
Y_{t}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-w}-\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}
for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Moreover, almost surely
\sup_{t\in[-T,T],i\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{i}(t)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|<\infty
for any
T>0
.

We note that (
3.3
) (which is a Wegner estimate) follows easily from (
3.2
), plus

|\{i\in\mathbb{N}:{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}(t)\in[y-1,y+1]\}|\leq C\sqrt{|y|+1},

which directly follows from (
1.9
).

Our general strategy is to obtain uniform in time estimates for
Y_{t}-\sqrt{w}
, and to apply
Lemma

2.3
.
The main tasks are (1) to estimate bulk pole densities, via bounding
|Y_{t}(w)-\sqrt{w}|
for
w
in a reasonable domain contained in
\mathbb{H}
(in particular, allowing for polynomially close to the real axis, when
{\rm Re}[w]\to-\infty
); (2) to bound the first pole
x_{1}
.
Both these are to be achieved through analyzing the SDE (
1.14
).

3.1.
Characteristic flow

We consider the characteristic flow,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\del_{t}w_{t}=-\sqrt{w_{t}},\quad w_{0}\in\mathbb{H},

which can be solved as

\del_{t}\sqrt{w_{t}}=-\frac{1}{2},\quad w_{t}=(\sqrt{w_{0}}-t/2)^{2}.

This flow is motivated by the equation (
1.14
).
More precisely, by ignoring the martingale and the second order derivatives, one considers the following PDE:

\partial_{t}y=y\partial_{w}y-\frac{1}{2},

for some smooth
y:{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\cup{\mathbb{R}}
. As we expect
y(t,w)
to be roughly
\sqrt{w}
, we can take
r(t,w)=y(t,w)-\sqrt{w}
, and the above equation becomes

\partial_{t}r=\sqrt{w}\partial_{w}r+r\partial_{w}r+\frac{r}{2\sqrt{w}}.

Then the flow (
3.4
) natually arises: if
r=0
at some
w\in\mathbb{H}
, we would have
r=0
along the flow (
3.4
) starting from
w
.

By plugging the characteristic flow (
3.4
) into (
1.16
), Itô’s formula gives the following semi-martingale decomposition for
Y_{t}(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}}
and
M_{t}(w_{t})

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\rm d}(Y_{t}(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}})=({\rm d}M_{t})(w_{t})+\left(\frac{2-\beta}{2\beta}(\del^{2}_{w}Y_{t})(w_{t})+(Y_{t}(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}})(\del_{w}Y_{t})(w_{t})\right){\rm d}t,\\
&{\rm d}(M_{t}(w_{t}))=({\rm d}M_{t})(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}}(\del_{w}M_{t})(w_{t}){\rm d}t.\end{split}

The quadratic variations of the martingale
\int_{0}^{\cdot}({\rm d}M_{s})(w_{s})
are the same as those of
M_{t}(w_{t})
, which are given by (recall from (
1.15
)):

(3.6)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}({\rm d}M_{s})(w_{s})\right\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{3\beta}(\del_{w}^{3}Y_{t})(w_{t}).

In the rest of this section, we fix
{\mathfrak{d}},C_{*}>0
, and take particle-generated
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.10
, and (unless otherwise noted) conditional on
Y_{0}
which is
({\mathfrak{d}},C_{*})
-Airy-like.
All the constants below (including those in
\lesssim
and
\OO(\cdot)
) can depend on
{\mathfrak{d}}
and
C_{*}
.
We take
T
to be a large number, and set
K=(\log T)^{8}
.

3.2.
Estimates for the bulk

We next prove the following bound of
|Y_{t}(w)-\sqrt{w}|
for
w
in a domain contained in
\mathbb{H}
.
It will be used to bound the bulk pole densities.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exist small
\delta,c>0
such that the following holds. Define the spectral domain

(3.7)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}=\{\kappa+\mathrm{i}\eta:\eta\geq K,\eta^{-1-\delta}K\leq\kappa\leq\sqrt{K^{2}+\eta^{2}}\}.

With probability at least
1-e^{-c(\log T)^{2}}
, for any
t\in[T,2T]
, and
\sqrt{w}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, it holds

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|Y_{t}(w)-\sqrt{w}\right|\leq\frac{{\rm Im}[\sqrt{w}]^{1-\delta}}{{\rm Im}[w]}.

Note that
Y_{t}
is Lipschitz away from the real axis by
Lemma

2.2
. Therefore,
Y_{t}(w)-\sqrt{w}
is also Lipschitz, and we only need to prove (
3.8
) for a set of carefully chosen mesh points.
Namely, we consider the following mesh of points in the upper half plane:

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\kappa+\mathrm{i}\eta:\eta^{3}\in\mathbb{Z},\eta\geq K,\kappa=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{\eta^{2}},K\leq\kappa\leq T+K+\eta\right\}.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
\kappa^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{D}}
as defined in (
3.7
) and any
t\in[T,2T]
, there exists
\kappa+\mathrm{i}\eta\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that
t\leq 2\kappa-2\eta^{-1-\delta}K
, and

(3.10)

\displaystyle|\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa+t/2|,|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}.

Proof.

Suppose that
\eta^{\prime}\in[i^{1/3},(i+1)^{1/3}]
for some integer
i\geq K^{3}
, and
\kappa^{\prime}+t/2\in[j\eta^{-2},(j+1)\eta^{-2}]
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we can take
\eta=i^{1/3}
and
\kappa=(j+1)\eta^{-2}
.

Then for
\eta
we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle|\eta-\eta^{\prime}|\leq(i+1)^{1/3}-i^{1/3}\leq i^{-2/3}/3=\eta^{-2}/3.

For
\kappa
we have
|\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa+t/2|\leq\eta^{-2}
, and
\kappa\geq\kappa^{\prime}+t/2\geq\kappa^{\prime}+T/2\geq T/2>K
. Moreover,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}+t/2+\eta^{-2}\leq\kappa^{\prime}+T+\eta^{-2}\leq\sqrt{K^{2}+(\eta^{\prime})^{2}}+T+\eta^{-2}\leq T+K+\eta,

where we used
\kappa^{\prime}\leq\sqrt{K^{2}+(\eta^{\prime})^{2}}
because
\kappa^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{D}}
. Also
\kappa-t/2\geq\kappa^{\prime}\geq(\eta^{\prime})^{-1-\delta}K\geq\eta^{-1-\delta}K
, thus
t\leq 2\kappa-2\eta^{-1-\delta}K
.
∎

Now
Proposition

3.3
follows from the following estimate on one point.

Lemma 3.5
.

The following holds true for small enough
\delta,c>0
.
Take any
\kappa_{0}+\eta\mathrm{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}
, and for each
t<2\kappa_{0}
let

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sqrt{w_{t}}=(\kappa_{0}+\eta\mathrm{i})-t/2=:\kappa_{t}+\eta\mathrm{i},\quad\kappa_{t}=\kappa_{0}-\frac{t}{2}.

Conditional on
Y_{0}
with
|Y_{0}(w_{0})-\sqrt{w_{0}}|\leq{{\rm Im}[\sqrt{w_{0}}]^{1-{\mathfrak{d}}}}/{{\rm Im}[w_{0}]}
, with probability at least
1-e^{-c\sqrt{\eta}}
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle|Y_{t}(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}}|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{t}\eta^{\delta}},\quad\forall\;0\leq t\leq 2T\wedge(2\kappa_{0}-2\eta^{-1-\delta}K).

Proof of
Proposition

3.3
.

By a union bound over all the points in
{\mathcal{L}}
,
Lemma

3.5
implies that

(3.15)

\displaystyle|Y_{t}(w_{t})-\sqrt{w_{t}}|\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{t}\eta^{\delta}},\quad\forall\;0\leq t\leq 2T\wedge(2\kappa_{0}-2\eta^{-1-\delta}K),\;\sqrt{w_{0}}\in{\mathcal{L}},

with probability at least

(3.16)

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\eta\geq K,\eta^{3}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-c\sqrt{\eta}}\eta^{2}(2T+2\eta)\geq 1-e^{-c(\log T)^{2}},

where we used that
K=(\log T)^{8}
and
T
is large enough.

For any
t\in[T,2T]
and
\sqrt{w^{\prime}}=\kappa^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\eta^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, thanks to
Lemma

3.4
, there exists
\sqrt{w_{0}}=\kappa+\mathrm{i}\eta\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that
|\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa_{t}|,|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|\leq\eta^{-2}
.
Then we have
\eta^{-1-\delta}K/2<\kappa_{t}<2\eta
.
It also follows that

(3.17)

|\sqrt{w_{t}}-\sqrt{w^{\prime}}|\leq\sqrt{|\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa_{t}|^{2}+|\eta^{\prime}-\eta|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}\eta^{-2},

|x_{i}(0)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\leq B,\quad\forall i\in\llbracket k/2,2k\rrbracket,

with probability at least
1-e^{-ck^{1/6}}
we have

\max_{0\leq s\leq\xi}|x_{k}(s)-x_{k}(0)|\leq Ck^{2/3}\xi^{1/2}.

Proof.

We first prove the (with conditional probability
>1-e^{-ck^{1/6}}
) upper bound

(4.2)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq s\leq\xi}x_{k}(s)\leq x_{k}(0)+Ck^{2/3}\xi^{1/2}.

The lower bound
\min_{0\leq s\leq\xi}x_{k}(s)\geq x_{k}(0)-Ck^{2/3}\xi^{1/2}
can be proven in the same way.

By (
4.1
) with (
1.9
), we conclude that there exists a large constant
C_{1}
such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle|\{i:x_{i}(0)\in[x_{k}(0)-1,x_{k}(0)+1]\}|\leq C_{1}k^{1/3}.

Take small
\delta\leq 1/(C_{1}k^{1/3})
. Then (
4.3
) implies that there exists some
1\leq\ell\leq C_{1}k^{1/3}
, such that
x_{k-\ell-1}(0)-x_{k-\ell}(0)\geq\delta
. We take the smallest such
\ell
, then
|x_{k-\ell}(0)-x_{k}(0)|\leq\delta\ell
.

Let
E=x_{k-\ell}(0)+\delta/2
, and take a small
b>0
and
w=E+\mathrm{i}b
. Next we show that, provided
\delta\geq 8bC_{1}^{1/2}k^{1/6}
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}[Y_{0}(w)]={\rm Im}[Y_{0}(E+\mathrm{i}b)]=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{b}{|E+\mathrm{i}b-x_{i}(0)|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4b}.

For this, from (
4.1
) and (
1.9
), we have
|\{i:x_{i}(0)\in[E-\delta/2-m,E-\delta/2-(m-1)]\}|\leq C_{1}m^{1/2}k^{1/3}
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i:x_{i}(0)\leq E-\delta/2}\frac{b}{|E+\mathrm{i}b-x_{i}(0)|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i:x_{i}(0)\in[E-\delta/2-m,E-\delta/2-(m-1)]}\frac{b}{|E+\mathrm{i}b-x_{i}(0)|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{C_{1}bm^{1/2}k^{1/3}}{(\delta/2+m-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{8C_{1}bk^{1/3}}{\delta^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{8b},

using that
\delta\geq 8bC_{1}^{1/2}k^{1/6}
.
By a similar argument, we can also upper bound the summation over
x_{i}(0)\geq E+\delta/2
, and (
4.4
) follows.

We now introduce a stopping time
\tau
:

\tau=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:{\rm Im}[Y_{s}(w)]\geq\frac{1}{2b}\right\}\wedge\xi.

For
0\leq s\leq\tau
, it follows from
Lemma

2.2

|\partial_{w}^{2}Y_{s}(w)|\leq\frac{2{\rm Im}[Y_{s}(w)]}{b^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{b^{3}},\quad|\del_{w}(Y_{s}(w)^{2})|=2|\partial_{w}Y_{s}(w)|\cdot|Y_{s}(w)|\leq\frac{|Y_{s}(w)|}{b^{2}}.

Thus by
Assumption

1.10
,

(4.5)

\displaystyle|Y_{s}(w)-Y_{0}(w)|\leq\left|M_{s}(w)-M_{0}(w)\right|+\frac{\int_{0}^{s}|Y_{u}(w)|{\rm d}u}{2b^{2}}+\OO\left(\frac{s}{b^{3}}\right).

For the martingale term,
using (
1.15
) and
Lemma

2.2
, it follows that (for
0\leq s\leq\tau
)

\left|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\langle M(w)\rangle_{s}\right|\leq\frac{2{\rm Im}[Y_{s}(w)]}{\beta b^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta b^{4}}.

Therefore, by the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, there exists a small constant
c_{1}>0
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq\tau}\left|M_{s}(w)-M_{0}(w)\right|\geq\frac{k^{1/6}\xi^{1/2}}{b^{2}}\right)\leq e^{-c_{1}k^{1/6}}

By plugging (
4.6
) into (
4.5
), it follows that with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{1}k^{1/6}}
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left|Y_{s}(w)-Y_{0}(w)\right|=\OO\left(\frac{k^{1/6}\xi^{1/2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{\xi}{b^{3}}\right),\quad\forall s\in[0,\tau],

provided that
\xi\leq b^{2}
.
Now we take a large
C_{2}>0
, and assume that
\xi\leq b^{2}/(C_{2}k^{1/3})
. Then for any
s\in[0,\tau]
, and using (
4.4
), we have

{\rm Im}[Y_{s}(w)]\leq|Y_{s}(w)-Y_{0}(w)|+{\rm Im}[Y_{0}(w)]<\frac{1}{2b},

which, in particular, implies that
\tau=\xi
. Since
{\rm Im}[Y_{s}(w)]=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{b}{|w-x_{i}(s)|^{2}}
, this further implies that
\{x_{i}(s)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\cap[E-b,E+b]=\emptyset
, for any
s\in[0,\xi]
.

In summary, we conclude that with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{1}k^{1/6}}
,
\{x_{i}(s)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\cap[E-b,E+b]=\emptyset
, for any
s\in[0,\xi]
.
Since
x_{k}(0)<E
, it follows that (with probability at least
1-e^{-c_{1}k^{1/6}}
) for any
s\in[0,\xi]
,

x_{k}(s)\leq E-b=x_{k-\ell}(0)+\delta/2-b\leq x_{k}(0)+\delta\ell+\delta/2\leq x_{k}(0)+2C_{1}k^{1/3}\delta.

Finally, we choose the parameter
\delta
and
b
, satisfying all the above constraints:

\delta\leq 1/(C_{1}k^{1/3}),\quad\delta\geq 8bC_{1}^{1/2}k^{1/6},\quad\xi\leq b^{2}/(C_{2}k^{1/3}).

Then we can take
b=C_{2}^{1/2}k^{1/6}\xi^{1/2}
and
\delta=8C_{1}^{1/2}C_{2}^{1/2}k^{1/3}\xi^{1/2}
.
By taking
C
large enough and
c
small enough (depending on
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
c_{1}
), the constraint
\delta\leq 1/(C_{1}k^{1/3})
is also satisfied since
\xi<ck^{-4/3}
; and (
4.2
) follows.
∎

5.
Recover Dyson Brownian Motion

In this section, we localize any line ensemble given by the poles of the SDE (
1.14
), by deriving another SDE satisfied by the first finitely many poles (in the sense of weak solution).

More precisely, for the line ensemble
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of poles,
we prove that, if for some large
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{k}(t)
and
x_{k+1}(t)
are bounded away from each other for certain amount of time, the evolution of
x_{1}(t)\geq x_{2}(t)\geq\cdots\geq x_{k}(t)
is then described by DBM plus a drift term, describing the effect of
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i=k+1}^{\infty}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
C>0
the following is true.
Take any particle-generated
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.10
, and that its poles are given by a line ensemble
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
.
Fix a large
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and denote the stopping time

\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0:x_{k}(t)-x_{k+1}(t)\leq 1/(Ck^{1/3})\}\wedge 1.

We consider the line ensemble
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
conditional on
x_{k}(0)-x_{k+1}(0)>1/(Ck^{1/3})
(i.e.,
\tau>0
).
For this conditional process,
there exist independent Brownian motions
B_{1},\cdots,B_{k}
adapted to the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}=\sigma(\{{\bm{x}}(u)\}_{u\leq t})
, satisfying (again in the sense of the differential form of the semimartingale decomposition)

(5.1)

{\rm d}x_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k\atop j\neq i}\frac{{\rm d}t}{x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)}+W_{t}(x_{i}(t)){\rm d}t,\quad\forall i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket,\;t\in[0,\tau],

where
W_{t}
is a random meromorphic function, defined as

(5.2)

\displaystyle W_{t}(w):=-Y_{t}(w)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-w}.

Moreover, almost surely the following holds:

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathds{1}(\exists 1\leq i<j\leq k:x_{i}(t)=x_{j}(t)){\rm d}t=0.

Remark 5.2
.

We remark that for
t\leq\tau
,
x_{k}(t)-x_{k+1}(t)\leq 1/Ck^{1/3}
. In this regime the simple poles of the sum in (
5.2
) at
w=x_{i}(t)
,
1\leq i\leq k
, cancel with the corresponding singular parts of
Y_{t}(w)
; hence these singularities are removable. Consequently,
W_{t}
is analytic at
w=x_{i}(t)
for
1\leq i\leq k
, and the values
W_{t}\big(x_{i}(t)\big)
are well defined (as limits
w\to x_{i}(t)
).

The rest of this section is devoted to proving
Proposition

5.1
.
Using that
\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are poles of
Y_{t}
, we derive (
5.1
) from
Assumption

1.10
, using a contour integral.
For this, we need first establish that the poles do not collide at almost every time (i.e., (
5.3
)).
The idea to establish the collision time estimate is to consider the process
(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))^{2}
for some
i<j
, showing that its level-
0
local time equals
0
. We mainly follow the standard argument used to study the Bessel process, see
[
117
, Chapter XI, Section 1]
.
To analyze such processes we again resort to contour integrals, and therefore an induction will be used.

We next give the semi-martingale decomposition of a process, which is the sum of
(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))^{2}
for
i,j
in an interval.

For any particle-generated
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.10
, with poles given by the line ensemble
\{{\bm{x}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, and any
a,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
with
\alpha\geq 2
, denote

(5.4)

W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(w)=-Y_{t}(w)+\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-w},

and

Z^{a,\alpha}(t)=\sum_{a\leq i<j\leq a+\alpha-1}(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))^{2}=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)\right)^{2}.

Lemma 5.3
.

In the above setup, take any
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and denote the stopping time

\sigma=\inf\{t\geq t_{0}:x_{a-1}(t)=x_{a}(t)\text{ or }x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)=x_{a+\alpha}(t)\text{ or }t=t_{0}+1\},

where we use the convention that
x_{0}(t)=\infty
, when
a=1
.
Then
Z^{a,\alpha}
in
[t_{0},\sigma)
satisfies
{\rm d}Z^{a,\alpha}(t)={\rm d}M^{a,\alpha}(t)+V^{a,\alpha}(t){\rm d}t
,
where

V^{a,\alpha}(t)=2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq\alpha}(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))(W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{i})-W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{j}))+\alpha^{2}(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\beta},

and
{\rm d}M^{a,\alpha}(t)
is the martingale term, with quadratic variation

(5.5)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle M^{a,\alpha}\rangle_{t}=\frac{8\alpha}{\beta}Z^{a,\alpha}(t).

Proof.

For simplicity of notations, we fix
a,\alpha
, and write
Z(t)=Z(t)^{a,\alpha}
,
V(t)=V(t)^{a,\alpha}
, an
{\rm d}M(t)={\rm d}M(t)^{a,\alpha}
within this proof.

For
t
with
x_{a-1}(t)>x_{a}(t)
and
x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)>x_{a+\alpha}(t)
, we take a contour
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{t}
enclosing
x_{a}(t),\cdots,x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)
, but not any
x_{i}(t)
for
i<a
or
i\geq a+\alpha
.
Since each
x_{i}(t)
is continuous in
t
, we have that
{\mathcal{C}}
encloses
x_{a}(t^{\prime}),\cdots,x_{a+\alpha-1}(t^{\prime})
, but not any
x_{i}(t^{\prime})
for
i<a
or
i\geq a+\alpha
, for each
t^{\prime}
in a small open neighborhood of
t
.
Then by
Assumption

1.10
, we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)&=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w{\rm d}Y_{t}(w){\rm d}w\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w{\rm d}w\left({\rm d}M_{t}(w)+\left(\frac{2-\beta}{2\beta}\del^{2}_{w}Y_{t}(w)+\frac{1}{2}\del_{w}(Y_{t}(w)^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\right){\rm d}t\right).\end{split}

We remark that for this expression, the corresponding integral semimartingale indentity is only for a small time interval containing
t
, with
{\mathcal{C}}
being fixed and enclosing
x_{a}(t^{\prime}),\cdots,x_{a+\alpha-1}(t^{\prime})
, but not any
x_{i}(t^{\prime})
for
i<a
or
i\geq a+\alpha
, for each
t^{\prime}
in the interval.

Note that

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w{\rm d}w=0,\quad\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w\partial_{w}^{2}Y_{t}(w){\rm d}w=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2w{\rm d}w}{(x_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}=0,

and

-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{w}{2}\partial_{w}(Y_{t}(w)^{2}){\rm d}w=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{Y_{t}(w)^{2}}{2}{\rm d}w=\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t)).

Note that the poles of
W_{t}^{i,1}
are
x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\ldots
, except for
x_{i}(t)
.
Then we have

(5.7)

{\rm d}\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w{\rm d}M_{t}(w){\rm d}w+\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{i}(t)){\rm d}t,

using that
\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t))=\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{i}(t))
.

Similarly, we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x^{2}_{i}(t)&=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w^{2}{\rm d}Y_{t}(w){\rm d}w\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w^{2}{\rm d}w\left({\rm d}M_{t}(w)+\left(\frac{2-\beta}{2\beta}\del^{2}_{w}Y_{t}(w)+\frac{1}{2}\del_{w}(Y_{t}(w)^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\right){\rm d}t\right)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w^{2}{\rm d}M_{t}(w){\rm d}w+\left(2\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{a,\alpha}(x_{i}(t))x_{i}(t)+\alpha(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha}{\beta}\right){\rm d}t.\end{split}

Here for the last equality, we used that

\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w^{2}{\rm d}w=0,\quad-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}w^{2}\partial_{w}^{2}Y_{t}(w){\rm d}w=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2w^{2}{\rm d}w}{(x_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}=2\alpha,

-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{w^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}(Y_{t}(w)^{2}){\rm d}w=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}wY_{t}(w)^{2}{\rm d}w=2\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t))x_{i}(t),

and that
\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t))x_{i}(t)=\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{i}(t))x_{i}(t)+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2}
.

Now using (
5.6
) and (
5.8
), and Itô’s formula, we can write
{\rm d}Z(t)={\rm d}M(t)+V(t){\rm d}t
, with

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(t)=&2\alpha\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{a,\alpha}(x_{i}(t))x_{i}(t)+\alpha^{2}(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\\
&-2\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)\right)\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t))\right)-\frac{2}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}\beta}\oiint_{{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}ww^{\prime}\del_{w}\del_{w^{\prime}}\frac{Y_{t}(w)-Y_{t}(w^{\prime})}{w-w^{\prime}}{\rm d}w{\rm d}w^{\prime}\\
=&2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq\alpha}(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))(W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{i})-W^{a,\alpha}_{t}(x_{j}))+\alpha^{2}(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\beta}.\end{split}

Here we used (
1.15
) in the first equality.
For the second equality, it is by evaluating the contour integral in
w
and
w^{\prime}
, via integration by parts.

As for
{\rm d}M(t)
, we have

{\rm d}M(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(2w\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w^{2}\right){\rm d}M_{t}(w){\rm d}w.

By (
1.15
), the quadratic variation
{\rm d}\langle M\rangle_{t}/{\rm d}t
therefore equals

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}\beta}\oiint_{{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}\left(2w\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w^{2}\right)\left(2w^{\prime}\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w^{\prime 2}\right)\del_{w}\del_{w^{\prime}}\frac{Y_{t}(w)-Y_{t}(w^{\prime})}{w-w^{\prime}}{\rm d}w{\rm d}w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}\beta}\oiint_{{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w\right)\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w^{\prime}\right)\frac{Y_{t}(w)-Y_{t}(w^{\prime})}{w-w^{\prime}}{\rm d}w{\rm d}w^{\prime}.

By taking the
w^{\prime}
residues at
x_{a}(t),\ldots,x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)
, we get

\frac{8}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\beta}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha w\right)\left(\sum_{j=a}^{a+\alpha-1}\frac{\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)-\alpha x_{j}(t)}{w-x_{j}(t)}\right){\rm d}w.

By further taking the
w
residues at
x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{\alpha}(t)
, this equals

\frac{8\alpha}{\beta}\left(\alpha\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}^{2}(t)-\left(\sum_{i=a}^{a+\alpha-1}x_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{8\alpha}{\beta}Z(t),

and the conclusion follows.
∎

We next establish the collision time estimate, for poles whose indices are in an interval.

Lemma 5.4
.

Under the same setup as
Lemma

5.3
, almost surely

\int_{t_{0}}^{\sigma}\mathds{1}[x_{a}(t)=x_{a+1}(t)=\cdots=x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)]{\rm d}t=0.

Proof.

Again, we write
Z(t)=Z(t)^{a,\alpha}
and
V(t)=V(t)^{a,\alpha}
in this proof.

We use the local time of
Z(t)
to analyze its boundary behavior at zero.
According to
Lemma

5.3
,
Z(t)
for
t\in[t_{0},\sigma)
is a semi-martingale. We let
L_{t}^{h}
be the level
h
local time in
[t_{0},\sigma)
(with
L_{t_{0}}^{h}=0
for each
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
). Then by
[
117
, Chapter VI, Theorem 1.7]
, almost surely
L_{t}^{h}
is continuous in
t
and cadlag (right continuous) in
h
, and
L_{t}^{0}=L_{t}^{0}-L_{t}^{0-}
satisfies

(5.9)

L_{\sigma}^{0}=2\int_{t_{0}}^{\sigma}\mathds{1}(Z(t)=0)V(t){\rm d}t=\left(\alpha^{2}(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\beta}\right)\int_{t_{0}}^{\sigma}\mathds{1}(Z(t)=0){\rm d}t,

where for the second equality, we used that if
Z(t)=0
, then
x_{a}(t)=\cdots=x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)
and
V(t)=\alpha^{2}(\alpha-2)+\frac{2\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\beta}
.

Thanks to the occupation time formula
[
117
, Chapter VI, Corollary 1.6]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h^{-1}L_{\sigma}^{h}{\rm d}h=\int_{t_{0}}^{\sigma}Z(t)^{-1}{\rm d}\langle Z\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{8\alpha}{\beta}<\infty,

where we used (
5.5
) which gives

{\rm d}\langle Z\rangle_{t}=8\alpha Z(t){\rm d}t/\beta
, and
\sigma\leq t_{0}+1
.
Since
L^{h}_{\sigma}
is right continuous in
h
, it follows that
L^{0}_{\sigma}=0
, and hence (
5.9
) implies that almost surely
\int_{t_{0}}^{\sigma}\mathds{1}(Z(t)=0){\rm d}t=0
.
Thus the conclusion follows.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.1
.

We take the following two steps.

Step 1: Non-collision.

We will first show (
5.3
), i.e.,
{\rm d}t
almost everywhere poles do not collide. More precisely, we will prove inductively on
\ell=1,2,3,\cdots,k-1

(5.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathds{1}(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\cdots,x_{k}(t)\text{ take at most $\ell$ distinct values}){\rm d}t=0.

The claim of
Proposition

5.1
follows from the case of
\ell=k-1
in (
5.10
).

For the base case where
\ell=1
, it follows from
Lemma

5.4
with
a=1
and
\alpha=k
, and
t_{0}=0
.
(Note that in this case, we always have
\sigma\geq\tau
)

We next give the induction step: if (
5.10
) holds for some
1\leq\ell<k-1
, then it holds for
\ell+1
.

Under the induction hypothesis,
{\rm d}t
almost everywhere, there exist
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{\ell}<k
such that

(5.11)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha_{1}}(t)>x_{\alpha_{1}+1}(t),\quad x_{\alpha_{2}}(t)>x_{\alpha_{2}+1}(t),\quad\cdots,\quad x_{\alpha_{\ell}}(t)>x_{\alpha_{\ell}+1}(t),

Fix the indices
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{\ell}<k
, the set of time
t\in[0,\tau]
such that (
5.11
) holds is a random open set, and we denote it by
I\subset[0,\tau]
.

Next we show that almost surely, for almost every
t\in I
,
x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\cdots,x_{k}(t)
take at least
\ell+2
distinct values. This implies that (
5.10
) holds for
\ell+1
.
For the convenience of notations, we denote
\alpha_{0}=0
and
\alpha_{\ell+1}=k
.
Since
\ell\leq k-2
, there exists some
\nu\in\llbracket 0,\ell\rrbracket
such that
\alpha_{\nu+1}-\alpha_{\nu}\geq 2
.
We then apply
Lemma

5.4
with
a=\alpha_{\nu}+1
and
\alpha=\alpha_{\nu+1}-\alpha_{\nu}
, and
t_{0}
taking any rational numbers.
We note that the union of all such
[t_{0},\sigma)
would cover
I
, therefore

\int_{I}\mathds{1}(x_{a}(t)=\cdots=x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)){\rm d}t=0.

Thus for almost every
t\in I
,
x_{a}(t),\cdots,x_{a+\alpha-1}(t)
would take at least two distinct values, so we finish the induction step.

Then by induction principle, we finish the proof of (
5.3
).

Step 2: Dyson Brownian motion.

We next prove (
5.1
). For that we need to construct the Brownian motions
B_{i}(t)
in (
5.1
).
By (
5.3
) and that each
x_{i}(t)
is continuous, for any
t\in[0,\tau]
outside a closed measure zero set (i.e., in a countable union of open intervals, whose closure is
[0,\tau]
), we have
x_{i}(t)>x_{i+1}(t)
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
.
Then we can take a small contour
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}={\mathcal{C}}_{i,t}
enclosing
x_{i}(t)
but not any other poles.
From (
5.7
) in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
, we have

(5.12)

{\rm d}x_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)+W_{t}^{i,1}(x_{i}(t)){\rm d}t,

where

{\rm d}B_{i}(t)=-\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}w{\rm d}M_{t}(w){\rm d}w.

We remark that, like (
5.6
), here the corresponding integral semimartingale indentity is for a small time interval containing
t
, such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is fixed and encloses
x_{i}(t^{\prime})
but not any other pole, for each
t^{\prime}
in the interval.
We can then further extend
B_{i}(t)
to all of
[0,\tau]
as a continuous process. The quadratic variations are given by

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle B_{i},B_{j}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\times{\mathcal{C}}_{j}}ww^{\prime}\del_{w}\del_{w^{\prime}}\frac{Y_{t}(w)-Y_{t}(w^{\prime})}{w-w^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\times{\mathcal{C}}_{j}}\frac{Y_{t}(w)-Y_{t}(w^{\prime})}{w-w^{\prime}},

which equals
\mathds{1}(i=j)
.
Then it follows that
\{B_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{k}
are independent Brownian motions.

Noting that
W_{t}=W_{t}^{1,k}
, we get (
5.1
) from (
5.12
).
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Another approach to study DBM developed in
[
66
]
is based on applying Itô’s formula to the elementary symmetric functions
\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\cdots<j_{n}}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{n}}
. There a large family of Dyson type interacting particle systems are considered.
For
\beta\geq 1
, they show that if the initial data has some particles at the same location, they will separate instantly. The method there could potentially be adapted and derive non-collision in the above proof as well.

6.
Coupling and Uniqueness

In this section we prove the uniqueness part of
Theorem

1.15
.

Take any
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfying
Assumption

1.9
and
Assumption

1.10
. Let
\{{\bm{x}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}=\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be the line ensemble given by its poles (from
Corollary

3.2
).
We also take another line ensemble
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}=\{y_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
through the same way.

Proposition 6.1
.

The two line ensembles
\{{\bm{x}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
have the same law.

Our general strategy is to construct a coupling of the dynamics in
t
, where
{\bm{x}}(t)
and
{\bm{y}}(t)
would get closer as
t
increases.
Then by sending the starting time of the dynamics to
-\infty
, one concludes that these two line ensembles must equal in law.

The coupling.

There are four parameters
\delta,C,T,n
in the definition of this coupling.
Here
\delta,C>0
are small and large real numbers;

-T
is among the sequence
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots\to-\infty
in
Assumption

1.9
, and
T
is large enough depending on
\delta,C
; and we let
n=\lfloor T\rfloor
.
We shall mainly consider the dynamics of the first order
n
many paths, for
t\in[-T,T]
.

For each
t\in[-T,T]
, let
{\mathcal{E}}[t]
be the event where

|x_{i}(t)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|,|y_{i}(t)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}|\leq\frac{C(\log T)^{40}}{i^{\delta}},

for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

For each
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2Tn^{3}\rrbracket
, denote
t_{\ell}=-T+\ell n^{-3}
.
Under
{\mathcal{E}}[t_{\ell}]
, by (
1.9
), we let
k_{\ell}
and
k^{\prime}_{\ell}
be the smallest numbers in
[\![n,2n-1
]​], such that

|x_{k_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})-x_{k_{\ell}+1}(t_{\ell})|\geq\frac{2}{C^{\prime}n^{1/3}},\quad|y_{k_{\ell}^{\prime}}(t_{\ell})-y_{k_{\ell}^{\prime}+1}(t_{\ell})|\geq\frac{2}{C^{\prime}n^{1/3}},

where
C^{\prime}
is a large enough universal constant. Note that when
n
is large enough depending on
C^{\prime}
, such
k_{\ell}
and
k^{\prime}_{\ell}
exist.

We introduce a stopping time
\tau
(with respect to the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}=\sigma(\{{\bm{x}}(u)\}_{u\leq t},\{{\bm{y}}(u)\}_{u\leq t})
), as follows.
If there exists any
t\in[-T,T]
such that
{\mathcal{E}}[t]
does not hold, or if there is any
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2Tn^{3}-1\rrbracket
and
t\in[t_{\ell},t_{\ell+1}]
, such that

|x_{k_{\ell}}(t)-x_{k_{\ell}+1}(t)|\wedge|y_{k_{\ell}^{\prime}}(t)-y_{k_{\ell}^{\prime}+1}(t)|\leq\frac{1}{C^{\prime}n^{1/3}},

we let
\tau
be the smallest such
t
.
Otherwise, we let
\tau=\infty
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta,C>0
, such that for
T
and
n=\lfloor T\rfloor
large enough, under the above coupling we have
\mathbb{P}[\tau=\infty]\geq 1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.1
, for small enough
\delta
, large enough
C
and
n
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcap_{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathcal{E}}[t]\right]\geq 1-\varepsilon/2
.
Then by the Hölder continuity estimate
Proposition

4.1
,

\mathbb{P}[\tau=\infty]\geq 1-\varepsilon/2-2Tn^{4}e^{-cn^{1/6}}\geq 1-\varepsilon,

where
c>0
is small enough depending on
C
, and the second inequality is by taking
n
large.
∎

By
Proposition

5.1
, we can find a family of independent Brownian motions
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n}
, such that for each
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2Tn^{3}-1\rrbracket
and
t\in[t_{\ell}\wedge\tau,t_{\ell+1}\wedge\tau]
,
i\in\llbracket 1,k_{\ell}\rrbracket
,

(6.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}x_{i}(t)&=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)+\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq k_{\ell}\atop j\neq i}\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)}+W_{t}(x_{i}(t))\right){\rm d}t,\\
W_{t}(w)&=\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}}\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}+\sum_{i=k_{\ell}+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{w-x_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right),\end{split}

where we used
Corollary

3.2
for the expression of

.
We can similarly find a family of independent Brownian motions
\{\overline{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n}
, such that for each
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2Tn^{3}-1\rrbracket
and
t\in[t_{\ell}\wedge\tau,t_{\ell+1}\wedge\tau]
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}y_{i}(t)&=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}\overline{B}_{i}(t)+\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq k_{\ell}^{\prime}\atop j\neq i}\frac{1}{y_{i}(t)-y_{j}(t)}+\overline{W}_{t}(y_{i}(t))\right){\rm d}t,\\
\overline{W}_{t}(w)&=\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}+\sum_{i=k_{\ell}^{\prime}+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{w-y_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right).\end{split}

We now couple
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n}
and
\{\overline{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n}
in a common probablity space so that they equal almost surely.
Thereby, we get a coupling between
\{{\bm{x}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
in a common probability space.

The following proposition states that under this coupling, these two line ensembles are close to each other with high probability.

Proposition 6.3
.

Fix any
\varepsilon,\theta>0
and
S>0
. Then there exist
\delta,C>0
, such that for
T
and
n=\lfloor T\rfloor
large enough, under the above coupling with probability at least
1-\varepsilon

|x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)|\leq\theta,\quad\forall t\in[-S,S],\;i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket.

In the following, we will prove that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)\leq y_{i}(t)+\theta,\quad\forall t\in[-S,S],\;i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket.

The lower bound that
x_{i}(t)\geq y_{i}(t)-\theta
can be proven in the same way.

Our strategy is to consider a shifted version of
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, which at
t=-T
is much larger than
{\bm{x}}(-T)
;
then we show that it is larger than
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
for
t\in[-S,S]
(under the coupling), while the amount of shift is
\leq\theta
in
[-S,S]
.

We now define the shifted version of
\{{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
. For any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we let

\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)=y_{i}(t)+M-\kappa(t+T),

where
M
and
\kappa
are taken as follows.
By
Assumption

1.9
and
Corollary

2.4
, we take
M
taken large enough (depending only on
\varepsilon
) such that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
,

(6.4)

\displaystyle y_{i}(-T)+M>x_{i}(-T),\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.

We then take
\kappa
such that

M-\kappa(T-S)=\theta.

Then for
n=\lfloor T\rfloor
large enough (depending on
M,S,\theta
), the above choice of parameters imply

(6.5)

\displaystyle\kappa<\frac{2M}{n},\quad M-\kappa(S+T)=\theta-2S\kappa\geq\frac{\theta}{2}.

We can now rewrite (
6.2
) in terms of
\{\widetilde{\bm{y}}(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
. For each
\ell\in\llbracket 0,2Tn^{3}-1\rrbracket
and
t\in[t_{\ell}\wedge\tau,t_{\ell+1}\wedge\tau]
,
i\in\llbracket 1,k_{\ell}^{\prime}\rrbracket
, we have

(6.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm d}\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)&=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)+\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq k_{\ell}^{\prime}\atop j\neq i}\frac{1}{\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t)}+\widetilde{W}_{t}(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))\right){\rm d}t,\\
\widetilde{W}_{t}(w)&=\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}-\kappa+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\ell}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}+\sum_{i=k_{\ell}^{\prime}+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{w-\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)}+\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right).\end{split}

Lemma 6.4
.

There exist
\delta,C
, such for any
T
and
n=\lfloor T\rfloor
large enough, under the above coupling the following holds. Take any
\ell\in\llbracket 0,(S+T)n^{3}\rrbracket
. Assuming that

\widetilde{y}_{i}(t_{\ell}\wedge\tau)>x_{i}(t_{\ell}\wedge\tau),\quad\forall i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,

then

(6.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)>x_{i}(t),\quad\forall t\in[t_{\ell}\wedge\tau,t_{\ell+1}\wedge\tau],\;i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket.

Assuming this lemma, we can now finish proving the uniqueness in law of line ensembles.

Proof of
Proposition

6.3
.

As already alluded to, it suffices to prove (
6.3
).
From our choice of
M
(see (
6.4
)), we have that
\widetilde{y}_{i}(-T)>x_{i}(-T)
for all
i\geq 1
. Then by repeatedly applying
Lemma

6.4
for
\ell\in\llbracket 0,(S+T)n^{3}\rrbracket
, and
Lemma

6.2
, we conclude that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, we have
\tau=\infty
and
\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)>x_{i}(t)
for all

and

. In particular for

, this gives

\displaystyle y_{i}(t)+\theta=y_{i}(t)+(M-\kappa(T-S))\geq y_{i}(t)+(M-\kappa(t+T))>x_{i}(t).

This finishes the proof of (
6.3
).
∎

Proof of

6.1
.

The conclusion follows from taking

to zero and

The rest of this section is devoted to proving

The idea is straightforward: from the coupling we take the difference between (

) and (

), to cancel out the Brownian motions; and the rest are deterministic arguments.

Proof of

.

For simplicity of notation, in this proof we fix

and

. Recall that

We can take the difference between (

,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{{}={}}{\rm d}(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t))=\sum_{j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,j\neq i}\frac{(x_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))-(x_{j}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))}{(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))}{\rm d}t\\
&+\left(\widetilde{W}_{t}(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))+\sum_{j=n+1}^{k^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t)}-W_{t}(x_{i}(t))-\sum_{j=n+1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)}\right){\rm d}t.\end{split}

Denote the stopping time

to be the first time after

, such that there exists at least one index

with

We prove by contradiction, and assume that
\sigma<t_{\ell+1}\wedge\tau
. By the definition of the stopping time
\tau

i\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\geq n/3
, and

, we have

(6.9)

x_{i}(t)\leq{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}+\frac{C(\log T)^{40}}{i^{\delta}}\leq y_{i}(t)+\frac{2C(\log T)^{40}}{i^{\delta}}<y_{i}(t)+M-\kappa(t+T)=\widetilde{y}_{i}(t).

We let
a
(resp.

(resp.

);

By (

.
Now for (

\sigma

\displaystyle\begin{split}0>&\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-x_{i}(t))\Big|^{\sigma}_{\sigma-\iota}=\int_{\sigma-\iota}^{\sigma}\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}\sum_{j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\setminus\llbracket a,b^{\prime}\rrbracket}\frac{(x_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))-(x_{j}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))}{(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))}{\rm d}t\\

Consider the first term on the RHS of (

\displaystyle\liminf_{\iota\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\iota}\int_{\sigma-\iota}^{\sigma}\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}\sum_{j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\setminus\llbracket a,b^{\prime}\rrbracket}\frac{(x_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))-(x_{j}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))}{(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))}{\rm d}t>0.

holds for any

Thus when

\displaystyle|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|,|\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t)|\leq Cj^{2/3}.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)}{(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))}+\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}-\kappa+\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{\widetilde{y}_{j}(t)-x_{j}(t)}{(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t))(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))}.

]​], and

]​] and

\liminf_{\iota\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\iota}\int_{\sigma-\iota}^{\sigma}\sum_{i=a}^{b^{\prime}}\bigg(\widetilde{W}_{t}(\widetilde{y}_{i}(t))+\sum_{j=n+1}^{k^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{y}_{i}(t)-\widetilde{y}_{j}(t)}-W_{t}(x_{i}(t))-\sum_{j=n+1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)}\bigg){\rm d}t\\

Combining (

7.
Convergence to the Airy
β
line ensemble

The dynamical versions of the three classical ensembles as in (
1.1.1
) correspond to Dyson Brownian motion (DBM), the Laguerre process, and the Jacobi process, all of which have been intensively studied in the literature, as seen in
[
9
,
28
,
45
,
95
]
.
In this section we prove that their edge limit is ALE
β
, i.e.,
Theorem

1.16
.
In particular, for DBM our method covers more general potentials (beyond quadratic ones).
Our strategy is to prove tightness, and verify that any subsequential limit satisfies
Assumption

1.9
and
Assumption

1.10
, and then apply
Theorem

1.15
.

We now formally introduce these processes, from a random matrix theory perspective.

DBM (and with general potential).

Let
B_{t}=(B_{ij}(t))
be an
n\times n
real/complex Brownian matrix (with each entry given by Brownian motion
B(t)
for the real case; and given by
(B(t)+\hat{B}(t)\mathrm{i})/\sqrt{2}
with
B(t)
and
\hat{B}(t)
being independent Brownian motions for the complex case), and define
X_{t}=(B_{t}+B_{t}^{*})/\sqrt{2}
(where
B_{t}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
B_{t}
). Then the eigenvalues of
X_{t}
(denoted by
\{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{n}
) satisfy

{\rm d}\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)+\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}t}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)},

with
\beta=1
(real case) or
2
(complex case).
At time

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}B_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\Bigg(m+\sum^{n}_{j\neq i}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}\Bigg){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4}{\beta}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}{\rm d}t.

Recall that
\partial^{k}_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)=k!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{k+1}}

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus summing over

i=1,\dots,n
yields

(7.26)

{\rm d}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}B_{i}(t)-(m\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)+\mathcal{I}_{t}){\rm d}t+\frac{4}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}\,{\rm d}t,

where

\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}\cdot\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}.

We symmetrize
\mathcal{I}_{t}
over pairs
(i,j)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}\left(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{\big(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)\big)\big(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-2z\big)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)}\right)

\displaystyle-2z\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)^{2}}.

Thus we get

\mathcal{I}_{t}=-(n-1)\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)-\big((m^{(n)}_{t}(z))^{2}-\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\big)-2z\Big(m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}^{2}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\Big).

This can also be written as

(7.27)

-(m\,\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)+\mathcal{I}_{t})=\partial_{z}\!\Big(z\,(m^{(n)}_{t}(z))^{2}+(z-m+n)m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\Big)-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}},

where we used

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}+\frac{z}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}\right)=\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)+\frac{z}{2}\partial_{z}^{2}m^{(n)}_{t}(z).

Substituting (
7.27
) into (
7.26
) yields

(7.28)

{\rm d}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\\
+\del_{z}\left(z(m^{(n)}_{t})^{2}(z)+(z-m+n)m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\right){\rm d}t+\frac{4-2\beta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t){\rm d}t}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}.

We next do the scaling substitution. Recall the edge parameters from (
7.11
):

E=(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2},\qquad\zeta=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2/3}(mn)^{-1/3},\qquad\chi=(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{4/3}(mn)^{-1/6},

and the rescaled eigenvalues

\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t):=\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(\zeta t)-E}{\chi},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

For
w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
set

z=E+\chi w
.
After the time change
t\mapsto\zeta t
, we again denote by
\{B_{i}(t)\}_{i\leq n}
independent standard Brownian motions
(absorbing the usual
\sqrt{\zeta}
-factor into the notation). With this convention,
(
7.28
) rewrites as

(7.29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi\,{\rm d}&m^{(n)}_{\zeta t}(E+\chi w)=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t))^{1/2}\,{\rm d}B_{i}(t)}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{2}}\\
&\quad+\zeta\,\partial_{w}\Bigl((E+\chi w)\,(m^{(n)}_{\zeta t})^{2}(E+\chi w)+(\chi w+2\sqrt{n}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}))\,m^{(n)}_{\zeta t}(E+\chi w)\Bigr)\,{\rm d}t\\
&\quad+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t))\,{\rm d}t}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}.\end{split}

Let
M^{(n)}_{t}(w)
denote the martingale term in (
7.29
):

M^{(n)}_{t}(w):=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\beta}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(s))^{1/2}}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(s)-w)^{2}}\,{\rm d}B_{i}(s).

In the following we show that the margintale term and the drift term satisfy the estimates as in
Proposition

7.3
.

For any
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, the quadratic variation of the martingale
M_{t}^{(n)}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\Big\langle M^{(n)}(w),M^{(n)}(w^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\beta(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{2}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{2}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime})^{2}}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,w^{\prime}),

where the covariance error term is

(7.30)

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,w^{\prime}):=\frac{2}{\beta(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{2}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime})^{2}}.

Lemma 7.4

(Bound on the covariance error)
.

For
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
,

(7.31)

\big|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,w^{\prime})\big|\;\lesssim\;\frac{\chi}{(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}}\,\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{{\rm Im}[w]{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]^{2}}\,{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}].

In particular, since
\chi/(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}=\chi/E\asymp n^{-2/3}
under the soft-edge scaling,

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,w^{\prime})
is of order
n^{-2/3}
(up to resolvent factors).
Moreover, the same type of bound holds for
\partial_{w}\partial_{w^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,w^{\prime})
,
with extra factors of
|{\rm Im}[w]|^{-1}
and
|{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]|^{-1}
coming from differentiating the resolvents.

Proof.

We have

(7.32)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-w|^{2}}=\frac{1}{{\rm Im}[w]}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\rm Im}[\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-\overline{w}]}{(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-w)(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-\overline{w})}\\
=\frac{1}{{\rm Im}[w]}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Im}\bigg[\frac{1}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-w}\bigg]=\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]},

where the last equality is from (
7.16
).
And then

(7.33)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-w|^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{|{\rm Im}[w]|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(n)}(t)-w|^{2}}=\frac{|{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]|}{{\rm Im}[w]^{2}}.

Using
|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)|\leq|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|+|w^{\prime}|
and

|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|\geq|{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]|
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)|}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{2}\,|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{2}\,|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|}+\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{2}\,|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]|}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}+\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]^{2}}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}.

Summing over
i
and applying (
7.32
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)|}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{2}\,|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|^{2}}\lesssim\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}=\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]^{2}}\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]}.

Multiplying by the prefactor
\chi/(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}
from (
7.30
) gives (
7.31
).
Differentiating in
w,w^{\prime}
only increases the power of
|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{-1}
,
|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w^{\prime}|^{-1}
,
hence produces additional
|{\rm Im}[w]|^{-1},|{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]|^{-1}
factors in the same way.
∎

Lemma 7.5

(Bound on the drift term)
.

For any
w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, the drift term in (
7.29
)

(7.34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\zeta\,\partial_{w}\Bigl((E+\chi w)\,(m^{(n)}_{\zeta t})^{2}(E+\chi w)+(\chi w+2\sqrt{n}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}))\,m^{(n)}_{\zeta t}(E+\chi w)\Bigr)\,{\rm d}t\\
&\quad+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t))\,{\rm d}t}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}.\end{split}

equals

\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{w}\bigl(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}+{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w),

where

|{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w)|,\;|\partial_{w}{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{t}(w)|\lesssim n^{-1/3}(1+|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|)^{2}.

Proof.

We now rewrite the drift terms in (
7.29
) in terms of
[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]
.
First note the constant identities (directly from (
7.11
)):

(7.35)

\frac{\zeta E}{\chi^{2}}=\frac{1}{2},\quad\zeta\Bigl(\frac{\chi n}{E}-\frac{\chi\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)=-\frac{1}{2},\quad\frac{\zeta}{\chi}=\frac{1}{2(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2/3}(mn)^{1/6}}

We decompose the drift term in (
7.34
) as
\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}
.
Using (
7.16
), we rewrite (
7.34
) in terms of

\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}
.
We then define
\mathrm{I}
to collect the terms that are quadratic in
\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}
together with the constant terms,

\mathrm{II}
to collect the terms that are linear in
\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}
,
and
\mathrm{III}
to be the final term in (
7.34
).

Term
\mathrm{I}
.

Define

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}

\displaystyle:=\zeta\,\partial_{w}\left(\frac{E+\chi w}{\chi^{2}}\,(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})^{2}+\Bigl(\frac{\chi\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)^{2}\frac{w}{\chi}-\frac{\chi\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\,w\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta\,\partial_{w}\left(\frac{E+\chi w}{\chi^{2}}\,(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})^{2}\right)+\zeta\left(\frac{\chi n}{E}-\frac{\chi\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\right)

(7.36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{w}\Bigl(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}\Bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w),

where we used
E=(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}
from (
7.11
), (
7.35
), and set

(7.37)

\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w):=\partial_{w}\left(\frac{\zeta w}{\chi}\,(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})^{2}\right).

By the product rule,

(7.38)

|\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w)|\lesssim\frac{\zeta}{\chi}\left(|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|^{2}+|w(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})\partial_{w}(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})|\right).

Moreover, since

|\partial_{w}(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})|=\Bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w)^{2}}\Bigg|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}=\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]},

we obtain the convenient bound

(7.39)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w)|&\lesssim\frac{\zeta}{\chi}\left(|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|^{2}+|w(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})|\,\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]}\right)\\
&\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2/3}}\left(|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|^{2}+|w(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})|\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]}\right),\end{split}

and similarly for
|\partial_{w}\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w)|
(with additional
|{\rm Im}[w]|^{-1}
factors).

Term
\mathrm{II}
.

Define

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle:=\zeta\,\partial_{w}\left(\left(-\frac{2\sqrt{n}(E+\chi w)}{\chi(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})}+\frac{2\sqrt{n}(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})}{\chi}+w\right)(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})\right)

(7.40)

\displaystyle=\zeta\,\partial_{w}\left(\frac{\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\,w\,(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})\right)=:\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{II}}_{t}(w),

where we used
E=(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^{2}
to cancel the constant terms.
Thus

(7.41)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{II}}_{t}(w)|&\lesssim\zeta\left(|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|+|w\partial_{w}(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})|\right)\\
&\lesssim\zeta\left(|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|+|w|\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]}\right)\\
&\lesssim\frac{|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|}{n^{1/3}}+\frac{|w|{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{n^{1/3}{\rm Im}[w]}\end{split}

and again the same type of bound holds for
|\partial_{w}\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{II}}_{t}(w)|
.

Term
\mathrm{III}
.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{III}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2-\beta}{\beta E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(E+\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t))\,{\rm d}t}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}

(7.42)

\displaystyle=\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\rm d}t}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}+\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{III}}_{t}(w)\,{\rm d}t,

where

\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{III}}_{t}(w):=\frac{2-\beta}{\beta E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\chi\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}}.

Using
|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}|\leq|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|+|w|
and (
7.33
),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{III}}_{t}(w)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\chi}{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|+|w|}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{3}}\lesssim\frac{\chi}{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}+\frac{|w|}{|{\rm Im}[w]|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}-w|^{2}}\right)

(7.43)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\chi}{E}\,\frac{|w|}{{\rm Im}[w]^{2}}\,|{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]|\lesssim\frac{|{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]||w|}{n^{2/3}{\rm Im}[w]^{2}}.

Similarly,
|\partial_{w}\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{III}}_{t}(w)|
satisfies the same bound up to an extra
|{\rm Im}[w]|^{-1}
factor.

Collecting the drift errors.

Define

\mathcal{E}^{(n)}_{t}(w):=\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{I}}_{t}(w)+\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{II}}_{t}(w)+\mathcal{E}^{(n),\mathrm{III}}_{t}(w).

Then (
7.36
)–(
7.42
) give a drift decomposition of the rescaled dynamics into
the main term

\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{w}\bigl(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w)^{3}},

plus the error
\mathcal{E}^{(n)}_{t}(w)
.
Moreover, the bounds (
7.39
), (
7.41
), (
7.43
)
(and their
w
-derivatives) yield the desired control of

|\mathcal{E}^{(n)}_{t}(w)|
and
|\partial_{w}\mathcal{E}^{(n)}_{t}(w)|
in terms of

|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|
and
|{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]|
.
∎

Jacobi.

We follow the same steps as in the Laguerre case.
By Itô’s formula and (
7.9
), we have

{\rm d}\frac{1}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z}=-\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\frac{4}{\beta}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)\bigl(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}\bigr)\,{\rm d}t.

Again using (
7.9
) and
summing over
i=1,\dots,n
yields

(7.44)

\displaystyle{\rm d}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))}}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}B_{i}(t)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p-m\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}t-\mathcal{I}_{t}(z)\,{\rm d}t+\frac{4}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}\,{\rm d}t,

where

\mathcal{I}_{t}(z):=\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t))}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}\,\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}.

We can write

(7.45)

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p-m\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}=-p\,\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)+m\,m^{(n)}_{t}(z)+mz\,\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z).

We next consider
\mathcal{I}_{t}(z)
. Set
q(x):=x(1-x)
. Symmetrizing over pairs
(i,j)
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{q(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))+q(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t))}{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)}\left(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)^{2}}\right)

(7.46)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{\bigl(q(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))+q(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t))\bigr)\bigl(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-2z\bigr)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}(t)-z)^{2}},

using

\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)^{2}}=-(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-\lambda^{(n)}_{j})\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-2z}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)^{2}}.

Next use the decomposition (valid for any
\lambda
)

q(\lambda)=\lambda(1-\lambda)=z(1-z)+(1-2z)(\lambda-z)-(\lambda-z)^{2},\qquad\lambda^{(n)}_{i}+\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-2z=(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)+(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z).

Plugging these into (
7.46
) and expanding reduces
\mathcal{I}_{t}(z)
to linear
combinations of the standard symmetric sums

\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)},\quad\sum_{i<j}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)^{2}}\Big),\quad\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)}.

Using the identities

2\sum_{i<j}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)}=\bigl(m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\bigr)^{2}-\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z),

\sum_{i<j}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)^{2}}\Big)=(n-1)\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z),

\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\frac{1}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}-z)^{2}(\lambda^{(n)}_{j}-z)}=m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\,\partial_{z}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{z}^{2}m^{(n)}_{t}(z),

a short bookkeeping computation yields

(7.47)

-\mathcal{I}_{t}(z)=\partial_{z}\!\Big(z(1-z)\bigl(m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\bigr)^{2}+(-2nz+n)\,m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\Big)-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)\bigl(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)\bigr)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}.

Finally, combining (
7.44
), (
7.45
), and (
7.47
),
we get

(7.48)

{\rm d}m^{(n)}_{t}(z)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t))}{\rm d}B_{i}(t)}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{2}}\\
+\del_{z}\left(z(1-z)(m^{(n)}_{t})^{2}(z)+(mz-2nz+n-p)m^{(n)}_{t}(z)\right){\rm d}t+\frac{4-2\beta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)(1-\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)){\rm d}t}{(\lambda^{(n)}_{i}(t)-z)^{3}}.

The remaining arguments follow from computations analogous to the Laguerre case, and we omit the details.
∎

7.2.
Tightness

This subsection is for the remaining tasks (2) and (3), i.e., tightness and verifying assumptions. They can be summarized as the following statement

Proposition 7.6
.

For any sequence of integers
\to\infty
, we can take a subsequence
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
, such that as
k\to\infty
,
\{\Delta^{(n_{k})}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}\}_{w\in\mathbb{H},t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and
\{\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n_{k})}_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N},t\in\mathbb{N}}
converge jointly under the uniform in compact topology.
Moreover, the limit of

\max_{t\in[-T,T]}\lambda_{1}^{(n_{k})}(t)<X
whenever
k
is large enough.

Suppose that for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda^{(n_{k})}_{i}(t)
does not uniformly converge to
\lambda_{i}(t)
in
[-T,T]
(as
k\to\infty
).
Then there is a sequence
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}=t_{0}\in[-T,T]
, such that as
k\to\infty
,
\lambda^{(n_{k})}_{i}(t_{k})
does not converge to
\lambda_{i}(t_{0})
.

However, by the uniform (in
t
and
w
) convergence of
\Delta_{t}^{(n)}(w)+\sqrt{w}
to
Y_{t}(w)
, we have that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{t_{k}}^{(n_{k})}(w)+\sqrt{w}=Y_{t_{0}}(w)
, uniformly for
w
in any compact subset of
\mathbb{H}
.
With the upper bound of
\lambda^{(n_{k})}_{1}(t_{k})<X
, by
Lemma

2.7
and
Lemma

2.8
we have that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda^{(n_{k})}_{i}(t_{k})=\lambda_{i}(t_{0})
for each

, thereby arriving at a contradiction.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

7.12
.

Take
p
to be a large constant.
In this proof, all the constants in
\lesssim
and
\OO(\cdot)
can depend on
{\mathcal{K}}
,
T
, and
p
.

We start with the martingale terms.
From (
7.19
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle M^{(n)}(w),M^{(n)}(\overline{w})\rangle_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{4}}+|{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\cdot\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}^{3}[w]}+|{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w})|,

and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\langle M^{(n)}(w)-M^{(n)}(w^{\prime}),M^{(n)}(\overline{w})-M^{(n)}(\overline{w^{\prime}})\rangle_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|w-w^{\prime}|^{2}|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w+\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|^{2}}{|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w|^{4}|\widetilde{\lambda}^{(n)}_{i}(t)-w^{\prime}|^{4}}+|{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w})-2{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w^{\prime}})+{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime},\overline{w^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta}|w-w^{\prime}|^{2}\left(\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}^{3}[w]{\rm Im}^{2}[w^{\prime}]}+\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime})+\sqrt{w}^{\prime}]}{{\rm Im}^{2}[w]{\rm Im}^{3}[w^{\prime}]}\right)

\displaystyle+|{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w})-2{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w,\overline{w^{\prime}})+{\hat{{\mathcal{E}}}}^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime},\overline{w^{\prime}})|,

for
w\neq w^{\prime}
.
Using (
7.20
) to bound the error terms in the Laguerre and Jacobi cases, we have that the above two are
\OO(1+|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|)
and
\OO(|w-w^{\prime}|^{2}(1+|\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}|))
, respectively.
Notice that
\overline{M_{t}^{(n)}(w)}=M_{t}^{(n)}(\overline{w})
. Then thanks to Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, for any
w,w^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, and
-T\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq T
,

(7.62)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|M^{(n)}_{t^{\prime}}(w)-M^{(n)}_{t}(w)\right|^{p}\right]\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}{\rm d}\langle M^{(n)}(w),M^{(n)}(\overline{w})\rangle_{s}\right|^{p/2}\right]\lesssim|t^{\prime}-t|^{p/2},

and

(7.63)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|(M^{(n)}_{t}(w)-M^{(n)}_{-T}(w))-(M^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime})-M^{(n)}_{-T}(w^{\prime}))\right|^{p}\right]\\
\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{-T}^{t}{\rm d}\langle M^{(n)}(w)-M^{(n)}(w^{\prime}),M^{(n)}(\overline{w})-M^{(n)}(\overline{w^{\prime}})\rangle_{s}\right|^{p/2}\right]\lesssim|w-w^{\prime}|^{p},

where in the last inequalities we used the above bounds and (
7.53
).

The tightness of
\{M^{(n)}_{t}(w)-M^{(n)}_{-T}(w)\}_{t\in[-T,T],w\in{\mathcal{K}}}
then follows from these estimates with
p>4
(see e.g.,
[
120
, Lemma 5.9]
, which can be proved by bounding the modulus of continuity via a union bound across scale, and using e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 7.3]
).

We now turn to
\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)
.
From the one time tightness in
Proposition

7.7
, it suffices to derive the tightness of
\{\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)-\Delta^{(n)}_{-T}(w)\}_{t\in[-T,T],w\in{\mathcal{K}}}
.
From (
7.18
), and using
Lemma

2.2
, for any
w\in{\mathcal{K}}
and
-T\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq T
we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{(n)}_{t^{\prime}}(w)-\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)=M^{(n)}_{t^{\prime}}(w)-M^{(n)}_{t}(w)

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\OO\left(1+|{\mathcal{E}}^{(n)}_{s}(w)|+\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]^{2}}+\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}(w)+\sqrt{w}]|\Delta_{t}^{(n)}(w)+\sqrt{w}|}{{\rm Im}[w]}\right){\rm d}s.

Then by (
7.21
), (
7.53
), and (
7.62
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta^{(n)}_{t^{\prime}}(w)-\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)\right|^{p}\right]\lesssim|t^{\prime}-t|^{p/2}.

Besides, for any
w,w^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, for each
r=0,1,2
we have

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}|\partial_{w}^{r}(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w})-\partial_{w}^{r}(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime})+\sqrt{w^{\prime}})|\leq(r+1)!|w-w^{\prime}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|\widetilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(n)}-w|^{r}+|\widetilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(n)}-w^{\prime}|^{r}}{|\widetilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(n)}-w|^{r+1}|\widetilde{\lambda}_{t}^{(n)}-w^{\prime}|^{r+1}}

\displaystyle\leq(r+1)!|w-w^{\prime}|\left(\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)+\sqrt{w}]}{{\rm Im}[w]^{r+1}}+\frac{{\rm Im}[\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime})+\sqrt{w^{\prime}}]}{{\rm Im}[w^{\prime}]^{r+1}}\right).

Then from (
7.18
), using this and (
7.21
), (
7.53
), (
7.63
), we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)-\Delta^{(n)}_{-T}(w))-(\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w^{\prime})-\Delta^{(n)}_{-T}(w^{\prime}))\right|^{p}\right]\lesssim|w-w^{\prime}|^{p}.

Thereby the tightness of
\{\Delta^{(n)}_{t}(w)-\Delta^{(n)}_{-T}(w)\}_{t\in[-T,T],w\in{\mathcal{K}}}
follows, from these moments bounds with
p>4
.
∎

Appendix A
Particle location and Stieltjes transform

In this appendix we give some results on particle-generated Nevanlinna functions, which are used frequently in the main text.

A.1.
Airy function properties

We first prove the Nevanlinna representation of
-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(w)}{\Ai(w)}
.

Proof of (
1.10
).

The Weierstrass factorization of
\Ai
is given by

\Ai(w)=\Ai(0)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}w\right)\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{w}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{w}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right).

See e.g.,
[
89
, eq.(2.8)]
. Then (modulos
2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
) we have

\log(\Ai(w))=\log(\Ai(0))+\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(0)}{\Ai(0)}w+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-\frac{w}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}}\right)+\frac{w}{{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}},

where the convergence of the summation in
i
follows from (
1.9
), and the fact that
|\log(1-z)+z|\lesssim|z|^{-2}
for
|z|<\frac{1}{2}
. Then by taking the derivative in
w
we get (
1.10
).
∎

We then establish the square root growth of
-\frac{\Ai^{\prime}(w)}{\Ai(w)}
, as
w\to\infty
.

Proof of (
1.11
).

The Airy function has the following asymptotic formula.
For
|\arg(w)|<\pi
,

\Ai(w)\sim\frac{\exp(-\zeta)}{w^{1/4}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+5/6)\Gamma(n+1/6)}{4\pi^{3/2}n!(-2\zeta)^{n}},

\Ai^{\prime}(w)\sim-w^{1/4}\exp(-\zeta)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1+6n}{1-6n}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(n+5/6)\Gamma(n+1/6)}{4\pi^{3/2}n!(-2\zeta)^{n}},

where
\zeta=\frac{2}{3}w^{3/2}
.
In particular, there is

\left|w^{1/4}\Ai(w)-\frac{\exp(-2w^{3/2}/3)}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left(1-\frac{5}{48w^{3/2}}\right)\right|\lesssim(\pi-|\arg(w)|)^{-7/6}\frac{|\exp(-2w^{3/2}/3)|}{|w^{3}|},

\left|w^{-1/4}\Ai^{\prime}(w)+\frac{\exp(-2w^{3/2}/3)}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left(1+\frac{7}{48w^{3/2}}\right)\right|\lesssim(\pi-|\arg(w)|)^{-1}\frac{|\exp(-2w^{3/2}/3)|}{|w^{3}|},

for any
w\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0}
.
See e.g.,
[
48
, Section 9.7(iv)]
and
[
103
, Appendix B]
.
By taking the ratio of the above two estimates, we get (
1.11
), for any
w\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|w|
large enough and
|\arg(w)|<\pi-|w|^{-9/7}
.
∎

The rest of this appendix connects the Airy-like property (from
Definition

1.7
) and the locations of the poles, for particle-generated Nevanlinna functions.
We note that most of the arguments to be presented are classical in random matrix theory.

Our starting point is the following relation between any Nevanlinna function and its corresponding measure. It can be viewed as a version of the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula (see e.g.,
[
56
, Section 11.2]
).

A.2.
Helffer-Sjöstrand formula

Take any compactly supported smooth test functions
f
and
\chi
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, such that
\chi=1
in a neighborhood of
0
.
Define
\tilde{f}(x+\mathrm{i}y):=(f(x)+\mathrm{i}yf^{\prime}(x))\chi(y)
.
Then for any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have (for
\del_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\del_{x}+\mathrm{i}\del_{y})
)

f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\frac{\del_{\bar{z}}\tilde{f}(x+\mathrm{i}y)}{\lambda-x-\mathrm{i}y}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

which further leads to the following statement.

Lemma A.1
.

Take any Nevanlinna function
Y:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\cup{\mathbb{R}}
, with Nevanlinna representation

Y(w)=b+cw+\int\left(\frac{1}{\lambda-w}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right){\rm d}\mu(\lambda),

for some
b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
c\geq 0
, and
\mu
being a Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then we have

\int f(\lambda){\rm d}\mu(\lambda)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{H}}{\rm Re}\left[\del_{\bar{z}}\tilde{f}(x+\mathrm{i}y)Y(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right]{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

These identities are classical and follow directly from standard complex analysis arguments; see e.g.,
[
56
, Section 11.2]
. We omit the proofs here.

A.3.

With (
