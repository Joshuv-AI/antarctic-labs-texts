Predicting protein–protein interaction sites based on dynamic perception mechanism within a hierarchical E(n)-equivariant graph
Authors: Xue Li, Suheng Qiao, Ziqi Li, Shihua Zhou, Jianmin Wang, Bin Wang, Tao Song, Ben Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of protein–protein interaction sites is crucial to understanding biological processes, elucidating disease mechanisms, and accelerating drug discovery. Although graph neural network methods have shown potential in this field, but existing methods are limited by the static integrate multi-group features and insufficient perception of hierarchical 3D spatial geometric information, leading to insufficient predictive ability of orphan sites. To address these issues, this paper proposes a Dperception mechanism within a Hierarchical E(n)-equivariant Graph architecture (DHEG). DHEG introduces a dynamic feature importance perception mechanism that adaptively perceives the contextual inter-dependencies of features and assigns weights to feature groups based on their relevance to the interaction relationship. And a hierarchical gated architecture based on E(n)-equivariant graph neural networks that effectively captures protein 3D spatial structures while mitigating over-smoothing problems. The results show that DHEG achieves improvements in 11 of 13 key metrics, with an enhancement 8% in Matthews correlation coefficient, indicating that DHEG not only predicts more interaction sites but also does so with greater reliability. Furthermore, case studies and visualization analyzes show that DHEG aligns better with the biological mechanism and has excellent predictive capabilities for both orphan sites and continuous regions, demonstrating interpretability, and application potential.Keywords: E(n)-equivariant GNN, protein–protein interaction, protein 3D spatial structures

Introduction

Proteins are executors of life activities [1, 2], and their interactions sustain the normal functioning of life. Identifying protein–protein interaction sites (PPIS) [3–5] is of great importance for studying life mechanisms and disease pathogenesis [6–8]. Traditional experimental biomedical measurements are reliable, but costly, time-consuming, and impractical for large-scale applications [9, 10]. With the accumulation of high-throughput data, the development of data-driven computational methods to constructing predictive models of PPIS can serve as a prescreening tool for wet-lab experiments [11–14], thereby accelerating the exploration of biological mechanisms and disease processes [15–18].

Building upon computational methods, PPIS prediction models initially relied on protein sequences and started with machine learning algorithms such as support vector machine (SVM) and Random Forest [19, 20]. For instance, Wang et al. [21] combined five evolutionarily conserved features of amino acids to represent residues and built an SVM-based predictor to infer interface residues. With increased computational power, deep learning has become the foundational architecture for advanced computational frameworks, enabling a wide range of applications from bioinformatics to drug discovery [22, 23]. Subsequently, deep learning approaches such as convolutional neural networks [24] (CNNs) were subsequently adopted to improve prediction accuracy [25, 26]. A typical example is the work of Zeng et al. [27], who concatenated the position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM), secondary structure, and one-hot encoded amino acid representations, and used CNNs to extract features for interaction site prediction.

As the availability of protein structural data grew, graph neural networks (GNNs) [28–31] demonstrated strong potential in naturally representing protein structures and showed significant effectiveness in PPIS prediction. For example, GraphPPIS [32] encoded amino acids by concatenating multiple features and employed a deep graph convolutional network (GCN) framework for site prediction. Similarly, RGN [33] used concatenated multi-feature vectors and incorporated both GCN and graph attention network (GAT) for PPIS prediction. AGAT-PPIS [34] further extended this idea by combining the concatenated residue feature vectors with an enhanced graph attention network. MVSO-PPIS [35] incorporates a subgraph convolution module into the augmented graph attention network to extract information from the concatenated feature vectors and predict the sites. In another effort, RGCNPPIS [36] applied GCN and GraphSAGE [37] on concatenated feature vectors to extract both local and global features for interaction site prediction.

The advent of more accurate protein structural data further catalyzed methodological innovations [38], particularly with Equivariant Graph Neural Networks (EGNNs) emerging as a powerful framework. These models incorporate coordinate information into GNNs, enabling more comprehensive learning of spatial protein features. Methods such as SEKD-PPIS [2], MEG-PPIS [9], and GTE-PPIS [6] representative of this trend integrated concatenated feature vectors with EGNN, capturing residue characteristics via multi-scale graph information and graph transformers, while using residual networks to alleviate over-smoothing issues. However, a common limitation across these approaches is their reliance on static feature concatenation, which struggles to adaptively capture the contextual importance of different features or model hierarchical 3D geometric structures, thereby missing capture the multi-scale nature of protein and hindering the learning of meaningful geometric patterns.

Therefore, to address these limitations, this paper proposes a novel framework named DHEG (Dynamic Hierarchical E(n)-equivariant graph neural network). DHEG introduces a dynamic feature perception mechanism that adaptively weights multiple protein features, based on their contextual relevance, overcoming the rigidity of static feature integration. Furthermore, leveraging the inherent geometric symmetry of 3D protein structures, DHEG incorporates an EGNN to construct a hierarchical gated architecture. Unlike traditional GNN that are sensitive to rigid transformations, the EGNN ensures that predictions remain invariant to the rotation and translation of protein structures in 3D space, which is fundamental for accurate biomolecular analysis. By integrating this equivariant framework into a hierarchical gated architecture, DHEG effectively captures complex geometric constraints and multi-scale spatial features through graph attention pooling. Crucially, this architecture employs an adaptive gating mechanism for cross-level information fusion, which not only facilitates the integration of hierarchical representations from graphs to subgraphs but also effectively mitigates the over-smoothing and information loss typical of deep networks. Comparative experiments demonstrate that DHEG significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, and its predicted interaction sites show strong agreement with experimentally determined interfaces.

Materials and methods

Datasets

To illustrate DHEG perception ability of dynamic protein sites information, the evaluation was conducted using the same datasets as those in previous works [6, 9, 32, 34] for fair performance comparison. Specifically, the training dataset is Train_334, and the test datasets include Test_60, Test_315-28, BTest_31-6, and UBTest_31-6. Among these, Train_334 and Test_60 were constructed by removing redundant and highly similar sequences from multiple overlapping datasets. Test_315-28 is a distinct dataset, curated specifically to evaluate model generalization. To ensure its independence from the training data, all protein chains sharing >25% sequence identity over 30% overlap (or with a BLAST E-value < 1e-6) with any protein in the training source datasets were explicitly removed during its construction. BTest_31-6 is a subset derived from Test_60, consisting entirely of monomeric bound proteins with complete structural information, while UBTest_31-6 contains the corresponding unbound structures of those proteins. These two datasets are used to assess the model’s robustness to conformational changes between bound and unbound states. The architecture of DHEG

The overall architecture of DHEG is illustrated in Fig. 2. It consists of three main components: a dynamic embedding module, a hierarchical equivariant graph neural network module, and an output prediction module. And this work characterizes proteins as undirected graphs , where nodes represent amino acids, and the edges represent connections between them. In alignment with established research, edges are established based on the Euclidean distance between residue C atoms, and a cutoff distance of 14 Å was applied as the threshold for edge formation. An edge exists if the distance between two residues is within this threshold; otherwise, no edge is created. The dynamic embedding module employs an adaptive weighting strategy to integrate multiple features derived from protein sequences and structures, effectively capturing contextually relevant representations. The hierarchical E(n)-equivariant graph neural network (EGNN) module utilizes graph attention pooling to extract and aggregate spatial structural information at different scales, forming a graph-to-subgraph representation learning framework. The gating mechanism is further incorporated to facilitate cross-layer information fusion, thereby reducing information loss and alleviating the over-smoothing problem. Finally, a multilayer perceptron is applied to predict whether each residue constitutes an interaction site. Module details are as follows.

Dynamic embedding module

The embedding of protein sequences integrates multiple features [9, 34] in DHEG, including 20D PSSM, 20D hidden Markov model matrix (HMM), 14D definition of secondary structure of proteins (DSSP), 7D physicochemical atomic features (AF), and 2D spatial geometric position feature (PF) representing absolute and relative positions. For a sequence of length , this ultimately forms a feature vector of size 63. These features are derived from both sequential and structural information of the protein, and their importance varies dynamically depending on the downstream task context.

Protein embeddings are crucial for PPIS prediction. The features selected in our approach systematically characterize protein properties from 2D: sequence and structure. At the sequence level, PSSM and HMM encode amino acid distribution probabilities and conservation patterns, respectively, providing evolutionary information and functional site characteristics. At the structural level, DSSP captures secondary structure and solvent accessibility, AF incorporates seven physicochemical attributes including atomic mass, van der Waals radius, crystallographic B-factor, partial electronic charge, side-chain identification, ring membership status, and hydrogen bond connectivity, while PF encodes both absolute and relative spatial positions of residues. Together, these features effectively capture the spatial conformation and physicochemical complementarity underlying protein interactions. The integration of these features comprehensively covers evolutionary constraints, structural configurations, and physicochemical properties, thereby constructing an information-rich and highly discriminative embedding representation for PPI identification.

But static concatenation methods fail to capture the inherent heterogeneity among these features and may introduce noise. Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of DHEG, this paper adopts the same evaluation metrics used in related works to quantitatively assess the advantages [9, 35]. A comprehensive comparison is conducted based on ACC (Accuracy), Precision, Recall, F1, MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient), AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), and AUPRC (area under the precision-recall curve). Furthermore, the -value statistical method was also considered. The -value [40] is a widely used method in statistical analysis, reflecting the likelihood of a specific event occurring. Based on the significance test, a -value in the range of .01.05 indicates a statistically significant difference, while P<.01 indicates a highly statistically significant difference. Additionally, the stability of model performance is reported using 95% confidence intervals (CI) derived from multiple independent runs. In addition, regarding the binary classification threshold, instead of using a static value of 0.5, we followed the protocol established in prior research (e.g. AGAT-PPIS). For each protein, the optimal threshold is determined by iterating through the predicted probability range [0, 1] to identify the value that maximizes the F1-score. This approach ensures that the performance reflects the model’s true discriminative capability across different protein.

Although the DE module assigns high importance weights to DSSP features, the model exhibits significant robustness even after the explicit removal of DSSP (w/ -DSSP). Since DSSP features are geometric derivatives of 3D coordinates, and the HEGNN module in the DHEG framework possesses powerful spatial geometry capture capabilities, the model is able to implicitly extract most of the structural information from the raw coordinates via HEGNN when explicit DSSP features are missing. Protein language model embeddings analysis

To further investigate the generalization ability of DHEG and address the potential overfitting concerns, we integrated two protein language models (PLMs), ESMC [42], and ESM2 [43], for comparative analysis (Table 9). The embeddings of the DNN-ESM2 and DNN-ESMC are PLMs with DNN, isolating the contribution of the sequence-level language model within the hierarchical equivariant structure framework. DHEG−ESM2 and DHEG−ESMC denote models where the original DHEG embeddings are entirely replaced with ESM2 and ESMC, respectively. DHEGESM2 and DHEGESMC represent models that combined the original DHEG embeddings by ESM2 and ESMC. The results demonstrate that standalone PLMs features, even when processed through a DNN, yield poor results (e.g. MCC of 0.086 for DNN−ESMC), indicating that PLMs representations alone are insufficient to resolve the complex spatial constraints of interaction interfaces. While the performance significantly improves when PLMs features are integrated into DHEG’s HEGNN layers, the original DHEG configuration still achieves the highest discriminative power, with an MCC of 0.580 on Test_60. This confirms that DHEG can capture essential geometric signatures that are highly generalizable across diverse protein complexes in generic PLMs-based architectures.

Model analysis and visualization of DHEG

Model structure analysis

To validate the architectural design of the proposed framework, the ablation study on the DHEG is conducted (Table 10). The model achieves superior results, significantly outperforming its ablated variants. Removing the hierarchical subgraph processing (w/o Subgraph) led to a substantial drop in F1 and MCC, underscoring its necessity for capturing multi-scale structural patterns essential for identifying PPIS. Similarly, eliminating the gated fusion mechanism (w/o Gate fusion) resulted in notably worse Precision and F1, demonstrating its key role in adaptively integrating information across different scales. Also, ablating the dynamic embedding module (w/o DE) also caused a clear performance degradation, particularly in MCC and AUPRC. This confirms that the dynamic embedding module is not merely an auxiliary step but a core component that provides a more discriminative input representation for the subsequent geometric learning stages. These results highlight that the synergy between hierarchical feature extraction and learned fusion gates is indispensable for achieving SOTA performance in this complex task.

Effectiveness of loss function configuration

To evaluate whether specialized cost-sensitive learning could further mitigate the inherent class imbalance, we compared the default Cross-Entropy (CE) loss with Focal Loss on the Test_60 dataset. Focal Loss is designed to focus training on hard negative samples by adding a modulating factor to the CE loss.

As shown in Table 12, DHEG trained with CE loss achieves significantly higher performance in terms of MCC and AUPRC compared with Focal Loss. We hypothesize that while Focal Loss emphasizes hard samples, the hierarchical gated architecture in DHEG, integrated with the dynamic perception mechanism, inherently achieves a similar objective. By adaptively re-weighting feature importance and getting important information across different scales, these modules allow the model to focus on discriminative interface-relevant signals. Consequently, the CE loss is sufficient to achieve SOTA results without the added complexity of hyperparameter tuning associated with Focal Loss.

Analysis of over-smoothing and gating mechanism

To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed architecture in mitigating over-smoothing [46], we performed a layer-wise comparative analysis of node representations (Fig. 11), including Mean Similarity, MAD [47], and Dirichlet Energy [48]. The results reveal that in the absence of gating, node features exhibit a steady trend toward homogenization, with Mean Similarity escalating to 0.9. Conversely, DHEG utilizes Intra-layer Gated Fusion to maintain a stable similarity of 0.6, effectively preserving residue-level distinctness. Furthermore, the steady and controllable increase in MAD and Dirichlet Energy within DHEG confirms that our framework actively counters feature collapse, while the hierarchical transition at Layer 6 ensures multi-scale information vitality. These quantitative findings demonstrate that our gating and hierarchical designs are not only principled but essential for maintaining discriminative power in deep structures.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed DHEG, a dynamic hierarchical E(n)-equivariant graph neural network, for accurate prediction of PPIS. By integrating a dynamic feature perception mechanism with a hierarchical equivariant architecture, DHEG effectively captures multi-scale spatial geometric patterns while adaptively emphasizing the most informative feature groups. Extensive experiments across multiple benchmark datasets demonstrated that DHEG is overall superior to the existing SOTA methods in terms of accuracy, robustness, and interpretability. The ablation experiments of the dynamic embedding strategy and the model demonstrated the necessity of each module. Crucially, the choice of E(n)-equivariance in our architecture specifically ensures coordinate-free learning, allowing the model to recognize interaction sites regardless of the protein’s rotation or translation in 3D space. This provides a more biologically intuitive representation of protein hierarchical organization compared to non-graph methods and other models. While DHEG currently utilizes a hierarchical gated architecture, recent advancements in geometric deep learning, such as HybridGeo [49], ProstaNet [50], offer promising avenues for future enhancement to further refine structural encoding. Extensive experiments and ablation studies, including the removal of the HEGNN module and subgraph components, confirmed the necessity of each design choice in maintaining discriminative power. Case studies further highlighted DHEG’s potential to assist in biological discovery and drug development by aligning predictions with experimentally validated binding sites.

Despite these advantages, DHEG also has limitations. Although the model achieves competitive results across multiple metrics on several datasets, the stability of its performance across different datasets requires further improvement, particularly when faced with varying data distributions. In addition, while we attempted to incorporate pretrained semantic representations from protein language models such as ESM2 and ESMC, the ESM-series models did not demonstrate the expected advantages in our task. Nevertheless, recognizing the vast potential of protein modeling, we acknowledge that other promising architectures, such as those explored in subsequent works [51, 52], warrant further investigation. This suggests that effectively integrating geometric graph representations with semantic sequence information remains an open research question. And, our current evaluation is based on established benchmarks, validating DHEG on truly independent and prospective data, such as recently solved protein structures, as well as on more challenging biological scenarios including transient interactions and complexes involving intrinsically disordered regions, will be crucial to fully assess its generalization and practical utility [53]. Furthermore, as a graph-based framework, it requires considerable memory consumption, especially when processing large protein complexes. This may restrict its scalability to extremely large systems or real-time applications. Future work will focus on developing more memory-efficient graph operators and exploring model compression strategies [16, 30, 31, 54, 55] to further enhance applicability to large-scale biological data [40, 56].
