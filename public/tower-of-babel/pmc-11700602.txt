Introduction

Since the rise of ChatGPT in late 2022, reactions to artificial intellegence (AI) within animal science, particularly in academia, have varied widely. In this article, we draw on our experiences as a professor and a first-year graduate student in animal science, along with existing evidence, to explore the challenges and opportunities AI presents. Offering a framework for ethically integrating these tools into animal science education, particularly through the lens of Bloom’s Taxonomy, which provides a standard for assessing learning across different cognitive levels.

The introduction of AI in academia has raised widespread concern. There’s a legitimate fear that over-reliance on AI could stifle creativity and hinder genuine learning. However, the inevitability of AI in future careers cannot be ignored. From a student’s perspective, Ally initially hesitated to embrace AI due to concerns about academic integrity, yet she recognized the necessity of mastering these tools to remain competitive with her peers. A review by Kelly et al. (2023) supports this viewpoint, noting that perceived usefulness, performance expectancy, trust, and societal influences are significant predictors of AI usage. Additionally, a study by Tran et al. (2021) revealed that Gen Z is more confident in their AI abilities compared to millennials and Gen X, leading to greater acceptance among younger demographics. This generational divide underscores a potential disconnect between professors and students regarding AI use in academia, which could lead to mistrust.

While many faculty colleagues were initially skeptical, Dr. Hansen approached AI with optimism, influenced by conversations with fellow fiction writers enthusiastic about its potential. She was eager to explore how AI could streamline administrative tasks and free up time for more engaging work. Ethan Mollick, a prominent advocate for AI, suggests that it takes approximately ten hours of using tools like ChatGPT to achieve a true level of comfort (Mollick, 2023). If faculty do not make this initial time commitment, they will miss out on AI’s benefits, leading to a growing divide between adopters and skeptics. This resistance could limit innovation and adaptation in an increasingly digital academic environment.

AI in Animal Science Education

To effectively implement AI in education, as suggested by Houman Harouni from the Harvard Graduate School of Education, educators must stop pretending AI does not exist and instead use it alongside their students (Ross, 2023). This approach helps students see AI as a tool, not a shortcut to bypass critical thinking. For faculty, modeling appropriate AI use is essential. Dr. Hansen, for instance, has been transparent about her AI use with graduate students, which has destigmatized the topic and encouraged them to share their experiences.

AI can be a powerful tool when used thoughtfully in animal science education and can be utilized at various levels of Bloom’s Taxonomy (Figure 1). For example, Ally has used AI tools to create flashcards and practice quizzes from her notes. In tutoring settings, AI is particularly adept at generating analogies to explain complex biological processes, such as the metabolic interplay between glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and electron transport. With the right prompts, AI can challenge students to rewrite concepts in their own words, demonstrating a deeper level of understanding.

AI, however, is not limited to lower levels of learning, it can also be used to achieve higher levels of Bloom’s Taxonomy, from application to creation. For instance, in Dr. Hansen’s graduate-level vitamins and minerals course, students were tasked with writing a short grant proposal on mineral metabolism in their chosen species. Although many students had little experience in grant writing, AI guided them through the process, offering brainstorming assistance, structural guidance, and critical feedback. Students were required to submit their ChatGPT conversation links along with their proposals, with a clear expectation that the ideas and writing were their own. This approach introduced students to grant writing while focusing on the mineral topic, using AI as a supportive tool and not a way to get out of thinking for themselves.

Technology, like AI, challenges educators to create assignments that cannot be completed with a simple input into ChatGPT but instead require students to digest, interpret, and apply what they have learned to real-world scenarios, thus reaching higher levels of cognitive understanding.

Ethical Considerations and Conclusion

As we conclude this article, it is important to emphasize that AI is not infallible. There is a propensity for AI tools to generate incorrect information, known as hallucinations, which requires the user to have a strong grasp of their concepts to avoid being misled. It is crucial to “don’t trust, and definitely verify” any AI-generated information to ensure quality is not compromised. Additionally, outlining what is ethically appropriate when using AI is paramount if it is to be integrated into the classroom. Ethics and morals may vary, but by setting clear boundaries on what constitutes acceptable AI use, students can begin to develop their own ethical framework. With open dialogue and clear guidelines, our community can thoughtfully explore AI applications while preparing students to use these tools ethically in the future. AI is here to stay, and it would be a disservice to our students’ education if we did not learn how to implement it.
