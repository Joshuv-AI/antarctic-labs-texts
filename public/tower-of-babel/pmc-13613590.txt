Development and internal validation of prediction model for 90-day mortality in older patients with hip fracture using admission blood parameters
Authors: Bo Gao, Qing Zhou, Xi Chen, Zhicong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate an early prediction model for short-term mortality risk in older patients with hip fracture using admission laboratory parameters.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, data from 1881 older patients with hip fracture (2013.01‑2023.12) were analyzed. All‑cause mortality within 90 days of admission was the primary outcome. From 29 candidate predictors, feature selection was performed on a training set (70%) using stepwise logistic regression, the Boruta algorithm, and LASSO regression. A consensus predictor set was determined by majority voting and used to construct a multivariable logistic regression model, presented as a nomogram. The model was evaluated on an independent testing set (30%) for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility.ResultsThe 90‑day mortality rate was 11.5% (217/1881). Ten predictors were selected: age, Charlson Comorbidity Index, lymphocyte, albumin, adenosine deaminase, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, prothrombin time, white blood cell, and β2-microglobulin. The nomogram showed good discrimination, with AUCs of 0.812 (95% CI: 0.779‑0.846) in the training set and 0.826 (0.775‑0.878) in the testing set. Calibration was satisfactory (Hosmer‑Lemeshow P = 0.191 and 0.778, respectively). Decision curve analysis demonstrated a higher net clinical benefit than treat‑all or treat‑none strategies across a wide threshold range.ConclusionWe developed and validated an early prediction model for 90-day mortality following hip fracture using admission blood parameters. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability and calibration, supporting its potential clinical utility in early risk stratification. Although it may aid in guiding individualized patient management, it should be used to complement, not replace, clinical judgment. Further external validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08078-2.

Introduction

With the aging of the global population, hip fracture has emerged as a major public health concern worldwide [1–3]. Older adults with hip fracture are at substantial risk of short-term mortality, with reported rates ranging from 1.4% to 5.5% for in‑hospital mortality, 6.5% to 9.6% for 30‑day mortality, and 7.3% to 15.6% for 90‑day mortality [4–6]. Early comprehensive geriatric assessment following hip fracture is associated with improved clinical and patient-reported outcomes, including lower complication rates, reduced hospital length of stay, shorter preoperative waiting time, and decreased mortality [7–9]. Therefore, timely and accurate stratification of mortality risk is essential to guide individualized management and clinical decision-making for patients with hip fractures [5, 7].

Currently, clinical prediction models for mortality risk in hip fracture patients are predominantly based on clinical indicators, with limited integration of laboratory parameters that can objectively reflect physiological status. Previous studies have demonstrated significant associations between various blood parameters and mortality risk in hip fracture patients, such as hemoglobin, albumin, and creatinine [10, 11]. In our prior work, we also identified up to 29 blood parameters that were significantly associated with 90-day mortality [12]. In recent years, a growing number of studies have incorporated laboratory parameters with clinical indicators to develop mortality risk prediction models for older patients with hip fracture [13–17]. Most of these models are designed to assess long-term mortality risk, such as 1-year mortality [13–15], whereas investigations focusing on short-term mortality risk remain limited [16, 17]. Moreover, the majority of these studies have been restricted to surgical patients, which may reduce the generalizability of the models to the broader hip fracture population. Real-world studies that include both surgical and non-surgical patients are acknowledged to more accurately represent the spectrum of clinical practice [18]. Therefore, this study aimed to develop an early prediction model for short-term mortality risk in older patients with hip fracture based on admission blood parameters​ in a real‑world cohort, with the goal of supporting clinical decision‑making.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of data extracted from our institutional hip fracture database, which has been employed in previous prognostic research [12, 19–23]. As previously described [12], 2713 patients with femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures were initially identified in the database between January 2013 and December 2023. Patients were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) age < 60 years (n = 385); (2) delayed presentation (> 3 weeks after injury), pathological fracture, or periprosthetic fracture (n = 148); (3) high-energy trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents or falls from greater than standing height (n = 99); (4) insufficient follow-up data (n = 156) [20]. In this study, patients with follow-up duration less than 90 days (n = 36) or missing hematological data (n = 8) were subsequently excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. 2022–04–040-K01), and all participants provided written informed consent for the use of their clinical data for research purposes.

Data collection

This study aimed to develop a prediction model for short-term mortality using admission-based variables available before any definitive treatment. To enable pre-treatment risk stratification, we deliberately excluded variables related to subsequent clinical management (e.g. surgical versus non-surgical treatment, time to surgery, operative approach, and perioperative complications) that would not be available at the point of hospital admission. Candidate predictors were selected based on preliminary findings [12]. Study outcome

The primary outcome was 90‑day all‑cause mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring within 90 days of hospital admission [12, 23]. Survival status was determined from hospital records, telephone follow‑up, and post‑discharge medical documentation [22]. Patients whose survival status could not be confirmed through all three verification methods were considered lost to follow‑up. These patients were excluded from the analysis cohort prior to outcome ascertainment, resulting in a loss to follow-up rate of 7.50% within the original database [12].

Data preprocessing

The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%) using stratified sampling implemented in the “caret” package prior to any preprocessing or modeling procedures. The proportion of missing data for each variable prior to imputation is reported in Table 1. To prevent data leakage, missing values were imputed using random forest-based multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), with the imputation model fitted exclusively to the training set​ and applied without refitting to the test set​ [24]. All candidate predictors and the outcome​ were incorporated to preserve multivariate relationships, with the number of imputations set at m = 5. Continuous variables were standardized using Z-scores prior to model fitting and back-transformed​ to their original scales for interpretation, ensuring that regression coefficients and the nomogram reflect the effect of a one-unit change in native clinical units. Consistent with previous reports [25, 26], preliminary analyses indicated that applying the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) induced overfitting (Supplementary Fig. 1). Sample size estimation

Following the approach described by Riley et al. [28], the required sample size for model development was calculated using the “pmsampsize” package. Parameters were informed by preliminary data: an anticipated area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82, a 90-day event rate of 11.5%, and an expected 10 predictors in the final model [12]. The calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 581 participants with 67 events, which was comfortably exceeded by the training set (n = 1317, events = 152), corresponding to approximately 15 events per predictor. However, conventional sample size calculations assume that predictor selection is independent of outcome data-an assumption partially relaxed by screening 29 candidate variables and retaining 10 rather than 8 predictors based on observed performance. This introduces model selection uncertainty not fully captured by the calculation. We therefore interpret the sample size as adequate but not definitive, with bootstrap validation and a held-out test set mitigating, though not eliminating, potential overoptimism.

Feature selection

Feature selection followed a prespecified two-step protocol. First, three complementary methods (stepwise logistic regression, the Boruta algorithm, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO] regression) were applied to the training set to identify candidate predictors, as previously described [12]. Second, a majority‑voting strategy retained variables selected by at least two algorithms, reducing algorithm-specific bias [29]. The final model included 10 predictors, chosen by balancing parsimony against incremental gains in discrimination (assessed by AUC comparison).

Model development

A multivariable logistic regression model was developed to predict 90‑day mortality risk using the finally selected predictors. A nomogram was constructed with the “rms” package to visualize the prediction model for clinical use.

Model evaluation

Discrimination was assessed using the AUC and confusion matrix metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value [PPV], and negative predictive value [NPV]). Internal validation via 2000 bootstrap resamples provided optimism-corrected performance estimates. Calibration was comprehensively evaluated with calibration plot, calibration slope and intercept, and Brier score. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was reported but interpreted conservatively given its susceptibility to sample size. Clinical utility was quantified using decision curve analysis (DCA) across a clinically relevant threshold range.

This study adheres to the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guideline for the reporting of multivariable prediction models. A completed TRIPOD checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1881 older adults with hip fracture were included in this study. Of these, 35.5% were male, the median age was 80.0 years, and 217 (11.5%) died within 90 days. The patients were randomly partitioned into the training set (n = 1317) and testing set (n = 564). Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two sets, and no statistically significant differences were observed (Table 1; all P > 0.05).

Feature selection

Nine predictors were selected by stepwise logistic regression based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC): age, CCI, white blood cell, lymphocyte, albumin, adenosine deaminase, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, and prothrombin time (Table 2). In parallel, the Boruta algorithm identified 24 important features, which were ranked by importance: β2-microglobulin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, albumin, hemoglobin, direct bilirubin, uric acid, prealbumin, bicarbonate, CCI, prothrombin time, lymphocyte, albumin-to-globulin ratio, phosphorus, calcium, age, total protein, MCHC, RDW-CV, adenosine deaminase, glucose, RDW-SD, monocyte, and white blood cell (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, LASSO regression was applied to identify optimal predictors. As the regularization penalty increased, coefficient estimates progressively shrank toward zero (Fig. 2B). The optimal regularization parameter (λ = 0.02456) was determined via 5-fold cross‑validation using the “lambda.1se” criterion (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, eight variables achieved consensus across all three feature‑selection methods (each receiving 3 votes): age, CCI, lymphocyte, albumin, adenosine deaminase, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, and prothrombin time. Two additional variables received 2 votes (white blood cell count and β2-microglobulin), while 14 features were selected by only one method. Notably, five of the single-vote features (hemoglobin, prealbumin, creatinine, uric acid and bicarbonate) were ranked within the top 10 of the Boruta importance.

Applying the prespecified majority-voting strategy (≥ 2 of 3 algorithms), 10 variables were retained as candidate predictors. To balance parsimony against predictive performance, we compared nested models comprising the 8 unanimously selected variables (3 votes) versus the 10 variables meeting the majority-voting threshold (≥ 2 votes). The 10-variable model exhibited a statistically significant but clinically modest improvement in discrimination (AUC: 0.819 [95% CI: 0.784–0.852] vs. 0.811 [95% CI: 0.775–0.846], DeLong test D = 2.059, P = 0.040; Fig. 2E). In contrast, further inclusion of the five top-ranked Boruta features did not yield a significant improvement (AUC: 0.822 [95% CI: 0.788–0.854] vs. 0.819 [95% CI: 0.784–0.852], D = 1.108, P = 0.268; Fig. 2E). All 10 candidate variables differed significantly between survivors and non-survivors in the training set (all P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2). Consequently, the final prediction model consisted of these 10 predictors: age, CCI, lymphocyte, albumin, adenosine deaminase, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, prothrombin time, white blood cell, and β2-microglobulin.

Discussion

Hip fracture is a serious health issue in the elderly and is often associated with high mortality. Consequently, accurate assessment of mortality risk is crucial for patient management and clinical decision-making [5, 7]. Based on two clinical indicators (age and CCI), the present study identified eight blood parameters significantly associated with short-term mortality risk in hip fracture patients, including lymphocyte, albumin, adenosine deaminase, direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, prothrombin time, white blood cell, and β2-microglobulin. Among these, β2-microglobulin​ merits particular attention. This low-molecular-weight protein, constitutively expressed on nucleated cells and exclusively renally cleared, integrates signals of renal excretory function and systemic immune activation [30]. This dual attribution enables β2-microglobulin to uniquely capture risk at the intersection of renal-metabolic health and inflammatory burden. Notably, our previous work has demonstrated its independent association with mortality in this population [12]. By utilizing these laboratory parameters, clinicians can comprehensively and objectively evaluate the physiological status of hip fracture patients upon admission, which may aid in early risk stratification​ and inform clinical decision-making [12]. Current guidelines recommend early surgical intervention for hip fracture patients [31, 32]. When these parameters suggest a stable or low-risk status, surgery should be performed promptly; otherwise, targeted interventions should be initiated to address the abnormalities. For instance, serum albumin levels below 35.0 g/L may warrant early nutritional support to facilitate recovery in older patients with hip fracture [33]. Moreover, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach has been demonstrated to significantly reduce mortality in hip fracture patients [34]. Thus, abnormalities in these blood parameters may help guide the initiation of individualized MDT protocols, potentially improving patient outcomes.

Based on these blood parameters, we developed an early prediction model for short-term mortality risk in older patients with hip fracture. The model demonstrated favorable discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.812 in the training set and 0.826 in the testing set. The model demonstrated comparable discriminative ability​ to existing models [13, 14, 16, 17, 35], although its clinical utility is limited by a modest PPV. Moreover, the AUC of the present model exceeds that of existing prediction tools developed without laboratory parameters, such as those for 30-day mortality (AUC = 0.76) [36], and 90-day mortality (AUC = 0.74) [37], suggesting that the incorporation of admission blood parameters improves discriminative ability beyond clinical variables alone. Also, sensitivity and specificity are essential metrics for evaluating the performance of classification models. As shown in Fig. 4E, at a threshold of 8.2%, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.831, indicating that 83.1% of actual deceased patients were correctly classified. This suggests that the model performs well in identifying mortality cases, with a relatively low missed diagnosis rate (16.9%). However, the specificity was 0.629, meaning that only 62.9% of actual survivors were accurately identified, resulting in a false-positive rate of 37.1%. In comparison, at a 10.0% threshold, the model by Geleit et al. [16] yielded a sensitivity of 0.632 and a specificity of 0.791, corresponding to a higher missed‑diagnosis rate (36.8%) but a lower false‑positive rate (20.9%) than the present model.

On the other hand, the model demonstrated a trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. In the testing set, only 11 false negatives were observed, resulting in a high NPV of 0.966. This indicates that 96.6% of patients predicted to survive actually survived, supporting the model’s utility in identifying candidates for expedited surgical intervention. Conversely, the model produced 185 false positives, yielding a PPV of 0.226. Consequently, only 22.6% of patients classified as high risk actually died. This low PPV highlights a key limitation: while the model effectively stratifies patients by relative risk, it lacks precision in identifying individual high-risk cases. Rather than attributing this to treatment effects, we acknowledge that this reflects the inherent difficulty of predicting rare events in imbalanced datasets, a challenge also reported by others (PPV 0.071–0.266) [38, 39]. Given this limitation, the model should not be used to exclude patients from surgery. Instead, predicted probabilities should be presented as individualized risk estimates​ to facilitate informed consent and shared decision-making among surgeons, patients, and families [36].

Currently, orthopedic surgeons seldom proactively inform hip fracture patients about their mortality risk to avoid causing undue anxiety or diminishing hope [40]. Among the reasons for ultimately choosing non‑surgical management, only 20% were attributed to patient-related factors, 54% to physician-related factors, and 26% to shared reasons between patients and physicians [41]. Some studies have even found that after undergoing a comprehensive geriatric assessment before surgery, more hip fracture patients opted for non‑surgical treatment [42]. Therefore, for patients whose predicted probabilities above the threshold (i.e., those classified as high-risk), decisions regarding surgical or non-surgical management should not be based solely on model predictions. Comprehensive clinical judgment and shared decision-making remain essential.

In addition to discriminative performance, model calibration is also essential. In the testing set, the calibration slope was 1.046, the calibration intercept was −0.002, and the Brier score was 0.081. Moreover, the P values of the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test exceeded 0.05, indicating that the model provided well‑calibrated mortality probability estimates. Additionally, the DCA showed that the model yielded a higher net benefit across a threshold range of 5% to 71%, supporting its potential clinical utility.

The primary clinical utility of this model lies in generating individualized, physiology-informed estimates of 90-day mortality at admission. The 8.2% threshold, derived retrospectively via the Youden index, is a candidate cut-off around which risk-stratified care concepts may be framed. Its high NPV may reassure clinicians considering expedited surgery for patients whose predicted risk falls below this threshold. However, the modest PPV precludes its use as a standalone determinant of care intensity. Any threshold-guided management strategy-including enhanced monitoring, physiological and nutritional optimization, or early multidisciplinary team engagement-remains hypothetical and requires prospective validation before implementation. The model should therefore complement, not replace, comprehensive geriatric assessment and shared decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the model’s predictive performance exhibits room for improvement.​ While discrimination was favorable, the modest PPV limits precision in identifying individual high-risk cases. Future work incorporating machine learning approaches or alternative modeling strategies may enhance performance, particularly in improving PPV while maintaining sensitivity [43]. Second, the model’s generalizability is limited by its single-center, retrospective derivation and by residual data-driven optimization during model development.​ Although the model was evaluated on a held-out test set and internally validated via bootstrap resampling, these methods assess internal performance only and cannot establish transportability. Derivation from a single-center cohort further limits generalizability. While a prespecified majority-voting framework constrained data-driven flexibility, the final retention of 10 versus 8 predictors was informed by observed performance differences within the training set, introducing model selection uncertainty that bootstrap correction and hold-out evaluation do not fully resolve. Accordingly, the reported performance metrics should be interpreted as upper bounds of true model performance, with definitive quantification of out-of-sample robustness-including shrinkage upon external deployment-requiring validation across temporally and geographically distinct cohorts. Such validation lies beyond the scope of the current study but represents a necessary next step for future implementation. Third, the sample size calculation, while meeting pmsampsize-derived targets for the final 10-predictor specification, does not fully account for model selection uncertainty introduced by screening 29 candidate variables and retaining 10 predictors based on observed performance differentials.​ Conventional sample size heuristics assume predictor selection is independent of outcome data-an assumption partially relaxed by our multi-algorithm feature selection workflow. This may impart optimistic bias to performance estimates that only external validation can reliably quantify and correct via recalibration. Fourth, the model was intentionally restricted to baseline admission parameters to support pre-treatment risk stratification, excluding treatment-related variables (e.g., surgical timing, perioperative complications) and dynamic laboratory trajectories.​ While this design enhances clinical applicability at the point of admission, it limits the model’s ability to capture disease progression or treatment effects and precludes causal inference regarding mortality. Future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal data are warranted to enable temporally updated risk assessment. Fifth, core geriatric domains-including frailty, pre-fracture functional status, and cognition-were not incorporated into the model.​ Although admission blood parameters provide an immediate, physiologically grounded risk estimate, the omission of these established mortality predictors limits the holistic assessment of vulnerability. The model should therefore be viewed as a physiological adjunct rather than a replacement for comprehensive geriatric assessment. Future models should integrate these dimensions to enhance clinical realism. Sixth, the inclusion of specialized biomarkers (prealbumin, adenosine deaminase, β2-microglobulin) may limit widespread uptake.​ While these assays are standard components of our institutional admission panel, they are not universally available in orthopedic departments globally. A simplified model restricted to ubiquitously available parameters (e.g., age, CCI, hemoglobin, albumin) is therefore warranted to quantify the performance trade-off and provide a practically implementable alternative for resource-constrained settings. Seventh, the 7.50% loss to follow-up, although relatively low, raises the possibility of informative censoring.​ Patients lost to follow-up may have experienced systematically different outcomes (e.g., higher mortality among frailer individuals), potentially leading to underestimation of true mortality risk. Future studies should leverage national death registries or compulsory reporting systems to minimize this bias. Eighth, as a retrospective study, this work lacked interventional evaluation of the model’s impact on clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.​ The 8.2% probability threshold and any attendant risk-stratified management pathways are retrospectively derived hypotheses requiring prospective corroboration. Definitive assessment of clinical utility-including net benefit, safety, and cost-effectiveness-awaits prospective interventional studies, ideally pragmatic trials comparing threshold-guided management with usual care, before any consideration of implementation.

Conclusion

An early prediction model for short-term mortality risk in older patients with hip fracture was developed using admission blood parameters. The model demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, along with potential clinical utility. It provides modest early risk stratification based on laboratory parameters; however, the limited PPV indicates that it should serve to supplement, rather than replace, clinical judgment.
