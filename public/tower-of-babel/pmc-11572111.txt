Physiotherapists as first-contact practitioners for patients with low back pain in French primary care: a pragmatic cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Amélie Kechichian, François Desmeules, Pauline Girard, Hugo Terrisse, Céline Vermorel, Nicolas Pinsault
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA new model of care enables French physiotherapists (PT) working in collaboration with family physicians (FP) to expand their usual scope of practice for patients with acute low back pain (LBP). The aim of our study is to evaluate the impact of this new first-contact physiotherapy (FCP) advanced practice model compared to usual FP care.MethodsA multicentre pragmatic non-inferiority cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) has been conducted in six multidisciplinary primary healthcare centres in France. Patients from 20 to 55 years old with acute LBP consulted either the PT or the FP. PT independently assessed and managed patients including medication prescription. The primary outcome measure was disability at six weeks (Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire, range 0–24). Secondary outcomes include pain, risk disability prognosis, satisfaction with care, healthcare resources use and wait times. Data were collected at baseline, six and twelve weeks. Outcomes across arms were compared using mixed models regression analysis. Except for non-inferiority analysis of the primary outcome measure, other analyses were performed with a two-sided significance level of 0.05.ResultsSixty patients were recruited (PT: 32, FP: 28). The adjusted mean difference between groups for disability at 6 weeks was 0.39 in favour of the FP group (95%CI: -2.03; 2.81, p = 0.753). Considering a 5 points minimal clinically important difference, the FCP-led model of care was not inferior to usual FP care for the primary outcome. There was no statistically significant difference between groups in disability at 3 months and pain at 6 weeks and 3 months. PTs prescribed significantly less medications than FPs (p < 0.001). No statistically significant difference was found for other healthcare resource use outcomes, patients’ satisfaction and wait times.ConclusionThis is the first RCT to evaluate the impact of a FCP advanced practice model of care including medical delegated acts in a primary care setting. Our results suggest that the FCP-led model of care is not inferior to usual FP care regarding disability at 6 weeks. The FCP model could result in possible benefits in terms of healthcare resources use. Further adequate powered studies with larger sample size are needed to draw stronger conclusions.Trial registrationThe study has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05200533) on the 20th of January 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11814-2.Keywords: First-contact physiotherapy, Advanced practice physiotherapy, Medical delegated acts, Low back pain, Randomised controlled trial, Primary care

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorder (MSKD) in the world and the most common cause of long term pain and disability [1, 2]. It affects hundreds of millions of people around the world [2, 3]. Most of the patients suffering from LBP are managed in primary care and usually consult a family physician (FP). In France, LBP is the second most common reason for consulting in family practice [4]. FP frequently refer patients with LBP to physiotherapists (PT) as French PT have yet to be granted direct access to patients and are not recognised as first-contact providers [5].

Current medical shortage and increasing healthcare needs lead to overcrowding of primary care services in France [6–8]. Time to access healthcare services and physicians’ workload are both increasing. Many patients with non-urgent conditions decide to not seek care for their health problems given this lack of access [6]. PTs are considered experts in the assessment, diagnosis and management of patients with MSKD [9–12]. Several studies suggested that early access to physiotherapy may improve patients’ clinical outcomes and decrease healthcare costs [13–16].

In some countries facing similar issues with access to primary care services, PTs are now working in advanced practice roles as first-contact practitioners [17–19]. First-contact physiotherapists (FCP) are able to receive patients without a medical referral, to diagnose MSKD conditions and in some cases, to perform tasks which are usually provided by physicians, such as prescribing medications or imaging tests [20, 21]. By allowing PTs to expand their usual scope of practice, FCP models conform to the globally accepted definition of advanced practice physiotherapy [22]. There is growing evidence that advanced practice PTs can efficiently manage patients with MSKD in various clinical settings including orthopaedic services and emergency departments [23–26]. Similar or better clinical outcomes for patients have been reported with advanced practice physiotherapy care compared to physicians’ usual care [23, 25, 27]. Advanced practice physiotherapy can also lead to lower healthcare resources use and reduce wait times for specialist consultations [23, 28]. Patients are also highly satisfied with care received [25, 29]. In primary care settings, the expansion of PT roles and scope of practice is also emerging worldwide [19, 30, 31] but only a few non-randomized studies have focused on the efficacy of advanced practice physiotherapy in primary care [30, 32, 33].

In France, the organization of primary care practice is expected to change in order to cope with increasing demand and to improve access to primary care services [7, 34, 35]. Several models of collaborative practice are emerging in multidisciplinary primary healthcare centres [36]. New French models entitled “Cooperation Protocols” are implemented between primary healthcare professionals including FPs, pharmacists, nurses or PTs [37–39]. These models enable tasks to be redistribute from medical to non-medical professionals. The “Cooperation Protocols” are a promising strategy to address medical workforce shortage by allowing non-medical professionals to expand their usual roles and scope of practice [40].

One of these models involves PTs for the care of patients with LBP [41]. Eligible patients may consult directly the PT instead of the FP. This model expands the usual scope of practice of French PTs allowing them to work as first-contact practitioners which include LBP diagnosis, medical sick leave certificates delivering, low class of analgesic medication prescription and additional outpatient physiotherapy referral. This approved healthcare pathway remains coordinated by FPs and PTs ensure that any necessary information regarding medical management of patients is accurately conveyed to FPs [41]. This model is intended to be deployed at a national level and could include other MSKD in the future [37]. In our study, FCP term will be preferred to advanced practice physiotherapy as the model is not formally defined as an advanced practice models by French regulatory authorities. A previous study demonstrated that PTs and FPs working in French multidisciplinary healthcare centres are generally receptive to this new FCP model. The perceived level of competencies of PTs to manage acute LBP was high [42]. To date, no studies have been conducted to evaluate the impact of FCP models involving an extension of French PTs roles and scope of practice in primary care settings.

Objectives

The overall aim of our study was to assess the impact of the new FCP model in France compared to usual FPs’ care for patients with acute non-specific musculoskeletal LBP. The specific objectives were to:

Assess the clinical non-inferiority of the FCP model of care compared to usual FPs care in terms of patient-related disability at 6 weeks using the Rolland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) as primary outcome measure;

Assess the clinical non-inferiority of the FCP model of care compared to usual FPs care in terms of patient-related disability at 6 weeks using the Rolland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) as primary outcome measure;

Compare both types of care on the following outcomes: disability at 3 months using the RMDQ, pain at 6 weeks and 3 months using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), LBP disability prognosis using the StarT-Back Screening Tool (SBST) at 6 weeks and 3 months and adverse events;

Compare both types of care on the following outcomes: disability at 3 months using the RMDQ, pain at 6 weeks and 3 months using the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), LBP disability prognosis using the StarT-Back Screening Tool (SBST) at 6 weeks and 3 months and adverse events;

Compare healthcare resources utilization between both types of care at 6 weeks and 3 months including proportions of medication prescription, sick leave and any related medical or physiotherapy consultations;

Compare healthcare resources utilization between both types of care at 6 weeks and 3 months including proportions of medication prescription, sick leave and any related medical or physiotherapy consultations;

Compare wait time for patient initial assessment between both types of care;

Compare wait time for patient initial assessment between both types of care;

Compare patients’ satisfaction with care between both models.

Methods

Trial design

This study was a multicentre pragmatic non-inferiority cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) conducted in six multidisciplinary primary healthcare centres in the Auvergne-Rhone-Alpes region in France. RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating the efficacy of an intervention [43]. A cluster RCT was chosen to avoid treatment contamination bias and due to the nature of the intervention within each primary healthcare centres [43]. Our choice of a non-inferiority design was based on the expectation that clinical non-inferiority of the FCP-led model of care added to benefit in terms of healthcare resources utilisation and/or wait times will be sufficient to favour the new model [44]. The non-inferiority hypothesis was assessed based on the primary outcome measure. The guidelines SPIRIT and CONSORT for interventional, cluster trials and non-inferiority trials have been consulted [43–45]. The study protocol was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05200533).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the French Research Ethics Committee of Ouest-IV Nantes (number 21.01537.000012) as a type II trial under the French Jardé law.

Study settings

Six multidisciplinary primary care centres were selected and recruited following a cross-sectional study aiming to explore FPs’ and PTs’ acceptability about the new FCP model of care [42]. The six following sites were favourable to the implementation of the model and volunteered to participate in the randomised controlled trial: interprofessional university primary healthcare center of Saint-Martin d’Hères, multidisciplinary healthcare centers of Moirans, Coublevie and Annecy, interprofessional healthcare centers of Chambéry and Lans-en-Vercors in the Auvergne-Rhone-Alpes region of France. These primary healthcare centres are primary care settings where primary care practitioners are working collaboratively to meet healthcare needs of their population. FPs, nurses, PTs, pharmacists, psychologists and other healthcare providers are working at a same site or close in proximity. They used interprofessional communication tools such as shared information software, secured messaging services and they regularly met to discuss clinical practice, education, research and organizational topics.

Randomisation process

The six eligible and consenting sites were randomly assigned to the experimental arm (FCP model of care) or to the usual care arm (FP care). Computer-generated sequence of randomisation was performed before the start of the trial by an independent researcher who was not involved in other aspects of the trial.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the participants in the trial were: 1- patients aged from 20 to 55 years old, 2- consulting for a new LBP complaint of less than four weeks in the past 12 months, 3- whose FP takes part in the FCP model of care, 4- are beneficiary of the French medical insurance coverage and 5- able to understand and speak French.

The exclusion criteria were: 1- inability to respond to the questionnaires, 2- LBP resulting from a major trauma (e.g.: high-velocity trauma or major motor vehicle accident), 3- LBP associated with systemic symptoms such as a fever or other signs of infections, 4- work-related LBP eligible to worker’s compensation benefits, 5- being pregnant.

Blinding procedure

Participants or healthcare providers blinding was not possible due to the study design (cluster RCT) and to the types of studied interventions (FCP or FP-led care). Statistical analyses were however conducted by two blinded statisticians (H.T. and C.V.).

Participants’ identification and recruitment

Potential participants were identified by medical administrative assistants when patients called to set up an appointment with the FP for a LBP complaint. The study was presented to prospective participants and if they agreed to participate, an appointment was scheduled with a PT or a FP. Inclusion criteria were further checked by a research professional just before the appointment. Trained research professionals in each clinical setting were the PTs or FPs working also as care providers. They explained the study including its purpose, required implication to eligible patients and answered any remaining questions. Research professionals obtained written informed consent from patients who agreed to enrol in the study. Participants’ process is illustrated in a flow chart (Fig. 1).

Interventions

Participants included in the experimental group received care according to the FCP model of care. They were independently assessed and managed by the PT. PTs examined the patient and checked that they were no signs or symptoms of any serious conditions requiring urgent medical referral [46–48]. He formulated a medical diagnosis and an intervention plan including therapeutic education, active exercise rehabilitation, physical activity promotion, could prescribe analgesic medication (acetaminophen or oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, NSAID) and deliver sick leave if necessary (up to five days). PTs were also able to prescribe additional outpatient physiotherapy treatments, to refer patients to a physician or to other professionals if required. Participants of the usual care group received usual FP care. This included independent medical assessment, management and discharge by the FP. A physician’s referral to outpatient physiotherapy or other professionals was possible. Overall care offered by PTs or FPs was not standardized but was systematically documented.

Outcomes measures

Baseline data collection

At trial inclusion, patients answered an online survey to collect data about their socio-demographic characteristics such as age, sex, gender, anthropometric data, living and professional status. The socio-demographic questionnaire is available in a supplementary file.

Primary outcome measure

The main outcome measure was disability using the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) at the primary endpoint, 6 weeks after the initial consultation. The RMDQ is a self-administered questionnaire that includes 24 items assessing disability related to physical activity, sleep, psychosocial factors, activities of daily living, appetite and pain. Scores range from 0 (no disability) to 24 (maximum disability). The French translation of the RMDQ is valid, reliable and responsive to change for patients with acute and chronic LBP [49–51]. A minimal clinically important difference of 2.5 points on the RMDQ was first considered for assessing the importance of the clinical effect [52].

Secondary outcomes measures

Pain was measured using the Brief Pain Inventory – Short Form (BPI). The BPI is a self-administered questionnaire that included two scales: a pain intensity and a pain interference scale. The pain intensity scale enables the patient to rate the intensity of pain on a 0 to 10-point Likert scale for each of the minimum, maximum, average and current level of pain. The pain interference scale measures the impact of pain on various functional activities using a 0 to 10-point Likert scale. For each scale, 0 represents no pain and 10 represents maximum pain (e.g., the worse pain you can possibly imagine). The BPI is valid, reliable and responsive to change in MSKD population and a French version has been validated [53–55]. The BPI interference scale and the severity scale have an estimated minimum clinically important difference of 1 point [56, 57].

The StarT-Back Screening Tool (SBST) was used to stratify individuals to low, medium or high risk of a poor outcome. SBST is a brief screening questionnaire designed for directing initial treatment for LBP in primary care. The tool is composed of 9 items that assess physical (leg pain, co-morbid pain and disability) and psychosocial (catastrophizing, fear, anxiety and depression) factors previously found to be strong indicators for poor prognosis. Patients were asked to either agree or disagree with each of the 9 statements. A total score and a psychosocial subscale score were both calculated and enabled to allocate the patient to the low, medium or high risk group. The French version of the SBST is a valid and reliable tool for patients with LBP [58–61].

Satisfaction with care was measured with the MedRisk Instrument at initial discharge. The MedRisk Instrument have been shown to be a valid and reliable tool to measure patient satisfaction, especially for patients receiving physiotherapy care [62].

Healthcare resources utilization outcomes

FPs and PTs completed a standardized form following their assessment and interventions. Suggested investigations and treatment plan including non-pharmacological interventions (therapeutic education, active exercises rehabilitation and physical activity promotion), medication prescription, sick leave duration, referral to outpatient physiotherapy or other professional or medical specialists were recorded at participants’ level. For PTs, referral back to a FP for additional medical investigations suggestions such as medical imaging or laboratory tests following clinical suspicion of serious pathology was also documented in the standardized form.

Patients were asked to report the number of medical visits (FPs, specialized physicians, emergency departments) and physiotherapy sessions they had in the 6 past weeks for their LBP onset at 6 and 12 weeks’ time points. The healthcare resources use questionnaire is available in a supplementary file.

Date of patients’ initial telephone contact with the primary healthcare centre to see a provider for their LBP was recorded to measure wait time to initial assessment either by a PT or a FP.

Trial procedure

Patient-reported outcomes measures and socio-demographic data were completed electronically via an online survey at baseline and at 6 weeks and 3 months following the initial assessment [63]. The Limesurvey platform was used for the online surveys. The platform was hosted in the Grenoble-Alpes University in France and secured using data encryption. A link to complete the online survey was sent by email to participants with instructions for completion at 6 and 12 weeks. If participants did not complete the survey on the required day, a reminder was sent twice in the 7 and 14 days to encourage completion. Participants were contacted by phone if they did not complete the survey within the expected timeframe. The recruitment period started on the 1st of March 2022 and ended on the 15th of July 2023.

Sample size

The main hypothesis of our study was that the FCP-led care was not inferior to usual FP care in terms of patient-related disability at 6 weeks using the RMDQ. The target sample size had been calculated for a one-sided significance level of 0.025 and a power of 0.80. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of the primary outcome measure (RMDQ at 6 weeks) had been calculated at 2.5 points (/24) with a standard deviation of 5 points (/24) [52, 64]. An intra-cluster correlation coefficient of 0.01 was assumed based on a previous study on the same topic [65]. Six primary healthcare centres were participating, divided up in two groups of three clusters. Considering these assumptions, the trial was adequately powered if 33 participants were recruited per cluster for a total sample size of 198 participants accounting for a potential loss to follow-up of 20%.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to present the participants’ characteristics. Descriptive statistics were also presented for all outcome measures at different time-points. Complete-case analysis was used and modified intention-to-treat analyses were conducted based on a loss to follow-up criteria [66]. Linear mixed model regression analyses were used to compare the primary and secondary reported outcomes (RMDQ and BPI) at 6 weeks and 3 months following the initial assessment. For the linear mixed model regression analysis of the RMDQ scores, the intra-cluster correlation, the RMDQ score at baseline and the SBST category at baseline were considered. The SBST categories for LBP patients have been shown to be predictive values of patients’ risk of a poor prognosis [58]. For the BPI scores, the intra-cluster correlation, the BPI score at baseline and the SBST category at baseline were considered. Non-inferiority analysis for the primary outcome measure was performed with a one-sided significance level of 0.025. Other analyses were performed with a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Non-inferiority was assessed comparing the one-sided upper bound of the confidence interval of the between-group difference to the minimal clinically important difference for disability at 6 weeks [52]. We concluded to non-inferiority if the upper bound was lower than the minimal clinically important difference. Healthcare resources utilization outcomes, wait time for initial consultation and satisfaction outcomes between groups were compared using linear or logistic mixed model regression analyses considering the intra-cluster correlation. When regression analyses could not be performed because of small sample size, statistical inferential tests were used (χ² or fisher’s exact test). Analyses were conducted by a statistician of the research team who was blinded to clusters’ allocation. The STATA 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas) software was used.

Oversight and monitoring

The research team THEMAS from TIMC laboratory (UMR CNRS 5525) coordinated the study. The principal investigator (A.K.) was also working as PT in one of the six primary healthcare centres (Interprofessional university primary healthcare center of Saint-Martin d’Hères), which was the coordinator centre. Data monitoring was conducted at the end of the trial. The study has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05200533).

Adverse event reporting

Any adverse event was documented and communicated to the research promoter (Direction of Clinical Research and Innovation of the Grenoble-Alps University Hospital).

Deviations from intended protocol

A MCID of 2.5 points for the primary outcome measure was initially considered for designing the trial and to calculate sample size based on previous studies [52, 67–69]. Literature showed that calculated values of MCID for clinical outcomes measures of LBP patients vary quite drastically depending on patient population being studied, follow-up intervals and methods used to calculate MCID [70, 71]. Following a complementary search in the literature, we found that a change score of 4 to 5 points on the RMDQ has previously been recommended as the smallest important change for patients suffering from LBP for less than 6 weeks [72]. Another study that compared MCID of functional outcomes in four subpopulations of LBP patients suggested a 5 points reduction in RMDQ score to be considered as the adequate MCID for patients with moderate disability (initial RMDQ score: 9.6/24, SD: 4.44) suffering from less than 30 days pain who were cared by FPs and PTs in primary care setting [73]. Finally, an international expert consensus following a literature review proposed a 5 point MCID for the RMDQ arguing that evidence was insufficient to set up different MCID values for chronic and acute LBP patients [74]. In order to be consistent with the inclusion criteria of our study participants who consulted primary care practitioners for less than 4-weeks duration pain, we decided to reconsider the initial MCID of the primary outcome of the study. We assumed that a 5 points change in the RMDQ score should be considered as an appropriate minimal clinical important difference for our study participants [73, 74].

Seventeen participants were lost to follow-up at 6 weeks (follow-up rate: 72%, PT group: 69%, FP group: 75%) and three more participants were lost to follow-up at 3 months (follow-up rate: 67%, PT group: 66%, FP group: 69%). Characteristics of the loss to follow-up participants is available in supplemental material. No adverse events were reported.

Disability

At 6 weeks and 3 months, both groups showed improvement in RMDQ score (Fig. 2). The mean difference between groups of the RMDQ score at 6 weeks was 0.058 in favour of the control group (95%CI: -2.23; 2.35, p = 0.959). The adjusted mean difference between groups of the RMDQ score at 6 weeks was 0.39 in favour of the control group (95%CI: -2.03; 2.81, p = 0.753). The upper bound of the confidence interval of the adjusted mean difference did not exceed the MCID of 5 points for the RMDQ. Based on a 5 points threshold and considering the 95%CI of the adjusted mean difference between groups, the non-inferiority hypothesis on the RMDQ score at 6 weeks was verified. At 3 months, no statistical significant between group difference in RMDQ score was found (95%IC:-4.55; 7.24, p = 0.655).

Effects on healthcare resources utilization

PT referral to FPs

After initial assessment, six participants (18.8%) analysed in the PT group were referred to FPs for further medical review. One of them experienced a third onset of LBP during the 12 past months, which was defined as a redirection criteria by the legislative text of the FCP model. Isolated or combined red flags were suspected by PTs for five other participants (infection-related symptoms n = 3, vascular-related symptoms n = 1, neurological-related symptoms n = 1, chest pain n = 2, lower limb radiating pain n = 1). The red flag clinical suspicions were finally ruled out by FPs without additional medical test or imaging.

Healthcare resources use at baseline

Healthcare resources use at baseline are described in Table 3. Statistical significant differences between groups at initial discharge were found regarding proportion of medication prescriptions. PTs recommended significantly less medication (paracetamol and NSAID drugs) than FPs (p < 0.001). No statistical significant differences were found in the delivery proportion and duration of sick leave certificates or referral to supervised physiotherapy sessions. FP also prescribed opioids (n = 5), anxiolytics (n = 5) as well as imaging test (n = 1) in the FP group but proportions could not be compared because PT were not allowed not prescribe these in the FCP model.

Healthcare resources use at 3 months

Healthcare resources use at 6 weeks are described in Table 3. After 3 months, there was no statistical significant difference between groups in the proportion of participants who have consulted the FP for LBP-related symptoms and in the proportion of patients that underwent physiotherapy sessions. Waiting time and patients’ satisfaction

Participants’ waiting times and satisfaction with care after initial consultation are presented in Table 3. Most of the participants were received within twelve hours after initial call for an appointment in both groups (PT group: 78%, n = 25, FP group: 71%, n = 20). High level of satisfaction with care were found for both groups using the Medrisk questionnaire (PT group: 4.64/5, 93%, FP group: 4.53/5, 91%). There was no statistical significant differences between groups for waiting time and for patients’ satisfaction with care.

Discussion

Our study aimed to assess the impact of a new FCP model compared to usual FPs’ care for patients with acute LBP in France.

For the primary outcome measure, our results showed that the upper bound of the confidence interval of the adjusted mean difference between groups was inferior to the established MCID of 5 points and thus, suggest that the new FCP model of care was not inferior to usual FP care in terms of disability at 6 weeks measured by the RMDQ [64, 75]. Regarding secondary clinical outcomes measures, both groups showed similar improvement and no statistical significant differences were found between groups at 6 weeks and 3 months. However, upper and lower bounds of the confidence intervals for pain and disability outcomes showed a large variability generating some uncertainty in the estimated effect of the intervention, either favouring PT group or FP group. These results did not enable us to formally conclude on several potential clinical benefits of one intervention over the other.

No other studies have evaluated the impact of advanced practice physiotherapy roles including medical acts on patient-related clinical outcomes such as pain and disability in primary care settings. However, our results are consistent with a previous observational study showing that patients managed by FCPs with advanced practice roles including medical delegated acts in primary care demonstrated significant clinical improvements at 6 months in terms of global perception of change and health-related quality of life [17]. One other study conducted in primary care settings consistently reported that direct triaging by PTs without medical act delegation leads to at least as positive health effects as primary assessment by FPs [76]. Previous studies including medical acts delegation have been mainly conducted in secondary care settings where the evidence consistently found advanced practice PTs to have their care as effective or even better than usual care [23, 25].

Regarding healthcare resources use outcomes, our results showed that PTs prescribed significantly less medications than FPs. Patients in the PT group were more likely referred to physiotherapy and less likely to specialist physicians but the differences were not statistically significant. Similarly, recent observational or feasibility studies showed that referral rates to physiotherapy were higher for patients seen by PTs in advanced practice roles and PTs were found to use or refer to fewer health services such as specialist physicians referral [32, 33, 77]. In previous studies, lower healthcare costs were associated with FCP advanced practice models in primary care showing the potentially medico-economic benefit of such models. Costs evaluation could therefore be an interesting future research area for the French FCP model of care [17, 77].

Consistently with other similar studies, no adverse events were reported in our study [17, 30, 32]. Although this study was underpowered for this outcome, our result suggests that PTs have a similar safety profile compared to FPs.

Results from patients’ satisfaction questionnaires showed a very positive response to this new model in accordance with other similar studies [17, 33]. Somewhat surprisingly, waiting times were extremely short in both groups as a majority of participants sought care in the same day they called for an appointment and no difference was observed between groups. This result is in contrast with one of our secondary hypothesis as we previously expected waiting times to be lower in the PT group. That can be explained because of the experimental context of the study as investigators encouraged clinicians to receive patients in a timely manner.

Strength and limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first RCT to assess the impact of FCP with advance practice physiotherapy roles such as medication prescription and referral to other providers on patient-related clinical outcomes in a primary care setting. The impact of advanced practice physiotherapy in primary care settings is an emerging research field, especially in France were the FCP model is not formally recognize as an advanced practice model of care. Our results provide a first step in assessing the efficacy of such models. The pragmatic nature of our trial fully represented care that PTs can offer to patients when enrolled in this innovative model [78, 79].

However, our study also has limitations. First, we were unable to recruit 198 participants as originally planned due to a very low recruitment process. We could only recruit about 30% of the initially targeted sample size calculated with a 2.5 MCID. This can be explained by various factors. First, the inclusion criteria established following the legislative text of the “cooperation protocol” were found very restrictive and did not enable us to target a large sample of participants. It appears the overall population is fairly small. For example, participants consulting for a 4 to 6-weeks LBP could not be included, as well as participants who had work related injuries and participants older than 55-years old. Medical administrative assistants had also trouble identifying potential study participants as they received substantial calls and lacked time to screen for eligibility criteria and to explain the study. Complexity of the administrative procedures required to set up and run the FCP model of care could contribute to weaken research professionals’ motivation to include study participants. Given the low recruitment rate over the first 16 months of the study and our inability to reach the estimated number of participants within the expected time, we decided to stop the study as we considered it was unethical to continue involve participants in a study which ultimately had a low statistical power [80, 81].

Additionally, we must note that the groups showed small differences in baseline characteristics, as there were more men and at low-risk chronicity patients in the intervention group. However, the SBST category at baseline was considered in the statistical analyses conducted for each clinical outcome, as this variable was used as an adjustment factor. This most likely reduced any impact of the initial imbalances on the results of our study.

One other reason that affected our study sample is the substantial dropout of participants who failed to complete follow-up outcome measures despite reminders. That could be explained because participants who rapidly recovered after initial consultation may not be interested anymore in completing questionnaires about their symptoms or because some other participants felt that answering questionnaires was too much of a burden. Descriptive analyses of the loss to follow-up participants did not suggest important differences between the lost to follow-up group and the group of participants who were followed at 6 weeks, except for gender distribution. It would seem that there is a higher proportion of men in this group of participants that did not complete the study. We decided not to perform multiple imputation as initially planned because of the small sample size and moderate proportion of missing data that could lead to imprecise results and may generate a risk of misinterpretation [82].

Due to the small sample size for primary outcome measure and imbalances in cluster sizes recruitment, the inadequate power of our study may affect the robustness of our results that should be interpreted with caution [43]. Noticeably, the larger MCID we finally assumed for the primary outcome measure could have led to significant reduction of the targeted sample size and could have increased the power of our study. The revision of the MCID represents an important deviation to the trial protocol and may generate a risk of interpretative bias for our study [83]. However, evidence recommends researchers to use MCIDs relevant for their individual study population and setting when reporting the results of a clinical trial as MCID has been shown to be population specific [73, 84–86]. Accordingly, we believe this deviation was necessary to draw valid conclusions based on a MCID fitting with our sample and our population of interest.We should also note that our results may not be generalizable to all French PTs and FPs working in primary care. In our study, PT and FP are working collaboratively in multidisciplinary healthcare centres and these clinics only represent around 5% of usual clinical practice settings of PTs in French primary care.

Lastly, advanced PTs roles in primary and secondary care settings are still underdeveloped in France and suffer from a lack of formal recognition [87]. That could contribute to make the FCP model implementation in clinical settings even more complex. The difficulties we were confronted with regarding patients’ recruitment should be considered to enhance the new model of care and facilitate its deployment.

Conclusion

The results of our study indicate that FCP-led model of care was not inferior to usual FP-led care on patients’ disability outcome at 6 weeks. No differences between groups were found for pain outcomes at 6 weeks and 3 months and disability outcomes at 3 months. The FCP model was significantly associated with lower medication prescription by PTs.

Although we have to consider the small sample size and inadequate power of the study, our results suggest that this FCP advanced practice model may positively impact LBP patients’ care by maintaining the quality care and even reducing associated healthcare resources use. This study encourages further deployment of new FCP models including advanced practice PT roles in primary care settings. Further adequately powered studies with larger sample size including costs analysis are however needed to formally conclude on the benefit of such models.
