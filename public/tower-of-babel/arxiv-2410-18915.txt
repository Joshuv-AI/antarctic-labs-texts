Testing Support Size More Efficiently Than Learning Histograms


Abstract
Consider two problems about an unknown probability distribution p p : 1. How many samples from p p are required to test if p p is supported on n n elements or not? Specifically, given samples from p p , determine whether it is supported on at most n n elements, or it is “ ε \varepsilon -far” (in total variation distance) from being supported on n n elements. 2. Given m m samples from p p , what is the largest lower bound on its support size that we can produce? The best known upper bound for problem (1) uses a general algorithm for learning the histogram of the distribution p p , which requires Θ ⁡ ( n ε 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) \Theta(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\log n}) samples. We show that testing can be done more efficiently than learning the histogram, using only O ⁡ ( n ε ​ log ⁡ n ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) O(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}\log(1/\varepsilon)) samples, nearly matching the best known lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( n ε ​ log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}) . This algorithm also provides a better solution to problem (2), producing larger lower bounds on support size than what follows from previous work. The proof relies on an analysis of Chebyshev polynomial approximations outside the range where they are designed to be good approximations, and the paper is intended as an accessible self-contained exposition of the Chebyshev polynomial method.

Introduction

Sadly, it is often necessary to make decisions based on probability distributions. For example, you
may need to make a decision based on the support size of an unknown distribution:

Example 1.1
.

There is a population of fish in the lake, which may be described as a probability distribution

p
over the set of possible fish species. You want to preserve a specimen of each species.
You have no idea how many fish or fish species there are, and you only have
10000 sample jars. Is that enough, or should you buy more? You would like to decide between:

1.

There are at most 10000 species of fish in the lake. (You have enough jars.)

2.

Any collection of 10000 species will miss at least 0.1% of the population, i. e. 0.1% of the
fish will belong to species
not
in the collection. (You need more jars.)

The naïve strategy is to sample random fish until you have filled all the jars, and if you
continue to see new species, buy more jars. Of course, you prefer to predict in advance if you need
more jars. How many fish do you need to sample before you can make this decision (and be correct
with probability at least 99%)?

The best known algorithms for making this type of decision work by learning the histogram of the
distribution (the unordered multiset of nonzero probabilities densities
\{p_{i}|p_{i}\neq 0\}
). In this paper we show there is a more sample-efficient way to do it. Formally,
the problem is to distinguish between distributions with support size at most
n
, and those which
are
\varepsilon
-far
from having support size at most
n
. We say
p
is
\varepsilon
-far

from having support size at most
n
when, for every probability distribution
q
with support size

|\supp(q)|\leq n
, the total variation (TV) distance between
p
and
q
is
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(p,q)>\varepsilon
. Then:

Definition 1.2

(Testing Support Size)
.

A
support-size tester
with sample complexity
m(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
is an algorithm
A

which takes as input the parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\sigma\in(0,1)
.
It draws a multiset
\bm{S}
of
m=m(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
independent samples from an unknown
probability distribution
p
over
\mathbb{N}
, and its output must satisfy:

1.

If
|\supp(p)|\leq n
then
\underset{\bm{S}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[A(\bm{S})\text{ outputs }\mathsf{Accept}\right]\geq\sigma
; and

2.

If
p
is
\varepsilon
-far
from having support size at most
n
, then

\underset{\bm{S}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[A(\bm{S})\text{ outputs }\mathsf{Reject}\right]\geq\sigma
.

Unless otherwise noted, we set
\sigma=3/4
.

Testing support size is a basic statistical decision problem (see e. g. the textbook
[
12
, §11.4]
and
recent work
[
18
,
2
,
3
,
26
]
) that underlies many other commonly studied tasks. For example,
it is a decision version of the problem of
estimating
support size – a problem whose history
dates back to Fisher
[
11
]
, Goodman
[
20
]
, and Good & Turing
[
19
]
; see

[
7
]
for a survey, and more recent work including
[
30
,
33
,
34
,
37
,
25
]
.
Testing support size is also
important for understanding the
testing vs. learning
question in property testing.

1.1
Testing vs. Learning Distributions

Testing support size is a
distribution testing
problem, which
is a type of
property testing
. A basic technique in property testing is
the testing-by-learning approach of
[
15
]
, where the tester learns an approximation of
the input and makes its decision based on this approximation. One of the guiding questions in
property testing is: When can this technique be beaten?

It requires
\Theta(n/\varepsilon^{2})
samples to
learn
a distribution
p
over domain
[n]
, up
to TV distance
\varepsilon
(see e. g. the survey
[
8
]
). Remarkable and surprising recent work

[
33
,
34
,
21
,
24
]
proves that the
histogram
of distributions
p
over
[n]

can be learned with only
\Theta(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\log n})
samples — a vanishing fraction of
the domain. This means one can test the support size – or perform several other tasks – using

O(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\log n})
samples to learn the histogram. As noted in the textbook

[
12
]
and recent work
[
18
,
3
]
, this testing-by-learning algorithm gives the best
known upper bound for testing support size when the true support size is promised to satisfy

|\supp(p)|=O(n)
. Without this promise there does not appear to be a simple way to obtain a
similar bound from known results (see
[
18
, pp.21]
and the discussion in

Appendix

C
, where we sketch arguments to obtain bounds of

O(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon^{3}\log n})
from
[
35
]
). We show that the testing-by-learning algorithm
can be beaten, while also removing any restriction on the true support size:

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the sample complexity of testing support size of an
unknown distribution
p
(over any countable domain) is at most

m(n,\varepsilon)=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}\cdot\min\left\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log n\right\}\right)\,.

In terms of
Example

1.1
, if you have
n
jars, you can decide whether to buy more jars
after taking
O(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}\log(1/\varepsilon))
samples, filling
less than a sublinear
O(\tfrac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon\log n})
-fraction of the jars.

Theorem

1.3
nearly matches the best known lower bound of
\Omega(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon\log n})
(which can be deduced from lower bounds of
[
33
,
37
]
). An important note about this
problem is that, in the setting of
Theorem

1.3
, the related problem of
estimating

the support size is impossible: the unknown support size of
p
is unbounded and the probability
densities
p_{i}
can be arbitrarily small. If one makes assumptions to avoid these issues, then there
are tight bounds of
\Theta(\tfrac{k}{\log k}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))
for estimating the support size
up to
\pm\varepsilon k
when each nonzero density
p_{i}
is promised to satisfy
p_{i}>1/k

[
37
,
25
]
(see also
[
30
,
33
,
1
,
34
]
). Recalling
Example

1.1
,
k

would be the total number of
fish
in the lake, whereas in our results
n
is the number of

species
.

1.2
Good Lower Bounds on Support Size

Quoting I. J. Good in
[
7
]
, “I don’t believe it is usually possible to estimate the number
of unseen species [i. e. support size]…but only an approximate lower bound to that number.” So,
in lieu of an estimate, what is the best lower bound we can get?

Question 1.4
.

Given
m
samples from a distribution
p
and parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, what is the biggest
number
\widehat{S}
that we can output, while still satisfying
\widehat{S}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\supp(p)|
?

Our algorithm can produce lower bounds as large as
\Omega(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}m\log m)
out of only
m
samples. Let us formalize the quality of these lower bounds. A reasonable
target for such a “Good” lower bound
\widehat{S}
is that it should exceed the

\varepsilon
-effective support size
, the smallest number of elements covering
1-\varepsilon

probability mass:

Definition 1.5

(Effective support size)
.

For any probability distribution
p
over
\mathbb{N}
and any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we define the

\varepsilon
-effective support size

\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)
as the smallest number
k\in\mathbb{N}

such that there exists distribution
q
with
|\supp(q)|=k
and
\mathsf{dist}_{\mathsf{TV}}(p,q)\leq\varepsilon
.

With no assumptions on the distribution, the effective support size is a more natural target for
estimation, because it accounts for the fact that an arbitrarily large number of elements in the
support may comprise an arbitrarily small probability mass. Effective support size was used in

[
9
,
4
]
, and algorithms for estimating it were analyzed in
[
14
,
27
,
13
]
,
but these algorithms assume the algorithm can learn the probability densities
p_{i}
by quering
element
i
. Our algorithm provides a Good lower bound using only samples:

Corollary 1.6
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there is an algorithm which draws at most

O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}\cdot\min\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log n\}\right)

samples from an arbitrary distribution
p
, and outputs a number
\widehat{S}
which satisfies (with
probability at least
3/4
)

\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),n\}\leq\widehat{S}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\supp(p)|\,.

We are not aware of any prior work focusing on this type of guarantee, although it seems
quite natural: the value
\widehat{S}
output by the algorithm can be used for problems like

Example

1.1
to predict the resources required for a task depending on support size. The
algorithm will tell us either:

1.

If
\widehat{S}<n
(which would be the case if we were promised, say,
|\supp(p)|<n/2
),
then
\widehat{S}
units (jars) suffice to cover the distribution up to the removal of
\varepsilon

mass, and fewer than
\approx\widehat{S}
units will fail to cover the whole distribution.
Furthermore, we obtain this estimate using fewer samples than required to learn the histogram.

2.

If
\widehat{S}\geq n
, then we have learned that we need more than
\approx\widehat{S}
units
to cover the distribution.

Let us translate this result into an answer for
Question

1.4
. For a fixed sample size

m
, the naïve approach, taking
\widehat{S}
to be the number of unique elements appearing in the
sample, produces a lower bound
\widehat{S}\leq|\supp(p)|
that exceeds
\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),\Theta(\varepsilon m)\}
(see
Proposition

2.1
). The histogram learners (if they were proved
to succeed on this task without an assumption on true support size) produce a lower bound
\widehat{S}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\supp(p)|
that exceeds
\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}m\log m)\}
.
Our algorithm improves this lower bound up to
\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),\Theta(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}m\log m)\}
.

1.3
Testing vs. Learning Boolean Functions

Testing support size of distributions is also important for understanding testing vs. learning of
Boolean functions. Arguably the most well understood model of learning is the distribution-free
sample-based PAC model: The algorithm receives samples of the form
(x,f(x))
where
x\sim p
is
drawn from an unknown distribution, and labeled by an unknown function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, which is
promised to belong to a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
. The algorithm should output
a function
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim p}[f(x)\neq g(x)]\leq\varepsilon
. (Requiring

g\in\mathcal{H}
is called
proper
PAC learning.)

Whereas the PAC learner assumes the input
f
belongs to the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, a
tester

for
\mathcal{H}
is an algorithm which tests this assumption:

Definition 1.7

(Distribution-free sample-based testing; see formal
Definition

5.1
)
.

Given labeled samples
(x,f(x))
with
x\sim p
drawn from unknown distribution
p
and labeled by
unknown function
f
, decide whether (1)
f\in\mathcal{H}
or (2)
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from all functions

g\in\mathcal{H}
, meaning
\underset{x\sim p}{\mathbb{P}}\left[f(x)\neq g(x)\right]\geq\varepsilon
.

Testing
\mathcal{H}
can be done by running a proper PAC learner and checking if it worked
[
15
]
.
One motivation for testing algorithms is to aid in model selection, i. e. choosing an appropriate
hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
for learning. To be useful for this, the tester should be more
efficient than the learner. The sample complexity of proper PAC learning is between

\Omega(\mathsf{VC}/\varepsilon)
and
O(\tfrac{\mathsf{VC}}{\varepsilon}\log(1/\varepsilon))
, where
\mathsf{VC}
is the VC
dimension of
\mathcal{H}
(and there are examples where either bound is tight)
[
6
,
23
,
22
]
.
But there is no similar characterization of the sample complexity for
testing
, and it is not
known when it is possible to do better than the testing-by-PAC-learning method.

In fact, very little is known about property testing in
the distribution-free sample-based setting, and there are not many positive results (see e. g.
[
16
,
12
,
31
,
5
,
32
]
). For example, the basic class
\mathcal{H}_{n}
of functions
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
with
|f^{-1}(1)|\leq n
has a tight bound of
\Theta(\mathsf{VC}/\varepsilon)
for proper learning
with samples, but for testing with samples, tight bounds in terms of both
\mathsf{VC}
and
\varepsilon
are
not known
1

If the domain
\mathbb{N}
is replaced with
[Cn]
for constant
C
, and queries are
allowed, then
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})

[

show lower bounds of the form
\Omega(\tfrac{\mathsf{VC}}{\varepsilon\log\mathsf{VC}})
for this class and several others (e. g. halfspaces,

still leaves room for interesting upper bounds: any bound of
o(\mathsf{VC}/\varepsilon)
means that the tester
is doing something significantly different from testing-by-PAC-learning.

Indeed,
[

show an upper bound of
O(\tfrac{\mathsf{VC}}{\varepsilon^{2}\log\mathsf{VC}})
samples for testing

\mathcal{H}_{n}
(with the extra promise that
f^{-1}(1)\subset[2n]
), by learning the
distribution

instead of the

: their algorithm learns the histogram of the underlying
distribution
p
on the subdomain
f^{-1}(1)
, with some adjustments to handle the fact that the
probability mass of
p
inside
f^{-1}(1)
may be small. This shows that the lower bound of

[

from support-size estimation is sometimes tight in terms of the VC dimension, and hints
at a closer connection to distribution testing. We give a simple proof of a tighter and cleaner
relationship – that testing support size of distributions and functions are essentially the same

Theorem 1.8

(Equivalence of testing support size for distributions and functions)
.

Let
m^{\mathsf{DIST}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
be the sample
complexity for testing support size with success probability
\sigma
.
Let
m^{\mathsf{FUN}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
be
the sample complexity for distribution-free sample-based testing
\mathcal{H}_{n}
with success
probability
\sigma
.
Then
\forall

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon,\sigma\in(0,1)
, and
\xi\in(0,1-\sigma)
,

m^{\mathsf{DIST}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)\leq m^{\mathsf{FUN}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)\leq m^{\mathsf{DIST}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma+\xi)+O\left(\tfrac{\log(1/\xi)}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

With
Theorem

1.3
, this improves the best upper bound for testing
\mathcal{H}_{n}
from

O(\tfrac{\mathsf{VC}}{\varepsilon^{2}\log\mathsf{VC}})

[
16
]
to
O(\tfrac{\mathsf{VC}}{\varepsilon\log\mathsf{VC}}\log(1/\varepsilon))
. So, not only can
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be tested more efficiently than learned, but
even more efficiently than the histogram of the
underlying distribution can be learned.

We note also that there are reductions from testing the class
\mathcal{H}_{n}
to testing several other
classes like
k
-alternating functions, halfspaces, and decision trees
[
5

10
]
, which
emphasizes the role of testing support size of distributions to the testing vs. learning question
for Boolean functions.

1.4
Techniques and Open Problems

The first part of our proof closely follows the optimal upper bound of
[
37
]
for the support
size
estimation
problem. In short, if domain element
i
has probability density
p_{i}
and
appears
N_{i}
times in the sample, the estimator of
[
37
]
outputs
\sum_{i}f(N_{i})
, where

f(N_{i})
is constructed such that
\mathbb{E}\left[f(\bm{N}_{i})\right]=Q(p_{i})
where
Q(p_{i})
is a function satisfying

Q(p_{i})\approx\mathds{1}\left[p_{i}>0\right]
. If this can be done, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i}f(\bm{N}_{i})\right]=\sum_{i}Q(p_{i})\approx|\supp(p)|.

They make the necessary assumption that every nonzero density satisfies
p_{i}>1/n
, allowing them
to use Chebyshev polynomials to define a suitable
Q
where
Q(p_{i})\approx\mathds{1}\left[p_{i}>1/n\right]
.

Section

2
explains why Chebyshev polynomials arise naturally.

We follow the same strategy, although we no longer have any assumption on the nonzero densities. We
construct a Chebyshev polynomial approximation
Q(p_{i})\approx\mathds{1}\left[p_{i}>\ell\right]
for carefully
chosen
\ell
(depending on
\varepsilon
and
n
). The main challenge is to account for the densities

0<p_{i}<\ell
, where the Chebyshev polynomial is not “designed” to be a good approximation, i. e.
Q(p_{i})\not\approx\mathds{1}\left[p_{i}>0\right]
.

Main idea:

We establish a tradeoff between the number of “heavy elements” with
p_{i}>\ell
(on which the polynomial approximation satisfies
Q(p_{i})\approx 1
), and the value of the
estimator on the “light elements” with
p_{i}\leq\ell
. This is proved in

Section

3.6
, with an easier version giving a worse bound in

Section

3.5
. The easier version uses concavity of
Q
on
(0,\ell)

to show
Q(p_{i})\gtrsim p_{i}/\ell
(
Proposition

3.19
). In this case, we use
\ell\approx\varepsilon/n
. This gives us a tradeoff of the form

\sum_{i}Q(p_{i})\approx\#\{\text{heavy elements}\}+\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\sum_{\text{light }i}p_{i}.

(1)

Then, what remains to show is that, when
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from
|\supp(p)|\leq n
, this
quantity is large (and then bound the variance of the estimator, which depends on the degree of the
polynomial approximation). To get a better bound on the sample size and prove our main theorem, we
use a different setting of parameters in our polynomial approximation, and we establish a more
complicated tradeoff than
Equation

1
(see
Proposition

3.35
), essentially

\sum_{i}Q(p_{i})\geq\left(n+\frac{\varepsilon}{p^{*}}\right)Q(p^{*}),

(2)

where
p^{*}
is the
n^{th}
largest density and
\varepsilon
is the distance to
|\supp(p)|\leq n
,
and we characterize the worst-case behaviour of this tradeoff.

Open questions.

The sample complexity upper bound from
Theorem

1.3
leaves a small gap compared to the best
known lower bound of
\Omega(\tfrac{n}{\varepsilon\log n})
from
[
33
,
37
]
. Can we close
this gap? We remark that our proof strategy does not allow us to improve any of our parameters (see

Remark

3.45
), and therefore we suspect that our upper bound is tight, unless
there is a better algorithmic strategy (say, a non-linear estimator).

We may also ask for a
tolerant
tester
[
29
]
, i. e. an algorithm that accepts not only
when
p
has support size at most
n
, but also when
p
is
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-close
to such a
distribution for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
. Equivalently, we wish to distinguish between

\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(p)\leq n
and
\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)>n
. Our tester from
Theorem

1.3
, like
any sample-based tester, enjoys the very small amount of tolerance
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\Theta(1/m(n,\varepsilon))
. Under the promise that the true support size satisfies
|\supp(p)|=O(n)
,
algorithms for learning the histogram
[
33
,
34
,
21
,
24
]
imply an upper bound of

O\left(\tfrac{n}{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\log n}\right)
for tolerant testing of support size, and
in
Appendix

C
we discuss how the techniques of
[
35
]
may plausibly
allow one to drop the support size assumption at the cost of higher dependence on

\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Can we achieve
\tfrac{n}{\log n}\cdot\widetilde{O}\left(\tfrac{1}{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}}\right)
?

2
Defining a Test Statistic

We begin by defining a “test statistic”
\widehat{S}
which the tester will use to make its
decision – the tester will output
Accept
if
\widehat{S}
is small and output

Reject
if it is large. But our test statistic will require
\log(1/\varepsilon)<\log(n)
, so
we first handle the case where the parameter
\varepsilon
is very small.

2.1
Small
\varepsilon

If
\varepsilon
is small enough that
\log(1/\varepsilon)=\Omega(\log n)
, we will use the following
simple tester. This result is folklore (appearing e. g. in
[
18
,
2
,
3
]
) but we include a
proof for the sake of completeness. We start with the following simple observation:

{observation}

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and probability distribution
p
,
p
is

\varepsilon
-far from having support size at most
n
if and only if
\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)>n
.

Using this observation, we give

Proposition 2.1
.

There is an algorithm which, given inputs
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and sample access to an
arbitrary distribution
p
, draws at most
O(n/\varepsilon)
samples from
p
and outputs a number

\widehat{\bm{S}}
which (with probability at least
3/4
) satisfies

\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),n\}\leq\widehat{\bm{S}}\leq|\supp(p)|\,.

In particular, there is a support size tester using
O(n/\varepsilon)
samples, obtained by
running this algorithm with parameters
n+1,\varepsilon
and outputting
\mathsf{Accept}
if and only if

\widehat{\bm{S}}\leq n
.

Proof 2.2
.

Write
k\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\min\{n,\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)\}
. Let
m\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=10n/\varepsilon
. The algorithm draws

m
independent random samples from
p
and outputs
\widehat{\bm{S}}
, defined as the number
of distinct domain elements in the sample.

It is clear that
\widehat{\bm{S}}\leq|\supp(p)|
with probability 1. To show that
\widehat{\bm{S}}\geq\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),n\}
, suppose we draw an infinite sequence of independent random
samples
\bm{S}=\{\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2},\dotsc\}
from
p
. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, define the random
variable
\bm{t}_{i}
as the smallest index
\bm{t}_{i}\in[m]
such that the multiset
\{\bm{s}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{s}_{\bm{t}_{i}}\}
is supported on
i
unique elements. Note that
\bm{t}_{1}=1
.

Claim 1
.

For all
i\in\{2,\dotsc,k\}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{t}_{i}-\bm{t}_{i-1}\right]\leq 1/\varepsilon
.

Proof 2.3

(Proof of claim)
.

Write
\bm{S}_{t}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{s}_{t}\}
as the prefix of
\bm{S}
up to element

t
. Fix any
t_{i-1}
. Conditional on the event
\bm{t}_{i-1}=t_{i-1}
, the multiset

\bm{S}_{t_{i-1}}=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{s}_{t_{i-1}}\}
is supported on
i-1<k
elements. Then

p(\supp(\bm{S}_{t_{i-1}}))<1-\varepsilon
since
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from having support size at
most
k-1
by
Section

2.1
.
Therefore for every
t>t_{i-1}
we have
\underset{}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\bm{s}_{t}\not\in\bm{S}_{t_{i-1}}\;\;\mid\;\;\bm{t}_{i-1}=t_{i-1}\right]>\varepsilon
, so
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{t}_{i}-\bm{t}_{i-1}\right]\leq 1/\varepsilon
.

From this claim we may deduce
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{t}_{k}\right]\leq\bm{t}_{1}+(k-1)/\varepsilon\leq n/\varepsilon
,
so by Markov’s inequality

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{t}_{k}>10n/\varepsilon\right]\leq 1/10\,.

Therefore if
m\geq 10n/\varepsilon
the tester will see at least
k
unique elements in a sample of
size
m
, with probability at least
9/10
.

2.2
The test statistic

We now turn to the case where
\varepsilon
is large. Specifically, we fix a small universal constant

a\in(0,1)
(e. g.
a=1/128
suffices; we prioritize clarity of exposition over optimizing
constants) and assume:

{assumption}

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume
n^{-a}<\varepsilon<1/3
,
so that
\log(1/\varepsilon)<a\log n
.

We will write
\bm{S}\subset\mathbb{N}
for the (random) multiset of samples received by the algorithm.

Definition 2.4

(Sample Histogram)
.

For a fixed multiset
S\subset\mathbb{N}
, the
sample histogram
is the sequence
N_{i}
where
N_{i}

is the number of times
i\in\mathbb{N}
appears in
S
. If
\bm{S}
is a random multiset then we write

\bm{N}_{i}
.

The goal will be to find the best function
f
to plug in to the following definition of our test
statistic; we will show how to choose
f
in
Section

2.3
and the remainder of the
paper, but for now we leave it undetermined.

Definition 2.5

(Test statistic)
.

For a given function
f:\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
(which is required to satisfy
f(0)=-1
),
we define a test statistic as follows. On random sample
\bm{S}
,

\bm{\widehat{S}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(1+f(\bm{N_{i}}))\,.

Given an appropriate function
f
and resulting test statistic, we define our support-size tester:

Definition 2.6

(Support-Size Tester)
.

Given parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
our tester chooses
m
independent samples

\bm{S}
from the distribution
p
, and outputs
Accept
if and only if

\bm{\widehat{S}}<(1+\varepsilon/2)n\,.

Remark 2.7
.

Our test statistic is a
linear estimator
(see e. g.
[
33
,
36
,
34
,
37
]
) because
it can be written as

\widehat{\bm{S}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{F_{j}}\cdot(1+f(j))\,,

where
\bm{F_{j}}
is the number of elements
i\in\mathbb{N}
which occur
j
times in the sample
\bm{S}
,
i. e.
\bm{N_{i}}=j
. The sequence
\bm{F_{1}},\bm{F_{2}},\dotsc
is known as the
fingerprint
.

A standard trick to ease the analysis is to consider instead the
Poissonized
version of the
algorithm.

Definition 2.8

(Poissonized Support-Size Tester)
.

Given parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
our tester chooses
\bm{m}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m)
and
then takes
\bm{m}
independent samples
\bm{S}
from the distribution
p
, and outputs

Accept
if and only if

\bm{\widehat{S}}<(1+\varepsilon/2)n\,.

The advantages of Poissonization are these (see e. g. the survey
[
8
]
):

Fact 2
.

Let
\bm{S}
be the sample of size
\bm{m}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(m)
drawn by the Poissonized tester.
Then the random variables
\bm{N_{i}}
are mutually independent and are distributed as

\bm{N_{i}}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(mp_{i})\,.

Fact 3
.

If there is a Poissonized support-size tester with sample complexity
m(n,\varepsilon)
and success
probability
\sigma
, then there is a standard support-size tester with sample complexity at
most
O(m(n,\varepsilon))
and success probability
0.99\sigma
.

2.3
Choosing a function
f

Now we see how to choose the function
f
to complete
Definition

2.5
. To motivate the
choice, compute the expected value of the statistic:

Proposition 2.9
.

For any given finitely supported
f\colon\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=-1
and parameter

m
, the (Poissonized) test statistic
\widehat{\bm{S}}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(1+e^{-mp_{i}}P(p_{i}))

(3)

where
P(x)
is the polynomial

P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f(j)\frac{m^{j}}{j!}x^{j}\,.

Proof 2.10
.

Using
Fact

2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\widehat{S}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[1+f(\bm{N_{i}})\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left(1+\sum_{j\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{N}_{i}=j\right]f(j)\right)=\sum_{i}\left(1+\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-mp_{i}}(mp_{i})^{j}}{j!}f(j)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(1+e^{-mp_{i}}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{m^{j}f(j)}{j!}p_{i}^{j}\right)=\sum_{i}\left(1+e^{-mp_{i}}P(p_{i})\right).

For convenience, we define

Definition 2.11
.

For given polynomial
P(x)
, define

Q(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1+e^{-mx}P(x)\,,

so that
Q(p_{i})
quantifies the contribution of element
i\in\mathbb{N}

to the expected value of
\bm{\widehat{S}}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\widehat{S}}\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}Q(p_{i})\,.

(4)

If we could set
Q(x)=\mathds{1}\left[x>0\right]
then
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\widehat{S}}\right]=|\supp(p)|
. But this
means
P(0)=-1
while
P(x)=0
for all
x>0
, which is not a polynomial.
Our goal is therefore to choose a polynomial
P
which approximates this function as closely as
possible.

Specifically, if we choose any coefficients
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d}
, we may then define

f(j)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}a_{j}\frac{j!}{m^{j}}&\text{ if }j\in[d]\\
-1&\text{ if }j=0\\
0&\text{ if }j>d\end{cases}

to obtain the degree-
d
polynomial

P_{d}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f(j)\frac{m^{j}}{j!}x^{j}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}x^{j}\right)-1\,,

which satisfies the necessary condition
P_{d}(0)=-1
. The other condition that we want
P_{d}(x)
to
satisfy is that
P_{d}(x)\approx 0
for
x>0
. This cannot be achieved by a low-degree polynomial,
but we can instead ask for
P_{d}(x)\approx 0
inside a chosen “safe interval”
[\ell,r]
. In other words,
we want a polynomial which satisfies
P_{d}(0)=-1
and

\max_{x\in[\ell,r]}|P_{d}(x)|\leq\delta\,,

for some small
\delta
. The lowest-degree polynomials which satisfy these conditions are known
as the (shifted and scaled)
Chebyshev polynomials
, which we define in

Section

2.4
.

Remark 2.12
.

In the support-size
estimation
problem, there is a lower bound
p_{i}\geq 1/n
on the
nonzero densities, so
[
37
]
choose a “safe interval”
[\ell,r]
with
\ell=1/n
,
so that there is no density
p_{i}
to the left of the interval,
p_{i}\in(0,\ell)
.
In our problem, we do not have this guarantee, and therefore we must handle values
p_{i}
that do not
fall in the “safe” interval
[\ell,r]
.

2.4
Chebyshev polynomials and the definition of
P_{d}

For a given degree
d
, the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{d}(x)
is the polynomial which grows fastest on

x>1
while satisfying
|T_{d}(x)|\leq 1
in the interval
x\in[-1,1]
.

Definition 2.13

(Chebyshev polynomials)
.

We write
T_{d}(x)
for the degree-
d
Chebyshev polynomial. These can be defined recursively as

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=x

\displaystyle T_{d}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=2x\cdot T_{d-1}(x)-T_{d-2}(x)\,.

T_{d}(x)
may also be computed via the following closed-form formula:

For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

T_{d}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)^{d}+\left(x-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\right)^{d}\right)\,.

We want the polynomials
P_{d}(x)
to be bounded in
[\ell,r]
and to
grow fast on
x\in[0,\ell)
so that
P_{d}(0)=-1
. So we define a map
\psi
which translates

[\ell,r]
to
[-1,1]
,

\psi(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\frac{2x-r-\ell}{r-\ell}

so
\psi(0)=\frac{r+\ell}{r-\ell}
. For convenience we write

\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\ell}{r}

so that
\psi(0)=1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. We now define

Definition 2.14

(Shifted & Scaled Chebyshev Polynomials)
.

For any
d
, define

P_{d}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(x))\,,

where
\delta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{T_{d}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)}
.

This way, as required,

P_{d}(0)=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(0))=-1\,,\;\text{ and }\;|P_{d}(x)|\leq\delta\text{ for }x\in[\ell,r]\,.

We illustrate the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{d}(x)
and the shifted and scaled result
Q(p_{i})

in
Figure

1
. The resulting function values
f(\bm{N_{i}})
which define our test
statistic are given in
Proposition

A.6
.

(a)

T_{11}(x),x\in[-1.01,1.01]
.

(b)

Q(p_{i})=1+e^{-mp_{i}}P_{11}(p_{i})
.

Figure 1
:
The polynomial
T_{11}(x)
and the resulting
Q(p_{i})=1+e^{-mp_{i}}P_{d}(p_{i})

for
d=11
and certain choices of
\ell,r,n,m
. The ‘safe’ interval
[\ell,r]
is between
the two vertical lines in
Figure

1(b)
. See that
Q(p_{i})
is an approximation of the
‘idealized’ function
Q(p_{i})=\mathds{1}\left[p_{i}>0\right]
.

3
Choosing Parameters

Our goal is now:

{goal}

Choose degree
d
and “safe interval”
[\ell,r]
such that the (Poissonized) support-size tester
in
Definition

2.8
, instantiated with the (shifted and scaled) Chebyshev polynomial
P_{d}
,
works with the smallest sample size parameter
m
, even though densities
p_{i}
may not belong to

[\ell,r]
.

To do this, we will set up a series of constraints that these parameters must satisfy in order for
the tester to succeed, and then minimize
m
with respect to these parameters.

3.1
Constraint I: Choose
d
such that
\delta\leq\varepsilon

We will want that
\delta\leq\varepsilon
so that for every density
p_{i}
in the “safe interval”

[\ell,r]
we have
Q(p_{i})\approx 1
, specifically
|Q(p_{i})-1|\leq\varepsilon
. We will write this
constraint in terms of
\ell
and
r
:

Constraint I
.

For constant
C_{d}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=4\ln(2)
, we will require for any choice of
\ell,r,\varepsilon

that
r\geq 3\ell
and that the degree
d
satisfies

d\geq C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{r-\ell}{2\ell}}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume
Constraint

I
. Then
\delta\leq\varepsilon/20
and for all
x\in[\ell,r]
,

|Q(x)-1|\leq\delta\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{20}\,.

To prove this, we require a lower bound on the growth rate of the Chebyshev polynomials. This is well
known but we provide a proof for the sake of completeness:

Proposition 3.2
.

There is a universal constant
c\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}
such that, for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and

\gamma\in[0,1]
,

T_{d}(1+\gamma)\geq 2^{c\cdot d\sqrt{\gamma}-1}\,.

Proof 3.3
.

Using
Definition

2.13
, we have

\displaystyle T_{d}(1+\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left((1+\gamma)+\sqrt{(1+\gamma)^{2}-1}\right)^{d}+\left((1+\gamma)-\sqrt{(1+\gamma)^{2}-1}\right)^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{2\gamma}\right)^{d}=\frac{1}{2}e^{d\ln(1+\sqrt{2\gamma})}\,.

Using the inequality
\ln(1+x)\geq\frac{x}{1+x}
, which is valid for
x>-1
, together with
the assumption that
\gamma\leq 1
, we obtain

T_{d}(1+\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{d\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{1+\sqrt{2\gamma}}}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{d\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{2\sqrt{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{1}{2}d\sqrt{\gamma}}\,.

Proof 3.4

(Proof of Proposition
3.1
)
.

Recall
\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\ell/r
and
\psi(0)=1+\tfrac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.
It suffices to show

T_{d}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\geq\frac{20}{\varepsilon}

so that, for
x\in[\ell,r]
(which satisfies
\psi(x)\in[-1,1]
and therefore
T_{d}(\psi(x))\in[-1,1]
),

P_{d}(x)=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(x))=-\frac{T_{d}(\psi(x))}{T_{d}(\psi(0))}

is within the interval
[-\varepsilon/20,\varepsilon/20]
.
By
Constraint

I
,
\tfrac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\leq 1
since
\alpha\leq 1/3
, so
we may apply
Proposition

3.2
to get

T_{d}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\geq 2^{c\cdot d\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}-1}\,,

where
c=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)}
is the constant from
Proposition

3.2
. Therefore when

d\geq\frac{2}{c}\sqrt{\frac{r-\ell}{2\ell}}\log(20/\varepsilon)=\frac{2}{c}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\log(20/\varepsilon)\geq\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}(1+\log(20/\varepsilon))
we have the result
with
C_{d}=\frac{2}{c}=4\ln(2)
.

3.2
Constraint II: Choose
m
such that
Q(p_{i})\approx 1
when
p_{i}>r

Next we need to choose large enough
m
such that the term
e^{-mp_{i}}
cancels out the growth of

P_{d}(p_{i})
to the right of the “safe interval”​, leading to the desired
Q(p_{i})=1+e^{-mp_{i}}P_{d}(p_{i})\approx 1
in this case. (
Figure

2
shows
1+P_{d}(p_{i})
without the

e^{-mp_{i}}
term.)

Figure 2
:
Untamed right tail.

We place the following constraint:

Constraint II
.

We require
m\geq 5.5\cdot d/(r-\ell)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume
Constraint

II
. Then

\forall x\in(r,1]\;\colon\;|1-Q(x)|\leq\delta\,.

Proof 3.6
.

Write
\gamma=2\frac{x-r}{r-\ell}
so that

-\psi(x)=\frac{2x-r-\ell}{r-\ell}=\frac{r-\ell-2r+2x}{r-\ell}=1+\frac{2x-2r}{r-\ell}=1+\gamma\,.

Note that
|T_{d}(z)|=|T_{d}(-z)|
for all
z
. We need to bound
|1-Q(x)|=e^{-mx}|P_{d}(x)|=\delta e^{-mx}|T_{d}(\psi(x))|
for
x>r
, and
|T_{d}(\psi(x))|=T_{d}(-\psi(x))=T_{d}(1+\gamma)
. So we require

e^{mx}\geq T_{d}(1+\gamma)
. Bound
T_{d}(1+\gamma)
by

\displaystyle T_{d}(1+\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((1+\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})^{d}+(1+\gamma-\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})^{d}\leq e^{d(\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})}\,.

If
\gamma<1
then
\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}<1+\sqrt{3}
, and therefore since

\frac{x}{r-\ell}>\frac{x}{r}>1
we have

d(\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})\leq d(1+\sqrt{3})\leq d(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{x}{r-\ell}\leq mx\,.

If
\gamma\geq 1
then
\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}\leq(1+\sqrt{3})\gamma
, so

d(\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})\leq d(1+\sqrt{3})\cdot 2\frac{x-r}{r-\ell}\leq d(1+\sqrt{3})\cdot 2\frac{x}{r-\ell}\leq mx\,.

We then conclude

T_{d}(1+\gamma)\leq e^{d(\gamma+\sqrt{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}})}\leq e^{mx}.

3.3
Constraint III: Variance

We will need the test statistic
\widehat{\bm{S}}
to satisfy two conditions.
First, when
|\supp(p)|\leq n
we need

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}>(1+\varepsilon/2)n\right]\leq 1/4\,.

Second, when
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from having support size
n
, we need

\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}<(1+\varepsilon/2)n\right]\leq 1/4\,.

We will bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}\right]
in each of these cases in
Section

3.4
. But first we
will bound the variance. We impose the following constraint:

Constraint III
.

We require
m\leq\tfrac{1}{4^{4}}\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}
,
and
d^{6}9^{d}\left(\frac{r+\ell}{r-\ell}\right)^{2d-2}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}m(r-\ell)^{2}n^{2}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume
Constraints

I
,
II
and
III
. Then

\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}\frac{n^{2}}{4^{3}}
,
and therefore (by Chebyshev’s inequality),

\mathbb{P}\left[|\widehat{\bm{S}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}\right]|>\frac{\varepsilon}{4}n\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}\,.

To prove
Proposition

3.7
, we will require bounds on the values of
f(N_{i})
in the definition of

\widehat{\bm{S}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(1+f(\bm{N_{i}}))\,.

For this we do some tedious calculations using the coefficients of the polynomial
P_{d}(x)
. We put
these in
Appendix

A
. The result is:

Proposition 3.8
.

For any
d
and any
k\in[d]
,

|f(k)|\leq\delta\cdot d^{2}\cdot 3^{d}\cdot\left(\frac{2d}{m(r-\ell)}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{r+\ell}{r-\ell}\right)^{d-k}\,.

We may now estimate the variance.

Proof 3.9

(Proof of Proposition
3.7
)
.

Recall that each
\bm{N}_{i}
is an independent Poisson random variable
\bm{N}_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(mp_{i})
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\bm{\widehat{S}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{Var}\left[1+f(\bm{N_{i}})\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{Var}\left[(1+f(\bm{N_{i}}))\mathds{1}\left[\bm{N}_{i}>0\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+f(\bm{N_{i}}))^{2}\mathds{1}\left[\bm{N}_{i}>0\right]\right]\leq\max_{j\in[d]}(1+f(j))^{2}\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\left[\bm{N}_{i}>0\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[d]}(1+f(j))^{2}\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(1-e^{-mp_{i}})\leq\max_{j\in[d]}(1+f(j))^{2}\cdot m\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i}=m\cdot\max_{j\in[d]}(1+f(j))^{2}\,.

From
Constraint

II
, which implies
m(r-\ell)>2d
, the upper bound in

Proposition

3.8
is maximized at
k=1
, so we have

\forall j\in[d]\;:\;|f(j)|\leq\delta\cdot d^{2}\cdot 3^{d}\cdot\frac{2d}{m(r-\ell)}\cdot\left(\frac{r+\ell}{r-\ell}\right)^{d-1}\,.

If this is at most 1, then the first part of
Constraint

III
gives the desired bound.

\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\bm{S}}\right]\leq 4m\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{4^{3}}

Otherwise,
(1+|f(j)|)^{2}\leq 4f(j)^{2}
, so (using
Constraint

I
and
Proposition

3.1
to
ensure

Assume

Proof 3.11

First note that

We also have

Remark 3.12

Fact 4
.

For each

Corollary 3.15
.

For each

is convex increasing in

.

Proof 3.16
.

Since
T_{d}(x)
has a positive leading coefficient, all of its first

. Moreover, all of its derivatives have

Corollary 3.17
.

P_{d}
is concave increasing on
[0,\ell]
.

Proof 3.18
.

This follows from the

3.15

P_{d}=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(x))

.

Proposition 3.19
.

For each

(1-\delta)\frac{x}{\ell}\leq Q(x)\leq 1

Proof 3.20
.

Recall that
P_{d}(0)=-1
by construction. It is also immediate to check that

Thus for any

we have

and, on the other hand,

Q(x)=1+e^{-mx}P_{d}(x)\geq 1+P_{d}\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\cdot\ell+\left(1-\frac{x}{\ell}\right)\cdot 0\right)\geq 1+\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\cdot(-\delta)+\left(1-\frac{x}{\ell}\right)\cdot(-1)\right)=(1-\delta)\frac{x}{\ell}\,.

Lemma 3.22

(Refinement of

Assume

I
,
II
and

. For any distribution

By

This establishes (

is
\varepsilon
-far from being

elements. We consider two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose

. Then, using

Suppose

, since otherwise

would
be
\varepsilon

We first sketch some rough calculations to motivate the asymptotic dependence that each of

,

and

and
\varepsilon
. Combining

Plugging back into (

(7)

Proof 3.29

•

.

•

d\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lceil C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{r-\ell}{2\ell}}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)\rceil
, recalling that

.

•

.

We claim that

,

,

and

Let us now verify

. Start with a calculation of

, which also gives the claimed bound of

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{r-\ell}{2\ell}}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)}{r-\ell}=\frac{2C_{d}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{2\ell(r-\ell)}}\leq\frac{2C_{d}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{2\ell\cdot r/2}}=\frac{2C_{d}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\cdot C_{\ell}\cdot C_{r}\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\frac{\log^{2}n}{\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle d-1

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{r-\ell}{2\ell}}\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C_{d}}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{r}{\ell}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C_{d}}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{C_{r}}{C_{\ell}}}\frac{\log(n)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot\log\left(\frac{20}{\varepsilon}\right)

Some notation.

\gamma=\gamma(\lambda)=\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)

To simplify the analysis, we will define and analyze an auxiliary function

that lower bounds

Recall

Q^{*}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}1+P_{d}(x)&\text{ if }x<\ell\\

Proposition 3.33

(Properties of

)
.

Q^{*}
is continuous, concave and non-decreasing on
[0,1]
, and for all

,

. Furthermore, if

I
and
II

for all

Proof 3.34
.

For

,

is concave and increasing
(

), so
Q^{*}(x)=1+P_{d}(x)
is also concave increasing. To ensure continuity,

at

. Observe that

P_{d}(\ell)=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(\ell))=-\delta T_{d}(1)=-\delta\,,

so
Q^{*}(\ell)=1-\delta=1+P_{d}(\ell)
, which makes

continuous. Then since

is concave increasing on

, and constant

on

, it is concave and non-decreasing on

.

Now we show

. If
x\in(0,\ell)
, write
x=\lambda\ell
so that
\psi(x)=1+\gamma
. Then

P_{d}(x)=-\delta T_{d}(\psi(x))=-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)\,,

so

for
\gamma\geq 0
. Since

Q(x)=1+e^{-mx}P_{d}(x)\geq 1+P_{d}(x)=Q^{*}(x)\,.

If

and
II

, by

and

{assumption}

3.6.1
Worst-case behaviour and the function
\Phi(\lambda)

We first show that the worst-case behaviour of
Q^{*}
is captured by two variables: the

n^{\text{th}}
-largest density
p_{n}
, and the total mass of elements lighter than
p_{n}
. Informally,
the worst-case distributions for our test statistic are those concentrating essentially all of their
mass at
p_{n}
.

Proposition 3.35
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a (sorted) distribution with
p_{n}>0
. Let
\mu\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{i>n}p_{i}
. Then

\sum_{i}Q^{*}(p_{i})\geq\left(n+\frac{\mu}{p_{n}}\right)Q^{*}(p_{n})\,.

Proof 3.36
.

Recall that
Q^{*}
is concave and non-decreasing by
Proposition

3.33
. Since
Q^{*}
is
non-decreasing and
p_{i}\geq p_{n}
for any
i\leq n
, we have
Q^{*}(p_{i})\geq Q^{*}(p_{n})
for any
i\leq n
. On the other hand, for any
i>n
, we have
p_{i}\leq p_{n}
and then, using the concavity
of
Q^{*}
,

Q^{*}(p_{i})=Q^{*}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\cdot p_{n}+\left(1-\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\right)\cdot 0\right)\geq\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\cdot Q^{*}(p_{n})+\left(1-\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\right)\cdot Q^{*}(0)=\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\cdot Q^{*}(p_{n})\,,

the last equality since
Q^{*}(0)=1+P_{d}(0)=0
. We conclude that

\sum_{i}Q^{*}(p_{i})\geq\sum_{i\leq n}Q^{*}(p_{n})+\sum_{i>n}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{n}}\cdot Q^{*}(p_{n})=\left(n+\frac{\mu}{p_{n}}\right)Q^{*}(p_{n})\,.

We can now transform the problem of lower bounding
\sum_{i}Q^{*}(p_{i})
into the problem of lower
bounding another function
\Phi(\lambda)
defined as:

Definition 3.37
.

For
\lambda\in(0,1]

\Phi(\lambda)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda\ell n}\right)Q^{*}(\lambda\ell)\,.

We will show a lower bound of
\Phi(\lambda)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4
below, in

Section

3.6.2
. Here we show why it works:

Lemma 3.38
.

Assume
Constraint

I
. Suppose
\Phi(\lambda)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then for any
k\leq n
and any
p
that is
\varepsilon
-far from having

\sum_{i}Q^{*}(p_{i})>(1+3\varepsilon/4)k\,.

Proof 3.39
.

Since

is sorted and
\varepsilon

, we have

and

\sum_{i>k}p_{i}>\varepsilon
. We also have

since

distribution. We consider two cases:

and

First, suppose

\sum_{i}Q^{*}(p_{i})>\left(k+\frac{\varepsilon}{1/k}\right)(1-\delta)=(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)k\geq(1+3\varepsilon/4)k\,,

by

The case

Proposition 3.40
.

Assume

I
and

. Then for all

Proof 3.41
.

Note that, by

3.1

To reason about

Proposition 3.42
.

For any

and

We may now complete Step 1.

Proposition 3.43
.

Assume
Constraints

I
and
IVb
. Then the limit
\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)
exists, is finite, and satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)\geq 2\,.

(10)

Proof 3.44
.

Recall from
Equation

9
that
Q^{*}(\lambda\ell)=1+P_{d}(\lambda\ell)=1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda\ell n}\right)\left(1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\left[1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)\right]+\frac{\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\left[\frac{1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)}{\lambda}\right]\,.

Since
\gamma=\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)
, and by the definition of
\delta
, the
first limit evaluates to

1-\delta T_{d}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)=1-\delta\cdot\frac{1}{\delta}=0\,.

As for the second limit, note that both the numerator (for the same reason) and denominator go
to
0
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
. Applying L’Hôpital’s rule,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\frac{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}(1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma))}{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}\lambda}=-\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}T_{d}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}T_{d}^{\prime}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)\right)=\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot T_{d}^{\prime}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\,,

where the last equality holds since

is a polynomial and hence continuous, and this
also establishes the existence and finiteness of the limit. Using
Proposition

3.42
and the

\delta
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{\ell n}\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{d}{\sqrt{3\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\delta\varepsilon d}{\ell n\sqrt{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\delta}-1\right)\,.

Since
\delta\leq 1/2
by

3.1
, we have
\frac{1}{\delta}-1\geq\frac{1}{2\delta}
. Using
Constraints

I
and
IVb
and the definition

\alpha=\ell/r
, we obtain

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{n\sqrt{3}}\cdot\frac{n}{C_{\ell}\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{2\delta}\geq 2\,.

Remark 3.45
.

The last step in the above proof shows that
Constraint

IVb
is the best possible
relaxation of our constraint on

, then we would only have obtained a

lower bound on

lower bound.

Thanks to
Proposition

3.43
, we hereafter consider the continuous extension
\Phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, i. e. we define
\Phi(0)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\Phi(\lambda)
.

The next step is to show that
\Phi
satisfies a certain differential inequality, which will help us
conclude that
\Phi(\lambda)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4
at any critical points where
\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
.
It is convenient to define
L\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\tfrac{\ell n}{\varepsilon}
, so that

\Phi(\lambda)=\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)(1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma))\,.

Lemma 3.46
.

Assume
Constraint

I
. Define
A\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}}\cdot C_{d}\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
. Then for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq-\Phi(\lambda)\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\right)+(1-\delta)A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\,.

Proof 3.47
.

Recall that
\gamma=\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)
so
\tfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}\gamma=-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\left(1-\delta T_{d}\left(1+\gamma\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{L\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\delta T_{d}\left(1+\gamma\right)\right)+\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot(-\delta)\cdot T_{d}^{\prime}\left(1+\gamma\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{1}{L\lambda^{2}}}{1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}}\Phi(\lambda)+\delta\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot T_{d}^{\prime}(1+\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+\delta\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot T_{d}^{\prime}(1+\gamma)\,.

Using
Propositions

3.42
and
I
and recalling that
T_{d}(1+\gamma)\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+\delta\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot\frac{d}{\sqrt{3\cdot\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\lambda)}}\left(T_{d}(1+\gamma)-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+\delta\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot\frac{C_{d}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{3}}\left(T_{d}(1+\gamma)-1\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+\delta\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot\frac{C_{d}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\sqrt{3}}\left(T_{d}(1+\gamma)-1\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\cdot\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)-\delta A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\,.

Now, we may use the definition of
\Phi
to rewrite the middle term:

\Phi(\lambda)=\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\left(1-\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)\right)\implies\delta T_{d}(1+\gamma)=1-\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}}\,.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\left(1-\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}}\right)-\delta A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\Phi(\lambda)+A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}-\Phi(\lambda)\right)-\delta A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Phi(\lambda)\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\right)+(1-\delta)A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\,.

We may now conclude the argument.

Lemma 3.48
.

Assume
Constraints

I
and
IVb
. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\leq 1/3
,

\forall\lambda\in[0,1]\;:\;\Phi(\lambda)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4\,.

Proof 3.49
.

Note that
Propositions

3.40
and
3.43
already give the result for
\lambda=0
and

\lambda=1
, and moreover
\Phi
is continuous on
[0,1]
(by

Proposition

3.33
) and differentiable in
(0,1)
(by the differentiability of
Q^{*}
in

(0,\ell)
).
Thus it remains
to check points
\lambda\in(0,1)
for which
\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
, if any exist. Suppose

\lambda\in(0,1)
satisfies
\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
. Then
Lemma

3.46

implies that

0=\Phi^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq-\Phi(\lambda)\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\right)+(1-\delta)A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)\,,

where again
A\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot C_{d}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
.
Rearranging, we obtain

\Phi(\lambda)\geq\frac{(1-\delta)A\left(1+\frac{1}{L\lambda}\right)}{\left(A+\frac{1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\right)}=\frac{(1-\delta)A\left(\frac{L\lambda+1}{L\lambda}\right)}{\left(\frac{A\lambda(L\lambda+1)+1}{\lambda(L\lambda+1)}\right)}=\frac{(1-\delta)(AL^{2}\lambda^{2}+2AL\lambda+A)}{AL^{2}\lambda^{2}+AL\lambda+L}\,.

Take
K\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=A/L\geq\tfrac{C_{d}}{\sqrt{3}C_{\ell}}
which satisfies
K\geq 4

under
Constraint

. Then the right-hand side is

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\left(1+\frac{KL^{2}\lambda+(K-1)L}{KL^{3}\lambda^{2}+KL^{2}\lambda+L}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\left(1+\frac{KL\lambda+(K-1)}{KL^{2}\lambda^{2}+KL\lambda+1}\right)\,.

If

then, by preserving only the term
(K-1)
in the numerator and using the

in the denominator, this is at least

(1-\delta)\left(1+\frac{K-1}{3}\right)\geq(1-\delta)\cdot 2\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4\,,

the last inequality since
\delta\leq\varepsilon/20
by
Proposition

3.1
. Otherwise, by preserving
only the term
KL\lambda
in the numerator and upper bounding each term in the denominator by the
maximum of the three, it is at least

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\left(1+KL\lambda\cdot\min\left\{\frac{1}{3KL^{2}\lambda^{2}},\frac{1}{3KL\lambda},\frac{1}{3}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)\left(1+\min\left\{\frac{1}{3L},\frac{1}{3}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)(1+\varepsilon)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4\,,

where the first inequality holds since
3KL\lambda\geq 3
and
\lambda\leq 1
, and the

by

.

4
An Effective Lower Bound on Support Size

To prove
Corollary

1.6
from the introduction, which we restate here for convenience,
we need to essentially do a binary search for the correct parameters, in order to control the
variance (since the variance depends on the parameter
n
).

Corollary 4.1
.

There exists an algorithm
A
which, given inputs
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/3)
and sample
access to unknown distribution
p
over
\mathbb{N}
, draws
O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon\log n}\cdot\min\left\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log n\right\}\right)
independent samples from
p
and outputs
a number
\widehat{\bm{S}}
which satisfies (with probability at least
3/4
over the samples)

\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),n\}\leq\widehat{\bm{S}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\supp(p)|\,.

Proof 4.2
.

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

For
i=0
to
\log n
, perform the following.

(a)

Set
n_{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=n/2^{i}
and
\delta_{i}=\tfrac{1}{4\cdot 2^{i+1}}
.

(b)

If
n_{i},\varepsilon
do not satisfy
Section

2.2
, use
O(m(n_{i},\varepsilon)\cdot\log(1/\delta_{i}))
samples to obtain an estimate
\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}
satisfying the conditions
of
Proposition

2.1
with probability at least
1-\delta_{i}
(using the median trick to
boost the error). Output
\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}
and terminate.

(c)

Otherwise, if
n_{i},\varepsilon
satisfy
Section

2.2
, use
O(m(n_{i},\varepsilon)\cdot\log(1/\delta_{i}))
samples obtain an estimate of the test statistic
\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}
from

Definition

2.5
, which (again using the median trick) satisfies the conditions of

Lemma

3.24
with probability at least
1-\delta_{i}
. If
\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}\geq n_{i+1}
output
\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}
and terminate. Otherwise continue.

2.

If the algorithm did not terminate in
\log n
steps, then output
1
.

The number of samples used by the algorithm is at most (for some constant
C>0
):

\displaystyle C\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\log n}\frac{n_{i}}{\log(n_{i})}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\min\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log(n_{i})\}

\displaystyle\qquad=C\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=0}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\log n}\frac{n/2^{i}}{\log(n)-i}(i+3)\cdot\min\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log(n)-i\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=1+\tfrac{1}{2}\log n}^{\log n}\frac{n/2^{i}}{\log(n)-i}(i+3)\cdot\min\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log(n)-i\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\cdot\frac{2n}{\varepsilon\log n}\min\{\log(1/\varepsilon),\log(n)\}\sum_{i=0}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\log n}\frac{1}{2^{i}}(i+3)+2C\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}\log n

The probability that any of the estimates

.
Assuming that every

satisfies its corresponding condition, we separately prove

Claim 5
.

The output satisfies
\widehat{\bm{S}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\supp(p)|
.

{subproof}

If the algorithm reaches its last step and outputs

coming from

, then

\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}\leq|\supp(p)|

The remaining case is that the algorithm terminates at some step

by outputting an estimate

satisfying the conditions of

implies that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{S}_{i}}

\displaystyle<(1+\varepsilon/4)|\supp(p)|+\frac{\varepsilon n_{i^{*}}}{4}<(1+\varepsilon/4)\cdot\frac{n_{i^{*}}}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon n_{i^{*}}}{4}

\displaystyle=n_{i^{*}}\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{12}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)=n_{i^{*}}\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\right)<\frac{n_{i^{*}}}{2}=n_{i^{*}+1}\,,

The output satisfies
\widehat{\bm{S}}\geq\min\{\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p),n\}
.

{subproof}

We first consider the edge case where
\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)=1

\widehat{\bm{S}}\geq\mathsf{eff}_{\varepsilon}(p)

\varepsilon

5
Testing Support Size of Functions

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathcal{H}_{n}
for the set of functions
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
which
satisfy
|f^{-1}(1)|\leq n
.
We will refer to pairs
(f,p)
, consisting of a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
and a probability
distribution
p
over
\mathbb{N}
, as
function-distribution
pairs. A function-distribution pair

(f,p)
is
\varepsilon
-far
from
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if

\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{n}\;:\;\underset{\bm{x}\sim p}{\mathbb{P}}\left[f(x)\neq h(x)\right]\geq\varepsilon\,.

For any multiset
S\subset\mathbb{N}
and function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
, we will write
S_{f}
for the
labeled multiset

S_{f}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{(x,f(x))\;\colon\;x\in S\}\,.

Definition 5.1

(Testing Support Size of Functions)
.

A
support-size tester for functions
, with sample complexity

m(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
, is an algorithm
B

which receives as input the parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\sigma\in(0,1)
, and is required to satisfy the following. For any function-distribution pair
(f,p)

consisting of a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
and a probability distribution
p
over
\mathbb{N}
,
if
\bm{S}_{f}
is a labeled sample of size
m=m(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
drawn from
p
, then

1.

If
f\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
then
\mathbb{P}\left[B(\bm{S}_{f})\text{ outputs }\mathsf{Accept}\right]\geq\sigma
; and

2.

If
(f,p)
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{H}_{n}
then
\mathbb{P}\left[B(\bm{S}_{f})\text{ outputs }\mathsf{Reject}\right]\geq\sigma
.

We write
m^{\mathsf{FUN}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
for the optimal sample complexity of a support-size
tester for functions.

We will also write
m^{\mathsf{DIST}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)
for the optimal sample complexity of support-size
testing of distributions, with parameters
n
and
\varepsilon
, and success probability
\sigma

(replacing
3/4
in
Definition

1.2
). The next two propositions establish

Theorem

1.8
from the introduction.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any

and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and any
\sigma\in(0,1)
,

m^{\mathsf{DIST}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)\leq m^{\mathsf{FUN}}(n,\varepsilon,\sigma)\,.

Proof 5.3
.

We reduce testing support size of distributions, to testing support size of functions. Let
B
be a
distribution-free sample-based support-size tester for functions on domain

, with sample

. Then, given
n
and
\varepsilon
, the support-size tester for input

p
is as follows.

1.

Let
\bm{S}
be
m
independent random samples from
p
.

2.

Output

, where the sample

is labeled by the constant function

.

Suppose that

\varepsilon
-far from having
