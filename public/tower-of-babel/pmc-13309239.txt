Seizures and arterial hypertension in a child: A case of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis evolving into posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome
Authors: U. Biswas, A. Sohrab, A.K. Singh, R. Ghosh, J. Benito-León
Published: 2026

Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is an immune complex-mediated disorder of childhood, characterized by oliguria, hematuria, arterial hypertension, and edema following a streptococcal (group B Streptococcus) skin or throat infection. Here, we report a case of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), a rare complication of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis in a pediatric patient.1,2

A 15-year-old boy presented to the emergency department with recurrent seizures, headache, periorbital puffiness for seven days, and fever with dark-colored urine for ten days. He had skin lesions two weeks before symptom onset. There was no history of frothy urine, recent upper respiratory tract infection, joint pain, exertional dyspnea, or exposure to drugs or toxins.

On admission, he was conscious and alert but confused. He was febrile (38.1 °C), with a heart rate of 110/min, blood pressure of 170/100 mmHg, capillary blood glucose of 96 mg/dl, and a respiratory rate of 16/min. Periorbital edema and residual scabs of skin lesions were evident, and urine was cola-colored. Cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital examinations were otherwise unremarkable. Apart from confusion and bilateral extensor plantar responses, the neurological examination was normal.

Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin 11.6 g/dl, total leukocyte count 14,000/dl, and platelet count 94,000/dl. Renal function tests revealed a urea level of 46 mg/dl and a creatinine level of 1.4 mg/dl; electrolytes were normal. Antistreptolysin O titer was 503 IU/ml, and C3 was 25 mg/dl (normal 90–180 mg/dl). Serology for dengue and scrub typhus (IgM and IgG), malaria parasite, and antigens were negative. Urinalysis showed 20–22 red blood cells/hpf, albumin 3+, and 24-h urinary protein of 1.9 g/day. Non-contrast brain computed tomography showed filiform hypodensity with accentuated gray–white differentiation in the left high fronto–parieto–occipital region, suggestive of cerebral edema. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed non-enhancing altered signal intensity in bilateral posterior parieto–occipital, frontal, and insular cortices (Fig. 1). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed 10 cells/dl (predominantly mononuclear), protein 46 mg/dl, glucose 77 mg/dl, and chloride 110 meq/L, with no micro-organisms detected. Herpes simplex and scrub typhus IgM titers were negative. Electroencephalography was normal.

The patient was diagnosed with PRES secondary to accelerated arterial hypertension related to post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. He was treated with intravenous furosemide, oral penicillin G, phenytoin, levetiracetam, amlodipine, and telmisartan. He improved rapidly: sensorium normalized, no further seizures occurred, fever and headache resolved, urine color returned to normal, and renal function tests normalized. Brain MRI repeated after four weeks was normal.

Acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is common after group B streptococcal skin or throat infection, but progression to hypertensive emergency and PRES is rare.1,2 PRES typically presents with seizures, headache, visual disturbances, and altered mental status, features shared with meningitis, encephalitis, or cerebral vasculitis, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.3 Differentiating PRES from these entities is crucial because management differs: antibiotics or antivirals are essential for infectious etiologies, whereas strict blood pressure control is the cornerstone in PRES.3 A preceding streptococcal infection, as in our patient, is an important clue to post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

The pathophysiology of PRES is not fully understood. Proposed mechanisms include hypertension-induced breakdown of cerebral autoregulation, which normally maintains constant cerebral perfusion,3 endothelial dysfunction of the cerebral vasculature and blood–brain barrier, hypoperfusion-related ischemia, and vasogenic edema.4 The posterior circulation is more frequently involved than the anterior circulation, probably because it is less adaptable to acute increases in hydrostatic pressure.5 A distinctive feature of PRES is its radiological and clinical reversibility.6 In our patient, recurrent seizures and headache on a background of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, together with typical MRI findings, confirmed the diagnosis.

In conclusion, PRES in the context of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis requires a high index of suspicion, careful clinical and radiological evaluation, and awareness of overlapping features with other neurological conditions. Early recognition and prompt blood pressure control allow timely, appropriate management and are critical for optimizing patient outcomes.
