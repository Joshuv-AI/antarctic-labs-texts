Assessing nurses’ knowledge and confidence in adult sepsis: evidence from two hospitals in eastern China
Authors: Wang Pei, Ju Xueting, Liu Jiali, Hou Qingyuan, Jiang Guomei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis continues to pose a serious worldwide health issue, with considerable rates of illness and death, making early identification and rapid management essential for improving patient outcomes. Nurses, being the first point of patient care, have a pivotal responsibility in recognizing sepsis early and initiating timely interventions. Nevertheless, even with evidence-based frameworks like Sepsis-3 available, nurses still show knowledge deficiencies, reduced confidence, and inconsistent management practices, which can lead to delays in diagnosis and compromised care. This research set out to evaluate registered nurses’ knowledge and self-rated confidence in identifying and handling adult sepsis, while also examining factors that may influence these skills. An online cross-sectional survey was carried out with 355 registered nurses from Nanjing Brain Hospital and Jiangsu Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine in eastern China. The survey included demographic data, a 15-item multiple-choice knowledge assessment aligned with Sepsis-3 guidelines, a 5-point Likert scale for confidence (range 5–25), and an open-ended item on organizational support needs. The data analysis employed descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, ANOVA, and multiple linear regression. The mean sepsis knowledge score was 10.04 ± 3.17 (out of 15), with notable gaps in recognizing early clinical signs and managing septic shock. Nurses practicing in high-acuity areas such as the ED, ICU, and the Respiratory Infection and Critical Care Unit (RICU) demonstrated significantly higher knowledge and confidence than ward-based nurses (p < .05). Higher education, senior job grade, and clinical exposure were independent predictors of better knowledge (R² = 0.156). The mean confidence score was 17.22 ± 3.62, showing a weak positive correlation with knowledge (r = .213, p = .007). Thematic analysis highlighted strong demand for structured sepsis training and standardized workflows. Significant gaps in sepsis knowledge and confidence persist among nurses, particularly in general wards. Integrating structured education, simulation-based training, and standardized protocols may enhance early recognition and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53878-6.

Introduction

S Sepsis, defined as a dysregulated host response to infection leading to life-threatening organ dysfunction, remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1. It places a substantial burden on healthcare systems because of its high resource utilization, complications, economic costs, and long-term consequences for survivors2–4.

In China, sepsis continues to represent an important public health challenge, accounting for a considerable proportion of deaths nationally and showing an increasing burden in vulnerable populations such as hospitalized patients with cancer5–7.

Early recognition and timely intervention are essential to improve outcomes in sepsis. Nurses play a central role in this process because they are often the first healthcare professionals to assess patients, detect clinical deterioration, and initiate escalation of care8. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) has developed evidence-based guidelines to improve sepsis outcomes, and adherence to sepsis care bundles has been associated with reduced ICU admission, shorter hospital stay, and lower mortality9,10. In contrast, delayed initiation or incomplete implementation of bundle elements is associated with worse outcomes11. However, sepsis remains difficult to identify promptly because its early manifestations are often nonspecific and variable12. Sepsis screening tools and performance improvement initiatives have therefore been introduced to support earlier recognition and management13.

Within this context, nurses occupy a critical frontline role in the early detection of sepsis and the prompt escalation to diagnostic and treatment pathways14. In emergency settings, triage nurses are often the first point of contact for patients with community-acquired sepsis, while bedside nurses in inpatient settings are well positioned to recognize hospital-onset sepsis through ongoing patient observation. Nurse-led screening initiatives have been shown to improve adherence to sepsis care bundles and reduce sepsis-related mortality15. These responsibilities require nurses to possess adequate sepsis knowledge, clinical judgment, and confidence in initiating timely action.

Nurses’ knowledge of sepsis has been examined in several international studies16–19. Overall, these studies have identified important knowledge gaps, particularly in relation to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), recognition of sepsis signs and symptoms, and management protocols. Much of the previous literature has relied mainly on lower-order factual recall questions rather than assessment of applied knowledge in clinically relevant scenarios8,20. In addition, several earlier studies were based on outdated definitions and management approaches that do not reflect current Sepsis-3 guidance16,17,21–23.

The publication of the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) marked an important shift in the conceptualization of sepsis, redefining it as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection24. This replaced the earlier Sepsis-2 framework based on infection plus SIRS criteria. In line with Sepsis-3, the SSC recommends rapid initiation of key management steps, including blood cultures before antibiotics, lactate measurement, intravenous crystalloid resuscitation for hypoperfusion or septic shock, and vasopressor therapy to maintain adequate mean arterial pressure when required25.

In addition to knowledge, a nurse’s self-assurance, conceptualized as perceived capability and competence, has been identified as an important factor influencing the recognition, escalation, and management of sepsis, with particular emphasis in pediatric settings26. However, limited research has examined nurses’ confidence in managing adult sepsis. Therefore, this study aimed to assess registered nurses’ knowledge of adult sepsis and their self-reported confidence in recognizing and managing sepsis in clinical practice, in alignment with Sepsis-3 definitions and current guideline recommendations. Specifically, the study sought to: (1) evaluate nurses’ knowledge of adult sepsis recognition and management; (2) assess nurses’ self-reported confidence in recognizing and managing adult sepsis; and (3) examine demographic, educational, and workplace factors associated with knowledge and confidence. These objectives were intended to clarify the focus of the study and to guide the methods and interpretation of the findings.

Methods

Participants and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted using an anonymous online survey. The study took place at Nanjing Brain Hospital and the Jiangsu Province Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, both of which belong to the same public healthcare cluster in the eastern region of China. Nanjing Brain Hospital, a specialized neurological institution affiliated with Nanjing Medical University, comprises multiple clinical departments and research laboratories, providing advanced care in neurology, neurosurgery, and related brain sciences. In parallel, the Jiangsu Province Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine is a premier Grade-III A facility affiliated with the Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, offering comprehensive services encompassing clinical care, teaching, scientific research, and disease prevention. Together, these two institutions are leading centers of innovation and excellence, combining high-volume patient care with strong academic and research capabilities.

Both hospitals maintain 24-hour urgent care centers (UCCs) where patients with acute or urgent conditions receive prompt attention, whether they arrive on foot or via private ambulance. In routine practice, both hospitals employ the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) system to evaluate patients’ physiological signs and determine the likelihood of clinical deterioration or in-hospital death (Royal College of Physicians, 2017). A NEWS2 score of 5 or above prompts clinicians to respond without delay and initiate an assessment for potential sepsis.

In China, registered nurses (RNs) complete their basic education either through a three-year nursing diploma program or a four-year bachelor’s degree program. Following this, RNs may pursue advanced specialty training via advanced diplomas, graduate diplomas, or postgraduate education. This study employed a convenience sample of RNs, including advanced practice nurses (APNs), who were actively involved in delivering inpatient care within general wards, intensive care units (ICUs), Respiratory Infection and Critical Care Units (RICUs), emergency departments (EDs), and the UCC. RNs working in pediatric departments, operating theatres, ambulatory surgery units, and outpatient clinics were excluded from participation. Eligible participants were identified through the nursing management structure of the two hospitals and invited from the included clinical areas. The survey was distributed to nurses working in these departments through nursing leaders and unit managers. A total of 355 registered nurses completed the survey. Because convenience sampling was used rather than random sampling, the sample may not fully represent the entire nursing workforce of the participating hospitals. However, nurses from a range of departments, job grades, and educational backgrounds were included to capture variation in sepsis-related knowledge and confidence across clinical settings.

Data collection and survey design

Data were obtained over a month-long period starting in early May 2025, following a structured three-phase recruitment process. In the first phase, nursing leaders responsible for research activities within the hospitals received an electronic information package comprising an invitation letter, a digital poster, a survey link, and a QR code for direct access. In the second phase, the invitation was subsequently circulated by these leaders to nursing officers, including nurse managers, nurse clinicians, and APNs, across their respective facilities. In the final phase, nursing officers from inpatient wards and the ED/UCC shared study information with frontline nurses, both face-to-face during roll calls and electronically through institutional announcement platforms. Printed posters were also placed on ward noticeboards to increase visibility. To further encourage participation, reminder emails were sent to nursing leaders at two weeks, four weeks, and three days before the survey closed. Participation was voluntary, and invitations were disseminated through existing nursing management channels rather than through random selection. Therefore, inclusion in the study depended on nurses’ availability and willingness to respond, which may have introduced self-selection bias. The response rate was calculated by dividing the number of completed questionnaires by the total number of eligible nurses who were invited to participate across the included departments in the two hospitals.

Wenjuanxing (WJX) was used to host the online survey. The questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the study objectives, the Sepsis-3 definitions, current Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendations, and relevant literature on nurses’ sepsis knowledge and confidence. The knowledge items were not adopted in full from a single previously validated instrument; rather, they were specifically developed for this study to assess nurses’ applied knowledge of adult sepsis recognition and management in accordance with current guideline-based practice. The research team drafted items to cover key domains including sepsis definitions, risk factors, early clinical indicators, laboratory assessment, patient monitoring, and initial management. In addition, scenario-based questions were constructed using common adult sepsis presentations, including diabetic foot sepsis, urosepsis, and catheter-related sepsis, in order to evaluate the application of knowledge in clinically relevant situations. The questionnaire was originally developed in English and subsequently translated into Chinese for administration in the participating hospitals. The Chinese version was reviewed by bilingual members of the research team to ensure linguistic clarity, comprehension, and contextual appropriateness. The draft questionnaire was pilot-tested before formal data collection to assess clarity, readability, ease of navigation, and accuracy of response capture. Feedback obtained during pilot testing was used to refine item wording and improve the overall usability of the survey. The final survey aimed to assess registered nurses’ (RNs) knowledge of sepsis as well as their self-reported confidence in recognizing and managing affected patients. It comprised four main sections (see Supplementary File 1).

Ethical approval for this study was secured from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Brain Hospital (IRB JPH 202546190 M). Participation was voluntary, and the act of completing and submitting the survey was considered to constitute informed consent. No personally identifiable information was obtained, and rigorous procedures were implemented to ensure the confidentiality and anonymity of all responses.

Instrument validity and reliability

Because the questionnaire was developed by the research team for this study, particular attention was given to establishing its content validity and face validity before use. The translated Chinese version was also reviewed during this process to ensure that the wording remained clear, accurate, and appropriate for the local clinical context. Both content and face validity of the survey instrument were established through evaluation by a panel of eight experts, consisting of advanced practice nurses (APNs), a nursing academic, and a specialist in intensive care. Each expert independently assessed the relevance of the items using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant) and offered qualitative suggestions for refinement. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) for the confidence scale and sepsis knowledge items ranged from 0.84 to 1.00, whereas the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) attained a perfect score of 1.00, indicating outstanding validity.

Based on feedback from the content experts and the outcomes of the content validation assessment, minor revisions were incorporated into the final version of the survey. These revisions mainly involved improving wording clarity, ensuring alignment with current sepsis terminology, and enhancing the clinical relevance of the scenario-based items. Furthermore, the internal consistency reliability of the confidence scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a coefficient of 0.860, which reflects high reliability and strong measurement rigor.

Statistical and qualitative data analysis

Data analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Descriptive methods were applied, including the calculation of means and standard deviations (SD), medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and the presentation of proportions and percentages to summarize demographic variables, workplace information, sepsis knowledge scores, and self-confidence levels of participants.

Comparisons of nurses’ total sepsis knowledge and self-confidence scores across demographic and workplace subgroups were performed using independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA, with Bonferroni adjustment applied for multiple comparisons. To identify predictors of sepsis knowledge as the dependent variable, factors that reached statistical significance at p ≤ .1 in univariate linear regression namely years of nursing experience, job grade, clinical work area, education level, sepsis education or training received within the past year, and the presence of a sepsis screening tool were subsequently incorporated into a multiple linear regression model.

Pearson’s product–moment correlation coefficients were applied to evaluate the association between sepsis knowledge and self-confidence scores. Furthermore, Chi-square analyses were performed to compare item-level self-confidence responses related to the recognition and management of sepsis, stratified according to the presence of a sepsis screening tool and the clinical work area. A threshold of p < .05 was adopted to determine statistical significance across all analyses.

In addition, qualitative data from Sect. 4 of the survey were analyzed using a content analysis approach. The short free-text responses were examined through line-by-line open coding, after which codes reflecting similar concepts were consolidated into broader categories. These categories were subsequently integrated to generate overarching themes27. Regular discussions among the authors ensured consensus and refinement of the derived themes.

Results

A total of 355 registered nurses completed the survey, reflecting a response rate of 44.3% based on approximately 801 eligible nurses who were invited to participate across the two hospitals. The participants’ demographic profiles and workplace-related data are summarized in Table 1. The majority of respondents were employed as staff nurses or senior staff nurses, accounting for over 58.6% of the sample. Nearly one-fourth (n = 85, 23.9%) reported six to ten years of clinical experience, while almost one-fourth (n = 84, 23.7%) had obtained a specialty certification in their area of practice. Thirteen of the nurses with a postgraduate nursing degree (master’s degree) were either working as APNs or completing an APN internship, out of a total of 30 master’s-prepared nurses. About one-third of nurses were working in general ward settings (n = 140, 39.4%), while only a small proportion (n = 47, 13.2%) had received formal sepsis-related education or training within the year preceding data collection. In total, 98 nurses reported having access to a sepsis screening tool within their clinical settings.

Sepsis knowledge scores among participants ranged from 3 to 15, with a mean of 10.04 ± 3.17 out of a maximum of 15. The highest proportion of respondents (n = 85, 23.9%) obtained a score of 12, while only four individuals (1.12%) achieved a perfect score. A detailed summary of item-level knowledge performance is presented in Table 2. For the four questions assessing general sepsis knowledge, correct response rates varied from 43.7% for identifying the definition of sepsis to 83.2% for recognizing risk factors. Despite this, a considerable number of participants misunderstood the definition, with 66 nurses (18.02%) selecting “bacteria in blood” and 51 (14.3%) identifying “infection” alone as the correct option. Over one-third of the respondents (n = 148, 41.6%) also answered incorrectly regarding the cause of sepsis, and 145 participants (40.8%) failed to select the correct epidemiological data.

In the section involving short case scenarios, four questions focused on treatment strategies. Correct response rates ranged from 59.2% for identifying the appropriate use of vasopressors in septic shock–induced hypotension to 83.2% for recognizing the need for blood cultures before initiating intravenous antibiotics. Regarding laboratory investigations, over half of the participants (n = 203, 57.1%) correctly identified blood cultures as the primary diagnostic test, while 48.7% (n = 173) recognized that a serum lactate level of 4.0 mmol/L signifies considerable clinical concern. In contrast, only 15.5% (n = 55) acknowledged an elevated respiratory rate as an early clinical indicator of sepsis, underscoring a marked gap in awareness of its initial manifestations.

As shown in Table 2, chi-squared analyses revealed significant differences in sepsis knowledge across clinical settings. Nurses employed in the ED/UCC, the Emergency Department, the Respiratory Infection and Critical Care Unit (RICU), and intensive care units (ICUs) demonstrated higher levels of knowledge than their counterparts in general wards, particularly in domains concerning the early recognition and initial management of sepsis and septic shock. Within these acute care groups, ICU and RICU nurses performed particularly well on questions concerning the evaluation and treatment of septic shock, outperforming both ED/UCC and ward-based nurses.

Table 3 illustrates notable differences in sepsis knowledge when data were stratified by years of experience, clinical area, job grade, and educational level. Nurses with over ten years of clinical experience attained higher scores compared to those with less than six years (F = 9.462, p = .004). Among clinical areas, ED/UCC nurses achieved the highest mean scores (mean = 14.23, SD = 2.12), followed by those in ICU/HDU (mean = 12.89, SD = 1.82), while nurses in general wards had the lowest scores (mean = 10.05, SD = 1.55). Job grade was also found to have a significant influence, with assistant nurse clinicians and nursing officers outperforming the other groups (F = 20.923, p = .016). Likewise, nurses holding a master’s degree, along with APNs and APN interns, demonstrated higher levels of knowledge compared to their counterparts (F = 17.79, p = .008; t = -5.67, p = .031). In contrast, no significant associations were identified with recent participation in sepsis education or the presence of sepsis screening tools.

With respect to self-reported confidence, scores ranged from 5 to 25, yielding a mean of 17.22 ± 3.62. The largest proportion of nurses (n = 78, 21.97%) obtained a score of 20, reflecting a generally moderate-to-high level of confidence. Correlation analysis indicated a weak yet statistically significant positive association between sepsis knowledge and confidence in its recognition and management (r = .213, p = .007). Higher confidence scores were observed among nurses with more than ten clinical experience years (F = 16.55, p = .003), those in senior job grades (F = 19.07, p = .005), and master’s-prepared nurses, including APNs and APN interns (t = -2.94, p = .022). Furthermore, nurses who had participated in sepsis-related education or training during the past year reported significantly greater confidence (t = -8.21, p = .015). Conversely, the availability of sepsis screening tools did not show a significant association with confidence levels.

As presented in Table 5, the majority of nurses (n = 290, 81.8%) reported confidence in escalating suspected sepsis to medical teams and in monitoring patients once sepsis was identified. In contrast, only 57.6% (n = 204) expressed confidence in accurately recognizing and assessing sepsis cases1 Subgroup analyses comparing nurses with access to a sepsis screening tool to those without revealed no significant differences in self-confidence across recognition, monitoring, escalation, or initial management domains.

A total of 302 participants (85.1%) responded to the open-ended question on organizational support for enhancing sepsis care, and the analysis identified three overarching themes, each supported by several subthemes (see Supplementary File 2). The most prominent suggestion involved implementing regular, structured sepsis training and education (n = 189), which should include in-service seminars, e-learning modules, case discussions, physician-led bedside teaching, and simulation exercises. Participants also emphasized the importance of developing a standardized sepsis workflow and protocol (n = 123), incorporating tools such as screening algorithms, escalation pathways, and management bundles, supported by visual reminders like cue cards and posters to facilitate adherence in clinical areas. Finally, nursing empowerment emerged as another recurrent theme (n = 55), where participants highlighted the need for greater collaboration between physicians and nurses, opportunities for ward nurses to initiate early sepsis interventions, and the introduction of a dedicated sepsis resource nurse to advocate for awareness and prompt recognition of the condition.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study explored the knowledge and confidence of registered nurses from Nanjing Brain Hospital and Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine in China regarding the recognition and management of adult patients with sepsis. Unlike many earlier studies that primarily assessed lower-order recall of factual knowledge, this study utilized a questionnaire designed to evaluate higher-order thinking skills, integrating application- and analysis-based questions through clinical case scenarios. The findings provide valuable insights into the current state of nurses’ knowledge and highlight critical gaps in understanding and clinical preparedness related to sepsis care. The findings of this study are in line with previous research, although direct comparisons remain challenging due to the variation in sepsis knowledge assessment tools and the differences in ward settings across the studies18,19,28–30.

The results revealed that, although nurses demonstrated a reasonable awareness of risk factors, significant deficits persisted in their understanding of updated Sepsis-3 definitions and epidemiological data. These findings imply a potential underestimation of sepsis severity, which could compromise timely recognition and appropriate intervention, ultimately affecting patient outcomes. A notable gap emerged in the recognition of tachypnea as an early clinical indicator, coupled with insufficient knowledge regarding the timing of blood cultures, appropriate serum lactate thresholds, and management of septic shock. Consistently, nurses with lower knowledge scores also reported diminished confidence in sepsis recognition, patient assessment, and initiation of early management, highlighting the strong link between knowledge limitations and self-efficacy in clinical decision-making.

The finding that 13.5% of nurses had participated in sepsis-related education or training within the past year likely contributes to these observed limitations. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that many respondents expressed a strong desire for additional learning opportunities, reflecting an awareness of their knowledge gaps and a willingness to engage in further development. While nurses who had recently undergone sepsis training reported significantly higher confidence levels, the improvement in their knowledge scores was only marginal, diverging from previous research that demonstrated substantial enhancements in both attitudes and knowledge following structured educational programs31–33. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the quality, duration, content, and delivery methods of sepsis education across settings. Some educational activities may increase nurses’ perceived familiarity with sepsis recognition and management, thereby enhancing confidence, without being sufficiently intensive or clinically focused to improve applied knowledge. This may be particularly true when training is brief, predominantly lecture-based, or not reinforced through simulation, case-based learning, or repeated clinical practice. In addition, the present study did not collect detailed information on the characteristics of the sepsis education received, which limits our ability to compare the relative effectiveness of different training approaches. A plausible explanation may lie in the variation in educational content and delivery methods across institutions. Unfortunately, these specific characteristics were not captured in this study’s survey. Evidence from a recent systematic review involving 32 studies suggests that active learning strategies, such as simulation-based training and case-based experiential learning, enhance knowledge retention and improve clinical transferability compared to didactic instruction34. Incorporating simulation exercises and rotations through critical care areas could therefore better equip nurses to manage sepsis effectively by bridging theoretical knowledge with practical application.

Analysis of contributing factors indicated that educational level, clinical practice area, and job grade significantly predicted sepsis knowledge. Nurses with specialty certifications or master’s degrees, those working in high-acuity units such as ICUs, RICU, or EDs, and those occupying senior clinical positions demonstrated higher knowledge scores. However, these variables explained only a modest proportion of the variance in knowledge scores, indicating that sepsis knowledge is likely shaped by additional factors beyond those included in the regression model. This finding suggests that nurses’ knowledge may also be influenced by contextual and organizational factors such as institutional policies, clinical workload, access to continuing education opportunities, frequency of exposure to sepsis cases, availability of mentorship, and the degree of implementation support for sepsis screening tools and protocols. These findings are consistent with expectations, as pre-licensure curricula for diploma and bachelor’s-level nurses often provide limited exposure to sepsis content compared to advanced nursing programs. Nurses lacking specialized training often have minimal exposure to sepsis-related education during their pre-licensure programs, with the majority of their knowledge and skills regarding sepsis usually gained through on-the-job training. Similar to findings reported in Australia35, the apparent lack of sepsis content within pre-licensure nursing education in China may stem from curriculum planners adopting a flexible and responsive approach to the evolving healthcare needs of the country. Furthermore, the intricate nature of sepsis poses challenges for educators in providing effective instruction to nursing students, who frequently have limited clinical experience. Conversely, higher nursing education programs may place a greater emphasis on sepsis-related content, which could explain why nurses with advanced educational qualifications tend to achieve higher sepsis knowledge scores, as similarly observed by Öztürk Birge et al.36.

Nurses practicing in acute care environments demonstrated superior knowledge and self-confidence compared to those based in general wards, aligning with findings from Stamataki et al.29. This outcome is unsurprising, given that ICU/ RICU nurses frequently manage patients with sepsis-induced multiorgan failure, requiring intensive monitoring and complex interventions25. Likewise, nurses in the ED often act as first responders for patients with community-acquired sepsis, which represents approximately 85% of sepsis cases admitted to hospitals37. Such frequent clinical exposure likely fosters stronger familiarity with early recognition and initial management strategies.

Interestingly, findings revealed a modest positive association between nurses’ knowledge scores and their self-reported confidence levels. This divergence suggests that knowledge assessments alone may not reliably reflect nurses’ actual clinical competencies or practical skills38. Objective performance evaluations, such as simulation-based testing, workplace assessments, or monitoring sepsis-related performance indicators, could provide more accurate insights into clinical capability. Furthermore, self-reported measures may be influenced by cognitive biases, as some nurses might overestimate their competence relative to their actual abilities39. The weak correlation observed in this study suggests that higher confidence does not necessarily correspond to higher levels of knowledge. This has important practical implications, as nurses who feel confident but lack sufficient knowledge may be at risk of making inappropriate clinical judgments, potentially delaying the recognition or management of sepsis. Such a mismatch between confidence and knowledge may have consequences for patient safety, particularly in time-sensitive conditions such as sepsis, where early and accurate decision-making is essential. Therefore, educational strategies should focus not only on improving knowledge but also on ensuring that confidence is appropriately aligned with actual competence, for example through simulation-based training, supervised practice, and competency-based assessment.

The study, however, revealed no meaningful link between the presence of a sepsis screening tool and nurses’ ability or confidence in detecting sepsis symptoms and carrying out clinical assessments of suspected patients. These findings are inconsistent with prior research, which reported enhanced nurses’ confidence in identifying sepsis following the implementation of screening tools32. A possible explanation is that approximately 48.7% of the nurses were unsure whether a sepsis screening tool was available in their area of practice, highlighting the need for better implementation and dissemination strategies for clinical protocols. Moreover, this highlights the critical need for continuous education and regular sepsis training for frontline nurses to enhance adherence to clinical protocols and optimize their combined effectiveness40,41.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results of this study. First among these is the cross-sectional design, which precludes the determination of causal relationships between nurses’ knowledge, self-confidence, and associated factors. Second, data were obtained via self-reported surveys, which may be influenced by response bias or social desirability, potentially causing participants to overstate their knowledge or confidence. Third, the use of convenience sampling introduces the possibility of selection bias. Nurses who chose to participate may have been more interested in sepsis, more confident in their clinical knowledge, or more motivated to engage in research than non-participants. As a result, the findings may overestimate the overall level of sepsis knowledge and confidence among the broader nursing workforce. In addition, although participants were recruited from two hospitals and from a range of clinical settings, the sample may not be fully representative of all nurses within these institutions. Fourth, the study was conducted at two hospitals within a single healthcare cluster in eastern China, and the response rate of 44.3% further limits the generalizability of findings to all registered nurses working in other tertiary care or community healthcare settings. Fifth, the study did not assess all contextual and organizational factors that may influence nurses’ sepsis knowledge and confidence, such as institutional policies, workload, access to continuing education, or local implementation practices related to sepsis protocols. Sixth, although the survey incorporated case-based multiple-choice questions to assess applied knowledge, such formats may not fully reflect real-world clinical competence or decision-making abilities under time-sensitive conditions. Objective assessments, such as simulation-based testing or direct observation of clinical practice, could provide a more accurate measure of performance. Finally, while an open-ended question was included to explore organizational support needs, the study lacked in-depth qualitative insights into nurses’ experiences and perceptions of sepsis care, which could have offered richer contextual understanding to guide policy development and educational strategies. Future research should adopt mixed-methods designs, include larger multicenter samples, and integrate qualitative approaches to enhance the validity, applicability, and robustness of findings.

Conclusion

This study identified persistent gaps in nurses’ knowledge and confidence regarding adult sepsis recognition and management, particularly among nurses working in general ward settings. Nurses in acute care areas demonstrated higher levels of knowledge and confidence, suggesting that clinical exposure and training may influence preparedness. These findings highlight the need for targeted sepsis education, standardized protocols, and practice-based training strategies to strengthen early recognition and improve patient outcomes.
