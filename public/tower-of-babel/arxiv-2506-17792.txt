Abstract
Software-intensive systems, such as software product lines and robotics, utilise Markov decision processes (MDPs) to capture uncertainty and analyse sequential decision-making problems. Despite the usefulness of conventional policy synthesis methods, they fail to scale to large state spaces. Our approach addresses this issue and accelerates policy synthesis in large MDPs by dynamically refining the MDP and iteratively selecting the most fragile MDP regions for refinement. This iterative procedure offers a balance between accuracy and efficiency, as refinement occurs only when necessary. Through a comprehensive empirical evaluation comprising diverse case studies and MDPs up to 1M states, we demonstrate significant performance improvements yielded by our approach compared to the leading probabilistic model checker PRISM (up to 2x), thus offering a very competitive solution for real-world policy synthesis tasks in larger MDPs.

II-A

Probabilistic Modelling

Markov decision process (MDP)
.
An MDP is a tuple

\ \mathcal{M}\;=\;(S,\bar{s},A,\delta,r,AP,L),
where
S
is a finite set of states;
\bar{s}\in S
is an initial state;
A
is a finite set of actions;
\delta:(S\times A)\to\mathit{Dist}(S)
is a probabilistic transition function mapping each
(s,a)
pair to a probability distribution over
S
;

r:S\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a reward structure;

AP
is a set of atomic propositions; and
L:S\to 2^{AP}
is a labelling function.

MDPs support nondeterministic actions as each state
s\in S
can have multiple enabled actions for which
\mathit{Dist}(s,a)
is defined, given by
A(s)
.
An MDP
policy
is a possible resolution of nondeterminism.
Resolving this nondeterminism enables to probabilistically reason about MDPs and synthesise
memoryless deterministic policies
, i.e., policies where the action chosen in a state depends solely on the current state.
Formally, a (deterministic memoryless) policy of an MDP is a function
\sigma:S\rightarrow A
mapping each state
s\in S
to an action in
A(s)
.

Probabilistic Computation Tree Logic (PCTL).

PCTL
[
24
,
25
]
specifies properties over MDPs.
State formulae:

\Phi::=\textit{true}\mid a\mid\neg\Phi\mid\Phi_{1}\land\Phi_{2}\mid\mathtt{P}_{\bowtie p}[\varphi]\mid\mathtt{R}^{r}_{\bowtie q}[\rho]
,
with
a\!\in\!AP
,
\bowtie\!\in\!\{<,\leq,>,\geq\}
,

p\!\in\![0,1]
,
q\!\geq\!0
.
Path formulae:

\varphi::=\mathsf{X}\,\Phi\mid\Phi_{1}\,\mathsf{U}^{\leq k}\Phi_{2}\mid\Phi_{1}\,\mathsf{U}\,\Phi_{2}
.
Reachability is the shorthand
\mathsf{F}\Phi\equiv\textit{true}\,\mathsf{U}\,\Phi
.
Typical query:
\mathtt{P}_{\max}[\mathsf{F}\,\textit{goal}]
which asks the maximum probability of eventually reaching a goal state.

Quantitative reachability.

Let
M=(S,\bar{s},A,\delta,r,AP,L)
be the MDP defined above and

T\subseteq S
a set of target states.
We denote by
\mathsf{F}\ T
the event that

is eventually reached.
For any state
s\in S

\sigma

\Pr^{\sigma}_{s}
(and the associated expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\sigma}_{s}

\pi=\pi_{0}\pi_{1}\ldots

\mathtt{P}_{\max}(s,\mathsf{F}\ T)=\sup_{\sigma}\Pr^{\sigma}_{s}(\mathsf{F}\ T)

The minimum expected reachability reward is

\mathtt{R}_{\max}(s,\mathsf{F}T)=\sup_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}^{\sigma}_{s}\bigl[\sum_{k<k_{T}}r(\pi_{k})\bigr]

is the first step index at which a state in
T
is reached.

III
Motivating Example: Warehouse Robotics

Consider an autonomous robot operating in a warehouse with multiple shelves (W1–W3 in Figure
1
), each possibly blocking certain aisles.
The robot should collect items from these shelves and deliver them to a packing station (Goal).
This environment is typically modelled as an MDP, where each state captures the robot’s position and which aisles are blocked, while actions enable the robot to move up, down, left, or right.
Since the robot does not always move precisely as intended, each action leads to multiple possible next states with known probabilities (e.g., it might fail to move or slip to an adjacent cell).
This probabilistic behaviour, combined with large grids of shelves, yields MDPs with millions of states, rendering policy synthesis prohibitively expensive.

This challenging scenario frequently arises in realistic warehouses that comprise
\approx 10^{5}
states or more
[
3
,
28
]
.
In these situations, policy synthesis techniques, such as VI, must repeatedly update every state, resulting in severe runtime and memory overhead.
Also, not all states are of equal importance. For example, open areas around
(0,0)
may be relatively safe and uninteresting; thus, they can often be treated coarsely with little loss of accuracy.
Likewise, crowded corridors near shelves (W1–W3) can be prone to jams or delays; thus, a fine-grained analysis is required to ensure the robot’s strategy remains robust.
Despite these semantic differences in the underlying system model, state-of-the-art PMC tools use VI and its variants
[
19
]
treat these regions the same, potentially wasting resources on trivial warehouse sections.

Driven by this important observation, SHARP focuses on devoting more computational resources to uncertain or congested areas (e.g., near W1–W3), thus leaving “uninteresting partitions” at a coarse level of detail.
Consequently, SHARP can retain high accuracy where it counts the most and, most importantly, substantially reduce the state-space overhead.

IV
SHARP

SHARP is a hierarchical partition-based approach for MDP policy synthesis that adaptively refines MDP partitions only where needed.
Its high-level workflow is shown in Figure
2
and comprises five stages (S1-S5).
SHARP takes as inputs an MDP model, a reachability property (probability or expected reward to be maximised/minimised), and refinement parameters that control the hierarchical decomposition of the approach.

The first SHARP stage (S1) involves executing an initial partitioning of the MDP into a set of
partitions
, where each partition is a sub-MDP.
SHARP uses this partitioning to
construct the hierarchy tree (S2), whose root node corresponds to the entire set of states, and the children correspond to the individual partitions.
Each partition, at this stage, is considered a leaf node in the tree and is solved
locally
using VI (S3). This local solution uses fixed boundary conditions, derived from its parent node (initially the root), to properly account for any transitions leaving the partition.
This enables obtaining refined value estimates and a partial policy for each partition.

Once these refined estimates and partial policies are available, SHARP recursively propagates them up to their parent nodes in the tree (S4), ensuring that higher tree levels also have access to this updated information.
Then, SHARP checks whether further refinement is needed.
If this holds (S5.1), it refines the sub-MDP by subdividing selected partitions into smaller partitions, extends the partition tree and repeats the local solve procedure from the new leaves.
Finally, if no further refinement is required (S5.2), SHARP extracts the synthesised policy
\sigma
.

IV-A

Partitioning and Tree Hierarchy Construction

Foundational approaches to managing large MDPs include partitioning the state space to construct a smaller, aggregate model where each block becomes a single abstract state
[
29
]
. While in our work we do not perform state aggregation,
we leverage the principle of partitioning to structure the policy synthesis process hierarchically. SHARP is underpinned by two core concepts:
(i) the partitioning of the state space into
blocks
; and
(ii) the
hierarchical, nested
arrangement of such partitions obtained by recursively subdividing blocks, represented by a
hierarchy tree
.

Definition 1

(Partitioning)
.

Given an MDP
\mathcal{M}

B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
);
and (iii) the union of all blocks covers the entire state space (
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}=S

IV-B

Adaptive Partition Refinement

SHARP achieves efficiency by adaptively refining partitions only where necessary.
The decision to refine a leaf node in the hierarchy tree is based on criteria assessing its current state and value distribution.
For a node
v
representing block
B_{v}
with current value estimates
V_{v}
, we define the
value spread

\Delta(v)=\max_{s\in B_{v}}V_{v}(s)-\min_{s\in B_{v}}V_{v}(s).

Block-refinement criteria.

A leaf node
v
is refined iff all of the following hold:

1.

Depth criterion
:
v.\mathrm{depth}<D
, i.e., the
v
node’s depth is less than the user-imposed maximum depth.

2.

Value spread criterion
:

•

Probability properties
(
\mathtt{P}_{\mathsf{opt}}
):
\Delta(v)>\theta
, i.e., the absolute spread
\Delta(v)
exceeds the threshold
\theta
.

•

Reward/cost properties
(
\mathtt{R}_{\mathsf{opt}}
):
\Delta(v)\big/\!\bigl(\operatorname{mean}(V_{v})+\beta\bigr)>\theta
, i.e.,
the relative reward spread exceeds
\theta
, where
\beta
is a small positive constant used for numerical stability.

If these conditions are satisfied,
v
is subdivided into two or more child blocks that form a partition of
B_{v}
using the
RefineNode
procedure shown in Algorithm
2
.
To this end, the algorithm
takes as inputs a node
v
representing block
B_{v}
; the maximum depth parameter
D
; and the refinement threshold
\theta
. The procedure computes the value spread
\Delta(v)
(line 3 of Algorithm
2
) and compares it against
\theta
. If the spread is below
\theta
or if
v
has already reached depth
D
(line 2),
RefineNode
terminates. Otherwise, it splits
v
into smaller sub-partitions (line 5), solves each sub-partition (line 6), recurses on the children (line 7), and finally propagates the refined values back to
v
’s parent (line 11). This ensures that only regions with large value variation are examined in greater detail while the parent’s estimates remain consistent.

The core idea is to solve each sub-MDP independently without considering the other sub-MDPs, using the boundary values inherited from the parent node to approximate the behaviour beyond the currently solved sub-MDP.

(i) include every state

any successor that also lies in

(ii) add each boundary state

\mathrm{bd}(B)=\{\,t\notin B\mid\exists\,s\in B,\,a\in A(s):\delta(s,a)(t)>0\}

giving it a self-loop (probability 1);
and (iii) initialise every boundary state
t
with the parent value

V_{\text{parent}}(t)

Storing and propagating.

After local convergence, SHARP stores both the value vector
\{V_{B}(s)\mid s\in B\cup\mathrm{bd}(B)\}
and the corresponding partial policy
\sigma
in the
node associated with block
B
.
If the node is a leaf, the sub-MDP solving is complete.
Otherwise, once all child nodes are solved, we propagate their values upward to update this parent node’s estimates; the full procedure is
detailed in Section

.

Solving sub-MDPs locally by utilising the boundary states derived from their parents’ values is fundamental in SHARP.
This principle avoids running a monolithic fixed-point iteration on the entire state space and focuses computation
only on sub-regions that actually require finer analysis.

Example 3 (continued).
Suppose we have partitioned B9 into three single-state blocks:
B9a
,
B9b
, and
B9c

B9a
(containing cell
(5,4)
), we include: (i) the state
(5,4)
with all its original actions and transitions, and (ii) any states directly reachable from
(5,4)
as boundary states - including the goal
(5,5)
and adjacent cells. These boundary states are absorbing (self-loops with probability 1) and assigned fixed values from block 9.
For such a small sub-MDP, local VI converges quickly. Since
(5,4)
is adjacent to the goal (whose value is 1.0), the converged value for
(5,4)
will be high.
After solving all three children sub-MDPs, we propagate their values back to update block B9’s estimates.

IV-D

Value Propagation Up the Tree Hierarchy

After solving each leaf node’s sub-MDP and yielding local solutions (Section
IV-C
), SHARP propagates these values up the hierarchy tree to obtain a consistent global policy (line 9 in Algorithm
1
, line 10 in Algorithm
2
).
This value propagation process recursively updates each parent node’s value estimates and policy based on the refined solutions of its children.
This essential step enables updating the boundary state values to reflect the more accurate estimates produced by the sub-MDPs.

The
PropagateAllUp
procedure (line 10 in Algorithm
1
) performs a recursive bottom-up traversal of the hierarchy tree
\mathcal{T}
.
For each node
v\in\mathcal{N}
, it first processes all children recursively, ensuring that their values are up to date. Then, for each state
s\in B_{v}
, the procedure determines which child block contains
s
and adopts that child’s value and policy. Since the children’s blocks
\{B_{c_{1}},\ldots,B_{c_{k}}\}
form a partition of
B_{v}
, each state belongs to exactly one child, making this assignment unambiguous.

Formally, for each state
s\in B_{v}
, if
s\in B_{c_{i}}
for some child
c_{i}
, then we set
V_{v}(s)=V_{c_{i}}(s)
and
\pi_{v}(s)=\pi_{c_{i}}(s)
. This propagation ensures that the refined value estimates and policy decisions from deeper levels of the hierarchy are incorporated into coarser levels, ultimately producing a globally consistent policy
\sigma
at the root node.

Example 4
.
With the local solutions for
B9a
,
B9b
, and
B9c
now computed, we merge them back into block B9 by assigning each single-state block’s value (and policy) to the corresponding cell.
For instance, if
B9a
determined that
(5,4)
has a probability
\approx\!1.0
to reach
(5,5)
, we set
V_{9}(5,4)\approx 1.0
. Similarly,
B9b
and
B9c
provide values for their respective cells.
At this point, block B9 value estimates are updated based on its children’s refined solutions.
Although in this example, we reached single-state blocks that cannot be subdivided further, SHARP, in general, checks in subsequent iterations if further refinement is needed based on the updated value spread.

V
Implementation

SHARP is implemented in Java on top of the probabilistic model checker PRISM
[
19
]
.
The core of our implementation lies in newly developed algorithms integrated within PRISM’s explicit engine, including hierarchical partitioning, adaptive refinement based on value spread, sub-MDP construction with boundary states, and bottom-up value propagation.
SHARP can be configured by adjusting the
initial partition dimensions
(N_{x},N_{y})
, maximum refinement depth
D
, refinement threshold
\theta
, and maximum global iterations
\mathit{maxIters}
.

VI
Evaluation

VI-A

Research Questions

We use the research questions below to assess SHARP and extract decision-making insights, aiding practitioners to effectively embed SHARP in their verification workflows.

RQ1 (Efficiency):

How efficient is SHARP compared to PRISM in terms of execution time and memory overhead across various problem instances?

RQ2 (Scalability):

How does SHARP scale with problem instances of increasing sizes in terms of states and transitions?

RQ3 (Hyperparameter sensitivity):

How SHARP’s performance is affected by adjusting hyperparameters: initial partition size, maximum depth, refinement thresholds?

VI-B

Experimental Setup

Case studies.

Our experimental evaluation uses robotic warehouse scenarios represented by grid-based MDPs of varying complexities (Table
I
), which have served as standard benchmarks for evaluating MDP algorithms in both reinforcement learning
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
and formal verification
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
We evaluate 512×512 and 1024×1024 grids with three obstacle configurations: no walls (NW), single wall (SW), and multiple walls (MW).
Each configuration has two variants:
(i) maximise probability to reach the goal state (0.9 move, 0.09975 self-loop, 0.00025 transition to failure state);
and (b) minimising expected steps without failures (0.8 move, 0.2 self-loop).

Further to the benchmarks in Table
I
, and to assess SHARP’s robustness in randomised environments, we conducted experiments using 5 randomly generated 1024×1024 MW MDPs, with various wall configurations (sparse, maze, dense, etc.).

Baselines and configurations.

We compare SHARP against PRISM’s explicit engine using both standard VI and its GS variant with and without pre-computations
[
19
]
.
For our evaluation, PRISM’s explicit engine served as a more suitable baseline than other tools like Storm
[
20
]
because it allows for a fair and controlled comparison.
Since SHARP is implemented as a Java-based extension within the PRISM ecosystem, benchmarking it against PRISM’s own engine effectively isolates the performance impact of our hierarchical algorithm, which is the core contribution of this work. Introducing an external, highly-optimised C++ tool like Storm would add significant confounding variables (language, compiler, specialised libraries), making it difficult to distinguish between algorithmic gains and implementation optimisations. Thus, PRISM provides a transparent baseline to validate the specific benefits of our hierarchical refinement strategy within SHARP.

We explored SHARP configurations with: maximum depth
D\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
; initial partition sizes from
8\times 8
to
25\times 25
; reward thresholds
\theta\in\{200,300,400,500,600,800\}
; and probability thresholds
\theta\in\{0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0\}
. All sub-MDPs were solved using VI (convergence threshold
\varepsilon=10^{-6}
).

VI-D

Threats to Validity

We mitigate
construct validity threats
that might arise due to the lack of global context in sub-MDPS due to the hierarchical decomposition by grounding SHARP in the well-established VI algorithm for probabilities and rewards.
SHARP also propagates values down the tree, while refinement is triggered when the value spread criterion is violated, therefore, ensuring that accuracy is recovered where needed across the tree hierarchy.

We limit
internal validity threats
by integrating SHARP into PRISM’s explicit-state engine, reusing its core and supplying our algorithms for hierarchical partitioning, adaptive refinement, and local sub-MDP solving.
By building on a widely tested code base, we minimise the risk that implementation bugs skew our measurements.
We reduce threats due to SHARP’s stochasticity by performing 30 independent runs per problem instance and reporting measures of central tendency and dispersion.

We mitigate
external validity threats
by evaluating SHARP on large benchmarks that correspond to realistic warehouse robot scenarios of varying complexity and model sizes
[
28
]
and comparing it against the VI and GS algorithms of PRISM
[
19
]
.
Assessing the performance gains yielded by SHARP against other state-of-the-art tools like Storm
[
20
]
entails implementing the core of our approach (Algorithms
1
and
1
) within these tools to leverage the highly-optimised numerical libraries used internally.
Doing this would allow for a fair and transparent comparison focusing purely on algorithmic gains and mitigating any potential bias strictly due to implementation optimisations.

Lastly, more experiments on non-grid and highly irregular MDPs (e.g., self-adaptive systems
[
36
,
6
]
, software product lines
[
37
]
) are needed to establish SHARP’s applicability and scalability in domains different from those in our evaluation.

Search-based methods.

Evolutionary approaches
[

tackle probabilistic verification tasks intractable for exact techniques. EvoChecker
[

,

[

[

approximates Pareto fronts for multi-objective models too large for exact verification.
SHARP is complementary; it accelerates exact synthesis for single objectives while preserving optimality, which evolutionary methods do not guarantee.

Hierarchical RL.

Early HRL frameworks such as Feudal RL
[
51
]
,
MAXQ
[
52
]
, and Options
[
31
]
decompose an MDP into subtasks or macro-actions, reducing sample complexity.
Recent abstractions (e.g., AVI
[
53

) extend these ideas to continuous spaces, while Monte Carlo-based approaches
[
54
]
treat abstracted MDPs as POMDPs to scale tree search.
Unlike SHARP, HRL methods usually learn from samples and
provide no optimality guarantees for the original model.

VIII
Conclusion

We introduced SHARP, a hierarchical adaptive refinement approach for accelerating policy synthesis in large MDPs.
SHARP is general to handle diverse MDP structures via configurable partitioning strategies, and flexible to balance accuracy and efficiency via adaptive refinement criteria.
We have implemented SHARP as an open-source extension to PRISM and demonstrated its effectiveness and scalability through comprehensive experiments on warehouse robotics scenarios with up to 1M states, showcasing that SHARP is faster than PRISM-based policy synthesis using VI and GS.
Our future work includes:
(i) extending SHARP to support non-grid domains and multi-objective properties;
(ii) enhance SHARP’s refinement strategy by incorporating domain knowledge; and
(iii) examine SHARP’s scalability in larger model instances.
