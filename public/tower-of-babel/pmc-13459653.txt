Facilitators and barriers to childhood immunization in Canada: a scoping review
Authors: Charlene Thompson, Alejandra Fonseca-Cuevas, Kofi Junior Effah Frimpong, Wanda Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunization is a primary prevention public health strategy that has saved more lives than any other health service. Achieving the recommended 95% childhood immunization coverage rate remains a challenge in Canada, especially post-COVID-19. This challenge highlights the need to better understand current immunization facilitators and barriers to improve coverage rates. This study aimed to determine: (1) Factors that support and create barriers to childhood immunization for families with children 0–6 years of age in Canada (2) If childhood immunization facilitators and barriers changed from pre-to-post-COVID-19.MethodsUsing Arksey and O’Malley scoping review framework informed by Levac et al. and JBI Scoping Review Guidelines, seven databases were searched: MedLine (OVID), EMBASE (OVID), Web of Science (Clarivate), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library (Wiley), APA PsycArticles (OVID), ProQuest Dissertations and Theses (Clarivate) for peer-reviewed literature. Articles were screened against inclusion/exclusion criteria, data extracted and thematically analyzed using the Braun and Clarke framework.ResultsThirty-one studies were included. Five identified themes had a dual role supporting and creating barriers to childhood immunization and included: access to healthcare services, cognitive reasoning, affective influences on decision-making, external influences, and information. Two standalone barriers included: use of alternative medicine and colonial institutional practices. Differences pre-to-post-COVID-19 in factors supporting immunization included: cognitive reasoning and access to healthcare services; barriers included cognitive reasoning, information, access to healthcare services, and colonial institutional practices.ConclusionsThis scoping review highlighted factors from caregiver perspectives that support and create barriers to childhood immunization including cognitive reasoning, affective influences on decision-making, and information. Post-COVID-19 considerations noted in our findings were affective influences on decision-making – trust and relationships and access to healthcare services. There appears an immunization information time gap in the prenatal period. Colonial institutional practices create access challenges for Indigenous families. To better understand these gaps, additional research is needed to understand current immunization information practices in the prenatal period, the contextual nuances of immunization service delivery, and Indigenous Peoples’ experiences with immunization services. Further exploration of these areas may help to inform strategies to address identified barriers, strengthen immunization services, and improve immunization coverage rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27858-4.Keywords: Immunization, Vaccination, Children, Canada, Public health, COVID-19

Background

Immunization is a primary prevention public health strategy that has saved more lives than any other health service [1]. The significance of immunization is underscored by the fact that coverage rates serve as indicators of population health and health service performance [2]. In Canada, childhood immunizations are publicly funded, meaning there is no cost to families for vaccines or health services to receive immunizations [3]. Despite its proven effectiveness and no cost to service users, achieving the national coverage target of 95% for recommended childhood immunizations remains a challenge in Canada, especially in the post-pandemic context [4, 5] When coverage rates decrease, the risk of vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks occurs [2]. The most recent example of this impact is the confirmed measles cases in Canada, which have climbed from 12 cases in 2023 to 146 cases in 2024 and 5045 cases in 2025 [6].

There has been an overall decrease in childhood immunization coverage rates post-pandemic from 2019 in both 2- and 7-year-old cohorts [4]. For instance, in 2023, 84.3% of two-year-olds in Canada were vaccinated against measles, a decrease from 88.9% in 2019 [4]. Additionally, 73.3% of two-year-olds received the full doses of the diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis vaccine (DTaP) in 2023, down from 79.6% in 2019 [4]. Addressing reductions in immunization coverage for vaccine-preventable diseases is essential to protect the health of children and the overall population [2, 7].

The current gaps in Canadian vaccine coverage rates post-COVID-19 pandemic highlight the need to better understand current immunization facilitators and barriers. To provide a broad overview of the facilitators and barriers to childhood immunization, our research team conducted a scoping review. A scoping review aligned with our purpose as it aims to address a broad topic in which multiple study designs have been applied, and identify the key factors related to the concept [8]. In addition, a scoping review does not require a quality assessment of evidence [8–10], broadening the scope of literature to best answer our research questions. Given that cost is often cited as a barrier in the international literature [11], we aimed to conduct a scoping review specific to the Canadian context, where families and caregivers incur no cost for vaccine and immunization services. As this scoping review aims to synthesize the facilitators and barriers to childhood immunization, we determined the following research questions:

What are the factors that support and create barriers to childhood immunization for families with children 0–6 years of age in Canada?

What are the factors that support and create barriers to childhood immunization for families with children 0–6 years of age in Canada?

Have childhood immunization facilitators and barriers changed from the pre-COVID-19 context (prior to 2020) when compared to the post-COVID-19 context (2021–2026)?

Have childhood immunization facilitators and barriers changed from the pre-COVID-19 context (prior to 2020) when compared to the post-COVID-19 context (2021–2026)?

Methods

Our review was grounded in the Arksey and O’Malley framework [8] informed by Levac et al. and the JBI Evidence Synthesis Methodology- Scoping Review Guidelines [9, 10]. We followed the stages: 1) Identifying the research question, 2) identifying relevant studies, 3) study selection, 4) charting the data, and 5) collating and summarizing results. We also followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [12]. See Supplemental PRISMA-ScR checklist. The protocol for this scoping was registered on January 6th, 2025, on Open Science Framework (OSF) with the associated registration10.17605/OSF.IO/XADPN. Deviations from the protocol included adding author WM to provide additional scoping review support and expertise to better inform our review. Our primary research question was: What current factors support immunization and create barriers to immunization for families with children aged 0–6 years in Canada? A subsequent secondary analysis was conducted to compare changes in immunization facilitators and barriers pre-and- post-COVID-19 pandemic.

Inclusion criteria

We identified the population, concept, and context (PCC) within the scope of this review to set our inclusion criteria. Population: Families and caregivers of children 0–6 years of age. Concept: Facilitators were elements reported in the literature as supporting, enabling, or increasing the ease of immunization of children. Barriers were elements creating challenges in accessing, receiving, or obtaining immunization for children [13]. Context: Studies conducted in Canada.

Studies focused on passive immunity were excluded (i.e. providing antibodies to protect against infection, such as transfer of maternal antibodies through the placenta or obtaining serum from immune individuals [14]. Studies that did not isolate data on children ages 0–6 years or studies on adult and adolescent immunization were excluded. Studies that were centered exclusively around healthcare providers’ experiences and perspectives on immunization were excluded. Given that one of the aims of this study was to explore the changes in facilitators and barriers to childhood immunization, no time limit on publication date was applied. The search was limited to English-language studies. Review articles, scoping reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, letters, commentaries, conference abstracts and non-reviewed entries were excluded.

Information sources

This scoping review considered qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods study designs. We searched seven databases: MedLine (OVID), EMBASE (OVID), Web of Science (Clarivate), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library (Wiley), APA PsycArticles (OVID) and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses (Clarivate). Initial searches were conducted on February 21 st, 2024. Updated searches of all databases were conducted on September 22nd, 2024, and April 8th 2026.

Search strategy

To avoid duplicate studies, a preliminary search for scoping reviews on our research question was undertaken and indicated the lack of a similar study in Canada. Pilot searches were designed and conducted in MedLine. The strategy was reviewed by health science librarians at the University of Saskatchewan (Supplemental Material, Table S1). To determine the search strategies, the research question was divided into four main concepts as follows:

Immunization: In Canada, each province and territory defines their routine immunization schedule for its population [15]. As such, immunization schedules across the country differ [16]. To address this discrepancy in our search, we included vaccines from all provincial and territorial schedules provided to children ages 0–6 years. These primary series and boosters included: tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough (pertussis), polio, Haemophilus influenzae type B, rotavirus, measles, mumps, rubella, chickenpox, meningitis and routine immunizations such as influenza and COVID-19 [15].

Specific search terms used for each main concept, along with adaptations to each database, can be found in the Supplemental Material (Supplemental Material, Table S1). When MeSH terms were unavailable in the database, a combination of Title, Abstract and Keywords was used.

Selection of sources of evidence

Following the search process, all identified records were uploaded to the Covidence systematic review software [22], and duplicates were removed. We conducted screening in two stages: Stage 1: Title and Abstract Screening, Stage 2: Full-text review. Two independent reviewers screened the data (Author KJEF, AFC, or CT). Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (Author AFC or CT). We did a pilot screening of 5 records for Stage 1 and 1 record for Stage 2 to assess alignment with the inclusion criteria and familiarize reviewers with the software.

Data extraction

Based on the research questions, we designed a standardized data extraction form in Covidence (Supplemental Material, Table S2). The data extraction form was tested and calibrated with five records. Data extracted included: Title, Authors, Year of publication, Year of data collection, Province of study, Study design, Objectives, Types of vaccines, Number of participants, Age of children, Ethnic origin, Recruitment Methods, Data Collection, Immunization facilitators, Immunization barriers, Outcomes of the study and Limitations. At least two independent reviewers (Author KJEF, AFC, or CT) conducted data extraction. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer (AFC or CT).

Data synthesis and analysis

The reviewers extracted data that were collated and synthesized in a tabular format. Qualitative data capturing immunization facilitators and barriers were thematically analyzed in NVIVO 14 [23] following Braun and Clarke’s six phased approach [24], 1) Familiarizing yourself with the data 2) Generating initial codes, 3) Generating initial themes (updated language from Braun and Clarke) [25]; 4) Reviewing the themes, 5) Defining and naming themes, 6) Producing the report. Although reported in a linear format, the analysis process was completed over multiple sessions from January to April 2025 and then in April 2026 to enable reviewer reflection, discussion, and co-construction of resultant themes.

Phase 1: Each reviewer (KJEF, AFC, and CT) read the included articles in full prior to data extraction, individually noting observations, points of interest, and reflective questions. Phase 2: Each reviewer worked in a separate NVIVO file and independently coded the extracted data through an inductive approach using structural and in In Vivo coding [26]. Phase 3: After individual coding, the reviewers met twice as a group to discuss interpretations of the resulting codes, cluster codes and their meanings, and develop candidate themes. A whiteboard and poster paper were used as visual working aids to support the discussion. If we determined that a code was evident in more than one cluster, we discussed the meaning and rationale of the code as a group and determined whether it would fit under more than one theme. As each reviewer has a different personal and professional positionality, i.e., CT is a caregiver of children, AFC is a newcomer to Canada. Our aim for collaborative coding was to optimize the diverse lenses each reviewer brought to the data and to create space to challenge each other’s perspectives in the coding and theme development. See acknowledgements section for author’s positionality statements.

If we determined a code was evident in more than one cluster, we discussed as a group the meaning of the code, rationale for cluster positions, and determined if the code would fit under more than one theme, resulting in some instances of double-coding. Reviewers primarily took a semantic approach to thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [24], where themes were explicit and generated from the data, versus a latent approach in which themes are interpreted beyond the data content to underlying ideas, assumptions and ideologies. As a research team, we determined the semantic thematic approach best fit our research questions.

Phase 4: The reviewers met for a third discussion meeting to review the analysis work to date and collaboratively develop a final set of themes. Phase 5: All authors met for a final collaborative discussion to review and refine themes.

To compare changes in immunization facilitators and barriers over time, we divided data into subsets by year of publication into two categories: 1) Pre-COVID (years of data collection prior to 2021) and 2) Post-COVID (years of data collection of 2021 and onwards). The themes coded were visualized in hierarchical charts to view the differences in immunization facilitators and barriers before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. We used tree maps of the data to represent the number of text segments in our references for each code [27].

Results

There were 3335 references imported into Covidence software [22] for screening. After deduplication, 1099 studies were screened based on title and abstract. Ninety-four full-text studies were assessed for eligibility. Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded and are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). To minimize the likelihood of missing relevant articles in our search strategy, we hand-searched the reference lists of any systematic and scoping reviews that addressed our research questions to identify additional primary research articles. No additional primary research articles were identified. Thirty-three studies were included, encompassing a variety of study designs such as cross-sectional, cohort, and qualitative. Two of those studies were dissertations with an additional published entry in an indexed journal. We combined those studies as one single entry. Thus, 31 studies were included in this scoping review. Data extracted from included studies

Characteristics of included studies

The 31 included studies were published between 1985 and 2026 with studies published between 1980 and 1990 (n = 1), 1991–2000 (n = 0), 2001–2010 (n = 7), 2011–2020 (n = 13), 2021–2026 (n = 10), Twenty-six studies were categorized as pre-COVID-19 and 5 as post-COVID-19. Studies were conducted in Alberta (n = 8), British Columbia (n = 3), Ontario (n = 3), Quebec (n = 4) and Saskatchewan (n = 1). Three studies were multiprovincial, and nine were conducted nationally. Vaccines considered in the selected studies included Diphtheria, Tetanus, Pertussis, Polio, Haemophilus influenzae type B, Measles, Mumps, Rubella, Pneumococcal conjugate 15, Varicella, Rotavirus and COVID-19. Only one included study, Zareef et al. [58], focused solely on the COVID-19 vaccine (see Table 1).

Reported demographic factors were found to impact immunization uptake. Older parents (> 40 years of age) [38, 57, 58], higher income (>$75,000) [38, 42], and having children with medical conditions [58], were associated with higher vaccination rates. On the other hand, factors such as being a first-time parent [32], having more than 1 child in the family [28, 32, 35, 43, 55], younger parents (30–39 years of age) [58], income (<$50,000-$60,000) [55, 57, 58], being divorced, separated, widowed or single parent [46], were described as factors contributing to incomplete immunizations and lower intentions to immunize. Being immigrant parents was found to be a determinant associated with both increased and decreased immunization uptake [46, 55].

Discussion

The findings from our scoping review identified factors that support and create barriers to childhood immunization for families of children aged 0-6years in Canada. Over the course of childhood immunization literature, themes of factors that support and create barriers to childhood immunization have included cognitive reasoning, affective influences on decision-making, information and access to healthcare services. Overall, in Canada these themes have remained consistent over time (1985–2026).

Canada in the international landscape

Although we specifically examined Canadian literature, comparisons with other countries that have universal healthcare systems and publicly funded childhood vaccinations, such as the United Kingdom and Australia, show similarities in vaccine coverage rates as well as facilitators and barriers to childhood immunizations. For example, like Canada, the United Kingdom is also not achieved the optimal 95% coverage rate for childhood vaccines [68, 69]. A 2024 national survey of healthcare professionals, parents/carers, and insights from young people in the United Kingdom [69], reported similar immunization barriers found in Canada. Barriers in the United Kingdom included issues related to access to services - such as clinic locations and transportation - and mistrust in authorities, including the healthcare system and government.

Australia also shares several challenges to immunization uptake that are comparable to those observed in Canada. Although in 2020 Australia achieved just under a 95% coverage rate for all children up to five years of age, coverage rates have declined in the post-pandemic period in 2023 to around 93% [70]. In 2024 Australia conducted its first national survey of parents of children under five years of age to assess vaccination barriers Consistent with findings from Canada and the United Kingdom, reported barriers included access-related or practical challenges such as a lack of transportation, social influences (i.e., people close to them not supporting vaccination), and distrust of vaccine information provided by health professionals.

When comparing Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia, Australia reports the highest childhood vaccination coverage rates [4, 69, 70]. It is noteworthy that, unlike Canada and the United Kingdom, Australia has implemented national policy measures aimed at promoting immunization [70]. Since 2016, the “No Jab No Pay” policy has required children to meet immunization requirements for families to be eligible for the Family Tax Benefit with no exemption for conscious objectors [71–73]. In addition, some Australian states have implemented “No Jab No Play” policies, which require children to be immunized to attend childcare facilities [71]. Although these policies were not explicitly identified as facilitators in the literature, the presence of public policies mandating childhood vaccination likely contributes to higher immunization coverage rates. More broadly, evidence suggests that vaccine mandates are associated with increased vaccination uptake and support the achievement of high coverage rates [62].

Post-COVID-19 considerations

There is a consistent challenge across countries to meet the target of 95% of childhood immunization coverage rates and Canada is not alone in the work to improve coverage rates in the post-COVID-19 era [4, 69, 70]. As vaccination programs attempt to navigate these post-COVID challenges and the complexity of vaccine hesitancy, additional research on strategies that support immunization uptake are areas for future work [69, 70, 74, 75]. On an international scale, the very definition of vaccine hesitancy from the World Health Organization (WHO) Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) on immunization (2022) has evolved. SAGE has updated the definition from 2014, which focused on the “delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services” [74], to develop a framework that includes a more robust group of factors that are measurable, potentially changeable, and specific to vaccination [74].

The WHO Behavioural and Social Drivers of Vaccination Framework includes factors such as thinking and feeling, social processes, motivation, and practical issues that contribute to vaccine uptake [74]. These are similar factors to themes identified in our review, i.e., cognitive reasoning, affective influences on decision-making, and access to healthcare services. As immunization programs attempt to improve coverage rates, the WHO Framework enables the breakdown of contributing factors to better understand context and, potentially, develop community-driven strategies to improve vaccine uptake [74]. The WHO (2022) approach is supported as a future research area in Canada that aims to examine the intersecting individual, societal, and structural factors that impact vaccine uptake, and to draw on community-based perspectives to inform vaccine programs and policies [75]. Contextual differences require targeted research to inform and evaluate provincial, territorial, and population specific strategies to improve coverage rates.

Although the amount of data and subsequent post-COVID-19 literature was limited, it appears that factors that support success and create barriers to childhood immunization have remained consistent. There were a few notable differences between the pre- and post-COVID-19 periods to highlight. Our review suggested factors shifting positionality in their influence on families and caregivers. Two areas noted in our findings were affective influences on decision-making– trust and relationships and access to healthcare services.

Trust and relationships

Trust has been an important factor in affective influence on decision-making to support childhood immunization and families accessing immunization services. For instance, caregiver participants reported that they trusted the information from healthcare providers, healthcare professionals, and the government to inform their decision to immunize [29, 41]. Conversely, mistrust is a prominent barrier in in affective influences on decision-making [32, 54]. For example, recent articles from our review illustrated that study participants lacked trust in the government, healthcare systems (including providers), the information shared with the public, and the pharmaceutical industry [32, 54]. Reasons for mistrust included biased information from government officials and a lack of transparency [36].

A contributing factor to mistrust in affective influences on decision-making may be the polarized landscape related to immunization and vaccines, which became emphasized during the COVID-19 pandemic. Polarization remains an ongoing challenge that includes the current politicization of vaccine hesitancy in the form of vaccine-hesitant agendas, mis/disinformation, and leveraging vaccines for political agendas [76]. Building relationships and fostering trust with families and communities in a politically charged climate poses a great challenge. A challenge that immunization scholars recommend can only be addressed through strategies at all levels, from individuals, such as caregivers, to systems such as healthcare [76]. Although political vaccine hesitancy is acknowledged as not a new problem that crosses international borders [76], it may be among the most challenging as we move forward with efforts to improve vaccine uptake and improve immunization coverage rates.

Although mistrust is a challenge, it is worth noting that a supportive relationship with healthcare providers remains to be an important influencer of immunization uptake post-COVID-19 [36, 53, 77]. For example, a qualitative study conducted in Northern Alberta with parents of under-immunized children highlighted the importance of a positive relationship with the healthcare provider, which increased the likelihood that parents would immunize their children [53]. Given the importance of relationships with providers and the existing mistrust parents and caregivers convey in current literature, strategies to build relationships with healthcare providers are essential [36, 53].

However, multiple system-level challenges in Canada impede relationship-building. For example, there is a gap in access to primary care providers [78], and with no consistent provider, building a trusting relationship is difficult. Furthermore, in the current landscape of public health services and primary prevention, there appears to be a gap in relationship-building opportunities. For example, schools were a primary entry point into the community, enabling relationship-building between public health nurses and families and supporting primary prevention strategies such as childhood immunization [79]. However, outside of providing immunization services [80] schools are one avenue where nurses have been removed, losing an important relationship-building opportunity with caregivers, families, and communities [79]. It is challenging to determine where and how relationships are currently being built to strengthen connections with families and, thus, build trust and relationships to support vaccine uptake. With fewer opportunities to build relationships and increase trust between families and healthcare providers, it is unclear how to foster trust, address misinformation, and improve immunization uptake.

Challenges accessing healthcare services

From our findings, one notable childhood immunization barrier reported in the post-COVID-19 era is the overall challenge accessing healthcare services, specifically immunization services. Although routine immunization services were disrupted during COVID-19, access challenges appear to persist. Service challenges related to the COVID-19 pandemic included disruption of routine immunization services, clinic closures, families unable to get an immunization appointment, and deployment of the workforce [30]. These challenges seem to continue with scheduling conflicts and long wait times for immunization services reported as specific access barriers in both rural and urban settings [36, 53]. Although reported by family participants in the literature, there does not appear to be a context-specific examination of how immunization services are offered or any changes that have occurred pre-to-post-COVID-19 pandemic, e.g., fewer available appointments, reduced services. With every province/territory delivering childhood immunization programs differently, specific access issues may need to be investigated within each service context.

Prenatal information gap

From our scoping review, information timing, type of format, and clarity were identified as barriers to immunization. Specifically related to timing, families reported an immunization information gap in the prenatal period [36, 77]. Families expressed specific information needs prenatally, including the recommended immunization schedule, the rationale for immunizations, potential side effects, risks of vaccine-preventable diseases to a child’s health, and the benefits and risks of vaccination [36, 77]. As the routine infant immunization schedule begins at 2 months of age, immunization information is provided in the postpartum period [81]. However, study participants identified the postpartum period as too overwhelming and not an ideal time to introduce immunization [36].

Family participants identified that the relationship with the healthcare provider provided a safe space to introduce childhood immunizations in the prenatal period [77]. Yet, several potential barriers exist to obtaining immunization information from a trusted healthcare professional. A major focus of prenatal discussion is on immunization eligibility for the prenatal person versus immunizations for the baby post-birth. For example, the Family-Centred Maternal and Newborn Care: National Guidelines in Canada contain a robust section on maternal vaccination/immunization, i.e., tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis, as a recommended practice in the prenatal period [82]. Although these guidelines also recommend that healthcare providers begin immunization discussions, pregnant persons report a gap in information on childhood immunization when transitioning to parenthood [82]. Given the shortage of primary care providers that provide prenatal healthcare and the limited time with patients [83, 84], it may not be possible to develop a trusting relationship and cover all topics, including immunization.

Prenatal classes may be another immunization information access point for caregivers, but not all families attend them or access classes online. Prenatal classes may also come with a fee that prohibits attendance. For example, in Saskatchewan, there is a $50.00 fee for classes in-person or online [85]. Evidence indicates that caregivers formulate their intentions to immunize their children in the prenatal period [77]. Gaps in information and barriers to these discussions at this important interval in immunization intention could contribute to reduced vaccine uptake.

As the intention to vaccinate may form in the prenatal period [36], this is an ideal time to share immunization information and initiate conversations with families. Additional research would be helpful to determine the current practice of prenatal immunization counselling in Canada from both the family and provider perspectives; the barriers to providing the counselling; and potential strategies to fill this gap, both within the provider domain and through the wider system level. Primary care providers such as family physicians, midwives, nurse practitioners, and public health nurses, form part of the prenatal care team [82]. These healthcare providers are in a prime position to contribute to research in this area to identify challenges and help develop solutions to address the prenatal information gap.

Colonial institutional practices

Within our scoping review, three studies focused on Indigenous Peoples (see Supplemental Material - Appendix A), specifically First Nations Peoples [37, 52, 56]. These studies identified challenges rooted in Canada’s colonial history including mistrust of government systems, intergenerational trauma, and overall negative healthcare experiences among Indigenous Peoples [37, 52, 56].

Although only a limited number of studies were specific to childhood immunization in Indigenous populations, there is substantial Canadian healthcare documenting how colonial institutional practices - often described as Western practices – create barriers to accessing health services, result in poor treatment during care, and contribute to disproportionate adverse health outcomes [86].

In our review, colonial institutional practice were illustrated in MacDonald et al. [52], who partnered with a First Nations community in Alberta to examine clinic practices alongside caregivers’ and nurses’ experiences with childhood vaccination. This study found that institutional processes, such as the one child - one appointment model, did not accommodate the immunization of siblings brought to the health centre. Appointment times were not flexible to include additional children, requiring families to return another day. Colonial institutional practices such as the exclusion of family members like siblings, have been found in maternal-child literature as a barrier for Indigenous families to access health promotion programs [87].

Although the MacDonald et al. study [52] was specific to a First Nations community, the one-child, one-appointment framework is common in immunization service delivery, illustrating that these challenges reach beyond a single community. Indigenous families may access services in any community and experience these challenges. This particularly relevant giving the growing Indigenous population in Canada, especially in urban centres [88]. As more Indigenous Peoples reside in urban areas, they increasingly access provincially and territorially governed healthcare services, including childhood immunization. Colonial practices embedded within these systems continue to create immunization barriers and negatively impacting the health of Indigenous children and families [89].

Institutions have a responsibility as outlined in the Truth and Reconciliation Calls to Action #22 [86], to create change within the existing healthcare system. A report prepared by Halseth for the National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health [56], provides a comprehensive overview of promising practices to support vaccination among Indigenous populations. These include building relationships and trust between providers and consumers; involving Indigenous leadership, information strategies; and holistic, wraparound, and culturally appropriate approaches. However, further research is needed to determine if these recommendations are implemented in practice and better understand the current experiences of Indigenous families accessing provincial and territorial immunization services. This includes the current landscape of immunization service delivery; identifying strengths and areas for improvement; and understanding overall family experiences. Such research would help inform current practices that may be working well and ones that could be enhanced and responsive to the TRC Calls to Action. Importantly, any service delivery or system change must be led by Indigenous community partners to foster change in a good way and support culturally safe immunization services [89].

Limitations

This scoping review was limited to Canada to maintain consistency of immunization programs, including universal, no cost to families, alike antigens. However, within Canada, there are differences across provinces and territories, as each jurisdiction determines its own childhood vaccine program and delivery [90]. Additionally, the majority of included studies were conducted in Alberta and Quebec, with no studies exclusively from the Atlantic provinces, the Northern territories, Nunavut, or the Yukon. Given these contextual variations and the limited number of studies from certain regions, the results of this scoping review may not apply to all areas of Canada.

The aggregated age group of children 0-6years of age, although present within the literature [29, 31, 37, 44, 47, 50, 51], may miss important nuances in facilitators and barriers that could be identified if age groups were stratified for example, between the infant primary series starting at two months and preschool/school-entry booster doses administered between four and six years of age. Furthermore, our review spans over multiple decades of changes and updates to immunization schedules in Canada. However, given the disparity in the number of papers published between decades, a more detailed analyses on immunization dynamics over time was not feasible. As such, our use of thematic analysis pre- and post-COVID periods may overlook important temporal variations within these periods. Moreover, given the imbalance of the number of papers pre-COVID and post-COVID, shifts in theme prominence could be overrepresented. In addition, there was no patient/family partner or Indigenous community inclusion in this scoping review. The interpretations of the data are limited to the research team.

The limitation in applicability also extends beyond Canada, as childhood immunization programs, healthcare systems, and service delivery vary widely across countries. Nevertheless, some findings from this review may be helpful to the broader international context of childhood immunization or areas for future research. Our search was limited to English studies, thus studies in other languages such as French might have been missed and might limit the findings of provinces with primarily French-speaking populations. Although we attempted a robust search of peer-reviewed articles, no time frame, and had expert advice from health science librarians, some articles may have been missed. Although resultant themes are reported and discussed as standalone, these are intrinsically connected factors and we suggest careful interpretation.

Conclusions

This scoping review provided an overview of the Canadian literature of factors from caregiver perspectives that support and create barriers to childhood immunization including cognitive reasoning, affective influences on decision-making, and information. We have highlighted persistent challenges in the post-COVID-19 era including trust and relationships and access to services. There appears an immunization information gap in the prenatal period and colonial institutional practices that create immunization access challenges specifically for Indigenous families. Additional research is needed to understand current immunization information practices in the prenatal period, the contextual nuances of immunization service delivery, and Indigenous Peoples’ experiences with immunization services. Further exploration of these areas may help to inform strategies to address identified barriers, strengthen immunization services, and improve immunization coverage rates.
