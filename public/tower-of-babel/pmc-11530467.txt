Induced hepatocyte-like cells derived from adipose-derived stem cells alleviates liver injury in mice infected with Echinococcus Multilocularis
Authors: Hui Liu, Xiaojuan Bi, Ning Yang, Xue Zhang, Bingbing Fang, Nuerbaiti Kusuman, Wenmei Ma, Jing Li, Jin Chu, Li Sun, Liang Li, Guodong Lü, Renyong Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccumulating evidence has shown that adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) have the potential to differentiate into hepatic lineages, which are ideal engraftments for tissue-engineered repair. In this study, we investigated the potential of transplanted induced hepatocyte-like cells (iHEPs) in treating hepatic alveolar echinococcosis and describe an efficient three-step protocol for the generation of iHEPs in vitro from ADSCs. The expression of hepatocyte lineage markers was assessed and iHEPs function was evaluated by Periodic acid-Schiff staining. iHEPs were intravenously transplanted into mice infected with Echinococcus multilocularis. Histopathological analysis and liver function tests were used to assess therapeutic effects. The iHEPs exhibit morphological features and a glycogen storage function similar to those of mature hepatocytes and demonstrate an upregulation in hepatic gene programs with increasing induction time. Following transplantation, iHEPs were observed surrounding the metacestode lesions in the liver parenchyma of E. multilocularis-infected mice. iHEPs transplantation effectively restored liver function and improved liver injury in the infected mice. Additionally, we observed significant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in the livers of infected mice transplanted with iHEPs. Our results provide evidence that iHEPs transplantation can alleviate E. multilocularis-induced liver injury, potentially creating new avenues for treating liver injury in end-stage hepatic alveolar echinococcosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77555-8.Keywords: Hepatocyte-like cells, Adipose-derived stem cells, Echinococcus multilocularis, Liver injurySubject terms: Parasite host response, Stem-cell differentiation

Introduction

Hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) is a fatal parasitic disease caused by persistent intrahepatic infection by Echinococcus multilocularis larvae. HAE distribution is confined to countries in the Northern Hemisphere. It is referred as ‘parasitic cancer’ due to its tumor-like features of infiltrative expansion1,2. The chronic form of HAE is usually asymptomatic, while the primary form, which involves the liver has a mortality rate of 75–90% within 10 to 15 years of diagnosis if untreated. Echinococcosis is a neglected tropical disease according to the World Health Organization (WHO)3,4. Infection by the larvae of E. multilocularis causes hepatic fibrosis, which destroys the normal liver parenchyma5–7. In advanced cases, metacestode lesions invade the bile ducts, portal veins, and hepatic veins, leading to loss of liver function or liver failure. Currently, liver transplantation or autologous liver transplantation is the only curative treatment for patients with end-stage HAE4,8,9. Primary hepatocyte transplantation is a viable alternative to organ transplantation for the treatment of various liver disorders and end-stage liver diseases10; however, acquiring a sufficient quantity of functional hepatocytes for transplantation remains challenging11.

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) that are a highly promising cell source for regenerative medicine and tissue repair. This is mainly due to their trilineage differentiation ability and lower therapeutic risk than induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)10. ADSCs can be easily obtained from adipose tissues via subcutaneous liposuction. Therefore, numerous ADSCs can be obtained using less traumatic surgical procedures12,13. Human ADSCs differentiate into hepatocytes14,15, known as human-induced hepatocytes (hiHEPs). hiHEPs are suitable candidates for hepatocyte regeneration and therapy for acute liver failure (ALF)16,17. ADSC-derived hiHEPs exhibit both periportal and perivenous functions, making them a potential option for treating region-specific liver cell damage and facilitating organ regeneration in both acute and chronic liver diseases18,19. Additionally, a bioartificial liver device loaded with hiHEPs improved liver functions and regeneration in pigs after hepatectomy and in extended liver resection in human patients11. However, the therapeutic potential of hiHEPs in HAE-induced liver injury has not yet been evaluated.

In this study, we isolated hADSCs from human adipose tissues and characterized their morphology, phenotype, and multi-differentiation potential. We established an efficient three-step protocol for the generation of induced hepatocytes (iHEPs) from hADSCs in vitro. Hepatocyte markers, specific genes and functional properties were analyzed using various methods. Finally, the therapeutic efficacy of the iHEPs was evaluated in an in vivo mouse model of chronic liver damage induced by E. multilocularis infection. This study developed a new auxiliary method for transplanting hepatocytes generated from stem cells to treat advanced HAE.

Methods

Ethics declarations

This study was approved by the institutional regulations of the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval No. K202110-18). This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Isolation, culture, and identification of hADSCs

Human subcutaneous adipose tissue was obtained from the Department of Cosmetic Plastic Surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University.Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers prior to participation and all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. A previously described ADSC isolation protocol was used20. Briefly, sterile adipose tissue was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, then digested with 1 mg/mL type I collagenase (Worthington, USA) at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Blood vessels and connective tissue were removed from the adipose tissue with sterile tweezers and then the mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and cells were seeded in cell culture dishes and grown in low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in an incubator with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was replaced every other day. Cells were digested and passaged with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) until a confluence of 80–90% was reached. First-passage cells were labeled as P1.

The cultured hADSCs were identified by flow cytometry analysis. Third passage (P3) hADSCs were digested with 0.25% trypsin and resuspended in pre-cooled PBS buffer at a concentration of 3 × 106 cells/mL. Aliquots of 1 × 106 cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min at 4 ℃ with fluorochrome antibody, including mesenchymal stem cell markers (CD29-PE, CD44-FITC, and CD90-PE), hematopoietic markers (CD31-FITC, CD34-PE, and CD45-FITC), and a major histocompatibility antigen (HLA-DR). The antibodies used in the experiments are listed in Table S1. The cell suspension washed with 400 µL PBS and detected by flow cytometry (FACS AriaII, BD, USA).

When cultured ADSCs reached 50% confluence, the growth medium was replaced with osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation medium (Weitong, Shenzhen, China) to induce either osteogenesis or adipogenesis and to determine the ability of ADSCs to differentiate into mesodermal cell types (adipogenic and osteogenic cells). The differentiation medium was changed once every 2–3 d according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Oil red O staining was performed 10 d after adipogenic differentiation and Alizarin Red staining was performed 21 d after osteogenic differentiation. Lipid deposition and calcium nodules were recorded using a phase-contrast microscope (Leica DM6000B; Germany).

Differentiation of hADSCs into iHEPs in vitro

ADSCs were differentiated into iHEPs using a previously established protocol21. Briefly, second (P2) and third passage (P3) ADSCs were digested with 0.25% trypsin and seeded on the cell culture plates at density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 and incubated in growth medium at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Hepatogenic induction was adopted as a three-stage differentiation protocol for in vitro differentiation of ADSCs into iHEPs. Briefly, when ADSCs reached 50% confluence, the medium was changed to hepatocyte differentiation medium (HDM) in which serum was removed and supplemented with cytokines according to induction stage. Activin AA and FGF-4 (20 ng/mL) were supplemented in the pre-induced stage (days 1–3); EGF (20 ng/mL), HGF (20 ng/mL), and ITS were supplemented in the differentiation stage (days 4–7); and OSM (40 ng/mL), dexamethasone (1 µmol/L), and DMSO (1‰) were supplemented during the maturation stage (days 7–21). The medium was changed every 3 d during the induction period. A schematic of the induction protocol is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Identification of iHEPs

Morphological changes in the iHEPs at specific differentiation stages were recorded using a phase-contrast microscope (DM6000B; Leica, Germany). The percentage of iHEPs was determined by counting the number of epithelial-like cells and the total number of cells within a specific field of view at 200× magnification.

To assess the hepatic function of iHEPs, media from undifferentiated (day 0) and differentiated cells (days 7, 17, and 21) were discarded. The cells were then washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Hepatic glycogen storage in ADSCs and iHEPs was visualized using a Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The glycogen-stained area was analyzed using Image Pro Plus software (version 6.0).

Hepatic-specific gene analysis for ALB, HNF-1B, Transferrin, ASGPR1, AAT, and MRP2 expression on different days (7, 17, and 21) was performed using quantitative real-time (qRT) PCR. Total RNA from undifferentiated (day 0) cells or iHEPs was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentration and purity were determined using the Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total RNA (1 µg) was reversed transcribed to single-stranded cDNA using the Prime Script RT reagent Kit (RR047A; Takara, Japan) to a final volume of 20 µL. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (RR820A, Takara, Japan) on an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Q6; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative mRNA expression of targeted genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method by normalization with GAPDH. Primer sequences used for this assay are listed in Table S2.

Immunofluorescence staining

P3 ADSCs or iHEPs at different stages were washed with pre-cooled PBS buffer and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Next, cells were permeabilized and incubated in PBS with 0.01% TritonX-100 for 5 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 60 min. Cells were incubated at 4 ℃ overnight with primary antibodies specific for mesenchymal stem cells (CD29, CD44, CD90, and CD105) and hepatocyte markers (ALB). The cells were than washed with PBS for three times and visualized with goat anti-rabbit or chicken secondary fluorescence conjugated antibodies (either Alexa Fluor 488 or 596). The antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining are shown in Table S1. Nuclei were visualized by DAPI (Sigma, USA) counterstaining. The cells were observed using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (SP8; Leica, Germany) and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.52a; NIH, USA).

Parasites and animal experiments

Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained on a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water at the Laboratory Animal Center of Xinjiang Medical University.

Grouping and treatment

Mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group) as follows: (1) Sham operation + vehicle injection (sham group); mice were injected with 200 µL of normal saline via the hepatic portal vein. (2) E. multilocularis infection + vehicle injection (Em group); mice were inoculated with protoscoleces (PSCs) in saline via the hepatic portal vein. (3) E. multilocularis infection + iHEPs treatment (Em + iHEPs group); mice were infected with E. multilocularis PSCs for 8 weeks, and 5 × 105 DiI-labelled iHEPs in 200 µL PBS was transplanted into the infected mice via the tail vein. Mice in the sham and Em groups were injected with 200 µL PBS via the tail vein. Four weeks after transplantation, mice in each group were sacrificed to collect liver and blood samples for further analysis.

Establishment of E. Multilocularis-infected mouse model

We isolated E. multilocularis PSCs from Mongolian gerbils naturally infected for 24 weeks and used them as sources of E. multilocularis, as previously described22. Briefly, PSCs were counted and adjusted to a concentration of 10,000 PSCs/mL after washing several times with PBS. Parasite viability was determined using eosin staining. Only PSC batches with 95% viability were used. Mice were gas-anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and kept under anesthesia during the procedure. PSCs in saline (200 µL; 2000 PSCs per mouse) were injected via the hepatic portal vein. The sham group was injected with an equivalent volume of saline.

Transplantation of DiI-labelled iHEPs into E. Multilocularis-infected mice

Cell labeling and tracking in the livers of E. multilocularis-infected mice have been described in our previous study23. Briefly, iHEPs were harvested and resuspended in DMEM supplemented with DiI (5 µg/mL) and labeled for 30 min in the dark at room temperature on a rotatory shaker at 110 rpm. The labeling efficiency and cell viability were assessed by fluorescence microscopy and trypan blue staining. DiI-labelled iHEPs (5 × 105 cells) suspended in 200 µL PBS buffer were injected into the tail vein of mice that were previously infected with EmPSCs for 8 weeks. Mice in the Em and sham groups were injected with 200 µL PBS. All mice were sacrificed by inhalation of pure CO2 at 4 weeks post-transplantation. Liver and blood samples were collected for further examination.

Detection of ALT and AST in serum

Blood samples were harvested and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and serum was collected to measure alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels using commercial detection kits (Mindray, Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological examination and immunohistochemical staining

The liver samples from the sacrificed mice were carefully checked for hepatic lesions as a preliminary assessment of the severity of E. multilocularis infection. The liver samples were weighed, and the area of hepatic parasitic lesions was measured and recorded in square millimeters2. Each liver sample was subsequently divided into two parts: liver samples allocated for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses, and liver samples frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA and protein extraction. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 4-µm thick sections. The sections were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess inflammatory cell infiltration and Masson or Sirius red staining to evaluate the liver fibrosis stage.

For frozen samples, the liver samples were cryoprotected with 30% sucrose solution and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h, then embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature compound (OCT, Leica, Germany) to prepare 10-µm thick cryosections using the CM1850 cryostatmodel (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized with water and Sirius red or Masson staining was performed to evaluate collagen deposition using a commercially available staining kit (G1340, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extent of fibrosis was analyzed at 200× magnification in three fields of view per section per sample. The area of positive staining was quantitatively analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0.0.260; Media Cybernetics, USA).

Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to a previously reported protocol24. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized and processed with citric acid buffer (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for heat-mediated antigen-retrieval. Sections were blocked with PBS containing 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature and incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-mouse PCNA, 1:2000; rabbit anti-mouse CD31, 1:50; and rabbit anti-mouse α-SMA, 1:1000) in PBS buffer. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit-HRP antibody for 30 min at 37°C. The 3, 3’ diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ab64238, Abcam) was then used to stain the sections according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using a light microscope (DM3000; Leica, Germany), and positively stained cells and microvessel density was analyzed at a magnification of 200× in three to five fields of view per section per sample by manual counting. The average optical density (AOD = IOD/Area) of α-SMA were quantified to determine the differences in each group. The antibodies used for immunohistochemical staining are listed in Table S1.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the mouse liver tissue of each group using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA was subjected to reverse transcription to form cDNA, and qRT-PCR was performed as previously described for the in vitro experiment (identification of induced hepatocytes). Primer sequences for target genes (β-catenin) are listed in Table S2.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins from the liver tissues were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF (Invitrogen). Proteins were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein lysate (20 µg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate and transferred to 0.22-µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After blocking membranes with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h. The proteins were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-β-catenin, 8480 S, CST, anti-GAPDH, ab181602, and Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L/HRP, ZB2301, and ZSGB-BIO; Beijing, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting reagent (BL520A; Biosharp, Anhui, China). We normalized the protein density data to GAPDH levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (version 8.0.1; CA, USA). The normal distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk (SW) normality test, with all samples yielding a P-value > 0.05. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to compare the means across three groups or more, with statistical significance set at *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

Functional characterization of the ADSCs-derived iHEPs

To induce functionally mature human iHEPs, we first generated primary hADSCs and confirmed that P0 and P3 ADSCs showed a spindle-like morphology (Fig. 1A), which demonstrates their adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential (Fig. 1B). Additionally, the presence of stem cell markers (CD29, CD44, CD90, and CD105), and the absence of hematopoietic stem cell markers (CD31, CD34, and CD45) and major histocompatibility protein (HLA-DR) were identified using flow cytometry, qRT-PCR analysis, and immunofluorescent staining (Fig. 1C, D). These results indicate that we isolated high-quality ADSCs with multilineage potential. We initiated the differentiation of ADSCs into iHEPs using a three-step, 21-day protocol (Fig. 2A). The iHEPs were characterized between 7 and 21 d post-induction. Phase-contrast microscopy images showed a transformation in the morphology of iHEPs from the spindle-like shape of ADSCs to the typical epithelial morphology of hepatocytes 14 d after induction (Fig. 2B). The percentage of iHEPs gradually increased as the induction time progressed (Fig. 2C). To identify whether iHEPs possess the functional characteristics of glycogen storage or hepatic gene expression, they were analyzed using PAS, immunofluorescence staining, and qRT-PCR. PAS staining showed that glycogen significantly increased during iHEPs differentiation on days 7, 14, and 21, but not in ADSCs on day 0 (Fig. 2B, D). On day 14, ALB protein expression increased markedly(Fig. 2B, E). The mRNA levels of hepatocyte-specific genes, including albumin (ALB), transferrin, hepatocyte nuclear factor-1B (HNF-1B), asialoglycoprotein receptor1 (ASGPR1) and multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2), were gradually increased during iHEPs induction on day 14 (Fig. 2F).

iHEPs can engraft in liver parenchyma and improve liver function in mice infected with E. Multilocularis

A schematic representation of the animal experimental setup used in this study is shown in Fig. 3A. The E. multilocularis-infected group (Em group) was used as a control for iHEPs treatment. Four weeks after transplantation, iHEPs were detected in the region surrounding the lesion in the livers of the Em + iHEPs group. Confocal images showed that DiI-labeled iHEPs migrated to the site of liver injury (Fig. 3B). Compared with the Em group, the volume of metacestode tissues observed in the liver tissue of the Em + iHEPs group decreased slightly, but was not statistically significant (Fig. 3C, D). Compared to the sham group, AST and ALT levels were considerably increased in the Em group; however, compared to the Em group, the serum levels of AST and ALT were significantly decreased (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E).

iHEPs transplantation attenuate the progression of liver fibrosis in mice infected with E. Multilocularis

H&E staining showed that the morphology of the liver tissue in the sham group was normal and the hepatic lobules were well arranged. Liver sections from the Em group showed severe infiltration of macrophages, lymphocytes, and fibrous myofibroblasts around the lesions with germinal layers. Meanwhile, both the cellular composition and thickening of the collagen layer in the Em + iHEPs group was considerable decreased (Fig. 4A). Quantification results of Masson’s trichrome and Sirius red staining in the Em group were dramatically increased compared to those in the sham group mice (Fig. 4A). However, the area of fibrosis in the livers of the Em + iHEPs group was significantly reduced (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B, C). Immunohistochemical staining showed that the expression levels of α-SMA were significantly higher in the liver tissues of the Em group than the sham group. However, compared with the Em group, α-SMA expression levels were significantly decreased in the Em + iHEPs group (Fig. 4D, E).

iHEPs transplantation facilitated proliferation of hepatocyte and microvessels in the liver of E. Multilocularis-infected mice

The number of PCNA-positive hepatocytes in the liver of the iHEPs transplantation group was significantly elevated (Fig. 5A, B) compared to that in the Em group. Moreover, neovascularization was mainly observed near the portal area in the livers of E. multilocularis-infected mice and the number of CD31 positive microvessels was significantly increased in the iHEPs group, compared to the Em group (Fig. 5A, C).

Discussion

iHEPs transplantation has great potential for treating chronic chemical-induced hepatic injury. However, it has not been evaluated for the treatment of chronic parasite-induced hepatic injury. In this study, we applied this technique to an HAE mouse model with severe chronic hepatic injury induced by the larvae of E. multilocularis. We found that iHEPs transplantation reduced liver injury in the HAE mouse model by activating the Wnt signaling pathway. These findings indicate that iHEPs transplantation has potential as an HAE treatment and may improve liver function in patients with HAE.

iHEPs differentiated from ADSCs have the same beneficial features of ADSCs, such as anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative functions, while preserving the function of hepatocytes14,15. In this study, we successfully induced iHEPs from ADSCs with weak immunogenicity. The iHEPs have a polygonal morphology and can express ALB, Transferrin, HNF-1B, ASGPR1 and MRP2 during iHEPs differentiation. In addition, iHEPs can store glycogen and secrete ALB. These characteristics and functions are typical of hepatocytes and similar to those of other pluripotent stem cell-derived hepatocyte-like cells25.

To track iHEPs after cell transplantation, they were labeled with DiI. We found that DiI-labeled iHEPs could migrate to the site of metacestode lesions in the livers of E. multilocularis-infected mice. ADSC-derived iHEPs reduced acute inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in liver tissue of a murine CCL4 model at 24 h post-injury, suggesting that they can mitigate acute inflammation26. The transplantation of iHEPs partially restored liver function in mice infected with E. multilocularis at an advanced stage, which indicates that iHEPs treatment facilitated the function of hepatocytes or reduced hepatocyte injury leading to decreased serum AST and ALT levels observed in the iHEPs group. Similar to the results of our study, transplantation of MSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells improved fulminant hepatic failure and protected against hepatic injury from CCl4-induced acute liver failure in mice27.

Liver fibrosis surrounding the metacestode lesion can damage the liver parenchyma, resulting in loss of liver function6. In this study, we found that iHEPs treatment suppressed the expression of α-SMA and collagen deposition and attenuated the progression of liver fibrosis in E. multilocularis-infected mice. Transplantation of iHEPs attenuated liver fibrosis in mice injected with CCl4 for 10 weeks19. The same effects were observed in our previous study, in which ADSC transplantation ameliorated E. multilocularis-induced liver fibrosis in mice by downregulating TGF-β1 and TGF-βR expression23. Therefore, we speculated that iHEPs transplantation could also be applied to attenuate the progression of liver fibrosis in HAE, similar to ADSCs transplantation.

Hepatocyte proliferation and angiogenesis are hallmarks of liver regeneration28,29. In this study, we found that transplanted iHEPs promoted hepatocyte proliferation and angiogenesis in the livers of E. multilocularis-infected mice. Hepatocyte transplantation promotes liver regeneration in mice with acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure30. Taken together, these results suggest that iHEPs transplantation mediates liver regeneration in chronic liver injury during E. multilocularis infection. In addition, Wnt/β-catenin signaling play crucial roles in liver regeneration31,32. In this study, we found that β-catenin expression, both at the mRNA and protein levels, was significantly upregulated in the liver after iHEPs transplantation. We hypothesized that iHEPs transplantation promotes proliferation of hepatocytes in individuals with an E. multilocularis infection and that it may be mediated by Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Some issues still require consideration and deserve further attention. First, some studies have shown that cytokines secreted by MSCs or iHEPs contribute to the recovery of the liver after acute or chronic injury33, such as hepatocyte growth factor34 and stromal-derived factor-135. Considering the particularity of echinococcosis, it is necessary to further explore the cytokines secreted by iHEPs in E. multilocularis-infected mice. Second, we found that iHEPs can improve liver function but do not affect the development of echinococcosis. Albendazole is clinically recommended to treat alveolar echinococcosis36. Thus, the combination of iHEPs cell transplantation with albendazole administration may effectively treat echinococcosis; however, this must be further explored. Finally, the duration of iHEPs survival in E. multilocularis-infected mice and their ultimate fate must be investigated.

Conclusions

iHEPs transplantation improved liver function, attenuated the progression of liver fibrosis, and promoted hepatocyte proliferation and angiogenesis in E. multilocularis-infected mice. In this study, we demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of iHEPs in an HAE mouse model. Our results suggest that iHEPs transplantation can be used as an adjunct therapeutic approach to improve liver function in patients with end-stage HAE.
