Spatial proteomic profiling reveals an association between NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling and immune cell infiltration in locally advanced triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Vasudeva Bhat, Emily Goebel, Carl Postenka, Anayra de Fatima Goncalves Santiago, Yang Wei, Muriel Brackstone, Alison L Allan, Armen Parsyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Spatial proteomics profiling reveals downregulation of NF1 protein levels in locally advanced TNBC patients compared to normal breast tissue.•Reduced NF1 is associated with immune hot tumors and immune hot regions within tumors, as well as activation of Ras/Raf/MEK signaling.•Downregulation of NF1 is associated with reduced disease-free survival in a treatment naïve TNBC patient cohort.•The majority of proteomic changes are conserved between primary breast tumors and the matched lymph node metastases.•NF1 protein levels could be used to stratify immune hot tumors into high and low risk groups.Keywords: Spatial proteomics, Biomarkers, Triple-negative breast cancer, TNBC, Neurofibromin type 1, NF1, Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality and morbidity in women worldwide [1,2]. It is a heterogeneous disease, where molecular classification largely depends on the expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [3]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) generally lacks expression of these biomarkers, limiting therapeutic options. It is considered particularly aggressive, treatment-resistant, and associated with poorer outcomes compared to other subtypes. TNBC is highly heterogeneous and can be further subdivided into clinically relevant subtypes [4,5]. These classifications highlight, among other factors, the importance of tumor-extrinsic factors, including the local tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), in contributing to TNBC heterogeneity [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Spatially distinct TIME can be used for TNBC stratification with potential implications for immunotherapy [7]. High levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in breast tumors, both before and after chemotherapy are associated with improved survival in early-stage TNBC [8,9] and confer survival advantages in patients with metastatic disease [8,10]. Increased TILs are a favorable prognostic factor for treatment response [11]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of TNBC pathogenesis, heterogeneity, and clinical vulnerabilities requires a granular understanding of both tumor-intrinsic factors and the surrounding microenvironment [6].

Spatial omics technologies provide a novel approach to investigate the molecular and cellular determinants of TNBC and to identify clinically actionable biomarkers and therapeutic targets [12]. Despite the established prognostic value of TILs, the molecular mechanisms governing their homing and spatial distribution within the TIME remain poorly defined in TNBC. In this study, we employed GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) to investigate potential molecular vulnerabilities in TNBC. We analyzed proteomic changes in primary breast tumors (BC) relative to normal breast (NB) and matched metastatic lymph node (LN) samples, and performed stratified analyses based on TIL levels both between and within primary tumors. This approach revealed multiple alterations in proteomic and immune cell marker profiles, highlighting the heterogeneity of TNBC. Notably, the tumor suppressor neurofibromin (NF1) was significantly downregulated in BC compared to NB and was associated with high TILs and correlated with poorer disease-free survival in our cohort. These findings provide a framework for understanding how tumor-intrinsic signaling may influence the spatial organization of the immune responses in TNBC.

Materials and methods

Clinical cohort characterization

Using the London Breast Tumor Biobank and clinical database, treatment-naïve patients with invasive ductal carcinoma TNBC patients diagnosed between 2014 and 2020 and treated at the St Joseph Health Care London and/or London Health Sciences Centre (London, ON, Canada) were retrospectively identified. These patients had metastases confined to the axillary lymph node, with no evidence of distant metastasis at diagnosis. All patients underwent upfront breast and axillary surgery (sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary lymph node dissection), followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. This cohort is unique, as patients are now routinely considered for neoadjuvant treatment. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained surgical pathology slides from primary breast tumor (BC), non-cancerous normal breast tissue (NB), and matched axillary lymph node metastases (LN) were retrieved. Archived H&E-stained primary tumor sections from original surgical resections were reviewed by a breast clinical pathologist (blinded to disease outcome) to determine average TIL percentage and identify regions with relatively high and low TIL percentage within each sample (see Supplementary Methods section for details on TIL assessment, tissue microarray generation and sample processing). The study was approved (#118021) by the Western University Research Ethics Board.

Disease-free survival analysis (DFS)

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for DFS and compared between groups using the two-sided log-rank test. Patients were stratified into high and low protein expression groups based on median cutoff (50th percentile) of the patient-level mean expression of PanCK+ (pan-cytokeratin positive to mark tumor/epithelial cells enriched areas) breast cancer compartment. Patients above the cutoff were classified as “high expression” and all others as “low expression” groups. Associations between expression groups and DFS was further evaluated using a Cox-proportional hazard models, with low-expression group as the reference. Hazard ratios (HRs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Wald p-values. Analyses were performed in R (survival (3.2-13) and survminer package (0.5.1)).

DSP data processing and analysis

Data were analyzed using the GeoMx Analysis suite. Quality control (QC) was performed using default settings, and data were normalized to positive hybridization controls to account for variability across the nCounter cartridge lanes. Following QC, PanCK+ and CD45+ (to identify immune cell enriched areas) marker data were normalized separately by scaling to the geometric mean of negative isotype controls, mouse IgG1, mouse IgG2a, and rabbit IgG to generate negative normalized values [13] (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Statistical analysis

The negative normalized values were then imported into RStudio (2024.04.2+764) for downstream processing and analysis. Linear mixed models (LMM) were used to determine the differences in biomarker levels in breast tumor and lymph node metastases relative to normal breast tissue using the Imer Test package (3.1-3) [14]. The EnhancedVolcano package (https://github.com/kevinblighe/EnhancedVolcano) was used to generate volcano plots [15]. The dplyr (1.1.4) and ggplot2 (v.3.5.1) packages were used to generate graphs. A false-discovery rate (FDR) of ≤0.05 was considered significant for LLM and a p-value ≤0.05 was considered significant for KM analysis.

Results

Pseudobulk analysis of protein abundance comparing normal breast tissue, primary breast tumor and matched lymph node metastasis

We first assessed protein abundance across NB, BC, and LN using pseudobulk analysis approach by aggregating data from both PanCK+ and CD45+ AOIs (areas of illumination) within each sample. Compared to NB, BC showed significant upregulation of apoptotic markers including BAD (Bcl-2-associated death promoter) (FDR<0.0005), cleaved caspase 9 (FDR<0.0004), and BCLXL (B-cell lymphoma-extra-large) (FDR=0.006), alongside increased phosphorylation of MEK1 (mitogen-activated protein kinase-kinase 1) (S217/S221) (FDR=0.0002), a critical component of Ras/Raf/MEK signaling. Tumor suppressor protein neurofibromin (NF1), a known upstream negative regulator of Ras/Raf/MEK signaling [16], was significantly downregulated in BC tissues, compared to NB (FDR=0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1D), together with fibronectin (FN1) (FDR<0.0001), phosphorylated PRAS40 (proline-rich Akt substrate 40kDa) (FDR=0.0003), GSK3A/GSK3B (glycogen synthase kinase 3 alpha/beta) (FDR=0.0002), and AKT1 (protein kinase B) (FDR=0.002) (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

Immune profiling revealed increased levels of CD8 (FDR=0.0002) and CD44 (FDR=0.0009) in BC relative to NB tissues, suggesting the presence of activated CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The dendritic cell marker CD11c was also significantly upregulated (FDR=0.0002), along with increased levels of markers for antigen presentation, such as beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) (FDR=0.0004) and HLD-DR (FDR=0.0002) (Supplementary Fig. 1D), and interferon pathway related genes, such as STING (stimulator of interferon genes) (FDR=0.0004) in BC. In contrast to the immune-activation protein signatures, several markers linked to immune regulation including BCL6 (B-cell lymphoma 6) (FDR=0.0005), FOXP3 (forkhead box P3) (FDR=0.003), the immune checkpoint protein, PD-L2 (programmed death ligand 2) (FDR=0.0011) and T cell costimulatory molecule CD80 (FDR=0.004), were significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Levels of the M2 macrophage marker, ARG1 (arginase-1) were significantly lower in BC (FDR=0.006) compared to NB (Supplementary Fig. 1D). There were no significant differences in protein abundance comparing BC to LN tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Overall, our pseudobulk analysis revealed potential activation of the NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling axis along with a partially activated immune state.

To further characterize functional relationships among differentially abundant proteins, we constructed a protein-protein interaction network, using STRING (v12.0) [17] and observed that the proteins were organized into two clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2A), a “kinase-signaling” cluster consisting of NF1, Ras/Raf/MEK, AKT, and GSK3B, and an “immune-markers” cluster. Using the TIMER3 [18] (Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 3) (Supplementary Fig. 2B) and UALCAN [19,20] (University of Alabama at Birmingham CANcer) (Supplementary Fig. 2C) web-based tools, analysis of breast cancer clinical database revealed lower NF1 transcript levels in breast cancer samples, particularly in TNBC, compared to normal tissue. To further ensure that the observed NF1 protein level represented a tumor cell intrinsic change, we verified area specificity by comparing the tumor/epithelial-cell enriched area (PanCK+) with the immune-cell enriched area (CD45+). As expected, NF1 abundance was observed within the PanCK+ area (Supplementary Fig. 2D), while most of the immune markers were confined to the CD45+ area (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Taken together, our analysis suggested a potential link between NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling and the activation of immune response.

NF1 downregulation correlates with potential activation of Ras/Raf/MEK signaling and shorter DFS survival

Next, we restricted our analysis to PanCK+ AOIs, which represent tumor/epithelial cell enriched areas. Consistent with the results from pseudobulk analysis, NF1 protein abundance was significantly lower in BC (FDR=0.01) as compared to NB (Fig. 1A&B). Low NF1 levels were inversely correlated with phosphorylation of MEK1 (FDR<0.0005) (Fig. 1A&C). Additionally, we detected significant increases in Ki-67, suggesting a highly proliferative phenotype (Supplementary Table 2). An increase in abundance of the anti-apoptotic protein BCLXL, and pro-apoptotic proteins, such as BAD (FDR=0.0001) and cleaved caspase9 (FDR<0.0001), was observed in BC. Phosphorylation of PRAS40 (FDR=0.0006), AKT1 (FDR=0.001), JNK (FDR=0.002), and GSK3A/GSK3B (FDR=0.001) was significantly lower in PanCK+ AOIs of BC compared to NB, suggesting dysregulation of key signaling pathways. There were no significant differences in the levels of proteins between BC and matched LN tissues, except for fibronectin, whose levels were significantly lower in BC (FDR<0.0001) as compared to NB, and further reduced in LN (FDR<0.00001) relative to BC (Fig. 1D&E, Supplementary Table 2).

We then assessed the clinical impact of differential protein abundance in primary breast tumor samples on disease-free survival (DFS). Our analysis revealed that lower levels of NF1 were significantly (p<0.029) associated with shortened DFS (Fig. 1F), suggesting that NF1 levels may serve as a predictor of disease recurrence in TNBC patients. Overall, this data highlights diverse changes in protein levels between breast cancer and normal tissues, with a notable observation of potential dysregulation of the NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling in TNBC.

As expected, in the immune cell enriched CD45+ AOIs, we observed a significantly higher presence of immune cell markers in BC as compared to NB, particularly higher levels of T lymphocytes, consistent with upregulation of CD3 (FDR=0.009) (Fig. 2A&C), CD4 (FDR=0.01) (Fig. 2A&D), and CD44 (FDR=0.03) (Fig. 2A&F) levels. Upregulation of the dendritic cell marker CD11c (FDR=0.007) (Fig. 2A&E), along with HLA-DR (FDR=0.0001) (Fig. 2A&G) and VISTA (V-domain Ig suppressor of T cell activation) (FDR=0.02) (Fig. 2A&H) was observed in BC. We also observed a significant decrease in abundance of neutrophil marker CD66b (FDR=0.01) (Fig. 2A&I), regulatory T cell marker FOXP3 (FDR=0.001) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2), T cell costimulatory molecule CD80 (FDR=0.01) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2), checkpoint protein PD-L2 (programmed death-ligand 2) (FDR=0,003) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2), T cell activation marker GITR (glucocorticoid-induced tumor necrosis factor receptor family-related protein) (FDR=0.01) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2), and M2 macrophage marker ARG1 (FDR=0.03) (Fig. 2A Supplementary Table 2). There were no significant differences in immune-related protein abundance between LN and primary BC tissues (Fig. 2B). Taken together, our analysis suggests that the tumor cell intrinsic changes in NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling axis may influence changes in TIME.

Stratified analysis of patients with “immune hot” and “immune cold” tumors reveals distinct changes in NF1 and immune cell profile

A high degree of variability in TILs is often observed in TNBC patients, which significantly impacts therapy outcomes, particularly with immunotherapy [21,22]. To understand the comprehensive relationship between the tumor proteome and local TIME, we first stratified our entire cohort into patients with “immune hot” versus “immune cold” groups based on the average percentage of TILs in BC (Supplementary Methods) and analysed proteomic profiles in PanCK+ and CD45+ areas. Compared to NB, “immune hot” tumors demonstrated increased levels of IDO1 (indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1) (FDR=0.04) (Supplementary Table 3), decreased levels of immune-checkpoint proteins, such as PD-L2 (FDR=0.001), and LAG3 (lymphocyte-activation gene 3) (FDR=0.005). Decreased abundance of proteins participating in various key signaling cascades, such as phosphorylated p38-MAPK (T180/Y182) (FDR=0.002), JNK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase) (FDR=0.001), and AKT (FDR=0.0009) (Fig. 3A) were observed. Additionally, significantly lower levels of fibronectin (FDR<0.0001) along with ARG1 (FDR=0.04) and CD34 (FDR<0.0001) were observed. Consistent with results described above, NF1 was significantly downregulated in PanCK+ areas in the “immune hot” tumors (FDR=0.04) (Fig. 3A) and accompanied by increased levels of phosphorylated MEK1 (FDR=0.0006) (Fig. 3A). Comparing data from the “immune cold” tumor vs NB, we observed elevated levels of BCLXL (FDR=0.02), MET (MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase) (FDR=0.04) and phosphorylated MEK1 (FDR=0.0005), but not in NF1 (Fig. 3C). There were no statistically significant differences in protein level in the “immune hot” patient group comparing BC versus LN (Fig. 3B).

In the corresponding immune-rich CD45+ area of BC, we observed the increased presence of T lymphocytes in both the “immune hot” and “immune cold” patient groups, evidenced by increased CD3 protein levels. We also observed increased levels of T cell activation marker CD44 and dendritic cell marker CD11c (Fig. 3E and 3G). Additionally, HLA-DR levels were consistently upregulated in the primary tumor of both patient groups (Fig. 3E and 3G). However, several immunoregulatory and myeloid markers such as CD66b (FDR=0.0006), FOXP3 (FDR=0.001), GITR (FDR=0.008), CD80 (FDR=0.004), CD163 (FDR=0.01), BCL6 (FDR=0.004), and ARG1 (FDR=0.04) levels were specifically downregulated in “immune hot” tumors as compared to NB (Fig. 3E). Specific to “immune cold” patient group, levels of B7-H3 (FDR=0.02), VISTA (FDR=0.001), STING (FDR=0.01), and p53 (FDR=0.02) were significantly higher in BC as compared to NB (Fig. 3G). In LN tissues, contrary to “immune hot” tumors, levels of fibronectin (FDR=0.02), CD66b (FDR=0.006), B7-H3 (FDR=0.02), and FAP (fibroblast activation protein) alpha (FDR=0.01) were significantly lower as compared to BC (Fig. 3H).

Stratified analysis by the TIL levels within various areas of the primary tumor

We next focused our analysis on primary tumors with areas of heterogenous infiltration by TILs (“immune hot” and “immune cold” areas within the same tumor (n=14)), to assess intratumoral variability in protein abundance (Fig. 4A). Consistent with results described above, we observed that downregulation of NF1 levels (FDR=0.003) was spatially restricted to “immune hot” vs “immune cold” areas. In “immune hot” areas of BC, tumor cells showed not only an increase in phosphorylation of MEK1 (FDR=0.001) but also significant increase in IDO1 (FDR=0.03), BCLXL (FDR=0.006), and B2M (FDR=0.02) levels as compared to NB (Fig. 4A). Conversely, the matched “immune cold” areas from the same patients had no significant change in NF1 levels, although MEK1 phosphorylation remained elevated (FDR=0.001) (Fig. 4C). Comparison between the “immune hot” areas in BC and matched LN suggested that LN environment appeared to be less immunosuppressive with decreased abundance of PD-L1 (FDR=0.024), CD40 (FDR=0.027), FAPɑ (FDR=0.031), IDO1 (FDR=0.031), B2M (FDR=0.032) and CD14 (FDR=0.032) (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in protein abundance in LN compared to “immune cold” areas in BC (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, fibronectin level was consistently low in both “immune hot” and “immune cold” areas of the tumor (Fig. 4A&C). We observed increased levels of pro-apoptotic cleaved caspase 9 and BAD proteins in both “immune hot” and “immune cold” areas (Fig. 4A&C). Taken together, our analysis reveals that loss of NF1 is associated with “immune hot” areas within the tumor and has the potential be a clinically actionable protein marker for TNBC.

In the immune-cell-enriched (CD45+) “immune hot” areas of BC, compared to NB, we observed significantly higher presence of T lymphocytes, evident from increased levels of CD3 (FDR=0.0005), CD4 (FDR=0.003), and CD8 (FDR=0.004) proteins. Additionally, levels of dendritic cell marker CD11c (FDR=0.0004), along with HLA-DR (FDR=0.0003), B2M (FDR=0.003), and STING (FDR=0.048) were significantly increased in BC (Fig. 4E). Simultaneously, these regions showed significant decrease in CD66b (FDR=0.008), FOXP3 (FDR=0.0001), PD-L2 (FDR=0.003), CD80 (FDR=0.02), and ARG1 (FDR=0.02) (Fig. 4E). No statistically significant differences in immune cell-related protein profile were observed in LN as compared to BC (Fig. 4F). In “immune cold” areas of BC, there was increase in the presence of CD4 (FDR=0.03) cells along with abundance of CD44 (FDR=0.01) and HLA-DR (FDR=0.003) as compared to NB (Fig. 4G). Whereas levels of CD34 (FDR<0.00001), and BCL6 (FDR=0.02) proteins, along with NF1 (FDR=0.03), were significantly low (Fig. 4G). Comparing the “immune cold” areas of LN with BC revealed that the fibronectin (FDR=0.04) level was significantly lower (Fig. 4H). Overall, our analysis further strengthens our observation that low NF1 level is associated with increased TILs in “immune hot” areas within the tumor.

Discussion

Spatial proteomic analysis of cancers, including TNBC, remains a frontier in precision oncology [23,24]. Using GeoMx DSP, we profiled a targeted immune-oncology panel in treatment-naïve TNBC and matched LN metastases, revealing pronounced spatial and intra-tumoral heterogeneity. NF1 protein, an upstream regulator of canonical oncogenic signaling cascades such as Ras/Raf/MEK and PI3K/AKT, was consistently downregulated, particularly in “immune hot” tumors and regions within heterogenous TILs. This was also associated with increased MEK1 phosphorylation, which supports a role of NF1 loss in driving Ras/Raf/MEK pathway activation in TNBC and local immune responses.

NF1 loss can activate Ras/Raf/MEK signaling across cancers [16,25]. Despite the low frequency of activating mutations in pathway components [26], dysregulation is commonly observed in TNBC, suggesting regulation by multiple factors [27,28]. MEK1/2 phosphorylation activates ERK1/2, downstream signaling [29]. NF1 gene mutation and downregulation are frequent in TNBC patients [30,31] and germline NF1 mutations are associated with increased breast cancer risk and adverse clinico-pathological features [32,33]. In our cohort, NF1 downregulation in BC correlated with increased MEK1 phosphorylation, suggesting NF1 loss as a driver of Ras/Raf/MEK pathway-mediated proliferation. MEK1 activation can inhibit AKT signaling and vice versa [34,35]. Similarly, inhibition of GSK-3 activates the MEK/ERK pathway [36]. Consistent with this, reduced AKT and GSK3ß phosphorylation, alongside increased MEK1 phosphorylation, indicates a potential Ras/Raf/MEK-mediated feedback inhibition on other signaling pathways. Reduced NF1 levels were associated with decreased DFS in univariate analyses, suggesting that NF1-low tumors may represent a higher risk subgroup. This association requires further validation in larger cohorts adequately powered for a multivariate analysis.

Although NF1 has been implicated in breast cancer progression [25], its association with the immune microenvironment remains unclear. We observed an association between NF1 levels and TILs. While high TIL levels are generally linked to improved survival in TNBC [37,38], the “immune hot” tumors and “immune hot” regions within the tumors were enriched for a NF1-low state. At the same time low NF1 levels were associated with shorter DFS. This suggests that NF1 levels may further stratify TIL-high tumors into distinct high and low risk groups. The Ras/Raf/MEK signaling has been shown to influence immune phenotypes through tumor-intrinsic signaling affecting antigen presentation, cytokine signaling, and immune checkpoint regulation [39]. Future studies are needed to determine whether the NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK axis is associated with specific immune cell states and whether this relationship varies across tumor regions.

Previous studies have linked MEK activation to reduced TILs recruitment in TNBC [40]. In contrast, we observed elevated phosphorylated MEK1 in “immune hot” areas, indicating that Ras/Raf/MEK activation can co-occur with high TILs. Increased MEK1 phosphorylation was also observed in low-TIL areas without corresponding NF1 reduction, suggesting that Ras/Raf/MEK activation may arise through multiple mechanisms beyond NF1 depletion [41]. These patterns highlight the complexity of Ras/Raf/MEK signaling within the TIME and underscore the need for further mechanistic studies. Such insights may inform combination strategies using Ras/Raf/MEK pathway inhibitors along with immunotherapy to treat TNBC. Accordingly, our findings support further investigation of NF1 and Ras/Raf/MEK signaling, highlighting NF1 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

A fundamental question in cancer biology is whether the tumor cells require a proteomic “switch” to survive outside of the primary site. Across our targeted panel, BC and matched LN showed broadly conserved proteomic features, supporting the possibility that early lymph node metastasis may retain key molecular signatures from the primary site rather than requiring a major proteomic “switch”. This observation was consistent with previous observations in head and neck cancer and small cell lung carcinoma [42,43], although it should be viewed cautiously given the cohort size and a limited number of proteins included. Interestingly, a limited subset of markers, including PD-L1, CD40, FAPɑ, IDO1, B2M, and CD14 were significantly low in LN tissues compared to “immune hot” areas within the tumor. Notably, several of these proteins are immune-related, indicating that the metastatic lymph node displays an immunosuppressed phenotype. It also suggests that the tumor intrinsic changes and the microenvironmental factors required to elicit antitumor immunity may be diminished in LN, warranting further investigation.

One of the notable observations was that fibronectin (FN1) was downregulated in BC compared to NB, with further reduction in LN compared to BC. These findings suggest that FN1 might participate in the clonal evolution and metastatic escape of TNBC to the LN. This observation contrasts previous reports that FN1 acts as a pro-tumorigenic protein in TNBC [44]. Bulk omics studies often report elevated FN1, likely reflecting stromal contributions. These findings may highlight the importance of cell-area-specific spatial profiling when interpreting extracellular matrix markers. Additionally, FN1 has also been described as a tumor suppressor [45], suggesting a context specific role that warrants further investigation. Furthermore, PI3K-AKT signaling has been shown to regulate FN1 expression in tumor cells [46]. In our analysis, reduced AKT phosphorylation may have contributed to the decreased FN1 levels.

Our spatial analysis also identified heterogeneity within the tumors. Immune cell enriched (CD45+) areas in “immune hot” tumors are areas within the tumors were characterized by abundant T cell (CD3, CD4, CD44) and antigen-presenting cell (HLA-DR, CD11c) markers, along with apoptotic markers including cleaved caspase 9 and BAD, suggesting activation of immune cells and subsequent activation-induced apoptotic priming. However, we also observed low abundance of FOXP3, ARG1, CD80, PD-L2, and CD66b suggesting a reduced immunosuppressive environment. In contrast, “immune cold” tumor and areas within the tumor retained residual immune component, evident from increased abundance of HLA-DR, CD11c, and apoptotic markers. Taken together, our analysis highlights the spatial and functional compartmentalization of immune cells, with NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling and immune engagement varying in a context-dependent manner.

Overall, our spatial proteomic analysis identified that NF1 protein levels are significantly reduced in “immune hot” TNBC tumors and “immune hot” areas within the tumors, exhibiting TIL heterogeneity, and are associated with reduced DFS. Furthermore, the association of low NF1 with increased MEK1 phosphorylation suggests that the NF1-Ras/Raf/MEK signaling axis may serve as a candidate biomarker for risk stratification and therapeutic combination strategies. Future studies with larger cohorts, multivariable survival modeling, and functional validations are required to clarify the role of the NF1/Ras/Raf/MEK signaling axis in shaping immune cell function and to determine whether NF1 abundance predicts sensitivity to Ras/Raf/MEK targeted interventions.

(A) Schematic of GeoMx DSP workflow. (B) Representative immunofluorescence images showing Areas of Illumination (AOIs) in normal breast (NB), primary breast tumor (BC) (“immune hot” and “immune cold”), and lymph node metastasis (LN), stained with anti-PanCK and anti-CD45 antibodies to identify epithelial/tumor cell and immune cell enriched areas. (C) Correlation heatmap of housekeeping proteins (Histone H3, S6, GAPDH), isotype controls (mouse IgG1, mouse IgG2a, rabbit IgG), AOI surface area and AOI nuclei count. (D&E) Volcano plots of significantly abundant proteins (FDR≤0.05) comparing NB vs BC (D) and LN vs BC (E).

Supplementary Figure 2

(A) Protein-protein interaction network showing associations of NF1 with proteins in the immune-oncology panel. (B) Box plot shows reduced NF1 transcript levels in breast cancer patient samples compared to normal analysed using the TIMER3 web-based tool. (C) TCGA analysis using the breast cancer clinical dataset showing reduced NF1 transcript levels in TNBC subtype compared to normal. (D&E) Volcano plots of significantly abundant proteins (FDR≤0.05) in PanCK+ areas (D) and CD45+ areas (E).
