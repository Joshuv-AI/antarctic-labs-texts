Synthesis of δ-bismuth oxide stable at room temperature for use in industrial applications
Authors: J. Camilleri, P. Zaslansky, N. Ramanan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The current research reports an inexpensive and effective method of synthesising δ bismuth oxide stable at room temperature by reacting monoclinic α-Bi2O3 and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite at 100 °C. The resultant product was washed, dried and characterized using optical imaging, microstructural analysis by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), thermographic assessment (TGA), X-ray diffraction (XRD), infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) and synchrotron XRD. The interaction with sodium hypochlorite resulted in a change in bismuth oxide microstructure and a colour change from yellow to dark brown/black. TGA showed a change in mass and colour with reversal at ~ 450 °C. Further analysis of bismuth oxide reacted with sodium hypochlorite revealed a stable δ-phase at room temperature with reformation of the α polymorph at 500 °C. The δ-bismuth oxide was modelled to show a defective fluorite structure similar to that proposed by Sillen. The current research is the first to report a cheap and effective method of stabilising the δ phase which is useful for a number of industrial processes such as an oxide ion conductor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33146-9.

Introduction

Materials with high oxide-ion conductivities are used in various industrial processes and require extensive stabilization pathways. The challenge for the chemist is to design a solid material that will allow oxide-ion conduction at a low enough temperature to be technically useful1. The conventional ones have a defective fluorite structure and are stable only at high temperatures, thus have limited use2.

The oxide-ion electrolyte is the main functional component of solid oxide cells and defines the chemistry and the working temperature of the system3. High working temperatures would invoke unwanted chemical reactions with the adjacent components, causing performance degradation.

The most used systems include yttrium oxide stabilized zirconia-based materials, doped cerium oxide, perovskites such as doped lanthanum gallate, Dion-Jacson type layered perovskites and hexagonal perovskites. The operating temperatures are usually between 700–1000 °C2.

The zirconium oxide is stabilized with yttria, scandia, calcia and ceria and adopts a distorted fluorite-type structure. The atomic displacement parameters of cations and anions increase with temperature, which indicates the thermal vibrations and dynamic positional disorders4. Zirconium oxide used as a solid-oxide fuel cell transitions from monoclinic to tetragonal, cubic and liquid phase with a rise in temperature. The doping stabilises and lowers the temperature of transformation with an increase in the oxygen vacancy concentration and an enhancement of the oxygen-ion conductivity5.

Perovskite and related oxides also exhibit properties making them useful as oxide ion conductors and are classified into structural groups namely AMO3 perovskite-type, AMO3-related, hexagonal perovskite-related AO3, and modular structures such as Dion-Jacobson6. A and M represent large and small cations. Their specific properties allows oxygen transfer making them very useful for various industrial processes7–9. Hexagonal perovskite-related oxides are composed of hexagonal close-packed layers or sequences of hexagonal and cubic close-packed layers with oxygen-deficient layers formed for both hexagonal and cubic layers10,11. The hexagonal perovskite-related oxides are used as electronic, proton, and mixed oxide-ion and electronic conductors.

Bismuth oxides exist in various polymorphs including monoclinic , tetragonal , body-centered cubic , face centered cubic , orthorhombic , triclinic , and high-pressure hexagonal phase12. These polymorphs can be synthesised at different temperatures and only the α-phase is stable at room temperature13. The δ-phase bismuth oxide is stable at higher temperatures (730–825 °C)14 but can be formed at room temperature under certain conditions including the formation of thin films by magnetron sputtering15 and doping with smaller rare earth cations16,17. The dopants act by suppression of the oxygen ordering and deviation18. Even when doped, δ-phase bismuth oxide still suffers from conductivity decay at lowered temperatures.

A reduction in operating temperature of bismuth-based ion conductors has been reported with bismuth vanadate with the formula Bi0.913V0.087O1.587 which had the highest oxide ion conductivity at 500 °C. This phase adopted a pseudocubic long-range ordered fluorite- structure, imparting stability with respect to transformation to a lower-symmetry lower-conductivity phase19. This phase is the only singly substituted δ-bismuth oxide-based material with conductivity comparable unstable Bi0.85Pr0.105V0.045O1.5457 and Bi12.5La1.5ReO24.5 before the conversion to a stable tetragonal form and an associated drop of conductivity. The oxide ion dynamics in this structure are due to a slower diffusion process through the Bi–O sublattice and a faster process which corresponds to more localized dynamics of the oxide ions within the vanadium oxide coordination spheres20.

There is currently no literature showing the synthesis and characterisation of δ Bi2O3 stable at room temperature. The current work demonstrates an inexpensive method for this synthesis.

Results

Scanning electron microscopy

Images of the control α-bismuth oxide (C) and the bismuth oxide mixed with sodium hypochlorite at 100 °C (B100) demonstrate a change in grain microstructure with finer grain boundaries seen at 100 °C, yet with smaller particle sizes (Fig. 1a). The analysis of the particle size and perimeter undertaken using ImageJ is shown in Fig. 1b. The total area of the control C was 327.8 µm2 with an average perimeter of 0.81 μm. The B100 had a total area of 212.6 µm2 and an average perimeter of 0.57 μm. The control also exhibited agglomeration as indicated by the image analysis software.

Thermographic analysis

Thermographic analysis (Fig. 2) reveals a distinct change in mass at ~ 400 °C which was more marked in B100 compared to the control α-phase (C). The change in mass was also accompanied by a visible colour change. The control also revealed a reduction in the weight loss. This could be attributed to the decarbonisation with heat.

Discussion

Interactions of bismuth oxide and sodium hypochlorite have been reported in Dentistry whereby the bismuth oxide in dental materials comes in contact with sodium hypochlorite used clinically as an antimicrobial agent. The main clinical concern was shown to be the change in colour and ensuing tooth discolouration21–23. These changes in colour were observed over a period of time at lower temperatures, and a range of phases were identified and were temperature dependent. The current interaction of bismuth oxide with sodium hypochlorite at 100 °C enabled the formation of the δ-phase without the other intermediary phases.

Various methods were used in the current research to investigate the changes observed. The combination of the methods allowed a complete analysis of the structural and chemical changes. Bismuth oxides polymorphs have been well characterised showing the changes at different temperatures13. The XANES spectra for δ-Bi2O3 were simulated using the FDMNES code24–27. The simulations were performed using the Finite Difference Method with self-consistent features. The current pattern obtained compared well to the simulated data for the structure reported by Sillen27 which transitions from the α-phase at 730 °C. The cubic structure was reported to be a fluorite structure with ordered defects in the oxygen sub-lattice. Further research28 on δ-Bi2O3 confirmed the calcium fluorite type structure with an oxygen defective lattice and random occupation of oxygen sites. The high oxygen conductivity supports the oxide ions in the oxygen lattice sites that move throughout the bismuth sub-lattice. A more complicated vacancy structure in which the oxygen atoms also deviate from their ideal sites in the fluorite structure is implied29. The structure is a combination of < 110 > and < 111 > vacancy arrangements30. The < 111 > orientation is preferable for pure cubic phases as suggested in the Sillen research27. The simulated XANES (using a cluster radius of 6 Å) used in the current study differs a lot for each of the defective CaF2-type structures. Further research is necessary to understand better the structure of the δ-phase that has been synthesised.

The current study reports the formation of δ-Bi2O3 at 100 °C used in combination with sodium hypochlorite. The phase was stable at room temperature. This is the first report to show this stable δ-phase at room temperature. Furthermore, the reversal of the δ-phase to α-phase at 500 °C has also not been reported previously. The process used is cheap and effective. Only one time point has been reported. This time point was chosen to match that of previous experiments that were undertaken at lower temperatures that yielded other phases22,23. Lowering of temperature of transformation has been reported for doped bismuth oxide with the vanadium oxide addition18. The room temperature structure of this compound has been shown to be composed of two sublattices, one of isolated VO4 tetrahedra, and the bulk of the material made of a network of corner and edge-sharing OBi4 tetrahedra with intrinsic oxide vacancies similar to δ-Bi2O318.

Previous research synthesising BiFeO3 determined the crystallite size of bismuth oxide phase at 450, 500, 550 and 600 °C resulting in 17.7, 21.1, 38.0 and 60.9 nm respectively31. The current crystal sizes were in the range synthesised at 550 and 600 °C. For the Bi-Fe phase synthesized in the previous research31, the particles were agglomerated. This was not shown in the current study where agglomeration was observed in the control as indicated in the analysis of the scanning electron micrograph but not the B100 which was the stable δ-phase bismuth oxide at room temperature.

Further research on the optimization of the process of formation of the δ-phase and characterisation of the product is necessary. The δ-phase at high temperature has been reported to be an optimal oxide ion conductor. Conductivity experiments will enable the verification of the use of the newly synthesised δ-phase stable at room temperature and its suitability as an oxide ion conductor.

Conclusions

The use of sodium hypochlorite enabled the formation of δ bismuth oxide to be synthesised and stabilised at lower temperatures. This phase showed a similar crystal structure to that proposed by Sillen. Its use as an oxide ion conductor should be investigated further.

Methods

Bismuth oxide (C; 223891, Sigma Aldrich, Gillingham, Kent, UK) reacted with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Cerkamed, Stalowa Wola, Poland) at 100 °C for 24 weeks. To enable the maintenance of temperature without evaporating the solution, a condensation set-up was used whereby the evaporated liquid was cooled and recycled back into solution. The experimental set-up is shown in Fig. 6. After 24 weeks, the solution was discarded and the powder retrieved, washed under running water and dried in a desiccator using silica gel as desiccant. The resultant product will be referred to as B100.

Experimental set-up for the production of δ bismuth oxide consisting of a round bottomed flask on a magnetic stirrer with a water condenser to maintain the temperature of the sodium hypochlorite at 100 °C without evaporation throughout the experimentation.

Scanning electron microscopy

C and B100 were embedded in cold-cure epoxy resin (Epoxy-fix, Struers, Ballerup, Denmark) and the surfaces were polished with an automatic polishing machine (Buhler, Lake Buff, USA) using MD Piano 250, 500 and 1200 grit diamond discs under water coolant (Struers, Ballerup, Denmark) and polishing cloths – MD Largo, Dac, Nap (Struers) with ascending grades of diamond-impregnated polishing liquids 9 μm, 3 μm and 1 μm (Struers). The specimens were imaged under low pressure 60 Pa in a Phenom XL at 10–15 kV at 5 K and 15 K magnification. The images were analysed using ImageJ software and the perimeter and total area of the particles was thus measured.

Thermogravimetric analysis

C and B100 were assessed using thermogravimetric analysis/differential scanning calorimetry, (TGA/DSC; Mettler-Toledo, Beaumont Leys, Leicester, UK) at a temperature range of 40–800 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C min− 1 and N2 flow rate of 40 ml min− 1. Based on the TGA analysis, colour changes were observed at 450 °C for B100 and 2 additional groups were created for further testing (B100-300 and B100-500). These groups were prepared by heating B100 to 300 and 500 °C respectively.

Optical microscopy

All the specimens (C, B100, B100-300, B100-500) were imaged and photographed with a Nikon camera to record the colour change of the hypochlorite-treated materials at different temperatures compared to the untreated bismuth oxide (C).

Data acquisition and analysis

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy

Two to five milligrams of the samples were mixed with 100 mg potassium bromide (Acros Organics, 99+%, IR grade). Pellets were formed between dry stainless-steel plates at 1 MPa and retrieved after at least 30 s standing. FT-IR was performed (Spectrum II, Perkin Elmer) in transmittance mode over the range 400 ~ 4000 cm− 1, with a resolution of 2.0 cm− 1 and using 80 scans per sample.

Phase analysis

Phase analysis of all specimens was performed by X-ray diffraction using a D8 Advance (Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation at 40 mA and 45 kV over 10–60° 2θ, stepping 0.02° per 0.6 s.

Furthermore, a Pilatus area detector was used to measure X-ray diffraction from the samples at 13 keV x-ray energy. XRD data were calibrated using the XRD spectrum of a Si pellet measured at 13 keV X-ray energy.The XRD spectra for data acquired using the Bragg-Brentano geometry and also from a 2D Pilatus area detector at the B18 beamline at Diamond Light Source, was evaluated by search-match software (EVA, Bruker) compared against Powder Diffraction File (PDF) data (International Centre for Diffraction Data, Newtown Square, PA, USA). Furthermore, the EVA software was used to individually fit peaks to determine FWHM (full-width half maximum). X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS)

Calculated amounts of each sample (C, B100, B100-300, and B100-500) were mixed with boron nitride using a mortar and pestle to form a homogeneous mixture and pressed into pellets. The pellets were loaded onto 3D-printed sample racks and mounted on beamline B18 at Diamond Light Source, UK33 A Si (111) double crystal monochromator was used for energy selection. The incident and transmitted beam intensities were measured using ionization chambers. Energy calibration was performed using a Bi metal foil. Bi L3 edge (13.419 keV) XAFS spectra were measured in transmission mode on all the samples, and also on relevant Bi reference standards (Bi metal, Bi(OH)3, NaBiO3) for comparison. Another sample of B100 was assessed after storage at room temperature for 2 years to assess its stability. The X-ray Absorption Near Edge Structure (XANES) spectra of Bi compounds at the L3 edge were characterized by three main features: A, B and C. Feature A is due to the 2p3/2→ 6s electronic transition, features B and C are due to 2p3/2→ 6d transition34.
