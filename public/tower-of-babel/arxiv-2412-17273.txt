The Hydrodynamic Limit of Neural Networks with Balanced Excitation and Inhibition
James MacLaurin, Pedro Vilanova

Abstract
The theory of ‘Balanced Neural Networks’ is a very popular explanation for the high degree of variability and stochasticity in the brain’s activity. We determine equations for the hydrodynamic limit of a balanced all-to-all network of 2 ​ n 2n neurons for asymptotically large n n . The neurons are divided into two classes (excitatory and inhibitory). Each excitatory neuron excites every other neuron, and each inhibitory neuron inhibits all of the other neurons. The model is of a stochastic hybrid nature, such that the synaptic response of each neuron is governed by an ordinary differential equation. The effect of neuron j j on neuron k k is dictated by a spiking Poisson Process, with intensity given by a sigmoidal function of the synaptic potentiation of neuron j j . The interactions are scaled by O ( n − 1 / 2 ) O(n^{-1/2}) , which is much stronger than the O ⁡ ( n − 1 ) O(n^{-1}) scaling of classical interacting particle systems. We demonstrate that, under suitable conditions, the system does not blow up as n → ∞ n\to\infty because the network activity is balanced between excitatory and inhibitory inputs. The limiting population dynamics is proved to be Gaussian: with the mean determined by the balanced between excitation and inhibition, and the variance determined by the Central Limit Theorem for inhomogeneous Poisson Processes. The limiting equations can thus be expressed as autonomous Ordinary Differential Equations for the means and variances.

We consider the following simple ‘balanced stochastic network’ model, motivated by the model of Van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky

excitatory neurons, and
n\gg 1

Although Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk considered sparse networks, we limit ourselves to studying all-to-all networks. The model is of a hybrid nature (also termed a Piecewise Deterministic Markov Process
[

,
15

[
43
]

,

,

,

The scaling of the interaction is different from the standard

The reason that the dynamics does not blowup is that each neuron receives a balance of excitatory and inhibitory inputs which approximately cancel out. This strong scaling requires the development of new techniques to make sense of the high-dimensional limit.

To obtain a hydrodynamic limit, we make use of the fact that the ‘balanced state’ is strongly attracting. This effectively damps down the
O(n^{-1/2})
terms that could cause blowup, and we are left with the fluctuations about the mean
[

: the variance of the system evolves analogously to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic process, and the mean activity is determined by the strong interactions pulling the system towards a balance of excitation and inhibition.

There are several other recent works that have explored the effect of inhibition on mean-field interacting particle systems. Erny, Locherbach and Loukianova consider interacting Hawkes Processes in the diffusive regime (so that interactions are scaled by
(n^{-/2})

consider a system of Hawkes Process in the balanced regime; the chief difference from our paper is that in their paper the noise is afferent on the neurons, rather than the synaptic connections. Duval, Lucon and Pouzat
[
25
]
consider a network of Hawkes Processes with multiplicative inhibition. Their work differs from this one insofar as the noise is directly afferent on the neurons, and the effect of one neuron on another scales as
O(n^{-1})
.

It is also worth comparing these equations to the ‘spin-glass’ dynamical models

However, in the balanced model of this paper, individual neurons are either purely excitatory, or purely inhibitory. Indeed, the hydrodynamic limiting equations are different (one can compare the limiting equations of this paper to for instance the equations in

Model Outline

For
\beta\in\{e,i\}
(i.e. ‘excitatory’ or ‘inhibitory’) and

count the number of spikes from neuron

\displaystyle u^{j}_{\alpha,t}

\displaystyle=u^{j}_{\alpha,0}+\int_{0}^{t}-\tau_{\alpha}^{-1}u^{j}_{\alpha,s}ds+C_{\alpha e}n^{-1/2}\sum_{k\in I_{n}}Z^{jk}_{\alpha e}(t)-C_{\alpha i}n^{-1/2}\sum_{k\in I_{n}}Z^{jk}_{\alpha i}(t)

(1)

where
\{C_{\alpha\beta}\}_{\alpha,\beta\in\{e,i\}}
are non-negative constants indicating the relative effects of excitation and inhibition.

The system is Poissonian
[
14
]
. It is thus assumed that there are propensity functions
f_{\alpha\beta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that the probability that there is a spike on the
(\alpha,k)\mapsto(\beta,j)
synapse (for
\alpha,\beta\in\{e,i\}
) over the time interval
[t,t+h]
(
h\ll 1
) is

\displaystyle hf_{\beta\alpha}(u^{k}_{\alpha,t})+O(h^{2}).

(2)

The solution may be represented using the following time-rescaled formulation
[
5
]
. Let

\{Y^{jk}_{\alpha\beta}(t)\}_{j,k\leq n\fatsemi\alpha,\beta\in\{e,i\}}
be independent Poisson counting processes, and define

\displaystyle Z^{jk}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=Y^{jk}_{\alpha\beta}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}f_{\alpha\beta}(u^{k}_{\beta,s})ds\bigg)

(3)

We underscore the fact that, in this model, the stochasticity is afferent on the synaptic connections, rather than the neurons themselves. This corresponds to the view that the dominant source of stochasticity in high-dimensional neural networks is synaptic transmission failure.

Notation

The topology on the space of measures is always that of weak convergence

]

\mathbb{E}^{\mu_{n}}[f]\to\mathbb{E}^{\mu}[f].

We endow

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{t}=\sup_{s\leq t}\big|x_{s}\big|.

(4)

For two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{C}([0,t],\mathbb{R})\big)
, the Wasserstein Distance is written as

\displaystyle d(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\xi}\bigg\{\mathbb{E}^{\xi}\big[\left\|x-y\right\|_{t}\big]\bigg\}

(5)

where the infimum is over all probability measures
\xi\in\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{C}([0,t],\mathbb{R})^{2}\big)
, such that the law of

is
\mu
, and the law of

is
\nu
.

3
Assumptions

We start by making some basic assumptions on the regularity of the propensity functions.

Hypothesis 1
.

It is assumed that for all
\alpha,\beta\in\{e,i\}
,
z\mapsto f_{\alpha\beta}(z)
are globally Lipschitz functions. It is assumed that there is a constant
C_{f}
such that for all

and all
\alpha,\beta\in\{e,i\}
,

\displaystyle 0<f_{\alpha\beta}(x)\leq C_{f}.

(6)

Typically the intensity functions are sigmoidal (smooth, bounded, and monotonically increasing), but we do not necessarily need to make this assumption
[
27
]
.

Thus far, we have not yet made sufficient assumptions that ensure that the system does not blow up in finite time as
n\to\infty
. One option would be to make various assumptions on the propensity functions that guarantee that for all time, the synaptic activity is with high probability below some threshold independent of
n
(in fact we do this in Section ). However, for the moment we avoid making stringent assumptions on the propensity functions, and instead we seek to determine which parts of the phase space are stable (in the limit as
n\to\infty
). We term this part of the phase space the ‘balanced manifold’. Our main theorem concerns the prediction of the dynamics as long as it stays near the balanced manifold (predicting the dynamics away from the balanced manifold is more difficult and will be pursued in a subsequent paper).

Intuitively, the balanced manifold is such that (i) the net excitatory inputs throughout the system are almost identical to the net inhibitory inputs throughout the network, and (ii) the manifold is locally stable (as long as the fluctuations are Gaussian). To this end, let us first define the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle F_{e},F_{i}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle F_{e}(v_{e},v_{i},K_{e},K_{i})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(C_{ee}\rho(K_{e},x)f_{ee}(v_{e}+x)-C_{ei}\rho(K_{i},x)f_{ei}(v_{i}+x)\big)dx

(8)

\displaystyle F_{i}(v_{e},v_{i},K_{e},K_{i})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(C_{ie}\rho(K_{e},x)f_{ie}(v_{e}+x)-C_{ii}\rho(K_{i},x)f_{ii}(v_{i}+x)\big)dx\text{ where }

\displaystyle\rho(K,x)=

\displaystyle J_{v}(v_{e},v_{i},K_{e},K_{i})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\partial_{v_{e}}F_{e}(v_{e},v_{i},K_{e},K_{i})&\partial_{v_{i}}F_{e}(v_{e},v_{i},K_{e},K_{i})\\

Hypothesis 2

Write the empirical measure of initial conditions

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e}=n^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{n}}\delta_{u^{j}_{e,0}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i}=n^{-1}\sum_{j\in I_{n}}\delta_{u^{j}_{i,0}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}).

\displaystyle C_{ee}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ee}(v_{e}+x)d\gamma_{e}(x)-C_{ei}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ei}(v_{i}+x)d\gamma_{i}(x)

(111)

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{i}(v_{e},v_{i},\gamma_{e},\gamma_{i})=

\displaystyle C_{ie}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ie}(v_{e}+x)d\gamma_{e}(x)-C_{ii}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ii}(v_{i}+x)d\gamma_{i}(x).

(112)

and we make the decomposition

q^{n}_{e}(t)F^{n}_{e,t}-q^{n}_{i}(t)F^{n}_{i,t}=q^{n}_{e}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{e}(\bar{v}_{e,t},\bar{v}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big)\\
+q^{n}_{i}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{i}(\bar{v}_{e,t},\bar{v}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big)\\
+q^{n}_{e}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big)\\
+q^{n}_{i}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big),

(113)

noting that (by definition of the dynamics in Lemma
4
)

\hat{F}_{e}(\bar{v}_{e},\bar{v}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})=\hat{F}_{i}(\bar{v}_{e},\bar{v}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})=0.

Let the Jacobian
J:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be such that

\displaystyle J(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\partial_{v_{e}}\hat{F}_{e}(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})&\partial_{v_{i}}\hat{F}_{e}(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})\\
\partial_{v_{e}}\hat{F}_{i}(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})&\partial_{v_{i}}\hat{F}_{i}(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i}),\end{array}\right).

The Intermediate Value Theorem implies that

q^{n}_{e}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e},v^{n}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})-\hat{F}_{e}(\bar{v}_{e},\bar{v}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})\big)\\
+q^{n}_{i}(t)\big(\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e},v^{n}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})-\hat{F}_{i}(\bar{v}_{e},\bar{v}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})\big)=\vec{q}(t)^{T}J(\tilde{v}_{e},\tilde{v}_{i},\mu_{e},\mu_{i})\vec{q}(t)

(116)

where for some
a\in[0,1]
,
\tilde{v}_{e}=a\bar{v}_{e}(t)+(1-a)v^{n}_{e}(t)
and
\tilde{v}_{i}=a\bar{v}_{i}(t)+(1-a)v^{n}_{i}(t)
.

Now, since the balanced manifold is by definition attracting, for the constant
\zeta_{t}
defined just after Lemma
4
,

\vec{q}(t)^{T}J\big(\bar{v}_{e,t},\bar{v}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t}\big)\vec{q}(t)\leq-\zeta_{t}\big(q_{e}(t)^{2}+q_{i}(t)^{2}\big).

Now the map
(v_{e},v_{i})\mapsto J(v_{e},v_{i},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})
is differentiable, which means that as long as
q_{e}(t)^{2}+q_{i}(t)^{2}
is sufficiently small it must be that the real parts of the eigenvalues of

J(\tilde{v}_{e},\tilde{v}_{i},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})
are less than or equal to
-\zeta_{t}/2
. We thus find that as long as
q_{e}(t)^{2}+q_{i}(t)^{2}
is sufficiently small (which is ensured by taking
\epsilon_{T}
sufficiently small), it must be that

\vec{q}(t)^{T}J\big(\tilde{v}_{e,t},\tilde{v}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t}\big)\vec{q}(t)\leq-\frac{\zeta_{t}}{2}\big(q_{e}(t)^{2}+q_{i}(t)^{2}\big).

(117)

For the other terms in (
113
), the definition of the Wasserstein Distance implies that there exists a constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\big|\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{e}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big|

\displaystyle\leq c\big(d_{W}\big(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\mu_{e,t}\big)+d_{W}\big(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{i,t}\big)\big)

(118)

\displaystyle\big|\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t})-\hat{F}_{i}(v^{n}_{e,t},v^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{e,t},\mu_{i,t})\big|

\displaystyle\leq c\big(d_{W}\big(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{e,t},\mu_{e,t}\big)+d_{W}\big(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{i,t},\mu_{i,t}\big)\big).

(119)

We can now conclude that the Lemma holds.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

We have determined equations that describe the hydrodynamic limit of high-dimensional ‘balanced’ interacting particle systems. Two of the important novelties in our model are that (i) noise is directly afferent on the synaptic connections (as those who postulate that spike-transmission failure is the dominant source of noise in the brain) (ii) the effect of one neuron on another scales as
(O(n^{-1/2}))
, (iii) neurons are either excitatory of inhibitory. Since the landmark work of Sompolinsky and Van Vreeswijk
[
57
]
this paradigm has been extremely popular in the theoretical neuroscience community, but the literature on rigorous mathematical proofs is comparatively underdeveloped.

An interesting aspect of our results is that the system-wide activity only concentrates (in the large
n
limit) upto the time that it leaves the balanced manifold. An intriguing further research direction would be to construct an example where this happens and try to understand what will happen afterward. One would expect an abrupt and discontinuous change in the neural activity. Such breakdowns of the balance between excitation and inhibition have certainly been conjectured in the theoretical neuroscience community.

In our numerical simulations, the functions
\{f_{\alpha\beta}\}_{\alpha,\beta}
are sigmoidal, with a relatively small gradient. This small gradient implies that the convergence to the hydrodynamic limit is relatively slow as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, writing
\bar{\zeta}_{t}=\inf_{s\leq t}\zeta_{s}
(and recalling that
-\zeta_{t}
is the real part of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian at the balanced manifold at time
t
, typically
\zeta_{t}\ll 1
for sigmoidal firing rate functions). Our proof demonstrates that

\displaystyle\big|v^{n}_{e}(t)-\bar{v}_{e}(t)\big|+\big|v^{n}_{i}(t)-\bar{v}_{i}(t)\big|\simeq O\big(n^{-1/2}\bar{\zeta}_{t}^{-1}\big).

(126)

Thus even for large
n\sim 10^{4}
(as in our numerical experiments) the stochastic fluctuations are still substantial. In future work, we will determine higher-order approximations that will be accurate for smaller
n
.

Appendix: Bounds on Counting Process Fluctuations

Let
y(t)
be a unit-intensity counting process,
n
a positive integer, and define

\displaystyle w(t)=y(nt)-nt.

(127)

Lemma 12
.

For all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\leq T}\big|w(t)\big|\geq x\sqrt{n}\big)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{4T}\bigg).

(128)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\leq T}\big|w(t)\big|\geq x\sqrt{n}\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\leq T}w(t)\geq x\sqrt{n}\big)+\mathbb{P}\big(\inf_{t\leq T}w(t)\leq-x\sqrt{n}\big).

(129)

Starting with the first term on the RHS of (
129
), thanks to Doob’s submartingale inequality, for any constant
b\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\leq T}w(t)\geq x\sqrt{n}\big)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\big(bw(T)-b\sqrt{n}x\big)\bigg]

(130)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(nT\big(\exp(b)-b-1\big)-b\sqrt{n}x\bigg)

(131)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(b^{2}nT-b\sqrt{n}x\bigg)

(132)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{4T}\bigg),

(133)

after substituting
b=x/(2\sqrt{n}T)
. We analogously find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\inf_{t\leq T}w(t)\leq-x\sqrt{n}\big)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{4T}\bigg)

(134)

Corollary 13
.

For any
b>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(bn^{-1}\sup_{t\leq T}\big|w(t)\big|^{2}\bigg)\bigg]=1.

(135)

Define the modulus of continuity
\phi_{\epsilon}:\mathcal{C}([0,S],\mathbb{R})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
to be

\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}(w)=\sup_{0\leq s,t\leq S\;:\;|s-t|\leq\epsilon}\big|w(s)-w(t)\big|

(136)

Lemma 14
.

For any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\overline{\lim}}n^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j\in I_{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(w^{j})\geq\delta n\bigg)=-\infty.

(137)

Proof.

Thanks to Chernoff’s Inequality, for any
b>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j\in I_{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(w^{j})\geq\delta n\bigg)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(b\sum_{j\in I_{n}}\phi_{\epsilon}(w^{j})-b\delta n\bigg)\bigg]

(138)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(b\phi_{\epsilon}(w^{1})-b\delta\bigg)\bigg]^{n}

(139)

Now for any
b>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\big(b\phi_{\epsilon}(w^{1})\big)\bigg]=1.

(140)

Hence by taking
b
to be arbitrarily large, we obtain the Lemma.
