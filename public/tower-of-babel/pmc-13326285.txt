Adherence to the Mediterranean diet and its association with atherogenic indices and lipid profile in individuals seeking a weight-loss dietary program: what role for body fat?
Authors: Francesca Menichetti, Silvia Gallosti, Ramona De Amicis, Federica Sileo, Andrea Foppiani, Giorgia Pozzi, Simona Bertoli, Alberto Battezzati, Alessandro Leone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Mediterranean diet is widely recognized for its cardiovascular benefits, but its specific effects on atherogenic indices remain unclear, particularly in individuals seeking a weight-loss dietary program, where excess fat mass may mitigate the diet’s protective effects.MethodsTo explore this relationship, a cross-sectional study was conducted involving 10,286 participants enrolled in a weight-loss dietary program. Anthropometric and biochemical data were collected, and adherence to the Mediterranean diet was assessed using the 14-item Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener questionnaire. Lipid profiles, including total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides, were analyzed to calculate the following atherogenic indices: atherogenic index of plasma, Castelli risk indices I and II, lipoprotein combine index, atherogenic coefficient, and atherogenic combined index.ResultsMultivariate linear regression models, adjusted for sex, age, body mass index, smoking, physical activity, sociodemographic factors, and use of lipid-lowering medications, showed that each one-point increase in the Mediterranean diet adherence score was significantly associated with reductions in the atherogenic index of plasma (− 0.003, 95%CI: 0.006, − 0.000), the atherogenic coefficient (− 0.013, 95%CI: − 0.024, − 0.001), Castelli risk index I (− 0.013, 95%CI: − 0.024, − 0.001), and the atherogenic combined index (− 0.004, 95%CI: − 0.007, − 0.000), with a marginal association observed for Castelli risk index II (− 0.009, 95%CI: − 0.019, 0.000). Significant associations were also observed for total cholesterol (− 3.453 mg/dl, 95%CI: –5.911, –0.995), LDL (− 3.225 mg/dl, 95%CI: –5.402, –1.048), and HDL-C concentrations (+ 0.255 mg/dl, 95%CI: 0.098, 0.412). However, except for HDL, these associations lost statistical significance after adjusting for body fat percentage. Significant interactions between Mediterranean diet adherence score and body fat percentage were observed for several atherogenic indices, including the atherogenic coefficient, Castelli risk index I, Castelli risk index II, and the atherogenic combined index as well as for total cholesterol and LDL, suggesting that the protective effects of the Mediterranean diet diminish as fat mass increases.ConclusionsAlthough adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with more favorable lipid profiles and atherogenic indices, these benefits are modulated by body composition, particularly fat mass. These findings highlight the importance of integrated dietary strategies that combine nutritional quality with body fat reduction to support cardiovascular prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02960-z.

Introduction

In 2021, the global number of cardiovascular disease (CVD) cases had reached 523 million, nearly doubling from 271 million in 1990 [1]. This gradual yet substantial increase has made CVD the leading cause of morbidity, disability, and mortality worldwide [2]. According to the European Society of Cardiology, CVDs account for approximately 45% of all deaths in Europe and 34% of total mortality in Italy [3]. In addition to the serious human consequences, there is a significant economic burden: current estimates place the indirect annual costs of $237 billion in 2015, with projections rising to $368 billion by 2035 [4].

CVDs are multifactorial conditions resulting from a complex interplay of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors [1]. A key pathophysiological mechanism underlying CVD is atherosclerosis, a progressive process characterized by the buildup of plaques in arterial walls that can begin as early as childhood. Although initially asymptomatic, atherosclerotic plaques may eventually grow large enough to impair blood flow, leading to major clinical outcomes such as coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, and aortic atherosclerosis which collectively represent the four major manifestations of CVD [5].

Traditionally, the assessment of atherosclerotic risk has relied on clinical history and standard lipid parameters such as total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides (TG). Although these markers remain fundamental for cardiovascular risk stratification, they may not fully capture the complexity of lipid-related atherogenesis [6]. In recent years, several composite lipid parameters, collectively known as atherogenic indices (AI), have been proposed to provide a more integrated view of lipid-related risk [7]. These indices, including the Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), Castelli’s Risk Indices (CRI-I and CRI-II), the Atherogenic Coefficient (AC), the Lipoprotein Combine Index (LCI), and Atherogenic Combined Index (ACI) combine multiple lipid variables into single measures. Emerging evidence suggests that AI may complement traditional lipid parameters and provide additional information on cardiovascular risk [8–10]. For example, one study reported that AIP, LCI, AC, CRI-I, and CRI-II were stronger predictors of the coronary artery aneurysm risk than conventional lipid parameters [8]. Another study found that AIP better predicted mortality risk than individual cholesterol measures in older adults [10]. Additionally, AIP has been shown to predict long-term cardiovascular outcomes in community-based cohorts independently of traditional lipid parameters [9].

Numerous modifiable factors contribute to the development of atherosclerosis, among which diet plays a central role [11]. In particular, the Mediterranean diet, characterized by high consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, low-fat dairy products, nuts, seeds, and extra virgin olive oil as the main source of fat, has been widely recognized for its cardioprotective properties [12, 13]. Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with reduced CVD risk and improvements in cardiovascular risk factors [12–14], including reductions in body fat [14], total cholesterol levels [15], vascular function [16], systemic inflammation and the progression of subclinical atherosclerosis [17]. However, its specific impact on lipid-derived AI remains unclear, as no studies to date have directly examined this association. This question is particularly relevant in individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk, such as those following a weight-loss dietary intervention. In these individuals, excess adiposity contributes to chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and disturbances in lipid metabolism through multiple interrelated mechanisms, including increased release of free fatty acids, elevated proinflammatory cytokine production, and enhanced hepatic VLDL-C synthesis, which may collectively attenuate the protective effects of the Mediterranean diet [18, 19]. It was therefore hypothesized that greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet would be associated with more favorable AI and lipid profiles in individuals seeking a weight-loss dietary program. Furthermore, body fat was hypothesized to play a key role in this relationship by acting either as a mediator or an effect modifier, potentially attenuating the cardioprotective effects of the Mediterranean diet in individuals with higher adiposity.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and AI and lipid profiles in a large cohort of individuals seeking a weight-loss dietary program, while accounting for body fat, thereby providing novel evidence on the role of adiposity in modulating diet–atherogenic risk relationships.

Material and methods

Study design and sample selection

A total of 10,286 participants were recruited from those attending the International Center for the Assessment of Nutritional Status (ICANS) at the University of Milan from September 2010 to September 2022. Enrollment was restricted to individuals aged over 18 who did not meet any of the exclusion criteria: diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer within the past 5 years, hepatitis C virus infection, hepatitis B virus infection, liver cirrhosis, neurological disease, gastrointestinal disease, cardiac failure, renal failure, pulmonary insufficiency and use of drugs known to cause lipodystrophy. At recruitment, all participants underwent a series of assessments, starting with a medical interview to evaluate their medical history, current drug therapies, smoking habits, and physical activity. Physical activity was assessed by asking participants whether they practiced any structured activity and, if so, how many hours per week they spent on it. Individuals reporting ≥ 2 h per week of structured physical activity were classified as active [20]. Additionally, participants underwent a nutritional evaluation, during which anthropometric measurements were taken, and a fasting blood sample was collected. Finally, all participants were instructed to complete a validated questionnaire to assess their adherence to the Mediterranean diet. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration, and each participant read and signed a written informed consent. The Ethics Committee of the University of Milan approved the study procedures (study protocol No. 23/2016).

Anthropometric measurements

All anthropometric data were collected based on the international guidelines. Before undergoing all measurements, participants were asked to wear only underwear and socks. Weight was measured using a column scale with a precision of 0.1 kg while height was assessed using a stadiometer and rounded to the nearest 0.5 cm. Body Mass Index (BMI) was then calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2) and categorized following World Health Organization (WHO) standards: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2), or obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Waist circumference was measured with a non-elastic tape placed horizontally between the lower border of the rib cage and the iliac crest. Additionally, skinfold thickness at the triceps, biceps, subscapular, and suprailiac sites was recorded using a plicometer with a precision of 0.2 mm. For each site, the measurements were averaged from three consecutive readings. Following international standards, skinfolds were measured on the non-dominant side of each participant. Body density (ρ) was estimated using the equations from Durnin and Womersley [21], which account for both gender and age. Body fat percentage was calculated using Siri’s formula: Body fat % = (4.95/ρ—4.50) × 100.

Adherence to the mediterranean diet

The 14-item MEDAS questionnaire was employed to evaluate adherence to the Mediterranean Diet, a short dietary screening tool validated in the PREMIDED trial [22]. This questionnaire consists of 14 questions designed to assess the frequency of consumption of foods typical of both Mediterranean and Western diets. A point is given for each food item that aligns with the Mediterranean diet criterion. Briefly, 1-point was attributed for each of the following: (1) olive oil as the main cooking fat; (2) olive oil ≥ 4 tablespoons/day; (3) vegetables ≥ 2 servings/day; (4) fruit ≥ 3 servings/day; (5) red or processed meat < 1 serving/day; (6) butter or cream or margarine < 1/day; (7) sugar-sweetened beverages < 1/day; (8) wine ≥ 3 glasses/week; (9) legumes ≥ 3 servings/week; (10) fish/seafood ≥ 3 servings/week; (11) commercial sweets and confectionery < 3/week; (12) nuts ≥ 1/week; (13) white more than red meats (yes) and; (14) use of soffritto ≥ 2/week [23, 24]. The total score, ranging from 0 to 14, is the sum of the individual points.

Blood sampling, lipid profile and atherogenic indices

A fasting blood sample was collected between 8:30 and 9:30 a.m. Fasting TC,HDL-C, LDL-C and TG were measured using enzymatic methods (Cobas Integra 400 Plus; Roche Diagnostics). Model 1 tests the unadjusted association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the outcomes of interest. Model 2 evaluates this association accounting for sex and age. Model 3 further adjusts for BMI, sociodemographic factors, and lipid-lowering treatment. Model 4 incorporates body composition into the analysis. This model investigates whether fat mass acts as a mediator of the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the outcomes of interest. Finally, Model 5 assesses the effect of adherence to the Mediterranean diet in relation to body composition, while controlling for all other potential confounders. This model investigates whether fat mass acts instead as a modifier of the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and the outcomes of interest. Collinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each predictor and for the overall model; all VIF values were < 5, indicating negligible multicollinearity. Homoscedasticity and potential violations of the linearity assumption between continuous predictors and the dependent variable were assessed both graphically, using residual-versus-fitted plots, and through statistical tests. The Breusch-Pagan test was applied to check for heteroscedasticity, and robust confidence intervals were calculated for all models where heteroscedasticity was detected. Multivariable fractional polynomials were used to model potential non-linear associations between continuous predictors and the outcome. Several transformations of age and BMI were selected depending on the model. However, there was only a modest gain in the linearity of continuous predictors when transformations were applied; therefore, to preserve interpretability and readability, all continuous covariates were kept in their original, untransformed form. The significance of interactions was formally tested using Wald tests, and all interactions reported in the manuscript are statistically supported. To address potential overadjustment, additional analyses were performed using a more parsimonious model that excluded covariates showing no association with any outcome of interest (Supplementary tables S1- S2). Results were consistent with those of the main analyses, suggesting that overadjustment was unlikely. In addition, we evaluated the interaction between MEDAS and sex. Analyses showed that the MEDAS#body fat and MEDAS#sex interactions were both significant when tested separately, with identical R2 values, suggesting that they explained the same proportion of variability. Moreover, when the two interaction terms were included simultaneously in the same model, both lost statistical significances, indicating redundancy between the modifiers. In light of this, and considering that body fat showed a greater number of significant interactions and is a continuous and modifiable variable, we decided to report the association between MEDAS, AI, and lipid parameters in relation to body fat. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software, version 18.5 (StataCorp LP).

After adjustment for sex, age, BMI, lifestyle factors, sociodemographic variables, and lipid-lowering drugs (Model 3), the MEDAS score was significantly associated with lower values of AIP, AC, CRI-I, and ACI, and marginally associated with lower CRI-II values. Specifically, each 1-point increase in the MEDAS score was associated with a reduction of 0.003 points in AIP (95% CI: − 0.006, − 0.000, P = 0.049), 0.013 points in AC (95% CI: − 0.024, − 0.001, P = 0.029), 0.013 points in CRI-I (95% CI: − 0.024, − 0.001, P = 0.029), and 0.004 points in ACI (95% CI: − 0.007, − 0.000, P = 0.049). No significant association was found between the MEDAS score and LCI. After further adjustment for body fat percentage (Model 4), all associations were lost statistical significance. However, for four out of six indices, a significant interaction between MEDAS score and body fat percentage (Model 5) was observed. In particular, the strength of the inverse association between the MEDAS score and AI such as AC, CRI-I, CRI-II, and ACI decreased as body fat percentage increased. Discussion

In the present study, adherence to the Mediterranean diet among individuals seeking a weight-loss program was associated with lower AI and a more favorable lipid profile, including lower levels of LDL-C and TC, and higher HDL-C concentrations. Although the effect sizes observed in the models were numerically small, their cumulative impact across the full range of adherence to the Mediterranean diet is clinically meaningful. When considered over the entire MEDAS scale, these associations translate into substantial reductions in AI, supporting the biological relevance of the findings at both the individual and population levels. Interestingly, for several AI and lipid parameters, a significant interaction between Mediterranean diet adherence and fat mass was identified, indicating that adiposity may act as an effect modifier. For AIP specifically, the associations disappeared after adjusting for fat mass, suggesting that adiposity may mediate this relationship.

Some previous studies have examined the relationship between diet and AI. In a study of 150 women aged 18 to 45 years diagnosed with PCOS, higher adherence to a diet rich in phytochemicals, measured by the Dietary Phytochemical Index (DPI), was associated with reduced AIP levels [25]. In another study involving 8,870 adults, Ravansar et al. reported a positive association between higher Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) scores and increased levels of AIP, CRI-I, CRI-II, LCI, and AC [26]. Furthermore, the Isfahan Cohort Study, conducted on 2,256 adults, found that higher scores on the Healthy Lifestyle Score were significantly associated with lower levels of AIP, CRI-I, CRI-II, ACI, and AC [27]. To the best of our knowledge, only one study, conducted on a small sample of 51 participants, has reported that the combination of the Mediterranean diet and physical activity was associated with a reduction in AIP [28].

This study, based on a large cohort of participants, further contributes to the literature by demonstrating that adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with more favorable AI, beyond AIP, and that this effect appears to be mediated by an overall improvement in lipid homeostasis. Specifically, lower levels of TC and LDL-C, and higher HDL-C concentrations were observed in participants with greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet, consistent with findings from several literature reviews [29].

Although the exact mechanisms remain partially unclear, several synergistic effects among the various components of the Mediterranean diet may explain its beneficial impact on lipid metabolism and atherosclerotic risk. This dietary pattern is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, particularly omega-3 and omega-9, mainly derived from extra virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, and oily fish. These healthy fats help lower plasma triglycerides and LDL-C while increasing HDL-C levels [30, 31]. The Mediterranean diet is also high in soluble fiber from fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains, which contributes to reductions in total and LDL-C through fermentation by gut microbiota and enhanced bile acid excretion [32, 33]. Moderate red wine consumption adds polyphenols, particularly resveratrol, which may prevent LDL-C oxidation, improve endothelial function, and reduce inflammatory responses involved in atherogenesis [34], while ethanol has been associated with increased HDL-C levels [35]. ​​Importantly, the Mediterranean diet limits foods high in saturated fats, such as red meat, full-fat dairy products, and ultra-processed foods, which are linked to adverse lipid profiles, higher LDL-C/HDL-C ratios, and increased oxidative stress and inflammation [36, 37], as well as excessive caloric intake and fat accumulation that exacerbate cardiovascular risk [38, 39].

Interestingly, for AI and lipid parameters, such as AC, CRI-I, CRI-II, ACI, TC, and LDL-C, a significant interaction between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and fat mass was observed. Specifically, the strength of the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and these markers decreased as fat mass increased. These findings suggest a modifying effect of adipose tissue, whereby excess body fat may progressively diminish the beneficial effects of the Mediterranean diet. This hypothesis is supported by previous studies. In the observational ATTICA study, adherence to the Mediterranean diet was only modestly associated with lower TC levels among individuals with overweight or obesity [40]. Similar results have been reported in interventional trials, where, in the absence of caloric restriction or weight loss, the Mediterranean diet failed to improve metabolic and lipid profiles [40–42]. Overall, this evidence suggests that the Mediterranean diet should be combined with weight reduction strategies in individuals with overweight or obesity. This finding may be partly explained by the metabolic activity of adipose tissue, especially visceral fat (VAT), which releases free fatty acids, adipokines, and inflammatory cytokines. These substances can impair lipid metabolism by increasing hepatic triglyceride production, reducing HDL-C levels, and promoting LDL-C oxidation [19, 43–46]. As a result, the excess release of these mediators may counteract the beneficial effects of the Mediterranean diet on lipid and atherogenic profiles in individuals with higher body fat. Importantly, excess adiposity is also associated with increased insulin resistance, which represents a key metabolic mechanism linking fat accumulation to adverse lipid alterations. Insulin resistance can further exacerbate hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL-C, thereby potentially counteracting the beneficial effects of the Mediterranean diet on lipid and atherogenic profiles in individuals with higher body fat. It is also plausible that the modifying effect of adiposity reflects underlying sex-related differences in body composition. Women and men differ substantially in total and regional fat distribution, and these differences are known to influence lipid metabolism and atherogenic profiles. In line with this biological framework, the interaction between MEDAS and sex was found to explain essentially the same variability as the interaction between MEDAS and body fat; moreover, when both interaction terms were included simultaneously in the same model, neither retained statistical significance. This redundancy suggests that sex-related differences in the association between Mediterranean diet adherence and lipid outcomes are largely mediated by differences in body fat, supporting adiposity as the biological driver.

For AIP only, the association with adherence to the Mediterranean diet disappeared after adjusting for fat mass, and no interaction was observed. This suggests that adipose tissue acts as a mediator in the association with the Mediterranean diet. In other words, greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet may lead to reduced fat mass, which in turn contributes to lower AIP values. This hypothesis is supported by a previous study, in which higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with lower abdominal obesity, particularly with reduced VAT [47].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The large sample size increased statistical power and the precision of the estimates. In addition, adjustment was made for a wide range of sociodemographic and lifestyle factors that could confound the relationship between diet and lipid outcomes. A further strength of this study is the analysis of lipid parameters and AI as continuous variables. This approach captures the full metabolic variability of the cohort and preserves statistical power by avoiding the limitations of categorical stratification. Furthermore, using continuous data ensures that the findings remain robust regardless of potential future updates to clinical cut-offs for dyslipidemia. Nevertheless, future research should explore whether specific dyslipidemic phenotypes exhibit differential responses to the Mediterranean pattern. Finally, a validated questionnaire to screen for adherence to the Mediterranean diet was used.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents any inference of causality. Second, body composition was evaluated through skinfold measurements, which, while not considered the gold standard, offer a practical estimation of fat-free and fat mass. Although more accurate techniques exist, their application in routine clinical settings is limited by high costs, lengthy procedures, and the requirement for highly specialized personnel. In contrast, skinfold assessments are faster, easier to perform, and, in our center, are characterized by very low intra- and inter-operator variability [48]. Third, participants were recruited from a single center and were primarily individuals seeking a weight loss dietary program. While this may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to the general population, the sample reflects a clinically relevant subgroup at increased cardiometabolic risk. Fourth, although the exclusion criteria were implemented to reduce confounding and strengthen internal validity, this approach may limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals with established chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease or diabetes. Nevertheless, the study population, which includes more than 10,000 individuals actively seeking a weight loss dietary program, reflects a relevant and expanding segment of the population at increased cardiometabolic risk. Fifth, the sample was predominantly composed of women, reflecting the higher tendency of females to seek a dietary weight-loss program [49, 50]. This sex imbalance may limit the generalizability of the findings to a more balanced population. Sixth, dietary adherence was assessed through a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Moreover, the absence of detailed quantitative dietary data (e.g., total energy intake or macronutrient distribution) prevented exploration of potential confounding or mediation through specific dietary components. Seventh, no formal correction for multiple hypothesis testing was applied, given the exploratory nature of the study and the use of pre-specified regression models. Therefore, the possibility of type I error cannot be completely excluded. Finally, as with any observational study, potential residual confounding could not be ruled out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adherence to the Mediterranean diet is associated with more favorable atherogenic and lipid profiles in individuals seeking weight‑loss treatment; however, these benefits are significantly modulated by body fat content. Excess adiposity may attenuate or even neutralize the positive effects of the Mediterranean diet on lipid parameters. Importantly, these findings highlight the need to implement Mediterranean diet–based dietary interventions within integrated clinical strategies that also target body fat reduction. Such an approach may optimize cardiovascular risk management and improve patient care by jointly addressing dietary quality and body composition in individuals at increased cardiometabolic risk.
